## 21. What the Water Left

Ivo woke on the crypt floor with her cheek against wet stone and the taste of iron in her mouth, and the first thing she understood was that she could not hum.

She had been humming for three days without meaning to—the melody from the dream, the drowned choir's tune, threading through every waking hour like a hair caught in a comb. Now she opened her mouth out of habit, out of some animal need to check that the world was still shaped the way she'd left it, and nothing came. Not silence exactly. Her breath moved; her throat worked; but somewhere between the wanting and the sound there was a door that had closed, quietly, the way a well cover closes.

She lay still and let herself feel it.

Not taken. That was the strange part, the part she would spend weeks learning to say correctly. The thing beneath Old Chapel Road had not reached up through the black water and plucked her voice out like a splinter. She had spent it—poured it into the eighth verse along with Maren's cracked alto and Ellery's soundless, shape-making mouth, given it away note by note because the song had to be meant, and meaning had cost everything she had in her chest. She remembered the last of it going: a high, wavering note near the end that she'd held longer than she'd ever held anything, while the water screamed and the vicar came apart like wet paper and the founders' mouthless bones finally, finally stopped singing through her.

She had thought, in that moment, that she was dying. She had been wrong about many things in Hollowmere.

The crypt was draining around her. She could hear it—a vast, slow exhalation, water sliding down through channels in the stone that Ivo suspected had not carried water in a hundred years, or perhaps had always carried it back where it belonged. The black was going out of what remained. Where the flood had stood waist-high against the walls, a tideline of grey silt marked its passing, and below that line the stone was simply stone again, old and cold and honest.

Maren was sitting against the far wall with Ellery's head in her lap. Both of them were looking at Ivo. Neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of language by now; they had all three learned, in the dark under the chapel, how much could be said without spending breath.

Ivo pushed herself upright. Every joint reported in, one by one, like villagers counting their doors. Her hands were shaking. She looked at them anyway, turned them over, examined the silt in the creases of her knuckles, and found that she was—there was no other word for it—calm. Not peaceful. Peace was a word for people who hadn't been underwater. But calm the way a field is calm after harvest: stripped, exhausted, paid out to the last stalk.

"It's done," she said. Her speaking voice worked. It was rougher than before, gravelled at the bottom, but it worked, and hearing it come out as ordinary words instead of song felt like putting on someone else's coat—close enough to warm her, wrong enough to notice.

Maren nodded slowly. Then, because she was Maren, and because grief and relief lived side by side in her the way bees lived in the hive—industrious, stinging, sweet—she started to laugh. It was a terrible laugh, hiccupping and half-sobbed, and Ellery joined it silently, his shoulders shaking, his ruined throat working around a sound that would never come, and after a moment Ivo laughed too, all three of them crouched in the draining dark of a haunted crypt, laughing like survivors of a shipwreck who have just realized the island has trees.

Above them, faintly, filtered down through the broken chapel floor, came a sound none of them had heard in Hollowmere before.

Someone was singing. In daylight. On purpose.

* * *

They climbed out through the collapse, Maren first, hauling Ellery up hand over hand while Ivo braced him from below, and emerged blinking into a morning so aggressively ordinary it felt like a practical joke. Sun on wet cobbles. Smoke from chimneys. The smell of bread from somewhere, actual bread, baked by actual human hands.

Old Chapel Road was running with water—but clean water, bright water, sheeting off the chapel steps and down the gutters toward the low end of the village, carrying leaves and silt and, here and there, small pale things Ivo chose not to look at too closely. The road itself seemed scrubbed. And standing in it, scattered along its length like people emerging from a long illness, were the villagers of Hollowmere.

They weren't celebrating. Ivo had expected—she didn't know what she'd expected. Bells. Embraces. Some ceremony. Instead they stood quietly in the morning light, looking at the water as it ran, and several of them were crying without any apparent awareness of it, tears just moving down their faces while they watched the gutters the way you'd watch a fever break.

Tam Weller sat on his own doorstep with a bottle between his knees, unopened, which Ivo suspected was the most eloquent prayer he knew how to make. The landlady from the pub stood in the middle of the road barefoot, letting the runoff go over her feet, and when she saw Ivo she didn't wave or speak. She pressed her hand flat over her heart and bowed her head, briefly, the way you'd acknowledge a debt you knew could never be entered properly in any ledger.

Vicar Ansel Crane was nowhere. Later Ivo would learn what the others already knew: that when the sixth verse he'd forced out of his own throat had turned on him—when the thing under the well had discovered, in its final convulsions, that a false singer is worse than no singer—it had taken him whole. There was nothing to bury. The vestry records would say he resigned his living and departed, and only four people in Hollowmere would know otherwise, and they would keep it, because some truths are heavier than villages can carry and lighter than silence.

The well itself, when Ivo walked to it—her legs steadying with each step, her body relearning the price of motion—was full.

That startled her. She had expected empty, drained to the bottom, a dry throat of stone. Instead it stood brimming to within a foot of the rim, and the water was clear. Not black. Not even green. Clear as glass over old coins, and utterly still despite the morning's breeze, and when Ivo leaned over the lip and looked down into it she saw only her own face, wavering, and beneath her face the suggestion of stones, and beneath the stones nothing at all.

Nothing looked back. For the first time since her arrival, nothing in Hollowmere looked back.

She stayed there a long moment, hands on the cold rim, breathing. Somewhere behind her a child laughed—one of the children who had been kept indoors since the well refilled, out now, running the length of the road with the uncomplicated ferocity of the young—and the sound went through Ivo like sunlight through water.

Her lips parted. The melody rose in her, automatic, the drowned choir's tune offering itself up the way it had for days.

And nothing happened. No sound. Just the shape of the tune pressing against the inside of her closed throat, patient, homeless.

She closed her mouth. She found, examining the feeling honestly, that it did not hurt. It felt like a room in her house that she would never furnish—not boarded up, not forbidden, just finished with. Paid for.

* * *

The tally marks washed from her doorframe on the second day.

She noticed it by accident, coming back from the pub with bread and a jar of Maren's honey, and stopped dead in front of her own cottage because the wood beside the door was bare. Nineteen chalk tallies, five-barred gates marching down the frame, counted night by night toward the well's cresting—and gone. Not smeared. Washed, cleanly, as though a hand had wiped them with a wet cloth, leaving the wood underneath pale and new-looking, as if the counting had never soaked in at all.

Ivo stood there until her arms got cold from holding the bread against her chest.

She understood what it meant, or believed she did. The arithmetic was finished. Whatever ledger the village kept—the doors counted aloud, the lamps shuttered, the nights tallied toward an ending—had balanced and closed. Hollowmere was out of the bargain now, and bargains left no marks once they were paid.

But standing there, she felt something more complicated than relief. Those nineteen marks had been a sentence passed on her, yes—an outsider measured like grain, counted toward use. They had also been, in a way she was only now honest enough to admit, the first time in her life anyone had bothered to keep track of her at all. Thirty-eight years of being unremarkable on purpose, of making herself small enough to slip through the world's attention, and the only place that had ever counted her nights was a village that counted them because it intended to spend her.

She went inside. She took the chalk stub from the drawer where she kept her field notebooks, and after a long moment she drew a single mark on the clean doorframe. One gate. One night.

Then another, the next evening. And another.

Not counting toward anything. Just counting. Because she had decided, somewhere between the crypt and the doorstep, that she was staying—staying the way you stay in a house you've chosen, not a house you've been placed in—and a person who stays should mark her nights herself.

* * *

The harvest failed that autumn, gently.

There was no blight, no rot, no dramatic ruin. The fields simply produced what fields produce when nothing extra is underwriting them: honest, moderate yields, a little thin at the edges, nothing like the impossible bounty the old-timers remembered from their grandparents' stories. Apples smaller than the ones in the founders' photographs. Barley that needed actual work. Tam Weller stood in his daughter's field in October and pronounced it "a proper crop at last," which everyone understood to mean: a crop we earned.

Some of the villagers grieved the change more than they admitted. Ivo saw it in the market square, in the way certain hands lingered on produce, weighing memory against reality. Forty years of surplus makes a habit of the soul. There were evenings at the pub when someone would start to say something wistful about the old yields and then stop, glancing at the window, at the dark, at the direction of the chapel, and the wistfulness would fold itself away like linen.

But no one asked her to give it back. That was the thing Ivo waited for, the request she braced against through the whole turning year—that some desperate neighbor would corner her and ask whether the voice could be recovered, whether the ritual could be run again, whether she might reconsider. It never came. Perhaps because of Ellery, whose silence sat in the village like a stone in a shoe, reminding everyone what the alternative to her choice had looked like. Perhaps because of the vicar's absence, which had its own eloquence. Or perhaps—she allowed herself to believe this on her better days—because they had all stood in the road that morning and watched clean water run down Old Chapel Road, and understood, in the marrow-deep way villagers understand things, that some prices are correct.

* * *

Winter came, and with it the long work of learning.

Ellery learned to write fast, then to whistle—thin, reedy whistles that made Maren laugh and made him grin with a crooked triumph—and eventually to speak again in a whisper, a few words at a time, hoarded like coins. His voice never came back whole. He said—Ivo read it in one of his notes, in his looping careful hand—that he didn't mind, that having words rationed taught him which ones mattered. He used his rationed words generously anyway. He spent most of them on jokes.

Maren's bees overwintered better than anyone's crops, which she declared vindication of some cosmic kind. She came to Ivo's cottage two or three evenings a week now without knocking protocol, letting herself in to the warmth with the ease of family, bringing honey or complaints or both. Some nights they sat in silence doing separate work—Ivo transcribing, Maren mending—and it was, Ivo thought more than once, the truest company she had ever kept. Company that didn't require performance. Company that had seen her sing badly on purpose, underground, and loved her for it.

Because that was the discovery that undid thirty-eight years of deliberate unremarkability: they had heard her at her worst. Off-key, terrified, voice shredding, meaning every word. And they had stayed. Belonging, it turned out, couldn't be bought with silence—but it could be built out of shared ruin, and it held weight.

The village learned her name, too. Not Yarrow, the folklorist. Ivo. It happened gradually, the way frost happens—unremarkable, then everywhere. Children called it across the lane. Old Mrs. Petchy, who had shuttered her lamp every night of Ivo's first month, now waved her in for tea and told her stories about her grandmother's grandmother, unprompted, unguarded, the archive opening at last not because Ivo had pried it but because the fear was gone and the stories wanted telling.

Ivo recorded all of it. Her recorder still worked; her ears still worked; her scholar's patience still worked. What was gone was only the singing, and she found the loss sat strangely lightly, like a coat returned to its owner. She had come to Hollowmere to collect songs that were guarded by fear. She was leaving—no. She was not leaving. She was remaining among songs that were given freely, and it occurred to her, one blue evening in January with snow coming down past the lamplight, that this was the only archive that had ever truly mattered: not the songs themselves, but the fact of a village that could sing them twice.

* * *

On the first of February, she walked out to the well.

Snow lay on Old Chapel Road unbroken except for her own prints and the stitched tracks of birds. The chapel still stood open-roofed and hollow, its floor a wound they had agreed not to mend yet—some wounds should be visible for a while—but the well beside it was serene, ringed in white, its water still and clear and deep and holding nothing but the reflection of the falling sky.

Ivo stood at the rim for a long time. Then she did the thing she had been circling for weeks, the test she kept postponing the way you postpone reading a letter you're afraid of.

She opened her mouth and tried to sing.

One note. Just one, the lowest note of the harvest lament, the one Tam Weller had refused to repeat in the pub a lifetime ago. She felt for it the way you feel for a step in the dark—and found the step gone, the staircase gone, the whole architecture of melody dissolved in her like sugar in water. Her breath came out plain. Unshaped. Hers.

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, fogging the air—and the laugh echoed slightly off the chapel walls, and nothing beneath the earth answered it.

"Right," she said aloud, to the well, to the bones resting easy under the clean water, to whatever part of herself had needed to hear it confirmed. "Paid in full."

She turned back up the road toward her cottage, toward the lamp she'd begun leaving lit past nine out of pure stubborn habit, toward the tally marks accumulating one by one on her doorframe like a heartbeat. Behind her the snow went on falling into the well, flake by flake, vanishing into still water that gave nothing back and asked for nothing either.

At her door she paused, brushed the snow from the newest chalk mark—seven gates now, seven nights, counted by her own hand—and let herself in.

Outside, far down the lane, a figure was trudging through the weather toward the village, pulling a suitcase, and stopped at the sight of her lamplight the way travelers stop at any light in any storm.

Ivo watched from her window, cup cooling in her hands, and wondered—not with dread, not yet, only with the particular attention of someone who has learned what arrivals mean—what the winter had brought her.
