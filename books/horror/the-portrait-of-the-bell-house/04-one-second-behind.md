## 4. One Second Behind

The delay was a full second now. Ada knew because they had measured it, because measuring things was the one prayer they still believed in.

They stood in the east hallway on the fourth morning, laser level humming against their palm, and watched themselves in the tall gilt mirror that leaned against the wall like a guest who'd overstayed a century. Ada raised their left hand. The reflection raised its left hand — one heartbeat later. Ada lowered it. The reflection held the pose a moment longer, fingers splayed against the glass from the inside, as if pressing to be let out, before it remembered to come down.

Ada said nothing. They had gotten very good at saying nothing in this house. It was becoming a professional skill, right alongside square footage calculations and depreciation schedules, and they hated how easily the skill came, how their fifteen years of explaining away noises — the settling of old buildings, the complaints of old pipes, the grief of old wiring — had trained them for exactly this: a house that moved a half-beat behind itself and expected to be excused.

But a half-second could be exhaustion. A half-second could be bad silvering in old glass, light scattering through flawed mercury, the eye's own lag filling in the gap. Ada had written those explanations down in the surveyor's log in neat, defensible handwriting, and they had almost believed them.

A full second was not exhaustion. A full second was a choice someone was making.

They timed it again, properly, phone stopwatch in one hand, the other rising and falling in crisp metronome strokes. *Up.* The reflection followed, late. *Down.* Late again. Then Ada stopped moving entirely, arm at their side, breath held, and the reflection did not stop. It went on moving — a small continuation, a finishing gesture, the hand completing an arc Ada's hand had never begun, then settling, then turning its head very slightly, as if checking whether anyone had noticed.

Ada noticed. Ada's whole body noticed; the skin along their forearms lifted like grass before weather.

"Okay," they said aloud, to the mirror, to the house, to whatever was keeping time in there. "Okay."

The reflection said nothing, but its mouth was a half-shape, the ghost of a word arriving late, and Ada had the terrible, precise sense that if they waited long enough — if they stood here past midnight, past some threshold the house kept like a landlord keeps keys — they would hear what the reflection was trying to say, and it would be in their own voice, and it would be older than they were.

* * *

The figures began that afternoon.

Ada was measuring the drawing room — twenty-two feet by nineteen, crown molding water-damaged, windows painted shut since some administration neither of them would ever learn the name of — when they glanced up into the long mirror above the mantel and saw the room behind them.

It was not the room they were standing in.

In the glass, the drawing room stretched deeper than it should have, walls where no walls were, a doorway opening onto a corridor Ada had never surveyed, and in that corridor stood people. Three of them. Still as furniture. A woman in a high-collared dress gone gray with age or with being-seen-through, her hands folded. A man whose outline kept trying to decide whether he wore a uniform or a shroud. And smaller, between them, at hip height, a shape that made Ada's chest close up like a fist, because it was child-sized and it was wet, and the wetness in the mirror did not reflect the lamplight the way water should.

Ada turned around. The corridor was plaster. The doorway was wallpaper over brick. The room was empty except for Ada, the level, the dust motes turning in the light like slow, indifferent snow.

They turned back to the mirror. The figures were still there. The woman had turned her head — in the glass only — and was looking directly at Ada, and her mouth was moving, working around syllables that arrived a full second after her lips shaped them, so that Ada watched her speak and then, one heartbeat later, felt rather than heard the words land somewhere behind their sternum:

*You're counting wrong.*

Ada dropped the laser level. It clattered off the floorboards and rolled under a chair, and when they looked back at the mantel glass, the drawing room in the mirror was only a drawing room, ordinary and dim, reflecting Ada alone, holding a stopwatch they didn't remember raising.

The stopwatch read 00:01.00. It always read one second now. Ada was beginning to suspect the number wasn't a measurement. It was a message. *This is how far behind you are. This is how much of you hasn't caught up.*

They found Wren in the conservatory, wrapped in a blanket the color of weak tea, doing a crossword in pencil with the patience of someone who had done every crossword ever printed and now did them mostly out of courtesy.

"You look," Wren said without looking up, "like a person who has been talking to mirrors."

"The mirrors are talking back."

"Mm." Wren filled in a square. "Seven letters. 'What the dead do instead of leaving.' I'm going with 'linger,' though I suspect the puzzle-setter wanted something crueler."

"Wren." Ada sat down across from them, hard, on the wicker chair that groaned like it resented the weight of the conversation coming. "I need you to tell me what this house is."

Wren set down the pencil. Up close, in the greenish light through the fogged glass panels, the ninety-one years showed all at once, the way a painting shows its brushwork when you stop squinting at the scene. Skin like tissue paper over a structure that seemed, suddenly, provisional. Eyes still bright, though. Bright and tired in equal measure, the way a lamp is both when the oil runs low.

"What did you see?" Wren asked.

"A second of lag. Then my reflection finishing movements I hadn't started. Then—" Ada's voice did something they didn't authorize; it gentled. "Then three people in a hallway that doesn't exist. One of them was small."

The pencil rolled off the crossword book. Neither of them reached for it.

"Yes," Wren said. "That would be the east hall. They favor it. Good light, even now." A pause, and then, with the mildness of a person discussing drainage: "The house sings, Ada. I should have told you sooner. I told myself you'd hear it eventually and think it was pipes, because everyone thinks it's pipes, and I let myself be selfish about it. But you've seen them now, so the polite fictions are spent."

"The house sings."

"Lullabies, mostly. Fragments. It's been singing the same song for a hundred years and it has never once managed to finish it." Wren smiled, and the smile was the charming thing it always was, and underneath it was something Ada could only call *held*, the way a breath is held, the way a door is held shut. "It hums through the walls at night. In the nursery it's louder. You've been in the nursery."

"I found sheet music. Missing its last page."

"Yes." Wren drew the blanket tighter. "Yes, you would have."

"And the people in the mirror—"

"Are the ones it sings about." Wren's voice stayed level, but their hands, Ada noticed, had gone very still in their lap, stiller than hands go naturally, the stillness of someone keeping something down. "Ada. Listen to me. You're a careful person. I've watched you measure doorframes like you're taking their statements. So be careful about this too. The house will sing for you if you listen closely enough. It wants an audience. It has wanted one for a century. And if you learn the song — really learn it, verse by verse, the way it wants —"

"What happens?"

Wren looked out at the fogged glass, at the garden gone soft and formless beyond it, and for a moment they looked less like ninety-one and more like a child who had been promised something a very long time ago and had stopped believing the promiser.

"I don't know," they said. "That's the truth. I've spent my whole life not knowing, on purpose. It's the only vow I've ever kept." They turned back, and took Ada's hand, and their grip was cold and light and absolutely deliberate. "So don't listen closely. Wear earplugs in the nursery. Hum when it starts, loudly, badly, anything. Because the song is the house's confession, and confessions, once heard all the way through, want something. And I cannot —" the word cracked, just slightly, a fine hairline fracture in the charm — "I am not ready for it to want what it wants."

Ada turned their hand over and held Wren's properly, both of theirs around the one cold one, and felt the fear in themselves braid together with something fiercer and less professional.

"Who is it a confession *about*, Wren?"

The fog thickened against the glass. Somewhere deep in the walls — below the floor, inside the joists, in the space between lath and plaster where houses keep their oldest silence — something began, very softly, to hum.

Wren closed their eyes.

"Don't," they whispered. Not to Ada. To the walls. "Not yet. Please. She's not finished measuring."

And the humming stopped — obediently, instantly, the way a dog stops at its master's voice — and Ada sat in the ringing quiet holding the hand of a person the house called *she*, and understood, with the cold clarity of a level finding true, that Wren Bell had just given away more than they meant to.

The house sang. The house obeyed Wren. Which meant the house loved Wren.

Which meant everything it had done for a hundred years, it had done *for* them.

Ada's own face looked back at them from the darkened conservatory window, and for the first time they checked, out of habit now, whether it was a second late.

It wasn't late at all.

It was smiling, and Ada was not.
