## 7. The Appraisal That Appraises Back

The house had stopped being appraised and started doing the appraising.

Ada understood this on the ninth morning, standing in the east corridor with a laser measure in one hand and a clipboard gone soft with damp in the other, when they noticed that the mirror at the end of the hall was not showing them the corridor at all.

It was showing them a different Ada.

Younger. Twenty-four, maybe twenty-five — the jaw softer, the hair long enough to be pulled back in the careless knot Ada hadn't worn since the year they'd stopped caring what anyone thought of how they looked. That younger Ada stood in the same corridor, in the same gray surveyor's vest, but the clipboard was different: older, water-stained, stamped with the logo of Halloway & Finch Property Services, where Ada had worked their first job out of certification.

Ada raised a hand. The reflection raised its hand on time now — the lag had closed, or been closed, deliberately, like a shopkeeper straightening a display before a customer arrived.

"Hello," Ada said to the glass.

The younger Ada mouthed something back. No sound came through, but Ada knew the shape of the word. They'd said it themselves, fifteen years ago, standing in the doorway of the Delgado family's apartment on Corliss Street with an eviction notice in one hand and a court order in the other, while Mrs. Delgado held her grandson's hand too tightly and asked, *Is there nothing you can do?*

*It's not my decision.*

That was what the mirror-mouthed word was. Ada could read it like braille on their own history.

They lowered the clipboard. The reflection did not lower anything. It only stood there, patient as furniture, waiting for Ada to understand that it was not a reflection at all but an exhibit — evidence entered into a record Ada hadn't known they were the subject of.

Behind them, somewhere deep in the house, a floorboard sighed. The sound of someone settling in to watch.

* * *

They found the second version of themselves in the nursery, of all places, sitting in the small chair by the window as if it had always belonged there. This one was even younger — twenty-three, the week before Halloway & Finch, still wearing the optimism like an unearned uniform. It looked up when Ada entered and smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because Ada remembered giving that exact smile to Mr. Okafor on the day he'd signed the papers that gutted his triple-decker, and they remembered believing, sincerely, that paperwork was neutral. That they were just the messenger. That the messenger's hands stayed clean because the message did the killing.

"You're not real," Ada told it.

"No," the young Ada agreed pleasantly, in Ada's own voice, pitched slightly wrong, like a recording played through a wall. "But I'm accurate. That's what this house does. It keeps things exactly as they were." The young self tilted its head toward the window, where the garden lay drowned in fog. "Wren says you've been very thorough. Room by room. Measurement by measurement. The house appreciates thoroughness."

"The house can appreciate whatever it likes. I'm here for a number, not a friendship."

"It knows." The young Ada folded its hands. "It's watched you work. It likes your hands. It likes that you touch the walls when you think no one's looking. It liked your mother's house, too — the way you measured every room the summer before she sold it, because you wanted to keep the dimensions somewhere safe." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You've been keeping houses safe your whole life. This one would like to return the favor."

Ada's skin went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the draft. Because it was true — all of it, every detail, down to the notebook where they'd written *12'4" x 11'9", Mom's room, don't lose this* — and there was no version of events in which Wren Bell could have known about that notebook. Only the house could know. The house, which heard everything said inside its walls, and apparently everything thought.

"What do you want?" Ada asked.

"To make you an offer," said the house, through the mouth of who they used to be.

* * *

It made the offer over three days, which Ada grudgingly respected. Even temptation, done properly, should be built like a case file.

On the first day, the mirrors showed them the Delgado eviction again — full length this time, the whole corridor become a gallery of that afternoon. Ada walking away from the building. Mrs. Delgado's face in the doorway. And then, branching off from the moment like a road not taken, the mirror showed the other version: Ada turning around. Going back up the stairs. Tearing the notice in half in front of the county marshal, taking the contempt charge, losing the job, and — this was the detail that undid them — Mrs. Delgado's grandson, ten years later, graduating. Alive in the mirror's imagining, alive because one person had refused to be a clean pair of hands.

"That person existed," the house murmured from the walls, from the pipes, from under the floorboards where Maren Hale's diary slept. "You met them once. You let them walk past you."

"I couldn't have stopped it," Ada said aloud, to nobody, knowing the house was everybody. "One surveyor against a bank. It would have changed nothing."

"It would have changed you."

* * *

On the second day, the house showed them comfort.

Ada woke in the blue bedroom — they'd begun sleeping at Bell House; Wren had insisted, and Ada had stopped pretending to resist — to find the room transformed. Not rearranged. *Finished.* The plaster repaired, the window glass clear, a fire burning in a grate that had been a cold black mouth since their first visit. On the nightstand: tea, exactly the temperature they liked, though they'd never told anyone how they took it. And on the walls, framed, the dimensions of every room Ada had ever loved. Their mother's kitchen. Their first apartment, the one with the good light. The reading room of the county archive where they'd spent three days learning how Thomas Bell's son had drowned and how Maren Hale had hanged for a murder she hadn't committed.

Every room rendered lovingly. Every dimension kept safe.

"Stay," said the house, gently, the way Wren said it. "You measure things so they won't be lost. Here, nothing is lost. Here, you will never fill out another form that ends in someone's grief. You will never again be the clean hands. We will love you specifically. We are very good at it — we've had a hundred years of practice loving one person, and we have capacity for more."

Ada sat with the tea and did not drink it, because drinking it felt like signing.

And God help them, they were tempted. Not by the fire or the tea or the repaired plaster, but by the arithmetic of it. Fifteen years of forms and fees and foreclosures processed without incident. Fifteen years of telling themselves the system was broken but they were only a component, and components bore no moral weight. The house was offering them the one thing no court had ever offered: a verdict on themselves. Guilty, it said. But forgivable. Stay, and be forgiven by being kept.

"Wren dies if I finish the song," Ada said quietly.

"Yes."

"And if I don't?"

"We keep you both." The word *keep* landed with terrible tenderness. "Forever is a large house. There is room."

* * *

On the third day, the house made its mistake.

It showed Ada the future — or a future, rendered in the long mirror at the stair landing with the confidence of a thing that had never once been wrong. In it, Ada stood in the finished nursery, silver-haired, content, teaching a child — some child, any child, a child the house had conjured or adopted or grown from the wallpaper — the first two verses of the lullaby. Never the third. The child sang along happily. Through the windows, the garden bloomed in permanent June. And in the corner of the reflected room, faint as breath on glass, stood the dead: Maren Hale with the rope's shadow still printed on her throat, the drowned boy Thomas dripping politely on the rug, a dozen others pressed against the inside of the mirror like faces against a train window, mouthing the words Ada still could not hear.

Everyone comfortable. Everyone kept. Nothing finished, nothing confessed, nothing freed.

And Ada looked at that shining future and finally saw it clearly — not as mercy but as the oldest trick in appraisal: *comparable properties.* The house was pricing itself against Ada's guilt and finding the guilt sufficient collateral.

"No," Ada said.

The mirror flickered.

"You're asking me to be what I've been afraid I already am," Ada went on, voice steadying as they named it. "A person who sees a wrong and files it instead of fighting it. You've spent three days showing me her face—" they pointed at Maren, at the rope-shadow, "—because you think shame is a leash. But here's what your comparable sales missed. Maren Hale tried to save him. She failed, and the house hanged her for its own crime, and she has been standing in your mirrors for a hundred years waiting for someone to say it out loud. She didn't stay silent to be comfortable. Neither will I."

The nursery version of their younger self, seated by the window, looked up with an expression that was no longer Ada's at all — something older wearing the old face, something wounded and vast.

"You would trade being loved," it said, "for being right?"

"I'd trade being *kept*" Ada said, "for being *free*. So would you, if you remembered what freedom was. That's what the last verse is, isn't it? Not a confession. A door."

The house went quiet in the particular way of a large animal deciding whether to be offended. Down the hall, a door Ada had never been able to open — the sealed room at the west end, the one Wren always steered them past — unlocked itself with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Ada stood in the corridor a long moment, heart hammering, clipboard forgotten. Then they walked toward the open door, because they were, whatever else, thorough.

Inside, on a desk beneath a sheet gone gray with a century of dust, lay Maren Hale's diary — the real one, not the copy Ada had found under the floorboards — opened to the back cover. And tucked into the binding, yellowed and torn along one edge, folded twice with the care of a woman hiding evidence from a house that saw everything:

The final page of the sheet music.

Ada reached for it. Behind them, from everywhere and nowhere, the house began — softly, desperately, like a child afraid of the dark — to sing.
