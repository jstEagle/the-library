## 10. Empty Lot

The last verse came out of Ada the way a splinter comes out of a wound — slowly, painfully, and with a strange relief on either side of the hurt.

They stood in the flooded nursery, water to their shins, black and mirror-still, and they sang. Not with the house. Not in harmony with the hundred voices the walls were offering, the sweet stacked chords of every Bell who had ever been kept, every servant who had ever been spent. Ada sang alone, flat and human and off-key in places, and that was the point. A confession could not be a duet. A confession belonged to the one who had done the thing, and the house had done the thing, and so Ada stood in its flooded heart and made it listen to its own name.

*Hush now, the water is only a door.*

The words Maren Hale had stitched into the binding of her diary a hundred years ago, in ink gone brown as old blood. Ada sang them and the water shivered around their knees.

*I held him and the house held me, and only one of us let go.*

Above them, in the walls, in the floors, in the glass of every mirror on every landing, the house made a sound Ada had no word for. It was not a scream. Screams were for creatures with throats. It was more like what a house would sound like if a house exhaled — a century of held breath leaving all at once through every crack and sill and seam, rattling the nursery window in its frame, blowing the smell of lake water and lye and old roses through the halls.

And the mirrors began to break.

Not all at once. Sequentially. Like a held breath released one syllable at a time. Ada heard it as they climbed the stairs, water streaming off them, the final verse still ringing in their chest: a crack from the hall mirror on the ground floor, then the gilt oval in the parlor, then the long cheval glass in Wren's mother's room, each one a bright percussive note going up the house like a scale, like the song finishing itself in the only language left to it. Through the doorway of the east bedroom Ada caught the last of them — a mirror the size of a door shattering inward, and behind the glass, for one half of one second, faces. Dozens. A young man with lake water in his hair. A woman with Maren's steady gray eyes. A child. All of them looking out, all of them mouthing the same word, and this time Ada heard it, or felt it, in the marrow rather than the ear:

*Free.*

Then glass, and then nothing, and then the house was only a house.

* * *

Ada found Wren in the front hall, beneath the place where the great mirror had hung for a hundred and forty years. There was a rectangle of unfaded wallpaper where the frame had been, pale as a scar, and Wren stood in front of it leaning on the banister, and Ada understood with a lurch that Wren had been waiting there — that Wren had known, the way you know a song you've heard your whole life, exactly which verse was coming and what it would cost.

"You finished it," Wren said. Their voice was different. Smaller. Not weaker, exactly — *unburdened*, the way a room sounds different after the furniture is gone.

"I finished it."

"And you didn't let it sing with you." Something moved through Wren's face that was too complicated to be a smile and too warm to be anything else. "Stubborn. I did tell you it liked stubborn."

The house was coming down around them in the gentlest possible way. Not collapsing — *settling*. Plaster sifting down like snow. Wallpaper loosening at the seams and curling back like pages. The smell of the place was changing too, the lake-rot and the lye thinning out, and underneath it something clean and green, the smell of the lot before the house was ever built, Ada thought, the smell of the world minding its own business.

Wren's hand found the banister and missed it.

Ada caught them before the second miss. Ninety-one years old, and lighter than they should have been — lighter every second, Ada realized, holding them, as though the century the house had been lending them was being called back loan by loan. Wren's hair had gone from silver to white in the space of a morning. The fine lines around their eyes had deepened into the honest geography of a long life, and still, even now, even sinking, there was that mischief in them, banked like a coal.

"You could have stopped," Wren said. "It offered you everything. It offered you *me*."

"I know."

"You chose the dead over the living. Over me." No accusation in it. Wonder, maybe. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone in this house chose the dead?"

Ada lowered them to the floor of the front hall, cradling the back of Wren's head, and the dust of the settling house fell around them like the first soft weather of a new season. "I didn't choose the dead over you," Ada said. "I chose the truth over the lie it was keeping you alive inside. There's a difference."

"There is." Wren's breath was coming slower now. Their eyes — gray, clear, finally entirely their own — moved past Ada to the bare wall where the mirror had been. "You know what I was, all this time? One second behind. All my life, one second behind everything. I'd laugh at a joke and my laughter arrived late. I'd reach for someone's hand and my hand got there after the moment had passed. The house kept me, and keeping is a kind of lagging." Their fingers found Ada's collar, curled into it, weak as a bird's foot. "But just now — when you sang — I felt it. I'm *ahead* of it now. Ahead of the house. Ahead of everything." A breath. The ghost of the flirt who had disarmed them in chapter one of all this, in the parlor with the rearranging chairs. "One second ahead of nothing at all. It's the freest I've ever been."

"Don't," Ada said, and hated how small it came out.

"Ada." Wren's thumb moved once against their jaw, the same gesture from the tour, from the first day, when Ada had told themselves they were only there for a signature. "I have been ninety-one years old for a hundred years. Do you understand? I have been *ready* for longer than your whole family has been alive. You aren't taking anything from me. You're the first person who ever gave it back."

The house exhaled again, longer, and somewhere above them the last mirror in the attic — one Ada had never found, had never been shown — broke with a sound like a struck bell, and Wren smiled at that, actually smiled, hearing it, and said, "There's the last of them," and then their eyes went to Ada's face and stayed there, and Ada held them while the settling became a stillness, while the breath went out of Wren Bell the way the breath had gone out of the house, unhurried, unafraid, one second ahead of nothing, and grateful — Ada would carry the gratitude of it to their own grave, the *gratitude* — grateful, Wren's lips shaping it at the very last, *thank you, thank you, thank you*, three words in the rhythm of three notes.

Then the front hall was quiet, and Ada was kneeling in the dust of Bell House holding someone who weighed almost nothing at all, and outside the windows the morning came in like it had never once been kept out.

* * *

The appraisal report took Ada three weeks to write, and they wrote it the way they did everything now: carefully, and with the lag gone from their own hands. They had not realized until Bell House how much of their life had been one second behind — the explanations, the deferrals, the tidy reasons not to look too closely at anything. The report was not tidy. It ran forty-one pages. It documented structural failure, water intrusion from an unmapped source, foundation settlement consistent with sudden subsidence, and a history of the property that Ada attached in full: the 1923 drowning, the inquest, the hanging of Maren Hale, and Ada's own findings regarding the diary recovered from beneath the nursery floorboards, which they had turned over to the county historical society with a letter requesting that Maren Hale's conviction be formally reviewed and her name restored. The letter was still pending. Ada suspected it would be granted. Dead men had been exonerated on less than a house's confession.

The report's conclusion was one sentence long, and Ada wrote it without hesitation, which was new for them:

*This property is condemned, and no appraisal of its value is possible, because it has already given everything it had away.*

The county voted to demolish in eleven minutes.

* * *

They came on a gray Thursday in late autumn with an excavator and a crowd of maybe forty people from the town, because a house like Bell House does not die privately. Ada stood at the property line with their clipboard they no longer needed and watched the bucket come down on the east wing, where the nursery had been, and felt — not grief, exactly. Grief had been there all along, a stone they had learned to carry. This was something adjacent to grief. The feeling of a long illness ending. The feeling of a held breath finally, fully released.

The house came down in four hours. It went the way it had settled — gently, almost cooperatively, walls folding inward as though it had been waiting a century for permission to lie down. When the excavator reached the front hall it found nothing beneath the floorboards but ordinary earth, and no trace of the mirrors, and no trace, the crew foreman told Ada afterward, scratching his head, of any of the glass. Hundreds of mirrors in that place, the old photographs showed it, and not one shard in the rubble. As though the house had swallowed its own reflection on the way out.

Ada stayed until the trucks left. Then they stayed longer.

The lot, when it was empty, was just a lot — churned clay and gravel and the stumps of foundation walls, ringed by the old oaks that had watched the whole century and told no one anything. Ada walked out onto it in the blue light of early evening, their boots printing the raw earth, and stood where the front hall had been, where they had held Wren, and let the wind come off the lake and move through them.

It was a wind with something in it.

Three notes. That was all. Carried up from somewhere — the grass, the ground, the air itself — three notes in the rhythm of *thank you, thank you, thank you*, in the rhythm of a finished song, the last three notes of a lullaby a hundred years late and finally, finally complete. Not sad. Not asking anything. The way a door sounds when it closes all the way, the way a confession sounds when it is believed.

Ada stood on the empty lot until full dark, and then they laughed — out loud, alone, one second ahead of nothing at all — and wiped their face with their sleeve, and walked back toward the road, and did not look behind them, because there was nothing behind them anymore that could lag.

At the property line they stopped once. The wind came again through the oaks, and in it, faint and clear and done, the song ended — on a note that was not a question, and not an apology, and not a goodbye.

Just a name. *Maren.* Spoken the way a house speaks when it has finally learned how.

And then the lot was only a lot, and the night was only a night, and Ada Ravensworth went home.
