## 1. The Commission

The lawyer's office smelled like toner and old carpet, and the man behind the desk had the particular pallor of someone who spent his life handling other people's deaths.

"Ms. Sterling." He stood, extended a hand that was damp and brief. "Marcus Deloach. Probate. You come recommended."

"I come expensive," Ada said, and sat without being invited. She'd learned years ago that the fastest way to establish terms was to make the other person uncomfortable first. "Vane estate?"

"You've heard of it."

"I've heard of the address." Marrow House sat at the end of Corvane Lane like a tooth that hadn't decided whether to fall out yet — three stories of gray limestone, a widow's walk, grounds gone to bramble. Everyone in the county knew it the way everyone knows a house where something happened, even if nobody can say what. "The Vane family. 1974."

Deloach's pen stopped moving.

"That was a long time ago," he said.

"It usually is." Ada crossed her legs and waited. She was forty-one, built like a question mark in reverse — all forward lean and direct angles — and she wore her competence the way other women wore jewelry: visibly, and on purpose. Her leather satchel sat between her feet with her laser measure inside it, charged, calibrated, bored. "You have a developer who wants the property, a probate court that wants a valuation, and one living heir who is — what, twelve? Thirteen?"

"Twelve." Something flickered across his face when he said it. Not grief exactly. Relief wearing grief's coat. "Iris Vane. She lives there with her grandmother. Halcyon Vane."

"And you need an appraisal before sale. Standard commercial-residential hybrid assessment, square footage, condition report, comparable properties." She ticked it off on her fingers. "I can have a preliminary figure in—"

"There's a condition," Deloach said.

Ada stopped ticking.

He smoothed his tie. It was burgundy, slightly too shiny, the kind of thing a man buys when he wants to look more successful than he is. "The client has been very specific about scope. Eight floors. You appraise eight floors. The basement counts as a basement, not a floor. And—" he looked at her then, directly, for the first time since she'd sat down, and she saw that his eyes were bloodshot in a way that suggested he hadn't slept well recently, or possibly in years, "—you don't go to the ninth floor."

Ada let the silence sit for a moment, because she was good at her job and good at her job meant knowing when a sentence was wrong.

"Marrow House has eight floors," she said.

"The elevator panel shows nine," Deloach said. "Ignore it."

"The blueprints—"

"Show eight. Yes. I've seen them. There are no permits for a ninth floor, no tax assessments, nothing in the county record. As far as the law is concerned, Marrow House is an eight-story building." He opened a folder and slid a single sheet across the desk. "Which is why the contract specifies eight floors. In writing. Initial there."

She didn't touch the paper. "Mr. Deloach. If a building shows nine floors on its elevator panel and eight on its blueprints, one of two things is true. Either somebody built an illegal floor and buried the paperwork — which affects value, which is my problem — or your client is playing games, which is also my problem. Either way, I don't sign exclusions before I've seen the site. That's not how appraisal works. You can't tell me what a building is before I measure it."

For a long moment Deloach just looked at her, and she watched him do some private arithmetic — how badly the court needed this done, how few appraisers would take it, what it would cost him to find one who would.

"Three times standard rate," he said finally. "And the exclusion stays."

"Four."

"Three and a half, and expenses."

Ada thought about her Q3 pipeline, which was thin, and about the fact that she hadn't turned down work in eleven years, and about the small cold voice in the back of her skull that had been quiet until about ninety seconds ago and was now saying, very clearly: *walk away.*

She'd ignored that voice before. It had a terrible track record of being right.

"Fine," she said, and initialed the page.

* * *

Marrow House announced itself a full quarter mile before you reached it.

The lane narrowed, then narrowed again, hedges rising on both sides until they formed a green corridor with the sky as a ceiling, and then the trees simply gave up and there it was — larger than the photographs, grayer than the photographs, sitting in its own acreage like a ship beached so long the land had grown around it. Limestone gone the color of old dishwater. Windows in ranks, most of them dark, a few reflecting the afternoon sun in flat white squares like eyes caught mid-blink.

Ada parked at the base of the steps and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, doing what she always did before a job: running the numbers. Lot size, roughly four acres. Structure footprint, substantial. Three stories visible from the front — but wait. She counted the window rows again. Four rows above the ground floor. So four stories plus attic space, or the windows were lying, or she was tired. She made a note to check the exterior against the interior. Discrepancies between outside dimensions and inside dimensions were rare but not unheard of in old houses, where additions got stacked on additions by owners who never pulled permits.

It was fine. Everything was fine. It was a big old house with deferred maintenance and a weird legal history, and she was going to measure it, price it, and leave.

She got out. The air smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet.

The front door was oak, black with age, and opened before she knocked.

The woman on the other side was ancient in the way furniture is ancient — still sound, still functional, worn smooth in places by decades of use. Halcyon Vane was small, silver-haired, dressed in a high-collared blouse buttoned to the throat despite the warmth, and she looked at Ada with pale eyes that did something Ada couldn't quite name. They moved over her once, top to bottom, unhurried, the way an assessor looks at a piece of property.

"You're the appraiser," Halcyon said. Not a question.

"Ada Sterling. We spoke on the phone."

"We did." The woman stepped back. "Come in. Mind the threshold — it's uneven. Everything here is uneven."

Ada stepped over it and into the foyer, and her professional brain switched on the way it always did, cataloguing automatically: grand double-height entry, original wainscoting, water damage along the cornice line, plaster cracking in a spiderweb pattern above the east archway, a chandelier that should have been worth five figures if it was original and wasn't, judging by the fixtures, which were wrong. Depreciation everywhere. Deferred maintenance measured in decades. And underneath all of it, a smell she couldn't place — not rot, not mildew, something drier. Like paper. Like a library that had been closed for a long time.

"I'll need access to every room," she said, getting her laser measure out of the satchel. "Including mechanical spaces. I'll try to stay out of your way."

Halcyon Vane folded her hands. "There are conditions."

"So I'm told. Eight floors. I know."

"Not eight floors." The old woman's voice didn't change at all; that was the strange part. It stayed level and dry and almost kind. "One floor. The ninth. Whatever the panel tells you, whatever the stairs suggest, however many steps you count — you will not go up past the eighth landing. Do you understand me, Ms. Sterling? This is not a legal formality. My granddaughter lives in this house."

Ada had prepared three responses to this, ranging from gracious to firm. What came out instead was honest: "Why?"

Something moved behind Halcyon's eyes — quick, submerged, gone. "Because the house decides what things are worth," she said. "And I would prefer it not decide about you."

Then she smiled, briefly, a hostess smoothing over a conversational stumble, and gestured toward the east wing. "Iris is in the morning room. She won't speak to you. She doesn't speak to anyone. I'll show you the service stairs and leave you to your work. Dinner is at seven, if you're staying — the drive back down the mountain in the dark isn't advisable."

It wasn't a warning about the road. Ada understood that much. It was a warning about the dark, full stop.

"I'll stay," she said, because turning back now would mean losing the fee and admitting the cold voice had been right, and she wasn't ready to do either.

* * *

She started, as she always started, with the foyer.

Baseline measurements. Overall volume, door positions, window positions, sight lines. The laser measure hummed in her hand, red dot steady against the far wall, and the number it returned was clean and reasonable and matched her visual estimate to within an inch. Good. Normal. She logged it, shot the width, logged that too, and then stood in the center of the marble floor with her notebook open and began the part of the job she could do in her sleep: counting doors.

Every appraisal started with doors. Doors defined circulation, circulation defined usable space, usable space defined value. She'd counted doors in six hundred buildings. Hotels, hospitals, lighthouses, a decommissioned missile silo once. She was very good at counting doors.

Foyer, ground floor. Main entrance — double door, counts as one. Service door to the kitchen corridor, left side. Door under the main staircase, probably a cloakroom or cellar access. Two formal parlor doors on the east wall. One conservatory door, glass. One door beneath the east archway, leading toward the wing where the grandmother had disappeared.

Seven. She wrote it down. Then she walked the perimeter to verify, because you always verified, because the whole discipline of appraisal rested on the assumption that a room contained what it appeared to contain.

Main entrance. Service door. Under-stairs door. Parlor, parlor. Conservatory. East archway.

Six.

Ada stood still.

She ran it again, slowly, touching each doorframe as she passed it, the way she'd done six hundred times in six hundred buildings. Main entrance, oak, iron hinges. Service door, painted shut at the bottom. Under-stairs, low, dark. Parlor doors, twin mahogany. Conservatory, glass and brass. Archway door, heavy, banded with iron.

Six doors. Six doorframes. Six sets of hardware under her fingertips.

Her notebook said seven.

She flipped back through the pages, checking her own handwriting, looking for the error — a miscount, a duplicate, anything. The entry was there, unambiguous, in her own neat print: *Foyer — 7 doors.* And below it, in the same ink, in the same hand, a second line she had no memory of writing:

*One extra.*

Ada closed the notebook.

She stood in the middle of the foyer of Marrow House, in the paper-smelling silence, and told herself the thing she told herself on every difficult job: *buildings don't lie, people do.* Somewhere in the last ten minutes she'd miscounted, or double-counted, or written a note while distracted. That was all. That was the boring, ordinary explanation, and boring ordinary explanations were right ninety-nine times out of a hundred.

Above her head — high up, near the top of the stairwell, near the ceiling of a floor that shouldn't exist — something went *shhk.*

Soft. Brief. Like a page turning.

Or a pen, finishing a stroke.

Ada looked up the stairwell into the dimness where the light gave out, and the house looked back down at her with all its dark windows, and she did what professionals do. She opened her notebook to a fresh page, wrote *Day 1 — begin measurements, upper floors tomorrow,* and did not write down what she'd heard.

But as she followed the service stairs up to the guest room Halcyon had given her, passing door after identical paneled door, she found herself counting them under her breath without meaning to.

And somewhere above her — patient, unhurried, already begun — the house was counting too.
