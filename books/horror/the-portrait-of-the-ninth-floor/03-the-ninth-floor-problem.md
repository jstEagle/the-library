## 3. The Ninth Floor Problem

The blueprints arrived by courier on the third morning, rolled in a cardboard tube and tied with a ribbon that had once been red.

Ada spread them across the dining table in the east wing — the one room Halcyon had designated as "yours to work in," delivered with the particular courtesy of a woman who wanted her contained. The plans were dated 1961, drafted by an architectural firm called Pellham & Rooke, and they were beautiful in the way old documents are beautiful: hand-lettered elevation notes, ink washes for shadow, a north arrow drawn as a small ornamental compass rose.

Eight floors. Basement, ground, six above. The roofline was drawn with care, slate tiles individually hatched, three chimneys labeled by function. Ada counted them twice because she had learned, in two days at Marrow House, to count everything twice.

Eight floors. No ninth.

She unrolled the second set — a 1989 survey done for insurance purposes, all photocopied reductions and rubber stamps. Eight floors again. The third document was the probate abstract itself, which listed the property as "eight-story single-family residence, approx. 31,400 square feet."

Ada sat back and looked at the ceiling of the dining room, which was not the top floor. Above her were four more storeys of Vane house, and above those, according to every instrument she owned, one more that did not exist.

Her laser measure sat in its case like a patient animal. She'd been running numbers since yesterday afternoon, and the numbers were wrong in ways she hadn't yet put into writing. The east wing's exterior footprint said forty-two feet from foundation to cornice. Her interior vertical measurements, summed floor by floor, said fifty-one. Nine feet of house that existed only from the inside. She'd assumed miscalibration. She'd recalibrated against the stairwell doorframe twice.

Nine feet. Almost exactly the height of one floor.

*Don't*, she told herself. *You're a professional. Professionals don't invent floors.*

But her hands were already packing the case.

* * *

The elevator was the strangest thing about Marrow House, and Ada had been carefully not thinking about it.

It was original equipment, 1920s or so, a brass-caged lift with a folding scissor gate, lovingly maintained in a house where the wallpaper hung in sheets. Someone had kept it oiled. Someone had kept it *working*. The call button glowed amber when she pressed it, and the machinery inside the wall made a sound like a throat clearing.

The panel beside the gate was enamel over steel, the numbers inlaid in black: B, 1 through 8. And below the 8, separated by a thin horizontal rule as if added later, a 9.

The 9 was not worn. Every other number showed the soft erosion of seventy years of fingertips. The 9 looked freshly painted, its edges crisp, its black still faintly glossy. As if it had appeared recently. As if it had appeared, say, this week.

Ada took out her phone and photographed the panel. In the photo, the 9 was there. She took a second photo from a different angle. Still there. A third, with the flash on, and in the blown-out white glare the 9 seemed almost to stand proud of the surface, though when she ran her thumb across it, the enamel was perfectly flat.

"Miss Sterling."

Halcyon Vane stood at the end of the corridor, hands folded at her waist, dressed — as always — as if expecting company that mattered. Today it was a dove-grey cardigan over a high-collared blouse, pearl buttons gone yellow with age. She was eighty-something and moved like water finding its level, no wasted motion, no hurry.

"Mrs. Vane. I have a question about your elevator."

"Do you." Not quite a question. Halcyon came closer, slowly, and looked at the panel with the expression of a woman reading a letter she already knew by heart. "I suppose you've noticed the nine."

"It's not on any blueprint."

"No," Halcyon agreed. "It wouldn't be."

"Was there ever a ninth floor? An addition? An attic conversion that wasn't permitted?"

Halcyon turned to look at her then, and Ada had the uncomfortable sensation of being measured. Not rudely. Thoroughly. The way you'd look at a piece of furniture you were considering buying, turning it in the light, checking the joints.

"You're the appraiser," Halcyon said. "Tell me what you think it is."

"I think it's either a very old joke or a very new problem."

Something moved across the old woman's face — not a smile, but the place where a smile would go, like a chair that had held one so long it remembered the shape. "My husband used to say the house has always had nine floors. He said we simply didn't use all of them." She reached out and did not touch the button; her finger hovered a half-inch from the 9, the way you'd hover near a sleeping animal. "Elias was a fanciful man. I am not. And I will tell you plainly what he never could."

Ada waited. The elevator machinery ticked behind the wall, patient as a clock.

"This house appraises back, Miss Sterling. That's the truth of Marrow House, and I'll thank you not to write it down." Halcyon's voice stayed level, kind, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "You value things for a living. Square footage, condition, comparable sales. You walk into a building and decide what it's worth. Well. This house does the same. It looks at what walks into it, and it decides. What they're worth. What they weigh. What they'd fetch." She lowered her hand. "So be careful what you write about it. It reads."

"That's—" Ada stopped herself from saying *not possible*, because nothing in this house had been possible since the foyer door count, and she was tired of the word. "That's a superstition."

"Yes," Halcyon said pleasantly. "Most true things are, the first time you hear them." She turned away, then paused, her back straight as a plumb line. "There's tea in the kitchen at four. Iris takes hers with too much honey. And Miss Sterling — whatever you hear tonight, don't come looking for it. Curiosity is just appetite wearing glasses. The house knows the difference between a woman who wants to understand and a woman who wants to climb."

She walked off down the corridor, and Ada noticed — filed it, professionally, in the growing drawer of things she'd deal with later — that Halcyon's footsteps made no sound at all on the runner, while Ada's own rang like someone knocking.

* * *

She spent the afternoon forcing herself into routine, because routine was the rope she climbed down into dark places and, more importantly, the rope she climbed back up.

Ground floor: measured, re-measured, logged. The ballroom's interior dimensions exceeded its exterior walls by eleven inches on the north side, which she attributed — wrote, actually wrote in her field notes — *settling, measurement error, irregular historic construction.* The pen hesitated on the word *error*. She crossed out nothing. Professionals commit.

Second floor: seven bedrooms, all proportioned for taller people, doorknobs at her shoulder height. Third floor: a library where the books were shelved spine-inward, every one of them, thousands of volumes showing their blank leather backs to the room, as if the titles were private. Fourth floor: empty rooms with clean rectangles on the wallpaper where pictures had hung for decades and been taken down — or where pictures would hang, someday, soon.

By five o'clock the light had gone the color of weak tea, and Ada found Iris sitting on the main staircase, exactly halfway up, knees drawn to her chest, watching the front door.

"Hello, Iris."

Iris didn't startle. She'd been aware of Ada's approach long before Ada arrived; that was another thing about the child, she registered people the way weather stations register pressure. "You went up to four today," Iris said.

"I did."

"The fourth floor doesn't like being measured. It stretches when nobody's counting." Iris picked at a thread on her sock. "Grandmother says you shouldn't say numbers out loud here. The house collects them."

"That sounds like something designed to stop me doing my job."

Iris considered this with grave fairness. "Probably," she admitted. Then: "Are you going to take the elevator?"

"No."

"You looked at the nine."

Ada crouched down to the girl's level, which felt less like condescension than like entering negotiations. "What's on the ninth floor, Iris?"

For a moment — just a moment — the child's face did something terrible. It relaxed. All the vigilance drained out of it and left underneath a plain, exhausted knowledge, the face of someone who has stopped waiting for adults to fix a thing because she has understood they can't. "Paper," Iris said. "Lots and lots of paper." Then the vigilance came back up like a drawbridge, and she slid off the stair and padded away toward the kitchen without goodbye, leaving Ada alone on the staircase with the sound of her own pulse.

* * *

Dinner was quiet. Halcyon spoke of the weather and the developer's lawyers in the same dry tone; Iris ate mechanically, eyes flicking to the ceiling at intervals so regular it might have been a metronome. At eight, Halcyon wished Ada good night at the foot of the stairs and held her gaze one beat too long, and Ada understood it was a warning being handed over politely, like a coat.

They'd given her the guest room on the sixth floor. It was clean, aired, furnished in a style thirty years dead — floral bedspread, a washstand, a wardrobe tall enough to hang a body in, though Ada told herself that was just how wardrobes were built in 1911. There was a mirror above the washstand. She did not look at it longer than necessary. She had started noticing, that morning, that mirrors in this house required a certain discipline, and she was not yet ready to examine why.

She worked in bed with the laptop balanced on her knees, transcribing field notes, keeping her language dry and defensible. *Irregular interior dimensions consistent with undocumented historical modification.* Outside, wind moved through the trees on the estate, and the house ticked and settled around her like a large animal digesting.

At 11:40, she heard the sound.

At first she took it for the pipes. A rhythmic scratching, faint, arrhythmic in the way mechanical things aren't — pause, scratch-scratch, pause, longer pause, scratch. She lay still and listened properly, the way she'd learned to listen to houses, cataloguing sources. Not plumbing. Not wind. Not the settling timbers, which groaned low and slow, whereas this was small and precise and *deliberate*.

It was a nib. A fountain pen, dragged across paper, signing.

And it was coming from above her.

Ada lay in the dark and did the geometry she didn't want to do. Sixth floor: her. Seventh: unmeasured. Eighth: unmeasured. Ninth: nonexistent. The sound came from directly overhead, filtered through plaster and lath, softened to a whisper — but directional, unmistakable, *up*.

Scratch. Pause. Scratch-scratch. Pause.

Not one signature. Many. Different weights of pressure, different tempos — a heavy deliberate hand, a quick nervous one, a slow shaky one, a child's careful block letters. Round and round it went, page after page after page, an assembly line of assent, and beneath it, so faint she might have invented it, a murmur of voices reading aloud in unison, too low to catch words, steady as liturgy.

Ada got up. She told herself she was getting up to close the window against the cold, and she did close the window, and then she stood at the center of the room with her head tipped back, staring at the ceiling as if plaster were transparent.

The signing paused.

In the silence she heard something worse: the sound of a page being turned. One page. Deliberate. And then the pen resumed — but slower now, and she could have sworn, could not possibly have sworn, that the rhythm had changed to match something. To match *her breathing*. Scratch on the inhale. Lift on the exhale. The house taking dictation from her lungs.

She got back into bed and did not sleep. At some hour past two, the signing stopped mid-word — a scratch cut off clean, a pen lifted — and the silence afterward had texture, had weight, had the quality of a held breath. Ada stared at the ceiling and thought about Halcyon's voice: *it decides what things are worth.*

At 3:07, her laptop, asleep on the bedside table, woke itself.

The screen glowed in the dark, and on it, in her own open appraisal file, in her own font, a single line had appeared in the valuation column where she had written nothing yet:

**Subject property: pending full inventory.**

Below it, smaller, as if typed by a hesitant hand:

**Ninth floor: occupied.**

Ada closed the laptop. She did not open it again. She lay awake until the windows went grey, listening to the house count its breaths, and when dawn finally came she was downstairs before seven with her laser measure in her hand, standing in front of the elevator, looking at the freshly painted 9.

She pressed it.

Somewhere far above, through eight floors of sleeping house, something began — happily, hungrily, like a dog hearing its leash — to descend.
