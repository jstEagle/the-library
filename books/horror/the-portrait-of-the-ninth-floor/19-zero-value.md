## 19. Zero Value

The pen was still in my hand.

I had sat down in the tenth chair, and the house had exhaled around me like a lung filling, and the master page lay before me on a table of black wood that drank the light. Nine Vanes sat along its length, signing, signing, their faces smooth as poured glass, their pens moving in unison like the legs of some enormous insect. The page they signed never ended. It ran off the edge of the table and pooled on the floor and kept going, out through the doorway, down through the floorboards, a river of terms and conditions flowing into the dark of the house's foundations.

Iris stood behind my chair with both hands on my shoulders. Her grip was small and cold and it was the only real thing left in the room.

The space where my signature belonged was warm. I could feel heat rising off it, the way you feel heat off a stove before you touch it. The blank line waited with something like patience. The whole house waited with it — every door on every counted floor, all forty-four of them, holding their breath behind their frames.

*Sign,* said the room, in no voice at all. *Appraise yourself.*

"No," I said.

And then I did what appraisers do.

I took out my report.

It was still in my bag, printed at the motel two nights ago, back when I'd believed numbers were numbers. Forty-one pages. Comparative market analysis. Structural assessment. Depreciation schedules. My name in the header, my license number under it, the whole dry machinery of my profession bound with a single staple that suddenly felt like the most honest object in Marrow House.

I laid it on the table beside the contract.

"Fair market value," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because inside I was nothing but vibration. "As determined by a licensed appraiser of record. Effective date of valuation: today."

The pens stopped.

All nine of them stopped at once, mid-stroke, and the silence that followed was worse than the signing had been. It was the silence of a machine encountering an input it cannot parse.

"The subject property," I read aloud, because I have learned that horror hates being read aloud, hates being made small and procedural, "consists of a residential structure of eight stories, per all recorded blueprints. The ninth story does not exist. It appears on no survey, no tax roll, no deed, no plan filed with any municipal authority in the history of this parcel." I turned the page. "A property that does not legally exist cannot be conveyed, cannot be encumbered, and cannot be sold."

The table creaked.

"Therefore," I said, "my professional opinion of value is as follows."

I looked at the nine glass faces. Somewhere beneath the smoothness there were eyes. I made myself look into them anyway.

"Zero dollars," I said. "Zero cents. This house is worth nothing. It has always been worth nothing. And I refuse to value anything else about it — not its rooms, not its memories, not its residents, not its dead. You can't keep what nobody will price."

The mirrors screamed.

There is no other word for it. Every reflective surface in that room — the table itself, polished to a black shine; the faces of the nine Vanes; the walls, which had gone glassy without my noticing — every one of them shrieked, a sound like a nail drawn the length of a windowpane multiplied by a thousand windows, a sound that wasn't sound so much as pressure, that went through my teeth and my sternum and the fluid of my eyes. Iris's hands tightened on my shoulders. I didn't stop.

"Structural condition: condemned," I read on. "Foundation: compromised by fifty years of deferred grief. Systems: none functioning. Occupancy: unlawful. Recommendation: demolition."

The first page of the contract began to blacken.

It started at the top margin, a scorch-bloom spreading like ink dropped in water, and where it spread, the writing lifted off the paper — not erased but *unwritten*, the letters curling up off the surface like leaves in fire though there was no fire, no smoke, only a smell like rain on hot stone. The nine Vane pens trembled against the table.

"You were built on a bargain," I told them, told the house, told whatever was listening from underneath the floors. "And bargains are just prices. And I am telling you your price is nothing. Zero. Null. Take it or take nothing, because those are the same thing now."

Page after page went dark. The river of paper running off the table browned and crisped and curled backward toward its source, retracting like a tide going out, fifty years of signatures lifting away in flakes that dissolved before they touched the floor. The names went first — I watched *Edmund Vane* burn off the page without flame, then *Thomas*, then *Wren*, then the smaller names, the children's names, each one going out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

And the nine of them stood up.

They rose together, chairs scraping back with a sound like a held breath released, and for the first time their faces weren't smooth. The glass cracked across them in fine webs, and through the cracks I saw them — not the 1974 photographs from Halcyon's album, not the figures in the mirror-world, but something more tired and more human than either. A tall man with his collar open. A woman with her hair coming loose from its pins. Six young people ranging from nearly grown to very small, the smallest still holding a pen too big for his hand.

They looked at me. Not hungrily. Not accusingly.

*Relieved.*

Then they came apart.

Not violently — that's the thing I'll never manage to explain to anyone who asks, if anyone ever asks. They didn't scream or claw or fight it. They simply loosened, the way a fist loosens, and came apart into dust. Fine gray dust that hung in the air of the ninth floor and caught light that had no source and drifted, slowly, toward the broken stairwell, and as it moved it carried a smell with it — clean rain, petrichor, the smell of the first storm after a long dry summer, the smell of something that has been waiting years to fall.

The dust went down through the house. I felt it pass us, a soft displacement of air, a whisper of it settling on my arms and in Iris's hair. Down past the counted doors. Down past the foyer with its wrong arithmetic. Out, somehow, into the night.

"They're leaving," Iris whispered. Her voice cracked in the middle of it. "Grandma said they couldn't leave. Grandma said—"

"Iris." I turned in the chair. The tenth chair was already softening behind me, the black wood going gray, going porous, going ordinary. "Look at me. It's over. Whatever she told you, whatever she believed — it's over."

But it wasn't. Not quite. Because the dust kept falling, and somewhere below us, on the stairs, someone had begun to speak.

* * *

We found Halcyon on the landing between the eighth floor and the ninth — the landing that shouldn't exist, that had been a wall when I first measured this house, that had grown a staircase the way a tree grows a ring. She was standing at the rail with both hands folded over it, and she looked, for the first time since I'd met her, exactly her age. Older, even. Small. Her spine had curved inward like a closing parenthesis, and her cardigan hung off her shoulders as if it belonged to someone larger who had left.

She was whispering.

Not to us. To the dust.

Each of the nine passed her on its way down — I don't know how I knew they were nine, how I knew they were individuals, but I did, the way you know the parts of a chord — and as each one passed, she whispered a name.

"Edmund." Her voice was a thread. "Edmund, love. There you are."

A pause. The smell of rain thickened.

"Thomas. Thomas, sweetheart, look at you. Look how tall."

"Wren. Oh — oh, Wrenny. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry."

She said it to each of them. Names I'd seen in probate documents, in the 1974 file, in the margins of blueprints: *Margaret. June. Peter. Little Edmund.* She said them the way you say names at a graveside, except there was no grave, there had never been a grave, there had only been this house keeping them the way a miser keeps coins — not because he loves them, but because he cannot bear to spend them.

Fifty years she had let this house hold her family hostage so that she could go on living in the same building with them. Fifty years of dinners set for ghosts, of warnings delivered kindly, of a rule about mirrors enforced with such tenderness that Iris had never once thought to question it. And now they were leaving, all of them, dissolving into weather, and Halcyon Vane stood at the rail of a staircase that was already forgetting itself and counted them out the way she must once have counted them into bed: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven.

Eight.

The last one paused beside her. The small one. Little Edmund, six years old in 1974, seventy-eight years old in the dust, and he leaned — I swear this, I will swear it forever — he leaned into her side the way a child leans, for one second, one full second, the length of a lagging reflection, and then he went down the stairs and out.

Halcyon's knees buckled.

I got to her before she hit the ground. She weighed almost nothing. Up close I could see that her eyes were wet but her face was calm, terribly calm, the calm of a woman who has finally been allowed to put something down.

"He waited," she said. "Did you see? He waited for me."

"Yes," I said. "He did."

"Fifty years, and he waited one second more." She laughed, or sobbed, or both; the sound was identical. "That's my boy. That's always been my boy."

Iris knelt down beside us on the landing, and the three of us stayed there while the last of the dust found the stairs and followed the others down, and the house around us began, very quietly, to die.

I could hear it happening. Doors sighing shut all through the structure — forty-four doors, forty-three, forty-two, counting themselves back down to the eight floors that appeared on the blueprints, to the doors that had been there honestly all along. Walls firming up where memory-rooms had been. The staircase shortening beneath us, steps folding away like a fan closing, and we rode it down gently, the three of us, Halcyon in my arms and Iris holding my sleeve, descending a house that was returning to its true dimensions one impossible foot at a time.

By the time we reached the foyer, the mirrors in the hall were only mirrors. Dark, ordinary, reflecting us at the exact speed of reality. I looked at my own face in the largest one and saw nothing looking back but a tired woman covered in gray dust that smelled like rain.

Halcyon stirred against my shoulder.

"It's not finished," she murmured. "You know that, don't you, dear. Contracts like this one — they don't end. They only change hands."

"Halcyon—"

"The front door," she said. "Count it."

I looked. One door. For the first time since I'd walked into Marrow House, the foyer held exactly one door, and it stood open onto the driveway and the night, opening outward, breathing cold clean air into a house that had spent half a century inhaling.

One door. Opening outward.

And standing in it, silhouetted against the porch light, a figure I did not recognize — until I did. A man in a good coat, holding a leather folder against his chest, smiling the smile of a professional arriving precisely on schedule.

"Ms. Sterling?" he said. "Forgive the hour. I represent the buyer. I understand there's been a change in the valuation — and I've been sent to discuss the terms of your withdrawal."

Behind him, at the edge of the porch light, the driveway stretched away into the dark, and I remembered what it had done to Iris, how it had lengthened, how the gate had become a horizon.

The house was dying. But paperwork, I was learning, is the one thing that outlives its building.

He stepped inside, and the door stayed open behind him — but only just, and only, I noticed, outward.
