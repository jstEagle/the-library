## 2. The Last Ashcombes

The woman who opened the door of Undercroft Hall was not what Wren had expected, though he couldn't have said what he had expected instead. Something more theatrical, perhaps. Haunted houses in his experience came with a certain self-consciousness—candles, shawls, a rehearsed weariness. Edith Ashcombe had none of it. She was small and upright and dressed as if for a committee meeting, cardigan buttoned to the throat, silver hair pinned with the efficiency of someone who had stopped wasting time on vanity decades ago. Her eyes went to his kit bag first and her face second.

"You're late," she said.

"Ten minutes. The lane's not signed."

"The lane hasn't been signed since 1987. It doesn't mean you drive past it." She stepped back without inviting him in, which he understood, after a moment's study, was an invitation—the only kind she gave. "You'll want the kitchen. Everything happens through the kitchen in this house. It always has."

Wren crossed a hall that made him slow his steps despite himself. Not because of anything supernatural—a rationalist did not get supernatural in the first four minutes—but because of scale. The staircase rose into shadow like something geological. A chandelier hung dead above him, its crystals furred with dust, each one holding a small grey world of cobweb. And on the walls—

He'd seen the photographs in the email thread. Greasy prints at shoulder height, a dozen of them, ascending the corridor toward the east wing. In person they were worse, or rather more specific: not smears but palms, whole hands, fingers spread, pressed with the confidence of a child showing a parent its work. He counted five before Edith cleared her throat.

"The corridor can wait," she said. "Rats first."

"Of course," Wren said, and did not believe her, and was fairly sure she knew it.

* * *

The kitchen was the warmest room in the house and somehow the least welcoming. It was enormous—an Aga the size of a small car, a scrubbed table that could seat twenty—and everything in it was arranged for fewer people than lived there. Three chairs at one end of the table, pulled close together, leaving the rest of the expanse bare as a stage after the show has closed. Two mugs out. A third shelf of cups untouched behind glass.

Two people were already seated when Wren entered, and they were not sitting together so much as failing to sit apart.

The man was perhaps fifty, with the collapsed posture of someone who had been tall once and had spent years apologizing for it. His hands were around a mug he wasn't drinking from, and when his mother came in he straightened by a degree, the way a plant leans toward light it no longer trusts. "This is the pest man?" he asked the room generally.

"Wren Okonkwo," Wren said, offering his hand.

"Hal." The handshake was brief and cold and ended too early, like a phone call cut off. Hal's eyes were red-rimmed, and Wren filed that away with the neutral professionalism of a man who had learned that everyone in a haunted house is grieving something, even when nothing has died yet.

In the corner, half-hidden behind a laptop and a tripod of some kind, sat a girl of about sixteen with her hood up and one earbud in. She looked up exactly long enough to assess him and return to her screen. "Nell," Edith said, in a tone that suggested the name was being submitted for the record. "My granddaughter. She won't shake your hand, she's filming."

"I'm not filming you," Nell said, without looking up. "I'm filming the room. You happen to be in it."

"Nell."

"It's fine," Wren said. He set his kit bag on the table and began unpacking with the deliberate theatre of his trade: the torch, the inspection mirror, the flour tray, the little tubs of non-toxic tracking dust. People trusted process. Process was a kind of promise—you could watch me work, and everything I do leaves evidence. "So. Tell me about the noises."

Hal and Edith looked at each other. It was a quick exchange, less than a second, but Wren had spent his childhood watching adults do that exact thing over his head—the glance that says *we agreed on a version*. He waited. He was good at waiting. Seven years of his own childhood had been an exercise in waiting for adults to say true things.

"Scratching," Hal said finally. "At night. In the walls. Upstairs mostly. The east corridor."

"And the prints?"

"That's—" Hal's mug clicked against the table as he set it down too hard. "That's damp. The plaster's old. It does strange things."

"Damp doesn't make fingerprints," Nell said, still not looking up. "Damp especially doesn't make left-hand fingerprints, all facing the same way, like something walking along the wall with its hand out."

"Nell, Mr. Okonkwo is here about rats."

"Then he should know we don't have rats." Now she looked up, and her eyes were her grandmother's eyes, the same pale grey, but unguarded where Edith's were shuttered. "We have something that walks on two legs and presses its hands on the wall at shoulder height. Shoulder height of *what*, though? That's the question, isn't it? Because I'm five-four, and my nan's five-two, and Dad's six-one, and the prints are at all three heights. Sometimes on the same stretch of wall."

The kitchen was quiet. The Aga ticked. Somewhere far above them, in the geography of the house, a door settled against its frame—or was closed—with a sound like a held breath released.

"They hired me for rats," Wren said mildly, to no one in particular, and watched all three of them flinch in their separate ways. Edith's chin lifted. Hal looked at his mug. Nell almost smiled, which on her face looked like a crack in ice.

* * *

He did the inspection properly, because whatever else was happening in Undercroft Hall, there might also be rats; houses this old were ecosystems, and ecosystems were honest. He worked the ground floor methodically—skirting boards, pipe runs, the void under the back stairs—laying tracking dust and flour trays with the calm of a man laying down instruments in an orchestra pit. Hal trailed him at a distance, asking questions that weren't questions. *Is it bad? Is it much? Will it take long?* Each answer seemed to relieve and disappoint him in equal measure.

It was on the east stairs that Wren found himself alone with the house.

Edith had gone to make calls—she announced it like a military operation—and Nell had declared she was going to film the corridor again at dusk, which Edith had forbidden and Nell had clearly scheduled anyway. So Wren climbed the east staircase with his torch and his mirror and his professional calm, and stood in the corridor where the prints lived.

Up close, in raking daylight, they were worse. He crouched and examined the lowest one, at what would be knee height on a child. The grease in it had a faint iridescence, like the sheen on meat gone slightly off. He touched the wall beside it—not the print itself; he had rules about touching things—and felt the plaster was cold in a band maybe a foot wide, running along the corridor at waist height, as if something large had leaned there for a very long time.

Above him, at the ceiling junction, a sound. Soft. Rhythmic. Like knuckles drumming a table, except slower, and patient, and coming from inside the wall rather than above the ceiling.

Wren stood very still and listened the way he had learned to listen as a boy: not with hope, but with inventory. *Knuckle. Knuckle. Pause. Knuckle.* Three beats, pause, three beats. Not random. Random was mice. Random was pipes and expansion and the honest physics of old houses. This was pattern, and pattern was the one thing his armor had never been able to file away, because pattern implied intent, and intent implied—

"Mr. Okonkwo."

Edith stood at the end of the corridor, and he would have sworn she had not made a single sound crossing the floorboards to get there. She was looking at the ceiling junction, not at him.

"You hear it too," she said. It wasn't a question, and her voice had changed—flatter, older, as if she'd taken off a coat she wore for visitors.

"I hear something consistent with—"

"Don't do that." For the first time she looked at him directly, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Don't give me the professional version. I've buried a husband in this house and I've buried other things in this house, and I am too old for the professional version. You heard it. Did it stop when I spoke?"

Wren realized his hand had drifted up toward the wall, toward the cold band of plaster, and lowered it. "Yes," he said.

"Good." She turned away. "Lunch is at one. We eat at one in this house. It always has."

She walked off down the corridor, upright as a mast, and Wren stayed a moment longer, looking at the prints ascending the wall like a tide mark of some sea that hadn't finished coming in.

* * *

Lunch was ham sandwiches and a war conducted entirely in subtext.

Wren ate quickly and observed, because observing families was a skill he'd never wanted and couldn't put down. They orbited. That was the word for it—he'd used it before, in his notes on other households, other jobs, and it fit here better than anywhere. Edith sat at the head and issued small gravitational corrections: *your collar, Hal; the salt's beside you, Nell; Mr. Okonkwo, more tea?* Hal answered in full sentences addressed slightly to the left of whoever he spoke to. Nell filmed her sandwich from three angles, posted nothing, and answered direct questions with data.

"How long have you lived here, Nell?" Wren asked.

"My whole life. Sixteen years." She turned the phone around briefly: on screen, the corridor, night-vision green, empty. "Born upstairs. Second bedroom on the west side. My dad was born in the same room. And his dad. And probably Adam, if anyone ever tells me who Adam was."

"Adam?" Wren kept his voice level, but something in the name landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He didn't know why. There was no reason for the name to matter. It was just a name.

"Firstborn," Edith said quietly. "There's always a firstborn."

"There's always a firstborn *what*?"

"Eat your lunch, Nell."

And there it was again—that glance between Edith and Hal, the agreed version passing between them like contraband, and Nell catching it and pocketing it with the fury of a girl building a case file. Wren understood then that he was not looking at a family keeping a secret from an outsider. He was looking at a family keeping a secret from itself, three ways, each of them guarding a different piece and pretending the others didn't exist.

Hal pushed back his chair. "I'll show you the cellar access," he said to Wren, abruptly, gratefully, the way a man seizes any excuse to leave a room. "For the—inspection. Drainage. Whatever you need."

They went down together into a stone passage that smelled of cold earth, and Hal talked the entire way, fast and low, about drainage and damp courses and the 1911 renovations, and Wren let him talk, because Hal's voice in the dark was doing something Wren recognized: filling space so the space couldn't fill itself.

At the foot of the stairs Hal stopped. Ahead, the passage ran toward a heavy door—oak, iron-banded—which stood closed. Beside it, a light switch. Hal reached for the switch and his hand stopped an inch short, hovered, withdrew.

"Fuses are temperamental down here," he said. "Better in daylight."

"Mr. Ashcombe." Wren kept his voice easy. "Your daughter showed me the corridor footage metadata. Timestamped Tuesday, 3:14 a.m. Your mother mentioned a husband. The village pub apparently has opinions. I'm not here to pry. But I can't treat this property honestly if everyone in it is treating it dishonestly, including themselves."

Hal laughed—one dry note with no humor in it, a laugh like a cough. "Honest. Right." He looked at the oak door for a moment, and his face in the gloom did something complicated: fear and longing and resentment all arriving at once, like relatives at a funeral. "You want honesty? Here's honesty. My father went into the undercroft eleven months ago to settle the accounts, and he hasn't come up, and my mother won't say the word *dead*, and my daughter thinks the house is a crime scene, and I think—" His voice cracked and reassembled itself. "I think I'm the last man in England standing in front of a door he's afraid to turn a light on next to. That's the family business, Mr. Okonkwo. Standing in front of doors."

Behind the oak, in the silence that followed, something shifted. Not a knock. Not a voice. Just a settling, the way a house settles—except the sound came from below the floor, from beneath their feet, deep and deliberate, like a great weight turning over in sleep.

Neither of them moved. The dust motes hung in the thin light from the stairwell, suspended, indifferent.

"We eat at one," Hal said at last, hollow, automatic, climbing back toward the daylight and his mother's schedule and every ritual that kept the shape of a family over the absence of one. "She'll be waiting."

Wren followed him up, and at the top of the stairs he paused, just once, and looked back down into the dark.

Far below, faint as a memory of a sound, something rang. Once. Softly. Like a bell wrapped in cloth, or a bell learning, patiently, how to begin.
