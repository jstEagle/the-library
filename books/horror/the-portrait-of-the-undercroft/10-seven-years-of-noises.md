## 10. Seven Years of Noises

The frost prints had stopped coming after the third night, which was somehow worse than if they'd continued. A pattern you could track was a problem to solve. An absence was a held breath.

Wren found Nell in the morning room, the one with the good light, filming a teacup.

"You're documenting the crockery now," he said.

"I'm documenting everything." She didn't lower her phone. "If this house eats us, there'll be footage."

"It's not going to eat you."

"That's very reassuring from a man who checks behind doors the way other people check their phones." She finally set the phone down, propped against the sugar bowl so it still recorded, and looked at him with that flat, appraising stare she'd inherited from no one in particular—or possibly from everyone, two centuries of Ashcombe skepticism distilled into one sixteen-year-old. "My nan thinks you're here for the rats. My dad thinks you're here to take the money and run before the equinox. I think you're here because you've seen one of these houses before."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere above them, a floorboard settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"What makes you say that?" Wren asked.

"Because when the frost prints showed up on the windows, you didn't photograph them first. You put your hand next to one. Same size. You measured yourself against it like you already knew what you were looking for." She turned the teacup a quarter-rotation, precise as a surgeon. "People who are seeing something for the first time flinch. You *filed*."

He should have deflected. He was good at deflecting; he'd had years of practice, seven of them specifically, and before that a childhood's worth. But the morning room was warm and the light was honest and Nell Ashcombe was the only person in this house who asked questions she actually wanted answered, and Wren Okonkwo was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Sit down," he said. "This takes a while."

She sat. She left the phone running. He decided he didn't mind.

* * *

"I grew up in a semi-detached house in Croydon," he said. "End terrace, actually. Red brick, bay windows, a garden the size of a bath towel. My mum rented it off a landlord who lived in Spain and never came. Nothing about it was interesting. That was the whole point of it—it was aggressively ordinary. The kind of house you walk past four hundred times without once seeing it."

"And?"

"And it whispered."

Nell's face did something careful. Not disbelief—she'd seen the corridor footage, she'd filmed the shape with too many joints—but the reflexive tightening of someone bracing for either a lie or a truth, unsure which would be worse.

"Not words," Wren said. "Not at first. It was more like—the sound a room makes when it's almost saying something. You know how you can hear a radio through a wall, and you can tell there's a voice but you can't make out the words? It was like that, except there was no radio. No wall on the other side, half the time. Just plaster and brick and then open air, and the whispering anyway."

"How old were you?"

"Six when we moved in. Thirteen when we left." He turned his mug slowly on its saucer, watching the tea ring it left. "Seven years. Which is why I call it that. Seven years of noises."

"Tell me the noises."

He'd never told anyone the noises. He'd told doctors, once, at his mother's insistence—a GP who referred them to a pediatric sleep specialist who used words like *hypnagogic hallucination* and *auditory pareidolia*, and twelve-year-old Wren had sat there nodding along, grateful, because the words were armor. They let him keep functioning. They let him stop checking under the bed every night, or rather, they let him check under the bed and call it a habit instead of a fear.

But nobody had ever asked him to just *tell* it. Plainly. Without a diagnosis attached.

"There was the whispering," he said. "That was the baseline. Then there were footsteps—not heavy ones. Careful ones. Like someone walking through the house trying not to wake anyone, except they always started at my door and ended at the loft hatch. Every night, same route. I timed it once. Forty-one seconds."

"You timed it."

"I was eleven and rationalism was the only thing keeping me upright. Yes, I timed it. Forty-one seconds, give or take. And here's the thing—" he leaned forward, and heard his own voice drop the way it does when you're about to hand someone something fragile, "—the loft hatch was directly above my bedroom. And sometimes, after the footsteps reached it, there'd be a pause. Long enough that I'd start hoping it was over. And then three knocks. Slow ones. Like a question."

"Like asking permission," Nell said quietly.

"Or asking if anyone was home." He hadn't thought of it that way before, and the thought landed somewhere under his ribs. "I never opened the hatch. Obviously. I told myself that was the rational choice—you don't investigate a noise source at night, alone, at eleven. But honestly? I was terrified that if I opened it, whatever knocked would finally have somewhere to come *down* to. As long as the hatch stayed shut, we were both contained. Me below, it above. Neighbors."

"What did your mum hear?"

"Nothing. Ever. That was the worst part, early on. I'd wake her up—'Mum, there's someone walking in the loft'—and she'd lie there listening with me, and the house would go silent, absolutely silent, like it knew an adult was awake. The moment she went back to her room: forty-one seconds. Three knocks." He laughed, short and without humor. "I thought for years that I was making it up. That's the thing nobody tells you about being a kid in a house like that. The haunting isn't even the bad part. The bad part is the gaslighting you do to yourself. Seven years old, standing in a dark hallway, hearing your name said in a voice that isn't your mother's, and deciding—deciding—that you imagined it, because the alternative is unbearable."

"Did it say your name?"

"Once." The word came out smaller than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Once. Near the end. I was thirteen. It wasn't whispering anymore by then—it had gotten closer to speech over the years, the way a radio comes into tune. And one night it said 'Wren' clear as anything, from inside my wardrobe, in a voice like mine but slower. Like it was practicing."

Nell had gone very still. Even the phone seemed to hold steady, as if it understood.

"What did you do?"

"I said 'no' out loud. First time I'd ever answered it. And it went quiet, and it stayed quiet for three weeks, and I thought—I actually thought—I'd won. That it had been testing me and I'd passed." He shook his head. "Then one night in March, the whispering came back, but different. Faster. Urgent. All night, from every wall at once, like the house was trying to tell me something before—"

He stopped.

"Before what?" Nell asked.

"Before the fire."

The radiator ticked. Down the hall, Hal's door closed softly, the way it always did, a man practicing at not existing.

"Electrical fault," Wren said. "That's what the report said. Wiring in the loft, above my bedroom. We got out—me, Mum, the cat. House was gutted. We moved to a flat in Thornton Heath and I slept with the light on until I was fifteen and I told everyone, including myself, that the noises had been my brain's way of processing anxiety, and the fire was a coincidence, and I became the kind of person who carries a moisture meter and a UV torch and believes in evidence, because evidence doesn't whisper your name from wardrobes."

"But you don't believe that anymore."

"I believe the wiring was faulty," Wren said carefully. "I believe the fire was real and electrical and had a cause a court would accept. What I don't believe anymore is that those things and the whispering cancel each other out. I spent twenty years treating them as alternatives. Either the house talked or the wiring burned. But maybe—" he gestured vaguely, taking in Undercroft Hall, its walls, its patient weight, "—maybe the house knew the wiring was going to burn. Maybe the urgency in the last month wasn't menace. Maybe it was a warning delivered badly, by something that had never learned to speak."

Nell stared at him. "You think it was trying to save you."

"I think," Wren said, "that I have spent my entire adult life assuming the worst about every unexplained thing, because the worst is simpler. Menace is simple. You can fight menace. You can seal a door and salt a threshold and invoice for it. But the idea that something in the walls might have been *lonely*—" His voice caught on it. He took a sip of cold tea to cover it. "That's harder. That asks things of you."

* * *

They sat with that for a while. Nell picked up her phone, checked the recording, and—to his surprise—deleted it.

"No footage?" he asked.

"This one's yours," she said. "Not evidence." She set it face-down. Then, in a lighter tone that fooled neither of them: "Forty-one seconds is weirdly specific. Did you ever work out why?"

"I have a theory." He hesitated. This was the part he'd never said aloud, the part that sounded insane even inside his own skull, and yet here, in this house, with these people, it felt almost tame. "The distance from my bedroom door to the loft hatch, measured along the route the footsteps took—through the hall, past the bathroom, up the landing—is about thirty meters. Average walking pace, unhurried, careful not to creak: roughly forty-one seconds. So the timing itself isn't strange. What's strange is that it never varied. Not once in seven years. Rain, snow, the night of the millennium—forty-one seconds. Real walkers vary. Tired legs, a full bladder, a bad knee. Something walking the same line at the same speed for seven years isn't walking. It's *replaying*."

"A recording," Nell said.

"Or a ritual. Or a debt being counted." He glanced toward the hall, where the corridor stretched away toward the staircase, beneath which a certain door waited behind its bell-shaped lock. "I've been thinking about that a lot since I got here. Houses that repeat themselves. Ledgers. Counts."

"The bell counts," Nell said. "One ring per heir."

"So I've heard."

"My grandad rang last Tuesday. Before Dad told you any of it. One ring." Her jaw tightened. "And now the frost prints, and the portraits, and Dad offering you money to leave—which, by the way, are you going to?"

"Are you?"

"I asked first."

Wren smiled despite himself. "No. I'm not leaving. Triple pay or otherwise. If I run from every house that scares me, I'm just a man who's good at doors." He paused. "Also—and don't tell Hal this—I couldn't live with myself. Whatever's down there, whatever this house wants, it's been fed by people too frightened to ask questions for two hundred years. Somebody has to be the one who asks."

"Nan says the same thing about Dad. That nobody in this family has ever asked a question they were afraid of the answer to." Nell pulled her knees up onto the chair, making herself small, sixteen going on much younger. "Can I ask you something? About your house. The real question."

"Go on."

"When it said your name—from the wardrobe. In your own voice." She watched him steadily. "Did it sound like it wanted something from you? Or did it sound like it *was* you? Like—something that had been you, or was going to be?"

The question went through him like cold water through cloth. Because he had never let himself answer it, in twenty years, and sitting here in the morning room of Undercroft Hall with a girl whose ancestors' eyes had been scratched out of their portraits, he found the answer rising anyway, patient, having waited all this time.

"It sounded like it was introducing itself," he said. "Like something that expected to meet me eventually and was getting the voice ready."

Nell nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something she'd suspected about her own house. "That's what the shape in the corridor does," she said. "It presses its hand to the wall right where the prints are. Same spot, every night. I thought it was marking territory. But watching it again last night—I slowed the footage down—it looks more like it's *checking*. Making sure the print still fits."

"Fits," Wren repeated.

"Like a hand measuring itself against a handprint. Like—" she stopped, and he saw her arrive at it the same moment he did, "—like trying on."

Neither of them spoke. Above them, faintly, through two floors of old plaster, came the sound of the loft hatch in the corridor settling a fraction of an inch on its hinges—a small wooden sigh, the kind of sound a house makes, the kind of sound a rationalist files under *thermal expansion*.

Except Wren had heard that exact sound every night for seven years, always after the footsteps, always just before the knocking, and he knew it for what it was.

A door, remembering it could open.

"We need to get into the parish records," Nell said suddenly, swinging her legs down. "The vicarage keeps boxes going back to the 1800s—Mrs. Fennimore told me when I was filming outside. Births, deaths, everything. If this house has been counting heirs since before the bell melted, there'll be paper. There's always paper."

"You want to go through a hundred years of parish records with a pest controller."

"I want to go through them with the only person in this house who's ever been haunted and honest about it." She was already standing, phone in hand, and there was something fierce and bright in her face that Wren recognized from his own mirror, decades ago—the look of a person who has decided that understanding is worth the fear. "Besides. You said it yourself. Somebody has to ask."

She was halfway to the door when she turned back.

"Wren. For what it's worth—your house. The whispering, the footsteps, the name. I believe you. Every word."

And he found, to his considerable surprise, that after twenty years of carrying it alone—of specialists and diagnoses and polite nods and his mother's worried silence—those four words from a sixteen-year-old with a camera undid something in his chest that no amount of evidence ever had.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it more than any professional sentence he'd ever spoken.

"Come on, then." She jerked her head toward the hall. "The dead kept records. Let's find out what they wrote about us."
