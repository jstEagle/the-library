## 11. The Parish Records

The parish office smelled of dust and old tea and the particular staleness of rooms where nothing has been decided in decades. Nell had expected a church—stained glass, a nervous vicar—but the records lived in a low brick annex behind St. Cuthbert's, presided over by a woman named Mrs. Tredwell who had one hearing aid, two cats at home, and the unshakeable conviction that any teenager who wanted to look at parish registers on a Saturday morning was either doing homework or up to no good.

"History project," Nell said, holding up her phone like a badge. "Local history. The bell."

"The bell." Mrs. Tredwell's mouth did something complicated. "Everyone asks about the bell eventually. Usually when they're older than your grandmother and have run out of other things to be frightened of."

"We're not frightened," Nell said.

It was the first lie she'd told in weeks that didn't make her feel sick. That was new. She filed it away to examine later, the way she'd started filing everything—the way her mother used to say she filed everything, receipts and ticket stubs and screenshots, as if evidence could keep you safe.

Wren was already at the far shelf, running his finger along spines of ledger books with cracked leather backs. He'd changed since the frost prints on the windows. Not visibly—he still wore the same grey fleece, still carried his clipboard everywhere like a shield made of stationery—but there was a looseness gone from him. He moved through Undercroft Hall now the way you move through a house where someone is sleeping: carefully, apologetically, listening.

He'd stopped saying *there'll be an explanation*. That was how Nell knew it was bad.

"Registers start here," Mrs. Tredwell said, thumping down three volumes the size of paving slabs. "Baptisms, marriages, burials. Then there's the vestry minutes, if you want committee business, God help you. And the vicar's private papers are in that box—" she pointed at a cardboard box with *1911* written on the side in biro, which struck Nell as deeply wrong, like seeing a pharaoh labeled with a Sharpie. "Curate donated them in the nineties. Nobody's asked for them since."

"Why 1911?" Nell asked, though she knew. Everyone in the village knew the year the way you know the year of a war.

Mrs. Tredwell looked at her for a long moment over her glasses. "Because that's when they melted the bell," she said. "And because that's the year Thomas Ashcombe went into the ground and never came out of it, whatever the burial register says." She gathered her cardigan around herself. "I'm making tea. Don't put the volumes back wrong; they're shelved by date and I will know."

Her footsteps faded. The annex settled into its own silence, the kind with weight to it.

"Burial register first," Wren said. "If Thomas Ashcombe's in it, we know what the official story was."

Nell pulled the volume toward her and opened it. The pages were foxed brown, the ink faded to the color of dried blood—not dramatic, just old, but her hands were not steady and she hated that. She found the Ashcombe entries easily enough; the family had filled half a page per generation, their names marching down the columns like soldiers. *Edmund Ashcombe. Constance Ashcombe. Edmund junior.* And then:

*Thomas James Ashcombe, aged 9 years. Buried 14th March, 1911.*

"Aged nine," she said aloud. Her voice came out flat, which was how she kept things from being real. "There's a kid buried under the yew tree behind the hall. I've walked past that grave a hundred times. There's a lamb on it."

"There would be," Wren said quietly. "For a child."

She turned pages backward, looking for the death record that should have preceded the burial. Baptisms, marriages, burials—that was the order, that was the whole architecture of a life as the church understood it. But between Thomas's baptism in 1901 and his burial in 1911 there was nothing. No death registered. No cause. Just a boy who got baptized and then, ten years later, got buried, with a gap in between shaped exactly like a missing person.

"No death certificate," she said. "You can't bury someone without—can you? Even in 1911?"

"You can if nobody asks questions." Wren had the vestry minutes open now, running his thumb down the columns of minuted business: roof repairs, coal accounts, the harvest festival. His finger stopped. "Here. February 1911."

Nell leaned over. The handwriting was cramped and Victorian-fussy, every letter standing at attention:

*Mr. E. Ashcombe attended the Vestry and requested that the bell be removed and recast, offering to bear the full expense. Asked his reasons, he stated the bell had become unsound. Mr. Prowse observed the bell rang true at Sunday service. Mr. Ashcombe repeated that the bell had become unsound, and did not elaborate. Carried unanimously, the Vestry being sensible of the family's generosity.*

"'Did not elaborate,'" Nell read. "They knew. Look at the wording—they *carried it unanimously*, which means they voted to melt a perfectly good bell because a rich man asked them to and wouldn't say why. You don't do that unless you're scared of him or scared of something else."

"People were scared of lots of things in 1911," Wren said, but he said it the way people say things they don't believe anymore.

* * *

The vicar's private papers were worse, because they weren't official. They were a man thinking out loud on paper, believing no one would ever read it, and that made them honest in a way the registers couldn't afford to be.

The curate's name was Reverend Abel Marsh, and his handwriting deteriorated across the spring of 1911 like a man walking downhill. In March the letters were upright and confident. By June they slanted. By August they'd begun to crowd the margins, spilling sideways, as if the words themselves were trying to get away from the page.

Nell photographed everything. It was instinct—her phone was the one thing she trusted, the one witness that couldn't be gaslit—and she filmed each document flat and slow, narrating in a whisper for the recording. *Vicar's papers, box marked 1911, folio four.* If this ended up being nothing, she'd have a very weird video essay. If it wasn't nothing—

It wasn't nothing. She already knew it wasn't nothing. The frost prints had seen to that.

"Listen to this," Wren said. He'd gone quiet in the way he did when he found something, folding inward around the paper. "March the ninth. Two days before the burial."

*Called at the Hall regarding the funeral. The household is in mourning, yet I saw no coffin, and when I offered to conduct a service in the church, Mr. Ashcombe declined most firmly, saying the family would grieve privately. I pressed him—I confess I pressed him, for a child's death ought to be marked by the parish that baptized him—and he took my arm and said, very quietly, "Reverend, you will preach no sermon over my son, because my son is not dead, and the day you treat him as dead is the day you make it permanent."*

"I don't understand," Nell said. "Is he saying the kid's alive?"

"He's saying the kid's *down there*." Wren's voice had gone careful, level, the voice of a man carrying something fragile across ice. "Not dead. Not alive. Somewhere the church can't reach, and Ashcombe thinks that if the church performs a funeral—if it says the words—it stops being temporary."

"That's insane."

"Yes," Wren agreed. "Keep reading."

The next entry was dated March the eleventh, the night before the burial, and Nell felt the temperature of the room change before she finished the first line. Maybe it was the heating. Old buildings did that. She told herself it was the heating and kept filming.

*A terrible thing. I was awake past midnight over my sermons when the bell began to ring. One stroke only, then silence, then one again, slow as a mourner counting. I dressed and went out, for I thought some prankster had climbed the tower, and I met half the village in the lane, all of us in our nightclothes, all of us drawn by the same sound. But when we reached the churchyard the tower stood dark and open-mouthed, and the ringing continued, and I will swear on anything you ask me to swear on that the rope hung slack and unmoving.*

*Nine strokes in all. One for each year of the Ashcombe boy. Then it stopped, and in the silence that followed, Mrs. Prowse fainted, and old Widow Gann, who has not spoken since her son drowned in '04, spoke for the first time in seven years. She said, clear as a bell herself: "It's calling roll."*

*Then she went silent again, and has not spoken since, and I begin to think she never will.*

Nell lowered the phone. Her arms had gooseflesh from wrist to shoulder.

"It rang nine times," she said. "One for each year of his life. Like it was—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence made it real. *Like it was counting him out. Taking attendance.*

"Calling roll," Wren echoed. He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "Your grandmother said the bell rings once for each heir it means to collect. This is the same thing. It's been doing it since 1911. Since before."

"But Thomas was the first. Wasn't he?" Nell flipped through the folios, hunting. "The ledger starts with him. Edith showed me—his name's the first one with the little bell symbol next to it. So why did the bell ring for him? Who taught it to?"

Neither of them had an answer, and the question sat on the table between them like a third person.

* * *

It was in the last folder—the August papers, the worst handwriting—that they found the report. Nell almost missed it: a single sheet, folded twice, tucked inside a copy of the service book for Harvest 1911 as if the vicar had meant to destroy it and lost his nerve.

The heading read: *Observations upon the Hall at Undercroft, being a true account of what I witnessed on the nights of the 21st–23rd of August, 1911, set down for the Bishop's eye alone, and may God forgive me if I am mistaken.*

"Oh no," Nell whispered.

"Oh yes," Wren said grimly. "Read it."

She read it aloud, because her grandmother always said things spoken aloud couldn't follow you home, and because her phone was recording, and because if she stopped reading she would have to sit in the silence after it.

*I had been asked to call upon the household, the servants having given notice one and all within the space of a week. I found the Hall nearly empty, the family shut into the east wing, and Mr. Ashcombe himself grey as ash and twenty years older than in March. When I asked after his son he did not pretend. He said, "He is below, Reverend. Where he has always been below. We are only keeping the account current."*

*I did not understand him then. I understand him now, and I beg whoever reads this to believe that I take no pleasure in understanding.*

*On the second night I heard scratching beneath the floor of the passage outside the morning room. Not the scratching of rats, which is quick and aimless. This was deliberate. Rhythmic. It paused, and resumed, and paused, and I knelt and put my ear to the boards, and I heard beneath the scratching a smaller sound, regular as breathing, and I am ashamed to write what I believed it to be. I believed it to be a child, humming.*

*The third night I went below. There is a door under the stairs that appears on no plan of the house, and the lock of it is cast in the shape of a bell, and I had brought the key.*

Nell's voice caught. "He had a key?"

"Someone gave it to him," Wren said. "Or he took it. Keep going."

*I will set down what I saw plainly, for euphemism is a coward's charity. The stair descended further than the house is deep. At the bottom is a chamber, and the walls of the chamber are covered in handprints, hundreds upon hundreds, ascending the stone like the mark left by a flood—except that a flood leaves its mark descending, and these ascend, as if made by things rising, or trying to rise, or having risen and been pulled back. Some of the prints are small. Child-small.*

*And in the center of the chamber, on a plinth of black stone, lies a bell. Not melted. Never melted. The bell they poured off in 1911 was a copy, a decoy, a thing to hang silent in the tower while the true bell slept below with the rest of it. It is cast in the shape of an open book, and its surface is worked all over with names, and I read enough of them to be sick, for they are Ashcombe names, generation upon generation, and the newest of them, cut shallow and fresh, is THOMAS.*

*It is not a bell. I have prayed on it for six days and I will say it plainly: it is not a bell. A bell calls to worship. This thing calls to account. It is a ledger that makes its own noise, and the undercroft is not a cellar, whatever the family tells itself, whatever they have told themselves for longer than the parish has kept records.*

*The undercroft is a throat. And this family has been feeding it.*

Nell stopped reading. The annex was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a car went past, absurdly ordinary, and she flinched at it.

"They knew," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, thin and young. "The vicar knew in 1911. He wrote it down and sent it to the bishop and then—" she rifled through the folder, finding nothing after August, the handwriting simply stopping, the man simply stopping— "and then nothing. No reply. No follow-up. He wrote *may God forgive me if I am mistaken* and then he just... ends."

"Maybe the bishop wrote back and the letter's lost."

"Maybe." She stared at the last page. "Or maybe the bishop read *the undercroft is a throat and this family has been feeding it* and decided that was a problem for the Church of England's grand-children." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "A hundred and fourteen years. Nobody came. He documented the whole thing—bell, door, prints, names—and it went in a box, and the box went in a room, and the family just kept going. Kept feeding it. My great-great-whatever kept feeding it."

"Nell."

"And the worst part—" she was talking fast now, the way she talked when she was trying to outrun a feeling, "—the worst part is the fingerprints. On our walls. At shoulder height. I've been filming them for weeks and I keep thinking, whose hands are those, how big are they, are they getting closer to the floor, and the answer was in here the whole time. They're not coming *down* the walls, Wren. They're climbing *up*. They're trying to get out. They've been trying to get out since before the Titanic sank, and every couple of generations my family catches one and puts it back."

She heard herself and stopped. The room tilted slightly. She put her palm flat on the table, on the photocopied margin of a dead man's terror, and breathed.

Wren let her breathe. That was one of the things about him she'd stopped resenting and started relying on: he didn't fill silences with comfort. He just held them open until you were ready.

"Okay," she said finally. "Okay. Say it's all true. Say the vicar was right. Then the bell downstairs isn't decoration and it isn't a trap. It's a *record*. Every name it ever counted is written on it. Which means—"

"Which means the ledger Edith showed you might be a copy too," Wren said slowly. "Like the tower bell. A working copy. And the original is under the house, with the names of everyone who ever went down there, going back—"

"—to whenever it started." Nell picked up her phone and checked the footage, frame by frame, the way she checked everything now. The vicar's words swam on the small screen. *Ascend. Trying to rise. Pulled back.* "Wren. The prints on the walls at the hall. I measured them last week, after the frost ones. Did I tell you? The lowest ones are at knee height now. They were at chest height when we moved in."

Something crossed Wren's face that she'd never seen on it before—not fear exactly, but the recognition of fear, a man meeting an old acquaintance somewhere he'd hoped never to see it again.

"How long," he said, "between the frost prints and your measurements?"

"Three weeks."

He nodded once, and started gathering the folders with hands that were almost steady. "We're taking copies of everything. All of it. And Nell—"

"Yeah."

"When we get back, before anything else, I want you to film the corridor again. All of it, floor to ceiling." He glanced at the vicar's last page, at the word *throat*, and closed the folder gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing. "Because if the prints are climbing, then something down there isn't waiting to be fed anymore."

Outside, faint across the churchyard, so distant it might have been wind in the yew trees—or might not—came a single soft stroke, like a bell remembering how to count.
