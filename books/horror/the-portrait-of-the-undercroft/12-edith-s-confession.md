## 12. Edith's Confession

Edith Ashcombe had spent seventy-one years learning the weight of a house, and she knew this one the way a widow knows a wedding ring: by its absence as much as its presence. She felt it now, in the small hours of the morning after the frost handprints bloomed on every window like lilies pressed in ice—felt it listening from below, patient as arithmetic.

She had not slept. Sleep was for people whose ledgers balanced.

She dressed before dawn in the dark, out of habit and out of shame, because she could not bear to see her own reflection while she did what had to be done. In the drawer of her writing desk, beneath forty years of insurance papers and Hal's school reports and Gerald's funeral card, lay the ledger.

Not Wren's ledger—the one Edith had shown him, the family record with its column of bell-marked names. That was the copy. The courtesy version. The one you showed a pest controller so that he would understand enough to leave and not enough to stay.

The true ledger was older. It was bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, and it had been her mother-in-law's before it was hers, and hers before that, all the way back to a woman named Constance Ashcombe who had written the first entry in a hand that shook: *The house asks. We answered. God forgive what keeps the roof on.*

Edith carried it down the corridor with both hands, the way you carry an infant or a bomb.

* * *

Nell's door was closed. Behind it, the blue flicker of a screen—awake at five in the morning, of course she was awake, the whole house was awake, even the walls were awake now. Edith stood outside that door for a long time with her knuckles raised to knock.

She had been standing outside doors her whole life. Outside Gerald's door, in the last year, when his cough went wrong and she knew—he had told her, quietly, over the washing up, that the house had begun speaking to him in his father's voice—and she had said nothing, done nothing, because the alternative was saying aloud the thing that could never be said. Outside Hal's door when he was nine and crying about noises under the stairs, and she had told him it was pipes. Pipes. As if any pipe in England had ever learned a child's name.

She knocked.

Nothing. Then, muffled: "If you're here to tell me it's mice, Gran, I've got four hours of footage that says otherwise."

"It's me," Edith said. "I need to talk to you."

A pause long enough to hold a funeral. "Is Grandad's ghost in there with you?"

"Nell."

"Coming."

* * *

They sat in the morning room because it was the only room in Undercroft Hall where Edith could breathe. The windows faced east, and the first grey light came through them flat and colorless, laying itself across the furniture like a sheet over a body. Nell sat curled in the corner of the sofa with her phone dark in her hand—for once, mercifully, not filming—and looked at her grandmother with the flat, unblinking patience of a girl who had decided years ago that adults were weather: something to be endured until it passed.

"I'm going to tell you something," Edith said, "and then you're going to hate me, and I want you to know before I start that I've already hated myself for longer than you've been alive, so you'll have to work hard to catch up."

"Gran—"

"The house takes someone." She said it plainly, because she had rehearsed plainness for decades and plainness was all she had left. "Every generation. On the count—which is coming, you've seen the frost, you've heard the bell—it names an heir, and that heir goes below, into the undercroft, and does not come back. And the family is permitted—" her voice caught on the word *permitted*, snagged like a nail on silk, "—the family may offer a substitute. A volunteer. Of the blood. Someone who chooses it in the heir's place."

The silence afterward had texture. Edith could feel it against her skin, fine as ash.

"That's insane," Nell said, but softly, without conviction, the way you say *that's insane* when you have watched a shape with too many joints press its palm against a wall in night vision.

"Yes," Edith agreed. "It is entirely insane. And it is also true, and those two things have coexisted in this family for two hundred years, which is why we are all the way we are. Why your father can't finish a sentence. Why your grandfather died with his boots on, walking toward a door under the stairs. Why I—" She stopped. Started again. "Why I am the way I am."

"You said Grandad died of his heart."

"He did. His heart stopped in the undercroft, three days after he walked into it. They found him at the bottom of the stairs inside, folded up neat as laundry, smiling." Her hands were shaking. She let them. There was no dignity left to protect; she had spent it all buying silence. "He volunteered for Hal."

* * *

It came out of her then, all of it, in the order she had forbidden herself to think it in for fifty years.

Her marriage, 1971. The first count she had witnessed, when Gerald's father, old Ambrose, had been named—named how? By the bell, by the portraits, by something in the walls that wrote its choice in frost across the nursery window. Ambrose had been sixty-two and ready, had gone down those stairs almost gratefully, waving from the threshold like a man boarding a ship. And the house had been satisfied for another twenty-six years, and the estate had stayed, and the village had stayed fed by the farm rents, and everyone had lived.

Everyone except Ambrose.

"And then it was Gerald's turn," Edith said. "When Hal turned thirty. That's when they name them—not children, never children, the house waits until the heir is grown. Hal was working in London, hating it, drinking too much, and the bell rang on a Tuesday in March and the frost spelled his initials on the glass of the morning room, right there—" she pointed at the window, at nothing, at the ordinary pane through which the grey light fell, "—right there, where you're sitting looking at me."

"What did you do?"

"We did what Ashcombes do." Edith's voice went very quiet. "We did the arithmetic. One life against the estate. Against the name. Against everything built on it since 1740. Your grandfather sat at this table for eleven days doing that sum, and on the twelfth day he kissed my forehead and wrote his own name in the ledger under Hal's, and he said, *Edith, love, it's only fair—I'm the one who taught him to hate this place.*"

Nell had gone white. Not film-star white; milk-left-out white; the white of a girl discovering that the floor of her life was painted on and had always been painted on.

"And Hal doesn't know."

"Hal knows the shape of it and has spent his whole life refusing to look directly at it. That's why he hired Mr. Okonkwo. That's why he wanted rats. Rats are a problem you can pay to make go away." Edith folded her hands in her lap, and they were an old woman's hands, spotted and trembling, and she hated them for their ordinariness. "But that isn't the worst of it. The worst of it is what I've done. What I've been doing since you were born."

* * *

She made herself say it looking at Nell directly, because anything less would be cowardice stacked on cowardice, and there was a limit even in this family.

"When you were born, the midwife put you in my arms and you had the Ashcombe chin—you have it still, look in any portrait upstairs—and I thought: *she's of the blood.* And I thought: *if it ever comes to it, if the count ever names Hal, or names some child of Hal's, I will write your name instead.* Because you were young. Because you'd have decades more than anyone else we could offer. Because the house accepts a volunteer of any age, and a young volunteer buys the longest peace."

Nell's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again. "You've had my name ready. Since I was a *baby.*"

"In pencil," Edith said, absurdly, and heard how it sounded, and pressed on anyway because the truth was worse than how it sounded. "In pencil, in the ledger, every year on your birthday, I've renewed it. Like a subscription. Like a—like a ration book. Sixteen entries. Sixteen birthdays where I held you and sang to you and taught you to bake and all the while, underneath, the arithmetic ran: *her, if it must be someone; her, because she's the most; her, because loving her this much must be worth something to the house.*"

"That's not love," Nell whispered. "That's accounting."

"Yes," said Edith, and the word broke in half on the way out. "Yes. That is exactly what it is. That is what two hundred years of this house does to love, Nell. It turns it into arithmetic. You start out willing to die for your family and you end up deciding *which* family member dies, and you call it duty, and you sleep at night because the numbers come out. I have slept every night of my life on numbers. And last night I lay awake and listened to the frost crawling on the windows and I understood, finally, at seventy-one, that I have become the house. I keep a ledger. I weigh lives. I mark names. I am the thing I should have burned."

* * *

Nell was crying without noise, the way she did everything without noise, filming the world for years without once asking to be in it. Edith reached across the space between them and Nell flinched—actually flinched, drew back like a struck animal—and Edith withdrew her hand and folded it back into her lap and accepted that too. That was part of the confession. That was the interest accruing on fifty years of secret sums.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Nell asked at last, voice raw. "Why not just—do it? Write the name. Go downstairs. You clearly know how."

"Because of the pest man." Edith almost smiled. "Because of Mr. Okonkwo, who walks around this house with his little rationalist armor on, checking for fingerprints, keeping his own notebook, asking questions no Ashcombe has dared ask in living memory. He asked me yesterday, standing in front of the undercroft door, why the lock was shaped like a bell. And I ordered him away like a woman defending a treasure, and he looked at me—not angry, just *looking*, the way you look at a stain you're trying to identify—and I realized I have spent my entire life guarding the door instead of asking what's behind it. Everyone in this family guards the door. Two centuries of guards. And not one of us has ever asked the dead what they want."

"They're dead. Dead things don't want."

"Don't they?" Edith said. "Then why do they scratch? Why do they press their hands to the walls at shoulder height, like people knocking? Why did your grandfather smile?" She leaned forward, and the grey light caught the wet on her cheeks, and she let Nell see it, let her film it later if she needed to, evidence, footage, proof. "I'm telling you this because you deserve to hear it from me before the bell rings again. Because when the count comes—and it is coming, three nights, maybe less—the house will name someone, and I will not let it be you, and I will not let it be your father, and I no longer believe the answer is me either. I believe, for the first time, that there might be another answer. A fourth column in the ledger that none of us ever wrote because none of us ever dared."

"Which is?"

"Burning it," Edith said.

Somewhere below them—below the floor, below the cellar, below whatever the cellar was the lid of—something shifted. Not a sound exactly. A settling, the way a great animal settles when it hears its name spoken carelessly.

Nell looked toward the door. So did Edith. For a moment neither breathed.

"Gran," Nell said, very quietly, "last night on the recording—the scratched eyes, the ones in the portraits—they weren't growling. I slowed it down. I ran it through transcription software twice." Her hands had begun to shake, and she gripped the phone with both of them. "They were saying one word. Over and over. All of them. Every generation."

Edith waited.

"*Ledger,*" Nell said. "They were saying *ledger*. They weren't calling for us, Gran. They were calling for the book."

And Edith Ashcombe, who had kept the family's accounts for fifty years, who had renewed a death sentence in pencil sixteen times, who had become the very arithmetic she feared—felt something move in her chest that was not dread. It was older than dread. It might, given time and courage, turn out to be hope.

"Get Mr. Okonkwo," she said. "Wake him. Bring the recordings. And bring me the fire tongs from the drawing room—we may soon have paper that needs burning."

Behind them, faint and far beneath the floor, the melted bell began, very softly, to hum.
