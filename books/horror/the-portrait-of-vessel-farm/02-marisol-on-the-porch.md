## 2. Marisol on the Porch

The light was going out of the day the way water goes out of a cracked cup—slowly at first, and then all at once, and then it was gone and you were holding something empty that used to be full.

Rafael stood at the edge of the yard with her camera bag on her shoulder and her notebook in her coat pocket, and she watched Vessel Farm assemble itself out of the dusk. That was the only word for it. The house didn't come into view so much as it gathered, the way a figure gathers out of fog: first the dark suggestion of roofline, then the pale rectangles of windows, then the porch, the whole long porch with its sagging rail and its swing hanging from two chains that had not been oiled in her lifetime. She had driven eleven hours to get here. She had planned the arrival for daylight, had meant to photograph the house in honest light, the way you photograph a body before the funeral so the family can remember the face as it was.

But grief is a bad navigator, and she had stopped twice on the road—once at a diner where the waitress called her *honey* and once at a gas station where a man stared at her license plate as if it had said something rude—and so the sun had gotten ahead of her, and now she was here at the hour her grandmother used to call *la hora de los prestados*, the hour of borrowed things, when everything looks like something it isn't.

The house looked like something it wasn't. Or rather, it looked exactly like what it was, and that was worse: a house that had been holding its breath since 1974.

She had the key. Of course she had the key; Consuelo had mailed it in an envelope with no note, only the key wrapped in a page torn from a church bulletin, and Rafael had carried it in her breast pocket the whole drive like a stone she was keeping warm. But she didn't go to the door. Something in her wanted to walk the yard first, to let her feet remember the ground, the way you let your hand remember the shape of a dead person's shoulder before you have to say goodbye to it in public.

The grass had gone to seed. The orchard stood at the back of the property like a congregation of thin dark men, and beyond it, invisible from here but present anyway, the way a scar is present under a sleeve, was the back acre where the well had been dry for as long as anyone would admit to remembering.

She raised the camera. This was the ritual she had made of her grief, the only liturgy she trusted: *look, record, look again.* Her brother was in a coffin in the back of the rental car—she had not been able to leave him at the funeral home overnight, had not been able to explain why, even to herself—and until the ground took him, her job was to see everything, to write down everything, because the Villaloboses had spent fifty years not seeing, and look what not seeing had grown in their garden.

The shutter clicked. The house held still for it, patient as a sitter for a portrait.

That was when she heard the chains.

*Chink. Chink. Chink.* The slow, rhythmic complaint of the porch swing bearing weight, moving, bearing weight again. The sound her childhood had been made of, summer evenings, her grandmother's hands pushing the swing in long lazy arcs, the smell of cilantro and dust coming through the screen door.

But her grandmother had been dead for nine years, and the house had been empty for six, and Consuelo had sworn on the phone—the only time she had called, her voice dry as old paper—that no one had set foot on that porch since the last storm took a shingle off the north side.

Rafael did not move. Her whole body had become a held photograph.

The swing moved again. *Chink.* And then a voice, high and sweet and unhurried, a child's voice with an old woman's patience in it, said:

"You came back late."

Rafael's knees nearly went. She gripped the camera bag strap with both hands and made herself turn, made herself walk toward the porch steps, because she was fifty-one years old and she had buried a mother and a father and now a brother, and she had spent her whole life learning that the only way through a terrible thing is directly, with your eyes open, writing it down.

The porch swing was occupied.

* * *

She was small. That was the first thing, the thing that broke something loose in Rafael's chest before her fear could catch it and pin it down. Marisol was small—small the way Rafael had been small, small the way a seven-year-old is small, with thin brown legs that didn't reach the floorboards and swung in the empty air, and a cotton dress printed with yellow flowers, and black hair cut in the blunt bangs that her own mother had given her, once, with sewing scissors and a trembling hand, in the summer of 1974.

She had not aged. Rafael had spent fifty years aging—knees, hands, the slow settling of her face into her mother's face—and Marisol had spent fifty years being seven.

"You came back late," Marisol said again, and swung her feet, and the chains sang.

Rafael stood at the bottom of the steps. The dusk had thickened around the porch light, which had come on by itself, or had never been off, or had been waiting; its yellow cone of light fell across the boards and stopped at the swing's edge as if the light itself were unwilling to go further.

"You're not real," Rafael said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "You were never real. I made you up. I was a lonely kid on a lonely farm and I made you up out of nothing, and then I grew up, and you went away. That's what happened. That's the whole story."

Marisol considered this with her head tipped, the way she used to consider things, the way Rafael had considered things, because a child inventing a friend invents a mirror.

"That's the story *you* kept," Marisol said. "You kept it in a box under your bed for a long time. Then you kept it in a box in your chest. It's a little wrinkled now. But it's not the whole story."

The words went into Rafael like cold water into a boot. Not because they were frightening—though they were—but because they were *right*. There had been a box under her bed. A shoebox, red cardboard gone soft at the corners, and she had kept in it a hair ribbon and a marble and a drawing of two girls holding hands under a tree with too many apples, and she had never told a living soul about the box, and she had never, in fifty years, been able to throw it away.

"How do you know that," she said. It wasn't a question. Nothing she said that evening would be a question. Questions require the possibility of an answer you can survive.

Marisol patted the swing beside her. The chains swayed. "Sit," she said. "You always sat on the left. You said the left side was the side where the good light came from."

And Rafael—fifty-one years old, a woman who had interviewed dying men and photographed flooded houses and sat with her brother's body in a rental car for four hours because she couldn't bear to leave him with strangers—Rafael climbed the steps and sat down on the left side of the swing, where the good light came from, and the chains took their weight with a sound like a sigh.

Up close, Marisol smelled of well water. That was the second thing Rafael noticed and the first thing she would remember: not the face, not the dress, not the voice, but the smell—cold stone and iron and something green and drowning underneath, the smell of the bucket coming up from the deep places, the smell her grandmother's hands had carried all the summers of Rafael's childhood, and Rafael had never once thought to wonder why.

"You look the same," Marisol said, studying her with those dark eyes that were too old for the face they lived in, that had always been too old, and God, why had she never wondered about that either, why had a lonely seven-year-old not wondered why her imaginary friend had eyes like a woman who had buried everyone she loved. "You got tall. You got sad. But you look the same."

"I came to bury my brother," Rafael said.

"I know." Marisol swung her feet. "I heard the car. I heard the coffin settle when you went over the cattle guard. It sounded like he was turning over in his sleep."

The evening had gone fully dark now, and the porch light made a small warm island with the two of them in it, and beyond the island the yard was black and the orchard was blacker, and somewhere out there, under the oldest apple tree, something waited that Rafael did not yet know she would have to dig for. The cicadas had started. They sounded, as they always had, like the air itself being slowly unzipped.

"Nobody told me about you," Rafael said. "When I asked, they changed the subject. Mamá used to leave the room. Abuela said—" she stopped, because Abuela had said, in the last summer, holding Rafael's hand too hard: *Don't call her. Not here. Not on this land. If you need her, need her somewhere else.* And Rafael, seven years old and furious, had thought it was about manners.

"Abuela was right to say it," Marisol said, and for a moment her feet stopped swinging, and the swing went still, and the silence was the silence of a held breath, of a house holding a breath for fifty years. "This land remembers names better than people do. You say a name here enough times, the land starts answering to it." She looked at Rafael then, fully, and the porch light seemed to dim, or to lean, or to be drawn toward the small face the way a moth is drawn, and her voice when it came was gentle the way a trap is gentle, the way a well is gentle, all that patience, all that depth. "Say your name for me. The one you haven't said since you left. The one they gave you in the kitchen in the winter of '74, when you were small and crying and the whole family was standing around you like a wall."

"I don't—"

"You do," Marisol said. "You remember it. You've been carrying it in the box in your chest, right next to the ribbon and the marble. You've been carrying it so long you've forgotten it isn't a stone. It's a name. It's *your* name. And I've been waiting fifty years for somebody to say it out loud, because I can't say it myself, and it's the only key that fits me."

Rafael's hands had gone cold around the camera bag strap. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and old, a rhythm from a body that was seven years old, standing in a kitchen, being told a secret by six adults who all looked like they were drowning.

"I don't know what you are," she said.

"I'm your friend," Marisol said simply, and the terrible thing, the thing that Rafael would write in her notebook later that night in handwriting that shook, was that it was true. Whatever else was sitting on that swing wearing the shape of a seven-year-old girl, the *friendship* was real; it had been real; it had been the truest thing in Rafael's childhood, and it was real now, and it sat beside her smelling of well water and wanting something from her with a hunger so old and so patient it made grief look shallow.

"I waited," Marisol said. "Every summer I sat on this swing and the chains rusted and the paint peeled and the family came and went and got small and got old and got buried, and I stayed seven, because that's how you made me and I can't change it, and I waited. And now you're here, and you brought a camera, and you brought a notebook, and you brought him home in a box." She reached out and touched the camera bag, one small finger, light as a moth landing. "Good. Write it down. Write all of it down. The house has been trying to say it for fifty years and nobody would listen. You're going to listen, aren't you? You're going to write it all down and make it true?"

The question hung in the mothy light.

Rafael thought: *I am fifty-one years old and I am sitting on a porch swing at dusk with an imaginary friend, and I am not leaving, and I am not screaming, and some part of me that I do not want to examine has been waiting for this my entire life.*

She unzipped the camera bag. Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than anything else had.

"Then show me where to start," she said.

Marisol smiled, and it was the smile Rafael had invented, the exact smile, gap-toothed and luminous, and it was also not that smile at all, the way a well is also a hole.

"Start with the rooms," Marisol said, and swung her feet, and the chains sang. "Start with the room at the end of the hall. The one they emptied. The one with the wallpaper. You'll know it when you see it. You'll know it the way you know your own face."

* * *

Later—much later, after the porch light had buzzed and died and Marisol had faded with it, not leaving, just *dimming*, the way a face dims when a car passes, still there, still there, still there—Rafael stood in the kitchen of Vessel Farm with the notebook open and wrote by the light of a lamp she'd had to screw in herself, the bulb rattling in the socket like a small bone.

*Marisol. On the porch. Aged not a day. Smells of well water. Knows the shoebox. Knows the kitchen. Wants a name said aloud—mine, the one from 1974. Says the house has been trying to speak for fifty years.*

She stopped. The pen hovered.

She thought about writing *imaginary friend, hallucination, grief-induced episode,* any of the clinical words that would let her sleep. But the notebook had become something else tonight, she realized. Somewhere between the cattle guard and the porch swing, between the coffin settling and the chains singing, the notebook had stopped being documentation and become devotion—*look, record, look again* had become the only prayer she knew how to say, and prayers, she understood suddenly, sitting alone in her dead family's kitchen, are not private. A prayer is a letter you send without knowing the address. Something reads them. Something has always been reading them.

She wrote, in small careful letters, the last line of the night:

*She said to start with the room at the end of the hall.*

And upstairs, faint through the ceiling, as if in answer—or as if in *acknowledgment*, the way a bank acknowledges a deposit—a floorboard creaked, once, in the room at the end of the hall.

Then, from the stairs, where no one had been standing when Rafael came in: the slow sound of someone sitting down to watch.
