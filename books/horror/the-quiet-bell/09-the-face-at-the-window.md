## 9. The Face at the Window

I had not slept properly in Pemberton House since I was nine years old, and the house seemed determined to keep my record intact.

It was the clocks that did it. Every clock in the building ticked at its own tempo, and none of them agreed, so that lying in my childhood bed was like lying inside a body with several arrhythmic hearts. The grandfather clock in the hall ran four minutes fast. The kitchen clock, a cheerful sunflower-yellow thing Aunt Sable had bought in 1987 and never replaced, ran seven minutes slow and ticked with a faint limp, as though one of its hands dragged. My bedside clock — the one Ysolde and I had won at the Midsummer fair, a plastic lighthouse whose bulb rotated through a smear of red every six seconds — kept perfect time, which I had always found vaguely sinister in an object so cheap.

3 a.m., said the lighthouse, glowing its little red accusation into the dark.

I had gone to bed at eleven. I had performed the rituals. Doors counted: ten. Locks checked: all. Windows latched: all, including the nursery window on the third floor that hadn't opened since 1961 but which we had always checked anyway, because the ritual was the ritual and rituals do not survive improvisation. I had walked the perimeter of my bed the way we used to, three turns clockwise, one turn counter — a sequence Ysolde had invented with the gravity of a general and which I had followed ever since with the obedience of a soldier who has forgotten which war she's fighting.

And then I had lain in the dark and listened to the house count its own doors, and somewhere in that listening I had drifted, and somewhere in that drifting the lighthouse bulb swept red across the ceiling and I woke, certain, absolutely certain, that someone was standing in the garden.

I want to be honest about the order of events, because the order matters. I did not see her and then feel that she was there. I felt it first — a pressure against my awareness, the way you feel a name you're about to be called by. The knowledge arrived whole, the way Ysolde's knowledge always arrived whole. She'd wake from naps knowing the phone was going to ring and who it would be. She'd know which elevator was going to open. I used to tell her she was a show-off. She used to tell me I was the same, only I didn't know it yet.

I got up. This was, in hindsight, the entire mistake, and I made it before I was even fully awake, my body moving on rails laid down twenty years ago. Because there had been nights, when we were small, when one of us would feel the other wake — not hear, not see, *feel* — and would go padding down the hall to check. It was the one ritual we never wrote down, the one that lived below the doors-and-locks liturgy, and it was still in me, still operating, still sending me to the window before my higher functions had a chance to file an objection.

The curtains in my old room were the same curtains: heavy, botanical, printed with ferns so dark they were nearly black. I pulled them back with two fingers, the way you'd lift the edge of a bandage to check on a wound you already knew was bad.

The garden was silvered with fog. Not the thick coastal fog I'd driven through on my way in — this was thinner, a ground-mist, the kind that pools in low places and makes a lawn look like the surface of a cold lake. The old birch stood at its center with its white limbs stripped bare, and the sundial Ysolde and I had knocked over in 1994 and never confessed to still lay on its side in the herb bed, its gnomon pointing at nothing, telling no time at all.

And there, at the far edge of the garden, where the lawn met the collapsed stone wall and the wall met the beginning of the orchard, stood Ysolde.

I want to describe this correctly, because everything that happened afterward depended on the correctness of it. She was not floating. She was not pale, or gaunt, or wrong in any of the ways the dead are wrong in films. She was standing in her grey coat — the one with the missing second button that she'd never sewn back on because she said a coat with a gap was more honest than one without — and her hands were in her pockets, and her head was tilted slightly, and she was looking up at my window with an expression I had spent my whole life cataloguing, because it was the face she made when she was waiting for me to catch up.

Unchanged. That was the horror of it, and I want to be precise: not that she was dead and standing in a garden, but that she was *unchanged*. Ysolde had died at thirty-four, and the woman at the wall was thirty-four, and there was no gap, no interval, no evidence that anything as enormous as death had happened to her in between. It was as if she had stepped out of the world for a moment — a week, a month — and come back to find me still at the window, still slow, still needing to be beckoned.

She raised one hand out of her pocket and beckoned.

Not a wave. A wave is for across a street, across a room, across a distance that has manners. This was the small, curling, impatient-tender gesture she had used our entire childhood to mean *come here, I've found something.* She'd found fox dens and wasps' nests and the loose board in the orchard shed where Grandfather hid his cigarettes, and every time, it was this gesture, two fingers curling inward, her head tipping like a bird's: *come here, I've found something.*

My hand was on the window latch.

I don't know when it got there. That's the thing I keep returning to, the thing I turn over at night like a stone looking for what's underneath. Somewhere between the bed and the window, my body had decided, and my mind had simply been brought along as a witness. The latch was cold under my fingers. The window is old; the latch is the original iron, a simple hook-and-eye, and I had checked it four hours earlier as part of the liturgy, and now my thumb was already pushing the hook up out of its eye, and the fog was lying on the garden like a held breath, and my sister — my dead sister, my sister who was dead, I made myself think the words in full, with their full weight, the way you press on a bruise to confirm it's real — was waiting for me to come and see what she'd found.

And it was at that moment — thumb on the hook, the window not yet open, the cold already beginning to seep through the gap in the seal — that the ritual surfaced.

*Never answer a Pemberton after midnight.*

Not a rule we'd invented. A rule we'd been *given*. I could suddenly see the scene with terrible clarity: Grandmother Odile, younger, her voice like a struck nail, standing over our beds — or over one bed, we'd shared one until we were seven — saying it the way she said everything, as though reciting terms and conditions. *If anyone knocks after midnight, you do not open. If anyone calls your name after midnight, you do not answer. If anyone beckons —* and here she had paused, and I had felt, even at six, even half-asleep, the weight she was putting on the word — *if anyone beckons after midnight, you do not go. Not even family. Especially family.*

*Why, Granny?* Ysolde had asked, because Ysolde always asked.

And Odile had said, in the flat voice she saved for the true things: *Because after midnight, the family is not only ours.*

I took my thumb off the hook.

It sounds so small. It was the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than identifying her body, harder than the phone call to Maren, harder than any of it, because everything in me — not just love, which would at least have been dignified, but the dumb animal machinery of twinhood, the half-of-a-whole-ness that doesn't consult you — everything in me was leaning on that window like a woman leaning on a door in a flood. She was *cold out there*. That was the thought that rose, absurd, maternal: she's cold, she's standing in the fog in her coat with the missing button, and I am the only one who knows she's there, and what kind of sister —

*What kind of sister,* the thought whispered, *left her unburied and unspoken-to for six years?*

And there it was. That was the blade under the beckoning. Not the fog, not the hour, not the dead face. The guilt. It had been waiting for me since the funeral home, since the housekeeper's cheerful, terrible *Miss Ysolde, you're here at last* — waiting, patient as the house itself, and now it came up through the floorboards of my resolve and put its hands over mine on the latch.

Because here is the arithmetic of estrangement, and every estranged person knows it: you do not stop loving someone. You simply stop *spending* the love, and it accumulates, and it accrues interest, and it sits in you like water behind a dam, and when the person dies the dam doesn't break so much as become meaningless, because there's no one left to hold it back from. Six years of unsaid things. Six years of birthdays I'd noted and not called on. Six years of the specific cowardice of twins, who can hurt each other in ways no one else can reach and so learn to stay out of reach entirely.

And now she was standing in the garden offering me the one thing I wanted more than safety, more than sense, more than life itself, probably: the chance to say it. All of it. *I'm sorry. I didn't mean for it to go six years. I thought you'd call. I thought you'd always be there, that's the whole sickness of it, I thought you were permanent, and I spent that permanence like it was free.*

The lighthouse clock swept its red light across the ceiling. 3:04.

She was still beckoning. She had not moved, not shifted her weight, not breathed — and I noticed that, God help me, I noticed it even through the wanting, because you spend a lifetime learning the way a person stands and the knowledge doesn't die when they do. Ysolde in life could not stand still. Thirty-four years of a body in constant small motion, weight shifting, fingers tapping, the restless economy of her. The figure at the wall stood the way furniture stands.

*Come here,* the gesture said. *I've found something.*

*What have you found?* I thought, and hated myself for thinking it, and thought it anyway.

And then she smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was a smile I knew. Not the public smile, not the one from the photographs Odile had arranged on the mantel for the wake. The private one. The one that started on the wrong side of her face and pulled it slightly crooked, the one she saved for me, the one that meant *we're about to do something stupid and wonderful.* No one else had ever been smiled at like that. It was mine. It had been mine for thirty-four years, and it was being worn now, the way you'd wear a coat, the way you'd wear a face.

My thumb found the hook again.

I would like to tell you I was strong. What actually happened is that the kitchen clock, seven minutes slow and limping, chose that moment — audible even through two floors, or perhaps not audible, perhaps only *counted* by me, because the house and I had an understanding by now — to chime the half-hour. A chime that was, by its own broken reckoning, 3:30, but by the world's was 3:23, and the sound of it, wrong and stubborn and *Sable's*, cheap yellow plastic clock with a chime it had no business having, broke the spell the way a wrong note breaks a hymn.

Because the ritual was not just *don't open the door.* The ritual had a second half. Ysolde had added it herself, age eight, with the solemnity of a supreme court: *and if you almost do, you say the count out loud, and the count will hold you.*

The count of the doors. Ten doors. I stood at the window with my dead sister's private smile fixed on me from the fog and I said it, out loud, in a voice that came out in pieces: *"Hall. Kitchen. Pantry. Parlor. Dining. Study. Odile's room. The blue room. My room. Ysolde's room. Ten."*

And I stood there and counted the actual doors of the house in my head, the way we had every night of our childhood, and the count held, and my hand came down off the window, and I stood in the dark shaking like something recently rescued from deep water.

Below me, Ysolde lowered her hand.

She did not look disappointed. That was the detail I would carry into every hour that followed, the one I would not tell Maren, would not tell anyone: she looked *patient*. She tilted her head, and the crooked smile settled into something gentler, something almost kind, and she gave a small nod — the nod of a teacher watching a student get the answer right, eventually, after too long, but get it right.

*Good,* the nod said. *You remembered. You'll need to remember.*

Then she turned and walked into the orchard, and the fog closed behind her the way water closes, without a mark, without a parting, as though the garden had simply agreed to have never contained her.

* * *

I did not go back to bed. I want that on the record too, because everything I did afterward grew out of that refusal. I dressed in the dark, badly, one sock inside out, and I sat on the floor at the foot of my bed with my back against the frame — the frame Ysolde and I had slept in together until we were seven, the frame that still had, underneath the rail on my side, the pencil marks where we'd recorded our heights, *X + Y, 44½, SAME* — and I made myself think.

Because here is what the window had taught me, and it was a lesson I would have paid any price not to learn: the thing in the garden did not need to trick me. Trickery is for strangers. It had used *her* — her coat, her button, her smile, my guilt — and it had used them well, and it would use them again, and the only defense I had was the one Ysolde had built when she was eight years old and the house had already taught her that love could be counterfeited.

*Trying to stop it,* the letters in her room had said. She had come home a month before she died to stop the count, and I had spent that month not answering her messages, and now she was dead and something was answering in her place, and the something had just shown me its favorite weapon, and the weapon was me.

The lighthouse clock swept red. 3:51.

I got up. I went down the hall — hall door, one — and past the parlor — two — and into the kitchen — three — and I put the kettle on, because ritual survives on small absurdities, and I sat at the table where the Count Ledger had lain open two nights ago with its generations of crossed-out names, and I waited for the window over the sink to go grey, and I thought about what Ysolde would have done at this hour, in this kitchen, with this knowledge.

She would have gone to the orchard, I thought. That was the unbearable answer. She would have counted her doors, and then she would have walked out into the fog and said *show me what you found,* because Ysolde's courage was always of the kind that got people killed, and it had finally, at the end, gotten her killed, in my place, in this house, and I had not even been speaking to her.

The kettle began its low climb toward a whistle. Outside, the fog lay on the garden, and the birch stood white and patient, and somewhere past the collapsed wall, in the black rows of the orchard, something that wore my sister's face was waiting to be found.

And I understood, sitting there with the whistle building and the grey coming up slow over the sink window, that the war for the rest of my life — however much of it the ledger allowed — had just begun, and that its battlefield was not the garden, and not the house, but the six years of unsaid things stacked up inside me like water behind a failing dam.

The kettle screamed. I made the tea. And when the sun finally came up, thin and colorless over the orchard wall, I went upstairs to Ysolde's room and unlocked the box under her bed, because the dead had left me homework, and I intended, this once in my life, to do it on time.
