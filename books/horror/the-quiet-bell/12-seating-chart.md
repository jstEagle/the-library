## 12. Seating Chart

I did not sleep after the night at the window. Sleep felt like a door left open, and I had learned — relearned, rather, in the way you learn things your body knew before your mind agreed to them — that doors were the whole problem.

So instead I sat in Odile's study with every photograph the house owned spread across the floor like a tarot deck nobody sane would read.

The study smelled of pipe smoke from a grandfather who'd died before I was born and lemon polish from a housekeeper who'd quit in 1998 and never been replaced so much as absorbed into legend. The lamp threw a yellow circle over the boards. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass with the patience of something that had all night and all century.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to myself was better than listening for footsteps on stairs that creaked whether or not anyone climbed them. "Let's do this properly."

Properly meant chronology. I am a copyeditor by trade — or I was, back when I had a life that fit in boxes — and there is a particular comfort in ordering chaos. Dates on the backs of photographs. Hairstyles as era markers. The slow geological record of the Pemberton face: strong jaw, deep-set eyes, and in the women a certain look of having just heard an insult they're choosing to ignore.

I started with the oldest thing in the box: a daguerreotype, or what passed for one, so silvered with age it was more mirror than image. Four figures posed outside the front door, stiff as fence posts. Rowena Pemberton in the center — I recognized her from the portrait Odile kept locked away, though I hadn't seen it since I was nine and had spent twenty years half-convinced I'd dreamed it. Flanking her, two daughters. Twins, by the matching faces and the matching dresses. Their hands were folded identically, palms down, like two lids closed over something.

And behind them, half in shadow at the edge of the frame, a wooden chair.

Empty. Plain-backed. Ordinary. The kind of chair that exists in every farmhouse in England and therefore should have been invisible, forgettable, nothing.

Except that in this photograph, taken — per the penciled date on the backing — in 1861, the chair stood eleven feet from the group. I measured with my thumb against the flagstones, which is not science but is a kind of faith.

"Eleven feet," I told the photograph. It declined to comment.

* * *

By three in the morning I had forty-one photographs arranged in rows on the study floor, ordered by decade, and my knees had filed a formal complaint. I had also made a discovery that I would have given anything, at that hour, to unmake.

The chair was in all of them.

Not most of them. All of them. Every family photograph in the house, going back a hundred and sixty years, contained the same chair — same plain back, same slightly splayed legs, same wood gone grey with weather or grief — positioned somewhere in the frame. And it moved.

In the 1861 image: eleven feet out, edge of frame, easy to dismiss as furniture that happened to be standing there.

In an 1887 studio portrait of Odile's grandparents: six feet, now inside the parlor's visible wall, tucked against the wainscoting like it had been placed for a latecomer.

In a 1923 wedding photo, great-uncle Silas grinning under his bride's veil: four feet, close enough now that its shadow touched the bride's train.

I laid them out and looked down the long corridor of decades and saw it plainly, the way you see a figure in wallpaper once you've seen it once and can never unsee it again. Foot by foot, decade by decade, generation by generation, the chair was walking toward the family.

Toward the center of the family.

Because here was the second thing, the thing that made me sit down hard on the floor among the dead: the chair wasn't approaching *them*, plural. It was approaching a place. In every photograph where the family had arranged itself formally — weddings, christenings, the grim annual Christmas lineup Odile had enforced until 2004 — there was a center position. The place of honor. The spot where the eldest stood, or the matriarch, or —

Or the twins.

Pemberton twins were a tradition, apparently. I hadn't known that. I'd thought Ysolde and I were an accident, a fluke, a genetic coin toss that came up twice. But there they were in the record: the 1861 daughters, Miriam and Agnes. A pair in 1893 — the year, I remembered with a cold slide of the stomach, that Maren and I had found the gap in the county records, the count that simply stopped. Another set in 1931, boys this time, in sailor suits, their smiles identical down to the missing tooth each wore on the same side. And in 1962, a faded Kodachrome of two girls on the front steps, arms slung around each other's necks, laughing at whoever held the camera.

Odile and her sister Vesper. One of whom I'd never heard mentioned. Not once. Not at a single Christmas, not in a single tipsy eulogy, not even by Ambrose, who never met a dead relative he couldn't monetize in anecdote.

In the 1962 photograph, Odile and Vesper stood dead center. And the chair stood directly behind them, close enough that if either girl had leaned back, she'd have felt wood against her shoulder blades.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the fog to go from grey to the greyer shade that means dawn is thinking about it. Long enough that when Odile shuffled in, dressing gown belted like a siege fortification, tea in one trembling hand, she took one look at the floor and said, "Ah. You've found it."

Not *what are you doing*. Not *why are my photographs all over the study*. She'd known. Of course she'd known. She'd probably done this exact exercise herself, kneeling on these same boards, some night fifty years ago.

"You could have told me," I said.

"I told Ysolde." She lowered herself into her armchair with the care of a woman negotiating with her own skeleton. "She didn't listen either. That was the trouble with both of you girls. You get an idea between your teeth and worry it like terriers."

"Ysolde knew about the chair?"

"Ysolde knew about everything. She came home in September full of questions and wouldn't tell me who'd been answering them." Odile sipped her tea. Her hand shook; the surface of the tea trembled like something beneath it was swimming. "Show me what you've got, then. Let's see if you've understood."

I showed her. I walked her down the decades, photograph by photograph, and she watched with the expression of a teacher grading work she'd assigned years ago. When I reached the 1962 picture and asked who Vesper was, she went very still, the way houses go still.

"My sister," she said finally. "My twin."

"What happened to her?"

"She sat down," Odile said. "In 1989. In the parlor. We found her in the morning, in the chair, smiling, quite gone. The doctors called it a stroke. The ledger called it what it was." She set the cup down with a click that sounded obscenely loud. "Go on. You haven't said the worst part yet. Say the worst part."

"The worst part," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "is that it isn't random. The count. Everyone talks about it like weather — names crossed out, seats claimed, bad luck, old bargain, nothing to be done. But it's not weather. Look." I knelt and swept my hand across the rows. "Every time the count has taken someone, in the years right before, the chair in the photographs moves closer to the center. To wherever the twins are posed. It's not collecting Pembertons. It's not even collecting seats. It's converging. Like — "

"Like a search pattern," Odile said quietly.

"Like a seating chart," I said. "It's arranging us. It's been arranging us for a hundred and sixty years, and the seat it wants — the final seat, the one the whole count is walking toward — is the center. Where twins stand."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere upstairs a door settled in its frame with a sound like a sigh.

"And why do you suppose," Odile said, "that Pembertons keep having twins?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because the answer had been sitting in my chest since the daguerreotype, since the two identical girls with their folded hands, and saying it aloud would make it load-bearing.

"It breeds them," I said. "The house does. Or the thing the house keeps out — or keeps in, or whatever the truth is — it needs twins. So it makes sure we keep producing them. Ysolde and I weren't a fluke. We were a crop."

Odile nodded slowly, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before, something worse than fear. Recognition. The look of a woman confirming a suspicion she'd carried so long it had grown into the bone.

"Rowena had twins," she said. "That's where it starts. The founding portrait — the real one, not the lie we hang in the hall — shows her with two daughters. And the bargain, whatever it actually is, whatever the ledger pretends it says — it's always come due in pairs. One twin goes early, softening the ground. And then—" She stopped.

"And then the other one sits," I finished. "Willingly. Out of love. Out of guilt. Out of whatever it can use." I thought of Ysolde at the window at three in the morning, unchanged, beckoning, wearing the face I'd loved before I'd learned to resent it. *Come out here. Come where I am.* "It doesn't want a Pemberton. It wants a twin who'll walk to the chair on her own feet."

"Yes," Odile said. "And do you know the truly elegant part? The part that kept me up nights for thirty years?" She gestured at the photographs, at the long slow march of empty wood through a century and a half of family joy. "It doesn't need to force anyone. It only ever had to wait, and arrange, and let us do what families do — love each other badly, die in the wrong order, leave each other unfinished business. Every death along the way, every name crossed out, was a step closer. Your parents. My Vesper. Poor Verity last week. And now Ysolde." Her voice didn't break. I think it had broken so many times over this exact sentence that it had calloused. "The count was never a tax, Xanthe. It's a courtship. A hundred and sixty years of opening moves."

I looked down at the floor, at forty-one photographs in which my entire family smiled and posed and married and christened while an empty chair crept toward them foot by foot, patient as erosion, and I understood — with the dreadful clarity Odile must have felt kneeling on these same boards half a century ago — that I had never been a guest in this story. I was the destination. I had always been the destination. The house hadn't summoned me home for a funeral.

It had summoned me home for a wedding, and I was the bride, and the chair was the altar, and everyone I loved had been walking me down the aisle one coffin at a time.

"There's one more thing," Odile said. She was looking past me, at the window, where the fog had begun to thin into a light that wasn't entirely natural. "You should hear it before you decide what to do. Because you're going to decide — tonight, I think. It's nearly done arranging."

"Hear what?"

"Why the count stopped in 1893." She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and crossed to the desk. From the locked drawer — the one I'd assumed held silverware and secrets of the ordinary kind — she took a photograph I had not found in any album, any box, any corner of the house. It was small, foxed brown at the edges, and very old.

Two young women stood in the garden, arms linked, squinting into a sun that had set a hundred and thirty years ago. Behind them, mid-frame, unmistakable, stood the chair.

But in this photograph — the only one, the single exception in a hundred and sixty years of images — the chair was not empty.

Someone was sitting in it. Blurred, as if she'd moved during the exposure. As if she'd been trying to get up.

"That's Edith," Odile said. "Edith and her twin, Eleanor. 1893. The year the records stop." She held the photograph out to me, and her hand was steady now, steady as a blade. "Eleanor refused to sit. Refused the count, refused the chair, refused the whole rotten inheritance — and the record stops, Xanthe, because whatever happened that year, the house could not write it down. But Edith sat in that chair long enough to be photographed in it, and Edith is the reason every Pemberton since has been born already promised."

I took the photograph. The blurred figure in the chair seemed to shift as I tilted it, the way faces do in candlelight, and I made myself look at her anyway, because someone in my family had to start looking at things.

"Where is Edith now?" I asked.

"Nowhere," Odile said. "That's rather the point. And Eleanor — " she turned toward the window, toward the greying fog, toward the garden where I had watched my dead sister beckon — "Eleanor walked out of this house in 1893 and was never seen again. No grave. No record. No trace. Which means one of two things, niece. Either she escaped, and lived, and the house has spent a hundred and thirty years making sure no Pemberton ever learns her name —"

"Or," I said, "it caught her."

"Or it caught her," Odile agreed. "And kept her. And she's been waiting in the garden ever since for another twin stubborn enough to follow her out."

Upstairs, faint but distinct, a bell rang. Once. There was no bell in Pemberton House. There had never been a bell in Pemberton House.

Odile went white. "It's starting," she said. "Tonight. God help us — it's starting tonight, and we're not ready."

Somewhere above us, unhurried, something began to drag a chair across the floor.
