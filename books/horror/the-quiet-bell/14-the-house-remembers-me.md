## 14. The House Remembers Me

The morning after Verity died in the parlor chair with her mouth curved like a comma, I woke up in the wrong bed.

Not the wrong bed in the alarming sense — the house was full of alarming beds by then — but the wrong bed in the sense that it was *mine*. My childhood bed. The iron frame with the chipped paint on the left rail where I'd once wedged a screwdriver trying to dismantle it, age eleven, in some doomed bid for independence that lasted exactly one night before the springs bit me and I crawled back to my mother's room. The paint chip was still there. The screwdriver, I realized with a slow crawl of cold up my spine, was on the nightstand. Where I had left it. In 2009.

I hadn't gone to bed in this room. I'd gone to bed in the blue room at the end of the hall, the one I'd claimed on arrival because it had a lock that worked and a window that faced the road, in case I needed to leave in a hurry. In case. As if leaving had been on the table since the moment I'd read my sister's name crossed out in a ledger older than the county.

I lay very still and did the inventory. Iron bed. Quilt my grandmother made — the ugly one, all mustard and rust, the one Ysolde used to say looked like a fire that had given up. My bookshelf: the horse books, the horror paperbacks I'd hidden behind the horse books because Ysolde tattled, the hollowed-out dictionary with my diary in it. My diary. I got up and opened it with hands that didn't want to and found my own handwriting, age thirteen, complaining about a boy named Devin and about Ysolde, always about Ysolde, and the last line of the last entry made me sit down on a bed that had been made for a woman and not a girl: *I wish she would just disappear.*

I closed it. I did not cry, which felt like a moral failure, or a mercy, I couldn't tell which.

The room smelled right. That's the thing I keep circling back to, the thing that undoes me more than the diary, more than the screwdriver. It smelled like dust and cedar and the particular ghost of my childhood shampoo, which has been discontinued for a decade. You can fake a photograph. I'd watched something fake a photograph. But a smell — a smell is a signature, and the house had forged mine perfectly.

*It remembers me,* I thought, and then corrected myself, because remembering wasn't the right verb. Remembering is passive. This was active. This was a house that had gone through my absence like a woman going through a lover's letters, keeping what it liked, arranging what it kept, setting the table.

The house wasn't remembering me. The house was *courting* me.

* * *

I went downstairs with the diary in my waistband like a weapon, which tells you something about how far my standards for weaponry had fallen.

Odile was in the kitchen, propped in her chair by the stove, and she looked at my face and then at the shape of the book against my ribs and said, "It's started with you, then."

"The room," I said. "My room. It's — it put everything back. Things I threw away. Things that were *donated*, Odile, there's a girl in Ohio wearing my horse sweatshirt right now and it's *here too*—"

"Hush." She poured tea she had no business pouring, her hands shaking the way they'd shaken since Verity, only worse, and pushed the cup at me. "Sit. And don't drink it if you don't want to. I only need something to do with my hands or I'll start wringing them, and I refuse to die looking like a caricature of myself."

"You're not dying."

"Everyone in this house is dying, child. Some of us are just doing it on schedule." She wrapped her hands around her own cup. "The house does this. When it wants someone. My mother told me — she said when it chose her sister, the house gave her back her wedding china, every piece, even the ones broken at the reception. Set it all out on the sideboard like a proposal."

"And did she take it?"

Odile's mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely a wound. "She took it. That's the trouble with being loved by a thing like this. It knows what you miss. It gives it back to you one piece at a time, and by the time you understand what the pieces cost, you're already setting the table yourself."

I thought of the diary. *I wish she would just disappear.* The house had read it. The house was showing me it had read it. Look, it said. I know your worst sentence. I kept it warm for you.

"Odile," I said, "what does it want from me? Specifically. Not a Pemberton. Me."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide, in real time, how much truth I could carry. She'd been making that calculation my whole life, I realized. Every Christmas, every funeral, every family dinner. Weighing truth like sugar rationed through a war.

"You're the last twin," she said finally. "And the house has always been partial to twins. Ask me why and I'll tell you I don't know, and I'll be lying, because I think I've always known, and I'm not ready to say it out loud. Not yet." She drank. "Until I am — stay out of the rooms that feel like gifts."

"That's most of them."

"Then stay out of all of them." She set the cup down. "And Xanthe. If you find a door open that you're certain you locked — don't go through it to close it. That's how it walks you. One courtesy at a time."

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of whoever finds this account, that I was not stupid. I want that on the record because what follows is going to read like the diary of a woman walking willingly into an oven, and the truth is more humiliating than that. The truth is that the house was patient and I was lonely and grief is a door that opens from the inside.

That first day, the small things. My coat — my actual coat, the one I'd left at a bus station in Leeds six years ago — hanging on the peg by the kitchen door. I stood in front of it for a full minute, telling myself it was a similar coat, and then I put my hand in the pocket and found the ticket stub from a film Ysolde and I had seen the last winter we were still speaking. I put the coat on. I want to be honest about that too. It fit like forgiveness.

The second day, the doors.

You have to understand that I had been counting doors since I was seven years old. It was our game, mine and Ysolde's, invented after the night we don't discuss — the ritual that kept us sane, or seemed to. Lights on. Doors counted. Ten doors in Pemberton House, always ten, and if you counted ten, the night was survivable. When I'd arrived for the funeral I'd counted eleven, and I'd told myself it was grief arithmetic, a brain doing sums with wet chalk.

Now the doors had opinions.

I would go up to bed — the blue room, the room with the lock, the room I defended like a border — and the hallway would be a hallway, and I would pass the bathroom door and the linen closet and the door to the attic stairs and count my way along like a rosary, and I would arrive at the blue room. But twice — twice — I arrived somewhere else. I would count the same doors, pass the same linen closet with its same swollen hinge, and open the door at the end of the hall and be standing in the doorway of my childhood room, the iron bed turned down, the lamp lit, my horse books squared on the shelf.

Both times I walked back down the hall and the hallway was longer than it should have been and the doors were in different places and I counted eleven, twelve, thirteen, and then, mercifully, ten again, and the blue room was where I'd left it, sulking.

The second time, I stood in my childhood doorway and said, out loud, to the hallway, to the wallpaper, to whatever was listening with the whole grain of the wood: "No."

The lamp on the nightstand turned itself off. Politely. Like a waiter acknowledging a refusal of dessert.

I have been threatened by many things in my life — deadlines, my aunt Serafina's opinions, a dog in Marseille — but I had never been *polited* at by architecture, and I went back to the blue room and did not sleep, and somewhere around four in the morning I caught myself thinking: *but it did look nice, the bed turned down like that,* and I understood that I was already halfway gone, that the house didn't need to drag me anywhere, it only needed to wait for me to miss something badly enough.

* * *

On the third day it gave me Ysolde's laugh.

Not her actual laugh — I need to be precise, because precision was becoming the only raft I had. It gave me the sound of her laugh, from the garden, at an angle, the way sound carries when someone is around the side of the house and you're in the kitchen. A laugh with no body attached, no person attached, just the acoustic fact of joy, and my body responded before my mind could veto it. I was through the kitchen door and halfway across the yard before I caught myself, barefoot in wet grass, in November, standing in the dead garden where I had seen her — or the thing with her face — at three in the morning a week ago.

There was no one there. There was the birdbath with the cracked basin. There was the rosebush that had been dying since the nineties. There was fog, doing its fog business.

And there, on the flagstone path, leading from the back door to the birdbath, were footprints. Bare feet. My size. My own, I thought at first — I'd been walking the garden in my sleep, that was the sane explanation, I'd been sleepwalking since I was nine, there was a whole humiliating family story about it.

But I was looking down at my own feet, and they were muddy, and the footprints on the path were not.

The footprints on the path were small. Child-sized. And they were spaced like someone walking *toward* the house, unhurried, and they ended at the back door, and the back door was open, and I had locked it.

I stood in the garden until my feet went numb, and then I went inside and locked the door behind me and put my back against it, and the house — I swear this, I swear it on whatever is left to swear on — the house *hummed*. Low, in the floorboards, under the soles of my bare feet. Not menace. Contentment. The sound a cat makes with its whole body when the hand it wants finally lands between its ears.

"Stop it," I said to the kitchen. To the coat on the peg. To the diary-shaped weight against my ribs. "I'm not her. I'm not the one you want me to be. Whatever you think I am, you've got it wrong."

The humming didn't stop. But it changed key, and I am a novelist, and I know a motif when I hear one, and the new key was the key of patience.

* * *

Maren found me in the library that afternoon. She'd taken to staying near me since Ambrose — since his line burned out of the ledger mid-word and his children had looked at me like I'd lit the match. Maren hadn't looked at me like that. Maren had looked at me like I was the only other person in the house doing arithmetic instead of praying, and she came in now with her laptop under her arm and her eyes red-rimmed and said, "The county archive opens at nine. I called. There's a records room. And Xanthe—" she stopped, and I watched her decide whether to say the next thing, and she said it. "Your room. My room did it too. This morning. My childhood room, my things, my — my dog's collar. Biscuit died when I was twelve. The collar was on my pillow."

I looked at her. She looked at me.

"It's not just me," I said.

"It's everyone. Theo found his old skateboard. Serafina found letters from her first husband, the one before the one she pretends was her first. It's giving all of us back—" her voice cracked, and she pressed on, furious at the crack, "—all of us back the exact thing we can't throw away twice. That's not random. That's *research*. That's a thing that studied us."

"Or a thing that's always been watching us," I said. "Since we were children. Since we were — " I stopped, because the sentence I'd been about to say was *since we were in the womb*, and I didn't know why I was about to say it, and sentences I don't know why I'm about to say have not been safe sentences in this house.

Maren sat down across from me and opened her laptop. "1893," she said. "Tell me about 1893."

* * *

We worked until the light went, and I'll set down what we found in the next chapter, because it deserves its own telling and because I need to tell you what happened after the light went first, while my hands are still steady enough to type it.

The light went. Maren went up to the blue room — she'd been sleeping in the room next to mine, a mutual defense pact neither of us named — and I stayed a moment to shelve the county ledgers, because even now, even with everything, I cannot leave a book on the floor. It's a character flaw. It may be the death of me.

The library lamp dimmed. Not off — dimmed, to the amber of a photograph, and the house went quiet in a way houses don't. Not silent. *Attentive.* And the hallway door, which I had closed, which I had *latched*, stood open, and beyond it the hall was lit all the way to its end, warm as a runway, and at the end of the runway was the door to my childhood room, standing open, and from inside it came a sound.

A laugh. Ysolde's laugh. Close this time. In the room this time. And under it, almost too low to catch, a voice — her voice, the voice I'd heard through the mirror the night before last, the voice that had said *come sit with me* — and it was saying something new.

It was saying my name.

Not the way the housekeeper had said it. Not the way Odile said it, or Maren, or the aunts. It said *Xanthe* the way you say the name of someone you've been calling for across a crowded room for years, someone who keeps not hearing you, someone you are about to give up on or never give up on, and both of those at once, and I stood in the library with my hand on the lamp and I knew — I knew the way you know a stair isn't there in the dark — that if I walked down that hallway, I would not be walking back as only one person.

"Tomorrow," I said to the open door. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to. "Whatever you are. Tomorrow. I have work to do first."

The hallway light held for a long moment. Then, one lamp at a time, like a breath released, it went out — all the way to my childhood door, which closed itself, softly, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

I slept in the library, in the chair, with the lights on and the diary under my hand like a talisman I didn't trust. And just before dawn, half-dreaming, I heard it one more time, not from the hallway but from everywhere, from the walls themselves, patient as mortar:

*Tomorrow, then.*

I have one day. And tomorrow, the mirror in the upstairs bath is going to have something to say to me. I can feel it waiting behind the silver the way the house waits behind its wallpaper.

Tomorrow I find out what my sister — what the thing wearing my sister — has been trying to buy.
