## 16. Maren's Research

My father burned to ash in the middle of a sentence.

That's the part nobody wants to talk about at the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, the part my aunt Cordelia keeps trying to smooth over with casseroles and the phrase "he went quickly," as if "quickly" were a comfort and not the whole horror of it. He didn't go quickly. He went *incompletely*. His ledger line — I saw it afterward, when Odile let me look — ended mid-word. *Ambrose Pemberton, who kept the count fed by* — and then nothing. Scorched paper. A sentence with no period, which is worse than a sentence with one, because a period at least means somebody finished thinking you.

I am his daughter, which means I inherited his arithmetic. I know exactly how many substitutes he must have fed into that house over forty years, because I helped him keep the books for the last six. He told me they were "property expenses." Drifters he hired to do repairs that never needed doing. Servants whose references he never checked on purpose. I balanced those entries every quarter and felt *competent* about it.

So yes. Grief and guilt. They travel together in my family like twins.

Which is why, three days after we put what was left of Ambrose in the ground — a small service, half the chairs empty, and I flinched at every one of them — I showed up at Xanthe's motel room with two boxes of county records and a thermos of coffee strong enough to strip varnish.

She opened the door looking like a woman who had been arguing with someone invisible and losing.

"Let me guess," she said. "You're here to blame me again."

"Everyone else already did that at the reception. I'm here to do something useful instead. It'll feel strange. Breathe through it."

* * *

Here is what I know about Xanthe Pemberton, twin of the dead, the woman half my family wants to burn at the stake: she counts things when she's scared. Doors, mostly. I watched her do it in the parlor the night after Verity died — lips moving, eyes ticking around the room like a metronome nobody could hear. The family used to call it a nervous habit. Now I understand it was armor. She and Ysolde invented those rituals as children, after whatever happened the night no one discusses, and every door counted correctly was a small vote in favor of the world continuing to exist.

The house has been voting against her ever since. Her childhood room restored itself overnight, down to the horse posters and the chipped moon-and-stars nightlight. Doors have started opening onto rooms they shouldn't. Aunt Cordelia swears the upstairs hallway now contains a window where there has never been a window, and through it you can see the garden at dusk even at noon.

So we did not work in the house. We worked in my car, parked at the far end of the motel lot, boxes of records between us, engine running so the heater worked and so — though neither of us said this aloud — so we could leave quickly if either of us changed her mind about wanting to know.

"County archive," I said, thumping the first box onto the seat between us. "Everything from 1840 forward. Deeds, probate filings, coroner's reports, census fragments. I pulled it all under the pretext of settling Dad's estate. Which, technically, is true."

"And the ledger?"

"Photocopied before Odile locked it back up. Every count she knows about, going back to Rowena." I handed Xanthe the stack. "I made you a copy too. I figured you'd want your own."

She took it the way you take news of a second diagnosis. Then she opened it, and for a long time the only sound was the heater and the tick of her thumbnail against the page edge, counting something only she could see.

* * *

The ledger reads like a butcher's inventory written by a poet with a grudge. Names in faded iron-gall ink, dates beside them, and — this is the detail that got under my skin and stayed there — little annotations in different hands across the generations. *Taken in her sleep, gentle.* *Fought. Left marks.* *Willing.* That last one appears more often than you'd think, and always in the same careful script, and I hate whoever wrote it.

Xanthe read in silence for twenty minutes. I drank coffee and watched the fog eat the far end of the parking lot. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone flat in the way voices go flat right before they crack.

"Maren. Look at the endings."

"I've looked."

"Every count ends with a twin." She turned the pages toward me, tapping each final entry. "1861. Elias and Edmund Pemberton. One crossed out, then the other, eleven days apart. 1879, the Halloway cousins — twins by marriage, but twins. 1907. 1934. 1958." Her finger stopped. "And our parents, 1999. Mom and Dad weren't twins, but the count took them together, same night, and everyone said it was the road conditions."

"The road conditions," I repeated. My father's phrase. He'd said it at every anniversary for years, with the conviction of a man reciting scripture he'd personally edited.

"Twins," she said again. "Or pairs. Always pairs, always at the end, always the last seats filled. And then—" she flipped to the last pages, the ones in Odile's hand, "—then Ysolde. And an empty chair walking toward the center of every photograph, where twins get posed."

I had done the math before I drove over, obviously. I'd spent two sleepless nights doing it. But hearing it out loud in daylight, in a Kia, with the radio murmuring some station neither of us had chosen, made it real in a way the ledger alone hadn't. Real, and personal. Because I'd looked at the seating chart of that house my whole childhood — the formal portraits in the front hall, arranged by generation, twins dead center — and I'd never once wondered why.

"It's not random," Xanthe said. "It was never random. It's been shopping for a specific thing for a hundred and sixty years, and it thinks it found it."

"You."

"Us." Her jaw tightened. "Ysolde first. Me for dessert."

* * *

But here is where the story bends, and where I earned my keep, because grief had made me obsessive in a productive way for once in my life.

"Keep going," I said. "Past 1958. There's a gap."

She frowned and paged forward. Between the count of 1958 and the next recorded one in 1971, the ledger's handwriting changes — older, spikier, pressed so hard into the paper it nearly tore. And the entry isn't a completion. It's a fragment.

Xanthe read it aloud, slowly, like translating: "*1893. The year the count broke. Great-grandmother's cousin —*"

"No," I said. "Read the date again. It's misfiled. Odile inserted it out of order — see the different ink? It belongs earlier. 1893."

"*1893,*" she agreed, and kept reading. "*— refused the chair. Rowena's own line, third generation. The record names her only as 'the sister who would not sit.' She was offered the center seat. She declined it. Named herself heir to nothing. The count—*" Xanthe stopped.

"The record simply stops," I finished. "Mid-sentence. Same as Dad's line. Except—" I pulled out the photocopy of the original ledger page I'd taken with Odile's grudging permission, the physical artifact, and laid it flat between us. "Except look at the paper."

She looked. So will you, if you're ever unlucky enough to see it: the entry ends mid-word, and the paper around it isn't torn or water-damaged. It's *scorched*, a perfect brown-edged burn that stops exactly where the sentence stops, as if the words themselves caught fire and the fire had manners enough not to spread.

"Something ended that entry," Xanthe said quietly. "Just like it ended Dad's."

"Yes. But here's the difference." I tapped the margin, where a later hand — Odile's mother's, maybe, or Odile's — had added a single line in pencil, faint as breath: *"One survived. The house did not forgive. Ask what became of her."*

We sat with that for a while. The fog crept closer to the car. Somewhere across the lot, a maid's cart rattled past, and I caught myself counting the squeaks of its wheel — seven, eight, nine — and made myself stop.

"So refusal works," Xanthe said finally. "Once. In 1893. One woman out of a hundred and sixty years of Pembertons looked at the chair and said no, and lived."

"One woman we know of. Whose name the record refuses to give. Whose fate the family penciled a warning about instead of writing down." I turned to face her fully. "Xanthe. I'm not offering you hope. I'm offering you a precedent with teeth. Refusal has a cost, and nobody in four generations wrote down what it was, which means it was bad, which means—"

"Which means it might still be better than sitting." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "That's the trade, isn't it? That's always the trade."

* * *

We spent the afternoon pulling the 1893 thread until our fingers bled — figuratively, mostly. Census fragments. A parish register. A newspaper, the *Carrow County Sentinel*, dated November 1893, with a piece on page four I found by lantern-light in my car while Xanthe slept badly against the window.

The article is short. Headline: **PEMBERTON HEIRESS RENOUNCES ESTATE; FAMILY "GRIEVED BUT LIVING."** It names her. Finally, a name, printed in ordinary newsprint where no curse thought to reach: *Miss Wren Pemberton, aged nineteen.*

Nineteen. A girl barely older than Ysolde had been.

The article says she stood up at a gathering of the family — the reporter uses the word "assembly," oddly formal, oddly wrong, like the reporter couldn't quite remember what kind of gathering it had been — and declared before witnesses that she renounced all claim to Pemberton House, its lands, and "every seat therein." The house, the article notes dryly, was thereafter sold to a distant branch and stood vacant for eleven years. Miss Wren Pemberton, the article concludes, "removed to the coast, where she lives quietly."

Where she lives quietly. Present tense, November 1893.

I woke Xanthe to show her. She read it twice. Then she did something I didn't expect: she laughed. One short, cracked, terrible laugh, there in the dark with the fog pressing on the glass.

"'Removed to the coast,'" she quoted. "'Where she lives quietly.' You know what that means?"

"That she got out."

"That she got out *and nobody ever checked.* No follow-up. No obituary. No marriage notice, no death record, nothing in any archive I can find. A woman who beat the count just — evaporates from the historical record the moment she walks away from that house." Xanthe's eyes were bright and wet and furious. "Either she lived happily ever after somewhere no one thought to look. Or the cost of refusal is that the world forgets you were ever in it. And I genuinely don't know which option is worse."

"Third option," I said. "Both."

* * *

It was nearly midnight when we packed the boxes back into my trunk, and I want to be honest about what happened then, because it matters.

The motel lot was empty except for us. The fog had come all the way in, thick as wool, and the security light above the office door made a soft orange island maybe thirty feet away. Xanthe walked ahead of me toward her room, arms full of ledger copies, and I lagged behind locking the trunk, and that's why I saw it and she didn't.

At the edge of the light, maybe forty feet off, there was a chair.

A plain wooden chair, the kind that comes with a kitchen set, standing alone on the asphalt. Facing us. And I want to be very precise here, because precision is the only inheritance my father left me that I intend to keep: the chair was not *placed* there. Chairs that are placed sit with their weight settled. This chair was *waiting*, the way a dog waits, weight forward, attention fixed.

"Xanthe," I said. Very calmly. My father's voice came out of my mouth, actually — his calm-at-the-table voice, the one he used when the property expenses needed explaining — and I hated it and used it anyway. "Don't run. Walk to the door. Count your steps if it helps."

She counted. I heard her — *one, two, three* — low and steady, and she reached her door, got it open, got inside. I followed. From the window, through the gap in the curtain, we both looked back at the lot.

The chair was gone. In its place, printed in the thin frost that had formed on the asphalt overnight — frost, in September, in a county where it doesn't frost until November — was a single line of small, neat footprints. Child-sized. Barefoot. Walking away from where the chair had been.

Toward the highway. Toward the coast.

"She's telling us where she went," Xanthe whispered. "Or it is."

* * *

We leave tomorrow. Both of us. Odile gave Xanthe the iron key tonight — I was there, in the sickroom that smells of camphor and old smoke, and I heard the confession that came with it, and I will carry that confession to my grave the way my father carried his ledgers: carefully, and at a cost. The nursery. Rowena's true portrait. Whatever Ysolde's shape is waiting beside.

Xanthe means to walk in there and refuse. Renounce. Say the words Wren Pemberton said in 1893 and take whatever forgetting comes with them.

I told her about the scorched paper. I told her about the pencil warning in the margin. I told her everything I found, because she deserves the truth and because guilt is a debt and this is how I pay mine.

She thanked me. That was the worst part.

Tonight, packing the car by the orange island of the security light, I checked the lot one last time. Empty. Clean. No chair, no frost, no prints.

Then, just as I turned to go inside, the security light flickered — once, twice — and in the dark between flickers I heard, very faintly, from the direction of the highway, the sound of a wooden chair dragging itself along the asphalt.

Coming with us, I think.

Or going ahead to save us a seat.
