## 3. Questions No One Asked

Ansel bought the recorder on a Tuesday, from the electronics shop in Ashford that still smelled of solder and dust, and he told himself it was for the chart. That was the phrase he used, even in his own head — *for the chart* — as though documentation could make a thing clean. Mrs. Ellery Vane's sleep study had been ordered weeks ago; Dr. Prasad wanted apnea data, oxygen desaturation curves, the ordinary machinery of a body winding down. The recorder was simply a better instrument than the ward's aging monitoring station. That was all.

It was not all, and he knew it, and he carried the small black device in his coat pocket for two days before he dared set it on her nightstand, its single red eye watching the dark the way he could not.

Hollowmere House at three in the morning was a held breath. The corridors ran long and narrow, wainscoted in oak gone amber with a century of lamp-oil and hands, and the radiators ticked like patient clocks. Ansel made his rounds by memory more than sight. He knew which floorboards announced him and which kept his secrets, and he had learned — though he would never have written this in any chart — to pause outside Room 9 and listen.

Mrs. Vane was counting.

*Forty-one. Forty. Thirty-nine.*

Her voice came thin and level through the door, the voice of a woman reciting a grocery list, not a woman dying of the thing blooming quietly in her lungs. She was ninety-three and had arrived at Hollowmere six weeks ago with a suitcase, a photograph of a man dead fifty years, and a countdown already in progress. Twenty-two, the intake nurse had written in the margins of her admission note, underlined twice, and no one had asked her about it, because at Hollowmere no one asked about the counting. It was simply what the dying did here. They counted backward, always backward, never up, and the numbers fell away like leaves and no one raked them.

*Thirty-eight. Thirty-seven.*

Ansel stood in the corridor with his hand flat against the door and felt the old familiar pressure behind his sternum, the one he had learned to call indigestion, or fatigue, or anything other than what it was. His mother had counted. Eleven days in the back bedroom of the house on Corben Street, down from some number he had never heard her begin, and he had been nineteen and furious and had told himself it was the morphine, the tumor pressing where it shouldn't, the brain doing what brains do when they come apart. He had built his whole profession on that explanation, brick by careful brick, and he was not going to let one old woman's arithmetic pull it down.

He went back to the nurses' station and turned on the recorder.

* * *

The first night's audio was eleven hours of breathing, and he listened to all of it, headphones on, at the desk in the records room where the furnace made the radiator pipes sing. Mrs. Vane's breath was wet paper being torn, slow and patient. At 2:14 a.m. her counting began — he could hear it now as a murmur under the breath, a second rhythm laid over the first like a watermark.

At 3:41 a.m., something else began.

It was beneath her. That was the only word his mind would give him, *beneath*, as though the voice came up through the floor, through the foundations, through whatever the house stood on before it was a house. It was low and unhurried and it did not breathe the way people breathe. It spoke between her breaths, fitting itself into the gaps the way water fits into cracks, and Ansel sat very still in the records room with his hand pressed over his mouth.

*"What is your name,"* it said.

And Mrs. Vane, asleep, dreaming or not dreaming, answered: *"Ellery. Ellery Vane."*

*"What do you owe?"*

*"I don't — I never meant —"* Her breath caught, tore, resumed. *"The ring. I kept the ring. I was supposed to give it back and I kept it."*

*"What did you keep it for?"*

*"For after. I thought there would be an after."*

Silence then, long enough that Ansel checked the waveform to be sure the recording hadn't skipped. It hadn't. Her breathing continued, and the counting continued underneath it — *thirty-one, thirty* — and then the voice again, softer, almost tender, the tenderness of a hand closing around a bird.

*"And what is behind your door, Ellery Vane?"*

*"Nothing,"* she whispered. *"Nothing. Please. Nothing."*

The recording ran on until dawn. Ansel played the passage four times, then five, hunting for the rational explanation the way a man hunts for his glasses when they're on his head. Cross-channel bleed. The ward's monitoring station picked up audio from six rooms; a voice from another resident could have bled into Mrs. Vane's feed. Except the monitoring station didn't record audio at all — he had checked, twice, that afternoon, standing in the equipment closet reading the model plate like scripture. And except that no other resident on the ward spoke in that voice. No *person* on the ward spoke in that voice.

He wrote in her chart: *Nocturnal vocalizations consistent with terminal delirium. Audio monitoring continued.* Clinical words, clean words, and his hand shook so badly the pen tore the paper.

* * *

He recorded deliberately after that. He told himself it was diligence, thoroughness, the habit of a good nurse, and he set the little black device beside three beds instead of one — Mrs. Vane in Room 9, Mr. Okafor in Room 4, sweet Mrs. Tull in Room 12, who was down to nineteen and counted in her sleep like a child reciting subtraction.

The voice came to all of them.

It came differently to each. To Mr. Okafor, who had been a customs officer for forty years, it asked about manifests, about the crate he had waved through in 1987 without opening, about what had been inside. To Mrs. Tull it asked about a door in her childhood house, a door her mother had told her never to open, and Mrs. Tull wept in her sleep and said, *I opened it, I opened it when I was seven, and there was nothing, there was nothing, why did they say never* — and the voice said, *because we said it,* and Mrs. Tull went quiet as a held note.

Names. Debts. Doors. Ansel catalogued them in a notebook he kept in his locker, in handwriting smaller than he'd written since university. Every resident, when the voice asked *what is your name*, gave it. Gave it freely, sleep-soft, the way you give your name to a doctor, to a priest, to anyone whose hands are on you. And after each name the voice would pause — Ansel timed it, four to six seconds, a gap with a texture he could feel through the headphones, like a tongue pressed to the roof of a mouth — and then it would say, in a tone he could only call *satisfied*:

*"Received."*

No. Not after each name. He corrected himself, sitting in the records room at midnight with his heart going like a trapped moth. It said *received* after each *debt*. The names it took somewhere else, or kept, or turned over the way a man turns a coin. But the debts — the ring, the crate, the opened door — those it acknowledged, formally, the way a bank acknowledges a payment.

*Received.*

He was still telling himself *hypnagogic confabulation, auditory pareidolia, the mind's hunger for pattern in static* when Mrs. Vane reached eleven.

* * *

She reached eleven on a Thursday, and Ansel knew it the way you know weather coming, though he could not have said how. The house felt thinner. The radiators ticked out of rhythm. In the day room, the residents who were not dying — the ones who had years yet, the ones who were only old — sat closer together than usual and did not know why.

At 11:52 that night, Mrs. Vane's count came up through the floor of the nurses' station. Ansel heard it through the headphones and, a half-second later, heard it again through the wall, unamplified, and understood with a lurch that her voice had grown loud enough to carry. *Nine. Eight.* The words had a strange doubled quality, as though two women were counting in unison and one of them was slightly ahead.

He was in Room 9 before he decided to be. Mrs. Vane lay small beneath the quilt, her eyes open and fixed on the ceiling, her lips moving. Her eyes were awake. That was the thing that undid him — she was awake, and she was still counting, and when he took her hand her fingers closed on his with terrible strength.

"Mrs. Vane," he said. "Ellery. You don't have to do this. You can stop. You can just — stop."

Her eyes came down to him and for a moment she was entirely present, a woman of ninety-three looking at a young man holding her hand in the dark, and she smiled.

"They count *for* us, dear," she said. "We just say it out loud. It's polite." Her smile flickered. "It's polite to answer when you're called."

"Called by who?"

*Seven. Six.*

"You'll find out," she said, not unkindly, "when it's your turn. Everyone has a number. I thought mine was twenty-two but it wasn't, it was never twenty-two, the twenty-two was just — the *door* —" Her breath tore. *Five.* "Nurse. Nurse, listen to me. When it asks you your name—"

*Four.*

"—don't be polite."

*Three. Two.*

"Ellery—" He was gripping her hand now, both hands, absurdly, as though he could hold her above the numbers. "Ellery, stay with me, look at me, you don't have to—"

*One.*

She looked at him. Her mouth opened.

*Zero.*

She did not say it. He would swear to his grave she did not say it — her lips shaped the word but no breath came, and her eyes stayed open and fixed on his, and they were not empty, they were *waiting*, and in the silence after the zero, in the gap where a breath should have been, the voice spoke.

It did not come through the headphones. It did not come through the recorder on the nightstand. It came from everywhere the room was not — from under the floor, from inside the walls, from the space behind his own ears — and it said, with the mild satisfaction of a clerk stamping a form:

*"Received."*

The heart monitor drew its long flat line. Ansel stood in the blue dark holding a dead woman's hand and did not move for a long time, and when he finally looked down at the recorder, its red eye was still lit, still watching, and he had the sudden, unshakeable certainty that it had been watching *him*.

* * *

He did not sleep. At four in the morning, with Mrs. Vane's body gone to the care of the night aides and the ward quiet in the way a mouth is quiet after a word, he went to the records room to put the night's audio with the rest — and found himself, instead, pulling the founder's files. He had no better reason than the hour. The hour made a kind of permission.

Hollowmere House had been built in 1898 by four men: Aldous Hollowmere, the Vane money, the Tull shipping interests, and a man named Corben whose name survived only in the street Ansel's mother had died on. The archive was thin — incorporation papers, land deeds, a photograph of four bearded men standing on the cellar steps with the particular stiffness of men posing above something. Ansel looked at the photograph for a long time. The four founders were smiling. It was the smiles that were wrong: too wide, too even, four identical smiles as though one smile had been divided among them.

The ledger was behind the deed box, wrapped in oilcloth, and it had no business being there — a private book in a public archive, hidden rather than lost. The leather was cracked and the ink had gone the brown of old blood, and the hand was Aldous Hollowmere's, precise and confident, an accountant's hand.

Ansel read standing up, because sitting felt too much like settling in.

It was a ledger in the true sense. *Received* and *paid*, dated across thirty years, and the entries were not sums of money. *Received: the name of Thomas Corben, given freely. Paid: prosperity of the shipping concern, threefold.* *Received: the debt of M. Tull, acknowledged. Paid: recovery of the Tull fleet, 1904.* Page after page, decades of it, names and debts flowing one direction and fortune flowing the other, and the house above it all growing wings and wings of rooms for the dying.

And then, near the end, in a shakier hand, a final entry — not a transaction but a plan:

*The rite below cannot be completed, for completion closes the account, and a closed account may be *forgiven*, and a forgiven account pays no more. It must therefore be left open. Deliberately. Forever. The house will feed it, and it will feed the house, and our heirs need only keep the door and never, never finish what we began. The final line of the circle is to remain blank. Blank, and the debt runs eternal.*

Below that, in the same shaking hand, one more line, pressed so hard the pen had torn the page:

*God forgive us. We did not understand what "received" meant until Thomas went down and did not come back up.*

Ansel stood in the records room with the ledger open in his hands and the furnace ticking and the house breathing around him, and every rational structure he had built over nineteen years and a dead mother's counting came down at once, quietly, the way a floor comes down — all at once, and from beneath.

The cellar door was at the end of the east corridor. He had walked past it a hundred times. It was always locked; the key had been lost, the staff said, before any of them were born.

As he stood there, ledger in hand, he heard — faint, patient, from below — the sound of a lock turning.

And then footsteps in the corridor behind him, quick and unfamiliar, and a woman's voice calling out: "Hello? Is anyone awake in this mausoleum?"
