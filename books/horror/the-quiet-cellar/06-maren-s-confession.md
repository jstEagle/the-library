## 6. Maren's Confession

Maren Voss had signed away buildings before. Warehouses in Rotterdam, a shuttered mill outside Ghent, her father's share of a shipping concern she'd never fully understood. Paper was paper. Ink dried, obligations transferred, and the past became someone else's problem to inventory.

Hollowmere House was not behaving like paper.

She sat in the small office off the east corridor — the administrator had called it the family room, with a smile that suggested the name was inherited rather than chosen — and the dissolution documents lay before her in a neat stack, tabbed and flagged by lawyers who had never set foot inside the building they were unmaking. She had read every clause twice. She understood all of it. And still her pen hovered above the signature line like a bird that knew something about the ground below.

The house was listening. That was the only way she could describe it to herself, alone, at eleven at night, with the radiators ticking and the corridors gone quiet. Not menacing, exactly. Attentive. The way an old dog lifts its head when it hears its owner's car on the gravel. Hollowmere had been waiting for her since she walked through the door, and now that the papers were on the table, its attention had sharpened into something she could feel against her skin, faint as static before lightning.

She had come here to bury her family's shame efficiently. Sell the property, dissolve the trust, donate whatever equipment was worth donating, and never think of the place again except as a line item in her grandmother's estate. Four days ago that had seemed not only possible but easy.

Then the nurse had started asking about the cellar.

Ansel Dravenmoor. She turned his name over in her mind the way she'd turn a strange coin found in an old coat pocket. He was younger than she'd expected for a night-shift hospice nurse — thirty-something, careful, with the particular stillness of people who spend their working hours near death and have made peace with it, or believe they have. He asked questions the way auditors ask them: flatly, without drama, as though the answers were owed to him. What was under the house. Why the foundation plans showed a room that no longer appeared on any survey. Whether her family had ever kept records from the founding years.

And when she'd told him — too sharply, she'd admit that — to stay out of the cellar, he hadn't argued. He'd just looked at her with those level gray eyes and said, *You're afraid of it. But you don't know why.*

Which was true, and which was unbearable.

Maren stood, unable to sit with the documents any longer, and went to the window. The grounds were dark, the old yew hedge a black wall against a sky the color of wet slate. Somewhere below her feet — two floors down, beneath the boiler room, beneath the oldest concrete — there was a room her great-great-grandfather had ordered built and then ordered sealed, and no one in her family had ever explained why, because no one in her family had ever known why. They had only ever known that it must not be opened, and that the hospice above it must never close while anyone could prevent it.

That was the inheritance, really. Not the money. The *must*.

A knock at the door, soft, and she knew before she answered who it would be. Only one person in this house knocked as though he didn't want to disturb the dying.

"Come in."

Ansel entered carrying a folder — a thin one, worn at the corners, nothing like the fat legal packets her lawyers produced. He closed the door behind him with the same care he used for everything, and Maren realized she had already decided to tell him. It had happened somewhere between the knock and the look on his face: tired, human, done pretending.

"You found something," she said.

"I found something." He set the folder on the desk beside her unsigned papers, and for a moment the two stacks sat together — the ledger pages he'd photographed, and the instruments of the house's death — like two languages describing the same catastrophe. "But I think you already know most of it. I think your family has always known most of it. They just never wrote it down where it could be read plainly."

* * *

Her grandmother's letters were in the hotel, in the false bottom of a steamer trunk that Maren had almost left in storage, and she drove back through the rain to get them because Ansel had shown her the ledger and she could no longer pretend the trunk held only linens and her grandmother's good china patterns.

Edith Voss, née Calloway, had written letters the way other women kept diaries — compulsively, privately, addressed to a sister who died young and who therefore never answered, which meant Edith could confess anything. Maren had read them as a teenager and found them dull: weather, servants, complaints about her husband's digestion. She had been looking for a woman then. She hadn't known to look for a witness.

Now, at midnight, in the hotel room with Ansel sitting across from her at the little table because she could not bear to open the packet alone, Maren untied the ribbon and let the letters breathe their camphor-and-paper smell into the room.

"This one," she said. Her voice came out strange. "This is the one that made me keep the trunk."

She read aloud, because reading silently felt like a kind of cowardice, and because she wanted him to hear it in Edith's own words:

*"My dearest Lottie — Father held the meeting again last night, all seven of them, in the blue parlor until three in the morning. I listened at the door as I have since I was a girl, and this time I heard the whole of it. The bargain is not what the town believes. There is no devil in it, no demon out of a sermon. It is older and more patient than anything with horns. Father calls it the Tenant. They built the house ON it, Lottie. Not over it — on it, the way you build a house on a well, drawing up what rises. Prosperity, long life, good fortune in trade. All of it drawn up from below, and all of it paid for."*

Maren stopped. Her throat had closed. Ansel said nothing; he had learned, in four days, when silence was the more generous act.

"Paid how?" he asked finally, though she could see in his face that he had already read the answer in the ledger.

"In names." She swallowed. "In the named dead. Read on, or I'll lose my nerve."

*"The Tenant eats names, not flesh. When a person dies inside these walls, their name goes down before their soul goes anywhere else, and the Tenant grows fat and quiet, and the town prospers another year. That is what Hollowmere is FOR. Father says the dying give freely — that at the end, everyone is willing to put down what they carry. But I watched Mrs. Aldous die in March and she did not go willingly, she went counting, backwards, trying to hold on to her numbers the way you'd hold coins, and the house took her anyway."*

"The counting," Ansel said quietly. "It's in the letters."

"It's everywhere once you know to look." Maren drew out the next letter, the one with the water stain shaped like a reaching hand. "Here. This is the part I've thought about every day since I arrived."

*"There was a rite. Seven founders, seven lines of it, written into the cellar floor the week the house was finished. Six lines bind the Tenant to its room. The seventh line was left blank — deliberately, Father says, so the debt could never be closed and the Tenant could never be refused its feeding. But Aunt Prudence, who was there when they cut the stone, told me different before she died. She said the seventh line was left blank because it COULD NOT BE WRITTEN IN ADVANCE. The binding is not complete. It waits. Someone must finish it — stand in the circle, speak the final line, and give the Tenant the one thing it cannot take: a true name, given freely, as the seal. Not taken. Given. That is the whole trick of it, Lottie. Everything the house has ever fed it, it took. The last line must be handed to it instead, and the hand that gives it closes the door forever."*

The rain filled the pause. Maren set the letter down because her hands were shaking, and looked at Ansel across the table, at the lamplight catching the exhaustion in his face.

"And the cost," he said. It wasn't a question.

"The cost is in the next paragraph." She picked it up again, forcing herself. *"Prudence said the name must be TRUE — the name the person is called by God and by their mother, not the name on their letters. And when it is given, it is spent. No one will remember it. No one will remember HIM. Wife, children, friends — the name goes down into the stone and everything it touched forgets it was ever spoken. Prudence begged Father to finish it himself and he wept and said he could not, that he had built his whole life on the taking and had no free gift left in him. And so it has waited. Grandfather would not. Daddy would not. I will not, Lottie, and I am ashamed, but I am a coward about this one thing, and I have decided to be a coward loudly, in writing, so that my granddaughter knows exactly what she inherits."*

Maren folded the letter along its old creases, carefully, as if it were a wound she was bandaging.

"Four generations," she said. "Great-great-grandfather built the trap and couldn't spring it. His son looked at the cellar stairs and went back to bed. My grandfather had the boiler room walled off so no one would even find the door. And my grandmother—" Her voice caught. "My grandmother wrote all of it down, and apologized to me across sixty years, and still wouldn't do it. We are a family of people who know exactly what is owed and have always found someone else to pay it. The residents. The dying. People who checked into Hollowmere because they had nowhere else to go, and whose names got eaten to keep the interest paid on my family's fortune."

"That's why the house can't just die quietly," Ansel said. "If you dissolve the trust and empty the building—"

"Then the feeding stops." She nodded. "And the Tenant stops being patient. Prudence was clear about that too — the binding holds it in the room, but the feeding is what keeps it *asleep*. A hundred years of meals, Ansel. If the meals stop and the seventh line stays blank, it doesn't starve. It wakes up hungry, and the room is only a room, and the door is only a door."

* * *

They sat for a long time after that, the letters between them like a body on a table.

It was Maren who finally said it, because she had driven back through the rain to say it, and because if she didn't say it now she would sign the papers tomorrow and become the fifth generation of cowards with better handwriting.

"Don't finish it."

Ansel looked up.

"I know what you're thinking. I've watched you think it for two days. You're going to go down there and speak the line and give it your name, because you're the kind of man who counts the dead as patients instead of losses, and because you'll decide your name costs less than theirs." She leaned forward. "I'm asking you — I am *begging* you — to let me sign those papers. Let the house close. Let it wake up angry in an empty building in a town that will scatter within a decade. No more feeding. No more residents walking in the front door with their names in their pockets. Yes, something bad happens when it wakes. But it happens to *stone and timber*, Ansel, not to you. You walk away whole. Everyone walks away whole. The debt dies unpaid and the thing dies unfed and in fifty years Hollowmere is a ruin teenagers dare each other to enter, and no one else is ever asked to pay."

"It won't stay in the ruin."

"You don't know that."

"I read the ledger, Maren. The founders' notes get very specific whenever they write about what happens if the feeding stops. 'It will seek the nearest named things.' That's us. That's the staff. That's the town." He shook his head slowly. "You want to buy our safety with everyone who comes after. That's the family trade, isn't it? I'd just be the first Voss-adjacent person to refuse."

"My family's sin is not yours to redeem."

"No," he agreed. "It's mine to end. Those are different things."

Maren pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she lowered them, Ansel was watching her with an expression she recognized from the ward — the look he gave families at the bedside when the end was near and they were still deciding whether to fight it. Patient. Certain. Already grieving.

"There's one more thing in the letters," she said hoarsely. "Prudence's last instruction. The person who finishes the rite can't do it alone. Someone has to hold the outer mark of the circle — the first line, the founder's line — while the name is given. Otherwise the circle won't hold and the giving won't take. It has to be someone who knows the truth and stays anyway." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Do you understand what my grandmother did? She wrote down the whole mechanism of our salvation and then made sure that whoever used it would need a witness. She was daring some future Voss to be brave, and I'm going to fail her test, and you're going to fail it with me, differently."

"Maren—"

"If you go down there," she said, "I will hold the mark. Because someone has to, and because I will not add *abandonment* to what my family owes. But hear me, Ansel Dravenmoor: I am holding that mark to keep the circle steady, not to bless the sacrifice. And the moment it's done, I am spending the rest of my life making sure your name isn't forgotten — I'll carve it somewhere, I'll hide it in a deed, I'll—"

"You won't remember it," he said gently. "That's the price. You just told me."

"I know." Her eyes burned. "I know, and I'm coming anyway."

Outside, far below the rain and the foundations and the century of careful forgetting, something in the dark turned over in its sleep — and, faint as a pulse through stone, began to count.
