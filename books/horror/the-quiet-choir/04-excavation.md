## 4. Excavation

The invoice arrived on a Tuesday, folded into the school's internal post between a memo about fire drills and a circular from the bursar reminding staff that personal kettles were not permitted in the dormitories. Cyrus almost didn't open it. It was addressed to *The Bursar's Office, attn: Temporary Staff Liaison*, and his name appeared only in the third line, in the flat courier's typeface of an outside firm: **Harrow & Sons Groundworks, Ltd. — Notice of Unforeseen Subsurface Conditions.**

He read it standing at the mail pigeonholes in the corridor outside the staff room, while somewhere below the floorboards — far below, it seemed to him, though he knew the boiler room was only one storey down — something enormous was being dug.

*Unforeseen subsurface conditions.* He had taught enough students to write lab reports to know what that phrase meant when it came from a contractor. It meant: *we have found something we did not price for, and we are about to charge you for the privilege.* The invoice itself was modest, almost apologetic — £412.50 for two days' standby time — but attached to it was a delay order, signed by a foreman named A. Kettleby, requesting an extension of works through the end of the month. In the space marked *Reason for Delay*, someone had typed, then deleted, then retyped a single sentence:

*Obstruction at depth. Nature of obstruction under assessment.*

Cyrus read it three times. There was nothing sinister in it. There was nothing sinister in anything, he reminded himself, folding the page back along its crease. Hollowmere Academy had spent three weeks teaching him that its wrongnesses were small ones: a roster with names struck through in ink gone brown as old tea; a records office where the withdrawal files were filed under *N* for *None*; a girl named Wren who spoke of a choir with the flat certainty of someone reciting a bus timetable. Small wrongnesses, all of them explicable. Schools were old buildings full of bad paperwork. Children were cruel and strange and loved mysteries. Teachers resigned mid-term all the time.

He put the invoice in his satchel and went to teach Year Nine the difference between correlation and causation, which was, he reflected later, rather like teaching fish the difference between water and air.

* * *

The east wing had been wrapped in scaffolding since his second week, sheeted in green mesh that turned the whole facade the colour of pond water. The new boiler room was to be sunk forty feet below the existing foundations — a matter, the site notice explained, of "modern plant requirements within a constrained heritage footprint." Cyrus had walked past the hoarding every morning on his way to the car park, past the skips and the Portakabin site office and the sign reading **HARROW & SONS — DIGGING DEEPER SINCE 1911**, without giving any of it a second thought. Groundworks were groundworks. Holes were holes.

But on Thursday morning the hoarding had grown a second sign, hand-lettered on a piece of plyboard in the foreman's blunt capitals:

**NO UNAUTHORISED PERSONNEL BEYOND THIS POINT. THIS MEANS YOU, SIR.**

The *sir* was underlined twice.

Cyrus stood at the barrier longer than he meant to. Below him, at the bottom of the excavation, the ground opened like a wound. They had cut a great square shaft into the hillside, timbered and stepped, and even from the surface he could see the strata laid bare in cross-section: topsoil dark as coffee grounds, then clay the colour of old brick, then a band of gravel glittering with flint, then more clay, darker, wetter, going down and down into a gloom that the October light could not follow. Men moved along the ledges with the unhurried economy of people who had done this ten thousand times. A dumper truck idled at the lip, its bed raised, engine ticking.

And at the bottom — he had to lean over the barrier, one hand on the cold galvanised rail, to see it — there was stone.

It did not look like a foundation. That was the first thing. Foundations were rubble, brick footings, Victorian concrete poured by men who hadn't heard of slump tests. This was a wall. Or the face of something wall-like: a single expanse of dressed stone rising out of the clay floor of the pit, black as a slate roof after rain, so smooth and so dark that the worklights strung along the shoring seemed to fall into it rather than off it. The men had cleared perhaps six feet of it across, and eight of it high, and it simply continued beyond their excavation on every side, into the earth, as if the hill were holding its breath around it.

A ladder descended the last section, and Cyrus watched a figure climb it slowly, without urgency, a stocky man in a hi-vis vest gone grey with clay dust. The man reached the surface, pulled off his hard hat, and stood looking at the hole behind him the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten him.

"You'd be the teacher." It wasn't a question. The man had a voice like gravel shifted by a shovel, and eyes that went to Cyrus's lanyard and dismissed it. "Kettleby. Site manager."

"Cyrus Montrose. I teach—"

"English. I know." Kettleby wiped his hands on his thighs, accomplishing nothing. Clay does not surrender to trousers. "You'll be wanting to know why your boiler room's late."

"The invoice said obstruction at depth."

"That's the official phrase, aye." Kettleby looked back down into the pit. His jaw worked, chewing on something that wasn't tobacco. "You want to see it? Properly?"

Cyrus should have said no. He was aware, distantly, of the bell that would ring in eleven minutes, of the Year Tens waiting with their essays on *Macbeth*, of the entire architecture of his ordinary Thursday morning stacked up behind him like plates on a shelf. But the stone at the bottom of the hole had done something to the light, or the light had done something to the stone, and he heard himself say, "Yes."

Kettleby grunted, which Cyrus would learn was the man's version of enthusiasm, and led him round to the site office to sign a form that was mostly liability waiver and barely legible carbon copy.

* * *

Forty feet is nothing, in principle. It is four storeys. It is the height of a house stood on another house. But descending into the earth on foot, rung by rung, with the daylight narrowing above you to a grey rectangle and the smell changing — topsoil and diesel giving way to something older, mineral, cold, the smell of a cellar in a house no one has lived in for a century — forty feet becomes a distance measured in some other unit entirely. Cyrus counted his breaths. At thirty breaths his feet found clay instead of ladder.

The pit floor was puddled and plank-walked. The worklights made pools of yellow that stopped abruptly, as if the darkness beyond them were a substance with a shoreline. And there, filling the southern face of the excavation, stood the stone.

Up close it resolved into something worse than a wall. It was a door.

Not a doorway — there was no frame, no lintel, no architectural language at all — but a door: a single slab of basalt so black it seemed to drink the worklights, set flush into the living rock of the hillside as though the rock had grown around it the way a tree grows around a nail. It stood perhaps nine feet tall and five wide, and its edges ran true as a machinist's line, disappearing left and right into the unexcavated clay. The surface was unornamented except for one thing.

In the centre, at the height of a man's chest, was a handle.

It was iron, or something like iron, gone the deep brown-black of metal that has been held by hands for a very long time. It was shaped in a simple loop, and where the loop met the door it had been worn — polished, actually, worn smooth as a river pebble, bright in a way the rest of the door refused to be. Cyrus looked at that bright patch and felt the hair rise on his forearms before his mind supplied the reason. Smoothness takes time. Smoothness takes *hands*. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, gripping this handle over years upon years, wearing the iron bright as a church pew.

"Go on," Kettleby said behind him. "Touch it. Everyone does."

"I'd rather not."

"Aye," said Kettleby, with something that might have been respect. "That's the sensible answer."

Cyrus crouched instead, examining the seam where the slab met the surrounding rock. It was tight. Not fitted — *grown*, if such a thing were possible; there was no mortar, no lead, no jointing compound, just a line so fine he couldn't have slid paper into it. He took out his phone and shone its torch along the seam, and the light revealed what the worklights had hidden: a faint halo of discolouration in the rock around the slab, a ring of paleness, as though at some point the whole door had been hot enough to scorch its own frame.

"When did you hit it?" he asked.

"Tuesday week. Bucket bit something at forty foot and the machine near threw the driver off his seat. Thought it was a boulder." Kettleby spat, expertly, into a puddle. "Boulders don't have handles."

"Have you tried opening it?"

Silence. Cyrus looked up. Kettleby's face, in the flat yellow light, had gone carefully blank, the way faces go blank when they are deciding how much truth a situation can bear.

"We don't try things down here," he said at last. "We dig where we're told and we stop where we're told. My grandad worked the quarry at Wether Fell, and he had a saying: the ground gives you what it gives, and you thank it, and you don't ask for change." He nodded at the door. "That's been shut a long time, sir. Shut from the inside, if you take my meaning."

"I'm not sure I do."

"There's no lock on it. No bolt, no bar, no keyhole. Nothing to open it *with* from our side." Kettleby's voice dropped, not dramatically, but the way voices drop in cellars, obeying some acoustic law older than manners. "So either it doesn't open at all. Or it opens from in there. And either way—" he spread his clay-grey hands "—that's not a door for the likes of us. That's a door somebody wanted kept shut, and they wanted it kept shut by folk who never once thought about turning that handle. Because if they'd thought about it, they'd have buried it deeper than forty foot. Forty foot's not hiding a thing, sir. Forty foot's *respecting* it."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of clay and October air, the school bell rang. Cyrus felt it in the soles of his feet before he heard it, a small tremor passing down through the timbering, through the clay, through whatever the hill was made of, and the door drank the vibration without echo.

"Your lads," he said, standing. "They're all right? Working down here?"

Kettleby was quiet long enough that the silence became an answer of its own.

"They're all right," he said finally. "Long as we're out by dusk."

"It's a building site, Mr. Kettleby. You're not farmers."

"No." Kettleby picked up his hard hat and turned it in his hands, and for a moment he looked much older than his shoulders suggested, a man carrying something heavy in a place designed for carrying dirt. "But my lad Danny's on the night shift this fortnight, and Tuesday night he comes home and won't say what he heard. Won't say. Just sits with the telly on loud and his back to the window." He put the hat on. "There's no night shift anymore. I cancelled it. Cost me a penalty clause, and I'll pay it, and the delay order's in your post, and you can bill me for the inconvenience right back if you've a mind."

"What did he hear?"

Kettleby was already climbing. Halfway up the ladder he paused, and did not look down.

"Singing," he said. "Four notes. Over and over. Like a tuning fork with words missing." Then he climbed on, and the darkness of the shaft folded him up rung by rung until only his boots showed, and then not even those.

* * *

Cyrus taught *Macbeth* that afternoon on autopilot, marking marginalia in a hand he didn't recognise as his own. *Fair is foul, and foul is fair* — he had always taught the witches as theatre, as atmosphere, as Shakespeare pandering to a king's superstitions. Now the lines sat differently on the page, like a description of a place he had visited without meaning to.

At half past four he told himself he was taking a walk for his health, and at twenty-five past four he was standing at the hoarding again, watching the light go out of the sky over the east wing.

The crew knocked off at dusk precisely. He watched them come up the ladder in single file, unhurried, silent, each man pausing at the lip of the pit to look back down — every one of them, without exception, a little ritual none of them seemed aware of performing — before walking to the site cabin and signing out. The floodlights stayed off. Kettleby locked the generator shed, then the fuel store, then the cabin, testing each padlock twice, and loaded the last two men into a van that coughed away down the drive trailing the smell of diesel and cold clay.

By six the site was empty. The green mesh stirred in the wind. The pit lay open beneath the first stars, its timbered walls pale as bone, and at the bottom of it, too deep now for Cyrus to see clearly from the barrier, the black door waited with its bright-worn handle, shut from the inside, keeping whatever counsel it had kept for however many hundred years.

He stayed until his breath fogged and his fingers ached on the rail. Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. Stone does not sing; doors do not listen; dusk is only dusk, and a cancelled night shift is a cancelled night shift, and £412.50 plus penalty clauses is the ordinary cost of doing business with old ground.

He wrote it all down anyway, that night, in the notebook he kept for grading notes. *Basalt slab, approx. 9 ft × 5 ft, flush-set, no frame. Handle worn smooth — prolonged use. Seam heat-scarred. No locking mechanism (exterior). Crew refuses night work. Foreman's son reports auditory phenomenon: four-note phrase, repetitive.* He looked at the last line for a long time. Then he added, in smaller writing, as though making the words smaller might make them mean less:

*Wren said: taken for the choir.*

Outside his window, the fog was coming up from the valley the way it did most nights now, thickening around the streetlamps, erasing the east wing first — as if the school were being taken away piecemeal, beginning with the part of itself that had been opened. Somewhere under all that white, forty feet down, the ground held its shape around a door that had never once, in all its long dark centuries, been opened from this side.

Cyrus slept badly, and dreamed — though he would deny it in the morning — of a handle, worn bright by ten thousand hands, turning gently toward him.
