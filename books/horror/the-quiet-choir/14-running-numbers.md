## 14. Running Numbers

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that a rational man performs when the world stops adding up. Cyrus had learned it young, from a father who kept ledgers for a living and believed, with quiet religious fervor, that every column balanced if you were honest enough with the entries. *Find the discrepancy,* his father used to say, tapping the page with a pencil stub. *The error is never in the sum. It's in what you refused to write down.*

Cyrus sat at his desk in the small flat above the porter's lodge, a telephone directory open before him, and wrote down everything.

The directory was old — the school's, inherited with the flat, its pages soft as cloth from decades of thumbs. Hollowmere's exchange was listed under H, between Harrowfield and Hoxley. He dialed the police station first, because that was what a sensible man did when children were being chosen by a piece of paper that wrote itself overnight. The line connected. He could hear it connect — the click, the faint breath of an open circuit, the ghostly hush of a room somewhere at the other end.

Then nothing. No ringing. No voice. Just that hush, which after eleven seconds he realized was not silence at all but something very like breathing — slow, tidal, in perfect unison with itself, as though the entire constabulary of Hollowmere had inhaled together and not yet bothered to exhale.

He set the receiver down without hanging up. Listened. The breathing continued, patient, unhurried, and he understood with a lurch that it was not coming from the line. It was coming from the floor. From below the floor. From forty feet below the floor, through bedrock and clay and the black basalt door that the excavation had uncovered and the earth had swallowed whole again.

He hung up. The breathing stopped. The flat was very quiet, the way a held note is quiet after the orchestra cuts out — full of the shape of what had been there.

*Fine,* he told himself, and wrote in his notebook, in his neatest hand: *Telephone exchange faulty. Possible explanation: rural infrastructure, storm damage, sabotage.* He looked at the sentence. He looked at the word *sabotage*, which he had written because it was the least impossible word available, and he crossed it out, because it was also the most frightening one.

He tried the directory again. County hospital: connected, breathing. Neighboring town, nine miles east: connected, breathing. He dialed a number at random, some stranger in a city two hundred miles south where the fog had never heard of Hollowmere, and the line rang — actually rang, bright and ordinary — and a woman answered, and Cyrus was so flooded with relief at the sound of a human voice that he nearly wept.

"Sorry," he said. "Wrong number."

"That's all right, love," she said, and hung up, and Cyrus sat with his hand still on the receiver and understood that he had just spent his one call to the outside world confirming that the outside world still existed, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. A prison with a window was crueler than a wall.

He tried the police once more before bed. Connected. Breathing. He lay down on the narrow bed with his clothes on and did not sleep so much as blink for a long time in the dark, and somewhere past three in the morning — he knew the hour because the corridor outside did its work at three, the sleeping students rising into their unison hum like a tide coming in — he heard the telephone ring, once, softly, in the empty flat below.

Nobody was down there. He did not go and look. That, too, he wrote down.

* * *

The road was the next column in the ledger.

He told himself it was a supply run. Chalk, coffee, a newspaper — a newspaper, that was the important thing, proof of the wider world, dated and printed and indifferent to Hollowmere's troubles. He told Wren, who insisted on coming, and he did not have the strength to refuse her. She had dark half-moons under her eyes. The seven names on the chapel noticeboard included hers, and the seven selected students were sleeping worse every night, waking hoarse, humming in their sleep with their mouths full of someone else's music. She carried her ledger everywhere now, hugged to her chest like a life vest. Inside her sleeves, he had noticed, she had pinned slips of paper with her own name written on them, over and over — *Wren Halloway-Blackwood, Wren Halloway-Blackwood* — as if to remind the dark who she was.

"It's a waste of time," she said, climbing into the school's ancient estate car. "The roads don't work. Miss Halloway told me. People try every year."

"People are wrong every year," Cyrus said. "That's most of what people are for."

The fog was thick even for October, a wet grey wool pressed against the windscreen, the wipers carving slow arcs that filled in again before they finished. The lane unwound ahead of them between hedgerows the color of drowned things. Cyrus drove with the confidence of a man following a recipe. Nine miles to the junction. Nine miles to the A-road. Nine miles to a town with a police station whose telephone breathed.

At mile six, the hedges changed. He noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar hymn — not the individual trees but their arrangement. There was a gap in the hedgerow on the left, a farm gate, a leaning post with a rusted sign. He had passed that gap at mile two. He was almost certain. He was *professionally* almost certain, which was to say he had built his whole life on the architecture of almost.

"Keep going," Wren said quietly. She had seen it too.

He kept going. At what the odometer swore was mile nine, the lane bent, descended, crossed a stone bridge over a stream he did not remember, and climbed again — and there was the farm gate, the leaning post, the rusted sign, now on the right-hand side of the road, which was geometrically impossible, which meant the road had not looped but *mirrored*, and Cyrus pulled over because his hands had begun to shake and a shaking man behind a wheel on a fog-bound lane was a danger to no one, since there was no one, since there had been no oncoming traffic in forty minutes and no other headlights and no other engine sounds in a fog that swallowed everything and returned nothing.

He turned the car around. Drove back the way they'd come. The school appeared out of the fog in eleven minutes, its towers rising like the masts of a drowned ship, and the odometer read eleven miles, and the trip had taken fifty-three minutes of driving, and Cyrus sat in the car in the empty lot with the engine ticking and did the arithmetic three times because the arithmetic refused to come out.

"Say it," Wren said.

"No."

"You have to say it eventually."

"The road is not a road," Cyrus said. The words came out flat, stripped of inflection, like a diagnosis. "It's a throat. And we're inside it."

Wren nodded, unsurprised, and hugged her ledger tighter, and Cyrus understood that for her this was not a revelation but a confirmation — that she had grown up inside the throat and had only just been given the vocabulary for it. He found he envied her the ledger. Evidence was a kind of company.

They sat in the car for a long time. Then, because there was nothing else to do and because a rational man's last resort is the experiment, Cyrus got out, walked fifty yards down the lane into the fog, and turned around. The school was gone. The lane ran in both directions, identical, hedged, silent. He walked the other way. The school was behind him again, materializing out of the wet grey like something surfacing to check on him.

"It lets you back," he said to Wren, when he'd stopped walking. "It just doesn't let you out. That's not a barrier. That's a courtesy."

"That's worse," said Wren.

"Yes," said Cyrus. "It is."

* * *

The hymnal was the third column, and the one he was least proud of.

He took it from the chapel archive on the third night, after the phone and the road, after two nights of three a.m. choruses drifting through the walls, after Voss had stopped meeting his eyes in the corridor. Miss Halloway had shown him where it lived — she had kept it safe for fifty years, waiting for a Montrose, she said, and now it was waiting for him — but she had not shown him how to destroy it, and he suspected, from the careful way she handled it, that the question had occurred to her too and that her answer had been *no*.

He took it anyway. He was done with other people's careful answers.

He built the fire in the flat's small grate with a methodical, almost ceremonial care: kindling, split logs, the newspaper he had never managed to buy, replaced with pages from a term-old copy of the school prospectus, which felt appropriate. The hymnal lay on his desk. It was a heavy octavo bound in boards covered with black leather gone bald at the corners, and it was warm — it was always warm, faintly, the temperature of a sleeping body, and when he picked it up to carry it to the fire the warmth seemed to shift, very slightly, toward his hands, the way a cat leans into a palm.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to the book, to whatever was in the book or behind the book or *was* the book. "I'm sorry. But you're a variable I can't control."

He held it over the flames.

The fire took the air in the room first. The flames leaned toward the hymnal — there was no other word for it, they *leaned*, bending out of the grate like grass in wind, like a congregation turning toward the pulpit — and Cyrus felt the heat climb his arms and he held on, jaw set, a man doing long division while the house burns down around him, and the book did not burn. The flames touched the cover and spread around it, parted by it, flowing off the leather like water off oiled stone, and the smell that filled the room was not smoke but something older and sweeter, incense and cut hay and the cold mineral breath of a cave, and the warmth in his hands deepened until it was nearly unbearable, and beneath it — beneath the leather, beneath the boards, in the marrow of the thing — he felt something *notice* him.

Not angrily. That was the worst of it. The noticing had no anger in it at all. It was vast and patient and pleased, the way a gardener is pleased by a seedling, and Cyrus understood that he had not been testing the book. The book had been testing *him*.

He dropped it on the hearthstones. It landed without sound. The flames recoiled from it, shrank, and guttered out, all of them at once, and the flat went dark except for the grey glow of fog through the window, and Cyrus stood in the sudden cold breathing hard, and the hymnal lay on the stones, dry, unmarked, its warmth rising gently into the dark like a question.

He did not touch it again that night. He slept — if it could be called sleep — in the chair by the window with the lights on, and at three in the morning the corridor hummed its four notes, and this time he hummed them back before he could stop himself, and the hum in the walls paused, and adjusted, and *included* him, and he pressed his hands over his mouth and did not sleep at all after that.

In the morning the hymnal was on his desk.

Not on the hearthstones. On his desk, squared neatly with the edge, closed, its bald leather corners catching the early light. He had not moved it. He had heard nothing in the night except the choir and his own pulse. The room had been locked — he checked the lock, which was unbroken, and the window, which was latched, and the chimney, which was narrow and full of last night's dead fire — and the book was on his desk, and beside it, placed with a precision that made his skin crawl worse than anything the book itself had done, lay a single sheet of paper.

It was a page of the hymnal's blank score. He recognized the stock, the faint warmth of it. But it was not blank.

Seven staves ran down the page. The first six were empty. The seventh was not. On the seventh stave, in notation of a hand he recognized from the founders' portrait — the same long, patient, downward-slanting script as Aldous Montrose's signature — a single line of music had been written, and beneath it, in the same hand, three words:

*The seventh voice.*

Cyrus sat down slowly. He looked at the page for a long time. Outside, the fog was burning off, and the towers of Hollowmere stood up into the pale gold morning, and somewhere below the east wing, forty feet under the foundations, behind a door of black basalt sealed from the inside, something breathed in harmony with itself and waited for him with what he could only describe, in the privacy of his own skull, where no ledger could reach it, as *affection*.

The rational exits were closed. He had run the numbers. Phone: no. Road: no. Fire: no. Every column balanced now, and the sum was the one his father had promised him all sums would be, if only he was honest enough with the entries. The discrepancy had never been in the arithmetic. It was in what he had refused to write down — that he had been expected here, hired here, *summoned* here, that the school had reached out into the world and chosen him the way the noticeboard had chosen Wren, and that there was exactly one door left open in the whole sealed throat of Hollowmere, and it was not a road and not a telephone line.

It was a verse.

Cyrus took up his pen. Below the three words in his ancestor's hand, he wrote, in his own: *Seven verses. Seven voices. One bloodline.* Then he underlined it twice, the way his father had underlined every final total, and he sat in the cold flat above the porter's lodge and began, very quietly, to learn the first verse by heart.
