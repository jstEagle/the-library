## 19. The Ritual Interrupted

The school had never been so quiet, and the quiet was wrong.

Cyrus stood at the top of the east stairwell with Halloway's hymnal open against his chest and listened to the silence the way a sailor listens to a wind that has stopped without reason. Hollowmere at three in the morning should have creaked — old buildings always creaked, pipes knocked, radiators ticked as they cooled. But the academy had gone still in a way that felt less like absence and more like attention. The whole building was holding its breath.

Below him, through four floors of stone and timber, the door waited.

He could feel it now. That was the thing he would never be able to explain to anyone who hadn't stood where he stood: somewhere in the last week his body had learned to perceive it, the way you learn to feel a splinter before you find it with your eyes. A warmth under the floorboards. A pressure behind the walls. The seams of the basalt door glowed like kiln cracks even now, he knew, though he couldn't see them — a dull orange light bleeding up through the foundations, through the boiler room's poured concrete, through forty feet of earth that had been told to forget what it held.

Wren was beside him on the landing, sleeves pinned with her written words, her face gray in the moonlight that came through the stairwell window. The harvest moon was not yet risen. It wouldn't rise for another hour. But its light already seemed to be arriving ahead of itself, the way thunder arrives before the storm front, silvering everything, making the dust in the air hang like something suspended mid-decision.

"The six are downstairs," Wren said. Her voice was hoarse from nights of fighting the singing in her sleep. "Halloway's with them. They're just... standing."

"I know."

"You can't know."

"I can." He closed the hymnal gently, as if it were sleeping. "I can feel all of them. Like teeth in my own mouth."

That was the truth of it, and the truth of it was obscene. Since the first verse — since he had stood alone in the chapel and sung those seven notes into the dark and watched blank paper fill with notation like frost forming on glass — some thread had been tied between him and the choir. He felt the six selected students the way he felt his own pulse. He felt the thing beneath the door the way he felt hunger. And he felt, with terrible clarity, that all of these sensations were converging on a single point, and the point was moonrise.

* * *

They descended together.

The boiler room excavation had been refilled by hands no one saw, but the school had opened it again — or rather, the ground had opened itself, the concrete split along seams too clean to be natural, a stairway descending into earth that smelled of turned soil and candle smoke and, faintly, of choir lofts and old varnish, the smell of every church Cyrus had ever sat in as a boy, bored and skeptical, watching light move through colored glass.

The six students stood in a half-circle before the door.

They were sleepwalking. That was the only word for it, though their eyes were open, and open eyes on sleepwalking children is a wrongness the mind resists accepting. Their faces were slack and serene. Their mouths moved slightly, shaping syllables without breath. Halloway stood among them with her hands hovering near their shoulders, not touching, a shepherd who had learned long ago that the flock could not be driven, only accompanied.

Miss Halloway looked up when they entered. In the glow of the door's seams her face was carved into planes of amber and shadow. She had been offered once, she'd told him — 1968, a girl of eleven, standing exactly where these children stood — and spared when the choir took another instead. Sixty years of guardianship had burned everything soft out of her expression. What remained was granite with grief running through it like a vein.

"It's time," she said. Not a question.

"Tell me again," Cyrus said. "All of it. I want it in order."

"Seven verses. Seven voices. One bloodline." She recited it like a catechism, because it was one. "Verses one through six, you sing; the six voices answer. The counter-ritual turns the choir inward, folds it back behind the stone. But the seventh verse—" She stopped. She had stopped there every time, and every time the stopping had the same shape: a door she could describe but not open.

"The seventh verse requires a willing voice of Montrose blood," Cyrus finished for her. "Freely given. I remember."

"And there is only one voice of Montrose blood left in the world."

"Yes," said Cyrus. "I'm aware."

Wren grabbed his sleeve. "You said if. You said *if* you could learn how. You didn't say—"

"Wren." He covered her hand with his. Her fingers were freezing. "We have maybe an hour. Let me spend it teaching you what to do after."

"After what?"

He didn't answer. Above them, through the shaft of the stairway, the first edge of the harvest moon cleared the tree line of the ridge, and the light came down the stairs like water finding a drain.

* * *

The moon rose the way tides turn — slowly, then all at once.

Cyrus positioned himself before the door with the hymnal open. The basalt filled his vision, black shot through with glowing fissures, and the handle worn smooth by generations of hands that had gripped it from the other side. He pressed his palm flat against the stone. It was warm as skin. Beneath it, breathing — not one breath but many, layered, harmonized, an inhale taken by dozens of throats in perfect unison and held, and held, and held.

*HELP US FINISH IT,* the scratches inside had read, under his fingertips, weeks ago. He understood now what the finishing meant. The founders had made a guardian out of their dead children's voices and bound it with a lie, and the lie was fraying, and fraying things do not wait politely.

"Begin," Halloway said.

Cyrus sang the first verse.

His voice came out steadier than he expected — Aldous Montrose's cadence lived in his throat whether he willed it or not, the same intervals, the same falling fourth at the end of each line, and hearing his ancestor's music come out of his own mouth was like hearing his own name spoken by a stranger. The notation on the page shimmered. The six students straightened as one, their slack faces tightening into focus, and their mouths opened, and they answered.

Six voices rose in the dark. They were beautiful. That was the horror of it, the horror he had been circling for weeks: the choir was genuinely, devastatingly beautiful, four-part harmony braided out of children's throats, a sound that made the hair on his arms lift and his eyes sting and some animal part of him want to kneel. The second verse. The third. The door's glow deepened from orange to white. The breathing beneath the stone quickened, matched itself to the tempo, became rhythm, became pulse.

Fourth verse. Fifth. Sweat ran down Cyrus's spine. The hymnal's pages were warm under his fingers now, warm as the door, warm as flesh, and the notation was moving — he could see it shifting at the edges of his vision, notes sliding into new configurations, the score rewriting itself around his voice like water around a stone.

The sixth verse ended on a suspended chord that refused to resolve.

And the door opened inward.

* * *

There was no explosion. There was no creature. What came through the opening was sound — sound given weight, sound with volume and appetite, a pouring darkness made entirely of harmony, spilling out of the doorway like smoke poured from a tipped jar. It did not walk. It *poured*, flowing across the earthen floor in slow billows, and everywhere it touched, the air itself began to sing.

"No," Halloway breathed. "No, no — the verses, Montrose, the verses hold it—"

But the six students were already turning.

Mid-verse — Cyrus was still singing, he would keep singing until his lungs gave out — the six choristers pivoted toward the outpouring with expressions of perfect peace, and stepped forward, and the sound folded around them like water closing over stones dropped from a bridge. One moment they were there, small bodies in school uniforms, mouths open in song. The next they were outlines, then suggestions, then nothing at all — absorbed, taken up, their individual voices subsumed into the vast chord that now filled the chamber and pressed against Cyrus's eardrums like deep water.

Their names were still on his class roster. He thought of that with insane clarity even as he sang: *their names are still on my roster, and I never got the paperwork.*

The choir turned its attention on him.

It had no face, but it had attention — a pressure, a focusing, the way a lens focuses light, and suddenly every harmonic in that vast dark chord bent toward the single note of his voice. It liked his voice. It had always liked his voice. It had been waiting generations for this particular timbre, this bloodline's particular grain, and now that it had found him it leaned in like a starving man leaning over bread.

Cyrus faltered.

It was not fear that broke him, or not only fear. It was the seventh verse. His eyes went to the final page of the hymnal and the notation there was complete, had been complete since the chapel, and he read it now with a musician's cold comprehension and understood at last why Halloway could never finish the sentence. The seventh verse was not a melody. It was a contract set to music. Every phrase was structured as an offering — *I give, I bind, I remain* — and the music would not carry unless the meaning was true. The founders' journal had said it plainly: freely given. Willing. Of Montrose blood.

You could not fake willingness in song. The verse would know. The choir would know. He could stand here and sing the notes perfectly and they would fall dead from his mouth like unpitched noise, because knowledge was not enough, had never been enough, and all his rationalist's certainty that understanding a system meant commanding it lay broken at his feet in the singing dark.

"Cyrus!" Wren's voice, raw, tearing through the harmony. She was grabbing at his arm. Behind her Halloway had fallen to her knees, hands pressed over her ears, lips moving — praying, or counting, or simply holding on. "The students — where are they — Cyrus, *where did they go*—"

"They're in it," he said, and his voice cracked, and the crack carried through the chamber, and the choir rippled with something that might have been pleasure. "They're inside the sound. They're not gone, Wren, they're *held* — I can feel them, all six, like fingers in my fist—"

It was true. That was the unbearable thing. Through the thread the first verse had tied between them he could feel the six students still present, still conscious, suspended in the chord like insects in amber. Not devoured. *Collected.* The choir did not eat its singers. It kept them. It had been keeping them since 1847, generation upon generation of voices stacked in that harmonized dark, and the seal had never been a prison for the thing behind the door — it had been a mercy for everyone outside it.

The door stood open. The glow spilled across the floor. And the choir, vast and patient and beautiful, waited for him with the courtesy of something that has already won.

"Sing the seventh," Halloway whispered from her knees. Her eyes were wet. "Freely given, Montrose. It's the only way it holds. It's the only way any of it has ever held."

"I know." His mouth was dry. The hymnal trembled in his hands. On the final page, the notation of the seventh verse seemed to lean toward him, hungry in its own quiet way, patient as the thing it served. "I know what it costs. I've known since the chapel. I was just — I was hoping—"

"Hoping what?" Wren demanded. Her grip on his arm was bruising. "Hoping the math would change? Hoping someone else's blood would do? You told me yourself — you ran the numbers, Cyrus, you *always* run the numbers—"

"—that knowing would be enough," he finished. "It was supposed to be enough. Understanding things is supposed to be enough."

The choir swelled. Beyond it, through the open door, he could see — or sense, seeing was the wrong word — the interior: not a room but a resonance, a space built of held notes, and within it the collected voices of a century and a half, and among them, newly arrived, six frightened children whose fear was being smoothed away note by note into serenity. He could feel them slipping. He could feel the exact rate at which they were forgetting their own names.

The harvest moon stood at the center of the sky above the stairway shaft, round and white and indifferent, and the light it poured down the stairs pooled at the edge of the spilled darkness and did not enter it.

Cyrus Montrose closed his eyes. He thought of a classroom roster with names crossed out in old ink. He thought of a girl who kept a ledger because someone had to. He thought of a librarian who had spent sixty years keeping a warm book safe for a family that had abandoned it. He thought of the scratched words inside the door, readable only by touch, the most desperate sentence he had ever known: *help us finish it.*

Then he opened his eyes, and looked at Wren, and saw that she already understood — had perhaps understood since the stairwell, since *after what*, since every deflection he'd offered — and her face was coming apart, and he loved her for fighting it.

"Wren," he said quietly, beneath the chord, beneath the swell, in a voice only she could hear. "When it's done — when the door closes — don't dig. Don't ask. Live your life so loudly that they can hear it through the stone."

"What are you—"

He stepped forward, out of her grip, toward the open door, and drew the breath that would carry the seventh verse — and the choir, feeling the intention form in him before the first note sounded, turned all of its impossible attention upon him like a single eye.

And from somewhere above, faint and furious and breaking, Wren began to scream his name.
