## 3. The Client Who Isn't

The letter lived in the glovebox of Tamsin's van for two days before he worked up the nerve to read it properly.

This was not cowardice, he told himself. This was *filing*. A professional kept his paperwork in order, and there was an order to things: first you finished the job, then you invoiced, and only then did you go poking about in correspondence like some kind of amateur detective. He had a system. The system had carried him through twelve years of wasps' nests and rat infestations and one memorable incident involving a badger in a church crypt, and the system said: finish the sweep, write the report, send the invoice, move on.

The problem was that the sweep had found nothing. No droppings, no gnaw marks, no nests, no entry points. Four hours of crawling through a farmhouse that should have been sagging with vermin — old houses were never clean, old farmhouses least of all — and he'd come up with nothing except those scratches on every doorframe, all at exactly the height of his own forehead, which he had measured with a tape measure because he was a professional, and professionals measured things even when the measurement made their stomach drop through the floorboards.

So now there was no report to write. You couldn't invoice someone for "found nothing, house is fine, please disregard the fact that I stood in your hallway for ten minutes staring at a mirror." Well. He could. He'd done it before. But something about this job wanted more than an invoice, and what it wanted, apparently, was for him to read the letter.

He took it out of the glovebox at half past nine in the morning, parked outside a café in the village of Lower Dinton, three miles from Hollowmere, and read it for the third time since it had arrived six weeks ago.

*Dear Mr. Ravensworth,*

*We are experiencing a persistent pest problem at Hollowmere Farm and would appreciate your professional assessment at your earliest convenience.*

*Yours faithfully*

And then nothing. No signature. No name. Not even a "regards," which Tamsin felt was the bare minimum of human warmth, though admittedly his standards had been shaped by years of correspondence with people whose kitchens were currently occupied by mice.

No name. He'd assumed, at the time, that he'd simply missed it — that the signature was on the back, or that the envelope contained a second page he'd lost. But the envelope was there too, tucked behind the letter, and it held no second page, and the postmark read HOLLOWMERE in cheerful black ink, dated the fourteenth of August.

Hollowmere didn't have a post office. He knew this because he'd grown up there, and because he'd checked yesterday, standing in the village shop buying a sandwich he didn't want, asking the woman at the counter in the most casual voice he could manufacture whether the post still got collected from the farm. She'd looked at him the way people look at a man who has asked a question with too many teeth in it.

"Post box went years ago," she'd said. "Everything goes through Lower Dinton now."

"So nothing gets postmarked Hollowmere."

"No." And then, after a pause in which she clearly decided against her better judgment: "You're the pest man, aren't you? Going up to the farm?"

"That's right."

She'd nodded slowly, rung up his sandwich, and declined to make eye contact again, which Tamsin had found frankly hurtful, because he was very good at eye contact. It was one of his few social skills and he deployed it generously.

Now he sat in the van with the letter on his knee and tried to think like a professional instead of like a man whose hands had gone slightly cold.

Options. One: the client was a recluse who posted their own letters somehow, or drove to Lower Dinton and — no, the postmark said Hollowmere, and postmarks came from sorting offices, and the nearest sorting office was Lower Dinton, so either someone at the sorting office was playing an elaborate joke, or —

Or the letter had been posted from a place that shouldn't have been able to post anything.

Two: prank. Someone who knew his history had sent him to his childhood home as a joke. This was the explanation he liked best, because it required only cruelty, and cruelty was common and comprehensible. People were cruel all the time. It was practically a civic service. But the scratches hadn't been part of any joke. He'd checked the doorframes himself; the wood was old and grey and the gouges were deep and dark with age, decades of age, and they matched each other across every door in the house with a precision no prankster would bother with.

Three: he wasn't going to think about option three yet. Option three involved words like *impossible*, and Tamsin had spent twelve years building a life on the principle that impossible things were simply things you hadn't found the boring explanation for yet. Boring explanations were like badgers: always there if you looked hard enough, usually under the floor.

He started the van and drove back toward Hollowmere, telling himself it was to deliver the invoice. He had, in fact, written an invoice. It sat on the passenger seat, addressed to *The Occupant, Hollowmere Farm*, because he had no other name to put, and he'd already decided he probably wouldn't post it, because posting it felt like admitting the whole arrangement was real.

The lane narrowed as it always did, hedges closing in like a crowd leaning forward, and then the farmhouse rose out of its dip in the land, grey stone and slate, looking — he had to admit — entirely ordinary in the morning light. That was the trick of it. Houses were just houses until they weren't, and there was no reliable way to tell the difference from the outside, which Tamsin considered a serious design flaw in reality.

He didn't go inside. He'd promised himself he wouldn't go inside without a reason, and "the letter is weird" was not, professionally speaking, a reason. Instead he walked the boundary of the property, along the drystone wall where the lane met the field, because the wall bordered the road and the road was public and therefore walking it was normal behavior and not the behavior of a man circling his childhood home like a nervous dog.

That was where he met Mrs. Ellery.

She was in her garden next door — the cottage he'd known as a child as the Potters', though the Potters were long gone — bent over a bed of runner beans with the posture of a woman who had been gardening since before the invention of bending. She straightened when his shadow crossed the wall, and he saw that she was older than he remembered, small and wiry, with white hair cut short and eyes the pale blue of skimmed milk.

"You're the pest man," she said. Not a question.

"I am. Tamsin Ravensworth." He offered his hand over the wall. She looked at it but did not take it, which he respected; gardeners had their reasons.

"Ravensworth," she repeated, and something moved behind her face like a fish under ice. "You'll be Ivy's boy, then."

The name landed in his chest like a dropped spanner. "You knew my mother."

"Everyone knew Ivy." Mrs. Ellery picked a bean pod off its vine and turned it over in her fingers, not looking at him. "Knew her, and lost track of her, same as everyone. Twelve years, is it?"

"Twelve years this October."

"Mm." She snapped the end off the bean with a small decisive motion, as though executing it. "And now you're back to catch pests."

"That's the job."

"And did you catch them?"

"No," Tamsin admitted. "There's nothing there. No sign of anything, really. Which is odd, for a house that age."

Mrs. Ellery finally looked up at him, and her milk-pale eyes held his with the flat steadiness of a cat watching a bird decide whether to land. "Odd," she agreed. "Yes. Odd is one word for it."

"Do you know who hired me?" He took the letter from his jacket and held it up. "I can't get a name out of anyone. The letter's unsigned, and the postmark says Hollowmere, which apparently doesn't exist anymore, postal-wise."

For a long moment she said nothing. A bee investigated the beans and left, having concluded they had nothing to offer. Somewhere down the lane a pheasant screamed its idiot scream, and Tamsin thought, absurdly, that the sound fit the morning perfectly.

"I'll tell you what I told the last one," Mrs. Ellery said at last.

"The last one?"

"The last person who went asking questions at that house." She set the ruined bean pod on the wall between them, deliberately, like a piece in a game. "My brother, as it happens. 1974. He was a curious man, my Gerald. Curious men do well in cities. In the country, curiosity is a liability."

"What happened to him?"

"He stayed," she said, "for supper."

Tamsin waited for her to elaborate. She did not. She brushed soil from her hands with the air of a woman concluding a transaction.

"Mrs. Ellery," he said carefully, "if you know something about that house —"

"I know what everyone knows, and I know a bit more than that, and none of it will do you any good, because you've already got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a man who belongs there." She said it without malice, without drama, the way you'd tell someone they had spinach in their teeth. "You had it when you walked up the lane. I watched you come. You walk that boundary wall like you're checking it's still holding. That's not a pest man's walk, love. That's a Ravensworth walk. Your mother used to do exactly the same, every autumn, right before harvest."

His mouth had gone dry. "I'm inspecting the perimeter. It's standard practice."

"It is not," said Mrs. Ellery, "and we both know it."

The pheasant screamed again. Tamsin became aware that he was gripping the wall with both hands, the stone cold under his palms, and that he had no memory of putting them there.

"Here's what I'll give you," Mrs. Ellery said, "since you're Ivy's boy and I owe her a debt I never paid. One thing. Free of charge." She leaned closer over the wall, and up close her eyes were not milk-pale at all but the color of frost on water, thin and bright and very old. "That farm keeps what it catches. Vermin, strays, wanderers, question-askers. It keeps them. And it has caught you already — it caught you the day that letter landed on your mat, because you opened it, and you read it, and you went. Didn't you."

"Yes," Tamsin said. There seemed no point lying. His face had never been good at it anyway.

"And you'll go again tomorrow, and the day after, and you'll tell yourself each time it's the last time, and it won't be, because that's how it works. It doesn't chase. It doesn't need to." She straightened, and the frost went out of her eyes, and she was suddenly just an old woman with beans to pick. "It waits. It's very good at waiting. It's had practice."

"With my family," Tamsin said quietly. "You think it caught my family."

"I think," Mrs. Ellery said, turning back to her beans, "that nobody leaves a farm with everything they brought to it. Now go home, love. Whatever home is these days."

He walked back up the lane. Halfway to the gate he stopped, because he'd realized something, and the realization sat in his stomach like swallowed river water.

She'd called him by name. He'd introduced himself, yes — Tamsin Ravensworth — so the surname was accounted for. But she'd said *you're Ivy's boy* before he'd mentioned his mother at all, and she'd said *love* twice, in exactly the cadence his mother had used, and when he turned around to ask her about it, the garden was empty. The bean bed was empty. The wall between the properties held nothing but a single snapped bean pod, drying already in the sun, as if it had been sitting there for days.

Tamsin stood in the lane for a long moment, doing the thing he did best, which was assembling boring explanations out of whatever lay to hand. She'd gone inside. Old women moved quickly when they wanted to. The pod had been there since morning; he simply hadn't noticed it on the way past. All of it was fine. All of it was explicable.

Then he looked at the farmhouse, and the farmhouse looked back — not with eyes, houses don't have eyes, he was very clear on this — but with the particular attention of a thing that has been waiting a long time and is pleased to see the waiting pay off. Every window held a small grey square of reflected sky. The front door stood shut. On its frame, at exactly the height of his forehead, the old scratch gleamed faintly, as though freshly oiled.

He got in the van. He did not drive away. He sat with both hands on the wheel and the engine ticking and told himself, in the reasonable voice he'd spent twelve years perfecting, that he was simply finishing his paperwork.

Inside the van, on the passenger seat, the invoice he'd addressed to *The Occupant, Hollowmere Farm* had changed.

He was almost sure it had changed. The ink was the same, the paper was the same, but the addressee — he stared at it, and stared, and the letters sat where they sat, patient as stones:

*Tamsin Ravensworth, Hollowmere Farm.*

Not *The Occupant.* His name. His full name, in handwriting he recognized the way you recognize a voice in a dream — round and careful and slightly old-fashioned, the handwriting of a woman who wrote shopping lists in fountain pen.

His mother's handwriting.

The engine ticked. Down the lane, Mrs. Ellery's curtains twitched once and went still. And Tamsin Ravensworth, pest controller, master of boring explanations, sat in his van outside the house that had eaten his family and understood, with the calm clarity of a man stepping off a ledge, that he had not been hired at all.

He had been invited home.

* * *

He drove to Lower Dinton because staying felt dangerous and leaving felt worse, and in the café he ordered tea he didn't drink and laid the letter out on the table like evidence at an inquest. The waitress refilled his cup twice without being asked and gave him the particular kindness cafés reserve for men who are visibly coming apart in a quiet way.

Unsigned. Postmarked Hollowmere. Addressed in block capitals that matched no handwriting he knew — and that was the detail that snagged him now, turning it over. The envelope was block capitals. Careful, anonymous, deliberate. But the invoice had been written in his mother's hand, flowing and certain, as if whoever — whatever — had composed the letter wanted the approach to be impersonal and the arrival to be intimate. A stranger's knock at the door. A mother's voice once you were inside.

"You right, love?" the waitress asked.

"Fine," said Tamsin. "Just — do you know much about the post here?"

"Enough to know you don't want to." She collected his untouched cup. "Anything else?"

"No," he said. Then: "Actually. Yes. Is there anyone in the village who'd remember the Ravensworths? From Hollowmere Farm?"

The waitress paused with the saucer in her hand. Behind her, a man in a waxed jacket at the corner table — who had been reading his newspaper with great commitment for twenty minutes — went very still.

"Ravensworths," the waitress repeated. "That'd be the family that left. 'Course I don't remember, I'm not old enough, but my mum talks about it. Sad business. Man walked out on his wife and kids, they say. Took the wife and daughter with him somewhere, and left the boy behind."

"Left the boy behind," Tamsin said.

"That's the story." She set the saucer down and lowered her voice, not unkindly. "Funny thing, though. My mum always said it never sat right. Said Ivy Ravensworth wouldn't have left that boy for anything. Said she loved him more than the whole sky." The waitress smiled, briefly, apologetically. "But stories are stories, aren't they? Anyway. You wanting anything else?"

"No," Tamsin said. "Thank you."

He sat in the café until the light through the windows went amber, and the man in the waxed jacket left without ever appearing to read a single line of his newspaper, and Tamsin understood that the village had already filed him. Not as a stranger, not as a contractor, not even as the poor abandoned boy returned after all these years. As something simpler and more final: as a man who belonged to Hollowmere, walking around outside it temporarily, the way a tide belongs to the sea.

Everyone knew. Everyone had always known. Twelve years of explanations, and the whole time the world had simply been waiting politely for him to stop explaining.

He drove back at dusk. He told himself it was to return the key the estate agent had left under the flowerpot, and the excuse was so flimsy he could see daylight through it, but excuses didn't have to be load-bearing; they just had to hold long enough to get you moving.

The farmhouse stood black against the last of the light. As he came up the lane, he heard it — faint, threading through the hedge like smoke, rising and falling in a tune he knew the way you know a scar.

Someone was singing inside the house. A lullaby. His mother's lullaby, the one she'd sung over the cots and the dishwater and the long dark drives home, and it was missing a verse — he could feel the gap the way you feel a missing tooth — and the walls of Hollowmere Farm hummed it low and patient, over and over, like a mouth learning the shape of a word.

Like a mouth waiting for someone to sing it whole.

Tamsin stood at the gate with his keys in his fist and did not go in. But he listened. God help him, he listened to the end.
