## 7. Mrs. Ellery's Debt

Mrs. Ellery's cottage sat at the end of the lane like a comma at the end of a long sentence—a small pause before the road gave up and became a field. Tamsin had driven past it four times in two days without stopping, telling himself he was surveying the property perimeter, which was technically true if you counted the perimeter of his own nerve.

On the third morning he stopped.

The garden was the first thing that surprised him. He'd expected neglect—Hollowmere seemed to rot everything it touched—but Mrs. Ellery's plot was riotous with late-summer abundance: runner beans climbing strings like they were escaping, marrows fattening under leaves the size of dinner plates, and roses along the front wall so red they looked recently fed. A scarecrow stood guard over nothing in particular, arms outstretched over an empty bed, as though guarding the absence itself.

Tamsin knocked. The door opened before the second knock landed, which was becoming a theme in his life lately, and there she was: small, wiry, seventy-something, with a face like a walnut that had opinions and a cardigan buttoned wrong by one, deliberately, he suspected, to keep people off balance.

"You're the pest man," she said.

"Tamsin Ravensworth. I'm working Hollowmere Farm."

"I know who you are." She looked at him the way you'd look at weather coming in from the west. "You've got your mother's chin and your father's habit of standing on doorsteps like he's waiting for permission to exist. Come in before the beans hear us gossiping."

He followed her into a kitchen that smelled of toast and lavender polish. Everything was slightly too neat, the way houses get when someone is holding the world together through sheer domestic willpower. Two cups already sat on the table. One had steam rising from it.

Tamsin stared at the second cup for a moment longer than was polite.

"Milk's in the jug," said Mrs. Ellery, as if this explained everything, and perhaps it did.

* * *

Her name, she told him, was Winifred Ellery, though everyone called her Winnie except the vicar, who called her Mrs. E., and her late husband, who had called her Fred until the year he died, which was a story she did not tell. She'd lived in the lane since 1961. She remembered when Hollowmere Farm still had cattle, when the Hollis family ran it, when the fields actually grew things instead of growing quiet.

"And then 1974," Tamsin prompted, because she'd paused there the way people pause at a step down in the dark.

Mrs. Ellery wrapped both hands around her cup. Her knuckles were enormous for such small hands, knuckles like knots in fence wire.

"My brother Peter came home that summer," she said. "From Leeds. He'd been away six years—music college, or what passed for it. He played the accordion badly and sang worse, and he was the only person I've ever known who could make a room better just by being loud in it." She smiled, briefly, at some interior photograph. "He came back thin. Said the city had chewed him up. Said he wanted to come home and be somewhere quiet."

Tamsin's tea had gone cold in his hands. He hadn't noticed drinking any of it.

"He took the farmhand's cottage at Hollowmere—the little one past the barn. The Hollises were glad of the company; their own boy had gone off to Australia and never written. Peter fixed fences. He mended the roof. And in the evenings—" She stopped. Started again. "In the evenings, he said the house sang to him."

"The house," Tamsin repeated carefully.

"Through the walls. Under the floors. A harvest song, he called it—old tune, older than anything, the kind of melody that feels like it was always in your head and someone's just reminded you of it." Her eyes came up and fixed on him, and Tamsin felt the peculiar sensation of being read rather than seen. "You know it. Don't lie to me, boy. I can see it sitting behind your face like a bird on a wire."

He thought about denying it. Twelve years of practice rose up in him, smooth and automatic: *I don't know what you mean, probably the wind in the chimneys, old houses settle.* But the words wouldn't come out. They kept snagging on something—on the tally marks in the cellar arranged like notation, on the fragment of lullaby the walls had hummed back at him, on the way his own throat had ached afterward with the effort of not joining in.

"Four bars," he admitted. "Maybe five. It's one verse short of complete. Or it was."

Mrs. Ellery set down her cup with a click that sounded, in the quiet kitchen, like a lock turning.

"That's how it works," she said. "It never sings the whole thing. It sings everything but the ending, and it waits for someone to supply it. That's the whole trick, Tamsin Ravensworth. That's the entire machinery of the thing. It makes you think the song is broken and you're the fix."

* * *

She told him about the autumn of 1974 in pieces, the way you'd hand over broken glass—carefully, edges turned outward.

Peter had started humming in September. By October he was singing openly, walking the fields with his terrible voice raised, and the Hollises said the farm hadn't looked so well in years—the hedges heavy, the apples dropping sweet, the cows giving milk like it was going out of fashion. Everyone remarked on it. Everyone was pleased. Nobody asked why a house that had been silent for a century had suddenly found its voice, or why Peter had stopped sleeping indoors and taken to lying in the barn at night, listening, with his hands folded on his chest like a man practicing.

"He said it wasn't lonely anymore," Mrs. Ellery said. "That's what frightened me. Not the singing. Peter had always sung. It was that he stopped being lonely, and I couldn't work out who with." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "A person who stops being lonely all at once, out in the middle of nowhere—it means something's moved in. Something's answered."

The last time she saw him was the third of November. He'd walked her to the gate, whistling, and told her the harvest was nearly in and that soon he'd finally know how the song ended. He'd laughed. She'd laughed too, because it was Peter, because laughing with him was reflexive, because she was twenty-two and stupid in the way that only loving someone completely can make you.

"In the morning the barn was empty," she said. "His coat was on the nail. His boots were by the door, side by side, toes pointing out, like he'd stepped out of them mid-stride. The Hollises said he'd run off—debts, a woman, the usual. The police said the same, eventually, because police say whatever's easiest to file." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands had gone white around the teacup. "Nobody ever found him. Nobody ever found so much as a button. And every November since, on the third, the farmhouse lights come on at dusk even though there's been no power connected to that building since 1989, and if you stand at my gate you can hear singing. Faint. Happy. Four voices now, where there used to be one."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "Four voices."

"One for every soul it's caught that I know of. There may be more. Older ones." She looked at him steadily. "The Hollis boy who went to Australia never wrote, did I say? Because he didn't go to Australia. He went into the walls in 1968, and his parents sang themselves after him within the year, and the farm changed hands twice more before it fell empty, and every family that lived there heard the song and every family lost somebody to it, and the county records call it bad luck and moving away and no forwarding address."

The kitchen was very warm and very still. Outside, a bee blundered against the window glass, patient and stupid, trying to reach a flower that existed only in reflection. Tamsin watched it for a moment and thought: *that's me, that's exactly me, four days of walking into glass.*

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "You could have let me walk in blind like everyone else."

"Because you're a Ravensworth." She said it like an accusation, which, he supposed, it was. "Because your mother knew. Your mother knew what that house was, and she stayed anyway, and she kept you children out of its reach for years—which is more than anyone else ever managed—and then twelve years ago the whole lot of you vanished in one night, and the village decided you'd all run off, and nobody asked questions, because asking questions about Hollowmere is how you end up in the parish newsletter as a sad story." She leaned forward. "And because you've been humming."

"I have not been—"

"At my gate. Yesterday evening. You were checking the boundary fence and you were humming, low, under your breath, and you stopped the moment you realized, and then you stood there looking sick as a parrot." She sat back, satisfied. "So don't tell me what you have and haven't been doing, boy. The house has been teaching you its song since the day you walked in, and you've been learning whether you meant to or not."

Tamsin opened his mouth. Closed it. Somewhere in the last two days—he couldn't pinpoint when, that was the horrible part—some fragment had indeed lodged itself in him, a phrase of melody that surfaced while he worked, while he drove, while he lay awake in the van listening to the farmhouse breathe. He'd assumed it was memory. His mother's lullaby, surfacing after twelve years. Now he wasn't sure there was a difference between remembering and being taught.

"What do I do?" he asked. It came out smaller than he intended.

Mrs. Ellery studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was weighing, it took a while; he had the sense of scales old enough to need oiling.

"There's one rule," she said finally. "One rule, and it's the only thing I've learned in fifty years worth the price I paid for it. Never finish the song. However it tempts you. However right the missing piece sounds in your head—however much it feels like coming home—you do not sing the ending. The moment you complete it, you belong to it. That's the bargain it makes without telling you. It doesn't drag anyone anywhere, Tamsin. It just waits for volunteers."

"And Peter volunteered."

"Peter finished it." Her voice cracked, just once, on the name, and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "November the third, 1974. Sang the last verse standing in the barn doorway, clear as a bell, happy as Sunday. And the house took him the way a tide takes a footprint."

* * *

She walked him out through the garden, past the beans and the marrows and the scarecrow guarding its empty bed. At the gate she stopped and gripped his arm with those knot-wire knuckles, and for a woman her size her grip was astonishing, like being held by the land itself.

"It'll offer you them," she said quietly. "Your mum and dad. Your sister. It knows what you want before you do—that's its oldest trick, older than the song. It'll show you faces. Voices. It'll feel more real than anything you've ever known, and the wanting will do the rest." Her eyes were wet and furious. "My brother's face is in there. I've heard him singing on November nights, fifty years of Novembers, and every part of me that's still twenty-two wants to walk up that lane and join in so I can stand next to him. So understand that when I tell you the rule, I'm not reciting folklore. I'm telling you the thing that has cost me everything I've got."

"Mrs. Ellery—"

"Winnie," she snapped. "If we're going to die horribly together we might as well be on first-name terms."

Despite everything—despite the cold in his stomach and the melody coiled in his skull like something nesting—Tamsin laughed. It startled out of him, short and cracked, and Mrs. Ellery's mouth twitched in answer, and for one moment they were just two people at a garden gate in the sun, and the horror was a country they'd merely visited.

"Here." She fished in her cardigan pocket and pressed something into his palm: a small iron nail, black with age, head worn smooth. "Off the original barn door. My dad pulled it the week after Peter went, said iron remembers what it held. Superstition, probably. But you keep it in your pocket, and when the song gets loud, you hold onto it, and you remember that you are a Ravensworth and a pest controller, and your job is to evict things."

"I charge by the hour," Tamsin said. "Just so we're clear."

"Charge it double," said Winnie Ellery. "This one's been nesting fifty years."

He walked back up the lane toward the van, the nail a small cold weight in his fist, and behind him he heard her gate creak shut, and her bolts go across—one, two, three, four—as many locks as a wooden gate could bear.

The afternoon had begun to lean toward evening. Across the fields, Hollowmere Farm stood in the long gold light with its windows dark and patient, and as Tamsin opened the van door he heard it—faint, carried on air too still to carry anything:

The song.

Five verses now.

When he'd arrived, it had been four.

He stood with his hand on the door handle, the iron nail biting into his palm, and understood, with the calm clarity of a man reading his own diagnosis, that the house had added a verse since yesterday.

And the new verse sounded, unmistakably, like his mother's voice.
