## 8. The Second Mirror

Tamsin told himself he was going up to the attic for purely professional reasons.

That was the trick of it, the little sleight of hand he'd been performing for twelve years now: never call a thing what it was. You weren't avoiding the kitchen where your family had last been seen; you were conducting a thorough inspection of the ground floor. You weren't lying awake in your van listening to the house hum through its walls; you were experiencing difficulty sleeping due to an unfamiliar acoustic environment. And you weren't climbing the attic ladder at nine in the morning with your heart going like a trapped bird because some part of you had been pulled toward it since you arrived—you were checking for bat ingress. Bats. Wonderful creatures, bats. Protected, most of them. Very legitimate reason to be up there.

The hatch was the one he'd been avoiding longest, which, he was learning, meant the house had been saving it. Every other door in Hollowmere Farm had opened for him before his hand reached it, swinging inward with a courtesy that made his skin crawl. The attic hatch did nothing. It sat there in the landing ceiling like a closed eye, and when he pressed the pole against its ring, the wood resisted with the stubbornness of something that had opinions.

"Fine," Tamsin said. "Have it your way."

He put his shoulder into the ladder and hauled the hatch up himself, and the house let him—let him do the work, let him feel the weight of it, which was somehow worse. The ladder unfolded with a creak that sounded almost like disapproval. Dust came down in a slow golden drift, turning in the light from the landing window, and Tamsin stood at the bottom of it breathing through his mouth because the attic smelled the way attics always smell, except underneath that, faintly, there was something sweeter. Cut hay. Apples going over. His mother's kitchen on a Sunday.

"Bat ingress," he reminded himself, and climbed.

* * *

The attic was bigger than it should have been.

He noticed that the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song—immediately, and then immediately trying to talk yourself out of it. From outside, the farmhouse had a low, squat roofline. He'd walked the perimeter twice on his first day, professionally noting the pitch of the roof and the absence of dormers, and he had done the maths without meaning to: there was no room up here. Not this room. This room ran the whole length of the house and then some, with rafters arcing overhead like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down and decided to be architecture.

Boxes everywhere. Furniture under sheets. The light came through two small gable windows at the far end, thick and amber with grime, falling in slanted columns that made the dust look like it was snowing upward.

Tamsin walked the aisle between the boxes, his torch beam sliding over labels in his father's handwriting—WINTER CLOTHES, TAXES 2009, MARA'S SCHOOL—and made himself keep moving past that last one. MARA'S SCHOOL. He'd have to come back to that one or he'd never leave. He'd have to come back to all of them, he supposed, eventually, because that was the trap of this house: it didn't hide things from you. It arranged them.

That was when he saw the mirror.

It stood against the rafter wall where no mirror had any business standing—too large for an attic, too grand for this house, a tall oval in a frame of dark carved wood that someone had once tried to make look like wheat sheaves. It was covered with a dust sheet like everything else, but the sheet had slipped, or been slipped, and a wedge of glass showed through, catching the amber light.

Tamsin's first thought—his honest, unguarded first thought—was: *I know you.*

His second thought was the professional one, arriving late and slightly out of breath: mirrors in attics are common, actually, people stored furniture all the time, this was a farmhouse, farms had attics, attics had furniture, furniture sometimes had mirrors, and none of it meant anything, and he would simply walk past it and check the gable windows for gaps.

He walked past it.

He got four steps.

Then he stopped, because in the sliver of visible glass, the light was wrong. The two amber columns from the gable windows should have been reflected in it, slanting the other way. Instead the glass held a different light entirely—cooler, greyer, the light of a morning in late September, and beneath it, unmistakably, a kitchen.

Tamsin turned around slowly.

The mirror showed the Hollowmere kitchen. Not the attic. The kitchen—the long table under the window, the dresser with the blue door that had never quite closed, the range with the kettle always on the back ring. But not the kitchen as it was now. He'd stood in that room two days ago and it was a ruin of dust and mouse droppings and a table with one leg propped on a folded newspaper. The kitchen in the mirror was alive. The window was bright with washed glass. The dresser door was shut. And the table was being laid.

His sister was setting three places.

Mara was fourteen in the mirror, and she was wearing the grey jumper with the thumb-holes she'd refused to throw out even after the cuffs went, and she was moving around the table with the particular efficiency she'd had at that age, the efficiency of someone who considered herself the only competent person in the household. She was laying out plates—three plates—and she was talking to someone out of frame, her mouth moving, her head tipping in that way she had when she was explaining something she considered obvious.

Three places. Not four.

Tamsin's breath went out of him slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere else in the house.

Three places, because he hadn't been there yet that morning. Three places, because this was the morning—the actual morning, the one the police had reconstructed from cereal bowls and unopened post and a van that was still in the yard. His parents and Mara had been seen at the farm that day, and by evening the farm was empty, and the official story had grown up around the absence like moss over a stone: the Ravensworths had left. Debt, probably. People did that. People vanished. It was sad, but it was a category of sad that the world had a folder for.

Tamsin had spent twelve years living in that folder. He'd built a whole self out of it—the man who didn't wonder, the man who took pest control jobs and never, ever looked up addresses before accepting them.

And now his sister was setting the table in a mirror, and she was fourteen, and she was real in a way that photographs had never managed. Photographs were flat. This was Mara mid-motion, Mara with her weight on one hip, Mara saying something to their mother that was making their mother laugh—Tamsin could see the edge of her at the frame's border, one hand raised, the laugh arriving.

He should have felt horror. He was aware, distantly, that horror was the appropriate response, the professionally recommended response, the response Mrs. Ellery would have if she could see him standing there with his torch hanging forgotten at his side. But what he actually felt, in the first raw seconds, was a longing so large and so simple it embarrassed him. He wanted to knock on the glass. He wanted to say *Mara. Mara, it's me, it's Tam, I'm right here, I'm twenty-six now, I kept your jumper—* no, he hadn't kept the jumper, he'd kept almost nothing, that had been the whole strategy—

Mara turned her head.

She looked directly at him.

Not past him. Not through him, the way reflections look at the room. *At* him. Her eyes found his through twelve years and a foot of silvered glass, and she stopped moving, one plate still in her hands, and her expression did something he had no name for. It wasn't surprise. It was recognition with the volume turned all the way down—the face of someone who has been waiting a long time and is trying not to frighten the thing they've been waiting for.

Tamsin's mouth was dry. "Mara," he said, and his voice came out in the wrong register, too high, a boy's voice. "Mara, can you hear me?"

She didn't answer. Of course she didn't. But she set the plate down—carefully, the way you set down something when you don't want to startle an animal—and she raised one hand, the one with the thumb-holes, and she pointed.

Not at him. Past him. Down.

At the floor.

Then the light in the mirror changed. The grey September morning warmed, brightened, and the kitchen in the glass began to move—not the people, the *light*, the sun crawling across the table the way it does over hours, and Mara's motion resumed but faster now, jerky, like film run at the wrong speed, plates appearing and disappearing, three places becoming four becoming three, days folding over each other, and her face was doing something it hadn't been doing before, and Tamsin understood with a cold, dropping feeling that he was watching time pass in the mirror. Watching the kitchen live on after the morning. Watching the three places stay three, and then two, and then—

He looked away.

He made himself look away, and the looking away cost him something physical, a tearing sensation behind his eyes, and when his gaze found the attic again—the dust, the boxes, the honest grey daylight—the mirror showed nothing but the rafters and the two amber columns and his own reflection, standing there with his torch, looking like a man who had just seen something.

His reflection was a half-second late. He knew that lag. He'd met it in the hallway mirror on his first day, tested it with a dropped pen, watched the glass hold nothing falling. He'd told himself it was a trick of the light, an old mirror, a tired brain. He had a whole filing system for things like that. It was very well organised. It had never once been right.

"Okay," Tamsin said to the attic, to the house, to the thing underneath everything. "Okay. You've got my attention."

The house hummed. It had been humming since he arrived, that harvest song threading through the walls, always a verse short of complete, and Tamsin had learned to stop hearing it the way you stop hearing a clock. But now, standing in front of the mirror, he heard it change—just slightly, just a half-step, the way a song changes when someone new starts singing along.

* * *

He sat down on a box marked WINTER CLOTHES and did what he always did when the world stopped making sense: he made a list.

One. The mirror showed the past, or some past, or a kitchen that remembered being a kitchen. Two. Mara had looked at him, which meant the mirror wasn't a recording. Recordings don't notice you. Three. She'd pointed down. Four. He was sitting in an attic that was larger than the house containing it, which meant the house had been lying about its dimensions the way he'd been lying about his reasons for coming, and honestly, fair play to it.

Five. He was talking to himself in lists because the alternative was feeling things.

He got up and crossed to the hatch and looked down at the landing below, and the drop seemed ordinary, and the floor seemed ordinary, and he thought about Mara's finger pointing down through the glass. Down. The cellar, then. The cellar with the tally marks carved into the beams, arranged like notation, that answered him when he hummed. He hadn't been down there since the second day. He'd told himself there was no reason to go back.

He'd told himself a lot of things.

Tamsin climbed down, and as his feet touched the landing, he heard it—faint, from below, through the floor, from the direction of the cellar stairs. Not the house's hum. Something else. A voice, thin and off-key and unmistakably fourteen years old, singing a verse he didn't recognise, in a kitchen twelve years gone.

The hatch above him swung shut on its own, gently, like a lid being closed on something precious.

Tamsin stood in the dim landing with his heart hammering and understood, finally, with total clarity, that he was not inspecting this house.

He was being inspected by it.

And somewhere below, his sister was still singing—and the song, he realised, the harvest song, the one the walls had been humming since he arrived, was one verse longer than it had been yesterday.
