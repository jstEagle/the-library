## 2. Documentation Is a Kind of Prayer

The funeral director's name was Harlan Voss, and he had the particular stillness of a man who had spent forty years standing beside open caskets, learning not to look at the faces. He kept his eyes on Gideon's notebook instead, and there was something in that attention — something too fixed, too hungry — that made Gideon hold it a little closer to his chest, the way one holds a Bible in a country where Bibles are forbidden.

"Mr. Whitlock," Voss said, "I understand this is difficult."

Gideon clicked his pen. He had filled eleven pages since arriving at Holloway & Voss Funeral Home at nine that morning, and the twelfth was waiting. *Subject: the body. Time: 10:14 a.m. Lighting: fluorescent, humming at a frequency that makes my teeth ache.*

"Let's begin simply," Gideon said. "When did you receive the deceased?"

"Tuesday last. Your brother was brought in from the county hospital."

"And you prepared him yourself?"

"I did." Voss's hands were folded on the desk between them, pale and precise, like two documents awaiting signature. "Embalming, dressing, cosmetic restoration. The full service."

Gideon wrote it down. *Full service. He says it like a menu.* "And at no point during that process did anything strike you as — irregular?"

The fluorescent light hummed. Somewhere down the hall a door closed with the soft finality of a held breath.

"Irregular," Voss repeated.

"The face," Gideon said. "The face was irregular."

He watched Voss the way he watched everything now — with the desperate, cataloguing attention of a man building a case against the universe. He had photographed the body forty-one times. He had measured the scar on its chin with a tailor's tape he'd borrowed from the front office: three centimeters, left to right, curving down toward the jaw. The same scar he'd carried since he was nine years old, since the day on the ice, since — no. Since *Ambrose* fell. Ambrose had the scar. Gideon had watched him get it. He had the photographs to prove that too, somewhere, in a box, in a life that was starting to feel like it belonged to a stranger.

Voss unfolded his hands and refolded them.

"Mr. Whitlock, the body in my care is Ambrose Whitlock. Forty-one years of age. Identified by dental records and by yourself, on Wednesday, at 2:30 in the afternoon. You signed the forms."

"I signed the forms," Gideon agreed.

"Your signature is quite distinctive. The way you cross your T's. Like you're striking something through them."

Gideon looked down at his notebook, at the T's crossed like little executions, and felt the floor tilt a degree he could not correct. He wrote: *He is watching my hands. He is not watching my face. Nobody will watch my face.*

"I'd like to see him again," Gideon said.

* * *

*The director's recollection, fragmentary, reconstructed from what he told his wife that night and could not afterward explain:*

The Whitlock man sat across from him with his notebook and his pen and his eyes that went through things instead of at them, and Harlan Voss felt — for the first time in forty years, since he was a young man learning to suture lips closed — felt like the body in the back room was not the thing being prepared.

He was being prepared.

He remembered Tuesday. He remembered the transfer, the gurney, the paperwork. He remembered unzipping the bag in the prep room under lights the color of old milk. He remembered thinking: *this man has been dead four hours and he is still warm,* and then he remembered thinking something else, something that arrived in his head the way a word arrives in your mouth before you know you're going to say it:

*Which one is this?*

He had not written it down. He had not said it aloud. But it had sat in him all week like a swallowed stone, and now the brother was here — the living brother, except that when Harlan had looked at the paperwork, at the emergency contact, at the signature, he had felt that same stone turn over. Because the signature on the identification form and the signature in front of him now were the same, and of course they were the same, they were brothers, twins, and yet.

And yet the pen moved the same. The same small hesitation before the capital G. The same strike-through on the T's, like a man canceling something.

Harlan Voss had buried four hundred and eleven people. He knew the arithmetic of grief, the stages the way a carpenter knows grain. But this man across the desk was not grieving. He was *documenting*. And there was a difference, and the difference was the stone, and the stone was getting heavier.

*Which one is this,* he thought again, and this time his mouth almost formed it, and he looked away, at the notebook, at the pen, at anything.

* * *

The viewing room was cold in the way funeral parlors are cold — not by thermostat but by covenant, a coldness agreed upon by everyone who enters. The casket stood open at the center of the room, half-mooned in lamplight, and the body lay inside it wearing Gideon's face.

Gideon had prepared himself. He had reviewed his photographs on the drive over, all forty-one, zooming in until the pixels broke apart and the face dissolved into gray weather. He had told himself: *You have seen it. You know it. It cannot surprise you a second time.*

It surprised him a second time.

Because the photographs were evidence, and evidence is flat, and this was not flat. This was a face with depth and blood-shadow and the particular slackness of a jaw that had been set by a stranger's hands. This was his own face doing the one thing his face had never done: lying still while someone else looked at it.

He raised his phone and photographed it. Forty-two.

"Mr. Whitlock." Voss's voice came from the doorway, and it had changed — thinner, tighter. "Sir, I have to ask you not to do that."

"It's my brother."

"It's — " Voss stopped. Gideon watched him stop, watched the sentence die in his mouth like something drowned. "It's customary to respect the deceased."

Gideon wrote it down. *Customary to respect the deceased. He almost said something else. What did he almost say?*

He moved along the casket, photographing from angles. The scar, three centimeters. The left ear, whose lobe — he checked his own in the black reflection of the viewing room window — whose lobe was attached the same way, that small fold like a page turned down to mark a place. The hands, folded on the breastbone, and here he leaned close, closer than grief would bring a man, closer than anything but obsession, and photographed the fingernails. There was a crescent of dirt under the right index finger. Dark, reddish earth. The kind of earth that comes from deep down, from under things, from places that have not seen light in fifty years.

*Ambrose died at the county hospital. Ambrose died of a heart attack in a bed with clean sheets. Why does the dead man have grave dirt under his nails?*

He wrote it down. His hand was shaking and he wrote it down anyway, because that was the whole point, that was the discipline, that was the only church he had ever belonged to: *if it is written, it happened; if it is measured, it is real; if I can hold the evidence in my hand, then the world must eventually agree to be the world.*

His mother had called it a sickness once, gently, the way you name a deformity in a child you love. *You write everything down, Gideon. You'll wear the pages out before you wear yourself out.* But she had also, he remembered — he *thought* he remembered — she had also been the one who bought him his first notebook, a red one with a broken clasp, and told him: *Then write it down, sweetheart, so it can't be taken from you.*

Taken from you.

He had not thought of that in years. He wrote it down that he had not thought of it in years.

* * *

*The director's recollection, fragmentary:*

His mother died in March. No. That wasn't — his mother died in March, and the notebook man's mother — he had a note somewhere, the intake form, *mother: deceased, name illegible* — and the name had been illegible because the ink had smeared, and the ink had smeared because the paper had been damp, and Harlan had stood in the prep room holding that form and thinking: *damp. The form was damp. Where did the damp come from,* and then the lights had flickered and he had decided, professionally, deliberately, with forty years of discipline, to think about something else.

He thought about something else now. He thought about the notebook.

There was something wrong with the notebook. Not the notebook itself — a plain black Moleskine, the kind students use — but the *fact* of it, the way it sat in the man's hands like a shield, like a weapon, like a thing that had been handed down. And when the man wrote in it, the pen made a sound Harlan could hear from across the room, a small dry scratching, and each scratch seemed to take something out of the air. The room got quieter with every word. The hum of the fluorescents faded. The traffic on Route 9 vanished. It was like watching a man pray, except that prayers fill a room, and this was draining it.

*Stop it,* Harlan told himself. *He's a grieving twin. They're strange, twins. Everyone knows twins are strange.*

But he kept looking at the pen. And the pen kept scratching. And the room kept getting quieter, and Harlan Voss, who had stood at four hundred and eleven gravesides and never once been afraid, found that his hands had gone cold.

* * *

Flashback — or what Gideon believed was a flashback, though lately he had begun to suspect that his memories had been handled by someone, arranged like flowers in a vase he didn't own:

The study door was oak, and it was always locked, and it was the only locked door in the house. Everything else in the Whitlock house stood open — doors ajar, drawers half out, their father's papers lying face-up on tables like a man with nothing to hide — everything except that one room, where Edmund Whitlock went every evening at seven and did not come out until the boys were asleep.

Ambrose and Gideon, seven years old, sitting on the hallway floor with their backs against the wainscoting, listening.

"What do you think he does in there?" Ambrose whispered.

"Writes," Gideon said. "He's a writer."

"He doesn't type. You can't hear a typewriter."

"Then he writes with a pen."

"With a pen," Ambrose repeated, and laughed, very quietly, the way they laughed — one sound split between two mouths. "With a pen for three hours."

They had a game they played on those nights, a silent game, because sound carried in that hallway the way sound carries over water. The game was called *What's Behind the Door,* and the rules were that you couldn't say anything, you could only point. Ambrose pointed at the floor: something under the house. Gideon shook his head and pointed at the wall: something *in* the house. Ambrose pointed at Gideon. Gideon pointed at Ambrose. Then they both pointed at each other at the same time, and that was the end of the game, always, because neither of them could stop laughing, and the laughter was the only loud thing they were ever allowed to be.

Their father had a rule about the twins. He said it once, at dinner, when Gideon was eight: "You two will be quiet about each other." Not *of* each other. *About* each other. And their mother had set down her fork very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and said, "Edmund, they're children," and Edmund Whitlock had looked at her — Gideon remembered the look, he was almost sure he remembered the look, he had the *shape* of the look — and said, "They're mine."

After that, the game stopped. After that, Ambrose and Gideon were quiet about each other in a way that outlasted childhood, that followed them into adulthood like a dog that wouldn't be put out. They loved each other. They loved each other enormously and wordlessly, the way you love someone you've agreed not to discuss.

And now one of them was in a casket wearing both their faces, and the other one was writing it all down, and Gideon could not remember — he pressed his palm flat against the notebook page and could not remember — which of them had pointed first.

* * *

"Mr. Voss," Gideon said, back in the office, "I want to ask you about the identification."

"Ask."

"On Wednesday. When I identified the body. Did I seem — " He stopped. He had rehearsed this question in the car, four versions of it, and now that it was time he heard how all four sounded: like a madman. He chose the fifth version, the one he hadn't rehearsed. "Did anything about that day stay with you?"

Voss looked at him for a long time. The fluorescents hummed. Outside, a hearse went by on Route 9, slow, even though it was empty, as if the building itself insisted on processions.

"Stay with me," Voss said.

"Something you can't explain."

The director's hands came apart on the desk and then, as if they'd been caught misbehaving, folded themselves back together. "I've been in this profession a long time, Mr. Whitlock. I've learned that most things explain themselves eventually. The body explains itself. The family explains itself. Grief explains itself, eventually, though it takes its time." He paused. "Your brother is explaining himself very slowly."

"Then he's not finished."

"No," Voss said. "I don't believe he is."

Gideon wrote it down. *Not finished.* And under it, because the pen was moving now and he could not stop it, because the notebook was the only thing in the world that agreed with him: *He knows. He won't say it. Nobody will say it. The whole world is a room full of people agreeing on a story, and I am the only one who read a different book.*

"Mr. Voss. The dirt under his fingernails."

Voss went still. Not the professional stillness from before — a different kind, the stillness of an animal that has heard something in the brush. "What dirt?"

"Under the right index finger. Dark soil. I photographed it. Forty-two photographs, if you'd like — "

"I'd like you to stop." It came out fast and hard, and then Voss seemed as surprised by it as Gideon was. He passed a hand across his mouth. "I'm sorry. That was — forgive me. It's been a long week."

"It's the notebook," Gideon said quietly. "Isn't it. It's something about the notebook."

Voss did not answer. Voss looked at the notebook the way a man looks at a well he has been told not to look into.

*The director's recollection, fragmentary, the last one he would ever permit himself:*

His grandmother had kept a book. That was the thing, that was the thing rising in him now like water into a basement, his grandmother in her chair by the window with a ledger open on her knees, writing in it every evening, and when he was small he'd asked her what she wrote and she'd said, *names,* and he'd said, *whose names,* and she'd closed the book and looked at him with an expression he had spent fifty years forgetting and was now, horribly, remembering, and she'd said:

*The ones who are owed.*

And the pen had made that sound. That same small dry scratching. And the room had gotten quieter.

* * *

Gideon walked out of Holloway & Voss at 11:52 a.m. with twelve pages of notes and the growing, glacial understanding that he was alone. Not lonely — alone, in the way a man is alone on a raft. The funeral director had agreed the body was Ambrose. The hospital records agreed. The dental records agreed. The signature agreed. The entire world had signed its name to a story, in identical handwriting, and the handwriting was his own.

He sat in his car in the funeral home lot and reviewed his notes, and it was while scrolling through the photographs that he found it.

Forty-two photographs. He had taken forty-two. He counted them.

There were forty-three.

The last one he had no memory of taking. It was of the viewing room, shot from the doorway, wide angle — but the casket in it was empty. Not made-up, not closed. Empty, the satin trough of it exposed, and standing beside the casket, facing the camera, was a figure he recognized with a recognition that preceded thought, that lived in the spine and not the mind:

Ambrose. Solid, warm-toned, alive, in the clothes he'd been buried in. And he was looking directly into the lens, and his mouth was moving, mid-word, and though the photograph of course held no sound, Gideon's mind supplied it instantly, in his brother's voice, in the voice they had once split between two mouths:

*You're writing down the wrong one.*

Gideon sat in the parked car with the engine off until the cold came up through the floor. Then he did the only thing he knew how to do. He opened the notebook to a fresh page, and his hand was steady, and he wrote:

*Day 3. The dead man is taking photographs of me.*
