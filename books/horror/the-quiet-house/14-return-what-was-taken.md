## 14. Return What Was Taken

The journal's last pages were stuck together, and I had to steam them apart over the kettle in the kitchen, which felt obscene — like performing a séance with domestic appliances. The paper had fused along the right margin, as though Edmund had wept on it, or spilled something, or pressed it shut with his thumb while the ink was still wet, sealing his own confession the way he'd sealed everything else in this house: from the inside.

I documented the process. Of course I did. Photograph of the journal on the counter, kettle steam rising past the lens. Photograph of the pages, half-separated, showing a single legible line: *the equinox*. Photograph of my own hand, shaking badly enough that the image blurred.

I have learned, over these weeks, that documentation is the only prayer I know. You write the thing down and the thing has to stay where you wrote it. The world has to honor the record. The world has been honoring no such thing.

* * *

Dana called at six in the morning, her voice scraped raw, and told me what Marcus had heard through the door seam the night before — the child's voice, thin as paper, counting down. Not numbers. Seasons. *When the leaves finish falling, the door shuts, and everything inside it shuts with it.*

"Everything inside it," Dana repeated. "That's us, Gideon. Me and Marcus are inside it. Have been since we stepped through, even when we came back out. Marcus says he can feel it — like a hook behind his sternum. He says the house reels him in a little more every night. Last night he woke up standing in the kitchen, facing the cellar stairs, and he doesn't remember getting there."

"And the way out?"

"The child says there's only one. The original goes back to the living world — carried out, walked out, whatever — and the copies surrender their claim. His words. *The copies surrender their claim.*" She paused. "Gideon. You know what a claim is. It's not a body. It's not a name. It's the whole — the whole right to have existed. If you surrender it—"

"I've read the journal, Dana."

"Then you know what I'm saying."

"I know what you're saying." I looked out the kitchen window at the maples, already going copper at the edges, the leaves letting go one at a time with a patience that felt like mockery. "The equinox is nine days away."

* * *

I took the journal into the study — Edmund's study, the room I'd been avoiding since the wall gave up its secret — and I read the final pages by daylight, because I could not have read them by lamplight. Some part of me believed daylight was a kind of witness. That if the sun was on the page, the words couldn't be as bad as they were going to be.

The sun did nothing. The words were worse.

---

*September 19th, 1974. The doctors are unanimous and the doctors are wrong. My son did not drown. My son is not dying. My son is whole and warm in the room beneath the garden and I will not bury him. I have buried a wife and I have buried a father and I will not do it a third time. God took His share. He will not take mine.*

---

I sat with that for a long while. The drowning. Ambrose and I had never been told — or we had been told and it had been taken back out of us, scooped from our childhoods like pulp from a fruit, because there was no room in my memory for a day at the water, any water, no lake, no pond, no river, not once in all those summers. I had catalogued my own gaps two nights ago, sitting at this same desk with the inventory notebook open, ranking my memories the way a man ranks his possessions before a fire. What could I afford to lose. What had already been lost without my consent.

The water was a gap. My mother's face was a gap. I had a photograph of her and I could describe the photograph perfectly — the blue dress, the crooked collar, the light behind her left shoulder — and I could not, for the life of me, make the photograph move. She was a still image in a house of moving men. Edmund had fed her to the thing under the garden, or fed us to it, and kept the rind.

---

*September 22nd. The rite requires two vessels. One cannot hold a whole child; the wholeness breaks it. But two can divide it between them, and neither will know what he carries. They will be brothers. They will love each other. That was not the design — that was the grace of it, or the cruelty, I no longer distinguish.*

*The original remains below. He must be sustained. A whole child, awake behind a door, cannot simply be left. He feeds on what is given freely, and so I have given freely, and I find that what I give I do not miss. This is the mercy of the method. You can amputate anything if you do it before you look at the wound.*

---

*Before you look at the wound.* I read that line four times. My father had written it about himself, about the surgery he performed on his own heart, and I understood — with the slow, sick click of a lock finally turning — what the jars in the cellar were. Not trophies. Not fuel. A man who could not bear to feel what he had done, so he removed the feeling and stored it in glass, and went on performing fatherhood for thirty years like an actor who has burned his own script and memorized someone else's.

And we had loved him. That was the part I kept circling back to, the part that made my documentation useless, because no photograph could hold it. We had loved him. Two copies of a drowned boy, walking around his house, kissing his cheek, and every ounce of that love had been poured into the jars and the man had felt nothing and we had never known.

---

*October 3rd. It is done. The door is bolted. I have written the date on the inside of my wrist so that I will remember this day, because I have arranged not to remember the others.*

*Let the record show — let some record show, if any record can survive me — that I did not do this to be cruel. I did it because I could not carry a small coffin. I have seen them carried. I have seen the size of them against a man's chest, how the pallbearers have to walk slowly so the box doesn't look like what it is.*

*I did it to avoid burying a son.*

*I have instead buried three.*

---

There it was. The confession, entire, in his own cramped hand. *Buried three people instead.* The original child behind the door. And the two boys walking around upstairs who were never born, who were carved, who were *issued* — and who would one day, one of them, be lowered into the ground wearing the other one's face.

I set the journal down. I picked it up. I documented the page, front and back, flash and no flash, and my hands were steady now, which frightened me more than shaking had. There is a calm that comes when the horror finishes arriving and simply lives in you, a tenant that pays no rent.

* * *

I brought the pages to the door.

The excavation site had become something else over the past week — not a construction trench but a vigil. Dana had the crew's equipment staged in neat rows like an altar. Marcus wouldn't work within ten feet of the opening. The steel door lay back against the bedrock, bolts sheared, hinges screaming whenever the wind moved it, and the dark beyond it was not the dark of a cellar. Cellars are dark the way a closed eye is dark. This was dark the way the inside of a closed mouth is dark.

I knelt at the seam and read Edmund's confession aloud. I don't know who I was reading it to. The child. The house. Myself. Dana stood behind me with her arms crossed, and Marcus sat on a spoil pile with his head between his knees, and when I finished, the silence had a texture to it, a listening quality.

Then, from inside, faint as a voice through a wall in a hotel: *"He wrote it down."*

"Yes," I said.

*"He wrote it down and he lived thirty more years and he died in his bed."* The voice was a child's and it was not. There were strata in it, like tree rings — a boy's pitch with decades packed inside it, compressed. *"You read it and your hands went steady. I heard your heart. It went steady. Do you know why mine doesn't?"*

I didn't answer. There was no answer that wasn't an insult.

*"Nine days,"* the child said. *"Then the door shuts and it doesn't open from either side. Bring me up, or everything in here is a grave with the lid on at last."* A pause, and when the voice came again it was smaller, and worse. *"Or don't. I've been in here fifty years. I know how to be a grave. I taught the house everything it knows."*

* * *

Dana walked me back to the house across the scarred lawn, and halfway there she said, quietly, "You understand what it costs."

"The copies surrender their claim."

"That's what it said. And you've read the journal. You know what you are, Gideon. You've known since chapter — since the wall." She corrected herself oddly, as if she'd been about to use a word from a different sentence, and I let it go. "If the original walks out whole, there's no room left for the copies. Not dead. *Unwritten.* You won't experience it. That's the part I can't—"

"Dana."

"—can't get past. I've pulled men out of collapsed trenches. They were scared, but they were *there*, the whole time, all the way down. This is different. This is you agreeing to have never—"

"Dana." I stopped walking. The maples went on letting go of their leaves behind me, one at a time, patient as ever. "I found the gaps in my inventory two nights ago. My mother's face. Every summer I ever lived. The water — there was water, wasn't there? There was a day at the water and I'm not allowed to have it."

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. The journal had answered.

"I have been unwritten already," I said. "In pieces. For fifty years, a spoonful at a time, so the thing under the garden could stay warm. Edmund started the account and the house kept drawing on it. I am a man standing in a room where the floor has been quietly removed board by board, and I have been calling the remaining boards *myself*." I looked at her. "The question isn't whether I surrender the claim. The question is whether I ever held one."

"That's not true," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and I loved her a little for the crack. "Gideon. The notebooks. Fourteen of them in the wall. Nobody *made* you document. That's yours. That was never in a jar. Whatever he carved out of you, the watching, the writing, the needing the world to keep its word — that's yours, and it's the most human thing I've ever—"

She stopped. Over my shoulder, her eyes went wide.

I turned.

Ambrose was standing on the porch.

He had been dead for three weeks. I had looked at his body in its casket wearing my own face. And here he stood in the late-morning light, solid, warm-looking, dressed in the gray sweater I'd last seen him in at Christmas, and he was smiling at me with an expression I recognized from the inside — the smile I wore in photographs.

"Little brother," he said, though we had been born eleven minutes apart, and the endearment had always run the wrong way, and I had never once questioned it until this moment. "You've been reading Father's journal."

"Ambrose." My voice came out level. Documented, even. "You're dead."

"I'm *buried*," he said, and came down the porch steps, and the grass did not bend under him, though I only noticed that afterward, in the photographs, in the record, in the prayer I would write down too late. "There's a difference. Come inside. We need to talk about the equinox — and about which one of us the house is actually asking for."

Behind the steel door, faint through fifty feet of earth and fifty years of silence, the child began to laugh — or to weep; at that depth, at that age, I have come to believe they are the same sound.
