## 15. Equinox Eve

The lights failed the way a held breath fails — gradually, and then all at once.

Gideon stood at the kitchen window and watched it happen down the length of Hollow Creek Road. The streetlamps didn't go dark; they went *thin*. Their orange halos shrank to coins, to embers, to the memory of light, and the houses along the road dimmed with them, windows going the color of old tea. Somewhere a transformer groaned like a man asked to carry something too heavy for too long. The brown-out rolled up the valley in both directions at once, as though the town were being slowly closed like a book.

Behind him, the house exhaled.

He had stopped pretending he didn't feel it. A cold that moved rather than sat — a long, tidal out-breath from the walls, from the floor, from the bedrock under the floor, carrying the smell of 1974 out of the open seam in the earth: fresh paint, pipe smoke, cut grass, and under all of it something sweeter and worse, like a nursery that had never been aired.

*Tomorrow,* he wrote in the notebook, because the notebook was the only prayer he trusted. *Equinox. The door closes. Everything inside it stays inside it.*

His hand was not steady. He had stopped pretending about that, too.

* * *

AMBROSE

I felt the lights go out the way a man feels a fever break — a loosening, a subtraction. I was standing in the upstairs hall, in front of the mirror that had hung there since before either of us was born, and I was watching myself fail to hold.

The brown-out had nothing to do with the power company. I understood that the way I understood the scar on my chin, the way I understood the shape of Gideon's handwriting: not by learning but by being. The town's lights were dimming because the house was drawing. Drawing everything toward tomorrow, toward the seam, toward the moment when the door would shut and the accounting would be final. And I was on the wrong side of the ledger.

I raised my hand. The mirror raised its hand a half-second late.

I could see the wallpaper through my wrist. Not faintly. Not in a trick of the light. The pattern — small blue birds on a cream field, the same paper that had been in the nursery, the same paper that was in *every* room now if you looked at the right hour — showed through my skin like a photograph held up to a window.

I had always been more echo than man. I think I had known it in the way you know a song is a cover version: everything in the right place, everything note-perfect, and something underneath missing. The weight. The grain. Gideon had the grain. Gideon had the weight, the scar tissue of real days, the calluses. I had the performance of a life, and the performer was tired, and the house was turning up its lights to show me so.

Downstairs, a floorboard spoke. Gideon, moving toward the stairs. Toward me, or away from me. With my brother it had always been impossible to say which.

* * *

GIDEON

He found Ambrose in the nursery.

Not the guest room Ambrose had claimed on his first night back — the one with the good mattress, the one he'd joked about, *finally a house with a bed that doesn't hate my spine.* The nursery. The room at the end of the hall that their mother had kept locked for thirty years and that had stood open since Gideon arrived, door swinging in whatever air moved through this house, which was not the air of weather.

Ambrose stood at the window with his back to the door, and the last browned light of the town came through him and laid his shadow on the floor — but the shadow was wrong, too solid, darker than the dusk allowed, as if the light were passing through the man and pooling in the ghost.

"You're fading," Gideon said.

"You're observant," Ambrose said. "It was always your worst quality."

The crib was still there. Edmund had never sold it. It stood against the wall with its rail down like an opened mouth, and someone — not Gideon, not Ambrose, not the crew — had made the small mattress up with a sheet. White. Fresh. Waiting.

"I brought something," Gideon said.

"I know what you brought." Ambrose turned. His face was still his face — their face — but it had gone the texture of paper held too long against a damp wall. "You've been carrying it around for three days like a grenade. You think I haven't felt it? It's the oldest thing either of us has, Gid. It hums."

Gideon took it out of his shirt pocket. Not a photograph. There were no photographs of that day — Edmund had seen to that, Edmund who documented everything else, who kept journals and measurements and jars, who recorded the whole machinery of his crime except the one afternoon that had made it necessary. What Gideon had was a memory, and he had carried it as a weight in the chest, and tonight he had written it out in full, four pages, in the notebook, in ink, because written things could be handed over. Written things could be *given*.

The day at the quarry. The ice that wasn't ice. The sound their mother made — no. The sound their mother *would have made*. That was the thing. That was the seam in everything. Gideon had carried the memory of his mother screaming, and Ambrose had carried it too, and they had carried it for fifty years like two men carrying one corpse, and only in the last week, reading the journal, reading the marginalia in his own hand, had Gideon understood: there was only one memory. It had been split. Edmund had split *everything*, and the halves had lived two lives believing themselves whole.

"You go first," Ambrose said.

* * *

AMBROSE

He held his oldest memory out — not the quarry one, the other one. The one from before. The one I had been hoarding like a dragon hoards the one true thing in a cave of copies.

I had it because he'd given it to me without knowing. Or the house had. Or Edmund's ritual had misfired in some small, human way, the way rituals do, the way fathers do. It didn't matter anymore whose fault the distribution was. What mattered was that Gideon Whitlock did not remember our mother's voice, and I did.

He had discovered it in chapter — no. In the journal. He had discovered it in the journal, and I had watched him come apart in the kitchen over the lost thing, turning his pockets out, checking his own head for the hole, and I had stood in the doorway and said nothing, because saying it would have meant admitting I was the thief, and admitting I was the thief would have meant admitting what I was.

The nursery smelled of talc and varnish and cold stone. The house was breathing around us, that long tidal exhale, and every breath took a little more of me with it. I could see the crib's rail through my own forearm now. I could see the *wallpaper* through the crib.

"Gideon." My voice came out wrong. Thin. A voice played at the wrong speed. "Before you do whatever you came here to do. Before the accounting. I want you to have it back."

"You don't get to give me my own—"

"It was never only yours." I heard the charm come into my voice, the old machinery, the thing people liked about me, the thing that had gotten me through fifty years of being a copy of a man — and I hated it, hated it the way you hate your own reflexes, and I let it fall. "That's what I came to say. That's the whole confession. It was never only yours. Nothing was. I have been living on your leavings since before I had a name."

The brown-out deepened. Through the window, the town was nearly dark now, just a few stubborn lights holding on down in the valley, and above them the sky did the thing the sky had been doing all week — the stars too sharp, too many, as if the atmosphere had thinned and the house's cold had climbed all the way up to heaven.

"Say it plainly," Gideon said. "For once. Plainly."

"I'm the echo," I said. "You're the voice. And tomorrow the door shuts, and the voice goes inside to fix what Father broke, and the echo—" I looked at my hands. Birds through skin. "The echo gets to find out what's on the other side of a recording."

* * *

GIDEON

"No."

The word came out of him before thought, and he was glad of it, because thought had been failing him all week. Thought had been a leaking boat. But *no* was solid. *No* was a floorboard that held.

"You don't get to narrate this," Gideon said. "You don't get to write your own ending and hand it to me like a script. That's the family disease, Ambrose. Father wrote our ending in 1974 and we've been *acting* it ever since — you in your role, me in mine, both of us so well-rehearsed we call it a life. I'm not doing it. Not tonight. Not with you."

Ambrose laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle like ice over water. "Then what are you doing? You came to the nursery with four pages of the quarry in your pocket. I can hear them humming. You came to give it back. To *me*? To the door? To the boy behind it? Which charity were you planning, Gideon? Because they all cost the same. You give the memory, you lose the memory. That's the exchange rate. Pete already paid. You've seen Pete."

Gideon had seen Pete. Pete sat in the crew's trailer most evenings now with a cup of tea he never drank, smiling at everyone with a face like a house with the lights on and nobody home, and when Dana said his daughter's name — *Rosie* — Pete would nod politely, the way you nod at a stranger's grief, and the nod was the worst thing Gideon had ever witnessed, and he had witnessed a corpse wearing his own face.

"I came to trade," Gideon said. "Not to give. To trade. You have something of mine. I have something of yours. And tomorrow one of us walks through that door and one of us—" he stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and he refused to look over.

"One of us doesn't," Ambrose finished, gently, cruelly, kindly. "Say it. We're twins. We finish each other's—"

"Don't."

"—sentences. We always did. Even when we hated each other we did it. Especially then."

The house exhaled again, longer this time, and the nursery's temperature fell through cold into something older than cold, and the sheet on the crib mattress stirred though there was no wind, and somewhere below them — below the floor, below the cellar, below the bedrock — the steel door groaned in its frame like a throat clearing before speech.

Gideon took the four folded pages and held them out.

* * *

AMBROSE

He wouldn't take them with his hands. I understood why. Hands make it real; hands are how you touch a thing into the world. So I didn't reach with my hands either.

We stood there like two men at a border crossing, each holding out papers to the other, neither crossing, and the light in the room went from dusk to nothing, and the only illumination left was the faint blue-gray of the wallpaper's birds, which had begun — I want to be honest, I am done lying, lying was the only craft I had and the house has repossessed it — the birds had begun to move. Faintly. The way things move in dreams. A flutter of wings printed on a wall, fifty years from the print run.

"Gideon," I said. "Tell me the truth. Not the notebook truth. The other one."

"Which truth?"

"The one you've been writing around for twenty-two chapters of your life." I smiled. It cost me. My face was going thin and smiles take surface area. "Do you want me to survive tomorrow?"

And my brother — my original, my source, my better half in every sense that mattered and several that didn't — my brother stood in our dead father's nursery holding four pages of the worst day of our childhood and said:

"I want *us* to survive tomorrow. Both of us. Whole. And there's exactly one way that happens and it isn't the way Father designed and it isn't the way the door wants, and it requires you to stop performing your death long enough to help me build the alternative."

I felt something I hadn't felt since the funeral home. Since before. Something with weight. Something with grain.

"Which requires what?" I asked.

"Which requires the trade," he said. "Right now. Before midnight. Before the house gets any stronger. Your memory of her voice for my memory of the quarry. Freely given, both directions. Not a payment. Not a feeding. A *trade between equals* — because that's the one thing Edmund never tried, Ambrose. That's the one thing the house has no mechanism for. He took. The door takes. Pete paid. Everyone in this family's history either takes or pays, and I am so *tired*—" his voice broke, and I heard our mother in it, or the shape where she should have been, "—I am so tired of being a transaction."

* * *

GIDEON

Ambrose crossed the room.

He moved like a man wading — the house's cold was against him now, dragging at him, and Gideon could see the effort it cost the fading twin to be *solid*, to put one foot in front of the other in a body that was becoming a rumor. But he crossed. He stood close enough that Gideon could see the scar on his chin, their scar, the one from the quarry fence when they were nine, the one the corpse in the casket had worn, the one that proved nothing and everything.

"If I give it back," Ambrose said, "I'll lose her voice. I'll be like Pete. I'll look at your face tomorrow and know I loved you and not know why."

"Yes."

"And you'll have the quarry back. Both halves of it. The ice, the crack, the sound she would have made. All of it. You'll carry the whole weight."

"Yes."

"And then tomorrow you walk into that door and give yourself to the boy behind it, piece by piece, freely, one memory at a time — and the last thing you'll surrender, the very last, will be her singing. And I'll be out here. Alive. Whole. Remembering everything you gave up and none of why it mattered." Ambrose's eyes were wet, and the tears fell *through* his cheek and reformed below his jaw, and neither of them remarked on it, because remarking would have made it real. "That's your plan. That's the alternative to Father's design. You've built a machine where I live and you don't."

"It's not a machine. It's a choice."

"They're the same thing in this house."

"Then let's be the first ones in this house who chose," Gideon said.

The clock in the hall — Edmund's clock, the one that had run fifty years without winding — began to strike eleven.

* * *

AMBROSE

I gave him her voice first.

I didn't know how to do it — there was no ritual, no jar, no journal instruction; Edmund had never documented a gift, only thefts — so I did the only thing left. I closed my eyes and I *told* him. I stood in the nursery of the quiet house on the last night of my existence and I described, in detail, in the plain words he had asked for, our mother singing.

The song about the lighthouse. The one she made up, the one that had never been written down, the one that existed in exactly two heads in all the world and was about to exist in one. I gave him the tune the only way tune can travel — humming, my voice going thin and wrong-speed, the notes coming out of a throat that was forgetting how throats work — and I watched it land in him. I watched my brother's face change as the hole in him filled. I watched fifty years of a missing thing come home, and he wept the way you weep when a bone sets: pain and gratitude in the same instant, indistinguishable.

And as it left me I felt it go — not like Pete's loss, not a wound, not a theft. Like a bird leaving a hand. Open fingers. The choice was the whole difference. The choice was everything.

"Your turn," I said, when I could speak.

He unfolded the four pages and did not read them. He knew them. He held them out over the crib — over the made-up mattress, the waiting sheet — and let them fall, and where they landed they were gone, absorbed into the house's accounting, and then he closed his eyes and gave me the quarry.

The ice that wasn't ice. The crack like a shot. The cold climbing our legs. And her — her face turning toward us from the shore, her mouth opening, the sound that never got made because Edmund got there first, because Edmund *always* got there first — and the sound, when Gideon handed it across, was not a scream. That was the thing I had never known. That was the half I had been missing for fifty years, the half that made my copy of the day unlivable, that had made me the frightened one, the charming one, the one who ran. Her mouth opened and what came out — what had come out, what had been there all along on the other side of Edmund's scissors — was our names.

Both of them. Together. The way you call twins.

*Gideon. Ambrose.*

One breath. One mother. One word with two halves.

* * *

GIDEON

They stood in the nursery holding each other's worst day, and it was not lighter. That was the lie of sharing he had half believed — that a grief split is a grief halved. It wasn't. It was a grief *completed*, and a completed grief is heavier than a broken one, the way a finished wall bears more than a ruin. But it was *theirs*. Both of theirs. Whole in both of them, for the first time since 1974, and the house did not like it. The house exhaled so hard the windows shivered in their frames, and down in the valley the last of the town's lights gave up, and the brown-out became a blackout, and the dark that came was not the dark of night but the dark of a held breath about to end.

Ambrose pulled back. He was nearly transparent now; Gideon could see the window's frame through his brother's chest, could see the stars, too many stars, sharp as nails.

"It's starting," Ambrose said. "I can feel the door from here. It's not waiting for midnight, Gid. It's pulling. It's been pulling all week and now it's pulling *me*."

"Then we go down together."

"To the door."

"To the door," Gideon said. "Before it closes. While it's still open. Because there's something down there I need you to see, and something down there that needs to see *you* — the echo and the original, face to face, before the accounting. And Ambrose—" he took his brother's hand, and the hand was cold and going thinner, and he held it the way you hold a bird, firmly enough to keep, gently enough to keep alive, "—whatever happens down there. Whatever the house offers. Whatever Father's voice sounds like coming out of that dark. We do it as a trade. We do it freely. That's the only weapon we have and it has to be both of us or it's nothing."

The hall clock finished striking eleven.

And then, without being touched, without winding, without fifty years of permission — it began to strike twelve.
