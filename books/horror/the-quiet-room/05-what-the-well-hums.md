## 5. What the Well Hums

Dez had a rule about sounds, and the rule was this: every sound was a machine, and every machine could be found.

Water in a pipe had a pitch. Air in a duct had a rasp. Settling stone had a tick, like a cooling engine, patient and mineral. In five years of crawling under the city with Ivo and the others, Dez had never met a sound he couldn't trace to its source — a junction box humming at sixty cycles, a mainline shuddering under the weight of the 2 a.m. freight, a broken flange whistling in a wall like a man with a secret. He carried a stethoscope in his pack for exactly this purpose, a real one, bought from a medical surplus shop, its diaphragm worn soft from pressing against concrete and iron and the occasional very confused rat.

The hum did not obey the rule.

He'd been hearing it since the second hour of the descent, back when the passages were still the ones he knew — the ones Ivo had mapped in careful graphite on waterproof paper, the ones with names. Dez named things too. It was how he fixed them. You couldn't repair what you refused to call by name.

But past the edge of Ivo's map, past the passages that felt freshly dug and impossibly old at once, the hum had started, and it would not resolve into a machine.

It wasn't electrical. Dez had held the stethoscope against the wall for a full ten minutes, eyes closed, counting. Electricity hummed at frequencies you could do math to — fifty or sixty hertz, or the harmonics stacked on top like plates on a shelf. This was slower. This was the pace of something at rest. He timed it against his own pulse, and then against Hal's breathing, and it matched neither, and it matched both, and he hated that.

"It's a pump," Hal said, somewhere behind him in the dark. Hal's voice always sounded like it was filing a complaint. "Old infrastructure. There's pumping machinery under half this city that the utilities themselves have lost track of. We're hearing a pump through forty feet of limestone."

"Forty feet of limestone would eat a pump sound alive," Dez said. "You wouldn't hear a pump through that. You'd feel it in your teeth if you were standing on it, maybe. This—" He tapped the wall. The hum didn't change. That was the other thing he hated about it. Machines changed when you touched them. They stuttered, they resonated, they answered. This just went on, low and even, like something breathing through stone. "This is coming up through everything. Like the whole rock is one speaker."

"Then the rock is one speaker," Hal said. "Geology does that. Caverns act as resonance chambers."

"Sure," Dez said, because arguing with Hal was like tightening a bolt that had been cross-threaded at the factory. You could keep turning, but all you'd do was strip it. He kept walking.

The passage narrowed and then opened, the way a throat opens, and the air changed. Dez felt it before he could name it — the pressure shifting against his eardrums, the faint cool smell of wet stone that hadn't been wet in a long time. His headlamp beam, which had been swallowed by the tunnel, suddenly had somewhere to go. It fell across a floor of packed earth, and then across a ring of iron.

* * *

The shaft was perhaps eight feet across, and it went down.

Dez's light couldn't find the bottom. That was the first thing. He swept the beam around the rim and down the inner curve, and the light went down and down and simply gave up, the way light does, thinning to a gray suggestion and then to nothing. He crouched at the edge and dropped a pebble over — a habit, a calibration, the way he tested any depth — and counted. One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

No sound of impact.

Six. Seven.

Nothing. Either the shaft was deep past the point where a pebble's tap could carry, or the bottom was soft, or—

"Old cistern," Hal said, arriving at his shoulder with the confidence of a man reading a label. Hal crouched, peered down, and nodded as if the shaft had confirmed his theory personally. "Nineteenth century, probably. The city's full of them. They'd sink a shaft to the water table, brick it up, draw water up by hand. When the mains came through, they abandoned them. There's probably forty feet of silt at the bottom. Your pebble landed in mud, that's all."

"Could be," Dez said.

It could be. That was the maddening thing. Dez had spent his whole life in the space between *could be* and *is*, and most of the time the space was small — a day's work, a replaced part, a tightened fitting. Most of the time the world, once you got your hands on it, turned out to be exactly as simple as it looked. A broken thing was a broken thing. You found the break, you fixed the break, the thing worked again. He had built his entire self around the reliability of that transaction.

He ran his light around the rim of the shaft, looking for the mechanism. If it was a cistern, there'd be a winch housing, or at least the bolt holes where one had been. There'd be a lip where a bucket rope had sawed into the stone. There'd be something.

There was the handholds instead.

They were cut into the inner curve of the shaft — iron rungs, or the stubs of them, driven into the brickwork at intervals a body could climb. Dez leaned out over the dark and followed them down as far as his light reached. They were worn. Not rusted-through, not snapped, but *worn*, the way stair treads wear, the way a door handle wears — the metal rubbed bright and thin along the top curve of each rung, polished by grip after grip after grip.

"Those are old," Priya said. She'd come up quiet, the way she always did, and was crouched on the far side of the rim with her camera already out, shooting the rungs without flash, her face lit blue by the little screen. "Look at the wear pattern. That's not decades. That's generations."

"Generations of what?" Hal said. "Municipal water inspectors?"

"Generations of hands," Priya said, and let the sentence sit there in the dark where everyone had to look at it.

Dez reached down and put his hand on the top rung. He didn't mean to. His hand just went, the way a hand goes to a railing on stairs. The metal was cool and smooth and slightly greasy with age, and it fit his grip the way a tool fits a grip, which is to say it fit better than it should have, as if the wear had been shaped by hands very like his own, over and over, until the rung had learned the shape.

He took his hand back and wiped it on his thigh without making a decision to.

* * *

It was on the third circuit of the rim that he saw it. He was doing what he always did — working the problem, circling it, looking for the seam — and he was halfway around, on the side away from the others, when his headlamp caught the far inner curve of the shaft at an angle, low, almost straight down.

The handholds were on the inside too.

Not the rungs. The rungs were on the near side, the climbing side, the side you'd descend and ascend by, and those made sense, those were cistern logic, maintenance access, a man going down to check the water level with a plumb line and a lamp. Dez could file those. Those had a category.

But on the opposite curve of the shaft, down in the dark below where his light wanted to go, there were more marks in the brickwork. Grooves. Long, shallow, parallel grooves, four of them, spaced the way fingers space, worn into the brick itself — not cut, *worn*, abraded into the hard old clay by something dragging against it, over and over, for a very long time.

He lay flat on his stomach at the rim and hung his head and light down into the throat of the shaft, and the grooves ran down past the reach of his beam, four pale lines descending into the black like a ladder drawn by something that had no rungs to use.

Fingertips. The thought arrived whole and unwelcome, the way the true ones do. Fingertips, dragged down brick, for years, by someone on the inside trying to get out.

Or — and here his mind, which was a fixer's mind, which wanted the repairable answer — someone on the inside climbing *up*, and the grooves were from their hands, and—

No. He turned it over. He was good at turning things over. Grooves worn by climbing would be on the rung side. Grooves on the far side, four parallel lines, spaced like fingers — that was a person pressed flat against the curve of the shaft, reaching, scraping, sliding. That was a person with nothing to hold.

"Dez." Hal's voice, from above him. "You find something?"

Dez lay there a moment longer, his chin on cold brick, his light going down and down the four pale lines to where the dark took them. The hum was louder here. He could feel it in his sternum now, in the flat of his palms against the rim, in his teeth. It hadn't changed pitch the whole time they'd been in the chamber. Not once. Machines cycled. Machines rested. Machines had on and off, high and low, the whole vocabulary of working and failing.

This was just — on. The way a heart is on.

"Dez?"

"Yeah." He pushed himself up. His knees cracked. He looked at Hal, at Priya, at Ivo's face turned up toward him, waiting, trusting, and he made the choice he always made, the fixer's choice: he reported the finding and withheld the interpretation, because the interpretation wasn't finished, because he hadn't confirmed it, because a man doesn't call a thing by its name until he's sure of the name.

"Grooves on the inside wall," he said. "Far side. Worn, not cut. Could be tool marks. Could be rope wear."

"Rope wear," Hal said, relieved, latching on. "See, a cistern. They'd lower things on ropes. Buckets, brushes, a chain pump. Rope wear on the interior curve is textbook."

"Textbook," Dez agreed.

But he didn't move away from the rim. He stood there with his boot toe an inch from the edge, looking down into the dark, and he did the thing he hated most in the world, which was to hold two explanations in his hands at once and feel them weigh the same. Rope wear. Fingertips. Rope wear. Fingertips. The hum rose through the floor and into his boot soles and up through his shins like water finding its level.

"Dez." Ivo's voice, quiet. Ivo always knew. Five years of crawling together, and Ivo could read him the way Dez read a gauge — the needle twitching before the problem shows. "What is it?"

"Nothing I can fix," Dez said.

It came out before he could stop it, and he heard how it sounded, and he hated that too. Hal laughed, short and dismissive, already turning away to argue with Priya about brick dating. But Ivo didn't laugh. Ivo looked at him across the shaft, in the light of two headlamps, and waited.

"It's not rope wear," Dez said. "I know rope wear. I've pulled a thousand feet of rope through this city's guts. Rope wears a groove with a polish on the bottom and a fray-mark at the edges, and it's continuous, one line. This is four lines. Spaced." He held up his own hand, fingers spread, in the lamplight. "Like that."

The chamber went quiet except for the hum.

Hal opened his mouth. Dez watched him do it — watched the skepticism assemble itself behind Hal's face like scaffolding, watched the man reach for the categories, the cistern, the silt, the municipal inspectors — and for the first time in the years Dez had known him, Hal didn't fire. He looked down the shaft instead, at the four pale lines descending into the black, and his face did something careful and complicated, and he said nothing at all.

That silence scared Dez more than the grooves had.

* * *

They made camp in the chamber because the passage behind them had taken longer than planned and Ivo wouldn't push the group tired. Dez drew first watch, which suited him. He didn't much want to sleep down here anyway, though he'd never have said so.

He sat with his back against the brick, the stethoscope in his lap, and he gave in to the thing he'd been circling all evening. He fitted the earpieces in, leaned forward, and pressed the diaphragm flat against the rim of the shaft itself, against the worn brick, two inches from the drop.

The hum came up through the instrument and into his skull, and it was different through the stethoscope. Closer. Structured. He could hear layers in it now, the way you can hear layers in a choir when you finally get close enough — a low fundamental that never wavered, and above it, faint, other tones. Not harmonics. He knew harmonics. These moved. They drifted against each other the way voices drift when people are humming together and not quite in unison, correcting, sliding, settling.

He counted the layers. He couldn't help it. It was what his hands did when his mind was afraid — they counted.

Four.

He sat back. He took the earpieces out. The chamber was dark and the others were breathing in their bags, slow and even, and the hum went on beneath everything, patient as water, wearing its groove in the world.

*Could be* an aquifer, he told himself. *Could be* resonance, a chamber acting on a deep water source, the rock singing over the water table. *Could be* all of it. He was a fixer, not a priest. He didn't deal in what things meant. He dealt in what things did, and what this thing did was hum, and what hums do, eventually, is stop.

Except that this one hadn't. Not once. Not for the hours they'd been down here. And machines that never stopped weren't machines. They were something else, something with no category he owned, no part number, no manual, no break he could find and fix.

He looked down the shaft one more time before he woke Ivo for the second watch. His light found the four grooves, pale against the dark brick, descending past the reach of the beam.

And it was probably the angle, and it was probably the hour, and it was probably his own tired eyes making patterns the way tired eyes do — but the lowest groove he could see looked fresher than the others. Brighter. As if something had dragged down that curve of brick recently. As if something, somewhere far below in the dark, was still trying.

He woke Ivo and didn't say any of it. But he sat up through the rest of the watch anyway, with his back to the shaft, listening to the hum breathe through the stone, and for the first time in five years under the city, Dez did not know what he was listening to — and knew, with the certainty of a man who has fixed ten thousand things, that it was not broken.

It was working.

Whatever it was, whatever it was for, down there in the dark at the bottom of a shaft with no bottom — it was working. And somewhere past the chamber, further along the passage Ivo wanted to walk in the morning, the hum was coming from a direction that had a room attached to it. Dez could feel it in the floor, a faint gradient, like heat. Like a house.

Like a home with the lights left on, waiting for someone to come down.

He didn't sleep. In the morning, when Ivo asked him how he'd slept, Dez said fine, and picked up his pack, and followed the hum toward whatever had built it a room — and the four grooves in the shaft stayed behind him in the dark, going down.
