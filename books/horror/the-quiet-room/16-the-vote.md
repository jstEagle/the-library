## 16. The Vote

They climbed back up to the third landing — the one Ivo had named years ago, in the giddy early days of the mapping, *the parlor*, because the ceiling opened into a vaulted pocket where their voices used to echo like laughter in a big room. It was the farthest point from the Quiet Room they could reach and still see its light, a faint amber thread down the passage behind them, thin as wire. Ivo had chosen it deliberately. Whatever was said here, they would say it out of earshot of the well.

Or so Ivo told themselves. The hum came up through the stone anyway, fainter here, softened by distance, but present — a pulse beneath the floor, patient as a heart that had never once needed to hurry. Fifty-three years of patience. Eleven days left. Eight now, after Hal's wrench. Eight days, minus whatever the well decided to take next.

Dez got the lanterns hung first. He always did — three of them on hooks he'd installed himself over two visits, wired to a battery pack he'd carried down in pieces like a man smuggling a religion. Light made the parlor honest. It showed the water stain on the east wall shaped like a reaching hand, showed Priya's folding table already set up with her notebooks, showed all four of them — five, counting Ren at the edge of it — with faces scrubbed raw by what they'd seen below.

Hal sat on his pack. He hadn't taken it off since the wrench dropped. His hands were still shaking, fine tremors he kept trying to disguise by clasping them between his knees, and every few minutes his eyes went to the passage leading down, as if expecting the dark there to have moved closer while no one watched it.

Priya laid out her materials like a surgeon laying out instruments: pens, the good notebook, the backup notebook, a sheet of paper torn clean down the middle for ballots she didn't need yet but wanted ready. Order was how she prayed.

"Say it," Ivo said. "All of it. Nobody interrupts until everyone's said their piece."

Nobody spoke. The hum filled the space where words should have been, and Ivo understood, suddenly, why Edith Vesser had rehearsed her silences for half a century. In this place, silence wasn't empty. It was full. It was a held breath, and everything you said into it was something the thing below could breathe back.

"Hal," Ivo said. "You first."

* * *

"I shouldn't have dropped it." Hal's voice came out flat, stripped of its usual skepticism, which had always been less a philosophy than a coat he wore against exactly this kind of night. "I know that. You don't have to say it. I've been saying it to myself since the dust fell." He unclasped his hands, looked at them like they belonged to someone else. "But I'm going to tell you why I did it, and then I'm going to tell you what we do, and you're going to hate both."

Priya's pen hovered. She didn't write yet. Even she knew some things weren't for the record.

"I dropped the wrench because I didn't believe it," Hal said. "And I still don't believe it — not the way you all believe it, not with candles and covenants and a count on the wall. But I believe *something* is down there, and I believe it took a day off our lives when a wrench hit concrete, and I believe Edith Vesser's face when the tally changed was the realest thing I've seen in fifty-one years of being alive." He swallowed. "So here's my piece. We leave. Tonight. All of us. We seal the upper shaft on our way out — Dez can do it, Dez can do anything with steel and a torch — and we file the site as unstable, contaminated, whatever gets a fence around it inside a week. The city loves a reason to pour concrete. We give them one."

"And the children?" Ivo asked quietly.

"The children are fed, clothed, literate, and *loved*, Ivo. They're safer down there than most kids in this city are above it. You want to pull two kids who've never seen sky out of the only home they know, away from their mother and grandmother, eight days before their whole world drowns — and deliver them to what? Foster care? A press circus? You think the world outside is gentler than the world below?" Hal's voice cracked, and he hated it, Ivo could see him hating it. "I've spent my life not believing in things. Don't make me start believing in them by making me complicit in a kidnapping."

"It's not kidnapping if we ask," Dez said.

"They'll say no. The mother will say no. The grandmother will chain herself to the floor."

"Then we don't take them," Dez said simply. "We offer. There's a difference, Hal. You're describing a theft. I'm describing a door held open."

Hal laughed — one dry, awful note. "A door held open. That's what we're calling it."

"That's what it is."

* * *

Priya wrote for a long moment before she spoke, and when she did, her voice had the careful evenness she used when the material mattered too much to trust to feeling.

"I need to say the ugly part first, so nobody says it later like it's a knife." She looked around the circle. "Everything down there — the room, the family, the covenant, the count — exists in exactly one copy. In my head and my notebooks. If we walk away and seal it, and the well refills, and the Vessers drown or get drunk or whatever the word is for what happens to them — then the only true record of fifty-three human years is four notebooks in a duffel bag owned by a woman who rides motorcycles." Her pen tapped once. "Documentation isn't a luxury. It's the only resurrection on the table. If they won't come up, then let them be *known*. Let there be a record that says: these people lived, and they loved each other, and they kept faith with something terrible for nineteen thousand days, and here are their names."

"Edith will never allow it," Hal said.

"Edith has allowed more than you think. She let me copy the archive. Three hours, supervised, while Lorna watched me like a hawk and Wren asked me questions about typewriter ribbon. I have photographs of every page. What I don't have is time to do it right — cross-references, the ledger of offerings, the letters Tomas's father wrote to the well like it was a bank he trusted." Priya's voice tightened. "Eight days. I need six of them down there with paper, and I need the family to keep talking to me, and I need nobody—" her eyes flicked to Hal, and she didn't finish it, and she didn't have to.

"So your vote is stay and document," Ivo said.

"My vote is document *first*, decide *after*. Because I've read the archive, Ivo. All of it. And there's a page you haven't seen — the covenant's original text, from 1971, in Aldous Vesser's hand. The door opens outward once per generation. Once per *generation*, not once per lifetime. Which means the last opening was due sometime in the nineties. Which means either they missed it—" her pen stopped moving, "—or they chose to miss it. Or were made to miss it. Somebody in that family stood at an open door and turned around. I want to know who, and why, before any of us decides what those children owe."

The parlor was quiet except for the hum, and for a moment Ivo felt it listening to the question, turning it over the way the well turned over everything it was given.

* * *

Dez spoke last of the three, and he spoke the way he fixed things: without ceremony, hands moving as he talked, turning a carabiner over and over like the argument was a mechanism he could feel the flaw in.

"I've been thinking about doors," he said. "It's my job, sort of. Locks, hinges, load paths. A door is just a decision you can touch." He held up the carabiner. "This gate holds two kilonewtons. I know because I tested it, because I test everything, because things you don't test fail people. So let's test the options the same way.

"Option one, walk away and seal it. Fine. Steel holds. Concrete holds. But we're sealing eight people in with a deadline, on purpose, knowing what we know. That's not a door, that's a lid. And lids fail, Hal — you taught me that tonight. Your wrench was a lid failing. One noise, one day gone. Now imagine the city builds on top, and a pile driver starts hammering forty feet above the Quiet Room, and nobody down there knows the rules anymore because the people who knew are gone." He set the carabiner down. "Sealing it doesn't end the story. It just makes sure we're not in the chapter where it goes wrong.

"Option two, take the kids by force. I'll say this once: I love you, Ivo, and I will help you carry those children up every ladder if you order it. But I've watched Sable hold her mother's sleeve. Kids torn out of a home like that don't thank you. They spend their lives looking back down the hole. I've met people like that. Haven't you?"

He didn't wait for an answer, because they all had.

"Option three. We go back down tomorrow, and we put the choice in front of the family straight — not Edith's version, not the covenant's version, the whole truth, including what Lorna told you, Ivo. Including the drinking. Including the voices. Every adult in that room hears it all, and then chooses, together, with the children in the room or out of it, their call. And whoever wants out, we take out — through the tunnels, tonight, no waiting for the door, no waiting for permission from a well. Dez carries the little one. Priya navigates. Hal runs sweep. Ren—"

Dez stopped. They all stopped. Because Ren had not spoken, and the silence where his voice should have been had grown so large that the whole circle had unconsciously oriented itself around it, the way plants orient toward light.

Ren sat apart, at the lip of the landing where the parlor opened into the descending dark, one knee drawn up, his headlamp off, his face lit only by the spill from Dez's lanterns. He was staring down the passage — not at anything, Ivo realized, but *into* it, the way you stare into deep water. Listening.

"Ren." Ivo kept it gentle. "You called the vote yes, the first night. Before anyone. You've known about this place longer than any of us. You've fed it longer than any of us. So say it now. Take the children, respect the choice, or seal it and walk. Pick one."

For a long moment Ren didn't move. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and even, worn smooth like the handholds in the well shaft, like something that had been gripped by the same hands for years.

"You want me to pick like it's a menu," he said. "Like the answer isn't already written somewhere below us, and we're just deciding whether to read it." He turned his head, and in the lantern light his eyes were wet, though his voice didn't shake. "My brother is down there. Has been since '98. I've heard him every visit for five years — getting closer, or farther, I can't tell which, and neither can he. And I will tell you what I know that none of you know: the well doesn't want the children. It's never wanted the children. Children are noise it hasn't learned yet. It wants the ones who *answer* it." He looked at each of them in turn. "So take the children. Respect the adults. Seal nothing. And when it calls to us on the way out — and it will call, it's already learning our names, it learned Hal's tonight — nobody answers. Not one word. Not to your mother, not to your brother, not to me. Promise me that, and I'll walk point, and I'll get you out."

"And you?" Priya asked softly. "Do you answer it, Ren? Have you been answering it all along?"

Ren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ivo had ever seen him do, and Ivo had watched this man bury a rope partner in a flooded sump and keep his voice level through the whole eulogy.

"I've been answering it for five years," Ren said. "That's how I found the way in. That's how I know the turns that aren't on Ivo's map. That's the price of the map I've been keeping you alive with." He picked up his pack, unhurried, a man who had already done his grieving in private and was only now letting witnesses in. "So that's my abstention. I can't vote on whether we save them. I can only volunteer for the part where somebody walks closest to the dark."

* * *

They voted anyway, because Priya insisted the record show a decision and not just a drift. Paper torn in thirds. Hal wrote *seal and walk* and handed it over like a confession. Priya wrote *document first, then choose*. Dez wrote *offer the door, take whoever reaches for it*. Ivo wrote *the children, however they'll come, and no one left who wants to leave* — and looked at the four slips lying together on Priya's table and understood that no majority existed, that the vote had failed, and that it didn't matter, because they were going back down. All of them. Together. The way they always had.

"We choose each other," Ivo said, gathering the slips, "and we figure out the rest on the way."

Hal made a sound that was almost a laugh. "That's not a plan."

"It's the only plan this family's ever had."

They broke camp in the practiced silence of people who had rigged a hundred anchors together — Dez coiling line, Priya sealing notebooks in wax bags, Hal checking everyone's lamp batteries twice, an apology in the only language he had. And it was Ren, shouldering his pack at the mouth of the descending passage, who went still first, one raised fist freezing them all mid-motion.

Down the corridor, faint and rising, threaded through the hum like a voice through surf, came singing. A child's song. Two voices, thin and clear, the words worn soft by generations — a lullaby Edith must have sung ten thousand times in the dark, and now the well was singing it back up the tunnel, note-perfect, in voices that did not belong to either child.

Sable's voice. Wren's voice. Learned in a single afternoon.

Ren looked back at them, and his face in the lamplight was calm and terrible.

"It's practicing," he said. "Move. We have seven days, and it just started rehearsing."
