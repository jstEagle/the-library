## 22. The Count Continues

The city forgets faster than it forgives. That was the first thing I learned in the months after — that concrete is a kind of mercy, poured over memory in slabs and troweled smooth before anyone can read what's written underneath.

They built the foundation in October. I watched from the far side of the construction fencing on a gray morning when the wind carried the smell of wet cement across four empty lots, and I stood there with my hands in my coat pockets while the trucks came and went, churning, pouring, leveling, until the ground where a steel door had once been sealed from the inside was nothing but a flat gray field with rebar teeth waiting for the next layer. The surveyors worked off my map. My falsified map. They unrolled it on the hoods of their trucks and measured against it and nodded to each other, and every line they trusted was a line I had drawn to bury the truth, and I stood there feeling like both an arsonist and a firefighter, watching the fire go out under the water I'd set.

Priya had told me it would feel like this. *You'll save them twice,* she said, the night she made her single copy of the Vesser archive — nineteen thousand days of journals, of chalk tallies photographed page by page, of Edith's careful hand describing rain she'd never seen again. *Once out of the dark, and once into silence.* She kept the copy somewhere even I don't know. She said that was the point. Some truths shouldn't be findable. Some maps should end at the edge of the paper.

But the city forgetting is not the same as me forgetting. Nothing is the same as me forgetting.

* * *

I still run the old routes sometimes. Habit, or penance — after five years I've stopped trying to tell the difference. The tunnels I mapped, the ones that appear on no survey, are mostly gone now too: sealed with foam and block, or flooded when they rerouted the storm mains, or simply bricked over by crews who never asked why the brickwork behind the brickwork was older than the street above it. I walk them the way you visit graves. I bring no lights I don't need. I make no noise I don't have to.

Dez went back to fixing things — elevators, mostly, which he says is honest work because everyone who rides one is already agreeing to be trapped. Hal left the city entirely. He sends postcards from places with too much sky, mountains and coastlines and one memorable shot of the Bonneville Salt Flats, where the horizon is so flat and so far away that a man could scream at the top of his lungs and no echo would ever come back to him. He didn't write anything on that one. He didn't have to.

And the children — Wren and Sable, the Vesser children born underground, who had never seen sky until the day I held a door open with my shoulder and screamed while they passed through me into daylight — the children are learning the names of things. Sable wrote to me in November, in careful pencil on lined paper, to report that she had seen rain for real and that it was not loud, exactly, but that it had a sound like the room's lamps used to make, if the lamps had been made of falling water. Wren has started sleeping with the window open. Edith says he says he likes to hear the traffic, because traffic is just the city breathing, and breathing means alive, and alive is better than quiet. Edith herself lives now in a house with Lorna and Tomas, upstate, where the well water tastes like ordinary minerals and no one keeps count of anything except birthdays. Edith still sets a place at the table some nights. No one comments. Grief gets its own chair in that house, and it is allowed to sit.

Ren does not get a chair. Ren got a door.

* * *

It's February now, five months since the pour, and I come at night because night is when the city's noise drops low enough that a man can hear what's underneath him, if something underneath him wants to be heard.

I don't decide to come. That's the honest truth of it, and I've decided to stop lying to myself about most things, so: I don't decide. I finish work, I eat standing up, I put on my boots, and an hour later I'm standing at the edge of the fenced lot where the building is going to rise — half-finished now, a skeleton of steel and plywood, the ground floor slab already down, thick as a tomb lid — and I stand there in the cold with my breath coming out in white flags and I listen.

The first time I heard it, I told myself it was machinery. A pump somewhere, a generator, the building's temporary power humming through a conduit buried in the slab. Cities hum. I spent five years telling myself tunnels were settling water and traffic, and I am very good at telling myself things; it might be the single greatest skill I have developed in my life.

But machinery doesn't keep time.

This hum keeps time. It rises and falls in long, patient swells, in and out, like breath drawn through stone, and between each swell there is a gap, and the gaps are all the same length, and I stood there in the February cold counting them the way I once counted chalk marks on a wall beside a door, and the count was perfect. Not roughly rhythmic. Perfect. The way a metronome is perfect. The way a heart is not.

I should have left then. I want to be clear about that, because whatever else this account is, I want it accurate: I knew, standing there, that the sound was not machinery, and I knew what it meant for a thing beneath a sealed floor to be keeping time, and I knew — because Lorna told me, in the last honest hour we spent underground, her voice steady as someone reading a will — that the well drinks whoever remains, and calls them by name afterward.

It knows my name. It sang my mother's voice at me through collapsing tunnels while I ran. Of course it knows my name.

I stayed.

* * *

Here is what I do, on these nights. I sit on the low wall across from the site, where the floodlight doesn't reach, and I take off one glove, and I press my bare palm flat against the cold concrete of the retaining wall that runs along the lot's edge, and I listen through my hand the way you listen through a door.

The hum comes up through it. Fainter than it was in the shaft, fainter than it was in the Quiet Room where the lamps burned without fuel and the dust never settled, but present, insistent, patient as arithmetic. Nineteen thousand days minus fifty-three years leaves eleven days, Ren read to us from instructions nailed by a door, and those eleven days burned down to hours when Hal's guilt woke the thing below, and the hours burned down to minutes in the dark of the long walk out, and the minutes ended at a threshold where a door would only open outward for those who stayed.

Ren stayed.

I have replayed it ten thousand times. In some versions I grab his sleeve. In some versions I go in with him and let the children pass through alone, two kids in the daylight with no one screaming, and God knows what happens to them, and God knows what happens to me. In one version — the one I visit least — I understand sooner, and I say goodbye properly instead of holding a door and howling like something animal while my friend walks backward into the dark with his hands raised, palms out, the way he always did when he wanted us to calm down, saying, *I've been keeping the count my whole life, Ivo. Let me finish it.*

He said it gently. That's the part that breaks me. He said it the way you tell someone you'll see them Sunday.

* * *

Tonight the cold is sharp enough to hurt, and the site is empty of everything but the skeleton of the building and one floodlight burning over the materials lockup, and I make my pilgrimage: wall, palm, listen.

The hum is there. In and out. Patient. Keeping time.

And then — and I want to describe this precisely, because precision is all I have left to give it — then, inside the swell of it, there is a second sound. Thin. High. Almost lost. A thread pulled through the fabric of the hum, rising where the hum falls, weaving.

A melody.

I know the melody. I have known it for five years. Ren hummed it on every descent, every climb, every long walk between known tunnel and unknown, a little tuneless thing he claimed he'd gotten from a commercial jingle and refused to name further, three notes up, two notes down, a hitch in the middle where he'd breathe. Dez used to complain about it. Priya used to catch herself humming it days later and accuse him of witchcraft. Hal said once, in the early days, that if we were ever separated in the dark, that song would be how we found each other.

We laughed. I remember laughing. I remember the specific warmth of laughing with them in the mouth of a tunnel that appeared on no survey, back when the worst thing down there was the dark, and the dark was only dark.

The thread of melody rises through the hum. Three notes up. Two notes down. The hitch where he breathes.

My whole body goes rigid. My hand on the concrete is shaking, and I can feel the vibration of the hum moving through my bones like a train passing under a station, and the melody threads through it, unmistakable, impossible, and every hair on my arms stands up under my coat, and somewhere in my chest a voice that sounds like reason and looks like my mother is saying *it calls you by name, it wears what it drinks, it is singing your friend's song because your friend's song is what it ate—*

And another voice, quieter, older, the voice I trust more and trust less: *or it's him. Or he's down there, in the room where the dust never settles, sitting against the door he holds shut from within, humming to keep the count company, humming the way he always did, because the count is long and he intends to finish it.*

I don't answer.

That is the discipline I have left, and I hold it the way he held the door. I don't answer. I don't hum the next phrase, though my throat aches with it, though the melody sits in my mouth like a word I'm not allowed to say. Because Lorna told me what answering costs. Because Edith kept the silence for fifty years and called it love. Because the covenant is simple and terrible: the room stays quiet so the well sleeps, and everything I want right now — to sing back, to call his name, to press my mouth to the concrete and give that thread of melody a harmony — everything I want would be noise, and noise feeds it, and light wakes it, and love, it turns out, is mostly knowing when to keep your mouth shut.

So I kneel there in the cold with my hand on the wall and I listen to my dead-or-living friend hum through five feet of poured concrete, and I do not answer, and it is the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the door, harder than the lie I gave the surveyors, harder than watching four people blink in sunlight they'd waited fifty-three years to see.

The melody finishes its phrase. There is a pause — the length of a breath, the length of a gap between tally marks — and then it begins again. Three notes up. Two notes down. The hitch where he breathes.

He isn't calling me. I understand that, finally, kneeling there. If he wanted out, he'd have taken it; the door opened outward once, and he chose the other direction with his eyes open. This is what he does on descents. This is what he's always done. He hums so the people walking with him know he's there. He's not asking me to follow.

He's telling me he's still walking.

* * *

I stay until my knees ache and my hand goes numb against the wall, and then I do the last thing, the small ritual I've built out of the wreckage of everything else.

I take the chalk from my pocket. A stub of white chalk, the same kind that climbed a wall beside a steel door in strokes that ended mid-count, the same kind that marked nineteen thousand days in a room where the dust never fell. I crouch at the base of the retaining wall, in the shadow where the floodlight dies, and I draw a single mark.

One stroke. Vertical. Clean.

Then I rub it out with my thumb, because leaving it would be a message, and messages travel downward, and I have promised the dark my silence.

But I draw it anyway, every night. One stroke, erased. A count of zero, kept faithfully, forever. A tally that never grows, which is its own kind of devotion — the negative image of nineteen thousand days, the shape of a promise to stay quiet carved and uncarved, carved and uncarved, until my thumb learns the motion the way Wren's fingers learned the route home by touch.

Some doors you keep shut because opening them costs more than silence. I know the price exactly now. I paid it in installments: a friend, a map, a version of myself who believed the noises were settling water and traffic. What's left is this — a man kneeling in the cold, drawing a mark and erasing it, listening to a song he cannot sing back to, keeping a count that starts over every night at zero.

Somewhere below the slab, below the rebar, below the foundations rising story by story toward a sky the Vesser children finally get to live under — somewhere down there, in a room where the lamps burn without fuel, a new line of chalk marks has begun. I'm sure of it the way I was never sure of anything in five years of mapping. Day one. Maybe day two by now. A fresh stroke on an old wall, in a hand I'd know anywhere, beginning a count that will run longer than the building above it, longer than the city, longer, maybe, than the patience of the thing that sleeps beneath the floor and dreams in borrowed voices.

Three notes up. Two notes down. The hitch where he breathes.

I stand. I pocket the chalk. I do not look back at the wall, because looking back is a kind of answering too.

Behind me, faint through the concrete, perfect as a metronome, the hum keeps time — and inside it, patient as devotion, the song begins again.
