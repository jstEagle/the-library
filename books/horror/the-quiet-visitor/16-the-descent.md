## 16. The Descent

The bolts were easier than I expected them to be.

I want to say that like it's a small thing, a footnote, but it isn't. I had spent two days telling myself the door would fight me — that stone would grind against stone, that the whole house would groan in protest, that some ancient mechanism would wake and object. Houses in my line of work don't fight you. They settle. They creak. They let you write your little numbers on your little forms and they wait. But this door — this door that had been bolted from the inside by a woman who died before my grandmother was born — this door opened its locks to me like a hand unclenching after a long time held shut.

Three bolts. Iron, black with age but not rusted, not seized, sliding back into their housings with a sound I can only describe as relieved. Each one made a noise like a word being finished.

I stood there in the cellar dark with my hand on the last one for a long time, listening to myself breathe, and I thought: *this is the part where I turn around.* This is the part where any sensible person turns around. Dana had offered me the truck again that morning, standing in the yard with her arms crossed and her jaw set in that way she has, the way that means she's already lost the argument with herself and is now losing it with me on principle.

"You don't owe this house anything," she'd said. "You don't owe Maren anything. You didn't sign anything."

"No," I'd said. "That's rather the point."

Because here is what I found in Maren Vane's marginalia, in her cramped, furious, beautiful handwriting, in the ledger that smelled of lamp oil and a hundred years of someone else's fear: every founder who came down these stairs came down as an offering. They came down armed with scripture, or with salt, or with the certainty that they were walking into a cage to lock themselves inside it. They came down believing the ritual was a sacrifice, because believing it was a sacrifice meant they never had to ask what it actually was.

A conversation. That's all it ever was. A conversation that seven frightened people were too afraid to hold honestly, so they dressed it up in Latin and iron and called it a seal, and then they spent a century teaching their children to be afraid of the thing they'd made a tenant instead of a neighbor.

Maren figured it out too late. Her notes get smaller toward the end, tighter, like she was running out of paper and time in equal measure. But the last page — the last page is almost triumphant, in a bitter, dying sort of way. *It does not want what we give it,* she wrote. *It wants what we refuse to say.*

So no. I wasn't going down there armed. I wasn't going down there with salt or scripture or a signed confession from the town council, though God knows Aldous had offered me all three by the end, the way a man offers a bribe to a judge he's already bribed. I was going down the way you go into any room where someone has been waiting a long time to be heard.

Unarmed. Unforced. And, if I'm honest with myself — which is apparently the entire job description now — terrified out of my mind.

* * *

Before I went down, I did the one professional thing left in me. I surveyed the stair.

Old habit. Five years of measuring everything because measuring keeps you safe, and even now, even here, my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. So I stood at the top of the cellar steps with my phone flashlight and I counted.

Thirteen steps down to the landing. Then the passage Maren described, and at its end, the door. I counted the thirteen twice, because the staircase upstairs counts wrong — eleven treads where there should be twelve, a step that exists only when it feels like it, a house that has been lying about its own dimensions since before I was born. I have documented that lie. It's in my report. Defect 14, "irregular riser height, northeast stair," noted with the dry precision of a woman who had no idea she was writing a ghost story in the language of building codes.

But the cellar stairs counted true. Thirteen, thirteen, every time. As if this part of the house had nothing to hide. As if the stairs down were the only honest architecture in the building.

I chose to find that comforting. I recognize that this says something about how far gone I am.

Dana waited at the top. She hadn't wanted to come into the cellar at all — Pell still won't come back for his tools, and his tools are a very good impact driver — but she came anyway, because that's who Dana is. She stood with her boots planted wide like the floor might try something, and she said, "How long do I wait?"

"Till dawn," I said. "If I'm not up by dawn—"

"I know what the papers say." She didn't look at me. She looked at the door, at the three open bolts, at the seam of darkness between the stone and the frame where the cold came through like breath. "Felix. If it hurts you, I'm coming down after you. I don't care what the ritual says. I don't care what the ledger says. I'll bring the crew."

"The crew is two men and a guy who quit by voicemail."

"Then I'll come down alone and I'll be extremely annoying about it."

I laughed. Actually laughed, down there in the dark, with the door open in front of me and the thing behind it listening. And I swear — I swear on the ledger, on Maren's grave that doesn't exist, on every defective staircase I've ever measured — the air behind the door warmed by half a degree. Like something hearing laughter for the first time in fifty years and leaning toward it.

"Terms are terms," I said. "I wrote them. Let me keep them."

She hugged me. Quick, hard, the hug of a woman who doesn't waste motion. Then she climbed the thirteen steps, counting them out loud as she went — "one, two, three" — because she knew what that would mean to me, and she stood at the top with the light spilling down over her shoulder like a held breath.

I turned to the door.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about doing the bravest thing you will ever do: it looks exactly like doing a chore.

I stepped through. The stone was warm under my palm — body-warm, blood-warm, the temperature of something alive and patient — and the passage beyond was narrow and old and utterly ordinary. Dressed stone. A floor swept clean. Lamp niches cut into the walls at intervals, empty for decades, and I felt a brief, absurd pang for whoever had filled them once, climbing down with oil and matches because the ritual required light and light required maintenance and maintenance is just love wearing work clothes.

I walked. I counted my steps, because of course I did, because measuring keeps you safe, and somewhere around step forty I realized I wasn't counting to keep track of the distance. I was counting the way you count when you're trying not to cry. One, two, three. My mother taught me that. Breathe in for four, out for four, and when the panic comes, count until it passes. She learned it in a hospital waiting room. I learned it in empty houses, listening to walls knock, telling myself it was pipes.

Five years of pipes. Five years of settling foundations and thermal expansion and wind in chimneys, five years of writing *no defect noted* in houses where something knocked politely at 3 a.m., five years of professional armor over a hairline fracture that ran straight through me. And underneath it all, the truth I'd been carrying since the first night: it wasn't knocking to get in. It was knocking to see if anyone was home. And I always, always pretended I wasn't.

The passage ended.

And there it was.

I don't know what I expected. Teeth, maybe. Darkness with a shape in it. Something out of the worst corners of the ledgers, all the words the founders used — *the hunger, the presence, the quiet visitor* — words chosen to make sure no child ever asked follow-up questions. I stood at the threshold of its room with my flashlight shaking in my hand and I braced for the monster.

What I got was a room.

Small. Round. Dry. There was a table — a plain wooden table, old, scarred, the kind of table people sit at — and around it, chairs. Seven chairs. Six of them pushed back at angles, abandoned mid-century, dust-furred. The seventh sat square and waiting, facing the door, facing me.

On the table: a candlestick, unlit. An inkwell, dry. Two cups. And a book — the twin of Maren's ledger, same binding, same age, lying open to a blank page.

And above the table, carved into the stone of the ceiling in letters worn shallow by time or by touch, four words:

*SIT. SPEAK. STAY TRUE.*

I turned off my flashlight. I don't know why. Maybe because the room didn't need it — there was a faint grey luminance in the air, sourceless, patient, the light of a place that had been waiting in the dark so long the dark had gotten used to company. Maybe because it felt rude, shining a light in someone's face. Because that's what this was. Not a lair. A face.

"Hello," I said.

My voice sounded ridiculous. Small and human and cracked down the middle. I said it anyway, louder.

"Hello. My name is Felix Vane. Maren Vane was my great-great-aunt, or my great-great-grandmother — the parish records disagree and frankly so do I. I've read her ledger. I've read yours." I touched the open book on the table. Blank pages, hundreds of them, waiting. "And I'm not here to feed you. I'm not here to seal you. I'm here because nobody in a hundred and thirty years has asked you a direct question, and I have one."

The room breathed. Not wind — breath. The walls moved the way a chest moves. The grey light gathered itself, drew inward, condensed, and for the first time I saw it had been a shape all along, folded into the corners the way grief folds into a house, and now it unfolded.

It was tall. It was thin. It was made of the same stuff as the space between knocks — the pause after, the held silence, the sound a house makes when it's deciding whether to speak. It had no face, and it looked at me anyway, and I understood suddenly, completely, with my whole body, what Maren had understood too late:

It was starving. Not for blood. For this. For someone standing in front of it, saying *I see you*, meaning it.

"What," it said — and the voice was every voice it had ever borrowed, my mother's, the founders', the polite triple-knock of 3 a.m., all of them braided into one sound like roots under a tree — "is your question?"

I pulled out the chair. The seventh chair. The one kept waiting.

"Why," I said, sitting down, "did they build you a table with seven seats, when they only ever sent one person at a time?"

The visitor went very still. And then — I will write this down because it happened, whatever it costs me to admit it — the thing beneath Hollowmere House sat down across from me, folded itself into the chair like it had practiced the motion for a century in the dark, and began, at long last, to answer.

Above us, faintly, through thirteen honest steps and forty more and a hundred and thirty years of stone, I heard Dana counting. And below us, in the blank pages of the second ledger, ink began to rise on its own — words forming, letter by letter, in handwriting I recognized from Maren's margins.

The visitor had started the conversation. Now it was my turn to write.
