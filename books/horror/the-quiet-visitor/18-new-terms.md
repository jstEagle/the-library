## 18. New Terms

The ledger opened to a blank page the way a mouth opens for a spoon.

I don't mean that it was eager. I mean that the paper had been waiting so long, and with such terrible patience, that when I finally turned past Maren's last entry — dated October 1893, her handwriting shrinking toward the gutter of the page like something being pulled under — the emptiness there felt less like absence than appetite. Two hundred blank leaves. A century and a half of nothing written down. The house had been starving on silence since before my grandmother was born.

I sat at the founders' table in the long room where the supper still lingered in the air — bread and tallow and something sweeter underneath, something like the smell of a room after a confession — and I laid out what I had.

Maren's papers. The ritual, or what passed for one: *the keeper shall sit with the visitor and renew the terms each hundredth year.* The old terms themselves, copied in Aldous's father's hand, cramped and joyless as a tax form. *Provisions for the quiet visitor.* Which meant, when you read the parish records alongside it, people. Names. The town's quiet disappearances, doled out across generations like an annuity, each payment small enough that no one ever had to call it what it was.

And below all of it, in Maren's marginalia, the thing that had kept me up until the gray hour: *It does not want what we give it. It wants what we will not say.*

I had a pen. I had the ledger. And I had, somewhere beneath my feet, behind a door whose hinges were on the wrong side, an ancient tenant that had been listening to me think for five years and had never once interrupted.

"You could have taken me at any point," I said to the floorboards. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only kind of steady I've ever managed. "Five years of houses. You followed me through every one of them. The knocking in Denver — that was you. The wall in Marlowe Bend. You could have done anything, and instead you knocked politely and waited for me to be ready."

The house said nothing, which from this house was a kind of agreement.

"So here's what I've got," I went on, and uncapped the pen. "Not much. A surveyor's training, which means I know how to write down exactly what exists and not one thing more. A family name that turns out to be a debt. And a theory."

I wrote the date first. Then, because I am who I am, I drew a line down the page and headed two columns, the way I'd head a condition report. *Existing damage* on the left. *Proposed remedy* on the right. If I was going to renegotiate a contract with something older than the town, I was going to do it in a format I trusted.

Under *existing damage*, I wrote: *Fed on secrecy for 131 years. Diet consists of unsaid things — crimes, griefs, names no one speaks. Kept by fear. Feared because kept. Starving.*

The pen moved easily. That was the first strange thing, stranger almost than the warm stone or the counting staircase: the ink flowed like the page wanted it, like the ledger had been dry-mouthed for a century and I'd finally brought it water.

Under *proposed remedy*, I hesitated.

Because here is the problem with honesty, the problem nobody tells you when they're selling you truth as a cure-all: honesty costs. The old terms cost the town its conscience, paid out slowly enough that nobody felt the bleeding. New terms would have to be paid all at once, in public, forever. I was about to write down a price and then spend the rest of my life making sure someone paid it.

But I thought about Dana, holding the stair with three men and a nail gun against a town council that preferred a fed monster to a confessed town. I thought about Pell, who wouldn't come back for his tools because the door breathed under his hand. I thought about Aldous Reeve, sixty years old, weeping into his hands in his own office because his father had left him a debt and he'd chosen resentment over duty and smaller payments over honest ones — disappearances, plural, that he'd signed off on with municipal paperwork and a clear municipal conscience.

Every one of them starving. Every one of them keeping quiet about what starved them.

"We're the same," I told the floor. "You and this whole rotten, lovely town. You eat what isn't spoken. So does everyone."

Below me — below the cellar, below the door, below whatever distance the house kept between its floors and itself — something shifted. Not a knock. Not a voice. More like the feeling you get when you say a dead person's name aloud in an empty house and the air rearranges itself around the sound. Attention. The visitor was listening the way the starving listen through a restaurant window.

I put pen to paper and wrote the terms.

---

*Terms of Keeping, revised this day by Felix Vane, surveyor, of the line of Maren Vane, founder.*

*One. The provisions of the former contract are void. No person of Hollowmere or elsewhere shall be given, taken, lured, or lost to satisfy the tenant of this house. This clause admits no exception and no emergency. Hunger is not consent.*

*Two. In place of flesh, the tenant shall be fed witness. Once each year, on the anniversary of the founding, the town shall gather within these walls and speak aloud its buried history: every crime committed in service of the bargain, every name struck from the record, every lie told to keep the peace. Nothing spoken in this house may be denied, minimized, or punished elsewhere. The telling is the payment.*

*Three. The keeper shall carry the ledger down and return by dawn. The door shall stand open by design, not sealed by terror. What is witnessed cannot be un-witnessed; therefore the seal is the speaking, renewed yearly, forever.*

*Four. Should the town fail to gather — should even one year pass in silence — the former terms resume, and the tenant may take as it was taken. This is not a threat. It is arithmetic.*

*Five. The tenant shall answer honestly any question put to it during the witnessing, as the town must answer honestly any question put to it by the tenant. Truth for truth. Witness for witness.*

I read it back twice. Then I did the thing that made my hands shake worse than anything the house had done to me: I signed it. *Felix Vane,* in my careful surveyor's print, the same hand that had catalogued settling cracks and cold rooms in forty houses across five years of running.

The ink dried dark and stayed dark. On the facing page, faintly, as though pressed through from some other pressure, letters began to surface — slow, like frost forming on glass, like something writing with a hand that hadn't held a pen since before pens.

*Accepted,* the page said. Then, after a pause long enough that I understood the pause itself was effort, the next word surfaced alone:

*Hungry.*

"Not for much longer," I said. "But I need you to understand what you're agreeing to. This isn't a trick. I'm not feeding you lies dressed up as truth. Next autumn, real people are going to stand in this room and say real things — ugly things, about their grandfathers, about themselves. Some of them will hate me for making them. Some of them will hate you. That's the diet. Ugly, true, and annual."

The page stayed still. Then:

*They will come?*

"They'll come," I said. "Because I'm going to make refusal harder than attendance. Because Dana will drag them if she has to. Because—" and here I stopped, because the honest reason was the hardest one. "Because some of them want to say it. Have wanted to for a hundred years. They just needed a room where saying it didn't destroy them. This is that room now. You're that room."

Another long stillness. When the writing came, it was slower, and the letters were different — rounder, older, as though the visitor had reached further back into itself for them.

*I was given names once. Seven. They spoke them down the stairs like coins into a well, and I learned that a name spoken in fear tastes like nothing. I have been eating nothing for a very long time and calling it full.*

"I know," I said. "I've been measuring houses for five years so I wouldn't have to measure myself. We're both professionals at it."

Something that was almost laughter moved through the floor — a vibration too low to hear, felt in the soles of my feet, in the roots of my teeth. The ledger warmed under my hands, not the awful warmth of the door but something gentler, the warmth of a page held near a candle.

Then, at the bottom of the terms, beneath my signature, a second mark appeared. Not letters. A sign — a spiral, drawn in one continuous line, tight at the center and opening outward, the way a conversation opens, the way a story opens once someone finally starts telling it. It pressed into the paper deep enough that I could feel the groove with my fingertip.

A signature. The first honest one the house had ever received.

I closed the ledger. The blank pages behind it rustled like something exhaling.

Above me, through the whole height of the house, every door stood open. I heard them — not moving, just standing open, letting air move through rooms that had been holding their breath since 1893. The staircase, when I climbed it later to check the windows, counted true. Fourteen steps up, fourteen down. I counted twice, because I'm who I am, and then a third time because I didn't believe it, and then I stopped counting altogether and just stood there in the landing light with my hand flat against the banister, feeling the house hold still around me like a dog that has finally been fed and forgiven.

It would hold. I believed that. What I did not yet believe — what I couldn't make myself believe, standing there in the ordinary evening light while somewhere below us both an ancient tenant waited, patient and newly fed on five words of honest contract — was that the town would keep its side.

Because I'd seen the council's faces. I knew what Aldous knew: that a town can agree to anything in a cellar and unsay it in daylight. The terms were signed. The seal was speech. But speech required speakers, and tomorrow morning I would have to walk into a town that had voted to imprison me rather than confess, and ask seven hundred people to pay annually, in public, forever, for a bargain their ancestors struck in blood.

I picked up the ledger and carried it down the stairs that counted true, past the door that stood open by design, and I began, then and there, to draft the guest list for the first witnessing.

The first name on it was Aldous Reeve's.

The second was mine.

* * *

Dana found me at dawn in the kitchen, boiling water I had no intention of using, because the kettle gave my hands something to do that wasn't shaking.

She looked like a woman who'd spent the night holding a stair against her own neighbors, which she had. She set her hard hat on the table like a gavel.

"Council's meeting at nine," she said. "Aldous called it. He's telling them you're alive and the door's open and—" she paused, choosing the words with a foreman's care for load-bearing accuracy, "—and that you've changed the deal. Half of them are going to want to bolt the door anyway. Old habits."

"Old bargains," I said. "Habits are just bargains you've stopped noticing." I slid the ledger across the table to her. "Read it. All of it. Then tell me if I'm insane."

Dana read. I watched her face do what faces do in my line of work when they find a crack in a foundation: the flicker of assessment, the quick math of whether it's cosmetic or structural, the decision about what to tell the client. When she got to clause four — *hunger is not consent* — she read it twice. When she got to the spiral signature, she put her palm flat on the page without being asked, the way Pell hadn't dared touch the door.

"It breathed," she said quietly. "Just now. When I touched the signature. Like something turning over in its sleep after a good meal." She looked up at me. "You actually did it. You sat down with the monster and renegotiated the lease."

"I'm a surveyor. It's what we do. We find out what a property actually is, and then we write it down, and then everybody has to live with the truth of it." I turned the kettle off. "The hard part isn't the monster, Dana. The hard part is the town. The monster took the deal in one night. The town's been refusing it for a hundred and thirty years."

"So what happens at nine?"

"At nine," I said, "we find out whether Hollowmere would rather be honest or comfortable. My money's on comfortable. It usually is." I closed the ledger and held it against my chest, and felt, faintly, through the cover, through my ribs, a low answering hum — patient, attentive, and for the first time in a century and a half, waiting for something other than meat.

"But either way," I said, pulling on my coat, "they're going to hear the terms read aloud. Starting with the part where the seventh founder walked out of this house and into their history, and they let everyone believe she simply vanished."

Dana shouldered her bag. Outside, the sky was doing its gray, noncommittal dawn, and down the hill the town of Hollowmere lay smoking its chimneys, seven hundred hearths built on a hunger, all of them about to get the bill.

"Fair warning," Dana said, opening the door. "Aldous looks terrible."

"Good," I said. "He should. He's about to look worse."

We stepped out together, the ledger between us like a loaded thing, and behind us — soft, distinct, unmistakable — the front door of Hollowmere House closed itself gently at our backs. Not sealing.

Seeing us off.
