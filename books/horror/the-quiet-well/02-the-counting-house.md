## 2. The Counting House

Here is a thing I have never said out loud: I count doors.

Not casually, not the way you might count the exits in a movie theater because you're bored and the film is bad. I mean I count them the way some people pray. Front door of the station: one. Hallway door to the break room: two. Break room itself: three. Supply closet, four. Studio A, five. Studio B — my studio, my kingdom of dead air and AM static — six. Then back again on the way out, because the count has to balance or something in my chest starts to itch like a wool sweater worn inside-out.

Six doors at WKRL. There were six doors yesterday, and six the day before, and if there are seven tomorrow I will know it before anyone tells me.

I also keep the lights on. All of them. The station manager thinks it's a budget problem; he sends me passive-aggressive emails about the electric bill with subject lines like "FYI" that contain FYIs nobody asked for. What I don't tell him is that the dark in this building isn't empty. It's *occupied*. It has been occupied since I was nine years old and my grandfather Aldous put his hand on the back of my neck — gentle as a priest, heavy as a headstone — and said, "Count what's real, boy. The rest is just weather."

Aldous Vane died when I was nineteen, which means I've now spent more of my life obeying him than knowing him. That's not unusual for this town. Half of Hollow Creek still does what Aldous told them to do, and most of them never met him.

* * *

Let me back up, because last night Marla Tetch called my show, and Marla Tetch drowned last Tuesday, and I have decided — provisionally, the way you decide to keep driving on a tire that's clearly going flat — that this is fine.

The rational explanations are all lined up like good little soldiers. Prank call. Grief-crazed impersonator. Some kid with a soundboard and too much time. I've done overnight radio for eleven years; I've gotten calls from people claiming to be Elvis, from people claiming to be me, from one man who insisted he was the ghost of the WKRL transmitter tower and wanted royalties. Overnight radio attracts the sleepless and the strange. That's the job.

But here's what keeps snagging: her voice didn't perform grief. It performed *patience*. She talked to me the way you talk to someone standing at the edge of a pool, coaxing them into water they've already decided to enter. And she knew things. Small things. The way I take a sip of coffee right before I read the weather so I don't cough on air. Nobody knows that. Ruth doesn't even know that, and Ruth has watched me broadcast through two divorces and a kidney stone.

I kept her on until dawn. I told myself it was ratings. I told myself it was kindness. Both true, probably, but underneath both there was a third thing, quieter, wearing their clothes: I didn't want to hang up and find out what the silence did afterward.

At 5:47 a.m., after she said goodbye — she said goodbye *first*, which felt wrong, hosts go first, that's the whole architecture of radio — I sat in Studio B with my head in my hands and noticed, without deciding to notice, that I had written something on the log sheet. My handwriting, unmistakably mine, the lazy leftward slant of it:

**14 Kettle Row — basement flooded — she's waiting**

Kettle Row is where Marla Tetch lived. Lived. Past tense, past tense, keep the tenses straight, Sebastian, that's how you stay sane.

I don't remember writing it. I remember every other second of that night with horrible clarity — I could recite the caller IDs — but that line arrived on the page the way frost arrives on a window. One moment blank, next moment written.

I burned the log sheet in the ashtray out back like a lunatic, then went home and slept four hours with every light in my apartment burning, and woke up counting the doors between my bed and the kitchen. Four. There had always been four.

There were four today too. So far.

* * *

Tonight's show started normal, which by current standards felt like a gift.

Ruth ran the board for the first hour before heading home — she's got a daughter now, and a hip that predicts weather better than I do — and she paused at the studio door on her way out, the way she does, and looked at me with an expression I pretended not to translate. Concern, filed under casual.

"You look like hell, Seb."

"I'm on-brand."

"Eat something that isn't vending machine. Call me if—" she waved a hand at the general concept of everything, "—you know. Anything."

"I always call you if anything."

"Yeah," she said, and left, and I counted the door closing behind her: six. Balanced. For about ninety seconds I felt almost human.

Then the phone lines lit up.

Now, I should explain the board. Line one is the regular request line. Line two is the contest line, which hasn't run a contest since 2019. Line three is unassigned — it exists, it's wired, it rings maybe twice a year, usually wrong numbers asking for a dentist. Last Tuesday, all three lines lit at once during the flood coverage, and I assumed it was panic callers, and I took them in order, and the first voice said, "Is anybody still up there?" and I said yes, ma'am, we're here all night, and she said, "No, honey. Up *there*," and the line went dead, and I filed it under adrenaline and moved on. You file a lot of things under adrenaline in this business. Eventually the drawer sticks.

Line three rang at 1:12 a.m.

I let it ring four times, which is professional composure, and picked up. "You're live on WKRL, Hollow Creek's home for the overnight hours. Who's calling?"

Silence first. Not dead-air silence — *listening* silence. The kind where you can hear someone deciding.

Then a boy's voice. Young. Ten, eleven. Wet around the edges, like he was talking through a mouthful of creek water, though he enunciated carefully, the way kids do when adults have scolded them about mumbling.

"Hi, Seb."

"Hey there, buddy. What's your name?"

"Danny." A pause, and then, with the small pride of a child reporting good news: "I'm a good swimmer. Everybody says. I was the best in my class."

Something cold walked down my spine and took its shoes off at the base of my skull. Because Danny — Danny Fenn, age eleven, Danny Fenn whose photo had been on every channel for a week, Danny Fenn who went into the water on Cutler Street trying to reach a dog — Danny Fenn had been in my school district trivia bowl the year I judged it, and the question he'd missed was about local history, and he'd laughed and said history was boring because it was all stuff that already happened.

"Danny," I said, keeping my voice level, doing the thing I do, the warm-velvet thing, the thing that has talked insomniacs off more than one ledge. "Where are you calling from?"

"The well place." Said simply. No drama. Like saying *the mall*.

"Okay. Well place. Is someone there with you?"

"Everybody's here." A giggle, wet and bright. "That's why we need you to come down. Not yet. But soon. We're practicing being patient. Patience is hard when you're little."

I reached for the dump switch. My hand hovered over it. Here's the thing about the dump switch: in eleven years I have used it twice, both times for obscenity, and both times I felt like a censor, like a man slamming a library book shut. And some stubborn, stupid part of me — the part that counts doors, the part that believes things can be balanced, managed, kept — did not want to slam the book on this kid.

So instead I said, "What do you want to talk about tonight, Danny?"

And he said, "Why do you count, Seb?"

The studio got very quiet. The ON AIR sign hummed its small red hum. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked as it cooled, tick-tick-tick, patient as a clock in a house where nobody winds it anymore.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, and heard immediately how it sounded — the exact tone of a man caught doing something shameful.

"Doors," Danny said. "Lights. You count 'em all. Grandpa Aldous taught you. He taught everybody, but mostly you, 'cause you're the one who stays." A pause, and then, gentler than any child should be able to be: "Why do you count, Seb? Does it help? Does it make the itching stop?"

The itching. The word for it, the word I have never given anyone, the wool-sweater word I invented alone at nine years old with my grandfather's hand on my neck. Not fear. Not anxiety. *Itching.* The sense that reality has a seam and the seam is coming apart and only the counting holds the thread.

Nobody knows that word. Ruth doesn't know it. My ex-wife thought my rituals were OCD and she wasn't entirely wrong, but she never once heard me say *itching*, because I have never said it aloud in my life.

"Danny," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted, stripped of velvet, just a man in a room. "How do you know that?"

"We listen," he said. "We're really good listeners now. It's mostly what there is to do." Another giggle, fading. "Grandpa Aldous says hi. He says you count better than he did. He says you'll hold longer."

"Hold longer. Hold *what* longer, Danny?"

But the line had gone soft, gone to that ocean-roar silence, and beneath it — faint, so faint I could believe I imagined it except that I have spent my whole life learning exactly what I imagine and this wasn't it — something else breathed. Low. Unhurried. Wet. Not Danny. Bigger than Danny. Older than the town.

It didn't speak. It just breathed, once, the way a large animal settles, and the line clicked dead, and the board showed line three dark again, innocent, unassigned.

I sat there for a long time. Long enough that the 2 a.m. weather feed auto-fired and filled the studio with a cheerful synthesized voice telling Hollow Creek to expect clear skies, mild temperatures, no precipitation expected through the weekend.

Clear skies. Sure. The sky isn't the problem. The sky was never the problem.

I got up and counted the doors. Six. I counted them again. Six. I counted them a third time, fast, like checking a lock you know you locked, and on the third pass I stopped with my hand on the supply closet handle, because I remembered something I hadn't thought about in thirty years.

When I was nine, Aldous walked me through this building — it was a different business then, a feed store, but the bones are the same — and he counted the doors with me. Out loud. Slowly. Teaching me, the way you teach a prayer.

And there were **seven**.

Seven doors, in 1989. Six, ever since. I have spent thirty years counting six doors in a building that used to have seven, and I never once wondered where the seventh one went, because Aldous put his hand on my neck and said *count what's real* and I obeyed, the way the whole town obeys, the way the whole town has always obeyed.

I stood in the hallway with my palm flat against the wall where a door should be, feeling for a seam in the drywall, and behind me, in Studio B, the board lit up.

Line three.

Ringing.

And this time, before I even reached it, before I touched the fader, before I said a word — the caller ID display scrolled out a name in blocky green letters, steady as a heartbeat:

**ALDOUS VANE.**

He's been dead for twenty-six years, and he's holding on line three, and God help me, my hand is already reaching for the button.
