## 10. Sign-Off

**Sebastian**

The well smells like a basement that has been thinking.

That's the only way I can describe it, standing at the lip of the old Vane shaft with a broadcast rig on my back and a flashlight between my teeth like a pirate who's given up on treasure. Wet stone and old wood and something underneath both — a patience so old it has a smell, the way libraries do, the way churches do. The well has been waiting fifty years, and it has developed a scent for waiting.

The phone line is open. Ruth's voice crackles in my earpiece, thin as thread: *"You're live, Seb. Whole county's listening. Half the state."*

"Good," I say around the flashlight. Then I take it out of my mouth, because you can't confess through clenched teeth. "Good," I say again, properly, into the mic clipped to my collar. "Then let's begin."

Below me, the water is black and still as an eye. It isn't moving. But I can feel it attending. Fifty years of listening upward through soil and pipe, and now it gets to look up and see me looking down. I'd expected terror. What I feel instead is something worse: recognition. This is where my family's money came from. This is where the town's quiet came from. I have been drinking this water my whole life, in one form or another.

"Marla Tetch," I say. "Drowned last Tuesday on Delmore Road. First caller. You all heard her."

*"We heard her,"* Ruth whispers, and I know she means the listeners — hundreds of them, awake at 2 a.m., keeping vigil with their radios like it's church.

"Danny Okafor. Nine years old. 1974."

The water shivers.

"Aldous Vane traded three children for a mill," I say, and my voice doesn't shake, which surprises me. I have spent my whole life counting doors because I was afraid of what happens when you stop counting, and here I am, standing over the one door nobody counts, speaking plainly. "He fed them to this well so Hollow Creek could prosper. The town knew. The town chose silence. My grandfather chose silence loudest of all, and then he taught my father to count doors, and my father taught me, and we called it a quirk. We called it nerves. We called it anything except what it was: maintenance."

The confession goes on. Names, dates, the ledger Aldous kept in his own hand — I read it out, every entry, every child, every payment the town received in exchange for its forgetting. It takes forty minutes. I don't rush it. The dead waited fifty years; they can have forty minutes of clear air.

And while I read, they come up.

I hear them before I see them — small feet on wet stone, below me, beside me, in the walls of the shaft itself. Danny first, of course. He was always the brave one. He surfaces out of the floodwater at the bottom of the well like a boy coming up from the deep end of a pool, and he looks at me, and he smiles, and his smile is kind in a way that will haunt me if I survive to be haunted by anything again.

"You did it, Seb," he says. "You counted us all the way home."

"I'm sorry," I tell him. "For all of it. For taking this long."

"We know," he says. "We heard you practicing."

And God help me, I laugh. Down at the bottom of the well, with the thing beneath the water rising slow and patient as a tide, I laugh, because Danny sounds exactly like a kid who caught his teacher mispronouncing a word, and grief and laughter were always the same room with the lights off.

The water rises. Or I go down. At some point the distinction stops mattering. The entity doesn't grab me — it receives me, the way a congregation receives a hymn. It is lonely. That was always the horror of it, the part I couldn't say on air without breaking something open in every listener: it is lonely, and loneliness is how it feeds, and it has been starving politely for half a century.

But tonight it isn't feeding. Tonight it's being paid.

The last thing I see is the rim of the well against the sky, and Ruth's silhouette leaning over it, and beyond her the first gray suggestion of dawn. The last thing I hear is my own voice, still broadcasting, still steady, saying the thing I wrote down in case I lost my nerve:

*"This is Sebastian Vane, signing off. Keep your lights on. Count your doors. And when someone calls you in the night just wanting to talk — answer. Somebody should."*

Then the water closes over the microphone, and the frequency carries me the rest of the way down.

* * *

**Ruth**

He went under at 3:47 a.m. I know because the board clock was right there, and because I am the kind of woman who looks at clocks when she cannot look at anything else.

I didn't pull him out. I want that on the record, wherever records go after this. I stood at the edge of that well with a rope in my hands and I did not throw it, because he asked me not to, and because the children were already climbing out past him — three small shapes rising out of the floodwater and walking toward town, toward houses that aren't there anymore, toward whatever home means when home was the thing that failed you. Marla Tetch went last. She paused at the well's rim and looked at me, and she said, "Keep the station on. He'll still be on it. Just... lower down."

I thought that was grief talking. Grief talks a lot of nonsense at 4 a.m.

I drove back to Studio B with wet shoes and dry eyes, because crying would come later, and later kept rescheduling itself. The ON AIR light was still lit. Of course it was. I hadn't touched the board since he went down, and the signal was still carrying — his voice, or the shape of his voice, reading the sign-off note he'd left in the rack like he knew I'd find it there, because of course he knew, he'd known me thirty years.

So I did the only thing I knew how to do. I sat down at the board, and I kept the show running.

* * *

Weeks pass. The town does what towns do: it pretends, badly, and then pretends less badly, and then starts telling the truth in small doses, like medicine. The mill closes — turns out prosperity built on drowned children doesn't hold its value. People leave. Some people stay, the ones who remember '74 from the inside, and those ones call in.

The show runs every night now. I host it, mostly. I play the requests, I take the calls, I read the weather like it matters. And sometimes, between segments, the board lights up on its own — channel open, no caller ID — and a voice comes through that I know better than my own mother's.

It's gentler than it used to be. It's slower. It sounds like a man who has finally stopped counting.

Tonight a kid calls in — college age, can't sleep, finals week. "I don't know why I'm calling," she says. "I saw the frequency written on a bathroom wall. Is this a real show?"

"It's real," I tell her.

There's a pause, and then the other voice comes in, low and warm and unhurried, the way deep water is unhurried. "Hi," it says. "Can't sleep either?"

She makes a sound that's half laugh, half sob. "Is this a recording?"

"Nope," the voice says. "Just somebody who's got nowhere else to be and all night to be there. Tell me about the exams."

They talk. I let them. I keep my hand near the fader but I don't touch it, because this is the arrangement, this is the vigil: the dead got to go home, and the thing that took them got what it actually wanted all along, which was never children — it was company. It got a Vane. It got the best listener in the county, patient and kind, answering call-ins from somewhere beneath everything.

Near the end of the shift, the girl hangs up, and the studio goes quiet, and the voice stays on the open channel the way it always does, not demanding, just present.

"Ruth," it says.

"Seb," I say.

A pause. Then, gentle as a lullaby, almost amused:

"There are four doors between you and the parking lot tonight. Same as always. You counted them on the way in — I felt it."

I look at the door. Four panels. There have always been four panels.

"Yeah," I say. "I counted."

"Good girl," says the voice from the bottom of the well, from the bottom of the frequency, from the quiet place where all the lost calls finally get answered. "Get some sleep. I've got the phones."

And the ON AIR light glows soft and red over the empty chair, and somewhere far below us both, patient as silt, the show goes on.
