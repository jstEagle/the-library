## 5. Frost Writing

Mara woke to a silence so complete it had texture.

Not the silence of the countryside she remembered from childhood visits—there had always been something underneath that, the tick of the boiler, the wind worrying the chimney, Nell breathing in the next room with that slight whistle in her nose. This was a held breath. A silence that seemed to be waiting for her to notice it.

She lay still under the quilt, watching her own breath ghost above her face, and understood before she moved that the temperature in the house had dropped. Not uncomfortably—not the brutal cold of a broken furnace—but by degrees, the way a room cools when a door has been left open somewhere far away.

The house had done this as a child too, she thought. Their mother used to say the Dunmore house breathed, that old houses drew air through their bones the way people did. Mara had believed it then. She had been happy to believe anything then.

Now she got up, pulled on Nell's cardigan—the one from the wardrobe, the one that still smelled faintly of woodsmoke and something floral and wrong—and went window to window like a sentry, checking locks she had already checked at midnight.

That was when she saw the frost.

* * *

It covered every pane of glass on the ground floor. Not the usual ferning of winter, not the feathered crystals that crept up from the corners of cold windows. This was deliberate. This was drawn.

Each window bore the same figure: a central stem rising from the bottom of the glass, branching outward in pairs—two limbs, then four, then eight—each branch splitting again and again until the pattern thinned into filigree at the top. It looked, at first, like a tree. It looked, for one merciful moment, like nothing at all.

Then Mara saw the proportions.

The stem was narrow. The branches descended from a rounded crown. Two of the lower branches bent at angles no tree would make—at elbows, at knees. The whole figure stood perhaps two feet tall on each pane of glass, arms raised, fingers spread against the inside of the frost as though pressing outward from within.

A person. A small person, rendered in ice, standing at every window of her mother's house with its hands against the glass.

Mara's breath fogged the kitchen window and she wiped it with her sleeve, an act of pure animal reflex, wanting to break the thing's gaze. The frost smeared, reformed where her arm hadn't touched, and where she had wiped it clean the glass showed only the grey morning and the falling snow beyond.

But the other windows were untouched. Eleven panes of glass, eleven figures, all identical down to the smallest branch. She counted them twice. She had always been good at counting; it was the first thing she and Nell had ever taught themselves to do together, counting stairs, counting doors, counting the seconds between lightning and thunder.

Eleven. And the twelfth window—the one in the upstairs landing, the one that had faced the churchyard since before either of them was born—was clear. Clean. As if whatever had drawn the others had saved it, or had already been let in there, or was waiting for permission.

She did not go upstairs to check it. She told herself this was because she wanted tea first. She knew it was because her legs would not carry her toward that staircase.

* * *

The kettle took an age to boil, and in that age Mara stood at the kitchen table with her hands wrapped around a mug she hadn't drunk from and tried to think like a rational woman. Condensation. Old single-glazed windows in a damp house. Patterns in frost were fractal by nature; the eye found figures in them the way it found faces in wallpaper. She had read about pareidolia. She could name the mechanism, and naming it should have helped.

But the eye did not find the same face in eleven different windows overnight, aligned, identical, symmetrical. The eye did not find elbows in condensation.

And the cold—she kept coming back to the cold. It wasn't outside cold leaking in. It was a cold with intent, a cold that had gone somewhere specific and done something specific, the way a hand goes to a pen. The house felt less like a building than like a page that someone had been writing on while she slept.

Her phone had one bar, flickering. She'd tried Edie in Leeds last night and gotten half a sentence of static and then nothing. The landline, when she lifted it, offered only that soft dead tone that meant the line existed and nothing lived on it. The blizzard had thickened overnight into something vertical and total; she could see perhaps twenty feet past the garden gate before the world dissolved into white grain, like static on a dead channel.

Snowed in. The phrase arrived in her mind unbidden, wearing someone else's voice, and she pushed it away.

There was a knock at the front door. Three knocks, unhurried, spaced evenly.

Mara froze with the mug halfway to her mouth. Last night's memory rose—the knocking, the polite circuit of the village, Mrs. Aldersey's hand clamped over hers in the dark saying *we don't answer, we don't answer, girl.* But that had been midnight. This was morning, grey and ordinary, and morning knocking was usually human.

Through the frosted pane beside the door she could see a shape. Small, stooped, bundled in wool.

"Miss Dunmore." Mrs. Aldersey's voice, muffled by weather and wood. "It's Enid Aldersey. I've brought bread, and I've brought my boots, and you're going to want both."

Mara unbolted the door and the old woman came in on a gust of snow, shaking it from her shawl like a dog, moving with the brisk authority of someone who had decided long ago how things would be done in any room she entered. She was eighty-something, maybe ninety, small as a wren, with a face like crumpled paper and eyes that missed nothing. She set a loaf on the hall table, looked at Mara's face, and stopped.

"You've seen them," she said. It wasn't a question.

"The windows," Mara said. "Yes."

Mrs. Aldersey nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis she'd already written. "Show me."

* * *

They walked the ground floor together, the old woman moving from pane to pane with her hands clasped behind her back, never touching the glass, studying each figure the way a doctor studies a rash. At the kitchen window she leaned close, breath misting the edge of the pattern, and made a low sound in her throat.

"All the same," she murmured. "Every year they're different, you know. My grandmother wrote about it. Some winters it's just the branches. Some winters it writes words nobody can read. But same figure, every window—" She straightened up. "That's not weather, Miss Dunmore. That's correspondence."

"Correspondence with what?"

"With whoever it wants." Mrs. Aldersey turned and looked at her, and her eyes in the flat grey light were pale and steady and terribly tired. "You're new here, so I'll say it plain and you can decide I'm mad over your tea. That frost is a letter. It's been writing letters to this village for a hundred years and more. Most winters it writes small. A branch here. A shape there. Folk pretend it's pretty and go on with their lives. But some winters—" she glanced back at the window, at the small branching figure with its hands pressed to the glass from the inside— "some winters it writes big. And it always starts the same way. It starts at the house of the person who called it."

"I didn't call anything."

"No," Mrs. Aldersey agreed. "But someone with your blood did. And frost doesn't much care which Dunmore opens the door, so long as a Dunmore does."

The word *blood* sat in the warm kitchen like a stone dropped in still water. Mara thought of Nell's journal under the floorboards, its last pages razored out, the remaining entries full of that desperate looping handwriting: *I found the way to ask. I found what Mother never told us we're allowed to ask.*

"Nell," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on the name.

"Your sister." Mrs. Aldersey said it without inflection, but her eyes flicked—just once, quickly—toward the stairs, toward the clear twelfth window above. "Aye. Well. Sit down, girl, and drink your tea while it's hot. Cold tea in a cold house is a misery I won't be party to."

* * *

They sat at the kitchen table, and Mrs. Aldersey ate a slice of her own bread with butter she'd brought in her pocket, and for a few minutes the scene was almost domestic enough to laugh at—an old woman and a grieving one, taking tea while the blizzard erased the world outside. Almost.

"Tell me about the patterns," Mara said finally. "You said your grandmother wrote about them. 1911?"

"1911 and before. The Alderseys have kept accounts of this village since before the church had a roof." The old woman brushed crumbs from her skirt with great precision. "Grandmother Edith—she was a Aldersey by marriage, a Corrigan by blood—she was fourteen the winter they melted the bell. She wrote it all down afterward, years later, when she was old enough that nobody could call her silly. Frost on every window, same as yours. Same figure. And folk tracing it."

"Tracing it? Why would anyone—"

"Because it's beautiful." Mrs. Aldersey said it simply, without judgment, and somehow that was worse. "That's the trap of it, Miss Dunmore. Grief makes folk stupid for beauty. You lose someone in the winter, and then the winter starts drawing on your windows, and the drawings look like them—or like what you remember of them, which is better than them, because memory forgives everything—and one night you've had a bottle of porter or you've just come from the graveside, and you put your finger on the glass. And you trace the line. Just once. Just to feel closer to them."

She paused. Outside, the wind rose and fell against the house like something testing its grip.

"And?" Mara asked, though her mouth had gone dry.

"And the line follows your finger back." Mrs. Aldersey set down her knife with a small click. "That's what Grandmother Edith wrote. You trace the figure, and the figure traces you. After that, the dreams start. Then the knocking. Then—" she waved a hand vaguely at the ceiling, at the village, at the white blindness beyond the walls— "then all this. The bell. The faces. The remembering that isn't remembering."

Mara thought of the villagers in the square at midnight, mouths moving in unison. Thought of Mrs. Trelawney describing Nell's birthday picnic by the mill stream—a day that had never happened, described with such tender specificity that Mara had gone home and lain awake wondering if she was the one who was broken.

"So I don't touch them," she said. "Don't trace the lines. Understood."

"That's the first rule and the easiest one." Mrs. Aldersey leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something older and flatter. "Here's the harder one, girl. They'll get lovelier. First nights, they're just shapes, and it's easy to keep your hands in your pockets. But it learns. It draws what you miss. By the third night or the fourth, you'll look at that window and you won't see a figure anymore—you'll see your sister's profile, or your mother's hands, or the exact curl of hair over a forehead you haven't seen since you were nine years old. And it will be perfect. Perfecter than photographs. Because it isn't copying what they looked like. It's copying what you can't stop seeing when you close your eyes."

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

"How do you know all this?" Mara asked. "Really know it?"

For the first time since entering, Mrs. Aldersey looked away. Her gaze went to the window—to the branching figure with its raised arms—and stayed there a long moment.

"Because in the winter of 1968," she said, "my son David traced one."

She said nothing else for a while. She gathered the bread knife, wiped it, wrapped it in her shawl with the economy of a woman who had spent a lifetime putting things away. When she spoke again her voice was level, but level the way ice is level—with weight beneath it.

"He was nineteen. Clever as anything. Wanted to be an engineer, wanted to see Manchester, wanted everything. His father died that October, sudden, and David took it hard, and that January the frost came early and came thick, and one morning I found his bedroom window drawn all over with that figure, and David standing there smiling at it, and he said, 'Mum, look, it's Dad, it's Dad writing to us,' and I told him—God help me, I told him not to be soft, and I wiped the window myself and thought no more about it." Her hands were perfectly still on the table. "He traced the next one that night. In secret. And by spring he wasn't David anymore. He smiled right, and he answered to his name, and he ate his supper and kissed my cheek, but there was nobody home behind his eyes except something wearing him like a coat. He walked out into the fields on the first of March and the snow took him and they never found so much as a boot. And the village—" she exhaled— "the village remembered him wrong after that. Remembered a boy who never was. Still do. There's a photograph in the church hall of the 1968 harvest festival and the boy in it has my David's coat and somebody else's face, and everyone swears that's normal."

Mara's skin had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the house.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to be tempted, and I'd rather you hear it from me than learn it alone at three in the morning with your finger already halfway to the glass." Mrs. Aldersey stood, tucking the shawl around her shoulders. "And because I promised your sister I'd watch over you, and unlike some things in this village, I keep my promises."

The words landed strangely. Mara frowned. "When did you promise Nell—"

But the old woman was already at the door, pulling it open onto the roaring white, and the wind swallowed the rest of the question. She paused on the threshold, small and dark against the blizzard, and looked back over her shoulder.

"One more thing, Miss Dunmore. The twelfth window. Upstairs, facing the churchyard." Her eyes were very pale. "It's clean because it's reserved. Whatever's writing on your glass has left that one blank the way a scribe leaves a page blank—for the last entry. Don't sleep in a room that looks out of it. And whatever you do, whatever you see in it, however lovely it gets—"

She stepped out into the snow. Her voice came back thin through the wind:

"—don't trace it. And don't let it finish."

Then she was gone, a shape, a suggestion, and the whiteness closed over her like water.

Mara stood in the hallway a long time, listening to the storm, feeling the house breathe around her. Above her head, at the top of the stairs, the twelfth window waited—clean, patient, blank as an unwritten page.

Somewhere in the walls, or under the floor, or out in the fields where the churchyard lay buried in white, something began—very softly, very sweetly—to hum a tune she almost recognized.

It sounded like a lullaby their mother used to sing.

It was being sung backwards.
