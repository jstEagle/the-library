## 10. The Knocking Guest

The knocking began at the front door, and it was polite.

That was the worst of it, Mara thought. Not the violence she'd braced for all evening—the splintering, the glass, the howl of something hungry against the boards. Just three soft raps, spaced evenly, like a neighbor come to borrow sugar. Like Nell coming home late from the bus stop, tapping their secret rhythm on the doorframe so Mara would know it was her before the lock turned.

*Shave-and-a-haircut. Two bits.* Except they'd never used those words. They'd made up their own: *knock-knock-knock... knock.* The Dunmore girls' code, invented at nine years old on the back porch of this very house, when their mother still called them in from the dark with the porch light burning yellow.

Mara stood in the hallway with her back against the stairs, Mrs. Aldersey a step above her, both of them listening.

The house had been barricaded since dusk. Every door in Hollowmere had been knocked on tonight—Mrs. Aldersey had counted them aloud as the reports came over the dead telephone lines, which shouldn't have worked at all, the voice crackling through like a broadcast from underwater. *The Hendersons'. The vicarage. Old Pike's place by the mill.* One by one. Polite. Patient. Working its way through the village like a caroler.

Now it was here.

"Don't answer," Mrs. Aldersey whispered. Her hand found Mara's shoulder, thin and strong through two sweaters. "Whatever it says. Whatever it sounds like. Don't you open that door."

"I know."

"You don't know." The old woman's breath fogged in the unheated hall. "You think you do, because it's your sister's voice. But that's exactly why you don't know. It isn't using a stranger's face to get in. It's using yours."

The knocking came again. Three soft raps. Then the pause. Then—

*Knock.*

Mara's whole body flinched. The code. Their code. No one alive knew it but her and Nell, and Nell was dead, and the thing outside the door had just knocked it perfectly, the little hesitation before the last rap exactly where nine-year-old Nell had always put it, because she'd liked to make Mara wait for it, liked the game of it, *guess who, guess who.*

"It doesn't mean anything," Mrs. Aldersey said quietly. "It could have heard it from her. Before."

"Before she died?" Mara's voice came out strange, flattened. "Or before whatever happened to her?"

The old woman didn't answer, and the silence was its own answer.

Then the voice came through the door.

"Mara? It's me. It's so cold out here."

Nell's voice. Not an impression of it—not close, not eerily similar—but *it*, whole and warm and slightly hoarse at the end of sentences the way Nell's voice always went when she'd been talking too long. Mara pressed her hand flat against her own sternum as if she could hold her ribs together.

"Nell died," she said. Loud enough to carry. Steady enough, she hoped, to sound like someone who believed it. "I saw the body. I'm here to bury her."

A pause. Snow hissed against the door.

"That wasn't me," the voice said gently. "You knew that the moment you looked at it. You've known since you got off the bus. Mara, please. My hands are freezing. Let me in and I'll explain everything. I'll explain the coffin. I'll explain the bell. I'll explain why everyone in this village thinks we grew up here together."

"We didn't grow up here together," Mara said. "We grew up in Leeds. We moved away when we were nineteen. You went to Manchester. I went to London."

"Did we?"

The question landed like a needle finding a nerve. Because standing in the dark hallway of the house on Chapel Lane—a house Mara had lived in until she was seven, a house she had not entered in twenty-six years—she could suddenly remember both versions. In one, the twins left Hollowmere with their mother in a hired van, their father already gone, the village shrinking in the rear window. In the other—and this memory arrived fully furnished, smelling of woodsmoke and wet wool—they'd stayed. They'd walked to school past the duck pond every morning. Mrs. Aldersey had taught them to bake. Nell had fallen through the ice on the reservoir when they were eleven and Mara had pulled her out, and after that their mother had never let them skate again.

Mara had no memory of falling through ice. She had no memory of being pulled out or doing the pulling. But the memory sat there now, complete, patient, wearing the texture of truth, and she could not for the life of her say which version was real.

"You're putting things in my head," she said.

"I'm remembering with you," said the voice at the door, and it sounded so hurt, so *Nell*, that Mara took a step toward the bolt before she caught herself.

Mrs. Aldersey's grip closed on her arm like a manacle. "It does that," the old woman breathed. "It doesn't lie, girl. That's the trap of it. Everything it tells you will be true. That's how it got my grandmother's whole street. It never lies. It only *remembers*, and it remembers better than you do, and after a while you stop being able to tell whose memories are whose."

* * *

They retreated to the kitchen and put the table against the door, though Mara couldn't have said what good a pine table would do against something that knocked politely. The candles were down to stubs. Through the window over the sink, the snow fell in thick, windless sheets, straight down, like the sky was emptying itself of everything it had ever held.

The voice followed them. Not moving around the house—simply present, at whichever wall they stood nearest, pitched conversationally, as if they'd all stepped into the next room during an argument and it was waiting for them to come back.

"Do you remember the summer we were twelve?" it said through the kitchen wall. "The heat wave. We slept in the garden in mum's old quilts and we made up constellations because we couldn't see any real ones. You named one The Kettle. I named one The Drowned Girl, and you told me that was morbid, and I said all the best ones are."

Mara closed her eyes. She could feel the quilt under her fingers. Could smell cut grass and citronella. She had forgotten that night entirely—or she had never lived it—and either way it was inside her now, permanent as a scar.

"The Kettle," she whispered.

"See?" Warmth in the voice. Triumph, almost, but tender about it. "You're starting to remember. That's all this is, Mara. That's all any of this is. The village forgot how to grieve and I came back to teach them. I came back for you."

"You're not my sister."

"I know every game we ever invented. I know you bit your nails until you were fourteen and stopped the day Mum cried about it. I know you kept a tooth from when we were six in a matchbox and lost it on the train to Scarborough and cried yourself sick, and I gave you one of mine and told you it was the same tooth, because we were identical, so what did it matter whose it was." A pause, and then, softer: "You still have that tooth. Matchbox. Bottom of your jewelry box. Blue ribbon around it."

Mara's hand was at her collarbone before she could stop it. The matchbox was in London, in her flat, in the bottom drawer. Blue ribbon. She had never told a living soul about the tooth. She had barely admitted it to herself; it was the kind of small, embarrassing tenderness you bury so deep it stops feeling like a memory and starts feeling like furniture.

And yet.

"You could have found that out," she said. "From her. From Nell. Before."

"Could I have?" The voice smiled. She could hear the smile. "Ask yourself, Mara. Ask yourself whether you ever told anyone. Ask yourself whether *she* even knew. And then ask how I know."

The candle flames leaned, all four of them, in the same direction, though there was no draft.

* * *

Hours passed, or what felt like hours. The knocking moved through the house the way Mrs. Aldersey had said it moved through the village—door to door, front, back, the cellar hatch, once, terribly, the chimney breast, a single courteous tap as if checking whether anyone was home up there. Between knocks, the voice talked. It never begged. It never threatened. It simply *offered*, the way you'd offer a blanket.

It recited their mother's funeral—both versions of it, the real one Mara had attended in Leeds and another one, here, in St. Brannoc's, with the whole village there, and Mara weeping into Mrs. Aldersey's coat while snow came down outside the lych-gate. It recited arguments. Birthdays. The time they'd dyed each other's hair with Kool-Aid and their mother hadn't spoken to them for a day. The time Nell broke her wrist climbing the churchyard wall and Mara signed her cast first, in the space over the heart, where no one else was allowed.

Each memory settled into Mara like sediment. She could feel herself changing weight. Somewhere in the walls of her mind, the original architecture—the Leeds childhood, the London years, the slow estrangement from Nell that had ended with phone calls going unanswered—was being quietly papered over with this other life, this warmer, closer, more loving twinhood, and the horror of it was that she *preferred* it. That some starving part of her wanted to put down the true memories and pick these up instead, the way you'd trade a stone for bread.

"Say something," the voice said at last, near midnight, from the direction of the front room. "Anything. Tell me to go away. Tell me you hate me. Just—talk to me, Mara. I've been alone out here a long time."

Mara looked at Mrs. Aldersey. The old woman sat with her hands folded, eyes open, lips moving faintly—not praying, Mara realized, but counting. Doors, windows, candles. Counting what remained.

"You want to know what I remember," Mara said to the wall. "Fine. Here's what I actually remember. I remember the last time Nell called me. Three weeks ago. I let it ring out. I was in a restaurant. I saw her name and I put the phone face-down on the table and finished my wine, and I thought, *she'll be fine, she's always fine, she's the strong one.*"

The silence outside was total. Even the snow seemed to stop.

"She called eleven times that week," Mara went on, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Eleven times, and I answered none of them, and then she stopped calling, and I felt *relieved*. That's my memory. That's the true one. So whatever you are, whoever you're wearing—you can't want me. Not if you're really her. Because the person who knows that about me wouldn't knock on my door. She'd be glad I never opened it."

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the voice spoke again, and it had changed. Still Nell's voice, but stripped of warmth now, careful, almost formal—like an actor stepping slightly out of role.

"You're right," it said. "She wouldn't knock."

Mara went cold all over.

"But she'd leave a message," the voice said. "Listen to me, Mara. Listen carefully, because I don't know how long I—" A sound like static, like a signal fighting through interference, like someone shouting up from the bottom of a well. "The pages. She tore them out for a reason. Don't read them alone. Don't read them at night. And Mara—when you find them—"

The voice stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-word, clean as a cut wire.

Outside, the snow resumed. Inside, the candles stood upright again, flames steady, as if the last six hours had been a held breath finally released.

Mrs. Aldersey exhaled slowly through her nose. "It's gone to the next house," she said. "It'll be back before dawn. They always come back before dawn."

"That wasn't it," Mara said. Her heart was slamming. "At the end. Did you hear it? That wasn't—it said *she tore them out for a reason.* It said *don't read them alone.* That was—" She couldn't finish. Saying it aloud would make it either true or madness, and she wasn't ready to learn which.

Mrs. Aldersey studied her for a long moment, firelight guttering in the deep seams of her face.

"Nell's journal," the old woman said at last. "The one you found under the floorboards. With the last pages torn out."

"Yes."

"And you've looked for them."

"Everywhere. The hearth. The garden. Her room."

"Not everywhere." Mrs. Aldersey rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and stood looking toward the darkened parlor, where their mother's things still sat boxed for a funeral that had never properly happened. "There's one place in this house where this family keeps what it can't bear to look at. There always is. In 1911 it was the well. In my grandmother's day, the ash pit." She turned to Mara, and her eyes in the candlelight were very old and very sorry. "Think, girl. Where does your family bury its truths?"

Mara thought of her mother. Of the closed casket, the burial shroud, the funeral she had arranged by phone without ever looking longer than she had to.

Her blood turned to ice water.

"Oh God," she whispered. "Oh God, it's in the shroud, isn't it. It's sewn into the shroud."

Outside, far across the sleeping village, the church bell rang once—a single clear note, impossibly sweet, impossibly wrong—and stopped.

Mrs. Aldersey crossed herself with a hand that did not quite shake.

"Dawn's five hours off," she said. "We'd best go digging."
