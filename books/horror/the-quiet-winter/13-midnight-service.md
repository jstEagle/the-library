## 13. Midnight Service

The frost had stopped growing.

That was the first thing Mara understood, standing at her kitchen window with a candle because the electricity had died an hour ago: the branching figure on the glass was finished. Complete. Every window in the house wore the same design now, and it did not spread, did not thicken, did not crawl. It simply waited, like a sentence that had been written out in full and now required only to be read.

She had not slept. Sleep had become a country she could no longer find the border of. She sat in her mother's old armchair with a blanket around her shoulders and watched the frost figure and tried not to see what it resembled, though it resembled nothing so much as a person seen through ice—arms raised, head tilted back, mouth open in a shape that might have been singing or screaming or both.

At half past eleven, the candle flame leaned toward the door.

Not from a draft. The windows were sealed; she had checked them twice, then a third time for the comfort of doing something. The flame simply bent, gently, deliberately, the way a dog turns its head toward its name, and held there, pointing down the hallway toward the front door, toward the village, toward the church whose bell had been melted into slag before either of their parents were born.

Mara knew what it wanted. She had known since the knocking stopped at dawn—stopped mid-knock, actually, three raps delivered and a fourth withheld, as if the guest on the other side of the door had smiled and decided there was no more hurry. The whole night since had felt like being kept. Like a thing set aside, wrapped carefully, saved for an occasion.

She dressed in her mother's black wool coat because it was the warmest thing in the house and because some stubborn animal part of her thought that if she was going to be buried in this village, she would at least choose the coat herself. She took the poker from the hearth and then, after a moment, put it back. It had seemed suddenly, obscenely beside the point.

The snow outside had stopped falling. That was wrong too—the forecast, what remained of the radio's dying signal, had promised another two days of blizzard—but the sky above Hollowmere was swept clean and crowded with stars, hard and white and close, and the village lay beneath them in a silence so total that Mara could hear her own blood moving.

Every house on Church Lane was dark except one.

Light poured from the windows of the church itself—not candlelight, not lamplight, but something cooler, a blue-white glow like moonlight forced through glass, spilling across the snow of the churchyard and laying long blue bars between the leaning gravestones. And from inside came sound. Not the organ. Not voices, exactly. A humming, low and layered, dozens of throats at once, rising and falling in a pattern that never quite resolved into melody. It sounded like a hymn heard through water. It sounded like a hymn played backward and almost, almost made sense.

Mara stood at the lychgate for a long time. Her breath hung around her face and did not disperse—it drifted instead toward the church door, thin threads of her own warmth being drawn inside, and she watched them go with the detached clarity of someone watching their own blood run downhill.

*You don't have to go in,* she told herself. *You could walk out to the road. You could dig. You could freeze honestly, on your own terms.*

But Mrs. Aldersey's voice was in her head, steady as a handrail: *It doesn't take you by force, girl. It never has to. It just makes everywhere else worse than where it wants you.* And beneath that, older and colder, her own memory of Nell's journal, the torn pages, the rite copied out in her sister's careful schoolgirl hand: *call back what was lost.*

Nell had gone looking for something in the dark. The least Mara could do was look too.

She pushed open the churchyard gate. It swung without a sound, though every hinge in Hollowmere screamed in this cold, and the snow accepted her boots without a single crunch, and she understood that the silence was not emptiness but attention. The whole night was holding its breath around her.

The church door stood open six inches. Blue light breathed through the gap.

Inside smelled of wax and cold stone and, faintly, unbearably, of Nell's shampoo—the cheap coconut kind she'd bought in bulk from the catalogue, the smell of a hundred ordinary evenings, and Mara's eyes flooded before she could stop them, grief ambushing her through her own nose.

The nave was full.

Every pew was occupied. The entire village was there—Mrs. Tregowan from the shop, the Hendersons from the farm, old Mr. Pike who hadn't left his house in years according to anyone Mara had asked, children she'd never seen, faces she half-knew and faces she didn't know at all but which nonetheless looked familiar the way a word looks familiar when you've stared at it too long. They all faced forward. They all wore their winter coats. Snow still clung to some of their shoulders, unmelted, and when Mara looked closer—at Mrs. Tregowan's collar, at the white dusting on a young man's sleeve—she saw that it wasn't melting. In the warm air of the church, packed with living bodies, the snow sat on them like frost sits on a window: in patterns. Branching figures. Small ones, over each heart.

No one turned to look at her. That was somehow worse than if they had.

At the front, where the altar should have been, the vicar stood with his back to the congregation. Beyond him, hanging in the tower arch where a bell should have hung, was nothing—and yet the nothing had weight. Mara could see the air there bending, the way air bends above hot tarmac, outlining a shape that her eyes kept sliding off: a curve, a shoulder, a mouth. The ghost-shape of a bell that had been broken up and carted away in 1911, rendered in negative space, ringing without sound. She could feel the ringing in her teeth. In the fillings of her teeth. In the bones behind her eyes.

And they were singing.

The congregation sang without opening their mouths past a certain point—lips parted, breath moving, the sound coming from somewhere deeper than lungs—and the hymn ran backward. Mara knew it. It was the evening hymn from their childhood, the one sung at every harvest festival and funeral, *Abide With Me*, and they were singing it end to beginning, last verse to first, each line reversed syllable by syllable, so that the words arrived in her ears already dead, already unmade, meaning dismantled into pure sound. *E-em em htiw ediba.* It should have been gibberish. It was gibberish. But underneath the gibberish she could hear what it was becoming, what it was being unwound toward: the first line, the opening plea, the original request. *Fast falls the eventide; the darkness deepens.* They weren't singing a hymn. They were un-singing it, walking it back to the moment before it was written, before anyone had ever needed comforting, back to the silence that hymns are built to hold off.

Mara's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the cold oak of the back pew.

*Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace.* The words surfaced in her mind unbidden, her grandmother's funeral, she'd been eleven, Nell had gripped her hand so hard throughout the service that the marks lasted a week. Nell had always hated funerals. Nell had cried at the death of a hedgehog. Nell, who was afraid of the dark until she was twelve and then decided, with typical Nell logic, that if she memorized the house in the dark she couldn't be afraid of it anymore, and had walked every room blindfolded while Mara timed her—

Stop. Stop. Thinking about Nell here, in this place, felt like leaving a door open.

The hymn ended—or rather, arrived at its beginning, the final note of the reversal landing soft as snowfall—and the silence afterward had texture, had intention. Two hundred people breathing slowly in the dark. The empty bell-shape swaying very slightly in the tower arch, though no wind reached them.

Then the vicar turned around.

He had been their vicar once—Reverend Calloway, a mild, stooping man who'd christened neither twin but had buried their mother with genuine kindness, fumbling his notes, crying a little himself. The man who turned now wore Calloway's body and Calloway's cassock, but he moved wrong, smoothly, all at once, like a puppet whose operator had finally gotten the hang of the strings. His face was calm and pleasant and attentive, and his eyes went directly to Mara at the back of the church as though he had known precisely where she was standing the entire time.

"You're late," he said.

His voice was gentle. Warm, even. The voice of a man welcoming someone to a service that had been arranged specially for her.

"You're late, Nell."

The name went through her like a needle finding a nerve. Around the nave, two hundred heads turned—not fast, not mechanical, but with the smooth unhurried synchrony of a field of flowers following the sun—and every face in the church settled on her with an expression of mild, fond reproach. The expression you give a sister who is late again. The expression that says *we waited, we saved your seat, we knew you'd come.*

"There's a seat for you," the vicar said, gesturing to the front pew, and Mara saw it: a gap in the second row, exactly one person wide, kept clear, cushion plumped, a order of service laid on it in careful typescript. She could read the heading from where she stood, because part of her brain insisted on reading it, insisted on processing the words even as the rest of her recoiled:

*A Service of Thanksgiving for the Safe Return of Nell Dunmore.*

"No," Mara said. Her voice came out small in the huge dark. "My name is Mara. I'm Nell's sister. Nell is—" *dead*, she meant to say, and found she couldn't, found the word snagging on something in her throat like a fishhook, because the coffin downstairs at the undertaker's had held a stranger's face and the journal had been torn and the villagers all remembered a life she'd never lived and somewhere in the last nine days the world had quietly rearranged itself so that the sentence *my sister is dead* no longer pointed at anything true.

The vicar smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the horror of it—that every face in that church was turned toward her with love, genuine, patient, bottomless love, the love of people who had known you your whole life, and none of it belonged to her. None of it had ever been hers. It was Nell's love, Nell's welcome, Nell's homecoming, and she was standing in it wearing the wrong name.

"We've missed you," said Mrs. Tregowan, from the third pew, and her voice was wet with real tears. "Eleven years, Nell. We lit candles every Christmas."

"You used to sit right there," said a boy of maybe ten, pointing at the empty seat, and his mother shushed him fondly, and Mara's knees nearly went, because the boy was right, she could feel it, some borrowed knowledge settling into her like sediment: the third pew, left side, next to the pillar, where the Dunmores had always sat, where *she* had always sat, except she hadn't, she'd sat nowhere, she'd never once set foot in this church before her mother's funeral, she was sure of it, she was almost sure of it, she was—

"Come and sit down, Nell." The vicar extended his hand. Behind him the empty bell swayed, and in the silence Mara realized she could hear it now, faintly, the way you can hear the sea in a shell: not a ring but a remembering of ringing, a sound worn into the shape of the thing that made it. "We're only waiting for you. We've been waiting such a long time."

And the terrible thing, the thing Mara would carry out of that church and into everything that came after, was that some part of her wanted to go. Wanted it with a physical ache, homesickness for a home that had never existed, exhaustion beyond describing, eleven years of running from a grief she wouldn't even look at—if she sat down in that pew, if she let them call her Nell, she would never have to be the one who survived again. They would do the remembering for her. They would hold the memories so she didn't have to. All she had to do was stop insisting on being Mara Dunmore, whoever that had ever been.

Her boot scraped the floor—one inch backward, entirely involuntary.

The vicar's smile widened by a degree. "There you are," he said softly.

And that was when Mara saw it. At the edge of the blue light, half-hidden behind the stone pillar beside the empty pew: a smudge on the wall. Frost, a small branching figure, the same design as her windows—but this one was wrong. One branch bent at an angle that broke the pattern, a flaw, an error, repeated on the frost-figure etched faintly on the pillar, and again on the icy bloom creeping across the floor near the altar. Three errors. Three places where the pattern didn't match itself, as if the hand writing it had trembled, or hesitated, or was trying, from somewhere deep inside the writing, to spell something else.

Someone was in there. Someone was fighting the pen.

"Nell," the vicar said again, patient, loving, extending his hand further, and the whole congregation rose to their feet in one motion, facing her, two hundred mouths beginning to move together in the reversed hymn once more, and the empty bell began, at last, to make a sound—

Mara ran.

She didn't decide to. Her body decided, hurling itself sideways out of the pew-gap and up the aisle while the hymn swelled behind her, and she burst through the church door into the freezing yard with the sound chasing her—not angry, not pursuing, just continuing, patient as weather, the un-sung hymn rolling out across the snow after her like a tide. She ran through the lychgate, down Church Lane, past the dark houses with their frosted windows, her lungs full of knives, and behind her the bell rang on and on, and woven into its tone now, unmistakable, she could hear what the flawed frost-figures had been trying to say, what the trembling hand inside the pattern had been spelling all along, in the only language the trapped can speak:

Not her name.

A warning. Three words, over and over, hidden in the ringing like a pulse under skin:

*Don't remember me.*

Mara stopped in the middle of the lane, doubled over, breath steaming, and stared back at the church glowing blue against the stars.

Don't remember her? After everything—after the journal, the rite, the knocking, the village wearing her sister's life like a stolen coat?

But even as the question formed, she felt the answer assembling itself in the frost on the nearest window, in the flaw in the pattern, in the one thing the entity could not copy because Nell would not let it:

Her sister was alive in there. And she was terrified—not of the thing that held her.

Of what Mara was about to remember.
