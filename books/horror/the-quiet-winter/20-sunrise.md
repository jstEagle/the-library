## 20. Sunrise

The bell cracked like a heart breaking.

Mara felt it before she heard it—a shudder through the frozen ground, up through her knees where she knelt in the snow beneath the yew tree, and then the sound itself: not a ring but a groan, a long metallic exhalation, as if something that had held its breath for a century finally let it go. The half-formed bell in the earth split along a seam of darkness, and out of that seam poured everything it had been holding.

Not light. Not shadow. *Sound.*

Voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Names spoken in the accents of generations—Hollowmere voices, flat and broad and old, calling children home for supper across decades that had already ended. A woman singing a lullaby in 1911. A man laughing in 1943. A boy crying for his dog in 1968, the same year Mrs. Aldersey's son went into the white and did not come back. All of it rising from the crack in the bell like heat from a broken oven, and all of it passing through Mara as though she were made of open doors.

She didn't try to stand. She understood, with the strange clarity that comes at the end of a long night, that she was meant to stay kneeling. That this was the last part of it. The rite had needed a truth spoken; the bell had needed to be heard.

So she listened.

* * *

The village woke all at once, the way a sleeper wakes from a nightmare—not gradually but violently, sitting upright in their beds with their hearts hammering and their hands pressed over their mouths.

In the house on Chapel Lane, Mr. and Mrs. Fenwick came awake in each other's arms, and Mrs. Fenwick said, "Edith," aloud—the name of a sister who had died of fever in 1952, whose face she had not been able to picture for three days without it sliding into someone else's—and then she could picture her again, freckles and chapped lips and the way she'd chewed the ends of her hair, and Mrs. Fenwick wept into her husband's shoulder with relief so fierce it felt like grief.

In the square, the vicar collapsed against the churchyard wall as the hymn he'd been singing in his sleep unwound from his tongue. He remembered his mother. He remembered his mother's funeral, forty years gone, and how he had stood dry-eyed because someone had to, and how the thing in the winter had found that dryness and used it, had worn her voice to him every night since, saying *you did well, Thomas, you did well,* until he could no longer remember whether she had ever said it or whether he had only needed it so badly he'd built the memory himself. Now he knew. She had never said it. She had died mid-sentence, reaching for his hand. And knowing it hurt more than anything the entity had ever done to him, and he was grateful for the hurt, because it was *his.*

All across Hollowmere, in beds and kitchens and doorways, people got themselves back. The false memories dissolved like frost on glass touched by a warm palm—the childhoods Mara never had, the picnics on the hill that never happened, the version of Nell who had baked bread with the Hendersons and taught the vicar's niece to skate. What remained underneath was thinner and truer: ordinary lives, ordinary losses, and one extraordinary shared night that they would spend the rest of their lives failing to explain to anyone outside the valley.

And in the square, in the church, in the lanes, they began to say her name.

Not Nell's. Not yet.

*Mara Dunmore.* They said it the way you say the name of someone who has pulled you from a river. They said it into the grey beginning of morning, some of them still in their nightclothes, standing in their doorways and looking toward the fields beyond the village where the yew tree stood black against the whitening sky.

* * *

Mara knelt in the snow and watched the bell die.

It came apart slowly, the way ice leaves a pond in spring—not collapsing but *releasing*, edges softening, the great dark mass of it sinking into the earth even as it thinned, as though the ground beneath the yew had been holding its shape in trust all these years and was now taking it back. The voices grew quieter as it went. Not fading—*finishing*. Each one sounded, to Mara, like a sentence completed. A name finally spoken at a graveside. An apology delivered sixty years late. A lullaby sung to the end instead of abandoned halfway.

The unmourned dead were being mourned. That was all the monster had ever been—that was what she saw now, kneeling in the ruin of it, and the seeing broke her open worse than any terror of the past week. It had been made of the grief nobody stayed for. The funerals skipped because the roads were bad. The deaths too shameful to mention. The children lost to the winter in 1911 whose parents had performed the Calling Back because the church gave them nothing else, and whom the church had answered by melting down the bell, as if you could silence hunger by destroying the dinner bell.

It had only ever wanted what Nell had wanted. To be called back. To be *known.*

"I know," Mara whispered, and her breath made a small cloud in the freezing air, and the cloud hung there longer than breath should hang, as if the morning were listening. "I know what you are. I'm sorry we left you out in the cold."

The last of the bell sank. The voices went with it, gently, like lights going out one by one down a long corridor. And then the field was silent, and the sky in the east had turned the colour of the inside of a shell, and Mara was alone under the yew tree with two graves and a truth she had spoken aloud for the first time in twenty-five years.

*Nell died saving me from the ice. I let myself forget.*

Her body ached with it—not dramatically, not the way pain is written in stories, but dully, thoroughly, the way you ache after carrying something heavy a very long way. Twenty-five years of carrying. She sat back in the snow, not caring about the cold soaking through her coat, and let herself feel the whole weight of it at last: the pond, the grey ice, the sound it made when it took her sister. The small hand letting go of hers. The terrible arithmetic of a nine-year-old's mind deciding that survival was the same thing as guilt, and that forgetting was the only mercy available.

It hadn't been. But she had been nine. And Nell—Nell would have told her that, if Nell had lived. Nell would have taken her face in both cold-chapped hands and said, *Mara, you daft girl, it wasn't your fault,* with that particular bossy tenderness that older-by-four-minutes sisters reserve for younger ones.

The thought arrived whole and warm and unbearable, and Mara laughed—a cracked, wet sound, half sob—and pressed her mittened hands over her eyes and cried for her sister properly, at last, in the place where her sister actually was, instead of in front of a coffin holding a stranger's borrowed face.

* * *

She didn't see Nell arrive. There was no arrival. One moment the field was empty except for the greying snow and the yew tree, and the next there was a figure standing a few yards off, and Mara's heart knew it before her eyes finished reporting.

Nell stood in the snow in the clothes she'd been wearing the night she died—or rather, the clothes Mara had always imagined: the red scarf their mother had knitted, the brown coat with the missing button. Nine years old forever, or wearing nine years old like a garment over something larger and older and nearly done. Her outline was not quite solid. Snow fell through her shoulders and did not land.

Mara got to her feet. Her legs shook. Every instinct honed by the past week screamed at her to run—but those instincts had been trained by the thing that wore faces, and this was not that thing. She knew it the way she'd known the wrong face in the coffin: instantly, in the blood.

Because the mimicry had always been perfect, and this wasn't. This Nell was *flawed*—her edges trembled, her features shifted subtly, the nose a little too long then right again, the eyes brown then grey then brown—as though she were being remembered rather than seen, and memory is an imprecise artist. The entity had copied Nell flawlessly because it had no love to blur its work. This Nell was blurred because she existed now only inside Mara's love, and love does not render faithfully. Love renders *truly.*

"Nell," Mara said. Her voice came out ruined.

"Hi, Mar." The smile was crooked, the way it had always been crooked, the left side higher. "You look awful."

"You're—" Mara stopped. Started again. "You've been in there. All this time. In it."

"In the bell? Yeah." Nell looked down at the flattened place in the snow where the metal had lain, and her expression was complicated—not trauma, exactly, not peace either, but the tired clarity of someone who has been through something enormous and is still sorting it. "It's hard to explain. It wasn't—it didn't feel like being trapped. It felt like being *unheard.* Like shouting into snow." She looked up. "I called and called, Mara. For years. You couldn't hear me because you wouldn't. And the thing that lives in the gaps where mourning should be—it heard. It learned my voice from your silence. Isn't that the worst thing?" Her head tilted. "It knew me better than you did, near the end. Because it was listening to everything you refused to."

Mara flinched. "I know."

"No—" Nell stepped closer, and the snow did not crunch under her, and her face was urgent now, urgent and gentle at once. "No, listen. I'm not here to make you sorry. I spent twenty-five years wanting you to be sorry and it got us here." She gestured at the field, the village, the melting frost. "Being known isn't the same as being pitied. You finally said it out loud. That's all I ever wanted. Not your guilt. Just—you, knowing me. Knowing *us.*"

"It was my fault," Mara said, because she had to say it once more, plainly, to the one person it mattered to. "You pushed me out. You went under instead of me. If I'd—"

"If you'd grabbed harder we'd both have drowned." Nell said it simply, the way she'd corrected Mara's sums at the kitchen table. "Nine-year-olds don't grab harder, Mara. You were a child. I was a child. The ice was ice." She smiled again, and this time the smile held everything. "You were my favourite person in the world. Do you know that? Even after. Especially after. I got to watch you grow up. From wherever I was—I got to see you become this stubborn, frightened, brilliant woman who counts the doors twice and locks them three times and came home anyway, in a blizzard, to bury a stranger because she thought it was me." Her voice caught—the first imperfect thing about her that wasn't love. "That's the bravest stupid thing anyone's ever done."

Mara crossed the distance between them. She knew—some deep animal knowledge knew—that she could not touch her, that her arms would close on cold air and falling snow. She reached anyway.

Nell reached too.

Their hands almost met. In the space between them, an inch of winter air, the snow seemed to slow, each flake hanging like a held note. And in that inch Mara felt warmth—not on her skin, but somewhere behind her ribs, in the sealed room where her sister had been locked for twenty-five years, the door swinging open at last onto light.

"Stay," Mara whispered. It was a child's word. She let it be.

"I can't." No cruelty in it. Only fact, the way Nell had always delivered facts. "I'm the frost, Mar. I'm the last of it. When the sun comes up—" She glanced east, where the horizon had begun to burn a thin gold through the grey, and did not seem afraid. "But listen. Before I go. The house. Leave the lights on. Not because of fear. Because—" and here she grinned, sudden and eleven-years-old, stealing biscuits, "—because a lit window is just a bell you can see. Someone might be out there listening. Someone always is."

"Nell—"

"And tell Mrs. Aldersey—" Nell paused, and her expression softened into something wondering. "Tell her Tom says the snow was quick. She'll know. She's known since 1968; she just needed permission to believe it."

The gold line of sunrise crept up the field toward them.

Nell began to fade—not dissolving, not tragically, but the way a guest leaves a party: gradually, turning back once, becoming less present and no less real. Her edges loosened into the brightening air. Her face was the last thing to go, and it was smiling, and it was *hers*, crooked and certain and four minutes older than Mara's own.

"Thank you," she said. "For coming back. For saying it. For knowing me." The words were already half wind. "Best twin. Don't count the doors anymore, yeah? Leave one open."

And then the sun cleared the ridge, and the light came down the field like a tide, and where Nell had stood there was only snow, unmarked, glittering, ordinary—except for one patch, at the height of a nine-year-old's shoulders, where the frost on a fence post had bloomed overnight into a small branching pattern, a fern of ice, a signature.

As Mara watched, the sun touched it, and it melted, and ran down the post like a wave goodbye.

* * *

She walked back into Hollowmere as the village came fully awake, and it was a different waking than any she had witnessed before. Smoke rose from chimneys in honest columns. Doors opened onto thresholds where people stood blinking in the daylight like divers surfacing. And everywhere she passed, the villagers turned to her—not with the blank complicit stares of the night, nor the too-warm familiarity of the stolen memories, but with something rawer and better: recognition. Gratitude. The awkward tenderness of people who owe someone their selves and don't yet know how to say so.

Mrs. Aldersey met her at the bridge over the frozen beck. The old woman looked hollowed out—she had given the bell her son, her dearest wound, and bought Mara the hour she'd needed—and also strangely light, the way a house looks after furniture has been carried out of it. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady.

"He came to me," she said, without preamble. "At the end. While I was walking. Tom. He said—" her voice wavered, steadied— "he said the snow was quick. Did Nell tell you?"

"She did. She said you'd know."

Mrs. Aldersey closed her eyes. For a moment she was ninety years of winters, and then she opened them and she was simply Edith Aldersey, who had survived 1911 in her grandmother's arms and 1968 in her own, and who had, in the end, chosen to walk into the white for a stranger because kindness was the only weapon grief had ever left her.

"Well then," she said briskly, and took Mara's arm in hers, gloved fingers gripping firm. "The kettle's on at mine. And then, Miss Dunmore, you and I are going to plan a proper funeral. Two of them, I should think. One for that poor soul in the coffin—whoever she was, she deserves her name back—and one for your sister." She squeezed Mara's arm. "And this time, we'll tell the truth at it. All of it. That's how you keep a thing buried, my girl. Not with bells melted down and pages torn out. With the truth, said out loud, in front of everyone, in the daylight."

They walked together up the lane, two survivors, as the sun climbed over Hollowmere and the frost let go of the windows one pane at a time.

Behind them, on the bridge rail, a single thread of ice bloomed—brief, branching, almost like handwriting—and melted in the strengthening light, leaving only a trail of bright water running downhill toward the village, like something hurrying home.
