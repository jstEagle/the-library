## 5. What the Walls Keep

The trouble with living in a house for twelve years is that you stop seeing it. Felix had learned this the way she learned most things — slowly, reluctantly, and only after the evidence became impossible to file under "coincidence."

She still ran her routines. Tuesday mornings were for the exterior walk: pacing the perimeter with her old measuring wheel, noting that the east wall was four inches longer than it had been in 2013 and six inches shorter than it had been last spring. She wrote the numbers down because writing things down was what surveyors did, and as long as she was doing her job — technically, faithfully, absurdly — she was not a woman who had been swallowed by a house. She was a professional conducting a very long site visit.

"You're not trapped," she told the kettle on Thursday mornings, because the kettle was the only one who never answered back. "You're thorough."

The kettle hummed its agreement.

But since the recordings — since the voice, patient and low, reciting her childhood address like a lullaby of its own — the routines had begun to feel less like armor and more like a hiding place whose location was already known. She'd stopped sleeping with the recorder running. Then, unable to help herself, she'd started again. The tape from Wednesday night held nothing but her own breathing and, at 3:07 a.m., the walls' hum, closer than usual. Almost fond.

It was the wallpaper that finally broke her.

* * *

The paper in the upstairs hallway had been bothering her for years in the way a splinter bothers you — ignorable until your attention snagged on it, then unbearable. It was a faded floral, roses gone the color of weak tea, and beneath the pattern there was texture. Bumps. Ridges. She'd always assumed plaster damage, settling, the ordinary scars of an old house.

On Friday morning, hungover on three hours of sleep and a stubbornness she couldn't locate the source of, Felix stood at the hallway wall with a cup of coffee and pressed her palm flat against one of the ridges.

It was a letter. A scratched letter, shallow, deliberate, under the paper.

She should have walked away. Twelve years of practice walking away from every wrong thing in this house, and she knew all the exits of her own denial by heart. Instead she went to the kitchen, got the steamer she'd bought in year two and used exactly once, and stood in the hallway looking up at the ceiling like a burglar checking for witnesses.

"Thorough," she said to herself. "This is just thorough."

The first strip came away in a long curl, releasing a smell like dust and something faintly sweet — cold water, stone, the inside of a well. Underneath: plaster, gray-green and damp-looking even though the house hadn't leaked once in twelve years. And under that, catching the light from the landing window, scratches. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

Names.

*MARGARET HOLLIS*, carved deep, confident, the letters of someone who expected to be read. Below it, smaller, shakier: *Margaret Hollis 1971–?* The question mark trailed off into a gouge, as if the hand had been interrupted mid-stroke.

Felix crouched. There were more. Layers of them, some scratched over others, a palimpsest of tenants: *E. VANCE*. *the Aldridges we are all here* — that one in two different hands, the second hand adding *we are all here* beneath *the Aldridges* like a footnote nobody wanted to write. A child's alphabet, practiced twice through and abandoned at *Q*. A name she couldn't read at all, worked so deep into the plaster it had gone glossy.

And every single one ended mid-word.

Not mid-sentence. Mid-*word*. *MARGARET HOLLIS BELOV—*. *E. VANCE FORGI—*. *SAMUEL ALDRIDGE I AM NOT AFRA—*. As if each writer had been plucked away between letters. As if the wall itself had run out of patience with them.

"Okay," Felix said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller; this had been her policy since childhood. "Okay. People lived here. People scratched their names in walls. This is called being human, and it happens in every house older than the moon landing."

Her voice sounded fine. Reasonable. It did not sound like a woman reading epitaphs.

She worked along the wall strip by strip, and the names kept coming, and the dates — where there were dates — kept getting closer to the present. The last legible one was near the baseboard, small and neat, in handwriting that made her stomach drop before her brain caught up:

*F.T., arrived April—*

April. Her arrival month. Her initial. In handwriting nearly identical to her own, though she had never — she had *never* —

She sat down hard on the stairs and drank her coffee, which had gone cold, which felt appropriate.

* * *

By afternoon she'd cleared the whole hallway wall, and the light was going amber, and she told herself she would stop after the next strip, and then after the next one, the way she'd once told herself she'd leave after the appraisal closed, after the market turned, after the winter, after.

It was while she was peeling the final section — behind where a mirror had once hung, judging by the un-faded rectangle — that the hum started.

It always started around now. Three o'clock, or close enough; the house kept its own hours and had never once consulted a clock. Usually it was just that: a hum, low and tonal, like a refrigerator dreaming, like power moving through wire. She'd catalogued it in year one (*audible vibration, source undetermined, recommend acoustic survey*) and had spent twelve years not thinking about it very loudly.

But tonight, with the wallpaper off and the wall's skin broken, the hum changed.

It resolved.

That was the only word for it. The way a radio resolves out of static when you find the station — except there was no station, no speaker, no anything, just plaster and lath and the bones of the house, and out of all of it a melody assembled itself note by note, unhurried, like something that had been waiting a very long time for her to take the paper off and listen properly.

Felix stood with the steamer still warm in her hand and did not move.

She knew the melody.

That was the impossible part, the part her mind kept trying to return for exchange. It wasn't an old hymn or a folk tune or something she could have absorbed from a neighbor's radio through two layers of siding. It rose in thirds and fell back, simple and looping, the kind of tune you make up without knowing you're making it up. It had a hiccup in the fourth bar where her mother had always — where her mother used to laugh, mid-phrase, because Felix as a baby had kicked at exactly that point, every time, like a tiny drummer keeping time.

A song her mother sang to her, thirty-five years ago, in a kitchen four hundred miles from here.

A song Felix had never sung for anyone. Never hummed. Never mentioned. Not to friends, not to lovers, not to the one therapist she'd seen for six weeks in her twenties. When her mother died, the song had gone into the ground with her, and Felix had locked the box and buried the key and built a quiet life directly on top of it.

And the house was humming it back to her. Note-perfect. Hiccup included.

"No," Felix said.

The melody paused — actually paused, the way a person pauses when spoken to — and resumed, gentler, coaxing.

"No." Her voice cracked on it. "You don't get that. You don't get to have that."

She looked down at the wall, at the names ending mid-word, at *F.T., arrived April—*, and the two facts collided in her chest and detonated quietly: the house didn't haunt randomly. It wasn't a generic hunger, a ghost's indiscriminate appetite. It collected. It took the thing you hid deepest — Margaret Hollis's beloveds, Samuel Aldridge's fear, E. Vance's forgiveness, whoever's alphabet — and it kept them, scratched them into its own body, sang them back. Every secret was a verse. Every tenant was a bar of music.

And Felix, who had spent twelve years perfecting invisibility, who had told herself that silence was safety, that if she asked nothing and offered nothing the house would have nothing of hers to take —

The house had been listening the whole time. Had taken the one thing she'd never said aloud and set it to music in the walls.

She laughed. It came out wrong, high and wet, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. Somewhere below, the kettle began to whistle, unprompted, on an empty stove, and she let it whistle because going downstairs meant turning her back on the wall.

"I was careful," she whispered. "I was so careful."

The hum swelled, almost tender, and for one vertiginous second Felix understood — really understood, in her body — that the care had been the point. That hiding was how you fed it. That every year of silence had been a year of the house learning her by heart, and that the names on the wall belonged to people who had also been careful, also quiet, also sure they were the exception.

The melody reached the end of its loop and began again, and this time, at the hiccup in the fourth bar, another voice joined it. Low. Patient. Familiar from a hundred nights of tape.

It harmonized with the walls perfectly.

And Felix's throat — her own traitor throat — tightened with the awful, involuntary urge to join in.

She bit it down. She gripped the steamer like a weapon, which it was not, and backed down the stairs with her eyes on the stripped wall, where in the last of the light the scratches seemed to shift, subtly, the way text shifts when you're falling asleep — and at the bottom of the wall, beneath *F.T., arrived April—*, in plaster that had been bare an hour ago, new letters were forming. Slowly. Like something being written from the other side.

*F.T., who hides her mother's s—*

Mid-word. Waiting for her to finish it.

Felix went downstairs, put the recorder on the kitchen table, and pressed record with a hand that almost obeyed her.

"Ruth," she said to the empty room, remembering the sister's name from the county file, remembering the phone number she'd written down weeks ago and never dialed. "I think I need to know how the song ends."
