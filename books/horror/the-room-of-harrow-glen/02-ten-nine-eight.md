## 2. Ten, Nine, Eight

Alma Reyes had been counting for eleven days.

Not constantly — she was not a woman given to constant anything. She had been a schoolteacher for forty years, and even now, morphine-thin and yellowed at the edges, she retained the particular authority of someone who had spent her life making children sit down and be quiet. When the count came to her, it came in that voice: low, unhurried, patient as a metronome wound by God.

"Ten," she would say, sometimes at three in the morning, sometimes at noon while the sun lay across her blanket like a sleeping cat. "Nine." And then she might stop for hours, drifting, before resuming somewhere in the middle — "Six... five..." — as though the numbers were a staircase she descended one step at a time, pausing on each landing to look out a window only she could see.

Quentin had documented it all.

He kept his notebooks in the bottom drawer of the nurses' station, beneath a false folder labeled *IN-SERVICE TRAINING 2019* that no one had ever opened. There were nine of them now, cheap composition books with marbled covers, filled edge to edge with his small, precise handwriting. He wrote in them the way other men prayed — not because he believed anyone was listening, but because the act itself held something at bay. Grief, mostly. The slow accumulation of endings that hospice work pressed against him daily, like water against a dam.

*Patient: Alma Reyes, 81. Admitted 14 October. Count onset: day two of admission.*

*Pattern: begins at ten. Does not always finish a sequence before sleep or medication interrupts. Has never reached below four.*

*Vocal quality: calm. No distress. As if reciting something learned long ago.*

He had asked her about it once, early on, when she still had the strength for conversation and the appetite for tea. He'd phrased it gently, the way he phrased everything — *I've noticed you counting, Alma. Does it help?* — expecting the usual answers. Pain management. A prayer. A habit from childhood, counting stairs, counting sheep.

She had looked at him for a long moment over the rim of her cup, and her eyes — brown going gray at the rims, like frost creeping inward across a window — had held something he hadn't expected.

"It isn't me doing the counting, dear," she said. "I'm just saying it out loud so you can hear."

Then she'd smiled, patted his hand with fingers like dry paper, and asked if there was any more honey for the tea.

He had written that down too. He told himself it was the morphine talking, the way the dying said strange things, spoke to people who weren't in the room, reached for hands that had been dust for decades. He had a whole taxonomy of deathbed phenomena catalogued in those notebooks, each entry a small act of classification that felt, if he was honest with himself, like an act of defense. If he could name it, file it, give it a heading and a date, then it belonged to the world of things understood. Things survivable.

He did not write down what he actually felt when Alma said *so you can hear*, which was a cold finger drawn slowly up the inside of his spine, and the sudden animal certainty that he should take his hand off her blanket and step back from the bed.

*

Harrow Glen in late autumn smelled of woodsmoke and antiseptic and, underneath both, the particular sweetness of the dying — a scent Quentin had stopped noticing years ago the way you stop noticing your own house's smell. The building was old enough to have opinions. It had been a private sanatorium once, back when tuberculosis was called consumption and wealthy families shipped their consumptive daughters to the hills to cough themselves elegant and pale. The east wing had been sealed since before Quentin's time — boarded windows, a door painted shut, a corridor that appeared on none of the current floor plans. Delphine Marchand, the head nurse, answered questions about it with a flat look and a single word: *Structural.* No one asked twice. That was the kind of place Harrow Glen was — old enough to keep its secrets comfortably, small enough that everyone agreed, without discussion, to let it.

Quentin loved it. He had never quite managed to say this aloud to anyone, because it sounded morbid — loving a building whose entire purpose was to receive the dying — but he loved it the way sailors love ships: for its competence, its creaks, its willingness to hold what it held without complaint. In eleven years he had sat with sixty-two people through their final hours. He knew the exact weight of a hand going slack. He knew how light changed in a room when breathing changed in it, though he could never have explained the mechanism. He knew that grief did not arrive like weather; it arrived like plumbing, quietly, through pipes you forgot were in the walls.

And he knew — had known for years, had written it down in notebook after notebook until the knowledge lost its power to frighten him — that the dying counted backwards from ten.

Not all of them. But most. Far more than chance could explain. They counted in whispers and in full voice, in languages they hadn't spoken since childhood, one woman in Portuguese, a man who had been a bomber pilot in a clipped radio cadence, an old farmer who got stuck on seven for two full days and then skipped it entirely, as though the number had been removed from his staircase without his notice.

Quentin had a theory, of course. He was a rational man; theories were how he kept the dark at a comfortable distance. The brain, starved of oxygen, cycling through its final patterns. Countdowns were everywhere in human culture — New Year's Eve, rocket launches, the last ten seconds of every close football game. The mind reaching for the most familiar ritual of ending. Of course the dying counted down. What else would they do?

The theory was load-bearing. He leaned on it nightly.

On the night Alma died, it snowed — the first real snow of the year, fat flakes falling straight down through the sodium glow of the parking lot lamps, erasing the world politely, one white layer at a time.

*

It was 11:40 when Priya found him at the station, refilling a syringe driver from the locked cabinet.

"She's asking for you," Priya said. "Not the on-call. You." She was twenty-six and still wore her stethoscope like jewelry, and she looked at Quentin with the open, unguarded admiration he found both touching and slightly embarrassing. "She said, tell Quentin it's time to listen."

"Listen to what?"

Priya shrugged. "That's what she said. Word for word. I didn't ask."

Room 7 was warm — Alma had always run cold, and the radiator in her room ticked like a patient clock. The lamp was on low, amber, and the snow pressed at the window with soft persistent fingers. Her daughter had gone home two days ago, promising to return Sunday; Alma had waved her off with the brisk affection of a woman clearing the deck. She had wanted to do this part unaccompanied, she'd said, except for the professional.

Except for him.

She looked smaller than she had that morning. This was the thing no one warned you about, the thing Quentin had documented in his own private vocabulary: the dying shrank in advance, the way tide pulls back from a shore before the wave. Their bodies seemed to sense the approach of some enormous pressure and drew inward to meet it. Alma lay with her hands folded on the coverlet — she had arranged them herself, he realized, folded just so, like a woman setting a table for company — and her face was turned toward the window, toward the snow.

"Quentin," she said. Her voice was thin but level. "Pull up the chair. I don't want you hovering. Hovering makes me feel like homework."

He pulled up the chair. Sat. Took the hand she offered, which was cool and light as a bird's foot.

"You're not in pain?"

"I'm past pain, dear. Pain is for people with plans." She smiled at the ceiling. Then, quieter: "It's coming tonight. I can hear it on the stairs."

"The count?"

Her eyes moved to him. For a moment something flickered behind them — amusement, maybe, or apology. "You've been writing us all down, haven't you. In your little books. Don't look shocked. Dying people know things. It's the one perk of the position." She squeezed his fingers with surprising strength. "Keep writing. Someone should."

"Alma—"

"Shh." She closed her eyes. "Here it comes."

And she began.

"Ten."

The word fell into the room like a stone into still water. Outside, the snow thickened; he could see it banking against the window ledge, silent, industrious.

"Nine."

Her breathing slowed between numbers. Not labored — *spaced*, as though each number required a journey to reach, and the journeys were getting longer.

"Eight."

Quentin watched the monitor. He had positioned himself, without quite admitting why, where he could see both her face and the screen. Heart rate: 61. Oxygen saturation drifting downward, 88, 87. All within the geography of a normal night. People lingered at these numbers for days.

"Seven."

A pause. Long. Her lips moved silently — he caught the shape of *six* forming, dissolving, reforming. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the hall a door sighed on its hinges, the house settling its old bones against the cold.

"Six," she said at last, barely above breath. "Five."

"Alma." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Your daughter — I can call her, if you'd like. There's time."

"There is exactly as much time as there is," she said, eyes still closed, "and not one second more. That's rather the point of the exercise." A ghost of her teacher-voice, the one that had made forty years of children sit down and be quiet. "Don't be frightened, Quentin. It's only a staircase."

"Four."

The monitor read 54. Her skin had begun, very subtly, to change — that translucence, that candle-behind-wax quality he knew better than his own signature.

"Three."

52.

"Two."

49. He felt the shift come into her hand first — not a grip, nothing so dramatic, just a change in the quality of her stillness, the way a clock changes quality in the instant before it strikes. Her breathing went long. Longer. The gap after *two* stretched out, five seconds, ten, and he found himself leaning forward, absurdly, willing the next number into the air, wanting to hear it, needing to hear it —

"One," Alma whispered.

And stopped.

Not the way people stop speaking. The way machines stop. The sentence simply ended mid-air, the final syllable still warm in the room, and the silence that followed had texture, had weight, like the hush inside a church.

The monitor flatlined at 12:07 a.m.

Quentin did what eleven years had trained into him. He checked her pulse with two fingers at the throat — nothing, and again at the wrist, pressing harder than necessary, wanting to be wrong. Nothing. He noted the time. He lifted the blanket and placed his stethoscope against her chest, and heard the great beautiful silence of a heart that had finished its work, and he straightened up, and he began — automatically, tenderly, the liturgy of the living attending the dead — to close her eyes.

Her eyelids resisted.

Gently, gently, he pressed them down with fingertip and palm, the way he'd done sixty-one times before, and they stayed down, and everything was proceeding exactly according to the protocol he could have recited in his sleep.

Which was when he saw her lips move.

They moved the way lips move when someone is speaking very softly to themselves — a small, precise articulation, the lower lip catching briefly against the upper. He stood absolutely still, stethoscope still in hand, and told himself it was a reflex. Agonal movement. The body's last static discharge, nerves firing their inventory, muscles twitching their goodbyes. He had seen hands twitch. He had seen feet draw up. He had seen, once, a man's jaw work for nearly a minute after everything else had stopped, and he had written *possible terminal reflexive mastication* in his notes and slept fine afterward.

But Alma's lips weren't twitching. They were *articulating*. And they were moving in rhythm.

He bent close. Close enough to smell the lavender cream she used, the faint mineral smell of a body cooling. Close enough to see, in the amber lamplight, the exact shapes her mouth made, one after another, unhurried, patient as a metronome wound by God:

*Three.*

*Two.*

*One.*

His own heartbeat was suddenly enormous in his ears. He straightened. Looked at the monitor — flat, green, unwavering. Looked at the clock: 12:09. Looked at her face, composed, eyes closed under his own gentle pressure, mouth moving with perfect calm diction, counting.

*Zero,* her lips said.

And stopped.

The room was very quiet. The snow ticked softly against the glass. Down the corridor, Priya laughed at something — a bright, ordinary sound, the sound of the living — and the ordinary sound made it worse, made this moment stand out against the fabric of the normal night like a black thread in white cloth.

Quentin stood there for a long time. He did not call Priya. He did not document the time of death on the chart, not yet. He stood beside Alma Reyes in the warm amber room and felt something shift in the foundation of himself — not collapse, nothing so dramatic. A hairline crack. The kind you find later, in spring, running through plaster you'd sworn was sound.

Because here was the thing his theory could not hold: reflexes did not have rhythm. Reflexes did not wait. Whatever had moved her mouth had waited — had paused after her whispered *one*, had let the silence stretch, had let him check her pulse, had let him press her eyes closed — and *then* it had continued. Three. Two. One. Zero. As though the first count had been interrupted and needed finishing. As though the numbers mattered more than the breathing. As though the counting were not a symptom of dying at all, but the actual event — and the death merely the interval between performances.

As though, somewhere, something had been waiting for her to get the numbers right.

Quentin sat down in the chair by the bed, the one she'd told him to pull up, and took out his notebook, and his hand was steady, which surprised him. He wrote the time of death. He wrote the clinical details, the dosages, the absence of distress. And then, beneath all of it, in the same small precise handwriting, he wrote what he had seen, plainly, without interpretation, the way you'd log a fever or a rash:

*At 12:09, approximately two minutes after cessation of cardiac function, patient's lips articulated the numbers three, two, one, zero, in sequence, over a period of eleven seconds. Movement ceased following the final number. Eyes remained closed (manually closed by this writer). No other motor activity observed.*

He read it back. It looked insane on the page. It looked true.

Below it, after a long moment, he added one more line — not documentation, not really. More like a question addressed to a room that had already stopped answering:

*Count resumed after death. Completed what the living count could not. Query: what was she counting toward?*

He closed the notebook. He called Priya, and they did the things that must be done — the care of the body, the dignity of the sheets, the phone call logged for morning. Priya cried a little, and he held her elbow in the corridor the way you comfort people you're fond of, and he said the true and useful things: *she wasn't in pain, she was ready, it was peaceful.* All of it accurate. None of it complete.

At 1:30 a.m., alone at the station with the snow erasing the parking lot lamp by lamp, Quentin took out the notebook again and reread his entry. Then he flipped backward through the previous pages, through Alma's eleven days of numbers, and found the note he'd written after the honey-and-tea conversation, and read her words again in his own handwriting:

*It isn't me doing the counting. I'm just saying it out loud so you can hear.*

So you can hear.

He had assumed she meant him.

He sat with that assumption for a while, turning it over, feeling its weight change in his hands. Then, carefully, he wrote one final line, and it was this line, more than the flat green line on the monitor, more than the moving lips, that kept him awake until dawn:

*Query amended: if she was saying it out loud so someone could hear — who else was listening?*

Outside, the snow kept falling on Harrow Glen, filling in the driveway, the garden paths, the tracks of the day, laying its white page over everything, smooth and blank and waiting to be written on.
