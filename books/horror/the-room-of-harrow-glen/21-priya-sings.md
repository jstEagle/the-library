## 21. Priya Sings

Priya did not remember walking into the east wing.

She remembered the door — how it had given under her hand like a held breath released, how the air beyond it had smelled of her mother's cardamom and old paper and something colder underneath, something like snow that had never fallen anywhere. She remembered the corridor stretching longer than the house could hold, and the walls with their thousands of small marks, and at the end of it all a doorway with no door in it, only light the color of late afternoon through gauze curtains.

And she remembered her mother's voice.

Not *Mija, come eat.* Not the scolding or the lullabies or the last wet whisper of her final week in this very house, four years ago, when Priya had been too young and too frightened to sit with her through the whole of the night. No. What she remembered was the counting.

*Ten,* her mother had said, from inside the light. *Nine.*

The way she used to count down to hide-and-seek. The way she counted down on New Year's Eve, Priya on her hip, both of them shouting the numbers at the dark as if they could command it. The way she had counted, the nurses said afterward, in her final hour — backwards from ten, over and over, her lips moving long after anything else in her had stopped.

Priya had thought, standing in that doorless doorway, that her mother was still playing the game. That if Priya just answered — if she joined the count — her mother would finally open her eyes and say *found you*, and laugh, and everything since would fold up like a finished afternoon.

So she had opened her mouth. And the number had come out of her in a voice that was hers and also not hers, deeper by one second, the way her reflection had begun to lag behind her in every glass she passed.

*Eight.*

After that, she did not remember. She only knew that she was walking now, and singing now, and that the song was a number, and the number was falling like snow.

* * *

Seven.

The word left her mouth and went ahead of her down the west corridor, and the house took it the way sand takes water. She heard it travel. She heard it enter the rooms.

In Room 4, where Mr. Abernathy lay with his lungs filling like slow tide, the word arrived before she did. Through the door she heard his cracked voice pick it up — *seven* — and then, wonderingly, as though he'd been waiting sixty years for permission: *six.*

He didn't know why. That was the mercy of it, or the horror; Priya could no longer tell them apart. He wasn't choosing. None of them were choosing. The count moved through Harrow Glen the way lamplight moves through a hallway at dusk, room to room, touching everything without asking, and everything it touched lit up and gave its voice back.

Five.

Delphine was coming down the stairs when Priya reached the landing. Priya saw her stop — saw the head nurse's hand close around the banister until the knuckles went white as candle wax — and saw her understand, in a single terrible instant, what was walking toward her wearing Priya's uniform and Priya's face.

"Priya." Delphine's voice was steady. Delphine's voice was always steady; it was the voice that had talked Marcus through panic attacks and families through farewells and once, famously, talked a dying man out of a decade of silence just long enough for him to forgive his brother. "Priya, look at me."

Priya looked at her. She was smiling. She could feel the smile sitting on her face like something set there, arranged, the way flowers are arranged on a table by hands that aren't yours.

"Four," Priya said.

"Stop." Delphine came down the last steps quickly, took Priya by the shoulders. Her grip was strong and her hands were freezing. "Priya. Whatever is in the east wing, whatever you heard — it isn't your mother. It wears voices. It has worn them for a hundred years. You have to fight it, do you hear me? You have to—"

"It isn't wearing her," Priya said, and heard how gently her own voice came out, how kind, how much worse than screaming. "It kept her. There's a difference, Delphine. You of all people know there's a difference."

Something moved behind Delphine's eyes — a door opening onto a room Priya had never been shown. "What did you see in there," Delphine whispered. "What did it show you."

"My mother." Priya reached up and touched Delphine's cheek, and felt the woman flinch as though the touch were cold. "Sitting up in bed. In the good light. Waiting. They're all waiting, everyone who ever counted here. They can't finish. They need someone to finish it *with* them. Don't you want to finish it with your sister?"

Delphine made a sound Priya had never heard a human being make — small, involuntary, the sound a house makes when its foundation shifts.

"You don't—" Delphine began.

"Three," said Mr. Abernathy, faintly, from Room 4. And then, from Room 7, old Marta Osei, who hadn't spoken in eleven days: "*Three.*" And from somewhere below, from the direction of the kitchen, a fourth voice joining in — low, male, reluctant, dragged along anyway, because no one in this house was exempt, not even the ones who cast no reflections.

Two.

The house was singing now. Not loudly. That was the thing Priya understood best, in the part of her that was still her own, watching from somewhere behind her own eyes like a girl pressed to the far side of a window: the house was not shrieking, not wailing, not doing any of the things houses did in stories. It was murmuring. It sounded like a congregation. It sounded like vespers. It sounded like every bedside prayer Quentin Thackeray had ever written down in his notebooks, except that now the prayers had stood up out of their pages and were walking the halls, and they all ended in the same falling number, and none of them ended in *amen.*

Priya walked. Delphine followed her, helpless as someone following an ambulance.

* * *

Two.

They passed the great window at the end of the east gallery — tall, leaded, black with storm, the snow flying sideways past the glass in long white ropes. The blizzard had sealed them in hours ago; the world outside had shrunk to whiteness and wind, and the house had turned inward on itself like a hand closing.

Priya stopped at the window. She didn't decide to stop. Her feet simply arrived there, the way water arrives at a drain.

"Look," she said to Delphine, and her voice was almost tender. "Look at the glass."

"I won't."

"You'll want to."

"No." But Delphine was already looking — not at the window, Priya noticed, but at everything else. At the hall mirror across the gallery, which showed the corridor one heartbeat behind their bodies, so that Delphine's reflected self was always arriving a moment after Delphine had already gone. At the dark television screen in the residents' lounge, which had begun, sometime after midnight, to hold a faint grey depth that no powered-off screen should hold. At the polished surface of the side table, where the storm-light pooled like oil.

Every reflective thing in Harrow Glen had become a window in the other direction. Priya could feel them all — dozens of them, scores, the whole house studded with eyes that looked not at the present but at the place one second behind it, where the Room had spread like ink through water until there was hardly any *now* left to stand in.

"Two," Priya sang again, softly, right against the cold glass, and her breath bloomed silver on the pane.

And in the bloom — in the fog of her own breath on the winter window, which was not a mirror, which was only breath and glass and night — a face appeared.

It was not Priya's face.

It was a young woman's face, pale and fine-boned, with dark hair pinned up in a style forty years out of fashion, and it was positioned exactly where a reflection should be, looking out at Delphine from the other side of the glass with an expression of such naked, patient longing that Priya felt her borrowed smile falter.

The woman raised one hand and laid it flat against the inside of the window. Snow swirled behind her, but the snow did not touch her. Nothing outside touched her. She existed in the thin country between the glass and the world, the same country the mirrors had been showing all week — the country one second late.

"*Liliane,*" Delphine said.

It came out of her like a bone coming out of skin. Priya watched the head nurse — the iron woman, the keeper of ledgers, the burner of pages — come apart in the space of a single syllable. Delphine crossed the gallery as though the floor were tilting, as though the whole house had become a deck in heavy weather, and pressed her palm to the window opposite her sister's palm, separated by a quarter inch of winter glass and one impossible second.

"Liliane, I'm here. I'm here, I never left, I stayed, I kept the house, I did everything—" Her voice climbed and cracked. "Tell me how. Tell me how to get you out. Tell me what it wants."

The face in the glass smiled. It was a lovely smile. It was the worst thing Priya had ever seen, because it was not cruel, and it was not hungry, and it was not a trick. It was the smile of someone who has waited so long that hope and grief have worn down into the same smooth stone.

The lips moved. No sound came through the glass — or no sound that ears could take. But Priya heard it anyway, the way she had heard her mother, in the part of her that was already living one second behind the world:

*Finish it. Finish it, Delly. We've been counting so long. Just finish it, and we can all come home.*

"Two," the face said — or mouthed, or meant — and behind it, in the deep grey of the glass, Priya saw others gathering. Shapes. A man in a sanatorium gown from some earlier century. A woman with her hair in curlers. A young man whose face Quentin had described to her once, gently, over cocoa in the kitchen — Alma's son, maybe, or Alma herself, small and patient at the back of the crowd. Dozens of faces, hundreds, a congregation assembled in the one-second country, all of them looking out, all of them waiting for the number to run out like children waiting for midnight.

"They're not ghosts," Priya heard herself say, in that voice that was hers and one second older. "You've known that for years, haven't you. That's why you burn the pages. That's why you keep the ledger locked. They're not haunting the house, Delphine." She turned from the window. The faces in the glass turned with her, all together, like fish. "The house is haunting *them*. And you've been feeding it."

Delphine didn't deny it. That was the thing Priya would remember, later, in whatever part of her survived the night to wake with an aversion to her own reflection. Delphine didn't deny it. She stood with her hand still flat against the window, against her sister's hand, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she had spent thirty years refusing to do — deaths divided by decades, small sacrifices balanced against one great sin, the count kept from zero on her watch by whatever means the watch required.

"How many," Delphine whispered. Not to Priya. To the glass. "How many does it need."

The sister's smile widened by a fraction. Behind her, the congregation stirred, and Priya felt the number rise in her own chest like water rising in a lock, inevitable, engineered, patient.

"One," said Mr. Abernathy, from his room, in a voice gone suddenly clear and strong, a dying man's voice on the best day of his death.

"One," echoed Marta Osei.

"One," said the kitchen, reluctantly. One, said the lounge. One, said the mirrors, all of them, in a rustle of sound like wind through dry grass — one second late, always one second late, so that the word seemed to chase them down the halls.

One, said the face in the window, and laid its fingers against the glass as if testing whether winter had thinned enough to break.

And then the house held its breath.

That was the sensation above all others — Priya felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the floor under her feet. The whole of Harrow Glen drew one long inhale and did not let it go. The storm outside seemed to pause mid-flurry, flakes hanging in the black glass like stars. Somewhere upstairs, Edmund Voss — the frightened old man who had bought himself a room at the threshold — made a sound that was not a word, and every mirror in the building turned its attention toward him at once, the way a congregation turns toward the pulpit.

The count had reached one. And it stopped there. It hung there, the way a held note hangs, the way a blade hangs.

Priya's mouth was open. The next number sat on her tongue like a seed, like a stone, like a name.

She could not say it.

She understood, in the last clear sliver of herself, that it was not hers to say. The count belonged to someone else now — to the man who had accepted the invitation sixty years ago and had been running from the final number ever since, and whose body was failing tonight, tonight of all nights, in the room at the end of the hall. The whole house was holding its breath for him. The whole congregation, on both sides of the glass, was waiting to see whether he would speak the word himself —

— or whether someone else would say it for him.

Delphine turned from the window slowly. Her hand dropped to her side. Her face was wet and terrible and calm, calm the way ice is calm, and Priya watched a decision move through her like weather.

"Not you," Delphine said — to Priya, to the house, to the faces in the glass, to her sister. "Not this time. If anyone finishes it—" her voice broke, and hardened, and broke again, "—it will be me."

Behind her, in the window, Liliane's smile became, for one instant, something that might have been fear.

* * *

One.

The number hung in the halls of Harrow Glen like frost on every surface. Priya walked on through the murmuring dark, leading a congregation that did not know it was praying, past windows full of patient faces, toward the room where an old man lay counting the last seconds of a sixty-year flight — and behind her, soft as snowfall, deliberate as a vow, Delphine Marchand began to follow.
