## 36. The New Nurse

The bus let Rosa off at the bottom of the hill because the driver would go no further.

"Road's not cleared past here," he said, nodding up through the windshield at the white lane winding toward a dark line of trees. "You sure this is the place? Nothing out there but that old house."

"That's the place," Rosa said, and pulled her bag onto her lap, and stepped down into snow that came to her shins.

She had taken jobs in strange places before. Three years of hospice work taught you that the dying did not cluster in convenient locations; they gathered in converted convents and former lighthouses, in houses that had once been something else and never quite stopped being it. Harrow Glen had been a private sanatorium once — she'd read that in the listing — and before that, according to the agency's file, a private residence built by a family whose name had been scrubbed from every record she could find. The photograph in the posting showed a long gray facade with too many windows, all of them reflecting nothing but sky.

She climbed the hill with her bag over her shoulder and her coat buttoned to her throat, and by the time she reached the door she could no longer see the road behind her. The storm two weeks ago had buried everything. The world was very quiet. She stood for a moment on the swept stone step, listening to that particular silence that large old houses make — not an absence of sound but a held breath — and then she knocked.

The woman who opened the door was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, with the posture of someone who had spent decades standing very straight so as not to bend under something. Her eyes were the color of wet slate, and they moved over Rosa's face with an attention that felt less like assessment than recognition, though they had never met.

"Rosa Lindqvist," Rosa said. "I have an interview. With Ms. Iversen— no. I'm sorry." She checked the paper in her hand. "Ms. Delphine."

Something crossed the older woman's face — quick, there and gone, like a bird's shadow on a window. "Delphine Marsh," she said. "Come in out of the cold. You're early."

"I'm sorry. The bus—"

"Don't apologize." Delphine held the door wide. "Being early is a kind of respect. Most people don't know that anymore."

* * *

The entrance hall smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and, beneath both, something older and harder to name — the smell Rosa associated with every hospice she'd ever worked in, the smell of a building that has absorbed decades of endings and holds them the way stone holds heat. Long windows looked out on white lawns. A staircase rose into shadow. And at the end of the hall, facing the front door, hung a tall mirror in a frame of tarnished silver.

Rosa caught herself in it as Delphine led her past: a small woman with wind-chapped cheeks and dark hair escaping its braid, looking tired and cold and hopeful. Ordinary. She looked away quickly — she had never liked being watched, even by herself — and noticed that Delphine had not looked at the mirror at all. Had, in fact, angled her body slightly away from it as they passed, the way you step around a person grieving at a funeral.

"You've worked hospice three years," Delphine said, leading her into a room off the hall where a fire burned low. Two chairs faced each other across a small table, closer together than interview chairs usually sat. On the table lay a single sheet of paper, face down. "St. Anselm's, then the Riverbend facility. Good references. Your supervisor at St. Anselm writes that you 'sit with the dying better than anyone she's trained.'"

"I don't do anything special," Rosa said. It was true, mostly. "I just stay."

"That's the whole job." Delphine sat, and gestured for Rosa to do the same, and studied her for a long moment without speaking. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them a floorboard settled. "Tell me why."

"Why I stay?"

"Why hospice. Why this work, when you could be anywhere doing anything. Everyone has a reason. I've learned to ask, because the wrong reason breaks people in this house."

Rosa considered lying, or offering the polished version — compassionate, called to service, all the words from applications. But there was something about the slate-colored eyes that made polish feel like an insult.

"My mother died when I was nineteen," she said. "Not here. In a hospital, in a city, surrounded by machines and strangers who were very kind and very busy. And in her last hours everyone kept leaving the room. Nurses coming and going, doctors with their charts, family stepping out to make calls. Everyone treated those hours as a waiting room they were passing through. And I remember thinking — this is the only time she will ever die. This is the one unrepeatable thing. And no one was watching it happen." She looked at her hands. "So now I watch. That's all. Someone should be present for the one unrepeatable thing."

Delphine was silent for a while. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, rougher, as if she were handling something fragile.

"And when they die, what do you feel?"

"Grief. Sometimes relief, if they suffered. Sometimes—" Rosa hesitated. "Sometimes I feel like I missed something. Like there was something they were trying to say or do at the end and I didn't catch it in time. Some of them talk, near the end. Counting, sometimes. Old habits, prayers, whatever. I always try to listen."

The fire ticked again. Delphine's hands, folded in her lap, had gone very still.

"Counting," she repeated.

"Some of them count backwards. From ten. I used to think it was a way of measuring what was left. Now I think—" She stopped, embarrassed. "I don't know what I think. I think maybe they're not counting down. Maybe they're counting toward something. Or calling it. I'm probably talking nonsense."

"No," Delphine said quietly. "You're not." She turned the paper on the table over. It was a single page, written in a careful, slanted hand, ink gone brown with age — or with something else. Rosa read:

*If you are reading this, you are considering working at Harrow Glen. Before you decide anything, you must read the notebooks. All of them. There are forty-one. Do not skim. Do not begin your shift until you have finished. This is not a request, and it is not superstition. It is the only thing standing between this house and what it remembers.*

It was unsigned.

"What is this?" Rosa asked.

"The condition of your employment," Delphine said. "Quentin Thackeray was our night nurse for eleven years. He wrote those notebooks — his documentation, he called it. Every death in this house since 1911 is in the ledger, and every death Quentin witnessed is in his notebooks, and the two together are..." She paused, choosing the word with visible care. "*Necessary*. You will live in the east guest room. You will read until you are finished. You will not work a shift, enter a patient's room, or speak to a resident until you have read every page. If that seems unreasonable to you, I will drive you back down to the road myself and pay your fare home, and I will wish you well."

Rosa looked at the page again. The handwriting was beautiful and urgent, the letters leaning forward like someone hurrying toward a cliff edge.

"Forty-one notebooks," she said. "How long does that take?"

"For most people, four days. For some, longer. One woman stopped after the seventh and asked to leave, and I drove her down myself, and she was right to go." Delphine leaned forward. "I will tell you one thing now, and then we will not speak of it again until you've finished. Something happened in this house last winter. Something ended, and something else began, and Quentin Thackeray is the only person who understood either of those things, and he is not here to explain them. The notebooks are how he explains them. To you. Specifically to you — you'll understand when you get to the last one." She stood. "Do you accept?"

Every professional instinct Rosa had said this was eccentric at best, a red flag at worst. Hospice attracted mystics and cranks; she'd learned to smile at both. But underneath the instinct was the other thing, the thing that had made her climb the hill — the memory of her mother's room full of leaving people, and her own vow to watch, and the sudden, irrational certainty that whatever these notebooks contained, it was the thing she had been watching for all along. The something they were trying to say.

"I accept," she said.

* * *

They gave her the east guest room, which looked out over the frozen gardens and the dark line of trees beyond. The bed was made. On the desk, stacked in neat rows, sat forty-one notebooks — cheap composition books, hospital logbooks, a few leather-bound volumes, their spines cracked and labeled in that same slanting hand: dates, years, numbers. The earliest was dated 2013. The latest had no date at all, only the word *FINAL* in letters pressed so hard the pen had torn the cover.

There was also a ledger, bound in green cloth, older than all the rest. Delphine set it on top of the stack with both hands, gently, the way you set down something sleeping.

"Start with the ledger," she said. "Then the notebooks in order. Meals will be left outside your door. Marcus — our caretaker — will leave them. You won't see much of us, and that's intentional. Reading these is a kind of... quarantine. Or a pilgrimage. Whichever metaphor serves you." At the door she paused, her hand on the frame, not turning around. "One more thing. If you find yourself wanting to stop — really wanting to stop, the way you want to look away from an accident — that's the moment to keep going. The books are hardest right before they're honest."

Then she was gone, and Rosa was alone with forty-one notebooks and the sound of the house breathing around her.

* * *

She started that evening, as the light went blue and then black over the gardens.

The ledger first, as instructed. Names going back to 1911, columns of dates and ages and causes of death, all ordinary, all human — pneumonia, heart failure, age, age, age. But beside each name, in a column headed only with a dash, was a number. Ten. Nine. Seven. Eight. Ten again. Page after page, decade after decade, hundreds of names, and not one of them marked with a zero. Rosa found herself running her finger down the column of dashes, faster and faster, looking for the zero that wasn't there, feeling something cold assemble itself in her chest — the shape of a question she couldn't yet ask.

Then the notebooks, in order.

The early ones were almost sweet. Quentin Thackeray, it emerged, had been a man who loved his work with a ferocity that frightened him. He documented everything: the weather, the residents' jokes, the exact quality of afternoon light in the solarium, the last words of the dying transcribed with a stenographer's precision. He wrote about Alma, who liked her tea scalded and sang hymns in Norwegian. He wrote about his own grief, carefully, at arm's length, the way a man handles a wound to check whether it's still bleeding. *Documentation is a prayer,* he wrote once. *Or it is a wall. I no longer know which. Perhaps a prayer is just a wall you build in the right direction.*

And then, slowly, the tone changed.

The entries grew shorter. Denser. The handwriting tightened. Somewhere around the twelfth notebook the weather reports stopped, and the jokes stopped, and what remained was the counting — every patient, every vigil, every backwards number recorded against the date and the hour, until the notebooks became less a chronicle than a seismograph, registering tremors in something deep beneath the house.

Rosa read through the first night and into the second day. She ate the meals Marcus left — she heard his footsteps, heavy and deliberate, and once glimpsed a broad gray shoulder disappearing around the corner of the corridor, and thought she heard him pause outside her door, and say something very quietly, and move on. She read. Outside her window the snow began to fall again, soft and endless, sealing the house further into winter.

By the fifteenth notebook she had stopped sleeping properly. By the twentieth she had stopped trying. The story assembled itself in her mind the way a photograph develops — slowly, all at once: the mirrors lagging, the sealed wing, Edmund Voss and his sixty-year flight, Priya singing in the halls, Delphine frozen between seconds, Marcus without a reflection, and Quentin — Quentin walking into the Room with his pen still moving, trading himself for a dying man's peace, ending a century-long count with a single spoken word.

She cried at chapter twenty-seven, the one called *Two*, where Edmund asks to be witnessed rather than saved. She cried again at thirty-five, Marcus's chapter, and had to put the book down and stand at the window for a long time, watching snow fall on the garden where, according to the notebooks, a woman named Delphine had once stood frozen mid-step, one second behind herself.

On the fourth night she reached the final notebook.

*FINAL*, the cover said, and inside, the pages were different — calmer, written in the weeks after, when the mirrors had gone eager and the house had gone quiet. Quentin described the aftermath the way a survivor describes a shoreline. And then, near the end, the handwriting changed. Slowed. Became almost tender.

*Rosa,* the entry began.

She sat very still. The fire had burned to embers. The house creaked its long slow creaks, and somewhere below her a clock she hadn't noticed before struck two.

*I don't know your name yet, but I know you'll be reading this, because Delphine will make sure of it — she understands now what I understood too late, that a house like this can't be protected by secrecy. Secrecy is what fed it. So the story has to be passed on, nurse to nurse, the way immunity passes. You are the antibodies. Forgive me for volunteering you.*

*You will be tempted, as I was, to treat the counting as a problem to be solved. It isn't. It was never a countdown. Remember that. Whatever happens here — however the mirrors behave, however the dying speak — your task is not to control it or explain it or pray it away. Your task is to witness. Presence, not power. That's the whole instruction. Everything else I learned, I learned too late to matter.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you sleep. If you ever find an entry in these books that you don't remember writing — in your own handwriting, describing things you haven't seen yet — don't burn it. Don't run. Sit down and read it, and then come find me. I'm easier to reach than you'd think. The glass is only ever one second away.*

Rosa closed the notebook.

Her hands were steady, which surprised her. She felt the way she felt after a long vigil — hollowed out, rinsed clean, awake in the specific way that comes from having sat with something enormous and not looked away. Forty-one notebooks, and the whole of them said the same thing in forty-one different ways: *someone should be present for the one unrepeatable thing.* Quentin had simply learned what the unrepeatable thing was.

She slept then, deeply, without dreams, and woke to pale morning light and the smell of coffee outside her door.

* * *

Delphine was waiting in the kitchen, and she looked older than she had four days ago — or perhaps Rosa was only seeing her clearly for the first time, having read the winter that had carved her. She poured two cups of coffee and pushed one across the table.

"Well?" she said.

"It's the strangest hiring process I've ever been through," Rosa said, "and I believe every word of it."

Delphine exhaled — a long breath, held, it seemed, for a year. "Most people need longer. One man laughed. He lasted two shifts."

"He didn't finish the books?"

"He skipped to the end. They always skip to the end." Delphine wrapped her hands around her cup. "And now you understand why the dying count. And why we listen. And why no one in this house will ever again pretend not to hear it."

"Yes," Rosa said. Then, because it needed saying: "Your sister. I read about her. And about what you did, and why." She met the slate eyes steadily. "I'm not here to judge you. I'm here to sit with people. That's all I know how to do."

For a moment something moved behind Delphine's composure — grief, gratitude, the exhaustion of a woman who had carried a secret until it carried her. "That," she said quietly, "is exactly the right amount to know how to do." She stood, and straightened her already-straight back. "Your first shift begins tonight. Seven o'clock. We have three residents currently — Mrs. Okafor, Mr. Brandt, and a new admission arriving Thursday, a gentleman named Hale. Terminal, but comfortable. He's requested a room facing the garden." She paused at the doorway, and for the first time since Rosa had arrived, she smiled — small, tired, real. "Welcome to Harrow Glen, Rosa."

* * *

At seven that evening Rosa began her rounds, and the house received her the way the house received everyone: silently, watchfully, holding its breath.

She checked on Mrs. Okafor, sleeping. She checked on Mr. Brandt, awake and watching the snow out his window, who told her about his daughter in Leeds and asked her to write down the address in case he forgot it, and she wrote it down, and he squeezed her hand and said, "You're the one who reads, aren't you? Delphine told us. Good. Good." As if reading were a qualification for something larger than nursing.

At half past eleven she walked the corridor on her final round, past the tall mirror at the end of the hall — the one she had caught herself in on her first day. She had avoided it for four days, the way Delphine did, out of a caution she couldn't justify. Now she stopped in front of it, deliberately, and looked.

A tired woman in scrubs, dark hair loose from its braid, candle-flame shadows moving along the wall behind her. Ordinary. Present. Here.

She raised her hand, and her reflection raised its hand, and for one heartbeat — one — she thought she saw the fingers hesitate, a fraction late, a breath behind. But when she looked again the mirror was only a mirror, silvered and honest, showing her the hallway and the night and nothing else.

Probably the notebooks. Probably four days of someone else's haunted handwriting, printed onto the backs of her eyes.

She smiled at herself — a small, steadying smile, the kind she gave patients — and turned toward the stairwell, and had taken perhaps five steps when she heard it.

Faint. From behind Mr. Brandt's door, thin as thread: an old man's voice, wavering, unhurried, keeping perfect time.

*"Ten."*

Rosa stood in the dark corridor with her heart going hard and even, and did not look away, and did not hurry, and remembered the last instruction in the last notebook — *presence, not power* — and turned the handle softly, and went in to sit with him, and to listen.

Inside, faint and clear, the voice continued: *"Nine."*

Outside the door, unseen, the tall mirror at the end of the hall held the empty corridor in its silvered glass — and held it, just barely, one second too long.
