## 15. The Voice Offers a Trade

The recorder sat on his nightstand like a small black stone, its red light burning in the dark the way a coal burns after everyone else has gone to bed. Cyrus had stopped turning it off three nights ago. There was no point. The voice came whether he recorded it or not — he could feel that now, the way you can feel a draft from a window you've nailed shut — but the tapes were proof, and proof was the last religion he had left.

He pressed play at 6:14 in the morning, coffee untouched, breath held out of habit even though nothing on these recordings had ever needed him to hold his breath.

*Static. The sound of his own breathing, slow and tidal. Then, at 2:31 a.m., the second voice.*

It never breathed. That was the detail he kept returning to, the one his rational mind circled like a moth around a bulb it couldn't land on. His own sleeping voice rose and fell with the wet mechanics of a body; the other voice simply *was*, each word placed into the room as if the room had always been holding it.

*"Where is the girl who draws the frost?"* his sleeping self mumbled.

*"In her bed,"* the voice answered. *"For now."*

*"How many winters?"*

*"One hundred and four. This will be the hundred and fifth."*

Cyrus stopped the tape. He had heard this exchange eleven times now, and each time the numbers landed in his chest like stones dropped down a well — he waited for them to hit bottom, and they never did.

He rewound further, to the part he hadn't let himself hear more than twice.

*"You could stop it,"* the voice said.

His sleeping self made a sound — not quite a word, something between a snore and a question.

*"You could stop all of it,"* the voice repeated, patient as frost forming on glass. *"A name for a name. A memory for a child. That is the oldest arithmetic there is, teacher. Older than your town. Older than your ledger-keeping diner women. I did not invent the trade. I only honor it."*

Cyrus's hand shook so badly the coffee cup chattered against its saucer. He set it down. He pressed play again, because that was what he did now — he listened to the thing that lived in his sleep the way other men listened to weather reports.

*"What would I have to give?"* his sleeping self asked.

And the voice said: *"Everything you remember. Everything you are remembered by. Your name, your griefs, your sister's face, the smell of your mother's kitchen, the sound of your own childhood — all of it, poured out, and I will drink until the cup is empty. And then a man will walk out of this house tomorrow morning who owes nothing to yesterday. He will not mourn. He will not count. He will not lie awake listening for voices in the static. Wren Halloway will wake in her own bed and grow old in it. Her brother will grow old beside her. The ridge will go quiet. All of this, for the small price of you."*

The tape hissed. In the hiss, if you listened long enough — and Cyrus had listened long enough — you could hear something almost like kindness.

*"Think about it,"* the voice said. *"I am very old. I can wait. Waiting is most of what I am."*

* * *

He didn't go to school that day. He told himself it was the flu, and left a note under Ada's door at the diner because she had become, without either of them naming it, the person he reported to — the keeper of the count, the woman who had buried her own daughter in the ledger forty years ago and called it bookkeeping ever since.

She found him at noon, sitting on the frozen bank of the creek itself, which ran beneath a lid of ice so clear he could see the water moving underneath, dark and alive and going nowhere.

Ada lowered herself onto the bank beside him with the careful stiffness of a woman whose knees had opinions. She didn't ask why he wasn't at school. She looked at the creek for a while.

"It talked to you," she said.

Not a question. He turned to look at her. Her face was composed in that way he'd come to recognize as Marrow Creek's native architecture — every feature load-bearing, nothing decorative, nothing wasted.

"On the tape," he said. "It's been talking for weeks. But last night it—" He stopped. Started again. "It offered me a trade."

Ada nodded slowly, the way people nod at news they've been expecting the way they expect winter.

"My grandmother said it used to talk to the ones who fought the count," she said. "Back before my time. It doesn't waste words on people who've already agreed. It talks to the ones still deciding." She pulled her coat tighter. "What did it want?"

"Me. My memories. My name. Everything." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It said it would drink me empty. And then Wren lives."

The silence between them lasted long enough for the creek to make three separate sounds under its ice — a groan, a tick, a whisper.

"That's how it was done," Ada said finally. "Before the covenant got organized. Before we started giving it children regular, like tithing. In the old days, sometimes a grown person would walk up the ridge and give themselves instead. My grandmother's grandmother saw it once. Said the frost took the whole man in an hour — name first, then face, then the rest. And the ridge went quiet for a season." She looked at him. "But it always comes back, Cyrus. A season isn't a century. Children last longer. That's why the covenant settled on children. Not cruelty. *Efficiency.*"

"You're telling me not to do it."

"I'm telling you what I know." Her eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "I'm telling you that forty years ago I stood where you're standing, except it was my daughter's name being weighed, and I let the arithmetic win because the alternative was watching the whole town freeze one family at a time. And I have kept the count every winter since, and I have hated myself in a way that does not have a bottom." She stood, joints cracking like the creek ice. "So no, I'm not telling you not to do it. I'm telling you that whatever you choose, you'll be choosing it forever, and forever is the one thing this town has plenty of."

She walked back toward the diner. Cyrus stayed on the bank until the light went blue and then gray, watching the living water move under the dead-looking ice.

* * *

That night he took out the shoebox.

It lived on the top shelf of his closet, behind the winter coat he'd never needed in Marrow Creek until now. Inside: photographs, a birthday card his sister Lila had given him when he turned nineteen — *To Cy, who explains everything, love Lila who explains nothing* — and a cassette labeled in her handwriting, *mixtape for the drive, don't crash.*

Lila. Who had vanished from a graduating class in a town two states away from Marrow Creek, twenty-three years ago, in circumstances the police had called a runaway and his parents had called a tragedy and young Cyrus had called, in the privacy of his own relentless head, *an unexplained sound with an explanation pending.*

He had spent his whole life pending explanations. That was the machine he was. Every creak in every house he'd ever lived in, every name called from another room by a voice that turned out to be no one, every dream of falling — catalogued, filed, explained away. *House settling. Wind in the eaves. Hypnagogic hallucination.* He had built a cathedral of explanations so vast and so well-lit that no darkness could survive inside it, and he had done it for one reason, though he'd only admitted it to himself recently, here, in this town that fed its children to the cold:

Because the night Lila disappeared, he had heard her call his name from the woods behind their house. Clearly. Twice. And he had stayed in bed, because he was fourteen and tired and sure there was a rational explanation, and by morning she was gone, and the explanation never came, and the cathedral had been under construction ever since.

The voice on the tapes knew that. Of course it knew. It had asked about her in Lila's cadence, had used details only Lila knew — the way she pronounced his name with the *y* soft, almost a sigh; the secret word they'd invented as children for the space under the porch stairs. It wasn't impersonating her. He understood that now with a certainty that felt like falling. It was *collecting* her. Keeping her. The way the frost kept names in its patterns on the ridge.

And part of him — the part he was most ashamed of, the part that had been whispering since the first night he played the tape — wanted to hear more. Wanted to sit in the dark and let the thing speak in his sister's voice forever, because it was the closest he had come in twenty-three years to hearing her again.

He understood, sitting there with the shoebox open on his knees, that this was the real temptation. Not the trade. The trade was just the shape the temptation wore so he could pretend it was noble.

He could save Wren Halloway. He could save the sleepwalking children who drifted nightly toward the treeline with frost blooming on their windowsills like signatures. He could break a covenant a century old and free a town that had forgotten it was captive. All he had to pay was everything — and God help him, some exhausted cellar of his heart whispered *yes, please, take it, take the grief, take the guilt, take the fourteen-year-old boy who didn't get up out of bed, take the man who became a teacher because he thought maybe teaching was a way of going back, take all of it, I have been carrying this so long—*

He closed the shoebox.

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to the recorder, to the thing that waited in the static. "No. You don't get my sister too."

The room said nothing. The radiator ticked. Outside, the first flakes of the season's real snow — the snow that meant the count was close — came down past the streetlight like ash rising in reverse.

But when he slept, the recorder's red light burned, and at 3:07 a.m. the voice spoke once more, gently, into the dark of his rented bedroom:

*"You said no to me tonight,"* it said. *"But you have not said no to yourself. When you do — and you will, teacher, you are closer than you know — the ridge will be waiting. Bring the name. I will bring the child home."*

* * *

Morning came white and silent. Cyrus woke with the shoebox still on his knees and frost on the inside of his window — on the *inside*, in patterns too deliberate to be condensation, and when he wiped it away with his sleeve his chest went cold, because he recognized the shapes now. Letters. Half-formed, unfinished, the way a sentence looks when the writer's hand has paused mid-word.

They spelled the beginning of his name. Just the beginning. *C-Y-R.*

Waiting for the rest.

He dressed fast, grabbed the recorder, and was halfway out the door when he saw it lying on the mat outside his apartment: a child's mitten, soaked through, crusted with snow that hadn't fallen yet tonight. Small. Red wool. He knew it instantly — Wren Halloway wore its twin, he'd watched her pull it off a dozen times to draw frost patterns on the margins of her assignments.

Someone had walked in the night. Someone small, asleep, bare-handed in the snow.

And down the hill, at the treeline where the pines went black against the gray sky, he could see them — three small figures standing motionless in the fresh powder, facing the ridge, waiting for morning the way the town waited for the count.

Cyrus ran.
