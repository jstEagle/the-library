## 3. The Room Behind the Plaster

The cellar of Number Fourteen smelled of cold stone and old iron, the way cellars do when they've been left alone for a long time. Edmund had been in a hundred cellars like it — urban explorers collect them the way other people collect stamps — and he knew the inventory by heart: a rusted boiler that would never boil again, a shelf of paint cans fused to the shelf, a bicycle frame with no wheels, the ghost of a coal chute bricked over sometime in the fifties.

Except this cellar was wrong in one small way, and the small way was the whole story.

The floor was swept.

He'd noticed it the first time he came down, two weeks ago, and had filed it under *squatter, tidy one* and moved on. But there were no squatting signs. No cans, no bedding, no cigarette ends. Just a cellar, forty feet by twenty, its flagstones clean enough to eat off, and along the far wall a stretch of brick that looked — he'd only registered it now, on his fourth visit, with the laser measure in his hand — *wrong*.

The wall was too new. That was the first thing. The rest of the cellar's brickwork was soft old stock, water-rotted in places, mortar gone to sugar. This stretch, maybe ten feet wide, was laid in harder brick, tighter mortar, and it had been pointed recently enough that the joints still held a faint crispness. Someone had built this wall. Not a hundred years ago. Not fifty. Within living memory.

The second thing was the dimensions.

His laser said the cellar was twenty feet deep. The plans — the one survey he'd found, from 1938, the one that didn't show the tunnel but did show the house — said twenty-two. He'd assumed surveyor's error. Surveyors made errors; he made a living correcting them. But he'd measured three times from three corners, and the numbers agreed with each other and disagreed with the 1938 plan by exactly the same two feet, every time, in the same direction.

Two feet of cellar that didn't exist.

Or two feet of something that existed and wasn't cellar.

He stood in the middle of the floor with his headlamp on and felt the particular thrill he'd chased across half the country's forgotten infrastructure — the thrill of a discrepancy, a number that refused to lie politely. Then he noticed the hum.

It had been there on every visit, that 432 Hz tone his recorder kept flagging, steady as a tuning fork. He'd assumed a pipe, a transformer, something municipal bleeding through the ground. But he'd walked the tunnel with the recorder held out in front of him like an offering, and the tone didn't come from the tunnel. It came from ahead of him. It came, he was almost certain, from behind the too-new wall.

"Okay," he said, to the cellar, to himself. "Okay."

* * *

He came back Saturday with proper gear, because Edmund Thornbury was many things — obsessive, contrary, recently and uncomfortably a man who'd learned his surname belonged to this street — but he was not careless. Drop cloths. A respirator, because plaster in houses this age meant asbestos until proven otherwise. A borescope. A reciprocating saw with a diamond blade. His camera on a tripod, recording, because he documented everything; it was the one discipline he'd never broken.

The wall gave up its secrets grudgingly. He drilled a pilot hole first, three inches above the floor, and the bit sank in with no resistance at all — not the bite of brick but the soft give of something else. He pulled the bit out and shone the borescope's camera through.

The screen showed him a void, and in the void, a chair.

He sat back on his heels for a long moment, breathing through the respirator, watching the little screen. A chair. A plain wooden chair, upright, facing the wall he was drilling through. Facing *him*. Furniture didn't face walls. Furniture faced rooms.

He thought about the neighbor, Ruth Okafor, and the way she'd stood in her doorway with her arms crossed and said, *The Thornburys built that house,* in the flat tone of someone stating the weather. He'd laughed. He'd actually laughed, told her it was a common name, coincidence, nothing. She hadn't laughed back. She'd looked at him the way you look at someone who's about to touch a live wire.

He was touching the wire now.

He cut the wall in a clean vertical line, then a horizontal one, and swung the section of brick-and-plaster out on its hinge like a door — because that's what it was, he realized as it moved; the thing was built to open, built to be opened, a door disguised as a wall, and the fact that no one had opened it in however many years didn't mean it had been sealed. It meant it had been *waiting*.

The air that came out was the second wrong thing.

Cellar air is damp and mineral and dead. This air was different. It was dry, and it was warm — warmer than the cellar by several degrees, warmer than it had any right to be — and it tasted the way the inside of a held breath tastes, that faintly sweet, pressurized nothing, the taste of air someone has been keeping in their lungs and deciding what to do with. He stood in the breach with the broken wall swinging at his shoulder and breathed it, and something in his chest answered it, a tightening like the moment before a held note.

"Hello?" he said. His voice came out smaller than he intended. Nothing answered, but the hum, the 432 hum, was louder now, and he could feel it in his teeth.

He stepped through.

* * *

The room was small — eight feet by six, he'd guess, though he didn't measure, not at first; measuring felt like an intrusion in a way he couldn't have justified to anyone. The walls were plastered and painted a deep, flat green gone gray with age. There was no window. There was no light fixture, no wiring, no vent. A room with no services at all, sealed behind a false wall in a cellar, and it was not damp. Not a bloom of mold, not a salt bloom, not the black tear-stains of water ingress. In a house that had been derelict for a decade, in a cellar that sweated moisture, this room was dry as a page.

A chair. The chair from the borescope, plain pine, its seat worn pale in the center as if by a single sitter, over and over, for years. It faced a music stand.

The music stand was iron, Victorian by the scrollwork, the kind that folded for travel, and on it, held by the clip, was a single sheet of music.

Edmund's first instinct — and he was honest enough with himself to note it — was to reach out and take it. His hands actually moved. He caught them, literally caught his own wrist with his other hand, and stood there like a man restraining himself from touching a hot stove, and asked himself why. It was paper. It was evidence. It was, by any reasonable standard, his to document.

But it wasn't. He knew that the way you know a door is locked without trying it. The sheet sat on the stand the way a crown sits on a cushion — not placed, *enshrined* — and everything about the room said: this is not abandoned. This is maintained. Someone, or something, had swept the floor and kept the damp out and left the chair facing the stand and the stand holding the page, and had done it for longer than any squatter's tenancy, longer than any caretaker's contract, and the doing of it was not over.

He photographed everything before he touched anything. Wide shots, details, the chair's worn seat, the stand, the plaster, the door-wall from the inside — and here was the third wrong thing, the one that finally made his professional thrill curdle into something colder. The inside face of the false wall was not brick. It was plastered smooth, and painted the same deep green, and on it, at the height of a seated person's eye line, directly opposite the chair, someone had drawn a small chalk mark. A single vertical stroke. Like a tally. One.

Just one. As if the counting had started and never continued, or as if everything that came after had been counted somewhere else.

Then he took the sheet music off the stand.

It was heavy stock, cream-colored, foxed at the edges but not brittle. Handwritten — a fine, controlled hand, ink gone brown, the kind of penmanship they stopped teaching before the war. At the top, in that same hand, a title:

*The Verse for the Last One.*

Below it, a melody. Single line, no harmony, no accompaniment — a vocal line, marked *solo*, with dynamic markings so soft they were almost apologetic: *pp*, then *ppp*, then, in the final bars, no marking at all, just a long decrescendo hairline tapering to nothing. The lyrics, if they were lyrics, were in no language he knew — or they were in English so old it had stopped being English, syllables that looked like words the way shadows look like people.

And it was incomplete. That was the thing he noticed last and would think about first, ever after. The final line of the staff simply stopped. Not with a double bar, not with a fermata, not with the traditional sign for *the end*. The line ran to the right edge of the paper and ended mid-phrase, mid-measure, the last note hanging on its stem like a man hanging from a ledge, and beneath it, in the same brown ink, smaller, almost squeezed into the margin, four words:

*to be continued by —*

And then nothing. A dash, and a space where a name should have been, and no name.

Edmund stood in the green room with the paper trembling very slightly in his hands — he was not a man whose hands trembled — and felt the held-breath air pressing against him from all sides, and understood, in the wordless way you understand a room before you understand a sentence, that he was standing inside something that had been built on purpose, with patience, by people who expected it to matter for a very long time.

*The Verse for the Last One.*

Last one. Last one what? Last one to find it? Last one of — of what? Of whom?

His surname sat in his mouth like a stone. Thornbury. *The Thornburys built that house.* A common name. A coincidence. He'd laughed at Ruth Okafor, and the laugh had been the sound of a man standing on a floor he hadn't yet tested.

He photographed the sheet front and back, both sides, raking light, macro on the marginal inscription. He did not take it. He put it back on the stand exactly as it had been, clip aligned, angle matched to his photographs, and the room — he'd have sworn this later, under oath if it came to that, and it never did — the room seemed to settle around him, the way a house settles at night. The hum dropped by a hair. The held breath, very slightly, exhaled.

He spent another hour documenting. The chair's seat, worn smooth. The chalk tally. The plaster, which on close inspection bore faint, regular marks, almost invisible under the paint — he'd have said nail holes, except they were too regular, spaced like the holes in a flute, a vertical column of them beside the chair, and he did not let himself think about what a column of small holes at the height of a seated person's mouth might have been for. He photographed them and left them for daylight and distance.

At the threshold, on his way out, he stopped. He didn't know why until he'd done it: he turned back and said, to the room, in the flat professional voice he used on sites, "I'm going to come back. I'm going to set up equipment. I'm going to record everything."

The hum held its note.

It sounded, he thought, climbing the cellar steps into the gray afternoon, like patience. Like something that had been holding a single tone for a hundred and thirty years and could hold it for a hundred and thirty more, and knew, with a certainty he couldn't match, that he would be back.

He locked the house, walked up Old Chapel Road, and did not let himself hum until he was three streets away — and only then realized that he'd been holding a tune in his head the whole way, a tune he didn't know, a tune he had no memory of learning.

He told himself it was the hum. 432 Hz, stuck in his head like any drone.

It wasn't. It had a melody. It had seven notes, and then it stopped, mid-phrase, the way a page stops when it runs out of room, and the space where the eighth note should have been ached like a missing tooth.

That night he ordered a digital audio recorder, a contact microphone, and a camera that ran all night on infrared. If the room wanted to say something, he was going to be there to write it down.

He fell asleep with the seven notes turning over in his head like a coin he couldn't stop flipping, and in the morning, he couldn't remember which of them he'd dreamed.
