## 4. Recording One

The cot came from a camping shop in town, forty pounds, aluminum frame, a sleeping bag rated to minus five that Edmund had no intention of needing. He carried it down the tunnel in three trips along with the rest of the kit: a Zoom recorder with fresh batteries, a second recorder as backup, two infrared cameras, a power bank the size of a brick, a headlamp, a thermos, and a laminated copy of his own risk assessment, which he'd filled out mostly as a joke and then, somewhere between the second and third trip, stopped finding funny.

The room at the end of the tunnel — the room behind the plaster — measured nine feet by eleven. He knew because he'd measured it twice with a laser distance finder that gave the same reading both times, which was more than he could say for anything else in this house.

He set the cot against the north wall, beneath the hairline crack that ran floor to ceiling like a seam someone had forgotten to close. The music stand he moved to the corner, out of frame of where he planned to point the cameras. The sheet music stayed where he'd found it, on the stand, because every time he thought about moving it, something in his chest tightened like a hand closing around a wire, and he was not a man who ignored his own physiology. He was a man who documented it.

"Night one," he said to the recorder, testing levels. His voice came back flat and close in the small space. "Edmund Thornbury, sleep study, informal. Subject: myself. Location: undocumented structure beneath and adjacent to 14 Old Chapel Road. Objective: determine whether the acoustic phenomenon — the hum, the — whatever it is — affects sleep architecture, dream content, or verbal output."

He paused. The room did not pause with him. That was the thing he kept noticing and kept failing to write down: rooms were supposed to be passive. Rooms were supposed to receive. This one felt like it was listening the way a stethoscope listens — not politely, but professionally.

"Rationale," he continued, because saying it out loud made it true, made it science. "The hum registers at 432 Hz on every instrument I've brought down here. That frequency has accumulated a great deal of New Age nonsense — 'the frequency of the universe,' healing crystals, all of it. I don't subscribe to any of that. What interests me is that the hum is *consistent*. No drift. No harmonic decay. A sustained tone in an unventilated cellar should decay. This one doesn't. Either there's a source, or there's a resonance I haven't found. I intend to find it."

He checked the cameras. The infrared feed bloomed grey-green on his phone screen: the cot, the chair in the corner — a wooden kitchen chair, seat worn pale — the music stand with its sheaf of pages, and the wall behind the cot where the plaster met the older brick. In infrared, the room looked like a photograph of itself. He found that comforting. Evidence was comforting. Evidence could be held at arm's length and examined.

He'd told himself the whole way down: you are not sleeping in a haunted house. There is no such thing. You are sleeping in an unmapped structure with anomalous acoustics, and you are doing it with three forms of redundant documentation, because that is what a serious person does.

He did not tell himself the other thing, the thing that had been sitting in him since he'd first put his palm flat against the false wall: that the room had not felt discovered. It had felt *opened*. Like a lock that had been waiting for the right key, and the key was a Thornbury with a headlamp and a bad attitude about coincidences.

He got into the sleeping bag at 11:40 p.m. The last thing he saw on the camera feed before he turned the phone face-down was the music stand, and the pages on it, and the title he still hadn't been able to stop reading every time his eyes crossed the stand:

*The Verse for the Last One.*

"Goodnight," he said, to the room, ironically. He was a man who joked at doors, at elevators, at graves. It was a habit, not an offering.

The room took it anyway.

* * *

He woke at 6:12 a.m. with the specific, chemical dread of a man who has dreamed of nothing and remembers it.

The sleeping bag was unzipped. He had no memory of unzipping it. His throat was dry in a particular way — not thirst, but use — the way it felt after a long phone call, after reading aloud, after singing, though he hadn't sung since a school concert in 1999 where he'd been relegated to the back row of the choir and had mouthed the words.

He sat up. The cot frame ticked as it cooled. The room was exactly as he'd left it, which should have been reassuring and instead felt like a stage set between performances.

"Playback," he said aloud, and his voice came out hoarse, and he frowned at that, and reached for the Zoom.

The first forty minutes were nothing. Room tone. The hum, faint but present, that 432 Hz thread woven under everything like a bass note under a hymn. His own breathing, slowing into sleep around 12:15. He scrubbed forward, half-listening, already drafting the report in his head. *Subject slept 6.5 hours. No anomalous —*

Then he heard himself speak.

He stopped scrubbing. Rewound. Played it at normal speed.

*"Where is the beginning?"* — his own voice, thick with sleep, slurred but intelligible, coming out of the speaker in the small room while he sat on the cot it had come from. The timestamp read 2:47 a.m.

He stared at the recorder.

He did not talk in his sleep. He knew this the way he knew his own blood type; Vera had told him once, decades ago, when he'd stayed over as a child — *"You sleep like the dead, love, quiet as a stone"* — and he'd never had a partner complain, never had a reason to think otherwise. But there it was, his voice, asking a question into the dark.

And then something answered.

The voice was dry. That was the first word that came to him and it was the right one — dry the way old paper is dry, the way a mouth gets when it has been talking a long time or a very long time. It was a woman's voice, or had been. It was patient in a way that made his skin prickle, because patience, he understood immediately, was worse than anger. Anger wants something from you now. Patience can wait.

*"At the first note,"* the voice said. *"It is always at the first note."*

Edmund sat very still on the cot, the recorder held in both hands like something that might bite.

He had not asked that question. He was certain — as certain as a man can be of his own sleeping mind, which was suddenly, appallingly, not certain at all. But the voice had *answered*. It had answered in the cadence of a reply. And beneath the exchange, so faint he had to turn the volume all the way up and hold his breath to hear it, the hum had shifted. Not stopped — shifted. Risen by a small interval, a semitone, maybe less, and settled there.

He played it again.

*"Where is the beginning?"*

*"At the first note. It is always at the first note."*

He grabbed his notebook and wrote the timestamp, then the transcript, his handwriting degrading as he went. Under it he wrote: *Voice unexplained. Not radio — no signal down here, verified. Not crosstalk — single recorder, closed room, no other source. Not my own voice pitched/processed — formants wrong, I've checked. It is a second person. It is answering me.*

Then, because he was who he was, he wrote: *Verify. Do not narrate. Verify.*

He scrubbed forward.

* * *

The transcript, as he built it that morning, sitting cross-legged on the cot with cold coffee from the thermos, ran to four pages. He reconstructed it here in full later, in a notebook he would come to think of as the first notebook, before the one he kept outside, before the one he burned. The excerpts below are verbatim.

**2:47 a.m.**
EDMUND (sleeping): *Where is the beginning?*
VOICE: *At the first note. It is always at the first note.*

**3:02 a.m.**
EDMUND: *How long —*
VOICE: *Longer than you have words for. Shorter than you fear. Sleep.*

**3:19 a.m.**
EDMUND: *Who taught you the —* (inaudible)
VOICE: *No one taught. It was always in the house. The house only keeps it. We only carry it.*
EDMUND: *Carry it where?*
VOICE: *Out. That is the whole of it. That is all any of us were for.*

**3:31 a.m.**
EDMUND: *Am I —* (three seconds of nothing; the hum audible beneath, risen again, holding) *— the last one?*
VOICE: *(a pause of eleven seconds; Edmund, reviewing this later, would time it, because timing it was the only way to keep his hands from shaking)* *You are the one who came back. The last one is not decided. The last one is a door, and doors are not decided until they are opened or closed.*

**3:44 a.m.**
EDMUND: *What happens if I sing it?*
VOICE: *Then you stay.*
EDMUND: *What happens if I don't?*
VOICE: *(dry; almost, horribly, amused)* *Then you stay differently.*

**4:15 a.m.**
EDMUND: *(a sound — not a word; Edmund played this eleven times and could not make it into language; it was a hummed fragment, four notes, rising, and on the fourth note it cut off as if a hand had been laid over his mouth)*
VOICE: *(softly, and this was the moment, when he played it the eleventh time at 9 a.m. in full daylight with the door of his flat open and traffic going by outside, when the dread went from cold to structural)* *Not yet. Not in the walls. You must not give it the notes in the walls. Do you hear me? Not in the walls.*
EDMUND: *(sleeping, obedient, a child's voice coming out of a grown man)* *Not in the walls.*
VOICE: *Good. Good. You listen. That is rarer than you know. That is everything.*

**5:50 a.m.**
EDMUND: *(no words; the sound of him turning over; the cot frame ticking)*
VOICE: *(to itself, or to the room, or — he could not stop thinking — to whoever might one day play this tape)* *He asks good questions. He will want evidence. They always want evidence, at first. Give him time. We have been patient for a hundred years; we can be patient a little longer. He has her chin. He has her way of standing in a doorway. Hello, Edmund. Hello, hello.*

The recording ended at 6:04 a.m., eight minutes before he woke.

* * *

He did not leave immediately, which he would think about later, the way you think about the ten seconds before a car crash — the ten seconds in which you did not brake.

What he did was this: he sat on the cot with the recorder in his lap and ran the debunking protocols, one by one, like a priest working down a rosary.

Radio interference: no. He held his phone up — no signal anywhere below street level, verified on three previous visits. The recorder had no wireless capability. He checked.

Crosstalk or contamination: no. Single device, sealed room, and he listened to the entire file at 2x and 4x speed for any sign of a second person breathing, moving, present. There was no one. The infrared cameras — he reviewed all seven hours — showed the cot, and him in it, and the empty chair in the corner, and the music stand, and nothing else, no thermal bloom, no shape, no flicker. The room had been, on camera, occupied by exactly one sleeping man.

And yet two voices.

Infrasound and hallucination: he wanted this one badly. 19 Hz, the "ghost frequency," the vibration that produces unease and a sense of presence — he'd read the Tandy paper, he'd cited it in a forum argument once. But the hum was 432 Hz, audible, and the voice on the tape was not a hallucination. A hallucination doesn't make it onto a recording. A hallucination has no timestamp. This had a timestamp. This had *eleven seconds of pause* before answering whether he was the last one, and he had listened to those eleven seconds enough times now that he could feel them in his body like a held breath that belonged to someone else.

He got to the end of the protocols. All of them came back negative. All of them came back *this is real*.

He sat with that. The room sat with him. The hum ran under everything, steady, patient, at exactly the frequency it had held since the day he'd found the tunnel, and he understood — not as a theory but as a fact settling into him like cold water into a boot — that the hum was not the anomaly. The hum was the room *breathing*. And the voice was the room *speaking*. And he had slept in its mouth.

"Who are you?" he said aloud. His voice cracked on *you*, hoarse from a night of use he couldn't remember.

The room said nothing. Rooms don't. He knew that. He had built his whole life on knowing that.

But the recorder was still running, and later — much later, when he finally forced himself to review the morning footage too, because documentation was the only prayer he knew — he would find that at 9:41 a.m., eleven seconds after he asked *who are you*, the hum had risen by a semitone and settled, the way it had risen at 2:47 a.m. when the voice had said *at the first note*.

The same interval. Every time it answered, the same interval.

He packed the equipment with hands that were almost steady. At the mouth of the tunnel he stopped, and looked back down the long dark throat of it, toward the room, and heard himself say — out loud, in his professional voice, the one he used for clients and editors — "I'll be back tonight. I'm doing seven nights. Full protocol."

He hadn't decided that. He had a life up there. He had deadlines. He had a flat with a leak he'd been meaning to fix since March.

But the words were out, and they were true, and they were not entirely his, and the worst part — the part he wrote in the notebook that night, underlining it twice — was that some part of him had known they were coming before he said them. Had known the way you know a chorus after the first verse. Had known the way you know a song you've never been taught.

Seven nights, he'd said.

The voice on the tape had said *we can be patient a little longer*.

He was already humming when he climbed the ladder into the grey morning — four notes, rising — and he did not notice, because you never notice the song that's already inside you, until someone plays it back.
