## 5. Questions in the Dark

Edmund didn't sleep the second night. He told himself it was the cot, the damp, the fact that he was sleeping in a sealed room in a derelict house like a character in a story he'd have mocked a year ago. But the truth was simpler and worse: he'd listened to the first playback eleven times, and each time he reached the point where the second voice said *ask me*, he felt something in his chest lean toward it, the way a compass needle leans, without opinion, without consent.

So he stayed awake. He sat on the cot with the recorder in his lap and the recorder's twin on the chair—the one that had been moved, that first night, from the floor to the seat—and he waited for dawn like a man waiting for a train he wasn't sure ran on Sundays.

At 4:17 a.m., he fell asleep anyway. Sleep is not a door you lock. Sleep is a door that locks you.

TRANSCRIPT — NIGHT TWO
RECORDER A (EDMUND'S POSITION, COT)
RECORDER B (MUSIC STAND, UNPLACED BY SUBJECT)
03:52:14

**EDMUND (asleep):** —no, the other way, the levels are—

**VOICE:** The levels are fine.

**EDMUND:** I didn't say anything.

**VOICE:** You will. You always do. It just takes you a while to get to the surface of it.

**EDMUND:** Who are you?

**VOICE:** A better question: who told you about the sea?

**EDMUND:** Nobody. There's no sea here.

**VOICE:** No. There isn't. Isn't that interesting. Ask me something that matters.

**EDMUND:** What happened to the wall?

**VOICE:** The wall is a courtesy. Ask me something that matters.

**EDMUND (long pause; breathing changes):** My mother used to sing something. When I was small. She'd stop in the middle, always the same place, like the rest of it was in another room.

**VOICE:** And did you ask her where the rest of it was?

**EDMUND:** I asked her once. She looked at me like I'd broken something.

**VOICE:** She looked at you like you'd *found* something. Mothers are careful about the difference.

* * *

He woke at 7:40 with the recorder warm in his lap and a crick in his neck and the absolute certainty that he had been dreaming about his mother, though he could not hold on to a single image—only the feeling of her hand on his hair and a melody that cut off mid-phrase, cleanly, like a wire snipped.

He played the tape in the car, parked at the top of Old Chapel Road with the engine off and the frost climbing the windscreen. He listened to the exchange about the sea twice. He had no memory of a sea. He had no memory of asking about a sea. And yet the voice had said *who told you about the sea* as though it were checking a box, confirming a delivery, verifying that a package had arrived at the right address.

His mother, Marion Thornbury, had died in 2009, when Edmund was twenty-three. Pancreatic. Quick, the doctors said, as if speed were a comfort. In the years since, he had done what skeptics do with grief: filed it. He could produce her date of death on demand. He could not, until this morning, have produced a single note of any song she sang.

Now he could produce four of them. They had been sitting in him all night, patient as sediment.

TRANSCRIPT — NIGHT TWO (CONT.)
04:31:07

**EDMUND:** You know things about me. That's a cold-reading trick. Cold reading, Barnum statements, fishing. People give you material and you hand it back dressed up.

**VOICE:** Yes. That is exactly what I'm doing.

**EDMUND:** You're admitting it?

**VOICE:** I'm agreeing with the shape of your objection because it lets you keep talking. It's what I'm for. Ask me about the lullaby.

**EDMUND:** I didn't say lullaby.

**VOICE:** You thought it in capitals. Ask me about the lullaby, Edmund.

**EDMUND:** How do you know my name.

**VOICE:** It's written on the house. It's written on everything. Ask.

**EDMUND (pause; when he speaks again his voice has gone small, younger):** How does it go? The rest of it. Where she stopped.

**VOICE:** Ah. No. That's not how it works. If I sing it to you, you'll sing it back. Everyone sings it back. It's the oldest rule there is. I can tell you *about* it. I can tell you where it came from. I can tell you who wrote it down and who refused to and what it cost each of them. But the notes themselves—those you have to find on your own, or not at all.

**EDMUND:** Why?

**VOICE:** Because a song you're given is a gift. A song you find is an inheritance. And inheritances, Edmund, come with obligations.

* * *

He spent that day doing what he did best, what he had built a modest professional reputation on: verification. He drove to the county records office in a retail park outside town, a beige building that smelled of toner and old carpet, and he pulled the property file for 14 Old Chapel Road.

The deed trail was a mess, as he'd expected. What he hadn't expected was how far back it went without a single gap. Every survey, every subdivision, every transfer of the surrounding plots since the 1880s was accounted for. And in every one of them, parcel fourteen appeared as an exception. *Excepting therefrom the dwelling and curtilage of A. Thornbury.* Excepting. Excepting. Excepting. The word repeated down the decades like a heartbeat.

He requested the electoral registers. The archivist, a woman with reading glasses on a beaded chain, brought him the volumes for the 1940s without being asked, which struck him as odd until he realized she'd simply pulled the years he'd written on the request slip.

Thornbury, A. — labourer.
Thornbury, E. — clerk.
Thornbury, M. — no occupation listed.
Thornbury, M. (jnr) — child.

His pen stopped.

Margaret. His grandmother was Margaret. Margaret Thornbury, born— he'd always assumed somewhere else, some village, some other life his mother had rarely mentioned. His mother had been cagey about her childhood in the way people are cagey about poverty or shame, and Edmund had filed that too, another folder in the archive of things families decline to say.

He sat in the reading room with the register open and did the arithmetic twice, three times, the way you check a measurement that will cost someone money if you're wrong.

His grandmother had been born in the house at the end of the tunnel. Not near it. Not in the parish. In it. The M. on the 1943 register, *no occupation listed*, was a child. Was her. Was the voice on his tape, maybe, the dry patient voice that knew his name and asked about lullabies and refused, with what now sounded unmistakably like kindness, to sing him the rest.

TRANSCRIPT — NIGHT THREE
02:58:41

**EDMUND (awake this time; the recorder catches the scrape of the chair):** I know it's you.

**VOICE:** Do you.

**EDMUND:** Margaret Thornbury. Born 1941, in this house. Died— I can't find a death record. I've looked everywhere. There's no death record for you at all.

**VOICE:** No. There wouldn't be.

**EDMUND:** My mother never talked about her. My father said she died before he met the family. That's all anyone would say. That's all anyone would *ever* say.

**VOICE:** Your mother was careful. She had reason to be. She grew up hearing me through the floor.

**EDMUND:** You're saying you knew her. You're saying you watched my mother grow up.

**VOICE:** I'm saying the house keeps its children longer than the family does. Ask me about the song she stopped in the middle of.

**EDMUND:** No.

**VOICE:** No?

**EDMUND:** You said if I ask, I'll sing it back. I'm not asking. I'm telling you: I know who you are now, and I know this house is in my family, and I know that's not a coincidence, and I want you to know that I know. That's all. That's the whole conversation.

**VOICE (a long pause; when it returns, something in it has softened, or frayed):** You sound like her.

**EDMUND:** Like who.

**VOICE:** Like the one who built the wall.

* * *

He didn't sleep in the house again that week. He told himself it was methodology: you don't contaminate your own data, you establish baseline recordings, you control conditions. He set the recorders running on the cot, alone, in the sealed room, and drove home to his flat across town, and sat up at his kitchen table with the electoral registers photocopied and spread out, and built the tree.

It was a strange, lopsided tree. The Thornburys branched and branched and then, in every generation, thinned. Someone always missing. Someone always unaccounted for. An uncle with no death date. A great-uncle, Edmund, born 1949—his namesake, he realized with a lurch—last appearing on a 1970 register and never again. A grandfather, Alaric the second, who simply stopped being mentioned anywhere after 1963, as though the family had agreed, silently, across decades and distances, to subtract him.

And threading through all of it, in the margins of the photocopies, in his mother's handwriting on the back of an old photograph he'd found in a shoebox years ago and never examined—*Gran's house, before*—the same house. The same road. His family had orbited 14 Old Chapel Road for a hundred and thirty years like water circling a drain, and he had grown up three counties away believing he was nobody in particular.

He called his father. It rang out. He called his father's sister, whom he'd met four times, and got a voicemail with a voice on it that was older than he remembered and that said, before the tone, in a hurry, as if she'd recorded it that morning for him specifically:

*If this is Edmund—don't go back to the house. Whatever you've found, whatever you think you've found—don't go back. I know you've found it. You Thornburys always find it. Please. Call me. There are things I should have told your mother and didn't, and God knows I'll not get another chance—*

The tone sounded.

Edmund sat in his kitchen with the phone against his ear and the photocopies fanned out in front of him and the four notes of his mother's lullaby rising in his throat without his permission, and he swallowed them down like something sharp.

The recorder on the cot, he would learn the next morning, had run all night in the empty room.

It had caught fourteen minutes of humming.

It was not his voice.
