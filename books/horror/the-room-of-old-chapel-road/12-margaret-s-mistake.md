## 12. Margaret's Mistake

*From the diary of Margaret Thornbury, 1922. Recovered from the attic of 14 Old Chapel Road, 1988, and returned to the same attic in 1991 by persons unknown.*

---

**14th March, 1922**

I have decided to write everything down. Father says a woman who keeps a diary is keeping evidence against herself, but I have never been much afraid of evidence.

The house has been singing to me again. Not the pipes — I checked the pipes, all of them, with a candle and my ear pressed flat against the cold iron until my cheek ached. Not wind in the chimney either; the flue was swept at Michaelmas and the sweep found nothing but soot and one small bird's nest, dry as paper.

It is the melody. The one Grandmother used to hum when she thought no one was listening, the one that stops just before it finishes, like a sentence interrupted by someone more important walking into the room. She died last winter and took the ending with her, or so I believed.

But the house knows it. The house has always known it. And lately — these past three weeks, since the frost broke — the house has been humming it back to me through the floorboards, patient as a teacher waiting for a pupil to find her place on the page.

I am not frightened. I want that written plainly, in case I am frightened later: **on the fourteenth of March I was not afraid.**

---

**19th March**

Found the room today.

Behind the plaster in the cellar — the wall that isn't a wall, the one Father tapped and called "solid brick, Margaret, stop filling your head with stories" — there is a door. Not hidden, exactly. Simply finished over, sometime before I was born, by someone who wanted it forgotten rather than gone.

I took a hairpin and a hammer and I was perhaps forty minutes about it. The plaster came away in sheets, grey and soft as old bread, and behind it: oak, black with age, and an iron ring, and hinges that turned without a sound, which frightened me more than any creak would have. A door that has been shut for thirty years should complain. This one opened like it had been oiled yesterday. Like it had been *waiting*.

Inside: a small room, clean, dry, absurdly tidy. A chair. A music stand. On the stand, under a fine skin of dust, sheet music in a hand I did not know — copperplate, careful, the notes precise as stitching.

The title page reads: **THE VERSE FOR THE LAST ONE.**

I stood there a long while. The candle behaved itself, which is more than I can say for my hands.

There is something else I should record, because it is true even if it sounds mad. The air in that room tasted like held breath. Like the moment after you ask a question and before anyone answers. Like the whole house had stopped to see what I would do.

I took nothing. I touched nothing. I closed the door and replastered it as best I could — badly; I was never the artistic one — and came upstairs and ate a whole plate of cold mutton standing at the kitchen window like a thief.

Tomorrow I will go back.

---

**20th March**

I went back. Of course I went back. A person does not find a room her family built and sealed and simply *not go back*; that would be a different kind of madness entirely.

I read the music this time. All of it. It is our melody — Grandmother's melody, the lullaby she sang me down to sleep with, the one that always stops short. But here it continues. Here, past the place where every version I've ever heard falls silent, the line goes on. Fourteen bars further than anything I knew. Then it stops again, mid-phrase, unfinished, and beneath the final bar someone has written in faded ink:

*For the last one. To be completed within these walls. Never without them.*

Within these walls. Meaning the house? Meaning the room?

Father's grandfather built this house. Alaric Thornbury, whose portrait hangs in the hall looking like a man who has swallowed a secret and found it nourishing. What did he bury down there? What did he build a room to keep?

I sat in the chair. It fit me. That is the detail I keep circling back to tonight, unable to put it down: the chair fit me exactly, as though it had been measured for my shoulders, my height, my particular way of sitting. A chair made in 1893 cannot know the shape of a girl born in 1904.

And yet.

---

**2nd April**

I have been down six times now. I tell myself it is scholarship. Our family history is a locked drawer and I have found the key, and what sort of Thornbury walks away from that?

But I notice things I do not say aloud, even here, even to the diary.

I notice that time moves strangely below. I go down meaning to stay twenty minutes and come up into late afternoon light, stiff-necked, my candle burned to a puddle.

I notice that the melody follows me up. Not in my ears — in my chest. I catch myself humming it while I darn socks, and it is always the fourteen new bars, never the part everyone knows. As if the house has given me its half and expects me to hold up my end.

I notice that when I hum it downstairs, in the cellar, near the wall — the air changes. Thickens. Leans toward me. There is no other word for it. The held-breath taste grows stronger, and somewhere beneath the floor I feel a pulse that is not a pulse, a patience that is not patience, an attention like a hand laid flat against the other side of my ribs.

Grandmother's last words to me were strange ones. She gripped my wrist with terrible strength for a dying woman and said: *"Never sing it inside. Promise me, Maggie. Learn it anywhere you like — learn it in the churchyard, learn it on the train to York — but never inside."*

I promised. I promised a dying woman, and I meant it.

But I did not ask her why. And a promise without understanding is only a rope you haven't yet tested.

---

**9th April**

Today I sang three notes.

Three. I want that recorded with the same terrible clarity as the fourteenth of March: three notes, no more, the first phrase of the final verse, sung softly, barely voiced, in the room, in the chair, with the music open on the stand before me.

Here is what happened, as precisely as I can set it down.

The first note left my mouth and the room *received* it. I don't know how else to describe it. Sound behaves in ordinary rooms — it bounces, it fades, it scatters. This note did not fade. It was taken. It went out of me and did not echo, and the silence that followed was not empty silence but full silence, silence with weight, silence like a swallowed thing.

The second note, the same. By then my heart was going hard enough that I could feel it in my teeth, and some sane remnant of me said *stop*, and I want it known that I heard that voice and overruled it, because I am a Thornbury and we are curious past all sense.

The third note was the mistake. Or perhaps the first note was the mistake and the third was only where the mistake finished its work. Because on the third note I felt something drink.

That is the word. Drink. A pull behind my sternum, gentle as a nursing lamb, and the note left me and kept leaving me, kept pouring out past the point where I stopped singing it, a thread drawn out of my chest like silk off a spindle, and I clamped my mouth shut and the thread snapped and I sat there shaking in a chair that fit me perfectly while the room settled around me, satisfied, the held breath finally — partially — released.

Three notes. Gone. Not sung and finished, but *taken*, the way a toll is taken.

I ran upstairs. I drank two glasses of water. I sang a music-hall song at the top of my voice in the kitchen to prove I still could, and I could, and I laughed until I cried, and then I cried a little longer than laughing accounts for.

In the ledger of my mistakes, let this be the entry for the ninth of April: I felt the house drink, and I told myself it was imagination.

---

**23rd April**

Two weeks. I have stayed away two weeks, and I record with pride that the melody has mostly left me. Mostly. There are still evenings — dusk, chiefly, that grey hour — when I find the fourteen bars sitting in my chest like birds on a wire, waiting.

My voice is not quite right.

This is difficult to describe without sounding theatrical, so I will be clinical. Certain notes are harder to reach than they were. Not higher notes — lower ones. The bottom of my range has gone slightly woolly, slightly *elsewhere*, as though those particular sounds have to travel further to arrive. And sometimes, mid-phrase, a note comes out a fraction flat, and I hear it happen and cannot correct it, the way you cannot correct a stone already rolling.

I sang "Abide With Me" at Sunday service and got through it, but Mr. Ellery, the choirmaster, asked afterward if I'd been ill. He said my voice had "a hole in it." His word. A hole.

I have decided it is the spring air. Everyone's voice suffers in the spring.

---

**11th May**

The hole is bigger.

I lost a whole verse of "The Ash Grove" yesterday, standing in the garden, alone, no one to hear me fail. My mouth shaped the words and what came out was breath with the ghost of tone in it, a whisper wearing a whisper's clothes. For perhaps four seconds I could not produce sound at all. Then it returned, thinner, like water coming back into a pipe after the supply has been cut.

I have begun marking it. Each evening I sing a scale and note which notes are weak. The map of my failing is not random. It is not spreading outward from the top or the bottom. It is spreading along the melody — along *that* melody, bar by bar, following the exact line of the fourteen new notes, erasing my capacity note by note in the order the verse requires them.

As though the house is not taking my voice at random.

As though it is taking precisely the parts of my voice that the verse needs, storing them, keeping them ready, so that when I finally stand in that room and sing the whole thing through, I will not even need to try. The notes will already belong to the walls. I will only need to open my mouth and let the house use me.

I understand Grandmother's promise now. I understood it too late, and I understand it in the worst possible way: not as a warning received, but as a rule discovered by breaking it.

Three notes. It began with three notes, and it has not stopped, and I do not think it will stop, and I am writing this at midnight with my candle guttering because I am afraid that if I sleep I will dream of the room, and if I dream of the room I will walk there in my sleep, and if I walk there —

No. I will not finish that sentence. Some sentences are doors.

---

**30th May**

A bad entry. I nearly didn't write it. But the diary is the only witness I trust now, and a witness must be told everything.

Last night I woke standing in the cellar.

Not walking to the cellar — standing *in* it, barefoot on the cold flags, facing the wall I replastered, my hands grey with fresh-plundered plaster, my mouth open. I was not singing. I checked. God help me, I checked, running my tongue over my teeth like a woman searching for a lost tooth, and my throat was silent, and the house was silent, and the silence between us was the most conversation we have ever had.

I had opened the door. The plaster was broken away in a neat circle, the size of my two hands. Behind it, the oak, and the ring, and I do not remember touching any of it.

I went back to bed. I lay awake until the birds started, doing sums. Counting the nights since the ninth of April. Counting the notes I have lost. Doing the arithmetic of how many remain, and how fast they are going, and what happens when the count reaches zero.

Here is the sum I cannot avoid: the house is not stealing my voice.

The house is *trading*. Note for note. Everything it takes from me, it takes because I owe it — because I sang three notes inside the walls, and a debt entered the ledger that night, and debts in this house are collected slowly, patiently, in the debtor's own voice.

Somewhere below me, in a room with a chair that fits me, my three notes are being kept warm.

---

**17th June**

I tried to leave. I want that written too, so that whoever finds this — and someone will find this, diaries are found, that is their nature and their doom — knows that I fought.

I packed a bag. I wrote to my cousin in Harrogate asking for a bed. I got as far as the station at Knaresborough with a third-class ticket in my glove.

On the platform, the melody started. Not in my chest this time — in my *ears*, external, unmistakable, coming from the rails themselves, from the vibration of a train still miles away, humming the fourteen bars with my own missing notes slotted neatly into place. My notes. Sung back to me by the track, by the gravel, by the whole patient earth, in a voice that was mine and was not mine.

And here is the shameful thing, the thing I will circle forever: I did not board the train.

I told myself the fare was needed elsewhere. I told myself Father was unwell, though he is hale as a horse. I told myself a dozen reasonable lies on the walk home, each one softer and warmer than the last, and the house received me at the gate like a mother receiving a child who has run away to the end of the street, and I felt — I felt *welcome*. That is the horror I can least bear to write. After everything, when the door closed behind me, I felt glad.

The chorus is not cruel. That is what nobody tells you. It is lonely in the way deep water is lonely, and it wants company the way drowning wants lungs, and it makes leaving feel like grief and staying feel like home, and I am twenty-two years old and I am losing my voice one borrowed note at a time and I *feel at home*.

---

**3rd July**

Almost done now. The scale I sing each evening comes out as breath and shape, a mime of singing. I can still speak — mostly. Words are different; the house has no appetite for words, only for melody. I can talk, thin and papery, and I talk to this diary every night because talking is proof I am still partly mine.

I went down today — walked down, awake, in daylight, by choice, because I wanted to see it once more with my own eyes before my eyes are all I have left that works.

The room was warm. First time. Warm as a held hand.

The music stand was turned to face the chair. The sheet music waited, open, the final verse gleaming faintly in the candlelight as though the ink were still wet after thirty years. And on the floor beside the stand, arranged with care, were fourteen small objects I had never seen before: a thimble, a pipe, a wedding ring, a child's shoe button, a brass whistle, and others, older, plainer, worn smooth as river stones.

One for each voice, I think. One for each Thornbury who ever hummed the incomplete song and came, note by note, to live inside it.

There is space on the floor for a fifteenth object.

I sat in the chair. It fit me better than ever. And I did not sing — I have learned that much, at the very end of my learning — but I listened, really listened, and beneath the floor, beneath the walls, beneath the whole patient weight of the house, I heard them. All of them. Humming. Waiting for the verse to be finished the only way it can be finished now:

by me.

I am not afraid anymore. I want that written plainly, in case I am frightened later: I am past fear, into something quieter, and I no longer fully trust the hand holding this pen, because some of the words arriving on this page are arriving in handwriting that is almost mine.

To whoever reads this — the next one, the last one, the heir who comes after — I leave you the only inheritance I have left:

**Learn it anywhere but here. Learn it outside the walls. And whatever the house offers you, whatever warmth, whatever welcome — witness it. Do not join it.**

Three notes was all it took for me.

Do not give it even one.

* * *

*Final entry, undated, in a hand grown faint and uncertain:*

*The house is singing my verse back to me tonight, all fourteen bars, complete, in my own lost voice, and it is beautiful, and I am so tired, and the chair is warm, and I think I will just sit here a while and listen —*

*— and the strangest thing, the thing I must set down before the pen stops answering me: it is not 1922 anymore. I can hear him thinking upstairs. Someone new. Someone with my chin and my curiosity and none of my warnings.*

*I will try to reach him. God forgive me, I will try to warn him the only way I have left —*

*— in his sleep.*
