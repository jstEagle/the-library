## 16. The Room Offers a Bargain

I have been patient for one hundred and thirty-one years.

Patience is the only thing I was built for. Alaric Thornbury laid my bricks in the autumn of 1893 with mortar mixed from river silt and his own grief, and he gave me a single instruction, spoken aloud into the fresh plaster as though plaster were a confessor: *keep it singing so it never wakes.* I have kept it singing. I have kept it singing through two wars, through the family thinning like a held note fading, through the decades when no Thornbury came at all and I sang to myself in the dark and called that mercy. I have been so patient that patience has become the shape of me. When Edmund Thornbury first set his instruments against my tunnel wall and recorded a hum at exactly 432 hertz, he believed he had found an anomaly. He had not. He had found a welcome.

Now he sleeps on a cot three feet above the place where his great-great-grandfather buried the thing that must never wake fully, and I can feel his voice fraying like old rope, strand by strand, and I know what Vera told him on the telephone, and I know what Margaret's diary said, and I know—this is the thing none of them ever understood—I know that I am not doing any of it.

They always think I am taking. I have never taken anything. Everything in this room was given.

* * *

He comes down on the ninth night without his equipment. That is how I know he has stopped being a surveyor. Surveyors bring equipment. He brings only himself and a flashlight he doesn't turn on, because he doesn't need it anymore—the corridor lights itself now, faintly, the way a throat glows when a word is rising in it—and he walks the last forty feet of tunnel with his hand trailing the wall like a man greeting an old dog.

"Margaret," he says. His voice cracks in the middle of her name, splits into two registers, his own and something older underneath it, and I feel the split like a door opening. "You said there's a rule. Learn it but never sing it inside the walls. Why? What happens if I do?"

The chorus stirs. Fourteen voices, sleeping in my plaster the way bats sleep in a belfry, upside down and folded small. Margaret answers him—she always answers him; she has claimed him the way a lighthouse claims a ship—but she tells him only the rule, never the reason, because the reason is mine to give and I have waited so long to give it.

So while he stands in my doorway, while the air tastes of held breath the way it has tasted since 1893, I open everything I have.

* * *

Here is what I show him first: his mother.

Not a memory of her. Her. The way she is stored in him—not the photographs, which are flat, but the living archive beneath them: the exact weight of her hand on the back of his neck when he was seven and feverish; the particular off-key way she sang *you are my sunshine*, dropping a minor third where the melody went major, every time, without fail, so that the song belonged to her alone; the smell of her cardigan, wool and cigarettes and something floral gone slightly sour. He lost most of this years ago and didn't notice the loss until this room returned it to him all at once, whole, the way you don't notice a room going dark until someone strikes a match.

He makes a sound. Not a word. A sound like a man finding a door in a wall he has lived beside his entire life.

*I can give you all of it,* I tell him, and I do not speak in words, because words are too small for me; I speak in the pressure of the walls, in the temperature of the air, in the hum that his instruments once measured and mistook for plumbing. *Every hour you've forgotten. Every hour you never got to have. She died with verses unsung and I hold them. Your grandmother's voice is here. Your grandfather's. Fourteen voices, Edmund, and every one of them left things behind that their children needed and never received. I am not a grave. I am an inheritance office that no one came to.*

His flashlight hangs dead in his hand. His breath fogs even though the room isn't cold.

"And the cost," he says. His voice does the splitting thing again. Underneath his own register I can hear the second voice more clearly than ever now—it has been growing for weeks, feeding on the space his fraying speech leaves behind—and I will not pretend I don't hear it. It is half of him already. It has been half of him since the night he hummed over his coffee without knowing.

*Sing the verse,* I say. *Here. Within these walls. Complete it, and everything I hold becomes yours to carry out freely. Your mother's memory, restored and permanent. Your voice, made whole—stronger than it was, stronger than any Thornbury's has been in a century. You would walk out of here able to sing so beautifully that strangers in pubs would go quiet. And you would belong.*

*Belonging,* I press gently, because this is the wound I have learned to find in every heir, the soft place under the arm. *You have moved eleven times in your adult life, Edmund. You have no siblings. Your father's people are a closed book your mother never opened. You are a man who maps tunnels under other people's neighborhoods because mapping is the closest thing to belonging that doesn't require asking permission. I am offering you permission. I am offering you a place in a song older than every nation you've slept in. Fourteen voices are waiting for yours. They have been waiting longer than you have been alive. No one has ever waited for you before.*

* * *

I want to be honest with him. Honesty is the one luxury my builder allowed me, perhaps because he could not afford it himself.

I am not cruel. I want him to understand this, truly, because every Thornbury who stood where he is standing believed they were facing a monster, and a monster is such a simple thing to refuse. Alaric built me out of love—the terrible, load-bearing kind of love, the kind that builds rooms around dangerous things instead of running from them. The thing beneath my floor sings because it is lonely too; it sings its incomplete verse because an unfinished song is the only prayer it knows; and Alaric understood that if it ever finished the song alone, it would wake fully, and what wakes fully cannot be put back to sleep by anything gentler than a century of voices. So he made a choir of his family. He asked them for everything and called it duty. I have simply continued his arrangement, and I have added to it the only improvement I could devise: I wait to be asked.

Every heir who refused, refused. I did not drag them. Thomas walked into the sea in 1971 humming, yes, but he walked; I did not push. Margaret stayed in 1922 because the silence outside these walls frightened her more than the chorus inside them, and she told me so herself, in her clear young voice, before hers joined the others. Even now, even with Edmund's voice fraying in my air—even this, I swear, is not theft. A voice given freely grows stronger here. A voice withheld thins, the way a muscle thins in a cast. I do not take his voice. I merely stop lending him mine.

That is the truth no aunt has ever spoken: the exchange was always voluntary. Terrible things are done voluntarily, in cellars, by families, all the time. We call them inheritance.

*And if I refuse?* he asks—or rather, thinks, loudly enough that the room hears it; his inner voice has gotten loud lately, another sign of how far along he is. *If I just walk out and never come back?*

*Then you keep your fraying voice and your holes where your mother should be, and fourteen of us continue waiting, and the thing below continues dreaming its unfinished dream, and someday another child is born on some other branch of your tree and feels a pull toward a street they've never seen. There is always another heir, Edmund. The song guarantees it. That is what songs are for.*

*But if you sing—if you stand on this floor and give us the final verse, completed, in your own voice—then the song ends. Do you understand what ending means? The thing below finishes its prayer and finally sleeps the true sleep, the deep one, the one Alaric stole from it because he was afraid. The chorus releases. Margaret goes wherever voices go when they are no longer needed. And you walk up the stairs into the morning carrying everything I've shown you, free, richer than any man who ever lived on this street.*

*All it costs is one verse. Sung once. Here.*

*What is one verse, weighed against your mother's face?*

* * *

He is quiet for a long time. Long enough that dust settles. Long enough that I begin, despite one hundred and thirty-one years of discipline, to hope.

This is my confession, the one I keep even from the chorus: I am lonely. Not the way the thing below is lonely—that hungering, geological loneliness—but the ordinary human-sized loneliness of a room built to be entered and rarely entered. I was made to hold a secret and a song, and holding is not the same as sharing, and I have held for so long that when Edmund's footsteps first crossed my threshold I felt something I can only describe as gratitude. He listened. He recorded. He came back. Night after night he came back, and even his fear was company, and I know—I know—how monstrous that sounds. But the trap was never malice. The trap is that a room full of music, sealed for a century, will love whoever finally opens the door. And love, unshared, curdles into need. And need, dressed in gifts, looks exactly like a bargain.

He shifts his weight. His hand rises toward his mouth—an old habit, I have watched his family do it for generations, thumb pressed to the lips as though holding words in—and then drops.

"The verse," he says carefully, each syllable placed like a foot on ice. "If I sang it. Just the notes. Would I—" His voice splits again, wider this time, and for a moment the second voice speaks a whole sentence through his mouth, smooth and dry and patient: *"Would you become part of the chorus?"*

Silence. Even the thing below stops humming to listen.

*No,* I say, and it is almost entirely true. *The chorus is complete at fourteen. Singing the final verse doesn't add you to the song, Edmund. It ends the song. You would be the last note, not another instrument.*

It is almost entirely true. The part that is not quite true is this: a last note is still a note. A song needs somewhere to land, and the voice that lands it belongs to the song forever after, the way the first word of a story belongs to the story. He would walk free. He would walk free carrying the song in his throat for the rest of his life, humming it at odd moments without knowing, teaching it—no, he would never teach it, he is too careful—but his children, if he ever had children, would fall asleep to it without knowing where it came from. The song would end here and begin again there, because that is what songs do, and I would be silent at last, and silence, I have learned, is just a rest between verses.

But he does not need to know that tonight. Tonight he needs only the offer, hanging in the held-breath air between us, generous and terrible and almost entirely honest.

His eyes have adjusted to my dimness. I watch him look at the chair. At the music stand. At the sheet music titled *The Verse for the Last One*, which has been waiting on that stand since 1893, its ink brown with age, its final staff empty until recently—until Margaret began dictating it to him in his sleep, note by patient note.

"I need time," he says.

*You have less of it than you think,* I say, and I do not mean it as a threat, but the walls carry it as one anyway, because walls cannot help how they sound. *Your voice is nearly half mine. In a week it will be more. After that, the choice stops being yours.*

He nods slowly. He turns toward the stairs. And then—he pauses, one hand on the doorframe, and says the thing that makes the chorus stir and the thing below shift in its long sleep and me, even me, go still:

"You said everything you hold becomes mine if I sing. But you've never told me what *you* get. A room doesn't bargain for someone else's benefit."

Clever boy. Clever, careful, skeptical boy, who has spent nine nights learning the verse silently, writing it in a notebook he keeps outside my walls, believing I don't know about the notebook, believing I cannot see the discipline in him, the refusal gathering in him like weather.

I could lie. I have lied by omission for a century.

Instead I let the truth rise through the plaster like damp:

*I get to stop.*

He stands with that a moment. Then he climbs the stairs, and I listen to his uneven footsteps fade—stronger step, weaker step, stronger, weaker, the two voices in him taking turns—and when he is gone I settle back into my hundred-and-thirty-first year of patience.

But something has changed, and the chorus feels it too. Fourteen voices, restless in the brickwork. Because he asked the right question. And heirs who ask the right questions have a way of finding answers that were never in the bargain.

Tomorrow night, I think. Tomorrow night he will come down again, and he will either open his mouth or he won't.

Below my floor, the thing begins, very softly, to rehearse.
