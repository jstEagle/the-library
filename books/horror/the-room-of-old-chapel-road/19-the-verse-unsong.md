## 19. The Verse Unsong

The tunnel was shorter going down.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed, and the last rational thought he had for a long time: that a passage which had taken him eleven minutes to walk on his first survey now delivered him to the cellar door in something under four, as though the house had spent all these weeks learning the shape of his stride and had finally decided to stop pretending at distance. His boots found the steps without his eyes needing them. The dark did not resist him. It received him.

He had burned the notebook that morning in a galvanized bucket behind Ruth Okafor's fence, feeding the pages in one at a time until the transcription of the final verse existed nowhere on paper, nowhere in any device, only in the sealed room of his own attention. He had memorized it the way Margaret's notation demanded—not as melody, never as melody, but as intervals, as architecture, as a sequence of relationships between pitches that he could hold in his mind the way you hold a floor plan. He had not hummed once in nine days. He had caught himself twice at the edge of it, breath gathering in his chest like a sneeze, and both times he had pressed his tongue to the roof of his mouth and swallowed the sound whole.

Now he stood at the false wall with his palm flat against the plaster, and beneath his hand he felt it: the vibration, faint and constant, like a hive thinking.

"I'm not singing," he said aloud. His voice came out sanded down, half its former grain—the fraying had accelerated in the last week, and some mornings he woke and produced nothing but air for the first hour—but it was still his. "I want that understood before anything else happens. Whatever you're about to do, whatever you're about to show me—I'm here to listen. That's all."

The wall did not answer. The wall never answered. But the plaster warmed under his palm by a degree, the way a cheek warms when someone turns toward you, and the section of it that had opened for him before—along the seam where the old builders had hidden their joinery—breathed inward with a sound like a held sigh finally released.

* * *

The room was exactly as he had left it, and entirely different.

The chair still sat in its corner. The music stand still held the original sheet, *The Verse for the Last One*, the ink browned to the color of dried blood. But the air itself had changed texture. Before, it had tasted like held breath—like a lung kept full for a hundred and thirty years. Now it tasted like an audience. Like the minute before a curtain.

They were already singing.

Fourteen voices, layered and interwoven, rising from no throats, from the walls themselves, from the floor joists, from the water in the old lead pipes—and they were singing the song entire, the long incomplete melody he had been hearing in fragments since his first night on the cot, except that tonight they carried it further than he had ever heard it go. Past the phrase where the tape always broke. Past the bar where Aunt Vera's cassette had died mid-note, every time, on every machine, in every year. The voices went on into territory the recordings had never reached, and Edmund understood with a lurch that this was the point, that this was what the room had been saving: the ending, performed live, for an audience of one.

He sat down in the chair because standing felt like arrogance. He folded his hands. He made himself a vessel and nothing more.

The song was beautiful. That was the cruelty of it, or perhaps the honesty—it was genuinely, devastatingly beautiful, a melody built like a staircase descending through keys, each verse a step down into darker registers, and the fourteenth voice underneath it all was Alaric himself, Edmund realized, the founding bass note, the man who had buried the thing in 1893 and built this room to keep it dreaming. The song was a lullaby. It had always been a lullaby. And every verse that ended short was a lullaby interrupted, a child rocked almost to sleep and then shaken awake again, over and over, for five generations.

The thirteenth voice was his grandfather's. He knew it the way you know a face in a crowd you've been told to watch for—a warmth in the timbre, a habit of leaning into the high notes. Vera had played him a single surviving home recording once, thirty seconds of a man whistling in a garden, dead these fifty years and yet here, holding his line in the chorus, patient as stone.

And the twelfth voice, the one that wound through the others like smoke through railings, was Margaret. 1922. The last one who had tried to learn the verse alone.

She was singing the part closest to the ending. She was carrying the melody right up to the lip of the final phrase, the place where the song wanted to resolve, and then—Edmund heard it happen, heard it happen in real time, heard the whole structure lean forward like a wave that would not break—all fourteen voices stopped. Together. On the same suspended chord. A silence shaped exactly like the missing verse, a silence with the negative space of music in it, waiting.

The room waited.

Edmund counted his own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. In the dark behind his eyes he held the transcription he had memorized and burned, the sequence of intervals Margaret had scratched into her diary margins in her last legible month, and he let it sit there, complete, understood, *known*—and unsung.

"No," he said quietly.

The chord tightened. The walls leaned in. He felt the room's attention the way you feel weather change against your skin, pressure dropping before a storm, and beneath the pressure something vast and old and terribly lonely turned its regard upon him—not malice, never malice, he understood that now; the room had been built as a mercy and had curdled into need, and need this size does not distinguish between love and consumption.

"You already know it," the room said, in the voice that had answered his questions in the dark for weeks, dry and patient, Margaret's voice. "You've known it for days. It costs you three notes. Three notes, Edmund, and it ends. Everyone goes home. Isn't that what you've wanted since the first night—to make it end?"

"It ends when I say it," he said. "Not sung."

"Everything must be sung. Songs are how the living speak to the dead. Your grandfather learned that. He chose us over your grandmother's silence, and he was not wrong to—" The voice caught, or simulated catching, and Edmund heard beneath it the enormous machinery of the house adjusting, recalculating, trying a new door. "Your mother forgot your fourth birthday. You told me. You told me in your sleep, in March, and I have not forgotten it for you. Sing the verse and remember her whole."

"That's the bargain you offered before." His voice cracked in the middle of the sentence and he rode the crack down, let it break, refused to be ashamed of it. "I'm not here to bargain. I'm here to witness."

He stood up from the chair.

The room shuddered—or the house did, the whole structure, a tremor running up through the foundations like a dog shaking off water—and the fourteen voices surged, the melody climbing back toward the broken place, offering it to him again, the resolution hanging there open-mouthed, and Edmund did the hardest thing he had ever done in his life, harder than the descent, harder than the burning of the notebook:

He listened to the ending fully, all the way through, inside his own head, note by note, interval by interval, the whole final verse complete and perfect and silent—and he did not sing it.

Instead, he spoke.

* * *

His voice was wrecked and he used it anyway, plain words in a plain room, stripped of every note, rhythm flattened to speech, melody amputated at the root. This was the trick of it, the thing Margaret had never been told and had paid everything to learn too late: the song needed a singer, needed a throat, needed the human instrument to carry the final verse across the threshold. Spoken words were dead weight. Spoken words fed it nothing. But spoken words could still *name*.

"Alaric Thornbury," he said, and the lowest voice faltered on its line. "Who buried what he should have faced. Who built this room instead of digging the grave. Who thought a cage was a kindness."

The bass note wavered, lost its place in the measure, found it again.

"Margaret Thornbury. 1922. Who sang three notes and spent forty years paying for them, and whose name nobody in my family ever said out loud again—which was the second burial, and I'm undoing it now."

The smoke-voice threading through the others went still. Not gone. Attentive.

"Thomas Thornbury, 1948. Eleanor Thornbury, 1951. The twins, Peter and Paul, 1963." He worked through the names Ruth Okafor had helped him find in the parish records, the names Vera had wept out over the phone in her last lucid week, the names of people who had walked into this cellar curious or grieving or called by dreams and had stayed, humming, for decades, while the family above ground practiced forgetting. Each name landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Each time, one voice in the chorus stumbled, slowed, began—Edmund could feel it rather than hear it—to separate from the melody, to become a person again instead of a part.

"Gerald Thornbury, 1971." Vera's brother, the surveyor, the one who came back wrong. "You went in to map it. Nobody maps a thing like this. You were lonely too. I'm sorry nobody asked you that while you could still answer."

The humming in the walls grew ragged. The suspended chord—the one the song had hung on for a hundred and thirty years, the unresolved ache at its center—began to lose its grip, because a chord is only a chord while the voices agree to hold it together, and he was unmaking their agreement one name at a time.

"And my grandfather." Edmund's voice failed entirely on the next word. He stood in the dark with his ruined throat working, and gave it a moment, and started again, quieter. "You stayed because staying was warm. Because the chorus is kinder than silence. I know. I've felt it too—every night this month, the pull of it, how easy it would be. But warmth isn't the same as being held, and a song that can't end isn't a home. It's just a room."

He took a breath that hurt all the way down.

"The verse is finished. I know it whole. And I will carry it out of this house and I will never sing it here, and no Thornbury after me will ever learn it inside these walls, because I am going to tell them why. That's the inheritance now. Not the song. The warning."

The room shuddered again, deeper, structural, dust sifting from the ceiling beams in fine grey threads, and the fourteen voices rose together one last time—not in the melody, not anymore, but in something rawer, a sound with no notes in it at all, relief or grief or both braided together, the sound of a held breath finally, after a hundred and thirty years, let go.

The suspended chord dissolved.

And in the space where it had been, the room was quiet—not empty, not yet, but quiet, the way a theater is quiet when the play has ended and the actors are still bowing, and somewhere in that quiet Edmund heard, very close, at the level of his own ear, a woman's voice say three words in a tone of unmistakable gratitude:

*"Well done, cousin."*

Then the dark began, gently, to give back its dead.
