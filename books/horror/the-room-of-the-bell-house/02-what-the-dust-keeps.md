## 2. What the Dust Keeps

Isandro Zhou had a rule, and the rule was this: if it could be written down, it could be survived.

He'd learned it young, in the years after his mother's diagnosis, when memory became something that had to be caught and pinned before it flew. He kept notebooks then—small ones, cheap ones, their covers softening with handling—and he filled them with everything: what she said, what she wore, what the weather did on days that mattered. Later, when he took up pest control, the habit followed him into attics and crawlspaces like a religion he couldn't shake. Every job got logged. Every anomaly got timestamped. Evidence was a kind of prayer, and Isandro prayed compulsively.

Bell House was going to require a great deal of prayer.

He arrived at seven-forty in the morning with two hard cases of equipment and a thermos of coffee gone lukewarm from the drive. The frost had not melted overnight; it lay on the fields in white sheets, and the house stood in the middle of it like a tooth in gum. He photographed the exterior first—north face, south face, the sagging porch, the chimney with its cap of ice—as per protocol, section one, subsection A: *Establish baseline before intervention.*

The front door was unlocked. He noted that. He noted the smell inside too: cold stone and old wool and, beneath it, something faintly sweet, like apples past their prime.

"Log entry one," he said aloud, because he narrated for his recorder as well as wrote. "Seven forty-three a.m., interior entry via front door. No resistance. Temperature—" He pulled the reader from his belt. "Four degrees Celsius. Ambient exterior was minus six. Interior warmer than outside by ten degrees. Noted."

He said *noted* the way other men said *amen.*

* * *

He worked the ground floor methodically, room by room, left to right from the entrance, the way he'd been taught and the way he'd refined over eleven years. Kitchen first. Then pantry. Then the parlor, the study, the dining room, the back hall. In each room he performed the same sequence: visual sweep, moisture reading, dust assessment, droppings survey, thermal scan. In each room he found the same thing, or versions of it.

The droppings were wrong.

Not absent—he'd been promised an infestation, and there was evidence of rodents, certainly—but arranged. In the kitchen, behind the kick panel of the lower cabinets, he found mouse droppings laid out in rows so straight they might have been set with a ruler. Forty-one pellets in the first row. Thirty-eight in the second. He counted them twice, photographed them three times, and stood there with his pen hovering over the notebook, unsure how to write down a thing like order.

"Rodents don't do this," he told the recorder. His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Rodents scatter. Rodents follow walls. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is curation."

In the parlor, the dust on the mantelpiece held fingerprints. Small ones, child-sized, pressed side by side like a family posing for a picture no camera would take. He documented them, measured them—nineteen millimeters across at the widest point—and moved on.

It was when he reached the study that he noticed the cold.

Cold spots were normal in houses like this. Drafts, poor insulation, dead chimneys breathing through blocked flues. He catalogued them without interest: corner by the window, minus one degree; beside the bookcase, zero. But the cold spot near the door was different, because when he moved to the desk, it moved too.

He tested it the way he tested everything: repeatedly, with variations. He walked to the far wall—the cold followed, lagging half a second behind, like a companion who didn't want to seem eager. He stood still for four minutes. The cold settled around him at a distance of roughly one meter, holding there, patient as weather.

"Thermal anomaly," he recorded, "exhibiting apparent directed movement. Repeat: apparent. Possible explanation includes concealed draft source, subfloor airflow, operator error." He wrote all of it down. The writing helped. The writing always helped. As long as the phenomenon existed on paper, it existed on his terms—a specimen, not a presence.

But when he left the study, he paused at the threshold and, against protocol, against his own better judgment, said quietly: "I see you."

The cold did not answer. But it felt, for one unmeasurable moment, less like a draft and more like attention.

* * *

The staircase to the second floor was oak, worn pale down the center of each tread, and it complained under his weight in a voice like an old woman listing grievances. He counted fourteen steps up. He always counted stairs. It was procedure—stair counts established spatial baselines, useful for mapping and for noticing changes in structure.

He would remember that count later. He would remember it the way you remember the last ordinary thing before the water goes cold.

The upstairs hallway ran east to west. At the east end: a linen closet, a bathroom, a bedroom that had clearly belonged to someone tidy—dust sheets over the furniture, corners sharp. At the west end: a nursery.

He worked the east rooms first. The bathroom was unremarkable except for a mirror fogged from the inside, which he logged as condensation from a poorly insulated pipe and chose, for the moment, to believe. The tidy bedroom gave him nothing but the sense of being read over his shoulder, which he declined to log because there was no instrument for it.

Then the nursery.

The door was closed. He knocked—habit, courtesy, superstition, he couldn't have said which—and pushed it open onto a room that stopped him in the doorway longer than any room had in eleven years of doorways.

Someone had cared for this room once. Wallpaper printed with faded rabbits. A crib beneath the window. A rocking chair with its cushion still plump. And dust everywhere, thick and grey and undisturbed—

Except for the handprints.

They were fresh. That was the first wrongness, and his mind reached for it gratefully: fresh prints meant recent intrusion, meant animal or human, meant a problem with a solution. He knelt and examined the nearest one on the floorboards, then the ones climbing the crib's rail, then—his stomach tightening—the ones on the ceiling, directly above the rocking chair, five prints splayed like a starfish pressed into plaster.

Too large. That was the second wrongness, and it did not bring comfort. The span of each print was nearly nine inches. A large man's hand. A very large man's hand, pressed into dust that had lain undisturbed for years, leaving impressions crisp enough to show whorls and ridges.

Fresh prints. Old dust. Both true. He wrote the contradiction down and felt the notebook tremble slightly in his grip.

"Handprints," he recorded, "inconsistent with any known local fauna. Span approximately twenty-two centimeters. Distribution suggests—" Suggests what? Climbing? Reaching down? He made himself finish. "Distribution suggests movement from floor to crib to ceiling. Timestamp: nine fifty-one a.m. Photographing now."

His hands were steady. He made sure of it. Steady hands were part of the procedure too.

* * *

By noon he had the house wired. Four cameras covering the stairwell, both landings, the kitchen, and the nursery. Two audio recorders—one in the nursery, one in the upstairs hallway—running on fresh batteries with forty-eight hours of capacity. A thermal logger in the study, where the cold liked to keep company. He labeled every device, logged every placement, drew a floor plan in his notebook with measurements taken twice.

The floor plan bothered him. He checked it three times before he let himself understand why.

On his initial pass, walking east to west, the upstairs hallway had measured eleven meters. He had the numbers in his own handwriting, taken at eight fifty-two that morning. Now, walking west to east with his tape measure, returning from the nursery toward the stairs, the hallway measured thirteen.

He remeasured. Thirteen meters, four centimeters. He walked it again, east to west. Eleven meters, one centimeter. West to east: thirteen.

"The hallway is longer on the return," he told the recorder, and heard how insane it sounded, and said it again anyway, firmer. "The hallway is longer on the return trip. Directional asymmetry of approximately two meters. Possible explanations: measurement error, uneven flooring affecting stride, structural irregularity." He looked down the corridor, at the wallpaper pattern repeating its small grey flowers all the way to the window light. "Possible explanations are getting thin," he added, and did not delete it.

Here was the thing about procedure: it held. Even now—even with cold that followed him courteously from room to room, even with a hallway that changed its mind depending on which way you hoped it went—the procedure held. Measure twice. Write it down. Name the anomaly, contain it in language, close the notebook, move to the next task. Anything written down could be survived, because anything written down had edges. It ended where the page ended.

He ate lunch in the van, deliberately, because eating inside felt like accepting hospitality and he wasn't ready to owe this house anything. Through the windshield he watched the windows and saw nothing watching back, which he logged as *no visible activity, 1200–1245 hrs,* and almost believed.

* * *

At four o'clock he began the treatment itself—bait stations, sealant, the standard work, the honest work, the work that reminded him he was a professional and not a man losing an argument with a building. He worked the ground floor while the light went amber and then blue, and the whole time he talked to his recorder, narrating placements and readings, keeping his voice level and professional, filling the house with the sound of himself.

That was the trick, he'd found, in old and hostile buildings. Keep talking. Fill the silence with documentation until the silence has nothing left to haunt.

He finished at dusk. He packed his tools, checked his cameras' indicator lights—four green eyes blinking steadily in the gloom—and stood in the front hall pulling on his gloves, ready to go home to his apartment and his second notebook and the small sane rituals of an ordinary evening.

That was when he remembered the stairs.

Fourteen steps up. He'd counted that morning; it was in the log, in his own handwriting, timestamped 8:47 a.m. He looked at the staircase now, rising into the dark of the upper hall, and something in him—some old instinct older than procedure—made him count them again on the way down.

He climbed to the landing and descended slowly, counting aloud, quiet, just for the recorder.

"One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen."

He stood on the bottom step, hand on the newel post, heart tapping at his ribs.

"Fifteen," he said. "Fifteen steps down. Log entry: stair count discrepancy. Up: fourteen. Down: fifteen." He clicked off the recorder. Then, because the silence in the house had gone dense and listening, he turned it back on.

"Correction," he said. "Up: fourteen. Down: fifteen. The house has one more step than it should." He paused, and heard himself add, in a voice he barely recognized as his own:

"Noted."

Outside, the frost was already forming on the windows behind him—in patterns, though he would not see them until morning, that looked less like frost every hour. And upstairs, in the dark nursery, the audio recorder's green light blinked on and on, drinking the silence, keeping whatever the silence said next.

He would listen to the tapes tomorrow. He would wish, for a long time afterward, that he never had.
