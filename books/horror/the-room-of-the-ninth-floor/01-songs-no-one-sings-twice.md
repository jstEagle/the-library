## 1. Songs No One Sings Twice

The road into Vado Seco loses its asphalt a kilometer before the village begins, as if the place had negotiated with the modern world and won a concession: you may come this far, but the last stretch you must do on gravel, slowly, rattling your teeth loose from whatever certainties you carried down from the city. I learned that on my first day, five years ago, in a borrowed truck with a broken radio and forty-one cassette tapes packed in wool socks so they wouldn't crack against each other on the switchbacks. I have made that drive perhaps two hundred times since, to the archive in the provincial capital and back, and every time the gravel takes something from me—a little impatience, a little certainty—and gives back nothing except the smell of dry thyme crushed under the tires.

Vado Seco sits at eleven hundred meters in a fold of the Sierra de la Umbría, which is not really an umbria at all; there is no shade worth the name. The houses are the color of the hillside they were cut from, so that from the ridge above, the village looks less like a settlement than like a wound that has healed over without quite closing. There are three streets and a plaza, one bar, one church whose bell rings only for funerals now, and above everything, on the shoulder of the mountain where the pines give up, the sanatorium—white, long, blind-windowed, built in 1931 for lungs ruined by the mines and abandoned in 1968 when the last patients were moved down-valley. Or so the official record says. The official record says a great many things about Vado Seco, and I believed all of them for longer than I should have.

I came for the songs. That was true, at least. The region's musical traditions were dying the way old people die—not suddenly but by subtraction, one singer at a time, until what remained was a name in a footnote and a melody someone half-remembered wrong. My doctoral committee had approved the project with the enthusiasm committees reserve for projects they expect to be safely unremarkable. *Field documentation of vernacular song in the upper Umbría valley.* I would record, transcribe, catalog, publish, and be rewarded with a lectureship in a city where no one would ever ask me about the other thing—the scandal, the accusation, the career that ended before it began. But that comes later. Everything comes later. On that first morning I knew only that the gravel road was loud, the light was merciless, and an old woman named Encarnación Ortuña was waiting at her gate with bread still warm enough to fog the paper bag, because her son had telephoned ahead to say a stranger was coming who wanted to hear them sing.

"Five years," Marisol says sometimes, when she catches me staring at nothing. "You've been here five years and you still flinch when we touch you." She is twenty-six, the youngest of the Ortuña grandchildren, and she says it without cruelty, the way you'd note that a dog flinches at raised hands—observing a fact about damage without requiring its explanation. The family never required explanations. That was the first miracle of Vado Seco, though I didn't recognize it as one. In the city, everyone had demanded my account of myself, my defense, my confession. Here, Amparo Ortuña looked me up and down on my first evening, saw whatever it is she sees, and said only: "You'll want the room facing away from the mountain. The others get cold."

I took the room facing away from the mountain. I have slept in it for five years, and in all that time I never once asked why the house had been arranged so that no bed faced the sanatorium, or why the shutters of the north rooms stayed closed even in August, or why, some nights, the whole family seemed to be listening to something with their mouths carefully neutral, the way people listen when listening is work.

I told myself these were mountain habits. Old houses make old customs. Cold drafts, superstition, the residue of hard winters. Everything strange has an explanation if you're willing to reach far enough for it, and I was always willing, because reaching for explanations was infinitely easier than standing still inside the strangeness itself.

* * *

The recording project went the way such projects go: slowly, then all at once, then slowly again. The first month I collected nothing but refusals delivered with enormous courtesy. Then Amparo's brother-in-law Fidel sang *La Virgen de la Niebla* for me in the bar, three verses, his voice cracked as drought earth, and word went around that the stranger paid attention—that he wrote things down, that he asked the second verse to be sung again and listened to it as if it mattered. After Fidel, the dam broke. Old men sang me ploughing songs and drinking songs and one extraordinary funeral lament that had supposedly come down from the Carlist wars. Women sang me lullabies and wedding satires and the long ballad of the miller's daughter, forty-two verses, which Rosario Delgado performed in a single sitting while her granddaughter braided her hair, and I sat with the microphone trembling in my hands because I understood, with the clarity that comes maybe twice in a working life, that I was hearing something that would otherwise vanish.

I filled notebooks. I labeled cassettes in my small precise hand: date, singer, place, tune family, variant notes. I was good at it. I had trained myself to be good at exactly this—to sit quietly, to ask nothing personal, to become a piece of furniture with ears. It was the skill my disgrace had left me, honed to a fine edge: invisibility, worn deliberately, like a coat.

And it was in those first months that I learned the rule.

Not learned—collided with. It was October, my second autumn, and I had gone up the hill to record Aurelia Sáenz, who was eighty-nine and had the largest repertoire of anyone in the valley. We sat in her kitchen among copper pots gone dull with disuse, and she sang me song after song, and I grew bold with success, and when she finished a lullaby I'd never heard—a slow, minor-key thing with a descending phrase at the end of each verse, like water finding a lower level—I did what I always did with new material.

"That's beautiful," I said. "Could you sing it once more?"

The kitchen changed temperature. I swear this. Not dramatically—no wind through closed windows—but the way a room changes when a door somewhere below has opened onto cold air. Aurelia's hands, which had been folded on the table, went flat against the wood, palms down, fingers spread, as if steadying herself against a jolt.

"No," she said.

"I'm sorry—I only meant, for the transcription, the second verse was—"

"You don't sing it twice." Her voice had dropped into a register I hadn't heard from her, flat and quick, the voice of someone reciting rather than deciding. "You can put it in your machine. You can write it in your book. But you don't ask a person to say it again. Once is given. Twice is called."

"Called?" I remember laughing, gently, the laugh you use with the elderly when superstition surfaces. "Called how? Like calling cattle?"

Aurelia looked at me for a long moment. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, the blue washed out of them by ninety years, and in them I saw something I had no framework for yet: not anger, not fear exactly, but a kind of weary pity, the look of someone watching a child play near a well.

"Some songs are counting," she said. "You don't interrupt a count. And you don't make it start over." Then she stood, thanked me for coming—it was the only rude hospitality I ever received in Vado Seco, that thanks, so formal it was a dismissal—and walked me to the gate, and stood there in the cold until I was well down the path, as if making sure I kept going.

That night I played the tape back in my room, headphones on, lamp low, and the lullaby was there, complete, four verses, the descending phrase falling away at the end of each one like steps going down. I transcribed it. I filed it under *Cuna / berceuse / unclassified variant*. In the margin of the notebook, because I was young-ish and arrogant in the specific way of people who think rigor is courage, I wrote: *S. refuses repetition — folk taboo, cf. similar prohibitions re: funeral psalms. Rationalize later.*

Rationalize later. That became my motto without my ever choosing it. Every anomaly in Vado Seco got the same treatment: noted, filed, deferred. The chalk marks on door lintels that renewed themselves—weathering, I said, or children's games. The way conversation stopped, cleanly, like a needle lifted from a record, whenever I approached certain topics. The fact that no one in the village, in five years, had ever once sung any song twice for me—not the same day, not the next, not ever—and that when I began keeping track, discreetly, in the back pages of a notebook I didn't label, the list of songs-never-repeated ran to sixty-one entries and included nearly everything the oldest singers knew.

Folk taboo, I wrote. Regional practice. Cf. comparable prohibitions.

I was very good at rationalizing later. Later was where I lived.

* * *

Here is what the loneliness was like, in those early years, because the loneliness matters—it is the load-bearing wall of everything that happened after.

I ate at the Ortuña table six nights out of seven. Amparo fed me until I had to undo a button on my trousers and teased me in front of her grandchildren until I blushed like a boy. Tomás Ortuña, Amparo's son, taught me to play mus and cheat at mus and lose gracefully at mus. Marisol read over my shoulder while I transcribed and invented better lyrics than the originals and was offended when I wouldn't use them. They folded me into the household the way you fold an egg into batter—completely, without leaving any part of me separate—and I loved them with a ferocity that frightened me, because loving people is dangerous when you are hiding something, and I was always hiding something. Even before Vado Seco, I had been hiding something. The village simply gave the hiding a roof.

But at night, alone in the room facing away from the mountain, the loneliness arrived on schedule, punctual as pain. Because being taken in is not the same as belonging, and being fed is not the same as being known, and I had constructed my entire survival on the difference between those things. They knew I was from the university. They knew I recorded songs. They did not know why a man with a doctorate and a reputation—what remained of one—had chosen a village so remote that the bus came twice a week, and they never asked, and their not-asking was both the kindest gift I had ever received and the loneliest room I had ever lived in.

Some nights I would take out the tapes just to hear voices. That's a confession I can only make now, looking back: I used the archive as company. Rosario's forty-two verses. Fidel's cracked Virgin. Aurelia's forbidden lullaby, which I played more often than any other, telling myself it was for analytical purposes—the unusual modal structure, the descending cadence—when the truth was simpler and sadder. It was the sound of someone singing to me. Aurelia had been dead two years by then, and her voice in my headphones was the closest thing I had to being spoken to after dark.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the whole of what I have left: I loved the recordings more than I feared the rule. When Aurelia said *you don't make it start over*, some part of me—the part that had already lost everything once and rebuilt itself out of paper and tape—heard only a research problem. A taboo to document. A boundary to map. I did not understand yet that boundaries in Vado Seco were not drawn around knowledge. They were drawn around hunger.

* * *

The winter of my fifth year—the last winter, though I didn't know it—began with the excavation.

I had known about the plans for months; the whole village had. A foundation grant, a heritage restoration scheme, money finally flowing uphill to the forgotten places. The sanatorium was to be stabilized, surveyed, partially renovated into a museum of mountain medicine. Tomás had taken a contract with the crew as a local foreman—he knew the ground, he said, better than any engineer from the capital—and he came home in the evenings smelling of diesel and talking about rebar and drainage with a satisfaction I'd rarely seen in him. Work was work. Money was money. The building was just a building, empty since before Marisol was born.

"Empty," Amparo said once, very quietly, when Tomás said that at dinner. Just the one word, set down between the plates like a stone.

"Apart from pigeons," Tomás amended, and Marisol laughed, and the moment passed, and I—well. I reached for an explanation. Grief, probably. Some memory of the tuberculosis years; half the village had lost someone up there. The old carry their dead in odd pockets. I wrote nothing down. There are things I chose not to write down, and I have had five years and one eternity to regret the ratio.

The crew began at the foundations in late November, because the grant required visible progress before the fiscal year turned. I watched them from the ridge path some mornings, small figures and one yellow excavator against the white flank of the building, and I felt the mild, abstract pleasure of a scholar seeing a neglected site receive attention. I even thought, idly, that I might apply for permission to survey the interior once the structure was safe. There were supposed to be patient registers, admission books, maybe photographs. An archive inside the building that loomed over the archive in my room.

I counted the floors, that morning on the ridge path, out of a habit I couldn't have explained if you'd asked me. It's something I do with large buildings, count the rows of windows; I told myself it was the same instinct that makes me count stairs, sheep, verses. Eight floors, the blueprints in the provincial office said. Eight floors, the historical plaque at the road bend said, in letters gone green.

I counted nine.

I counted again. Nine rows of windows, the top row smaller, darker, its glass gone the grey-brown of old mirror-backing, so that the panes reflected nothing—not the sky, not the pines, not me standing on the ridge with my breath steaming—reflected nothing at all, as if the light that reached them was being used up before it could bounce back.

Old buildings settle, I told myself. Additions get bricked over; cornices confuse the eye. I stood there in the cold doing arithmetic with my eyes until the numbers agreed to behave, and then I walked down into the village, and bought bread, and let Amparo scold me for not wearing the scarf she'd knitted, and said nothing about the ninth floor, because there was no ninth floor, and a folklorist does not go around reporting buildings that contradict their own plaques.

That night, for the first time in five years, I dreamed of the sanatorium. In the dream I was in my room, the room facing away from the mountain, and through the wall—through the closed shutter, through the glass, across the whole dark distance of the valley—came the sound of many people humming a single lullaby in perfect unison, and beneath the humming, steady as a metronome, patient as dripping water, a voice counting.

It was only a dream. Dreams are the mind's filing system; Freud said so, and worse men than Freud have been believed. I woke, I made coffee, I labeled tape number two hundred and fourteen.

Two nights later, Tomás came home from the excavation with plaster dust in his hair and a look on his face I had never seen there before, and behind him, at the edge of the lamplight, the whole village seemed to lean in to listen.

They had found a door.
