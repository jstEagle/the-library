## 10. The Two Who Left

Tomás had spent twenty-two years in excavation and he knew every way a man could go missing from a site. Drink. Debt. A woman in the next valley. A foot through rotten flooring and a long slide into dark. He'd found men in all of those conditions and none of them had frightened him the way Fidel and Rupe were going to frighten him at the treeline, though of course he didn't know that yet. At six that morning he only knew that two bunks were empty, that their boots were gone but their jackets hung on their hooks like shed skins, and that the coffee pot was full and cold, which meant neither man had touched it before leaving. Fidel never left coffee untouched. Fidel would drink it off a shovel blade.

The night before, the whole crew had dreamed the same corridor. Tomás hadn't said so out loud. Neither had anyone else. They'd stood around the fire pit eating stew with the careful silence of men who have all seen the same ghost and agreed not to name it. But Tomás had felt it in the way they passed the bread — hands moving too fast, eyes down, everyone counting under their breath without knowing they were doing it. He'd caught himself tallying spoonfuls. One, two, three. He'd stopped at nine and put the spoon down.

Now the morning was grey and thin, the kind of mountain light that makes everything look like it's already been abandoned, and Tomás walked the perimeter of the site twice before letting himself admit what his gut already knew: they hadn't run. Running men take food, money, the good jacket. Fidel and Rupe had taken nothing but themselves.

He followed the track up past the water tank, past the last of the terraces where the olives grew crooked and old, and there the trees began — black pine mostly, dense as wool, the treeline running along the shoulder of the mountain like a seam. The sanatorium sat above and behind him now. He didn't look at it. He'd gotten good, these three days since the door, at not looking at it. A man could live a long time not looking at things.

"Fidel," he called. His voice went flat against the pines. "Rupe. This isn't funny, cabrones."

The pines gave back nothing. Then they gave back something worse than nothing: a voice. Two voices, actually, braided so tightly together they made one sound, rising and falling in a rhythm Tomás recognized from somewhere he couldn't place — a counting rhythm, patient as dripping water.

"—siete, ocho, nueve—"

He pushed through the last rank of trees and stopped as if he'd walked into glass.

They were standing in a small clearing where the forest floor opened onto bare stone, maybe forty meters apart from each other, facing uphill. Facing the sanatorium, though the building itself was hidden by the ridge; they were looking at empty air and seeing something in it. Fidel stood with his arms loose at his sides, his head tilted slightly, like a man listening to music through a wall. Rupe's hands were folded in front of him, almost formal, almost prayerful. Both men's eyes were open. That was the detail that would stay with Tomás for the rest of his life, longer than the door, longer even than what came after: their eyes were open, wide and dry and fixed, and they were awake. You could tell they were awake because when Tomás stepped on a branch and it cracked, both heads turned toward the sound — smoothly, together, like one animal wearing two bodies — and then turned back to the ridge.

"—diez, once, doce—"

"Hey." Tomás kept his voice level, foreman-level, the voice that moved rock and payroll alike. "Fidel. Look at me."

Fidel looked at him. That was somehow worse. His eyes came around and focused on Tomás's face and there was a man home inside them, Tomás would swear to this later, a tired man who wanted help, looking out through his own face like through a window painted shut. His mouth kept moving.

"—trece, catorce—"

"Stop counting." Tomás grabbed his shoulders. "Fidel. Stop. Count backwards, count sheep, count your mother's rosary, I don't care, just stop."

Fidel's mouth stopped. For one second his whole body sagged, and his hand came up and gripped Tomás's wrist with a strength that surprised them both, and he said, in a voice scraped down to the wood: "No puedo. Si me calla, oigo el otro." If I stop, I hear the other one.

Then his mouth started again. "—quince, dieciséis—"

Tomás shook him. He shouted at him. He slapped him, open-handed, twice across the face, hard enough to leave marks, and Fidel took it the way a wall takes rain. Rupe didn't react at all, which was its own horror; Rupe and Fidel had been friends since school, had fought each other and for each other, and now Rupe stood forty meters away counting in perfect unison with a man being struck, and the unison never wavered. It was as if they weren't two counters but one counting distributed across two throats, and you could hurt either throat and the count went on.

Tomás tried dragging Fidel. He got him ten meters downhill, boots furrowing the pine needles, before his knees simply quit — not pain, exactly, but a deep structural refusal, as if some part of him understood that pulling against this was like pulling against gravity or debt. He let go. Fidel walked back to his spot. Not staggered. Walked, unhurried, and resumed his exact position, feet finding the same two prints in the needles.

"—veintidós, veintitrés—"

Tomás sat down on a stone and watched them for a long time. The sun climbed. The light came down through the pines in dusty columns. The count went on, and here was the thing he noticed only after an hour of sitting there, the thing that finally made the cold come up through his boot soles into his teeth: they weren't counting up forever. They counted to a hundred, and then they started again at one. Over and over. A hundred numbers, then silence for the length of a held breath — and in that silence, both men flinched, both faces tightened, both mouths pressed shut as if holding a door closed with their lips — and then the count began again.

They were buying time. Tomás didn't know how he knew it, but he knew it the way you know a load is shifting before the crane groans. Whatever was in that corridor they'd all dreamed, whatever voice had walked through the village in the dark collecting sleepers, Fidel and Rupe were standing at the treeline doing the work of keeping it slow. And nobody had asked them to. Nobody had trained them. They were crewmen, diggers, men who measured their worth in cubic meters moved per day, and they were spending themselves like fuel on something no contract covered.

At noon he brought water. They wouldn't take it. Their mouths kept moving. By late afternoon their lips were cracked and bleeding and still moving. When Tomás tried to physically carry Rupe — Rupe was smaller, Rupe might come — Rupe's body went rigid as poured concrete, and the count stuttered for the first time, and in the stutter Tomás heard, faint and far up the mountain, a sound he refused to describe even to himself. So he set Rupe down gently, and apologized out loud to a man who couldn't hear him, and sat back on his stone.

It was full dark when he gave up. He built a small fire ten meters below them, close enough to watch, far enough that he told himself the light wouldn't reach whatever they were aimed at. He wrapped himself in a tarp and did not sleep. Around two in the morning, in the gap between one hundred and one, he heard them whisper — both at once, the same words:

"No llegamos a mil."

We won't reach a thousand.

* * *

He went to the village because there was nowhere else to go. That was the truth of it and he dressed the truth up as strategy on the walk down — need to talk to the mayor, need to pull the crew off the site, need to make calls — but by the time the first roofs of Vado Seco showed grey in the dawn, he'd stopped lying to himself. There was one person in this valley who might understand what was happening, and she wasn't the mayor, and she wasn't a priest, because the priest had driven up to the sanatorium two days ago, stood at the gate for eleven minutes by Tomás's watch, and driven back down without getting out of the car.

The archivist. Halloran. The quiet one who'd been haunting the village for years with a microphone and a canvas bag, who ate at the Ortuña table and spoke maybe forty words a day. Tomás had barely exchanged a sentence with them in three seasons of work. But he'd seen them at the sanatorium fence more than once, notebook out, watching the ninth floor — the floor that wasn't on any blueprint — with an expression Tomás now recognized, because he'd been wearing it himself for three days. The expression of a person measuring a thing they hoped was smaller than it was.

He found the house by asking at the bakery, and the baker's wife gave him directions with a look he couldn't read, a look that said *you too?*

Paloma Halloran answered the door already dressed, already holding a cup of coffee they clearly weren't drinking, and Tomás understood they'd been awake all night too. Up close they were younger than he'd assumed and older-looking than their age, with the particular worn quality of people who listen for a living.

"You're the foreman," Paloma said. Not a question. "From the excavation."

"Tomás Ferrer." He heard his own voice come out wrong, too loud for the small hallway, and lowered it. "I need to tell you something and I need you to not look at me like I'm drunk, because I'm not, I've had nothing, I've been afraid to."

Something moved behind Paloma's eyes — recognition, Tomás thought later, the look of someone hearing their own private weather forecast read aloud by a stranger.

"I've been dreaming about a corridor," Tomás said. "Every man on my crew has. Same corridor. Grey tile, doors on one side, and a voice counting. And two of my men are gone. Not gone gone. They're standing at the treeline above the site, both of them, facing the sanatorium, and they're counting. Out loud. In unison. To a hundred, over and over, all day, all night. I couldn't move them. I dragged Fidel ten meters and he walked back to the same spot like I was a draft under a door." His voice cracked on the last of it and he hated that, hated it, forty-six years old and his voice cracking in a stranger's hallway. "And in the gaps, when they hit a hundred, they whisper the same thing. *No llegamos a mil.* We won't reach a thousand. Señor — señora — Halloran. What is a thousand?"

For a long moment Paloma didn't answer. They stood aside, wordless, and gestured him into the kitchen, and Tomás saw the room properly for the first time: shelves of tapes, hundreds of them, labeled in a small precise hand, dates going back five years. A wall of voices. The whole kitchen hummed with the sense of stored sound, like a room where singing had soaked into the plaster.

Paloma set the cold coffee down and looked at their hands.

"The songs," they said quietly. "The folk songs. The ones nobody in this village will sing twice." They laughed, a short terrible sound with no humor in it. "I thought it was superstition. Sing a song twice, bad luck. I catalogued it. I wrote a paper section about it — *repetition taboo, function unclear.*" They looked up, and Tomás saw that they were as frightened as he was, and that their fear was older and better organized than his. "But it's not a taboo, is it. It's a rule. Like a load limit. You don't sing a song twice because each singing spends something, and the count—" They stopped. Started again. "Marisol Ortuña chalked a number on her door yesterday. Forty-one. The grandmother sang a whole song through, start to finish, for the first time in five years, and she wasn't singing it *to* anyone. She was counting with it."

"A thousand," Tomás said. "That's what my men keep saying. We won't reach a thousand."

"And your men are at the treeline buying numbers with their own mouths." Paloma stood up abruptly, paced to the tape shelves, stood there with their fingers resting on a spool as if it were a pulse. "Two strangers. Untrained. Nobody taught them the songs, nobody gave them the office — and they're doing it anyway, because the thing takes whoever it can get and the count doesn't care whose throat it borrows." They turned. "How long can they last?"

"They haven't had water since yesterday morning. Their lips are bleeding. They'll stand until their legs give out, and then—" Tomás couldn't finish it. He made himself. "And then I don't know what happens to the number they're carrying."

Paloma closed their eyes. When they opened them, something had settled in their face, some decision arriving like a stone reaching the bottom of a well.

"Then we have to relieve them," they said. "And we can't do it with two people, or ten. Tomás — the Ortuña family has been doing this alone for eighty years. Eighty years, one household, generation after generation, and they let me sit at their table and eat their food and I never once asked why the grandmother stopped mid-verse, why Marisol looks fifty years old at nineteen, why this village holds its breath every October." Their voice was shaking now, and they let it. "You came to confess. Good. Confess to the whole village. Because if my men — if your men — if any of us are going to stand at that treeline and count, then everyone is going to know what they're counting *for*. No more secrets bought with other people's lives. Starting with mine."

Tomás frowned. "Yours?"

Paloma lifted one tape from the shelf, turned it over, and read the label aloud — a date, a singer's name, a song title — and their hand was steady but their face was not.

"I recorded them," they said. "Five years of them. Every song this village used to hold the line, and I put them in boxes and wrote papers about them, and I think — God help me, I think every time I played one back, something *heard* it. Something learned the words." They set the tape down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something that might be armed. "So yes. I'm coming to the treeline. And then I'm going to the concilio, and I'm telling them what I did, and you're going to stand next to me while I do it, because you dug up the door and I thinned the walls and between us we've spent enough of this village's blood to owe it the truth."

Outside, far up the mountain, faint as a memory of a memory, the wind carried something down through the pines. Tomás heard it and went cold, and saw by Paloma's face that they heard it too.

Two voices. In unison. Patient as dripping water.

"—cuatro, cinco, seis—"
