## 4. Twelve Counts

The bell rang eleven times.

Freya counted them because counting was what he did—counting was the whole architecture of his sanity, the scaffolding he had built over a lifetime of other people's half-remembered songs. He lay in the narrow bed above the Fox and Hounds with his notebook open on his chest and the pencil already in his hand, and when the first toll came up through the floorboards like a fist knocking on the underside of the world, he wrote *1* in the margin of the page where he'd recorded the parish inventory: *Great bell, cast 1743, removed and melted down, 1911.*

He wrote *2*. He wrote *3*.

The sound itself was wrong in a way he could not yet name, though by the fourth toll he was trying. It was not the bronze voice a bell should have. Melted bells do not ring again; that is the entire point of melting them down, that is the mercy of it—the metal goes into new things, kettles, ploughshares, a war memorial's railings, and whatever soul the casting held is dispersed past any hope of recall. This bell had no right to a voice at all, and so its voice came out thin and flattened, like a note sung through closed lips, like a woman humming to herself in another room because she does not wish to wake the child but cannot stop singing.

He wrote *5*. His hand was steady. He made himself notice that his hand was steady, and took comfort from it, the way a man falling takes comfort from the neatness of his own shoelaces.

Between the tolls there were intervals, and the intervals were worse than the ringing. Each one lasted long enough for him to think: *it has stopped, it was the wind, it was a lorry on the ridge road, it was my own pulse in my ear against the pillow*—and then the next toll would come, patient as tide. The intervals lengthened slightly as the count went on. He timed them without deciding to. Seven seconds. Nine. Eleven. As if the bell were not merely sounding but *considering*, choosing each stroke with care, laying them down one at a time like coins on a table.

At *8* he got up and went to the window.

Hollowmere lay below him in the dark, forty roofs and the silver thread of the mere and the black hump of the church tower against a sky that had no stars in it tonight, though the evening had been clear. The village showed not one light. Not a lamp behind a curtain, not a candle, not the blue flicker of a television. Forty houses full of people who must all be hearing exactly what he was hearing, and every one of them lying in the dark pretending not to, the way children lie still when they hear something on the stairs, believing that stillness is safety, that what walks at night only takes the ones who move.

He wrote *9*.

And here is the thing he would not write down, the thing he kept off the page the way you keep your hand off a hot stove: between the ninth toll and the tenth, in that long considering silence, he heard—or believed he heard, and the distinction felt suddenly, desperately important—a second sound underneath the silence. Faint. Rhythmic. A slow creaking, weight on old wood, as if something in the tower were leaning back and forward, back and forward, keeping time for the bell the way a singer rocks to her own song.

He wrote *10*.

The eleventh toll was the loudest. It did not fade the way the others had faded, out and down into the valley; it stopped. Cut clean, mid-resonance, the way a throat closes. And then Hollowmere was silent with a silence so total that Freya could hear the blood moving in his own ears and the small dry tick of the radiator cooling, and he stood at the window until the sky went grey at its eastern edge, waiting for a twelfth toll that did not come, telling himself that this proved something, that an absence of sound was evidence, that evidence was a rope and if he held on tight enough it would hold him.

It was nearly four before he slept, and he dreamed of nothing, which frightened him more than a dream would have.

* * *

The village woke changed.

He understood this gradually, the way you understand a room has been searched—not by any single thing but by the accumulation of small wrongnesses. Mrs. Prowse at the Fox and Hounds set down his breakfast without a word and stood looking out the window at nothing, her hands flat on the tablecloth as if steadying it. The bread was stale, which it had not been the day before; she had baked fresh every morning since he arrived, and now there was only yesterday's loaf, cut thick, as though someone had lost interest in the future tense.

Outside, the livestock was still. Not dead—still. The sheep in the fold along the lane stood in a cluster with their heads all turned toward the church tower, motionless, and when Freya stopped at the gate they did not startle or bleat or do any of the nervous animal things sheep do. They watched the tower the way the villagers watched their doorsteps. Down at the mere, the ducks sat on the water in a perfect, evenly spaced line, facing the shore, and did not move when he walked the length of the bank. He threw a stone. It landed a foot from the nearest duck. The duck blinked. That was all.

The well behind the Tull cottage tasted of iron.

He had gone there because going places and writing things down was the discipline that kept the night at arm's length, and Sian Tull—Marta's daughter, a broad, tired woman in her fifties with her mother's dark eyes and none of her warmth—was drawing water when he arrived. She filled his cup without being asked and he drank, and the taste hit the back of his throat like a coin held too long in the mouth: cold, mineral, and beneath it something red and old, the taste of rust, of blood dried into cloth.

"It's the pipes," Sian said, before he said anything.

"Is it?"

"No." She took the cup back from him and poured the rest onto the ground, carefully, as if returning something borrowed. "It's been like that since dawn. Every well on the street. Mr. Aldous says his tastes of iron, and the Hendersons', and ours." She looked at him then, directly, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "You heard it too."

"Eleven times," Freya said. "I counted."

"I know you counted." She almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, exhausted and knowing. "You're the sort that counts. My mother says—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "My mother sang last night. In her sleep. All the way through, both verses, first time in fifty years. And then she woke up and asked me why Ivo hadn't come home yet."

The name went through him like a splinter finding its depth. *Ivo.* He turned it over in his mind and found nothing, no purchase, no meaning—and yet some part of him flinched at it, the way the body flinches at a sound before the mind identifies it.

"Who's Ivo?" he asked.

Sian looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face made her decide something, and what she decided was to say nothing at all. She carried the bucket inside and shut the door, and through the wood he heard the bar come down.

* * *

Edith Parr's house stood at the end of Church Lane, the last before the lych-gate, close enough to the tower that the shadow of it crossed her garden by mid-afternoon. She was the vicar's widow, eighty-some years old, a small upright woman who received him in a parlour that smelled of lavender and dust and, faintly, underneath everything, of iron.

"You'll want to know about the bell," she said, before he had finished sitting down.

"If you're willing to tell me."

"Willing." She considered the word as though tasting it. "I've been willing for fifty years, Mr. Crane. Willingness was never the difficulty. The difficulty is that saying a thing makes it real, and we have all of us in this village spent our lives agreeing not to make certain things real." She folded her hands in her lap. They were very still. Everything about Edith Parr was very still, in the way of people who have practised stillness until it became a kind of armour. "You counted eleven."

"Yes."

"And it stopped."

"Yes. At eleven."

She nodded slowly, and he saw—for the first time, and it chilled him more than the tower had—that she was afraid. Not of him. Of the answer she was about to give, perhaps, or of the question he was going to ask next, or simply of the fact that after fifty years the arithmetic had changed.

"The bell doesn't ring, Mr. Crane. You'll have read the records; I expect that's why you walked up there the other night with your torch, very sensible, checking the tower for ropes and machinery. There is no bell. There hasn't been since 1911. What rings is the *tolling*—the memory of the bell, if you like, or the debt of it. Once a year. On the anniversary." Her eyes came up to his. "One stroke. For what was taken."

"What was taken?"

"A child." She said it plainly, the way you'd say *a roof tile* or *a hymnal*, and the plainness was the horror of it. "In 1974. The village took a child, and lied about it, and buried a lie in place of the child, and ever since, once a year, the bell has tolled once—to remind us. To keep the count. One stroke, one theft, one year marked. We learned to live with one stroke. You can live with one stroke, Mr. Crane, the way you live with a bad tooth. It aches on the anniversary and then it stops."

"But last night—"

"Last night it rang eleven times." Her hands tightened in her lap, knuckles rising pale under the skin. "Eleven strokes. Counting. Not remembering—*counting*. Do you understand the difference? A memorial counts nothing. A clock counts. Last night the bell began to behave like a clock, and a clock, Mr. Crane, is always counting toward something."

"Toward twelve."

The words left his mouth before he decided to say them, and the moment they were spoken the parlour seemed to contract around them, the lavender smell curdling, the light through the lace curtains going thin and grey as pond water. Edith Parr did not answer. She looked at him with an expression he had seen once before, years ago, on the face of an old woman in a Hebridean village who had just realized that the stranger asking about the drowning song was the grandson of the drowned.

"Twelve," she agreed at last, barely above a whisper. "And then it will have finished counting, and whatever it has been counting toward will be due."

Freya took out his notebook. It was reflex, pure reflex, the scholar's hand reaching for the scholar's instrument, and he saw Edith watch the movement with something close to pity.

"You're writing this down."

"I'm a folklorist. Recording is what I do."

"Recording." She weighed the word. "We recorded it too, in our way. In the register. In the minutes of the vestry. In the grave that isn't a grave. Paper keeps things, Mr. Crane, but paper doesn't keep them *honestly* unless the hand that writes is honest, and ours wasn't. Your notebook will hold the truth of it fine. But the truth won't stay held unless somebody is willing to carry it out loud, in daylight, in front of the whole village—and nobody here has been willing for fifty years." She rose, stiffly, one hand on the mantelpiece, and looked out the window toward the tower. "You should go home. Back to wherever you came from. Finish your archive somewhere the wells taste of water."

"Mrs. Parr—if the bell is counting toward something, then someone knows what. Someone always knows. That's how these things work; I've documented a hundred covenant stories, and in every one—"

"In every one," she said, turning, "there is a debt. Yes. I know the shape of the story better than you do, Mr. Crane, because I am *in* it, and have been since before you were born." Something moved across her face then, quick and unreadable, gone before he could name it. "Go and ask your questions. Write in your book. But hear me: the bell rang eleven times, and each stroke was a year, and the years are running out faster than your ink."

* * *

That evening he sat in his room at the inn and laid the evidence out on the bedspread like a surgeon laying out instruments, because that was the ritual, that was the rope: facts, in order, weighted and examined.

Item: a melted bell, rung. Eleven strokes, timed intervals, cut off mid-resonance. Item: livestock unstartled, oriented toward the tower. Item: five wells tasting of iron since dawn. Item: Marta Tull singing two verses in her sleep for the first time in fifty years, and waking to ask after a boy named Ivo. Item: Edith Parr, who used the word *child* and the year *1974* and the phrase *a grave that isn't a grave*, and who looked at him, twice, as though comparing his face to some other face she remembered.

He wrote all of it down. His handwriting was neat. His hand was steady.

And beneath the steadiness, in the place where he actually lived, panic was rising like floodwater, slow and cold and patient, filling rooms he had thought were sealed. Because here was the fact he could not lay out on the counterpane with the others: the fear in this village was not the fear of a haunting. He knew hauntings; he had sat up in a dozen haunted houses with a tape recorder and found, nine times out of ten, a draft and a guilty conscience. This was different. This was the fear of people who owed something. The sheep faced the tower the way debtors face a door when footsteps come up the path. The wells tasted of iron the way a mouth tastes of blood when you've bitten through your cheek in the night and are trying not to swallow.

They were not afraid of the bell. They were afraid of what the bell had come to collect.

And underneath even that—deepest, coldest, in the flooded cellar of himself—was the thing he had noticed at Edith Parr's table and refused, with the whole force of his trained and reasonable mind, to look at directly: the way she had said *since before you were born*, and paused. The way Sian Tull had looked at him when he asked who Ivo was, and chosen silence. The way the name had scratched at something in him with no face and no memory attached, a splinter working deeper, and the sense—he could not call it knowledge, he would not call it knowledge—that the bell had not begun counting when he arrived in Hollowmere.

It had begun counting when he was born.

He closed the notebook. Outside, the last light was failing, and the tower stood black against it, and the village below was lighting its lamps one by one, forty small flames against the coming dark, and Freya Crane sat among his ordered facts and listened to the silence where the twelfth toll should have been, and understood at last that the silence was not an absence.

It was a held breath.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he would go to the county archive. Tomorrow he would find the register, the records, the paper trail—something solid, something with a date and a signature, something he could hold.

He did not sleep. And sometime before dawn, in the grey hour when the birds should have started and didn't, he heard—faint, from the direction of the church—a single sound. Not a toll.

A hum. Thin and tuneless and terribly patient, like a woman in another room, singing to a child who has not yet come home.
