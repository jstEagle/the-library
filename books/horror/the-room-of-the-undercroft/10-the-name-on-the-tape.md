## 10. The Name on the Tape

The reel-to-reel machine had come with him from London in a padded case he treated, in those days, like a reliquary. It was his mother's before it was his—Ruth Crane's old Uher, its leather scuffed to the colour of weak tea, its reels replaced twice but its microphone still carrying her initials scratched into the casing: *R.C., 1963.* He had grown up falling asleep to the sound of it turning, her voice coming out of the dark of the sitting room as she edited field tapes late into the night, singing snatches of songs she'd collected from women whose names he'd known only as entries in her card catalogue. *Mrs. Aldous, Suffolk. The Barley Straw. Take two.*

He set it on the desk in his room at the Lamb and Flag and did not touch it for an hour.

Outside, Hollowmere lay under a light that had no business existing at four in the afternoon—a grey so even it seemed painted, the hills beyond the church tower dissolved into it, the rooks gone quiet in the yew. Since the eleventh count—no. Since the bell had rung eleven times and stopped, since the wells had turned to iron and the cattle stood in their fields like furniture, Freya had kept to a discipline: make lists, verify sources, do the work. A folklorist's catechism. Evidence first. Feeling later, if there was time.

But now he sat looking at the box Ruth had sent him, three days after his phone call, wrapped in brown paper and tied with the kind of string she used for parcels of jam.

*The 1974 material,* she had written on the enclosed card, in handwriting that trembled worse than it had at Christmas. *You asked. Here is all of it. I never listened to these again after that summer. I don't imagine you'll understand why until you have. — M.*

*M* for Mother. She had signed herself that way for fifty years, and he had never once thought to wonder what she was mother *of.*

He cut the string. Inside were seven tape boxes, each labelled in her small upright hand, each dated July or August of 1974. *Hollowmere: carols (off-season), Mrs. Tull.* *Hollowmere: church bells, discussion w/ Rev. Parr.* *Hollowmere: Nell Marsh, servant, age 19 — songs.*

He lifted that last one out and held it a long moment. The label had been written over—there was something beneath it, erased with such force the cardboard had gone furry, and over the erasure, in fresh ink, in the same hand but pressed harder, slower:

*Ivo M. — placed, with the village's consent.*

He read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves. *Placed.* As one places a vase. As one places a stone in a wall.

His hands were steady. That was the strange thing; he noted it the way he noted everything, the archivist's reflex, cataloguing even his own body's failures to respond. Steady hands. Dry mouth. A ringing in the ears that might have been the tape machine's idle hum or might have been something else entirely, something that had been ringing across a valley every midnight for a week.

He threaded the reel.

* * *

First came the hiss, that oceanic sound, the sound of half a century waiting. Then a click, and Ruth's voice—young Ruth, thirty years old, brisk and warm, the voice that had read him bedtime stories and corrected his Latin—filled the little room.

*"Testing. Hollowmere, the vicarage kitchen, the twenty-ninth of July, nineteen seventy-four. Present: myself, and—"*

A pause. A rustle. Then another voice, young and shy, with the flattened vowels of the valley: *"Nell Marsh."*

*"Thank you for agreeing to talk to me, Nell. Marta Tull says you know more songs than anyone your age in the parish."*

A laugh, quickly smothered, embarrassed. *"Marta says that about everyone she likes."*

*"And does she like you?"*

*"She has me round Tuesdays to help with her washing. She sings while we work. That's how I learned most of mine."*

Freya closed his eyes. He could see the kitchen—the vicarage kitchen, though he'd never been inside it; he could see any kitchen, because he had spent his childhood in a hundred of them, sitting on stools with his legs swinging while his mother's microphone drank the voices of strangers. He knew this recording the way he knew nothing else in the world. Not this tape. This *kind* of tape. The genre of his own upbringing.

*"Tell me about the lullaby,"* young Ruth said. *"Marta mentioned there's one nobody will sing twice."*

The pause that followed lasted long enough that Freya opened his eyes and checked the counter, thinking the tape had jammed. It hadn't. Nell Marsh was simply not answering.

*"Nell?"*

*"It's not a lullaby exactly,"* she said at last. *"It's a promise. You sing it to a child so the child knows you'll come back for them. No matter what. Even past—"*

Another silence.

*"Even past what?"*

*"Even past dying,"* Nell Marsh said, very quietly. And then, brighter, forced: *"I'll sing it once. But not twice. Never ask me twice."*

And she sang.

Freya had heard the fragment Marta Tull had begun in the taproom, that scrap of melody cut off mid-verse by her neighbours' sudden, terrible courtesy. This was the whole of it—or the whole of what Nell would give—and it was worse than he had imagined, because it was beautiful. A simple rising line, a tune like water finding its way downhill, patient, certain of arrival. The words were in the valley dialect, half of them, and he caught them the way you catch things said in dreams:

*Sleep, my love, and count the stones,*
*One for every step I come.*
*Though they tell you I am gone,*
*Count them all, and I'll be home.*

Beneath her voice, faintly, there was another sound. At first he took it for the tape hiss changing character, the hiss breathing. Then it resolved, and his whole body went cold from the scalp down.

A baby. Crying. Newborn crying—that thin, furious, unmistakable complaint, muffled as if through cloth, as if through a door, somewhere off-microphone in the vicarage kitchen, the twenty-ninth of July, nineteen seventy-four.

Nell sang on. She did not falter. But she must have heard it—she must have heard her own child crying behind her—and she sang the lullaby to the microphone instead, the promise-song, the *I will come back for you*, while the child it was promised to cried ten feet away and she could not go to him, because she was making a record, because this was the price, because—

The song ended. The baby's crying continued a moment, then hushed, as if someone had picked it up.

*"Lovely,"* said young Ruth Crane, and her voice was gentle and professional and completely, perfectly composed. *"Now, Nell. Tell me about the christening that wasn't."*

* * *

Freya stopped the tape.

He sat in the grey light with his hands flat on the desk, and the room seemed to be very far away from him, seen down a long corridor, and the corridor was lined with doors, and behind every door was a version of his life that was not the one he had lived.

He had asked himself, over the past week, in the rational hours, what the bell wanted. He had made lists. *Subject: bell, melted 1911, audible regardless. Subject: register page, razored. Subject: photograph, woman and bundle. Subject: name, Ivo Marsh, scratched inside tower at a height suggesting a child or a kneeling adult.* He had assembled the evidence the way he'd been trained, laid it out in rows, and refused, with real scholarly discipline, to draw the obvious conclusion, because the obvious conclusion required him to believe that a village had buried a lie in place of a baby, and that the visiting folklorist who had carried that baby away was the woman whose voice had sung him to sleep for eighteen years.

The tape turned slowly between reels. He had not rewound it. In the hiss he could almost hear the kitchen still, the kettle, the baby, the girl keeping her voice level.

*Placed, with the village's consent.*

He pressed play.

*"—the vicar told everyone the child was stillborn,"* Nell Marsh was saying. Her voice had changed; the shyness had burned off it, leaving something flat and hard, a voice like a scraped bone. *"There was no christening because there was no child, officially. My father believes it. My mother believes it. They stood at the funeral—"*

*"There was a funeral?"*

*"An empty coffin. The vicar said it was kinder that way. Kinder."* A sound that was not quite a laugh. *"He said the church couldn't survive a scandal, not after the last one. He said a girl in my position should think of the parish. So I thought of the parish. I thought of it every hour. And then a lady came."*

Silence on the tape. Freya realized he had stopped breathing.

*"What lady, Nell?"*

*"The one collecting songs. Like you. She'd been here in the spring. She came back in August and stayed three nights at the vicarage, and on the third night the vicar brought her up to see me, and she looked at my son—"*

The word *son* cracked down the middle.

*"—and she held him, and she said, 'I can give him a life.' Just like that. Like handing over a parcel. And the vicar nodded, and Marta Tull was in the corner and she had her head bowed like in church, and nobody asked me. Did you notice that? Nobody asked me. They talked about consent. The village consented. The vicar consented. The lady consented. I consented the way weather consents to rain."*

*"Nell,"* young Ruth said, and her composure had frayed at last, just at the edges, *"are you telling me the child is still here? Tonight?"*

*"He's upstairs,"* Nell Marsh said. *"He's asleep. He's nine days old and he has never once been outside."*

The tape ran on in hiss for several seconds. When young Ruth spoke again, her voice had dropped, almost inaudible beneath the surface noise, and Freya leaned into the machine like a man leaning into wind.

*"What was the lullaby for, Nell? Why did you want me to record it?"*

*"Because she's taking him tomorrow,"* Nell said. *"And I can't go where he's going. But the song goes anywhere. It's a promise, isn't it? I told you. You sing it so the child knows you'll come back. Even past dying."* A breath, wet, steadied. *"So I'm putting it in your machine, miss. Put it wherever machines go. Because if I can't follow him alive, I'll follow him the other way, and I want the song to get there first."*

The tape ended. The reels clicked, spun, clicked.

* * *

Freya did not remember standing up. He was standing at the window with the curtain clutched in one fist, looking down at the empty street of Hollowmere, and his reflection in the glass was a pale smear over the rooftops, over the tower, and he could not make his eyes focus on his own face.

Nine days old on the twenty-ninth of July, 1974. Taken the thirtieth.

Ruth Crane had visited Hollowmere in the spring of 1974. He knew this the way he knew his own birthday, because it was in her papers, in the archive he had inherited along with the Uher and the card catalogue and the whole apparatus of her life's work: *Hollowmere, preliminary survey, April 1974.* He had read that entry a hundred times as a student, proud of her, proud of the thoroughness. He had never once asked why a folklorist doing a preliminary survey in April would return in August for three nights at the vicarage and leave with nothing in her published notes—not a song, not a name, not a single line about the valley. Fifty years of scholarship, and Hollowmere appeared in it only as a blank. He had assumed the material was lost.

It wasn't lost. It was *buried.* She had buried it the way the village had buried the empty coffin—carefully, respectfully, with a marker over the place where the truth should have been.

*Ivo M. — placed, with the village's consent.*

He turned back to the desk. His own face looked up at him from the small mirror above the washstand, and for the first time in his life he examined it as evidence. Dark hair—he was the only dark-haired Crane; Ruth was fair as ash. Brown eyes; hers were blue. A cleft in the chin that she used to laugh about, saying it must have skipped a generation, skipping from whom? From whom, Mother? And his hands—his long, narrow hands, which he had always privately considered too fine for a farmer's stock, because he had never known what stock he came from, because the story had always been vague, a cousin's orphaned boy, a family arrangement, *don't fuss, Freya, you're mine and that's enough*—

Enough. It had been enough. It had been everything, and it had been a lie with a lullaby folded inside it.

"Ivo," he said aloud.

The word fell into the room and did not stop. It went through the floorboards, down through the foundations of the Lamb and Flag, down into the cold earth of the valley, and everywhere it passed, things went still. The fire in the grate sank. The pipe under the sink, which had been ticking all afternoon, ticked no longer. Outside, the rooks rose off the yew all at once, a black exhalation against the grey sky, and did not come down again.

"Ivo Marsh," he said, and his voice broke on the surname, because the surname was the proof, the surname was the razored page and the empty grave and the newborn crying under the hiss of a tape recorded nine days after he was born. "That's—I am—"

He could not finish the sentence. He tried the other direction instead, the scholar's direction, the list: *Born 20 July 1974, Hollowmere. Mother, Eleanor Marsh, d. 1975. Declared stillborn, falsely, by the Rev. Mr. Parr, for the protection of the church. Removed 30 July 1974 by R. Crane, folklorist, aged 30, with the consent of all parties except the one who mattered.* He got through it in a whisper, standing at the window, and when it was done he discovered he was crying without any of the usual machinery of grief—no sobbing, no contortion, just tears running steadily down his face as if a well had struck water somewhere behind his eyes and would not be capped.

*Nobody asked me,* Nell Marsh had said. *They talked about consent.*

And Ruth—Ruth, who had loved him, who had taught him to read music off a hymnal upside down, who had sat up all night with him when he had scarlet fever, whose death he had already begun to dread with the specific terror of an only child—Ruth had walked into a vicarage kitchen and held a nine-day-old boy and said, *I can give him a life,* and had meant it kindly, had meant it as salvation, and had carried him away in the same padded cases she carried her tapes, and had raised him on songs, and had never, not once in fifty years, sung him the one that mattered.

Or had she? He cast back through his childhood, hunting. Lullabies—she had sung him dozens, from six counties, a repertoire. Had one of them been the counting-stones song? He reached for it and found only a shape, a feeling, a rising line like water going home, heard from a great distance through sleep. Perhaps. Perhaps she had sung it once, early, guiltily, and stopped. Or perhaps the song had waited in him all this time the way the bell waited in the roofless tower, melted down and ringing anyway.

The light was failing. He lit the lamp, and in its small yellow circle the seven tape boxes sat in their row, and he understood suddenly why she had sent them—why, after fifty years of silence, after his phone call and her single sentence (*Don't go back to Hollowmere, Freya*), she had wrapped them in brown paper and good string and posted them into the valley she had begged him to leave. She hadn't sent him evidence. She had sent him a confession, the only kind she was still able to make. *Here is all of it. I never listened to these again after that summer.*

Because she couldn't bear to hear the baby crying. Because she had heard it every day since, in every silence, in every room.

He sat down and took out a fresh cassette, the archive's blank stock, and loaded it, and set the microphone on the desk facing him, and pressed record. The red eye opened. The hiss began—the same oceanic sound, the same waiting.

"This is Freya Crane," he said, and stopped, because the name would not come out whole. He tried again. "This is—" His voice sounded young on the machine, younger than Nell Marsh's had, younger than the room. "Field note. Hollowmere. The twenty-ninth of—" He didn't know today's date. He genuinely did not know it. "Present: myself."

The tape turned. Somewhere below the hearing of the house, below the hearing of the world, something counted.

"My name," he said finally, "is Ivo Marsh. Born the twentieth of July, nineteen seventy-four, in this parish. Declared dead the same day. Alive ever since, apparently, without anyone's permission—including mine."

He let the machine take it. Let it go wherever machines go. Let it get there first.

When he switched off the lamp much later, the last thing he saw was the row of boxes, and the label on the seventh, and beneath his window the street of Hollowmere lying silent under a sky that had begun, very faintly, very far off, to carry a sound like a struck bell remembering itself—one toll, soft as a pulse, then another, then a third, though the night was hours yet from midnight, and the tower had no bell.

Three. It was counting toward him.
