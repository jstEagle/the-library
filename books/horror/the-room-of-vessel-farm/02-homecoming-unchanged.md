## 2. Homecoming, Unchanged

The taxi wouldn't drive all the way up the drive.

"Road's washed out past the gate," the driver said, though the road looked fine to Casper—gravel, rutted, ordinary. He set her suitcase on the shoulder and was gone before she'd finished paying, tires spitting stone, and she stood alone in the cold March air with the farm rising in front of her like a sentence she'd been avoiding for twenty years.

The house hadn't fallen down. That was the first wrong thing. She'd expected ruin, expected the sag of abandonment, the soft collapse of a place no one loved. Instead the roofline was true, the porch boards unwarped, the white paint—faded, yes, but sound—clinging to the clapboard in long unpeeled sheets. Someone had been maintaining it. Someone had been keeping it.

She told herself: Theo. Theo lived here. Theo and Mara had kept it up, and after Mara died Theo had simply gone on keeping it up, because that was the kind of man he was, the kind who kept things. It was a reasonable explanation. She held it in front of her like a lantern and walked up the drive.

The gate didn't squeak. She noticed that too. Twenty years of weather and the hinge swung silent as a held breath, and she stood there a moment with her hand on the cold iron, listening to the nothing it made.

*Catalog it,* she told herself. *Name it. Move through it.* This was the discipline she'd built her whole life on, brick by brick, in therapist's offices and rented apartments and the careful, well-lit life she'd made three hundred miles south of here: the world is explainable, and if you explain it fast enough, it can't get its teeth in you.

The gate was oiled. Theo oiled the gate. Fine.

The yard was the second wrong thing. Not overgrown—she'd braced for that, for the tangle of dead bramble and knee-high grass that grief leaves behind—but mowed, or recently so, the grass short and pale and lying flat as if something had pressed it down. The big maple her father had hung a rope swing from was gone, cut off at the stump, and the stump was smooth. Not rotted. *Sanded.* She crouched and touched it before she could stop herself, and the wood was warm.

March. Shade. Warm as a sleeping animal.

She stood up fast and wiped her hand on her coat and did not catalog that one. Some things you catalog and some things you walk past, and she had gotten very good at knowing the difference, and the difference, she understood with a cold settling in her chest, was that she'd been practicing this her entire adult life.

* * *

Theo opened the door before she knocked.

He was grayer than the last time she'd seen him, at the funeral, thinner, with the look of a man who has been awake for a very long time in a room where the light is wrong. But his smile was warm, and his voice, when he said "Casper," was gentle, and she clung to that.

"You shouldn't have taken a cab," he said. "I would have come for you."

"The road was fine."

"It washes out," he said, "when it wants to."

She waited for him to laugh. He didn't. He took her suitcase instead, and his hand brushed hers on the handle, and his fingers were cold, and she thought: *he's been in the cellar.* Then she thought: *it's March, everyone's hands are cold,* and she was doing it again, the fast explaining, the bricklaying, and she made herself stop, made herself look at him plainly.

He was watching her the way you watch a kettle.

Not rudely. Nothing so obvious. He stood aside to let her in, and then he stood there, and his eyes went from her face to her hands to her coat, unhurried, thorough, the way you'd check someone for injuries—or for changes. When she caught him at it he didn't look away, exactly. He just smiled a little more.

"You look like her," he said.

"Mara?"

"Wren."

The name went through her like a needle finding a nerve she'd spent two decades numbing. She heard herself say, "Everyone says that," which was a lie, because no one had said it in twenty years, because she had left and built a life where no one knew there had ever been a Wren at all.

"Tea?" Theo said. "I put the kettle on when I saw the cab turn off the county road."

"You couldn't have seen that from here."

"No," he agreed, pleasantly. "But I put it on anyway."

* * *

The kitchen was warm. That was the third wrong thing, and by now Casper was counting them whether she wanted to or not, because the discipline of denial, she was discovering, had a failure mode: it worked right up until the explanations ran out, and then all you had left was the list.

Warm kitchen. Oiled gate. Sanded stump. Mowed yard. A man who put the kettle on for cabs he couldn't see.

She sat at the table where they'd eaten a thousand childhood suppers and wrapped her hands around a mug he gave her, and Theo sat across from her and did not drink his own tea, and said nothing, and the silence had a texture to it, a held quality, like the house was listening through him.

"I'll only need a few weeks," she said. "Probate, the estate sale, whatever the lawyer says. I can stay at a motel in town if—"

"You'll stay here."

"Theo."

"It's your house," he said. "It's where you're meant to be." He said it simply, without emphasis, and that was what made her spine prickle—the *meant*, sitting there in the sentence like a stone in a shoe.

"I'll take my old room, then," she said, to fill the space. "If it's not full of boxes."

Something moved across Theo's face. She'd have called it a flinch, except it was slower than a flinch, more like the shadow of a cloud passing over a field.

"Your room's ready," he said.

* * *

He carried her suitcase up the stairs himself, though she offered twice, and set it down at the end of the hall, and then he stood with his hand on the doorknob of her childhood bedroom and did not turn it, and looked at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere.

"It's been kept," he said. "I want you to know that before you go in. I didn't keep it. I want that understood. Mara didn't either. It just—" he turned the knob— "stayed."

The door swung open and Casper's breath went out of her.

Her bedroom was exactly as she'd left it.

Not approximately. Not the way a room looks when people have avoided it for twenty years—she knew that look, the gray felt of dust, the dead air, the way fabric fades and paper yellows. This room had *none* of it. The posters on the wall were unfaded, their colors bright as the day she'd pinned them up at sixteen: a band she'd loved, a map of the constellations, a postcard of the ocean. Her desk held her old lamp with the green glass shade, and beside it a stack of paperback novels she'd abandoned mid-read, and she could see, from the doorway, the folded gum wrapper marking her page. The closet door stood open a few inches. Her winter coat hung inside. Her boots stood under it, laces tucked in, exactly the way she'd been taught to store them.

The bed was turned down.

The covers folded back in a neat triangle, pillow plumped, the sheet beneath it smooth and pale and—she stepped closer, hating herself, unable not to—*warm-looking*. Not rumpled. Not slept in. Turned down the way a bed is turned down in a hotel that is expecting you. Turned down by someone who knew you were coming, who had known for a while, who had perhaps known since the night you left.

She touched the pillow. The cotton was cool, but only just—cool the way a thing is cool that was warm recently and is now politely pretending otherwise.

"Theo," she said, and her voice came out strange, "who sleeps in this room?"

"No one." He was standing in the hall, not crossing the threshold. She noticed that. Twenty years and he wouldn't cross it, and she filed that away with the gate and the stump. "No one sleeps in this room. It just stays ready."

"Rooms don't stay ready."

"This one does." He said it without drama, the way you'd say *the tap drips* or *the porch light burns out*. A fact of the house. "I'd have burned the whole thing down years ago if it were up to me," he added, and then seemed surprised he'd said it, and looked at the floor.

"Then why—"

"Ida." He said the name like a door closing. "Ida's mother is buried in the churchyard and Ida was born in the front bedroom and Ida knows nothing, Casper, and I have spent nine years making sure of it. So the house stays, and we stay, and you—" he looked at her then, fully, and she saw the exhaustion under the warmth, deep as a well— "you being here is either the best thing that's happened to this family in twenty years or the worst, and I honestly don't know which, and I'd thank you not to ask me again which, because I'll only say I don't know, because it's true."

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You were going to." He almost smiled. "You have your mother's way of asking questions without asking them. She did it her whole life. Right up until the ledgers."

He stopped. Casper felt the word land between them like something dropped from a height.

"What ledgers?"

"Nothing." He stepped back from the doorway. "Supper's at six. Ida will want to meet you properly—she's been asking about you since the funeral. Since before the funeral, actually, which I didn't love." He was retreating down the hall as he talked, and the hallway seemed to take him, the light at its far end dimmer than it should have been at four in the afternoon. "There's a lock on your door," he called back. "Use it."

* * *

She didn't unpack right away. She stood in the middle of the room that had waited for her and turned in a slow circle, and the discipline came apart in her hands like wet paper.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she had buried so deep she'd nearly lost it herself: she had always known the room would be like this. In twenty years of rented apartments and storage units and one brief, disastrous marriage, she had never fully unpacked a single box. Her partners had called her transient, commitment-averse, afraid. None of them understood. You don't unpack when you know, somewhere below the level of knowing, that a room is already kept for you somewhere else, that somewhere a bed is turned down, that somewhere a lamp with a green glass shade sits on a desk beside a paperback with a folded gum wrapper in it, and the house that holds all of it is patient, and the house does not fade, and the house does not forget, and one day the road washes out behind you whether or not there has been any rain.

She sat down on the bed. The mattress received her without a sound.

On the nightstand—she hadn't noticed it before, and she was certain it hadn't been there when she looked from the doorway—sat a glass of water. Full. Clear. Beads of condensation just beginning on its sides.

She had not been in the room five minutes. Theo had not crossed the threshold.

Casper looked at the water for a long time. Then she did the thing she had trained herself to do for twenty years, the reflex that had kept her functional, employed, insured, alive: she reached for the explanation. Condensation meant the glass had been there a while. She'd simply missed it. Rooms hold objects; eyes skip them; memory is a bad witness.

The explanation fit. It fit perfectly.

She drank the water anyway. It tasted like cold iron and, underneath, faintly, like the cellar.

She was setting the glass down when she heard, through the floor, faint and rhythmic and unmistakable, a sound she had spent half her life pretending she'd never heard: someone, something, *counting*.
