## 3. Noises Have Explanations

The house made its first sound at 11:47 p.m., and Casper knew the time because she was awake to hear it, and she was awake because she had never once slept well in this house, not even as a child, and some part of her had known that coming back would mean returning to that too—the lying in the dark with her ears pricked like a dog's, waiting for the house to say what it always used to say.

What it said, at 11:47, was a footstep.

One footstep. Directly overhead.

Casper lay in her childhood bed—her childhood bed, which someone had made up for her with clean sheets and a turned-down corner, which no one should have been able to make up because no one lived here—and stared at the ceiling and performed the arithmetic she had been performing since she was old enough to know that arithmetic could save you.

This is a single-story farmhouse. She had walked its whole footprint that afternoon, measuring it without meaning to, the way you measure a cage. Kitchen, pantry, living room, study, two bedrooms, one bath. The attic above was crawlspace only; she'd seen the hatch in the hall ceiling, painted shut, the paint thick and yellowed like an old scar. There was no second floor. There was no floor above her ceiling except boards and insulation and, above that, shingles and sky.

Therefore: no footsteps.

And yet the footstep came again, at 11:49, precisely two minutes later, a soft deliberate compression of wood, the sound of a heel settling into grain, and then—this was the part her body objected to, this was the part that raised the hair on her forearms in the dark—a second footstep, following the first, moving from the east end of the house toward the west, passing directly over her bed, pausing there, as if the walker had stopped to look down through the plaster at the woman lying beneath it.

Cats, said the rational voice in her head, the voice she had spent twenty years training like an athlete trains a muscle. Raccoons. Squirrels in the soffits. Thermal expansion—old houses tick and groan at night when the temperature drops, everyone knows that, everyone who has ever owned an old house knows that the sounds are nothing, are plumbing, are wind, are—

The footstep resumed. It crossed the rest of the ceiling unhurried, reached the west wall, and stopped.

It did not come back.

Casper lay there until three in the morning doing breathing exercises she'd learned from a therapist in Portland whose name she could no longer remember, and at some point she must have slept, because she woke at dawn with the journal open on her chest and no memory of opening it.

* * *

She had kept journals since she was eleven years old. This was not a personality quirk; it was a load-bearing wall. Her therapist in Portland—it came back to her now, Dr. Amara Osei, a small precise woman with reading glasses on a beaded chain—had called it "externalizing the witness." *When your own mind can't be trusted to tell you what happened,* Osei had said, in the careful voice people used with Casper once they understood the shape of her history, *you put the record somewhere outside yourself. Paper doesn't flinch.*

Paper didn't flinch. Paper also didn't lie, didn't round off the edges of memory, didn't quietly agree with whatever story Casper needed to survive the week. Twenty years of notebooks filled a trunk in her storage unit in Seattle: dated, cross-referenced, boring in the way that truth is boring when you work hard enough at it. Every strange thing that had ever happened to her had been entered into a journal within twenty-four hours, followed by a numbered list of mundane explanations, followed by the explanation she chose. It was a discipline. It was, if she was honest, a religion. Denial wasn't passive for Casper Winterbourne. Denial was a practice, daily, like scales for a pianist.

So on the first morning back at Vessel Farm, she sat at the kitchen table with coffee going cold beside her and opened a fresh notebook to the first page, and wrote:

*Oct 3. Night one. Sounds: (1) Footsteps overhead, approx. 11:45–11:55 p.m., four to six distinct steps, east to west, terminating at NW corner. No return passage. (2) Low rhythmic sound from below floor level, beginning approx. 2:10 a.m., continuing—estimate—forty minutes. Patterned. Counting-like.*

She looked at the word *counting-like* for a long time. Then she underlined it, because paper didn't flinch, and neither did she.

*Explanations:* she wrote. And began the list, the litany, the rosary.

*1. Animals. Raccoons are nocturnal, dexterous, and heavy enough to produce discrete footfall sounds on joists. Check: soffits, gable vents, chimney cap. Note: raccoons do not walk in straight lines across open ceilings and stop at walls. Note: I wrote that note anyway.*

She paused over that last sentence. Then left it. Paper didn't flinch, but it did keep score.

*2. Thermal contraction. Temperature dropped to 41°F overnight per phone. Old balloon-framed houses pop and shift. Possible for contraction to produce step-like reports. Not possible for contraction to produce a sequence of steps with even spacing and directional travel. Not possible for contraction to pause above my specific bed.*

*3. Settling / structural. See #2. Same objections.*

*4. Wind. Calm overnight. Checked weather log. Zero relevant gusts.*

*5. Water in pipes. House water was shut off at the street by Theo before I arrived; he showed me the valve. No pressure = no pipe noise. Also: pipes do not run through attics in houses built in 1911, and pipes do not count.*

There it was again. *Count.* Her pen had written it twice now without her permission, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

Because that was the second sound, the one under the floor, and she had to write about it eventually, and writing about it meant admitting how long she had lain there listening to it, cataloging it, trying to give it a shape that wasn't the shape it obviously had.

It had started at 2:10 or thereabouts—not from the cellar exactly, though the cellar was below, but from everywhere below, from the subfloor itself, rising up through the joists and the old pine boards and the legs of the bed like sound traveling through bone. A rhythm. Slow. Patient. One beat, a pause, two beats, a pause, three beats, a pause. Rising by ones. Never hurrying. Never stumbling. Reaching nine—and she had counted along with it, hating herself, unable not to, her lips moving silently in the dark like a child at prayer—and then stopping at nine.

Not ten.

Every time. Nine, and silence, and then after a long pause, beginning again at one. Nine times she had listened to it climb to nine and stop. Nine times. She had not decided to count the cycles; counting them had happened to her, the way bleeding happens to a cut.

At nine, something in the rhythm leaned forward. That was the only way she could describe it, and she hated the description, but paper didn't flinch: each cycle's ninth beat carried a weight the others didn't, a little extra pressure, like a held breath, like a hand resting on a door handle. Like something asking a question and waiting, just short of asking it out loud.

Never ten. As if ten were not permitted. As if ten were being saved.

*6. Mechanical/structural origin of subfloor rhythm,* she wrote, her hand steadier than she felt. *Water hammer: no pressurized water. Furnace ignition cycle: furnace is cold, pilot disconnected since 2019 per inspection report. Tree branches against foundation: no trees within thirty feet on the north side where sound was loudest. Pipes expanding: see above.*

*7. Infrasound / geological. Low-frequency vibration from traffic on Route 9 (3 miles) or quarry blasting (inactive since 2008). Infrasound can induce unease and auditory hallucination. Does not typically produce structured, patterned, integer-ascending sequences. Does not stop at nine.*

She sat back. The kitchen around her was bright and ordinary, morning sun through windows that needed washing, a fly bumping lazily against the glass. On the counter sat the casserole a neighbor had left—Mrs. Halloran, presumably, though Theo hadn't said, just set it inside the door with a look Casper couldn't read. Everything was explainable. Everything in this kitchen was explainable. That was the trick of daylight; she knew it well. Daylight was the great enabler. Daylight let you write lists.

She flipped to a new page, because there was one more entry to make, and she almost didn't make it, and then she made it, because the whole point—the entire architecture of her survival—was that she wrote down everything, especially the things she wanted to leave out.

*8. Memory. Consider: the sounds matched sounds I heard in this house as a child. Consider further: expectation shapes perception. I expected this house to make these sounds. Therefore I may have constructed them. The brain fills in patterns; it is documented; it is common; it is benign.*

She stopped. Pen hovering.

Then, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page:

*I did not construct the pause at nine. I don't know how I know that. But I know it the way you know a stranger's hand on your shoulder in the dark.*

* * *

She spent the day working, deliberately, physically. Estate paperwork spread across the dining table: Wren's death certificate (cause of death: cardiac arrhythmia, age thirty-six, which Casper did not believe, because Wren's heart had been the strong one, always, the twin who never got winded running), the deed, the insurance forms, the funeral home's final invoice. She cleaned the gutters because the ladder was there and because her arms burning was preferable to thinking. She called the pest control company listed in the yellowed magnet on the fridge and left a message and was told, by a cheerful recorded voice, that the earliest appointment was October 21st.

Nine days past the equinox. She noticed the date and told herself she hadn't.

Theo came by at five with Ida in tow, ostensibly to check the oil tank. He moved through the house the way he always moved through it—efficiently, eyes down, touching nothing he didn't have to, a man defusing a bomb he'd long ago stopped hoping to find. Ida, nine years old and solemn as a churchwarden, trailed him and then didn't, drifting instead to the doorway of the study, standing there looking in at the locked desk with an expression Casper couldn't parse and didn't try to.

"Did you sleep okay?" Theo asked, on his way out. Casual. Too casual. The question of a man checking a gauge.

"Fine," Casper said. "Old house. It makes noises."

Something crossed his face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Yeah," he said. "It does that." And then, at the door, with Ida already on the porch steps: "If you hear anything, it's better if you don't—" He stopped. Restarted. "I'll bring over a space heater tomorrow. Nights get cold."

He didn't finish the first sentence. Casper stood in the doorway watching his truck go down the drive and turned the unfinished sentence over like a stone. *It's better if you don't.* Don't what? Investigate? Answer? Listen?

She went back inside and added to the journal: *T. knows something about the noises. His reaction to "it makes noises" was recognition, not sympathy. File for later.*

*File for later.* The great engine of her denial ran on deferred maintenance.

* * *

Night two. She prepared for it the way she prepared for everything: methodically. Earplugs on the nightstand, unused—she needed to hear, needed the data, needed to be the witness even when the witness role was killing her. Journal open. Phone charged, voice memo app ready. She lay down in the made bed, in the room that smelled faintly of cedar and nothing else, no dust, no decay, a room preserved like a specimen under glass, and she waited.

11:47 p.m. The footstep came.

Directly overhead. East to west. Unhurried. Pausing above her bed—that pause again, that awful attentive pause, the sense of a face at a window—and continuing to the northwest corner, and stopping.

Casper lay rigid, heart slamming, and reached for the list. *Raccoon. Raccoon. Straight line, even gait, forty pounds minimum, intelligent animal, learned route, animals do develop routines, they patrol, they—*

The footstep came back. West to east. Back over her bed. Back to the east end.

And stopped there, and did not move again, and Casper realized with a slow cold flood that the walking hadn't been random at all. It had been a circuit. A patrol. Something checking the perimeter of the space above her ceiling, the way she checked the locks in every apartment she'd ever lived in, twice, before bed.

*We do the same things,* she thought, and immediately buried the thought, because thoughts like that were how the cracks started.

At 2:10 a.m., the counting began beneath the floor.

She was ready for it this time—or believed she was—and still it got in under her skin faster than it had the night before, because knowing a thing is coming is not the same as being armored against it. One. Two. Three. The beats rose through the floorboards with terrible patience, and Casper lay with the journal on her chest and wrote in the dark, by feel, blind, her handwriting collapsing into a seismograph of fear: *night two, same time, same pattern, ascending, stops at*

Nine.

But tonight—tonight, on the ninth beat, in the held-breath pause that followed—another sound joined it. Faint. Distant. From upstairs, from the impossible attic, from behind the painted-shut hatch: a voice. Small. Child-sized. Or child-remembered. Saying something.

Saying a number.

Casper's blood went to ice water. Because she knew the voice. You know your own voice; everyone does; it's the one voice you can pick out of any crowd. And the voice in the attic—faint, muffled, filtered through decades of closed paint and sealed boards—was hers. Sixteen years old. Saying *ten?* like a question. Like a knock on a door. Like a girl asking whether she was allowed.

Then silence, total, both directions, above and below, a silence so complete it rang.

Casper did not sleep. At dawn she sat at the kitchen table and looked at the journal and saw that in the dark she had written, beneath the collapsed seismograph line, one more sentence, in handwriting she didn't recognize as her own because it was steady, calm, unhurried:

*It isn't making the sounds to frighten me. It's making them to remind me. It's keeping count of how many nights I've been home.*

She counted backward from today. Two nights. Two cycles of nine.

Eighteen.

She didn't know why that number sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. She didn't know why, when she heard Ida's bicycle bell ring out on the road that morning—Ida pedaling over to spend Saturday with her aunt, calling *"Aunt Casper! Aunt Casper!"* up through the yard, bright and alive and nine years old—she felt the number eighteen shift, somewhere deep beneath the floorboards, and lean toward nineteen.
