## 15. Equinox Eve

Theo had not slept in the house on the equinox since the year Mara died, and even then he had only managed it by drinking until the floorboards stopped meaning anything. Tonight there was no drink in him. He wanted his hands steady. He wanted to see everything.

By four in the afternoon the light had already begun to fail, the way it did at the turn of the season—the sun dropping early behind the tree line like something being called home. Theo stood at the kitchen window with a mug of coffee he'd forgotten to sip and watched Casper come up from the barn carrying an armload of what looked, at a distance, like kindling.

It was not kindling. It was dried yarrow and bundles of rue, the stems bound with twine in the old way, the way Mara's grandmother had done it, the way nobody in this family ever admitted to doing it. Casper carried them against her chest like an infant and did not look up at the window where he stood. She knew he was watching. He could tell by the set of her shoulders—braced, deliberate, a woman performing composure for an audience of one.

She hung the bundles over the doorframes. Front door, back door, the cellar door last. At the cellar door her hands slowed. Theo saw her press her palm flat against the wood, briefly, the way you'd check a child for fever, and then she stepped back fast, as if the wood had answered.

He went out to her.

"You don't have to do all this," he said. "The salt, the—" He gestured at the yarrow. "Ida doesn't need to see it."

"Ida already knows," Casper said. "That's the whole problem, Theo. She's known longer than we have."

* * *

They worked through the evening without once saying what they were working toward. That was the family genius, Theo thought—not the bargain itself, but the language around it, the elaborate architecture of euphemism that let three generations of Winterbournes live beside a thing in the cellar and call it nothing. Salt was "for the water softener." Mirrors were covered because "the glass is old and rattles." Ida was dressed in white because tomorrow they were going to church.

There was no church. There hadn't been a church within forty miles that would take them since 1994, when Reverend Aldous had seen something in the Winterbourne pew one Sunday and never came back from the parking lot.

But the white dress was real. Mara had bought it two years ago, tags still on, hanging in Ida's closet with a garment bag over it like a body waiting for its occasion. Theo had found it while covering the mirrors upstairs—I'da's mirror last, always last—and he'd stood in front of the closet for a long time with his hand on the zipper before he took the dress down.

Ida put it on without being asked. That was the worst part. Nine years old, and when he held the dress out to her she looked at it the way other children look at snow boots in November—with recognition, with a kind of grim practicality—and said, "Okay," and lifted her arms.

"It's pretty," she said, turning in front of the covered mirror, seeing only herself as a blur in white. "Am I allowed to look at myself tomorrow? In the glass?"

"Why wouldn't you be?"

"Mama said the day after the equinox, everything goes back to normal." A pause. Her small fingers smoothed the skirt. "So today isn't normal."

Theo knelt and buttoned the collar, though it didn't need buttoning. His hands were shaking and he needed something to do with them.

"Today is completely normal," he said. "Pancakes in the morning. Cartoons. You and me."

Ida looked at him with her mother's eyes—Mara's exact gray, the color of weather coming—and patted his cheek twice, gently, the way you'd calm a dog during a storm.

"Daddy," she said, "you don't have to pretend for me. Aunt Casper already told me the rules."

"What rules?"

"The real ones." She counted them off on her fingers, and Theo felt the room go cold around him. "Don't answer if your name comes twice. Don't count past nine, not even in your head. If the floor gets warm, stand on the cold spots. And if somebody knocks on my window tonight"—she said this one lightly, matter-of-factly, tucking it between the others like a card sharp slipping in the ace—"it won't be a person, so I shouldn't open it."

"Who told you about the window?"

"Aunt Casper." Ida shrugged. "And Mama used to say it too. Before." The word *before* landed in the room and stayed there. "Daddy, can I sleep with the light on just for tonight? Not because I'm scared. Because it says in the rules light helps."

"Where does it say that?"

"In the cupboard."

* * *

After Ida was down—door barricaded with the dresser, nightlight burning, Theo checking on her every twenty minutes like a man minding a pot about to boil over—he found Casper in the study with their mother's ledgers spread across the desk like a surgeon's instruments.

She had chalk in her hand. On the desktop, in a careful column, she had written:

**C.W. — offered freely**
**I.W. — withdrawn**

"You're still writing yourself down," Theo said from the doorway.

"I'm making sure the arithmetic is right." She didn't look up. "Twenty years, one vessel. It's been twenty years. The ledger doesn't care which name, only that a name gets paid. So I'm telling it which one."

"And if it disagrees?"

"Then it disagrees." Casper finally raised her head, and Theo saw something in her face he'd spent two weeks pretending not to see: peace. Actual peace, settled into her features like silt in still water. "Theo, I've been running from this farm since I was sixteen. Twenty-four years of denial, of building a life out of not-thinking. You know what I learned this month? The running was the debt accruing interest. Every year I stayed away, the price went up. Wren paid it. Then Mara tried not to pay it, and Mara—" His breath caught. "Mara died refusing to name a vessel, and the house took her anyway, slowly, and left Ida here holding the account."

"I know how my wife died."

"Do you?" Casper's voice wasn't cruel. It was worse than cruel—it was gentle. "Because I've read her letters now, Theo. All of them. She wrote to Wren's grave every month. Do you want to know what she wrote in the last one?"

"No."

"*'I will not choose. Let it take me instead.'*" Casper closed the ledger. "And it did. But it didn't close the account, because refusal isn't payment. Refusal is just a different kind of offering. She offered her life and kept Ida off the books for nine years. Nine years, Theo. The grace period ends tomorrow at moonrise."

"So we run. Tonight. Right now, all three of us, we get in the truck and we drive until the sun comes up and we don't stop."

"We tried that." Casper's voice flattened. "Tuesday. The road loops. You know it loops—you've driven it."

"I haven't. I've never tried. I stay because—" He stopped. What was the truth? That he'd marked days off a calendar in the pantry for nine years without knowing why, crossing them out each morning, and only understood last week that he'd been counting down to this? That some part of him had married into this house and never left it, the way you don't leave a hospital room while someone's still breathing?

"I stay because Ida stays," he said finally. "And Ida stays because the house lets her."

"Then hear me." Casper stood and came around the desk, and took his face in both her hands, and made him look at her. She had Wren's face. She had Wren's face and she was using it to speak to him with a steadiness neither twin had ever managed in life. "Tomorrow at moonrise, I walk down those stairs. I give them the wrong name on purpose. I tell the count that I am the sister who stayed—that I'm the debt, that I've been the debt since 2004, and Wren was only keeping my seat warm. If the house believes me, the ledger closes clean. One vessel, twenty years, paid in full by the twin who broke the bargain. And Ida comes off the page forever."

"And if it doesn't believe you?"

"Then it takes what it takes, and at least I'll be standing between it and her when it does." Her thumbs moved once across his cheekbones, almost tenderly. "Which is more than anyone did for Wren."

"Casper—"

"Beg me to run," she said quietly. "I know you want to. I can see it. So say it, and then watch me say no, and then we'll both feel better."

"Begging seemed beneath us both," Theo said, and heard his own voice crack on the word *both*. "Fine. I'm begging. Take Ida and go. Leave me the salt and the ledgers and the cellar. I'll hold the door. It's what I've been doing for nine years anyway—holding doors, marking days, keeping the light on in her room. Let me keep doing it. Just take her and go."

"No."

"Damn it, Casper—"

"She's your daughter. She's my blood-debt. And I am the only person alive who knows what's down there well enough to lie to it." She released his face and stepped back, and the peace in her expression didn't waver. "You begged. I refused. Now we're done, and we have maybe six hours, and I'd rather spend them being your family than being your argument."

* * *

They spent the evening the way families spend evenings before executions: making dinner nobody ate, washing dishes nobody dirtied, sitting in the living room with the lamps turned low because Casper said bright light "gives it something to push against," and Theo hadn't asked her to explain because he suspected she was right.

At nine o'clock, Ida came downstairs in her white dress, having changed into it secretly, and stood in the doorway with her hair brushed and her hands folded.

"Can we say goodnight properly?" she asked. "Since tomorrow might be church."

They sat on either side of her on the couch. She took one of Theo's hands and one of Casper's and held them in her lap like something she was keeping safe for them.

"If I fall asleep counting," Ida said, "will one of you stop me?"

Theo's throat closed. Casper answered for both of them.

"Every time," she said. "All the way down to zero if we have to."

"That's what Mama said." Ida nodded, satisfied, and leaned her head against Theo's shoulder. Within minutes she was asleep, breathing slow and even, her small white dress pooling around her like milk spilled across the couch cushions.

They carried her up together, one holding her shoulders and one her feet, and laid her in bed behind the barricaded door, and stood in the hallway afterward not speaking, listening to her breathe.

At eleven-forty, Casper touched his arm.

"Whatever happens tonight," she said, "thank you for staying. Not for the house. For her."

Then she walked toward the kitchen, toward the cellar door, and Theo stood alone in the hallway with his daughter sleeping behind him and the tallies on the cellar wall reaching twenty, and heard, very faintly, from below the floorboards, a voice begin to count.

"One," it said. Patient. Glad. Almost home.

Theo pressed his ear to the wall, and the voice paused—listening for him—and then, softly, in a register no human throat should own, it added:

"Bring her down. We've waited so long for the right twin."
