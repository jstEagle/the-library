## 4. One Second Late

Lena woke on the couch because the bedroom door had been open when they went to sleep and was closed when they woke, and they had decided, in the gray arithmetic of dawn, that this was a thing to think about later.

Later. The word had done fifteen years of heavy lifting in Lena's life. Later they'd call the lawyer about the deed. Later they'd sort Edith's things. Later they'd figure out why every clock in the house stood at 11:58 like a congregation of witnesses who had all seen the same crime and agreed to say nothing.

The kitchen tap ran brown for three seconds, then clear. Lena cupped water against their face—cold enough to feel honest—and looked up into the small mirror above the sink.

Their reflection blinked.

That wasn't the strange part. Reflections blink; that's the whole arrangement. What was strange was the order of events. Lena blinked first. Then, a beat afterward—a full, distinct beat, like a note held too long—the reflection followed.

Lena stood with wet hands dripping onto the porcelain and watched their own face watch them back.

*You're tired,* said the rational part of their mind, the part that had spent a decade and a half converting dread into chartable symptoms. *You slept badly on a stranger's couch in a dead woman's house. Your eyes are dry. You're seeing lag where there's only light bouncing off glass at 186,000 miles per second.*

Light didn't work that way, of course. Light didn't wait its turn. But the rational mind was generous with physics it half-remembered, the way a hospice nurse is generous with medical reassurance she doesn't fully believe: *the pain is temporary, the confusion is normal, everyone goes sometime.*

Lena blinked again. Deliberately. Left eye, then right.

The reflection did the same—one heartbeat behind, maybe less, but behind, undeniably behind, a shadow of motion trailing its source like thunder after lightning.

"Okay," Lena said aloud, and their voice sounded wrong in the kitchen, too flat, as if the room were absorbing it rather than echoing it. "Okay."

They dried their hands on a dish towel gone stiff with age and went looking for something to test this properly, because if you were going to lose your mind, you wanted documentation. Fifteen years of nursing taught you that much. Symptoms without charts were just stories, and stories could be dismissed. Lena needed numbers.

There was no phone signal worth the name out here—the farmhouse sat in a pocket of the county where towers went to die—but there was a metronome in the parlor.

It sat on top of the piano nobody played, a brass-cased antique with a key still in its winding slot, and when Lena picked it up, dust fell away from it in a sheet, revealing polished wood beneath. Someone had kept it clean. Someone had wound it recently enough that the spring took the key with an eager, oiled click.

*Edith's?* Lena thought. *But Edith died months ago.*

They set it on the mantel between two stopped clocks—11:58, always 11:58—and turned the key. The pendulum caught on the second swing, and the tick began, steady as a pulse: *tick, tock, tick, tock*, sixty beats to the minute, the tempo printed on a brass scale that some long-dead Lockridge had adjusted by hand.

Lena positioned themselves in front of the tall mirror over the mantel—the good one, beveled, older than the house's paint—and raised their right hand on a tick.

The reflection raised its left hand on the next tick.

Not instantly. Not within the same beat. On the *next* one. A full second of silence between what Lena did and what the glass admitted to doing.

Lena lowered the hand. The reflection lowered its own a second later, and this time Lena saw the fingers settle slightly differently—not mirrored exactly, but *interpreted*, the way a person mimics another person rather than the way light obeys physics.

"No," Lena said.

The reflection's mouth moved a moment later. No sound came through the glass—that never happened, that was not how mirrors worked—but the shape of the word was unmistakable even delayed. *No.* Agreeing with them. Or mocking them. With a one-second delay, it was impossible to tell which.

Lena stepped back until their shoulders hit the wall, and the metronome kept ticking into the silence, sixty beats a minute, patient as a heart monitor in a room where nobody was dying yet.

---

They tested every mirror in the house before noon.

The hall mirror: one second late. The vanity upstairs, its silvering spotted black like frostbite: one second late. A hand mirror found in a drawer, its handle wrapped in yellowed ribbon: one second late. Even the dark window over the kitchen sink, when Lena stood before it at dusk with a lamp lit behind them, produced a ghost-Lena a full second adrift, blinking after them, breathing after them, existing on a delay like a broadcast from somewhere very far away.

One second. Exactly one second, every time, everywhere, as if the whole house shared a single clock and that clock had decided Lena's actions required review before transmission.

Lena sat on the stairs with the metronome in their lap and made themselves think it through, because panic was a luxury and they were, professionally speaking, an expert in watching other people panic and talking them down.

*Theory one: neurological event.* Migraine aura, temporal lobe mischief, some small seizure rearranging their perception of simultaneity. But a brain couldn't misjudge *every* surface identically. And the brain theory didn't explain the fingers settling differently. Perception distorted; it didn't improvise.

*Theory two: trick of the house.* Old mirrors, warped glass, odd angles. But old mirrors distorted images—they didn't *delay* them. Distortion was geometry. Delay was time. And the window hadn't been warped. The window had simply decided to run a second behind, the way a lazy employee runs behind, the way a liar repeats your question back to buy himself a moment to think.

*Theory three.*

Lena didn't want to write theory three down, even mentally. Theory three was the one where the house was rehearsing something. Where the delay wasn't a malfunction but a *practice*—something learning Lena's face the way an understudy learns a lead's blocking, one second of separation now so that someday the separation would close, and the performance would begin, and no one in the audience would know the difference.

Mr. Aldous had smiled at his window the morning he died. Lena remembered that smile now with a new and unwelcome precision: he hadn't been smiling at the sunrise. He'd been smiling at something *in* the glass, something arriving, and he'd counted backwards toward it like a man greeting a train he could finally see coming down the track.

*Thirty. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight.*

Four patients this month, all counting toward the harvest moon. All with the same surname buried in their charts, a surname Lena had noticed and filed under coincidence because filing it anywhere else would have meant driving out here years ago, asking questions, opening doors.

Lena set the metronome ticking again and faced the tall mirror and made themselves do the thing they'd been avoiding since dawn: they watched, closely, for a full five minutes, cataloguing every divergence.

Mostly the reflection matched. That was the horror of it—it was a *good* copy. It breathed when Lena breathed, shifted weight when Lena shifted weight, tucked the same strand of hair behind the same ear. If you weren't looking for the gap, you'd never find it. It was only in the small moments, the blinks and swallows and involuntary twitches, that the seam showed: a full second of independence, a full second in which the thing in the glass was alone with Lena's face and free to do with it whatever it liked.

And twice—in five minutes, twice—the reflection used those seconds.

Once it glanced toward the study door at the end of the hall. Lena hadn't looked at the study door. Once it smiled, briefly, privately, the way a person smiles remembering a joke, and then smoothed itself flat a half-beat before Lena's own face arrived in the glass, so that by the time Lena's real mouth curved, the smile was already waiting for it, already worn in.

Like a glove someone else had broken in.

Lena stopped the metronome. The silence rang.

"You're practicing," Lena said to the glass. Their voice cracked on the second word, and they hated that, hated being a woman in her forties talking to a mirror in a haunted house like a character in a story she'd have rejected as too on-the-nose. "I can see you practicing."

The reflection said nothing. It waited its one second, and then it copied the words—mouth shaping *practicing* with careful, exact articulation, a student mouthing vocabulary—and tilted its head the way Lena tilted theirs, but slower, savoring it.

Then, in the gap before Lena moved again, it looked past them. Down the hall. At the study door.

Which was, Lena realized with a cold drop through the center of their chest, locked. Had been locked since they arrived. Had no key on any hook or ring in the house.

The reflection knew what was in that room.

Lena turned away from the mirror and made herself walk, not run, down the corridor, past the stopped clocks, past the framed photographs of Lockridges whose faces seemed to slide out of focus whenever looked at directly, and stopped in front of the study door with their palm flat against the wood.

The door was warm.

Houses in October were cold. Houses with dead furnaces were colder. This door was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a forehead is warm before the fever breaks, and beneath Lena's palm, faint but rhythmic, something pulsed.

At sixty beats a minute.

The tempo of the metronome. The tempo of a resting heart. The tempo, Lena thought, of a countdown that had learned to keep time.

Behind the door, in the room that had been locked since before Lena arrived, something shifted—a soft, deliberate sound, like a page turning in a ledger.

Lena took their hand off the door and stepped back, and the warmth stayed in the wood like a handprint pressed into wax, fading slowly, reluctantly, the way warmth fades from a bed someone has just left.

"I'm not sleeping past midnight," Lena told the empty hallway, to the clocks frozen at 11:58, to the instruction in a dead woman's letter that had seemed like superstition a week ago and now seemed like the only sentence anyone had ever bothered to write honestly. "Whatever you are. Whatever's counting. I'm not sleeping past midnight."

Down the hall, in the tall mirror, a second behind, their reflection nodded.

It had not been asked to.

* * *

Lena didn't sleep that night. They sat in the kitchen with every lamp burning and the metronome going on the table—because the ticking was terrible, but the silence was worse, the silence was where the house did its thinking—and somewhere around two in the morning, they heard it.

Footsteps overhead. Slow, unhurried, crossing the floor of the bedroom above the kitchen, pausing, crossing back. Lena's footsteps. Their exact gait, the slight drag of the left heel they'd carried since a fall in nursing school, reproduced overhead with loving fidelity by something that had clearly been studying.

Except Lena was sitting in the kitchen. Both Lenas were accounted for. Which meant the footsteps belonged to neither of them—or to the one who no longer needed to be in the same room as its original to practice walking in its shoes.

The footsteps stopped directly overhead. Directly above the kitchen chair where Lena sat.

And then, muffled by floorboards, by distance, by one second of delay that might as well have been an ocean, Lena heard their own voice—dry, tired, pitched exactly the way their voice sounded at the end of a double shift—say two words.

Two syllables. Patient. Almost kind.

*"Stay here."*

Lena sat very still until dawn, the metronome ticking, the coffee going cold, and when the sun finally came up they packed a bag, because there was one person left in the county who had known Edith Lockridge before Edith stopped speaking to the world, and Lena was done pretending the questions could wait until later.

Ruth Calloway's farmhouse sat a quarter mile down the road, and its windows, Lena noted as they walked the frost-silvered lane, had no mirrors in them at all.
