## 7. What the Dying Know

The first time Lena heard a patient counting backwards, they were twenty-four years old and three weeks into their first real job, and they assumed it was the morphine.

Room 14, St. Ambrose Continuing Care. The chart said *Hollis, Margaret, 81, end-stage renal failure*, and the woman in the bed had been pleasant and vague for the entire shift—asking twice for water, once for a husband who had been dead eleven years—and then at 11:40 p.m., with no transition at all, she had opened her eyes and begun to count.

"Thirty," she said. "Twenty-nine."

Her voice was clear. That was the thing Lena remembered most, later, when everything else had blurred: how *clear* it was. Not slurred, not confused, not the wandering arithmetic of a sedated mind. Each number arrived whole, spaced evenly, like footsteps on stairs.

Lena checked her pump. Checked her vitals. Everything normal.

"Twenty-eight. Twenty-seven."

"Hollis, hon? You with me?"

Margaret Hollis turned her head on the pillow and looked directly at Lena, and her eyes were the clearest thing about her. Present. Awake. Looking at Lena the way you look at someone you're glad came, because you didn't want to do this alone.

"Twenty-six," she said. "You can hear it too. I knew you could hear it."

"I hear you, Mrs. Hollis." Lena took her hand. It was cool and light as paper. "What are we counting?"

She smiled then—not frightened, not sad. Relieved, almost. The smile of someone setting down something heavy after carrying it a long way.

"Down," she said. "We always go down. Nobody ever counts up at the end. Did you ever notice that? Twenty-five. Twenty-four."

Lena had noticed. Fifteen years of noticing, by now. But that night, that first night, they only stood there holding an old woman's hand while she counted toward something, and at "one" Margaret Hollis exhaled—a long, satisfied breath, like a sigh at the end of a good meal—and did not breathe in again.

Her face stayed smiling. Lena closed her eyes against it and pressed the call button anyway, out of habit, though there was nothing anyone could do, and nothing that needed doing. People died at St. Ambrose every night. That was the business. That was the work.

But nobody else counted.

* * *

Present-day Lena sat on the edge of Edith's bed—the guest room bed, the dead woman's spare mattress, still smelling faintly of cedar and disuse—and pulled the hospice records up on a laptop that caught the lamplight wrong, screen too bright, room too dark.

They hadn't slept. They'd told themselves it was the house, the clocks, the footprints, the mirror that blinked a full second late. But underneath all of it, under the dread like groundwater rising, there had been this other thing, this older thing, scratching to get out. And tonight, alone in a dead woman's house, Lena had finally let it.

Four patients this month. Counting backwards. All of them stopping at the same date, spoken aloud or written on walls or traced on windows with a fingertip in condensation: the last night of September. The harvest moon.

Lena had told themselves it was coincidence. Hospice nurses see patterns in everything; grief makes gamblers of us all. Four patients in one month was unusual but not impossible. Terminal delirium clusters. Barometric pressure. A bad flu season emptying the wards.

But now, with the ledger downstairs listing Lockridge names like a menu, Lena did what they should have done fifteen years ago.

They searched the charts.

Not the current month's charts—all of them. Every patient Lena had sat with over fifteen years, every deathbed, every final shift, cross-referenced against the notes Lena kept privately, obsessively, in a series of cheap spiral notebooks they'd never shown anyone. Because Lena had always kept notes. Had always tracked the counting, even while insisting to themselves they weren't tracking anything. Even while learning, carefully, deliberately, to stop listening.

The search took two hours. When it finished, Lena sat very still in the cedar-smelling dark and looked at the number on the screen.

Thirty-one patients. Over fifteen years, thirty-one of Lena's patients had counted backwards in their final hours—out of maybe four hundred deaths total. That was not a pattern. That was a *selection*. And Lena scrolled down the column of names, thirty-one rows, and felt the floor of their professional detachment give way like rotted decking, because every single name, every one, shared a surname with someone in Edith Lockridge's ledger.

Hollis. Born Margaret Lockridge; married name on the chart, maiden name in the obituary Lena found in ninety seconds of searching. Aldous—Aldous was Edith's cousin, twice removed; the ledger had his father's line. The others: Pemberton, Voss, Crane, Merrick. Names that meant nothing scattered across fifteen years of memory, and everything arranged in a single column under a dead woman's roof.

They weren't Lena's patients by chance. They were the family. Distant branches, married-off limbs, names changed and scattered so thoroughly that no one alive would ever lay them side by side—but the dying knew. The dying had known exactly whose blood was drying in them, and they had counted down to the harvest moon the way you count down to a debt coming due.

And Lena had been there for thirty-one of them. Holding their hands. Hearing the numbers. Writing them in notebooks and filing the notebooks in a box and telling themselves it was grief-processing, self-care, a nurse's way of metabolizing loss.

It was a record. They had been keeping a record for fifteen years without admitting what it was a record *of*.

Lena closed the laptop. In the dark, the house made its small sounds—the tick of cooling wood, the settling of old timber—and each one sounded, now, like a number being spoken softly somewhere below.

* * *

But that wasn't the whole of it. The whole of it went back further than Margaret Hollis. The whole of it was about the second time, and the third, and the slow education of a young nurse in the art of not hearing.

Because here is what Lena had never told anyone, not supervisors, not therapists, not the partners who came and went: the counting spread. Like a rumor through a ward. Like damp through plaster.

After Margaret Hollis, it was Mr. Okafor in Room 9, counting down from thirty in a voice gone rusty with disuse, and when Lena asked him—gently, professionally—what he was counting, he said, "The same thing she was. Can't you tell? We're all counting the same thing." Then he frowned, as if he'd said more than he meant to, and added, "Don't listen too closely, nurse. Listening is how it gets your address."

That was the phrase that lodged. *Listening is how it gets your address.* Lena had laughed about it afterward in the break room, a laugh with no floor under it, and a senior nurse named Dolores had looked up from her crossword and said, without looking away from the page: "Some of them talk strange at the end. Best not to make too much of it. You'll burn out quick if you make much of things."

Dolores, Lena understood much later, had worked the ward for thirty years. Dolores had heard it all before. Dolores had learned the same lesson Lena was about to learn, and had learned it so well she could deliver it as advice over a crossword.

So Lena learned. That was the ugly truth of it, the truth sitting in their chest now like swallowed stone: it wasn't that they'd failed to see. It was that they'd been taught to unsee, and they had been an excellent student.

By the third year, Lena had a system. When a patient started counting, Lena would check the pump, adjust a blanket, turn on the television low—some noise, any noise, a ballgame, a home shopping channel, the cheerful idiot murmur of the living—to lay over the numbers like a cloth over a birdcage. When a patient asked, *Can you hear it too?*—and some of them asked, more of them than Lena liked to remember—Lena would say, "I hear you," which was true and also a lie, and the patients would look at them with such tired kindness that Lena sometimes had to leave the room and stand in the supply closet until their hands stopped shaking.

Because the patients weren't afraid. That was the worst part. That was the part Lena had spent fifteen years refusing to think about. The dying weren't afraid of the thing they were counting toward. They were afraid of *dying before they finished*. Of being interrupted. Lena once walked in mid-count on old Mr. Voss and he grabbed their wrist with shocking strength and said, "Don't—don't talk, I'll lose my place, I have to start over," and the terror in his face was the terror of a man watching his train pull away.

Counting toward something. Willingly. Gratefully. As if the number were a door and one was the last lock opening.

And at "one"—always at "one"—the smile. Thirty-one smiles. Lena had catalogued them without meaning to, the way you catalogue anything you refuse to examine: the relief, the satisfaction, the look of a debt paid in full. As if whatever waited at the bottom of the count had accepted them. As if they'd been *expected*.

Lena had explained it all, of course. Had built explanations the way you build a seawall, course upon course: terminal lucidity, hypoxia, the brain's dying habit of reaching for rhythm and ritual. Counting is comforting. Counting is control. People pray the rosary, people recite psalms, people count. It means nothing. It means nothing. It means nothing.

Fifteen years of meaning nothing. And now, in a farmhouse where every clock read 11:58, Lena finally said it out loud to the empty room:

"It was never the morphine."

The house received the words without echo. Old houses swallow sound. This one swallowed it like it was hungry.

* * *

There was one more thing. There was always one more thing, Lena was learning; this family's history came in installments, each worse than the last.

They went downstairs barefoot, past the stopped clocks, into the kitchen, and dug the box of notebooks out of the car. Set them on the kitchen table under the lamp: fifteen years of cheap spirals, spines cracked, covers soft with handling. Thirty-one entries marked with a small symbol Lena had invented in their first year and never questioned—a circle with a line drawn down through it. A moon, half-eclipsed. They had thought they were being poetic.

Now they went through the notebooks page by page, matching symbols to names, names to the ledger upstairs, and building a timeline. And the timeline had a shape. The shape was the shape of the ledger itself: every counting death clustered in late September, within days of the harvest moon, across fifteen years. Never in March. Never in July. September, always September, the way tides obey a moon that isn't even in the sky yet.

And one entry more. Near the back. Eleven years ago. A patient Lena had almost forgotten—had, Lena admitted now, *chosen* to forget, burying the memory deeper than the rest, and feeling the burial site ache whenever it was disturbed.

Ruth Calloway's sister-in-law. Admitted under a different name, but Lena had placed her the moment she saw the intake paperwork: the Calloway farm bordered the Lockridge property, and everyone in that county was related to everyone within three generations. She hadn't counted. That was what made her different. She had spent her last night begging instead—begging Lena, gripping their sleeve with papery strength, saying, "You have to tell them to stop feeding it, tell them it doesn't want what they think it wants, it wants—" and then the morphine fog had taken her mid-sentence, and she'd died at dawn with her mouth still shaped around the word.

*It wants.*

Lena sat in the kitchen of the house that wanted them, surrounded by fifteen years of willful blindness made paper, and understood that they had known. Not suspected. Known. Known since the beginning, since Margaret Hollis's clear voice and clear eyes, and had chosen, night after night, year after year, to build the seawall higher rather than look at the water.

Because looking would have cost something. Looking would have meant admitting that the world had a door in it, and that Lena had been standing next to the door their whole life, holding it politely open for the dying, and never once asking what was on the other side.

The lamp flickered. Outside, past the dark fields, the sky was clear and moonless—two nights early, the moon not due yet—and still the light through the window seemed to have a color to it, a faint tarnished gold, like brass remembered rather than seen.

Lena gathered the notebooks into a stack, and their hand stopped on the top one, the oldest, Margaret Hollis's entry, and read their own handwriting from fifteen years ago, the note they'd written at 3 a.m. in the break room, shaking, before the seawall went up:

*She said "we're all counting the same thing." Asked her what. She said: "The harvest, dear. Someone has to be harvested, and I'm ripe."*

Below it, in younger Lena's careful print, the explanation that had held the wall together for fifteen years:

*Terminal delirium. Farm imagery — she grew up rural. Means nothing.*

Lena touched the ink. Somewhere above them, in the hall, the mirror hung on its nail, waiting a second behind the world, rehearsing.

"Mr. Aldous counted to the harvest moon," Lena said quietly. "Mrs. Hollis said she was ripe. Ruth Calloway's sister-in-law said it *wants* something. And my grandmother left me a house with a blank line in a ledger dated for the end of this month."

The house said nothing. The house was very good at saying nothing.

Lena looked at the stack of notebooks—all those years of listening without hearing, of seeing without looking—and felt the guilt settle into its permanent place, a stone at the base of the spine. They had been the door-holder. They had held the door open thirty-one times and called it nursing.

Tomorrow they would find Ruth Calloway. Tonight, they carried the notebooks to the study, set them beside the ledger, and noticed—for the first time, though it must have been there all along—that the ledger's final blank line was not entirely blank.

Above the empty space where a name would go, in faded brown ink older than Lena's birth, someone had already written a single word.

Not a name.

An invitation: *Welcome home.*
