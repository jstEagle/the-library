## 13. Ruth's Confession

Ruth Calloway had spent forty years learning how to carry a thing without looking at it.

You got good at it, if you practiced. You learned to fold it small and tuck it behind the breastbone, where it sat like a swallowed stone. You learned which roads to avoid—the back way past the Lockridge property, the church pew where Edith used to sit, the county records office with its smell of dust and old ink. You learned to change the subject when the harvest moon came up fat and orange over the fields, and you learned never, ever to count backwards, not even the stairs, not even the seconds waiting for water to boil.

But the girl—Lena—had come to her door three days ago with her grandmother's eyes and her grandmother's stubborn jaw, asking questions Ruth had spent four decades not answering. And now the moon was rising a night early, wrong and swollen at the edge of the sky like a bruise coming up through skin, and Ruth knew the stone behind her breastbone was going to come up whether she wanted it to or not.

She found Lena in the farmhouse kitchen, sitting at the table where Edith used to sit, with the ledger open in front of her. The girl looked up when Ruth came in, and there was something in her face that Ruth recognized from mirrors—not fear exactly, but the particular exhaustion of someone who has stopped being able to lie to herself.

"You knew," Lena said. It wasn't a question.

Ruth stood in the doorway a moment. The kitchen smelled of lemon polish and old wood and underneath it something sweeter, something like overripe fruit, that she chose not to name. Every clock on the walls said 11:58. They had said 11:58 for forty years, as far as she knew. She'd stopped checking.

"Sit down, girl," Ruth said, "if I'm going to tell it."

* * *

"I was fourteen," Ruth began, "the summer your grandmother's father did what he did in the root cellar. I told you that much already. What I didn't tell you is that it wasn't the last time. And it wasn't the worst."

Lena's hands were flat on the ledger, as if holding it down. As if it might get up and walk.

"Edith and me, we were close as sisters growing up. Closer, maybe, because sisters fight and we mostly didn't. We told each other everything. That's the part that shames me most now—not the thing itself, but that after it, we stopped telling each other things, and then we stopped talking altogether, and I let that happen because silence was easier than the truth."

Ruth sat down across from the girl. Her knees cracked. Everything about her cracked these days; she was an old woman held together with habit.

"The year I'm telling you about now was 1984. Forty-one years ago this September. Edith had been married two years by then. Her husband, Gerald—a good man, dull as unsalted bread, but good—they couldn't conceive. Doctors, prayers, everything. And the moon was coming, and Edith's mother was dead two years past, and by rights the offering should have come out of Edith's own life. Her memories. Herself."

"She told you this?" Lena asked quietly.

"She didn't have to tell me. I could see it on her. She'd gone gray around the mouth the way people do when they're carrying something heavy up a long hill. So I asked her. I made her tell me, because that's what sisters do, even the ones who aren't." Ruth's hands folded themselves on the table, knuckles like a row of small stones. "And she said, 'There has to be another way. There's always been another way, hasn't there? Daddy found one.'"

* * *

The summer of 1984, Ruth remembered, had been hot enough that the tar on the road went soft and the corn grew tall as secrets. Edith had come to her house on an evening in late August with a canvas bag and a look on her face that Ruth had never seen before and hoped never to see again: the look of a person who has decided something terrible and is now just arranging the furniture of it.

"I need your help," Edith said. "And I need you to never ask me why, and I need you to take this to your grave."

"That's three things," Ruth had said, trying for lightness, "and I don't like any of them."

Edith set the bag on the kitchen floor between them. It clinked, softly, like dishes packed for a move.

"It's not a person," Edith said. "I want you to understand that first. Whatever you're thinking—it's not that. It's never been that. It doesn't want flesh. You know what it wants."

Ruth did know. Everyone in the hollow knew, in the wordless way children of certain families know things—knew that the Lockridges' money came from somewhere, knew that old men in town went quiet when the subject came up, knew that certain photographs in certain houses showed faces nobody could put a name to anymore, though they'd known those faces all their lives.

"What's in the bag?" Ruth asked.

"Belongings," Edith said. "That's all it needs. Belongings and a name spoken over them. It eats the remembering, Ruth. Not the person. The person can walk away whole and never know they've lost anything, because the loss is the knowing." She laughed then, a short ugly sound. "Do you see how clean it is? How merciful? That's the filthiest part of it. It lets everyone off. It takes a life out of the world so gently that the world doesn't even feel the hand."

"So whose—" Ruth started, and stopped, because she'd understood.

The bag belonged to somebody.

"There's a boy," Edith said, and her voice went flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading terms and conditions. "State home, down past Marion. No family ever claimed him. No name anyone uses—he's got a number instead. He's eleven. He likes drawing airplanes. I've watched him through the fence twice." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "If it isn't him, it's me. If it's me, it's Gerald next, or our child someday, or yours. Somebody feeds it, Ruth. The only question is whether it's someone who'll be missed."

"And you decided," Ruth said slowly, "that an orphan wouldn't be."

The silence between them lasted long enough for the ice in Ruth's glass to crack.

"He won't feel it," Edith whispered. "He won't even know. He'll just—stop having been loved. That's all. That's the whole of it. Whoever loved him will forget they did, and he'll forget them, and it'll be like he was never—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth. "God forgive me. God forgive me, I've been telling myself it's mercy. For him too. He's got nothing, Ruth. Nothing but the airplane drawings. At least this way he stops wanting."

Ruth should have said no.

She had thought about that sentence every day for forty years—I should have said no—and every day for forty years she had answered it the same way: But I didn't.

Because Edith was crying, and Edith was her sister in every way that mattered, and because some cold practical corner of Ruth's heart had done the arithmetic too. One unclaimed boy against a whole line of claimed ones. One nobody against everybody.

They are very reasonable, the devils we make deals with. Even secondhand. Especially secondhand.

* * *

"We gathered the bag together," Ruth said aloud, in the kitchen, in the present, while the false-early moon climbed the window like something pulling itself up a wall. "That's my confession, girl. Not that I knew. That I helped. We drove down to Marion in Edith's husband's truck, and I kept watch while she went through the boy's footlocker in the dormitory room. A tin of pencil stubs. Three drawings of airplanes—one of them had a boy's face drawn in the cockpit window, smiling. A wool sweater with a hole in one elbow. A photograph of a woman—we never knew who, some mother or aunt, dead probably—that was the only picture of anybody who'd ever loved him. And his shoes. His good shoes, Sunday shoes, because Edith said it needed a whole self, not scraps."

Lena had gone very still. "Did it work?"

"Oh, it worked." Ruth heard her own voice come out bitter as dregs. "That October the bank approved Edith's loan for the north acreage. Gerald's shop doubled its business inside two years. And the boy—" She stopped. Started again. "Here's the thing I need you to understand about what it does. Nobody died. Nobody vanished. The state home still had its records, its number, its bed assignments. But the boy himself—when we drove past the home that Christmas, out of some sick need to see—I saw him in the yard, playing alone, and Edith rolled down the window and called his number out to him, the way the matron did, and he turned around and looked straight through us. Not rude. Not frightened. Just—nothing. Like we were weather. Because whatever love had ever touched that child had been eaten, and whatever he'd been to anyone was gone, and he was standing in the sun with a full belly and an empty heart and no idea there'd ever been anything missing."

Ruth's eyes were burning. She let them.

"Edith cried for a week. Then she stopped crying forever, more or less. That's the other price nobody writes in the ledger. You feed it once and you spend the rest of your life unable to feel the full weight of anything, because you know exactly what you'd find if you lifted the stone. We stopped speaking five years later. Not because of a fight. Because every time I looked at her I saw that yard, and that boy turning around with nothing in his face, and I think she saw the same thing when she looked at me. Two women keeping the same grave. You can't be sisters over a grave like that. You can only be strangers who happen to remember."

* * *

"But that's not the end of it," Lena said. Her voice had a strange thin quality. "Is it. Because the ledger has entries after 1984."

"No," Ruth agreed. "It's not the end." She reached into her coat pocket, and her fingers closed around the envelope she had carried for three days now, and for forty years before that in a shoebox under her floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth like something venomous. "After that time, Edith never fed it a stranger again. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Once you've done it to someone with no one to miss them, the next time is easier, and the next, and the faces get closer to home. A hired man in '89. A cousin passing through in '94. By the time it was her own husband's turn, she barely argued with it at all. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. It doesn't just eat the offering. It eats the offerer, a spoonful at a time, until feeding it is just... farming."

She set the envelope on the table between them.

It was yellowed at the edges, soft at the corners from decades of handling. On the front, in Edith's upright, careful hand, was written: *For Lena. When she asks.*

"She wrote this the winter before she died," Ruth said. "She called me—first time in thirty-five years—and asked me to keep it. Said she couldn't trust the house with it. Said the house reads your mail, if you follow my meaning." Ruth pushed it across the wood, an inch at a time, like sliding a coin to a fortune-teller. "I've never opened it. I want you to hear that from me before you hear it from anything else. Forty years of cowardice, but I never opened it. Some part of me knew that once it was read, there'd be no going back to not-knowing. And I have built my entire life on not-knowing, girl. It's load-bearing."

Lena looked at the envelope. Then at Ruth. "Why give it to me now? Why not let me leave?"

"Because the moon's up a night early," Ruth said, "and the clocks in this house have been saying 11:58 since before you were born, and because I am eighty-one years old and I am done running." She stood, joints protesting, and looked down at the last of the Lockridges—or the second-to-last, depending on what the mirror upstairs was doing these days. "And because Edith asked me to give it to you when you asked. And you asked three days ago. And I made you wait because I'm a coward, and I'll answer for that however it comes due."

Lena picked up the envelope. Turned it over. The paper made a dry whisper against her fingers.

"Ruth," she said. "What did she do, the last year? The ledger's final page—there's a blank line. Dated for this moon. But Edith died before it. Did she pay? Did she—"

"That," Ruth said, "is what the letter's for."

She turned for the door, because she could not sit and watch the girl read it, because some things you cannot witness twice in one lifetime. At the threshold she paused, hand on the frame, and said the last true thing she had left:

"When you read it, girl—whatever she tells you in there about your mother—remember that Edith loved you. Remember that first and last. Because the letter's going to hurt, and the hurting's going to make you forget it, and forgetting is how that thing wins even when it loses."

Then Ruth Calloway walked out into the early-risen moonlight, an old woman at last setting down a stone she had carried forty years, lighter and emptier than she had felt since she was fourteen years old—and behind her, in the kitchen, she heard the dry whisper of paper unfolding, and the house seemed to lean in to listen.
