## 3. The Photograph with Her Face

The attic hatch was in the ceiling of the smallest bedroom, the one Hazel had decided to sleep in because its window faced the marsh and the marsh, at least, made no pretense of being friendly. She hadn't planned on opening it. She'd planned on a day of work — the recorder charged, her notebook open to a fresh page, the half-hummed lullaby transcribed from memory before it could fade the way songs did when you tried too hard to hold them.

But the notebook stayed shut. The lullaby wouldn't come.

What came instead was dust. It hung in the morning light that slanted through the landing window, and every time she walked beneath the hatch she felt it settle on her like a fine grey snow, patient, as if the house had been waiting years for someone to disturb it and was now quietly celebrating.

She told herself she was looking for storage space. Blankets, spare bulbs, anything to make the house feel less like a museum and more like a place a person lived. That was the story she gave herself, standing on the wobbly chair with her arms raised, fingers finding the cold brass ring of the hatch cord.

The truth was simpler and worse: she wanted to know who had lived here. Not the village's version — the village gave her nothing but tight mouths and turned shoulders. *Ravensworth House? Oh, it's been empty a long while, love.* Empty. As if emptiness were an answer. As if houses simply emptied themselves, the way cups did, the way lungs did.

She pulled. The hatch swung down with a groan, and the folding ladder unfolded into the small bedroom like something being offered.

Cold air came down to meet her. It smelled of paper and mouse and, faintly, of water — not damp exactly, but the memory of water, the way a shell smells of the sea long after.

Hazel climbed up into the dark.

* * *

The attic ran the full length of the house, low-beamed, lit by two round windows caked with grime so old it had gone opaline. Grey light fell in soft coins across the floorboards. Boxes everywhere — dozens of them, stacked in towers, labeled in a slanted, careful hand: *WINTER CLOTHES. THOMAS'S BOOKS. SCHOOL. CHRISTMAS — DO NOT OPEN BEFORE DECEMBER.*

That last one stopped her. Someone had written *do not open before December* decades ago, and no one had opened it since, and somewhere in the world a December had come and gone without anyone keeping that promise. She stood in front of the box for a long moment, absurdly moved, then made herself turn away. Folklorists learned early: grief kept in boxes was still grief, and it wasn't hers to unwrap.

Not all of it, anyway.

Near the far window, under a sheet gone stiff with dust, stood furniture — a rocking horse, its paint worn to bare wood at the saddle; a child's desk with a single chalk stub in the tray; a hat stand wearing a man's coat, shoulders squared as though he'd only just stepped out of it. And beside the coat, leaning against the beam where someone had placed it carefully, face to the wall:

A picture frame.

Hazel knew before she touched it. Some animal part of her knew, the way you know a stair is missing before your foot finds the gap. Her hand rose anyway. Dust sifted from the frame's top edge. She turned it around.

It was a family portrait, studio-shot, the kind that cost real money in 1974 — she could tell by the clothes, by the grain, by the formal stiffness everyone wore in photographs back then, as if cameras were still slightly holy. A backdrop of painted clouds. A family arranged in a shallow arc.

A mother first. Dark hair pinned up, a face that was handsome rather than pretty, mouth set in the particular line of a woman holding very still so the photograph would go well for the children's sake. Her hand rested on the shoulder of a boy of maybe eight, gap-toothed, squirming even in stillness, caught mid-laugh at some joke the photographer would never hear.

A father next. Tall, thin, glasses that reflected the studio lights as two white coins over his eyes, so that his expression was unreadable — kind or severe or frightened, impossible to say. One hand on the boy's other shoulder. One hand on the head of a smaller child, a girl of five or six with braids and a dress with a collar like a doily, staring directly into the lens with an intensity that children weren't supposed to have. Like she was memorizing the camera. Like she expected it to remember her back.

And there — between the father and the mother, slightly forward of them both, the place of honor —

A young woman.

Nineteen, maybe twenty. Long dark hair loose past her shoulders. A green sweater. Hands folded in front of her, chin lifted, looking at the camera with an expression Hazel recognized instantly because she'd seen it in her own passport photo, her own student ID, her own reflection a thousand times: that slight, skeptical tilt of the head, one eyebrow a fraction higher than the other, the almost-smile of someone who finds the whole business of being photographed faintly ridiculous but is willing to play along.

Hazel's breath went out of her in a slow, controlled stream, the way you breathe when you're trying not to make a sound in a house where something might be listening.

Because the young woman was her.

Not resembled her. Not favored her, the way cousins favor, the way strangers on trains sometimes make you look twice. Was her. The same narrow jaw. The same left ear that sat a little lower than the right — a thing Hazel had hated about herself since she was twelve, a thing no one else on earth had, as far as she knew. The same small scar through the right eyebrow, pale now with age in the photograph but unmistakably the scar Hazel had gotten falling off a wall at seven years old, three hundred miles from Marrowfen, in a town she could name and date.

She looked at the frame. She looked at her own hands, held out in the dusty light, palms up, as if presenting evidence to a court.

The same hands. Younger in the portrait. But the same.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, and the attic took the words and gave nothing back.

* * *

She carried the photograph downstairs the way you carry something sleeping — level, slow, barely breathing. She set it on the kitchen table in the best light and sat across from it and made herself be a professional about it.

Studio portraits had dates. Studios stamped their work; it was vanity and advertising at once. She tilted the frame. In the bottom right corner, embossed into the card mount in faded gold: *H. WILLOWS & SON, PHOTOGRAPHERS, THETFORD. EST. 1931.* And beneath, in pencil, in the same slanted careful hand that had labeled the boxes:

*Ravensworths. Summer '74.*

No name for the young woman. Just *Ravensworths*, plural, as if she were folded into the word like a letter into an envelope.

Hazel got her laptop. She told herself this was research, and for ten minutes it almost worked. Thetford existed; H. Willows & Son had existed, closing in 1988 according to a local history forum; a thread from 2011 mentioned that Willows' negatives had been sold off in lots, most destroyed, some in private collections. Dead end, but a normal dead end, the ordinary friction of archival work, and she clung to it gratefully.

Then she searched *Ravensworth Marrowfen* and got four results.

One was a land registry record, dry as bone: Ravensworth House and associated land, transferred 1975, "consideration paid," to a name she didn't recognize, then a chain of transfers that ended nowhere, the house apparently owned by no one, which was legally impossible and yet there it was.

One was a genealogy page, abandoned mid-build, listing Thomas Ravensworth (b. 1931), Edith Ravensworth née Carrow (b. 1934), and three children: Peter (b. 1966), Rose (b. 1969), and Sam (b. 1972). Three children. The page listed three children and the photograph showed four faces, and Hazel counted them twice, three times, the way you recount money when the total comes out wrong.

The third result was a scanned page from the *Eastern Daily Press*, March 1975, six column inches on an inside page, headline half-cut by the scan: *MARROWFEN FAMIL—* and below it, visible text: *...the tragedy, which claimed the lives of the entire household, has prompted the parish council to...* The rest was behind a paywall that had outlived the newspaper itself.

*Claimed the lives of the entire household.*

Hazel read the sentence until it stopped looking like words. Five people. Five names on a genealogy page, five faces in a photograph, an entire household, and the article was a paragraph long, tucked inside a paper from nine months after the date penciled under the portrait, and no one in the village had said one word about any of it.

The fourth result was a cached image of the churchyard, posted by a walking club, captioned *Marrowfen, St. Brannoc's, interesting stones.* She zoomed in. Behind the lychgate, in the middle distance, a row of headstones leaned together like conspirators, and on the nearest one she could just make out, even at that resolution, a carved word: *RAVENSWORTH.*

She closed the laptop.

The kitchen was very quiet. The tap over the sink dripped once, and she flinched at it as if it had spoken.

"Who are you?" she asked the photograph.

The young woman with her face didn't answer. Of course she didn't. But her eyes — Hazel's eyes, the exact grey-brown, the exact heavy upper lid — held the camera with that same skeptical patience, and the longer Hazel looked the more the question curdled into a different one, colder, closer to the bone:

*If you are me, then what am I?*

* * *

She went to the village because there was nowhere else to put the photograph down.

She carried it in a tote bag, wrapped in a jumper, and she walked the mile into Marrowfen along the raised path between the ditches, reeds hissing on either side, and she told herself she was going for milk and bread, which was true, and also that she was going to ask one simple question, which was truer.

Mrs. Aldery was behind the counter of the shop, as she had been yesterday, sorting tins with the unhurried authority of a woman who had run the same shop for forty years and intended to run it for forty more. She nodded at Hazel. "Milk's in the cooler, love. Bread came this morning."

"Mrs. Aldery." Hazel set the bag on the counter, unwrapped the jumper, and turned the frame to face her. "I found this at the house. I was hoping you could tell me—"

The tins stopped.

Mrs. Aldery looked at the photograph for perhaps two seconds. Hazel watched her face do something complicated and fast — a whole weather system crossing it in the time it took to blink — and then the face closed, door slammed, bolt shot, and what remained was shopkeeper-neutral and immovable.

"I couldn't say," Mrs. Aldery said.

"It's dated 1974. It says Ravensworths. I'm staying at their house, and this woman—" Hazel tapped the glass above the young woman's face, and her finger shook, and she hated that it shook, "—this woman has my face. My actual face. There's a scar, Mrs. Aldery. Through the eyebrow. I've had it since I was seven."

"Aye, well." Mrs. Aldery began restacking tins that did not need restacking. "Folk used to say the Ravensworths kept to themselves. Good family. Quiet. That's all I know."

"You must know more than that. There was an article. Nineteen seventy-five. An entire household died."

The tin in Mrs. Aldery's hand went down onto the shelf with a click too loud for its weight. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious at being wet, an old woman's anger at her own body for betraying her in front of a stranger.

"You'll want to mind the ditches on your walk home," she said. "They flood quick this time of year. That'll be a pound forty for the milk when you've got it."

Hazel stood there a moment longer. The shop smelled of paraffin and newsprint and bread. Somewhere in the back, a radio murmured a phone-in program to an audience of nobody.

"Was she called Rose?" Hazel asked quietly. "The girl in the photograph?"

Mrs. Aldery picked up her cloth and wiped a clean stretch of counter, over and over, and did not answer, and did not look up, and Hazel understood with terrible clarity that she was not being refused information. She was being refused the act of speaking. Whatever the words were, they had been locked away so long and so hard that opening the door was the one thing the village had agreed, together, never to do.

She bought her milk. She left the photograph on the counter for one last second — *look at her, please, someone look at her* — and Mrs. Aldery looked instead at the clock, and Hazel wrapped the frame back in the jumper and carried it home.

At the door of the post office, old Mr. Tice was sweeping the step, and he straightened as she passed, and for one moment — she would swear this later, swear it to herself in the dark — his eyes went to the shape of the frame in her bag, and his broom stopped, and his lips moved around a word he swallowed whole.

Everyone in Marrowfen knew. Everyone in Marrowfen had agreed.

* * *

The light was failing when she got back to the house. She didn't turn on the lamps. She set the photograph on the kitchen table again, in the last grey wash of daylight through the window, and sat down opposite it, and this time she didn't ask questions.

She looked.

The mother, Edith — the genealogy page had given her that much — with her hand on her son's shoulder, holding him steady against the world. The father, Thomas, his eyes hidden behind twin coins of studio light. Peter, laughing. Sam, the little one with the braids, staring into the lens like she meant to be remembered.

And the young woman. Herself. Standing in the place of honor, front and center, the way families positioned the child they were proudest of, or the guest they were honoring, or—

Or the daughter they had lost, Hazel thought, and the thought arrived fully formed, cold as well water. *Or the daughter whose place they kept. Families did that. Families left a chair empty, a coat on a hook, a space in the photograph. No. Not in the photograph. You can't leave a space in a photograph unless—*

Unless the photograph had been altered. Unless the young woman had been added later, printed in, pasted in — she leaned close, hunting for seams, for a halo of mismatched glue, for the lie that would let her breathe again. The grain matched. The light on the green sweater matched the light on Edith's blouse. The shadow the young woman cast fell correctly across the painted clouds behind her.

There was no seam. There was no lie. There was only a family of five, photographed in the summer of 1974, containing a woman with her face, her scar, her crooked left ear, her skeptical tilt of the chin — photographed months before Hazel Ravensworth, folklorist, thirty-one years old, born in Norwich to a mother who had never heard of Marrowfen, existed at all.

The drip in the kitchen sounded again. Once. Twice.

Hazel became aware, slowly, the way you become aware of a sound you've been hearing for a while without registering it, that the house was not silent. Underneath the drip, underneath the wind working at the eaves, there was a hum — very low, felt in the floorboards more than heard, rising up through the soles of her feet and the legs of the chair and the bones of her sitting. Not machinery. The house had no machinery. It was more like a note held on an organ, far away, in another room, in another year.

The hum swelled, almost imperceptibly, and settled.

As if the house had exhaled. As if it had been waiting, all these hours, for her to bring the photograph down out of the attic and set it on the table where the family used to eat, and having gotten what it wanted, it could relax.

Hazel sat very still. The daylight drained out of the window. In the glass, faintly, the kitchen reflected back at her — the table, the frame, her own shape hunched over it — and she noticed, distantly, that her reflection seemed to be sitting a fraction straighter than she was.

She decided she was tired. She decided it was the light.

She reached across the table and turned the photograph face-down, gently, the way you'd turn away from something sleeping, and went upstairs to count the doors.

Eleven. Always eleven.

Behind her, in the dark kitchen, the hum went on — and if you had been listening, really listening, the way Hazel was learning to listen, you might have heard that it wasn't a hum at all.

It was a tune. Half of one.

The half the old woman in the lane had refused to finish.
