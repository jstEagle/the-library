## 4. One Second Late

The mirror in the hallway was the first to lie to her.

Hazel noticed it on her fourth morning at Ravensworth House, in the grey hour before the kettle finished boiling, when she passed the tall Victorian glass with its foxed silver and its carved frame of oak leaves. She had walked past it a dozen times already that week without looking — mirrors were furniture to her, functional things, like taps and door handles. But something made her glance up as she crossed the hall, a mug of tea in one hand, hair still wet from the shower.

Her reflection was still standing by the stairs.

She stopped. The reflection stopped too — a full stride behind where she stood, tea raised halfway to lips she hadn't sipped from yet. Then it caught up, the way a video catches up when a stutter clears, snapping into place so smoothly that for a moment she doubted what she'd seen.

*Bad sleep,* she told herself. *Old house. Bad light.*

But she stood there another thirty seconds, watching, and the woman in the glass watched her back, and they moved together, perfectly, and there was nothing wrong with the mirror at all except that Hazel could not shake the feeling that it had been caught doing something and had now corrected itself.

She drank her tea. She went upstairs to make notes on the lullaby fragment — six bars of melody, half a verse, the old woman's voice cracking on the word *down* before she'd pressed her hands over her mouth and refused to give another note. Hazel transcribed what she had, hummed it, wrote *incomplete — return to Mrs. Aldery* in the margin, and did not think about the mirror again until evening.

That was when she realized it wasn't just the hallway.

* * *

There were four mirrors in Ravensworth House that she'd found so far: the tall hall glass, a round vanity mirror in the main bedroom, a small rectangular one above the bathroom sink, and a hand mirror with a cracked ivory handle that lay in a drawer of the writing desk like something put away rather than left. On the fourth evening, washing her face, she looked up water dripping from her chin and saw herself still bent over the basin, eyes closed, hands cupping water that had already run down the plughole.

She straightened slowly. The reflection straightened slowly, a beat behind, like a dancer counting under her breath.

Hazel gripped the edge of the sink. Her knuckles whitened; the reflection's knuckles whitened a second later, an echo, a copy arriving late to its own life.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud had always been how she took the terror out of them. Folklore was full of mirrors — covered at deaths, turned at funerals, never looked into after dark in half the counties of England. She knew the catalogue cold. *The soul lingers in the glass. The dead look back. Break a mirror and you break seven years.* None of it had ever frightened her before, because none of it had ever been about the mechanics of reflection being *wrong*. Mirrors in folklore were windows. This was something else. This was a mirror that had forgotten its job.

She tested it. Raised her right hand. The reflection raised its right hand — no, wait. She watched carefully this time, and it wasn't a delay of thought or imagination. It was measurable. She counted in her head, the way you count seconds between lightning and thunder. One. The hand came up on *one*. A full second behind her.

She went room to room with the methodical calm she used for fieldwork, the calm that was really just fear wearing a cardigan. Hallway: one second late. Bedroom vanity: one second late. Bathroom: one second late. The little hand mirror: she held it up and her own face looked back at her from 1970s silvering, and it was late too, and worse — when she lowered it, she had the distinct, sickening impression that the face in it had kept looking at her a moment longer than faces are supposed to.

By ten o'clock she had written three pages in her notebook, most of it crossed out. What survived read:

*All reflective surfaces lag approx. 1 sec. Consistent across rooms. Not my perception — verified by counting against watch sweep. Reflections accurate but delayed. EXCEPT —*

The *except* was the thing she couldn't make herself write down plainly, because writing it would make it data, and data was real.

Here is what happened: at quarter past eleven, passing the hall mirror on her way to bed, she saw the reflection raise its arm and point — not at her, but past her, toward the kitchen door, which stood shut at the end of the hall. She had not raised her arm. She had not pointed anywhere. She stood frozen in the middle of the hall while her own face regarded her with an expression she did not recognize and could not name — not menace, nothing so simple — something more like *impatience*, the way you look at someone who is slow to understand.

Then the reflection lowered its arm, caught up to her actual body, and became ordinary again: tired woman, wet hair, cardigan, going to bed.

Hazel slept with the lights on. All of them. It was a habit she'd carried since childhood without knowing why — every bulb burning, doors counted — and for the first time she wondered where the habit had come from, because it fit this house like a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Eleven doors. She counted them again from her bed, reciting their locations like a rosary: front, back, pantry, cellar, three bedroom doors, bathroom, the linen cupboard, the study, the attic hatch. Eleven. Always eleven. She fell asleep mid-count and dreamed of water rising up through floorboards, warm as bathwater, gentle as a hand.

* * *

In the morning the pointing was easier to dismiss than the delay. Daylight did its work. She made coffee, opened the curtains, and decided — firmly, the way you decide things you're afraid won't hold — that she had imagined the arm. Sleep deprivation, an overactive folklorist's imagination, a house that creaked and settled and got into the blood. She had a job to do. She had half a lullaby and a village that clammed up whenever she said the name Ravensworth, and she would get further with Mrs. Aldery today than she would standing in hallways interrogating antique glass.

Mrs. Aldery gave her nothing. Worse than nothing — she gave her fear, served with tea Hazel was afraid to drink, in a cottage that smelled of lavender laid over something older. When Hazel described the house — casually, conversationally, *I'm staying up at Ravensworth House, lovely old place* — the old woman's hands had gone still around her cup, and she had said only: "Do you keep the lights on?"

"I— yes. Why?"

"Keep them on." And then, when Hazel pressed — *why, what happens in the dark, what happened to the family, why does no one say their name* — Mrs. Aldery had stood, with the terrible slow dignity of the very old, and shown her to the door. At the threshold she'd paused, back turned, and said: "Whatever looks at you from the glass, girl — don't answer it."

Hazel walked home through the fen in a red evening, telling herself the woman was senile, telling herself the whole village was senile, telling herself stories the way she always did, building explanations like sandbags against a flood. By the time she reached the gate of Ravensworth House the light was nearly gone and the house stood black against the last orange band of sky, and every window reflected the sunset a moment after the sky itself had finished burning, so that the house seemed to glow with light that had already ended.

She stood at the gate and watched that, and understood she was watching the delay, and understood that it was getting worse, or that she was simply seeing it everywhere now, the way you see a word everywhere once someone has said it to you.

Inside, she did not go near the hall mirror. She cooked pasta in the kitchen with her back to the dark window over the sink, and ate standing up, and washed up fast, and kept her eyes on her hands.

It was almost midnight when she heard the humming.

* * *

It came from the direction of the hall — low, tuneless at first, and then resolving into the six bars she'd transcribed, the half-verse, the forbidden melody, sung softly and slightly wrong, the way you sing something you learned by ear from someone else's memory of it. Hazel stood in the kitchen doorway with her heart going like a trapped bird and listened, and the voice was thin and childlike and patient, and it was coming from nowhere, because the hall was empty.

Empty except for the mirror.

She made herself walk down there. She made herself stand in front of the tall glass with the lights blazing all around it, and look.

Her reflection looked back, one second late, and behind her reflection — faint, silvery, like breath on cold glass — something else stood in the mirrored hall. Small. Child-height. Its face was smooth as an egg where features should have been, not blank exactly but *unfinished*, a sketch of a person waiting for the artist to come back. And it was humming. She could see the mouth move — the suggestion of a mouth — opening and closing in time with the melody, and the sound arrived in the room a half-beat after the lips parted, sound lagging behind image, everything in this house arriving late, everything delayed, everything *owed*.

The child-thing in the mirror raised its hand. Not pointing this time. Waving. Slowly. The way children wave goodbye from train windows.

Hazel ran.

She didn't decide to run; her body simply removed her from the hall, and she found herself in the kitchen with the poker from the fireplace in her hand and no memory of picking it up. She stood shaking, listening to the house tick and settle, and the fury that rose in her then surprised her with its heat — fury at the house, at the village, at Mrs. Aldery and her *don't answer it*, at the whole conspiracy of silence that had put her alone in this place with a mirror full of unfinished children.

She went back into the hall with the poker held like a weapon, which is to say badly, and she swung it into the tall glass with everything she had.

The crack was enormous, a gunshot in the narrow hall. The mirror burst into a spiderweb and then into falling pieces, silver and glass raining across the floorboards, and for one instant — she would swear this later, swear it on anything — the falling fragments each held a piece of the child's face, dozens of small smooth faces tumbling through the air, and then they hit the floor and were only shards, only broken mirror, only mess.

Hazel stood breathing hard in the wreckage. The hall felt different immediately — lighter, airier, as though the house had been holding its breath and now let it out. The silence where the humming had been was total.

"Right," she said to the empty house, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "Right."

She swept up the glass, wrapped the big pieces in newspaper, bagged the splinters, and worked until two in the morning until the hearth was swept and the floorboards were clean and there was no mirror in the hall at all, only the pale rectangle of unfaded wallpaper where it had hung for a hundred years. She noted the time in her book. She wrote: *Destroyed hall glass, 1:40 a.m. Will monitor others.* Underneath, her handwriting went shaky: *It waved at me. Why did it wave at me?*

She slept badly, lights on, doors counted twice, the poker leaned against the nightstand within reach.

* * *

She woke to sunlight and the smell of the house — dust and old wood and, beneath it, that faint mineral coolness she'd noticed her first day and never placed, like the air over deep water. She lay in bed cataloguing the night, sorting dream from event, and had gotten as far as the kettle when she remembered the mirror and went downstairs braced for the pale rectangle, the proof, the thing she could point to and say *this happened, I did this, I am sane.*

The mirror was hanging on the wall.

Whole. Unbroken. The oak-leaf frame undamaged, the silvered glass flawless, reflecting the morning hall with perfect fidelity — her own astonished face arriving a full second after she made it.

Hazel backed away until she hit the newel post. She looked at the wallpaper. No pale rectangle. No outline, no shadow of a century's hanging, no seam in the paper. As if the mirror had never been anywhere else. As if the wall had grown it overnight, the way a tree grows a knot.

Her legs took her to the kitchen. In the bin, under the coffee grounds, the newspaper bundles were there — she pulled one out with trembling hands and unwrapped it, and inside was newspaper, folded around nothing, clean and empty and creased as if it had always been empty, as if glass had never touched it.

She sat down hard on one of the kitchen chairs. The window over the sink showed her the garden, the grey-green fen beyond, the sky doing its ordinary business. In the window's reflection, a second behind, a woman sat down at a table in a kitchen, and Hazel watched her do it, watched herself arrive late into her own morning.

"It's counting," she said aloud, to nobody, to the house. Her voice sounded very small. "Something in here is counting."

And beneath her feet — so faint she might have imagined it, so faint she would spend the rest of the day failing to imagine it away — she heard, or felt, or sensed, a sound like water moving in a deep and patient dark, far below the kitchen floor.

Rising.

She got her coat. She got her recorder and her notebook. There was one person in Marrowfen who had started to tell her the truth before stopping, and Hazel was done accepting stops. Whatever Mrs. Aldery knew about mirrors and lights and names nobody spoke, Hazel was going to stand in that lavender-smelling cottage until the old woman gave it up — because last night a child with no face had waved at her from inside her own reflection, and this morning the house had healed its wound and pretended nothing happened, and pretending was a language the whole village spoke fluently.

At the front door she paused, one hand on the latch, and made the mistake of glancing back down the hall.

The mirror showed her the hall, and the door, and a woman with a coat on, leaving.

It showed her that a full second before she took a single step.
