## 7. What the Village Buried

The old woman's name was Agnes Pell, and she lived at the end of the lane where the fen began, in a cottage with a low door and a garden that grew nothing but rosemary. Hazel had learned her name from the shopkeeper, who had learned it from his mother, and who had also learned — and this was the part he delivered with the particular flatness of a man repeating something drilled into him — that Agnes Pell did not like visitors, did not like questions, and above all did not like being asked about the song.

Hazel went anyway. She was beginning to understand that everything in Marrowfen worked that way: a rule stated plainly, with the expectation that it would be obeyed, and no explanation offered, because explanation was itself a kind of breaking.

She walked out at mid-morning, under a sky the color of dishwater, with her recorder in her coat pocket and her hands buried deep against the wind. The lane was a raised causeway across the black water, and the fen on either side was perfectly still. Not calm — still. There was a difference. Calm water moved when you looked at it. This water held itself like a held breath, and Hazel caught herself walking faster, and then caught herself slowing down again, because hurrying was a kind of admission.

The chalk tally was still growing. She had checked it that morning, third day in the house, before she'd let herself make tea, before she'd let herself do anything. Eleven doors counted the night before — eleven, always eleven — and the tally on the pantry wall now read six. Six strokes, the last one chalk-bright, the other five gone gray and soft with fifty years of kitchen steam. She had stood in front of it with her tea going cold and told herself the reasonable explanations in order of plausibility: damp, old chalk, a child's game, a coincidence of arithmetic. She had gotten as far as *someone has been in this house* before she'd made herself stop, because the next thought after that one had a shape she didn't want to look at.

*Six. And I am the sixth thing in this house that breathes.*

So she went to find Agnes Pell, because the alternative was to stay in Ravensworth House alone with that thought, and she was not yet desperate enough for that. Not quite.

* * *

The cottage door opened before Hazel knocked. Agnes stood in the gap, small and dry as a winter stalk, her white hair pinned flat, her eyes the pale washed-out blue of old porcelain. She looked at Hazel the way you look at weather you've been told is coming.

"You're the one at Ravensworth House," she said.

"I am. Hazel Ravensworth. I'm a folklorist — I wrote to the parish council about the—"

"I know what you are." Agnes didn't move from the doorway. Behind her the cottage was dim and smelled of woodsmoke and something else, something green and cold, like the fen had gotten in. "I sang you half a song in the churchyard and I've been sorry every day since. You shouldn't have come here. You shouldn't have stayed there."

"The house was the only—"

"I know that too." Something moved behind her eyes, some old arithmetic finishing itself. "That's how it gets you. It makes itself the only option. It's done that for a long time." She stepped back from the door, which was not an invitation, exactly, but was not a refusal either. "Well. Come in, then, since you're going to stand there until you drop. You've got the look of a woman who won't be put off, and I haven't the strength for a siege."

The cottage interior was one low room and a kitchen beyond. On the mantelpiece, among the ordinary clutter of a long life — photographs, a clock that had stopped, a horse brass — Hazel saw a small framed picture and went still.

It was a family portrait. 1970s stock, the colors gone amber. A mother with dark hair and a tired, watchful face. A father with his hand on a boy's shoulder. Three children arranged by height. And at the end of the row, slightly apart, a young woman of perhaps nineteen, with Hazel's face — the same face Hazel had found in the attic of Ravensworth House, staring back at her from a photograph she had no memory of being taken.

"Where did you get this?" Hazel asked. Her voice came out wrong, too level.

"It was given to me," Agnes said. "At the funeral. Everyone who came was given one. The village wanted to remember them, and then the village spent fifty years trying to forget them, and the photographs were the first thing to go. I kept mine because somebody had to." She looked at Hazel looking at it. "You've seen the one in the house. In the attic. I can tell by your face. You have her face, you know. The girl's. Rose."

"Rose," Hazel repeated. The name sat strangely in her mouth, familiar the way a word gets familiar when you've been saying it in dreams.

"Sit down," Agnes said. "I'm going to make tea, and while I make it I'm going to decide how much to tell you, and you're going to let me decide, because I'm old and it's my house. And then you're going to ask your questions anyway, because you've got the look, so we may as well skip to it."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way people tell things they have rehearsed in silence for decades — no ornament, no drama, each sentence set down like a stone in a wall.

"The Ravensworths came to that house in 1961. Thomas and Edith, and the children — Peter, the eldest, then the twins, Sam and Alice, and the baby was coming. Rose, she was to be. Born in the February of '74, and she didn't live the day. Cord round the neck. The midwife did everything right; it was just one of those things, the doctor said. Just one of those things." Agnes set the tea down with a click. "Edith didn't say it was one of those things. Edith said the house took her. Said she'd heard it under the kitchen floor — water, rising. Said the house had counted the family and come up one short, and it doesn't like coming up short."

"The house counted," Hazel said.

"You've seen the tally."

Hazel said nothing. Agnes nodded, as if the silence were an answer, which it was.

"After Rose died, Edith started counting them out loud. Every night. Five names, five doors, lights on. The children thought it was a game. The children always think it's a game, that's the mercy of children. And for a while — a year, maybe more — it held. Whatever it is under that house, it counts, and if the count is true it stays settled. Edith understood that before any of us. She understood it and we laughed at her. Called her strange. Kept our children from hers." Agnes's hands were wrapped around her own cup, knuckles pale. "That's the first thing this village buried. Not the family. The warning. Edith Ravensworth told us what the house was and we decided she was mad, because it was easier, and because the alternative was to admit we'd known about the well for a hundred years and done nothing."

"The well," Hazel said. "Under the kitchen."

"You've heard it." It wasn't a question. Hazel's silence again, and again Agnes took it for the answer it was, and something in her old face seemed to give way, like a bank of earth finally sliding. "Then you know. Then I don't have to tell you and I will anyway, because you need to hear it from a living mouth, from someone who was there, or you'll start telling yourself it's your imagination, and the imagination is exactly what it uses."

She told it. The spring of 1974, a year after Rose, a wet spring, the fen high. Thomas Ravensworth went down into the kitchen one morning in April and the floor was wet. Not a leak — wet, all of it, the flagstones weeping, and beneath them, when he pried one up with a crowbar, a shaft. A well, old, older than the house, older than the village record, brick-lined and black, and the water in it rising. Not fast. Patient. The way water rises when it has all the time in the world.

"They tried to fill it," Agnes said. "Gravel, rubble, a lorry-load of concrete. By the autumn it was clear again. They tried to move out. The house — " she paused, and chose her words with visible care, " — the house made itself difficult to leave. Doors that wouldn't stay shut. The van wouldn't start, three mornings running, and then the fourth morning it started fine and they'd unpacked the van and stayed, and none of them could say why, afterward, when there was an afterward to ask. And all the while the water rose. Slowly. An inch a month, Edith said. An inch a month is nothing, we told her. An inch a month is a tide, she told us, and tides come in."

"And in October," Hazel said. Her mouth was dry. She didn't know how she knew the month. She knew it the way she knew the house's corridors, the way she'd known which stair creaked on her first night, the way she'd known to leave the kitchen light on.

"October the ninth," Agnes said. "The water came up through the floor in the night. All of them. Edith, Thomas, Peter, Sam, Alice. The house was full of water and then it wasn't, and when the village got in — we got in, I was there, I was twenty-six — there was no water and no family. Just the well, open, and the water in it black and calm, and on the pantry wall, in chalk, in a child's hand, a tally of five."

The tea was cold in Hazel's hands. She hadn't drunk any of it.

"We searched the fen for a month," Agnes went on. "Dragged the black water. Found one shoe. Alice's, they thought. And then we buried the shoe, because you bury what you have, and at the funeral — " and here her voice did something Hazel had never heard a voice do, a kind of buckling, a load-bearing wall giving way — "at the funeral, old Mother Cade stood up and said we'd sing them home, the way you sing a child home, the way Edith used to sing to them at night. And we sang the lullaby. And then, God forgive us, someone — I don't remember who, I've spent fifty years trying not to remember who — someone started it again. Because once felt too small. Because grief is greedy. Because we wanted to do it *properly*."

"Twice," Hazel said.

"Twice." Agnes set her cup down. Her hand was not steady. "Once to sleep, twice to wake. That's what the song is. Edith wrote it — did you know that? Not an old song, not a fen song. Edith wrote it herself, for her children, and she wrote it the way you'd build a fence. Each verse a number, each verse a name, each verse a door shut. Sung once, it settles the count. Sung twice — " She stopped. Looked at the cold fireplace as if the words might be written there. "Sung twice it's not a lullaby anymore. It's a roll call. It's the house answering *present*."

"The second singing wakes the count," Hazel said slowly. "And the count — takes."

"The count collects," Agnes said. "That's the word Edith used, in the letter she left. *Collected.* Like a debt. Like a harvest." The old woman's eyes came up and fixed on Hazel's face, and Hazel watched her look at Rose's face looking back out of it, and watched her flinch. "The village hasn't sung it twice since. We don't sing it at all, most of us. Half a hum in the churchyard and then I stop, and I stop because my mother grabbed my wrist so hard she bruised it, at that funeral, when the second verse started up again. *Once is a kindness,* she said. *Twice is a summons.* And I have kept that rule my whole life, and I have kept my mouth shut my whole life, and now you've come, with her face, into that house, and the tally on my conscience has started adding again."

* * *

The wind had come up while they talked, and the fen beyond the window had finally broken its stillness — small waves, worried, moving. Hazel stood at the mantelpiece, looking at the photograph, at Rose's face, at her own face, and asked the question she had been carrying since the attic like a stone in her shoe.

"Who was she? Rose. In the photograph — she's not a child, she's nineteen, but the family — "

"She was the cousin," Agnes said. "Edith's sister's girl. Came to stay the summer of '73, to help with the little ones, and stayed on. Sweet thing. Quiet. Used to sing with Edith in the kitchen, two voices, the song going round and round like a rope being turned." Agnes's voice dropped. "She was in the house that night too. We counted six graves' worth of searching. We found nothing of her at all. Not a shoe. The well took her clean, and I have sometimes thought — God forgive me — that it took her first, and best, because she was the one who helped sing the count."

Hazel's hand was on the mantelpiece. The wood was cold, colder than the room, cold in the specific way that Ravensworth House was cold, and she took her hand away.

"I'm not her," she said. "I'd know. Wouldn't I? I'd know if I were — if I was — " The sentence wouldn't finish. Fifty years, a life, a degree, a flat in Leeds, a mother who had died when Hazel was small and a father who had never spoken of his family, never, not once, and she had always thought it was grief. She had always thought it was grief. "I have no memory of this house. I have no memory of any of them."

"No," Agnes said. "And that's the thing that should frighten you most of all. Because the house doesn't need your memory, Hazel. Memory is yours. The house only needs your *name*. It counts names. It counts what it can call, and what it can call, it can keep." She rose, stiff, and took the photograph off the mantelpiece, and pressed it into Hazel's hands with a force that had nothing old in it. "Take it. Take it and go home, wherever home is, and don't sing that song, not once, not half of it, not a hum. And if the water starts — "

"It's already started," Hazel said. "The taps. They run warm. And last night the well — I heard it. Under the kitchen floor."

Agnes closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked, for the first time, not old but ancient, a woman standing at the end of a very long debt.

"Then it's not waiting for you to leave," she said. "It's counting you in."

* * *

Hazel walked back along the causeway with the photograph inside her coat, against her chest, and the wind pushing at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades, hurrying her, hurrying her home.

The fen lay flat and black on either side, and in the middle of the afternoon, in the middle of the wind, she heard it — faint, carried across the water from the direction of the village, from the direction of the house: a tune. Simple. Rising and falling. A lullaby, hummed by a voice that was not a voice, in a throat that was a house, and it hummed the first verse, and stopped.

And waited.

It was waiting for the second verse. It was waiting for her to sing it back. And Hazel understood, walking the last half mile with the photograph over her heart, that Agnes had told her everything except the one thing that mattered, the thing the old woman had perhaps not let herself know:

The house wasn't waiting for the village to sing.

It had a singer already. It had her.

And tonight, when she counted the doors, she was afraid — she was almost certain — there would be twelve.
