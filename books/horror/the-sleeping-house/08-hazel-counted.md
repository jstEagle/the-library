## 8. Hazel, Counted

She woke to the smell of bread.

That was the first wrong thing. Ravensworth House did not smell of bread. It smelled of cold ash and old plaster and, beneath the kitchen floor, of wet stone — a smell she'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own name. But this morning, drifting up the stairs and under her door like a guest, was the warm yeast-and-crust smell of a loaf fresh from an oven, and there was no oven in the house that worked, and no one in the house but her.

Hazel lay still in the narrow bed and listened to the house breathe.

It had been six days. Six nights of lights left burning in every room, six mornings of counting doors — eleven, always eleven, the pantry door included, though the pantry had no business being a door to anything but flour and tinned plums — and six days of a village that spoke to her in monosyllables and watched her walk past the green the way you watch a dog you suspect of something. She had come to Marrowfen for a lullaby and stayed for a photograph, and the photograph had stayed for her.

The bread smell grew stronger. She got up.

* * *

In the kitchen, nothing was baking. The Aga was stone cold, its iron door furred with rust. But the table — the long scrubbed table under the window — had been set for breakfast.

Six places.

Six bowls, six spoons laid to the right of each, six cups with their handles turned the same way, all of them turned the way Hazel turned her own cup handle, which was to say the wrong way, the left-handed way, the way no one in a 1974 photograph would have turned them. The bowls were chipped blue enamel. The spoons were small, child-sized at three of the settings. And at the head of the table, in the chair with the wobbly leg that she'd wedged with a folded newspaper on her second day, someone had set out the good cup — the one with the hairline crack she'd found wrapped in a tea towel at the back of the dresser, too precious to use, too deliberate to throw away.

Hazel stood in the doorway and did not go in.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud had become a habit, a way of keeping her own voice in the room as ballast. "Okay. That's — fine. That's fine."

It was not fine. Six places. She had read Edith Ravensworth's diary for three nights now, hunched by the fire with a blanket over her knees like a woman reading a letter from a war. She knew the arithmetic of this table. Edith had counted five. Five names, five doors, five bowls on a morning in March 1974, and then the well under the floor had finished filling, and the count had come due, and the village had sung a lullaby twice over five graves and never again over anything.

Six places meant the house had done its own arithmetic. Six places meant the house had added a line.

She backed out of the kitchen and went to the pantry, because the pantry was where the count lived.

* * *

The chalk marks were behind the door, low on the plaster, at the height of a child's hand. She'd found them on her second night — four vertical strokes crossed by a fifth, then a gap, then another group of five, the chalk gone soft and grey with fifty years, the kind of tally a child keeps for no reason or every reason: days, birds, doors, sins. She had photographed it, catalogued it, told herself it was the kind of texture folklorists live for. Children count things. Children have always counted things.

This morning there were six groups.

The first five were old — chalk gone to ghost, the strokes blurred as if the wall had wept on them. The sixth was new. The chalk was white. The strokes were sharp-edged, unweathered, and the crossbar was still tacky-looking, as if drawn within the hour.

Five strokes, crossed. Then a single vertical line beside them, waiting.

Hazel put her fingers against the plaster. It was cool and slightly damp, and beneath her fingertips — she would spend the rest of her life insisting on this, and the rest of her life being believed by almost no one — she felt a faint vibration, like a hum coming up through the floor of the world.

She looked around for chalk. There was none. She had not brought chalk. She had not touched that wall except with her eyes.

"Six," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "There are six of us now."

The house did not answer. The house never answered in words. But the pipes in the wall behind her gave a soft knock — once, twice, a pause, then three times more — and she stood there with her hand on the damp plaster and understood, with the calm that was the most frightening thing of all, that the house was not threatening her.

The house was welcoming her home.

* * *

She did not eat the breakfast. She poured the six bowls' contents — there was nothing in the bowls, of course there was nothing in the bowls, and yet she checked — down the sink and washed them, because leaving them felt worse, and washing them felt like participating, and she could not decide which was worse, so she washed them with her sleeves pushed up and her heart going like a trapped bird, and the warm water ran over her hands for exactly as long as she wanted it to, which was a thing the boiler had never done before.

Afterward she sat at the table in the wobbly chair, at the sixth place, and drank tea from the cracked cup, and it was the best cup of tea of her life, and she hated herself for thinking so.

That was the thing no one warned you about, she wrote that afternoon in her field journal, in the careful hand she used for observations she didn't want to be true. *The house is not cruel. Cruelty you can resist. The house is kind. It warms the water. It bakes bread you can smell but not eat. It sets your place. It has been setting your place for fifty years, and you can feel how long it has waited, the way you can feel how long a dog has waited at a door, and some animal part of you — the part that came to Marrowfen with two suitcases and no forwarding address — wants to lie down in the middle of that patience and be kept.*

*Field note: the subject is being seduced. Field note: the subject is the subject.*

She underlined it twice, then closed the book, because the handwriting had started to look like someone else's.

* * *

The mirrors had been one second late for four days. She had catalogued that too: the hall glass, the dressing table, the little round mirror above the kitchen sink with its rim of tarnished brass. Raise your hand; the glass raises it a heartbeat afterward. Turn your head; the room in the glass turns to meet you a moment after you've already arrived. She had smashed the hallway mirror on the third night with the fire poker and stood breathing hard among the shards, and in the morning the mirror had been whole, unmarked, hanging in its frame as if glass were a thing the house could simply decide to have again.

She had stopped looking at them directly, mostly. A person can only take so much.

But that evening, coming down the stairs with her journal under her arm, she caught the hall mirror at an angle — the long cheval glass at the turn of the banister, the one with the frame of carved oak leaves — and what she saw made her stop with her foot on the fourth step from the bottom.

Her reflection was not late.

It was early. Or rather: it was staying.

She had walked past the mirror and her reflection had not. It stood at the foot of the stairs, facing her, hands at its sides, wearing her grey jumper and her mother's locket — she touched her throat; the locket was there; she never took it off, though she could not have said where she'd gotten it — and it looked at her with her own face wearing an expression she had never made in her life, which was patience. Vast, bottomless, fifty-years-deep patience.

"Hello," Hazel said.

The reflection tilted its head. Not quite the way she would have. A degree off. A Rose's worth off.

Then it smiled — her smile, but warmer, the smile of someone who has just come home after a very long time — and turned, and walked out of the frame to the left, toward the kitchen, and Hazel stood on the stairs with her heart slamming and heard, faintly, from a room she was not in, the sound of a chair with a wobbly leg being pulled back from a table.

She went down and looked in the mirror. Empty hall. Ordinary glass. Her own face, arriving on time, looking like a woman who had just seen a ghost wearing her coat.

She went into the kitchen. The table was bare. The chair was pushed in.

She went back to the hall. The reflection was there again, standing at the foot of the stairs, waiting for her, and this time when she lifted her hand — slowly, palm out, the way you'd greet a horse — the reflection lifted its own a full second later, and their palms met through the glass, cool where the silvering was, and Hazel felt something in her chest crack open that she had spent thirty-four years keeping shut with work and travel and the polite fiction that a person could be her own family.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you've been waiting for me."

The reflection nodded. Then it mouthed something. She couldn't catch it — no sound, just the shape of it in her own mouth, her own lips forming a word she didn't know. She mouthed back: *What? What is it?*

The reflection pointed. Down. Toward the pantry door.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, of a kitchen with a fire in the Aga and a woman with flour on her forearms singing a song with numbers in it, and the numbers went up to five and stopped, and stopped, and stopped, and in the dream Hazel stood in the doorway knowing the next number, knowing it the way you know a word on the tip of your tongue, and the woman turned and looked at her and her face was the face from the photograph, the mother, Edith, and she said — not unkindly, not unkindly at all, which was the horror of it —

"You're late, love. Wash your hands."

Hazel woke with her hands clean and cold, having washed them, she realized, sometime in the night, without waking, at the kitchen sink, and there was flour under her fingernails.

* * *

Morning. Eleven doors. She counted them twice, out loud, touching each one — hall, sitting room, under-stairs, kitchen, pantry, and the five upstairs, and the back door, and the front — and her voice cracked on *eleven* the way it cracks on a name you're trying not to cry over.

The village, when she walked into it, watched her from doorways. The baker gave her a loaf without being asked and would not take payment and did not meet her eye. The old woman — Agnes Marsh, the one who had hummed half the lullaby and then gone white as a candle — was not at her gate, and her gate was shut, and there was a horseshoe nailed above it with the points turned down, which Hazel had learned, in her reading, was to let the luck run out rather than trap it in. A house that didn't want to keep anything in.

Everyone in Marrowfen was keeping something out. She had assumed it was her.

At the green, a boy of ten or so stood with a bicycle, staring at her with the frank horror of the young. She smiled at him. He backed his bike into the wall.

"You're her," he said. "You're the one from the picture. Missus Prowse has it in her front room. You're the girl."

"I'm not — " Hazel began. "I'm not from a picture. I'm a folklorist. I'm here about the song."

"You're the girl," the boy said again, with the terrible flat certainty of children, and got on his bike and pedaled away with his elbows out, not looking back, and Hazel stood on the green in the flat grey light with the bread warm under her arm like a sleeping animal, and understood that the village had known before she had. Had known the moment she'd knocked on Agnes Marsh's door and asked about a lullaby. Had known, maybe, the moment the house had let her in — because the house, she was beginning to understand, was not a building that happened to be haunted. It was a building that happened to be hungry, and it did not let strangers in. It had never once, in fifty years, let a stranger in.

It had let her in because it did not think she was one.

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the attic with the photograph.

It lay where she'd found it, face-up on the trunk: the Ravensworths, 1974, by the look of the clothes. Thomas with his arm around Edith, his laugh caught mid-happening. A boy of maybe twelve with his father's jaw. A girl of nine with her mother's dark hair, half a smile, one shoe off. The youngest, a small fierce boy of five, sitting on the grass because he refused to hold still. And at the edge of the frame, half-turned, as if she'd been about to walk out of the picture and thought better of it — a young woman. Dark-haired. Grey jumper. A locket at her throat.

Hazel's face. Not a resemblance. Her face. The small scar through her left eyebrow that she'd had for as long as she could remember, which was a sentence that had begun, this week, to mean less and less. *As long as she could remember.* Her memory was a house with most of the doors locked, and she had always told herself that was ordinary, that everyone's was, but everyone's did not have a photograph from 1974 in it, and everyone's did not have a scar that appeared in a picture taken — she did the arithmetic with numb lips — at least a decade before she was born.

She held the photograph up to the little round attic mirror.

In the glass, the young woman at the edge of the frame turned fully around. Faced out. Faced her.

Hazel dropped the photograph. It landed face-down on the boards, and the attic went very quiet, the particular quiet of a room where something has just decided to stop pretending.

"Rose," she said.

She didn't know where the name came from. It came up out of her the way the bread smell had come up the stairs — from somewhere below, somewhere warm, somewhere that had been waiting. Her mouth knew it. Her mouth knew it the way it knew the shape of the word the reflection had mouthed at the foot of the stairs, and she said it again, and the third time she said it she was crying, great stupid silent tears, for a child she had never been, for a mother she had never met, for a place at a table kept warm for fifty years.

"Rose," she said. "My name is — "

And stopped. Because it wasn't. Her name was Hazel. Hazel Ravensworth — and there it was, the second half of it, the half she'd carried all her life without asking where it came from, the surname of a family in a village no one had ever heard her mention, and she stood in the attic with both names in her mouth like two halves of a broken thing and understood at last what the pantry wall had been telling her.

The house was not counting her as a guest.

The house was counting her as the fourth child. The one who died before the count began. The one the well was cheated of. The one the house had been keeping a place for since 1974, warming the water, baking the bread, setting the bowl, and the chalk on the wall had waited fifty years for a sixth stroke, and she had walked through the front door with the right face and the right name and the right empty places inside her, and the house had said: *there you are. There you are. We kept your chair.*

And the worst of it — she wrote nothing down, because there was nothing honest left to write — the worst of it was that some part of her, the part with two suitcases and no forwarding address, the part that had been counting doors in rented rooms her whole life, wanted it. Wanted the chair. Wanted the bowl. Wanted to be the sixth stroke on the wall, wanted the count to complete, wanted to belong to something so badly that it would have let the well fill to the brim just to have a mother sing her name.

She went downstairs in the dark without turning the lights on. She didn't need them. The house lit her way — the bulbs warming as she approached, dimming behind her, a corridor of small attentions — and at the pantry door she stopped, and put her palm flat against the plaster, over the fresh white chalk.

"I'm not her," she whispered.

The house hummed, low, under the floor. Not the well. Not yet. A melody. Four notes, five, six — a tune she almost knew, a tune with numbers in it, a lullaby rising up through the foundations like water finding its level, and Hazel stood with her hand on the wall and her heart going soft and traitorous, and thought: *I have to learn that song. All of it. Before it learns me.*
