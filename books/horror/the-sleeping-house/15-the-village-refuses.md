## 15. The Village Refuses

Hazel woke with Edith's plan still ringing in her ears, if you could call it a plan. A song sung backwards, verse by verse, six voices for six names. *It might free us,* Edith had said in the glass, her face flickering like a candle behind smoked pane. *Or it might open the well's mouth early and swallow everything that breathes.* Terrible hope. That was the phrase Hazel kept returning to, because it was the only honest one. Hope, but terrible. The kind you carried the way you carried a lit match through a dry field.

She dressed in the grey morning light, and the hallway mirror moved with her now — perfectly, faithfully, which was somehow worse than the lag. She had spent a week learning to fear the glass. Now it watched her like a friend.

"You're sure?" she asked it.

Edith's reflection pressed her palm flat against the inside of the pane. *The village sang us into the ground,* she said. *Only the village can sing us out.*

* * *

Marrowfen on a Tuesday morning was a village pretending very hard to be nothing at all. Smoke from chimneys. A dog trotting the lane with the self-importance of a dog who has never once been late. Gulls somewhere beyond the fen, crying like hinges. Hazel walked the high street with Edith's diary in her satchel and the lullaby's melody folded up inside her chest, and she felt every door she passed the way you feel a held breath.

She started with the church, because that was where communities kept their courage, and found the vicar — a soft-spoken man named Aldous Crane with ink-stained fingers and the particular tiredness of someone who has spent years not answering questions.

"Mr. Crane," she said. "I need the village to sing with me. The lullaby. The Ravensworth lullaby. Sung a second time, but backwards — un-counting the names. Edith wrote it that way. There's a second function built into the song, and I think — I *know* — it's the only thing that will free them."

Crane set down his pen very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Mrs. Ravensworth," he said — not Hazel, *Mrs.*, and he said it the way you'd say the name of a weather system — "the last time this village sang that song twice, five people went into a well. I was nine years old. I stood at the funeral and I sang, because my mother's hand was on my shoulder and her fingers were digging in, and I have not sung a note of it since. Not in the bath. Not in church. Not once."

"I'm not asking you to sing it forward."

"You're asking me to sing it at all." He stood, and she saw his hands weren't steady. "You don't understand what you're proposing. The second singing *wakes* the count. That's not superstition, that's the parish record. 1974. The well took the Ravensworths in a single night and the water table hasn't behaved since. Every generation it rises. We've measured it. We've *prayed* over it. And you want to stand in that house and sing the waking verse with the whole village as your choir?"

"I want to stand there and sing the *ending* verse. The one Edith built into it. Backwards, it doesn't count them in — it counts them out."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she saw the terrible thing in his face: not disbelief. Belief, and the exhaustion that comes of believing for fifty years without once being allowed to act.

"Even if you're right," he said quietly, "even if the song could un-count them — do you know what happens to a village that sings a thing like that and is wrong? We buried them once, Mrs. Ravensworth. We are not equipped to bury them twice."

"Then help me be right."

"I'm sorry." He turned to the window, to the fen, to the flat grey water that had taken his childhood neighbors. "Lock your doors at night. And whatever the house asks of you — don't answer."

* * *

She went door to door. That was the only word for it, though it felt more like walking a gauntlet.

The baker, Mrs. Tallow, wouldn't open the door at all; Hazel heard her moving behind it, breathing, and then the bolt going home like a small gunshot. The fisherman on the corner — a huge man with forearms like mooring rope — listened to her whole speech with his arms folded and then said only, "My grandad helped fill that well in '75. Concrete and gravel. He never slept a full night again. You think a song's going to fix what a song broke?" And he shut the door with a gentleness that was somehow more final than slamming.

At the Fen & Anchor, the publican, a woman named Sorrel with a voice like gravel in honey, at least let Hazel stand in the doorway.

"Six voices," Sorrel repeated. "You need six voices that knew them."

"Or knew *of* them. Anyone the house might recognize as village. Anyone who was here in '74."

"I was here in '74. I was six." Sorrel wiped a glass that was already clean. "You know what the village did after the funeral, girl? We didn't just stop singing. We stopped *saying*. No Ravensworth names. The house went on the tax rolls as an empty property. The churchyard plot got a stone with no names on it, just a date. Fifty years of not saying, and you want us to say six names backwards into the dark?" She leaned on the bar. "There's a reason we didn't say the names. Names are handles. You give a thing a handle, it can be *pulled*."

"They were your neighbors."

"They were a lesson," Sorrel said, "and the lesson was *don't*."

By late afternoon Hazel had knocked on eleven doors — she counted, out of a habit that had stopped being hers alone — and eleven doors had closed against her. The village didn't argue. That was the worst of it. No one called her mad, no one laughed, no one offered the comfort of skepticism. They simply refused, the way you refuse to step off a cliff, and their refusal had the flat certainty of people who had seen the bottom.

She was walking back up the lane as the light went copper, past the last cottage before the fen, when a man stepped out of the hedge shadow and stood in her path.

She knew him vaguely from the pub — one of the older ones, sixty-something, a face like a closed gate. Wren, she thought the name was. Gerald Wren. He was holding a canvas sack and he did not move aside.

"You're the one living in the house," he said.

"I'm the one trying to save the people in the house."

"There's no one in the house." His voice was very level, the levelness of a man who has rehearsed this. "There's no one in the house, there's nothing in the well, and the only thing wrong with Marrowfen is you. You came here. You started counting doors. You started *singing*."

"The singing started long before me."

"Then end it." He stepped closer. She could smell peat smoke on him and something underneath, sharp and chemical, and her stomach dropped as she understood what was in the sack. "Fifty years we've kept the count asleep by keeping our mouths shut, and in two weeks you've hummed it, recorded it, written it down, and now you want a *choir*. You're not a folklorist. You're a bell, and you're ringing."

"Wren—"

"My sister was Edith's friend," he said, and for one second the gate of his face swung open and she saw the boy inside it, nine years old, standing at a graveside with no names on the stone. "She cried for a year. Then she stopped crying and started locking things, and that's what this village *is* now. Locked things. And you want to unlock the door with the well behind it."

He raised the sack.

Hazel ran.

* * *

She heard him behind her for the first hundred yards — boots on the lane, heavy, not fast but tireless, the run of a man with nothing left to lose and a very specific thing to burn. She cut off the lane at the stile, across the wet meadow, the fen water flashing in the copper light, and the whole way she was doing the arithmetic she hated: the house was half a mile off and she was carrying a satchel and forty-one years of bad knees were not carrying anything.

The house came into view across the field, grey and patient, its eleven doors all closed, and Hazel understood with a clarity that felt like falling that she was not running *from* Wren.

She was running *to* the house. To warn it. To warn *them*.

That was the moment the isolation finished its work on her. Somewhere between the stile and the garden wall, Marrowfen stopped being a village and became a wall — fifty years of locked doors and unsaid names, a whole community that had decided the dead were safer as a lesson than as people. And on the other side of that wall was a kitchen where a dead mother had caught her wrist, and a pantry wall with chalk tallies, and three children who had asked her, with the terrible courtesy of the drowned, to come downstairs where it was safe.

The village would not sing. Fine. The village had made its choice, and its choice was concrete and gravel and locked doors and, if it came to it, fire.

Hazel came through the garden gate at a dead run and got the kitchen door open and the bar down behind her, and she was still breathing hard when she smelled it — smoke, faint, wrong, not peat but petrol, drifting through the west windows.

He hadn't followed her across the field. He'd gone around. Of course he had. You don't chase the thing you mean to burn; you circle it, and you choose the dry side.

She got to the west room in time to see him at the corner of the house, the sack upended, petrol sheeting down the old timber cladding in a dark glistening fan. He had a storm lantern in his other hand. He looked up at her through the window, and his face was not angry. That was the horror of it. It was *relieved* — the face of a man finally setting down fifty years of carrying.

"The house sleeps or the house burns," he called through the glass. "Either way, it stops counting."

"You'll take me with it," Hazel shouted back.

"I know," said Gerald Wren, and struck the match.

The flame went up the west wall like it had been waiting fifty years for permission, and the house — the house *screamed*. Not timbers, not wind. A sound out of the foundations, out of the well shaft, a rising note that bent the window glass in its frame and knocked Hazel to her knees, and in every mirror on the west wall the reflections did not flee the fire.

They turned toward it.

Edith's face appeared in the burning glass, lit from behind by flames that were only on Hazel's side, and her mouth was moving — *the well, Hazel, the well, it's drinking the heat, it's waking early* — and beneath the floorboards, beneath the kitchen, beneath everything, Hazel heard water begin to move.

Not rising.

*Boiling.*

The fire climbed. The house screamed. And out past the garden wall, down in the village, Hazel saw doors opening — eleven, twelve, a dozen small rectangles of lamplight — the whole of Marrowfen coming out to watch the thing they'd spent fifty years not naming finally burn.

She stood up in the smoke with Edith's diary under her arm and made the choice the village wouldn't.

If no one would sing the un-counting with her, she would sing it with the dead.

Six names. Six voices. And the well already waking.

She turned for the stairs, to find her family in the glass, as the west wall came down in fire and the water beneath the kitchen began, softly, to sing.
