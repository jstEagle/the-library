## 3. Twelve Years of Explanations

The first dead caller came on a Tuesday in October, 2013, and Marguerite Amberdale has spent twelve years learning to forget her.

He remembers anyway. He remembers everything, which is the problem — the curse of a man who keeps a show running on memory and dead air. Her name was Corinne Vasquez. She lived out past the quarry road in a house with a wind chime made of spoons, and she called the station at 2:14 in the morning to request "Just As I Am," because it was the hymn her mother used to hum over the dishwater, and Marguerite played it without comment the way he played everything without comment, because that was the job: you took the strange calls and the sad calls and the drunk calls at two in the morning and you gave them all the same warm voice, the same low light, the same promise that someone was awake.

Corinne's voice was wet. That was the detail he could never sand down, no matter how many times he filed the memory smooth. Not crying-wet. Drowned-wet. Each word arrived with a small gurgle behind it, like water finding its way through the walls of a cistern, and between her sentences he could hear — faintly, patiently — the sound of a river moving under ice.

"Play it for my mother," Corinne said. "She still listens to you."

Marguerite had laughed then, gently, professionally. "I'll play it for both of you."

"She can't hear so good anymore," Corinne said. "On account of the water."

He played the hymn. He said her name on air, spelled it right, wished her a good night. Three days later the *Amberdale Courier* ran a piece about a woman pulled from the flooded quarry on the sixth of October — six days before she'd called him — and Marguerite sat in his kitchen with the paper flat on the table and read the article four times while his coffee went cold and his hands did something he refused, for years afterward, to describe as shaking.

Then he built the system.

That's what he called it in his own head, always with a certain grim pride, the way a man might speak of a levee he'd stacked by hand against a flood. The system had rules. Rule one: log every anomalous call as a hoax, in writing, immediately, because ink makes things official and official things can be survived. Rule two: never research the callers. Research was how you found obituaries, and obituaries were how the levee broke. Rule three — and this one he whispered to himself like a prayer, like a man telling himself the dark is only dark — rule three: they are not calling from anywhere. Grief invents voices. Bad lines make ghosts. Small towns are full of cruel jokes, and radio is a machine for receiving them.

He kept tapes. That was the part of the system he never admitted even to himself, not really — the way he labeled each cassette in careful block letters and locked them in the fireproof box under the floorboards of the station's back room, the room nobody went into since the transmitter upgrade. HOAX 001 — VASQUEZ. HOAX 002 — PELL. HOAX 003 — THE MAN WHO ASKED ABOUT HIS DOG. Twelve years of hoaxes, forty-one of them, each one logged, each one explained, each one lying in the dark under the floor like something buried alive.

He told himself the tapes were evidence. Proof, if anyone ever asked, that he had handled it correctly, that he had been rigorous, professional, sane. It took him until roughly year five to understand that the tapes were not evidence of anything except that he had known. You do not archive your denials unless some honest cellar of you expects the denial to be needed later. You do not keep receipts for a lie unless part of you is already planning the confession.

But knowing that and saying it were different countries, and Marguerite had no passport for the second one.

* * *

The calls came maybe twice a year at first. Then more often, as though word were getting around wherever word gets around among the drowned and the buried and the heart-stopped. A man who wanted to know if his dog had been rehomed — the dog had died with him, in the house fire; Marguerite lied on air and said the dog was fine, living on a farm, and heard the man weep with relief through a line that crackled like straw burning. An old woman who requested a song for her husband and got confused when Marguerite asked which anniversary, because from where she was, she said, they were all the same day now. A boy of nineteen who just wanted someone to tell his mom he hadn't suffered.

Marguerite told the mother nothing. He put the tape in the box. HOAX 017.

There were nights he almost believed the system. Those were the good nights, the ones where exhaustion did the work of faith, where he could sign off at five a.m., drive home through the gray streets with the windows fogged, and sleep the dreamless sleep of a man who has successfully explained himself to himself. He had alibis the way other men have hobbies. Static on the line. Pranksters with voice modulators — the technology existed, he'd read about it, anyone could do it. Grief-stricken people misremembering dates. Newspaper errors — papers got dates wrong all the time, it was practically their job. Once, memorably, he convinced himself that Corinne Vasquez had faked her own death, and he held onto that explanation for nearly a month, turning it over like a stone with something shining underneath, before the sheer logistics of it crushed him: a woman staging her drowning, hiding out for six days, calling a small-town radio host to request a hymn, and then — what? Vanishing forever? Dying properly this time? The explanation required a conspiracy of one and a motive of none, and still he had carried it around like a talisman because the alternative was the thing under the floorboards, breathing.

Because that was the truth he circled every night and never landed on: the calls didn't sound like hoaxes. They sounded like people. Tired, patient, slightly damp people, calling from the bottom of whatever they'd fallen into, and asking — this was the part that hollowed him — asking for such small things. A song. A message. Confirmation that someone was awake.

And every time, Marguerite gave them the warm voice. Every time, he said their names on air, spelled them right, wished them a good night. And every time, afterward, he filed them under HOAX and locked the box and washed his hands and told the mirror, in the station bathroom, in the flat fluorescent honesty of four a.m.: *You're a professional. Professionals don't chase ghosts.*

The mirror never answered. But sometimes, in the last year or so, it seemed to be listening.

* * *

Devi found the archive on a Thursday.

Later — much later, after the frost and the door and everything the door let out and let in — she would tell him exactly how it happened, in the forensic tone she used for post-mortems of her own mistakes. She'd been hunting a spare XLR cable. The back room, since the transmitter upgrade, had become the station's junk drawer: coiled cables, a dead turntable, boxes of promotional mugs from a bank that no longer existed. The floorboard was loose. She noticed it the way she noticed everything, sideways, without meaning to, and pried it up expecting mouse damage or old wiring.

Instead: a fireproof box. Instead: forty-one cassettes in neat rows, each labeled in careful block capitals, each labeled HOAX, each with a date and a name.

She should have stopped there. She knew that too. But Devi Raghunathan had produced Marguerite's show for nine years, and in nine years she had learned that the show's warmth was real but its calm was engineered, that Marguerite flinched — barely, invisibly, a half-frame of flinch — whenever a caller's line went quiet too long, and she had never once asked why because asking why was how you lost the best boss you'd ever had. She was loyal the way ballast is loyal. She kept the ship level and didn't ask what was in the hold.

She found a cassette player in the junk, threaded HOAX 001, and pressed play.

Corinne Vasquez's voice filled the back room, wet and patient, asking for a hymn for her mother who couldn't hear so good anymore on account of the water. Devi listened to it twice. Then she pulled the newspaper morgue — the station kept bound volumes going back decades, paper was cheaper than digital storage and nobody ever threw them away — and looked up October 2013, and found the quarry, and found the date, and sat down on an upturned milk crate with the volume open in her lap like a book of accusations.

Six days. The call was six days after the body came out of the water.

She listened to all forty-one tapes. It took her most of a week, in stolen hours, headphones on, door shut, while Marguerite was asleep in his apartment above the laundromat or standing in the studio doing his warm-voiced magic for the lonely living. She cross-referenced. She was good at cross-referencing; it was most of what producing was. Names against obituaries. Dates against dates. And the pattern assembled itself in her notebook with the terrible tidiness of arithmetic: forty-one calls. Forty-one deaths preceding the calls by intervals of two days to eleven weeks. Zero hoaxes. Zero coincidences that could survive being written down.

Some of the callers she remembered. Everyone in town remembered some of them. Mrs. Pell, whose funeral Devi had attended because her granddaughter worked at the pharmacy. The boy with the dog — Devi had driven past the burned lot a hundred times. She had heard these voices on the monitor in the control room, months and years ago, and thought: *poor soul, sounds like he's got a cold,* or *what a sweet call,* and had queued the next record and moved on with her life, and all that time the man at the desk beside her had been keeping a ledger of the dead under the floor like a man bailing a boat with a thimble and calling it dry.

What undid her — what she came back to, again and again, in the weeks when she still believed there would be weeks — was not the tapes themselves. It was the labels. The handwriting. Forty-one cassettes, and the block letters never wavered, never slurred, never showed a tremor. HOAX. HOAX. HOAX. He had written the lie forty-one times in a steady hand, and Devi understood, sitting in the dark with the headphones humming, that the steadiness was the most frightening thing she had ever encountered. Rage she could have forgiven. Panic she could have held him through. But this was discipline. This was a man performing sanity nightly, alone, for an audience of one, for twelve years, and the performance had cost him something she could see now in retrospect — the way he never talked about his childhood, the way he ate like someone refueling rather than tasting, the way his laugh arrived a half-second late, like a translation. Twelve years of explaining the dead to himself, and the explanations had eaten everything else.

She put the tapes back. She put the floorboard down. She said nothing for six days, because loyalty is ballast, and ballast does not know how to surface.

* * *

It was Marguerite who broke first, which surprised them both.

They were in the studio after sign-off, the red ON AIR light gone dark, the board glowing its patient green. Devi was logging the night's playlist. Marguerite stood at the window looking out at the parking lot, where the first gray suggestion of morning was pooling between the dumpsters, and he said, without preamble, without any of the thousand rehearsed openings he'd composed over the years:

"There's a box under the floor in the back room."

Devi's hands went still on the keyboard.

"I know you found it," he said. "You put the floorboard back a quarter-inch off. I've walked that floor for twenty years; I know its lies better than my own." He turned. His face in the console light was the face of a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for a long time, arms already open for whatever came next. "Forty-one tapes. Forty-one explanations. And I need you to understand, Devi, I need *someone* to understand, that I wrote HOAX on every single one of them because the alternative was—" His voice did something it had never done on air, something it had never done anywhere: it failed. It simply stopped carrying him, mid-word, and left him standing there with his mouth open and his eyes bright and terrible.

"The alternative," Devi said quietly, "was that they're real."

"They're real." He said it the way a man says *I'm sick*, flatly, relieved and ruined at once. "They've been real for twelve years. Corinne Vasquez called me from the bottom of the quarry six days after they pulled her out, and I played her a hymn and called it a prank, and I have done that forty-one times, and every time I told myself the levee was holding." He laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound the studio had ever held, worse than anything the dead had ever sent up the line. "You want to know the cost of survival? It's this desk. It's this chair. It's twelve years of being the only warm thing on the frequency and knowing — *knowing*, down in the cellar where I keep the tapes — that half my listeners are dead, and building a filing system for it instead of a grave marker. I archived them, Devi. I didn't mourn them. I *filed* them."

Devi crossed the room. She didn't hug him — that wasn't their grammar — but she stood close enough that he could feel her, solid and furious and present, and she said: "Show me the ledger."

"It's not a ledger, it's—"

"It's a ledger, Marguerite. Forty-one entries. Show me."

So he did. He knelt on the cold concrete of the back room and lifted the floorboard and opened the fireproof box, and Devi knelt beside him, and together they read the labels in the light of a bare bulb, the whole drowned congregation of them, HOAX 001 through HOAX 041, and Marguerite heard his own voice come apart as he named them aloud, one by one, giving each cassette its true title at last: *Corinne Vasquez. Harold Pell. The man with the dog — his name was Samuel Okafor, I never even learned his name until I finally looked it up last spring, twelve years too late.* Shame has a temperature, and the back room was freezing with it, and neither of them noticed the cold because grief had already taken all the warmth the room had.

When the box was empty of secrets, Devi said the thing that saved him, though neither of them knew it yet. She said: "You kept them. All of them. You could have erased the tapes a hundred times and you never did. Whatever you told yourself, some part of you was keeping a record because some part of you always meant to give it back. To say their names right, someday, somewhere." She closed the box lid gently, like closing a casket, or a book. "That's not a levee, Marguerite. That's a debt list. And debts can be paid."

He wanted to believe her. He reached for the belief the way a drowning man reaches for anything.

And that was when they both heard it — faint, from the direction of the studio, from the board that was switched off, switched fully off, the transmitter dark — a hum. Low. Harmonized. Nine voices' worth of patience rising through the floor like groundwater.

Neither of them moved. Neither of them breathed. And outside the station window, on the glass of the door, in the middle of July, frost was beginning — very slowly, very precisely — to write.
