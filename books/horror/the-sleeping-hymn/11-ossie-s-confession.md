## 11. Ossie's Confession

The kettle in the station's back room had a whistle like a child holding its breath, and Ossie had been listening to it for the better part of an hour without getting up to pour. He sat with his hands flat on the table — big hands, spade hands, hands that had closed over more earth than any man in Amberdale County and knew the weight of every shovelful by heart — and he watched the frost climb the window glass in front of him.

It was July. The frost did not care.

The notes came in slow, patient lines, five of them now where there had been three that morning, etched from the inside out as though the glass were paper and something on the far side of the world was writing with a cold pen. Ossie had spent fifty-two years telling himself he would never see that pattern again outside of his own memory, and here it was, blooming across a radio station window at two in the afternoon while the sun stood high and yellow over the parking lot.

He knew the tune. That was the thing nobody else in this building understood yet, not fully, not down in the marrow where knowing lived. Devi had photographs spread across her desk in the other room, and Marguerite was pacing with his sleeves rolled up like a man trying to outrun his own name, and Wren sat cross-legged on the couch with her frost-marked arms folded, watching everything with eyes too old for her face. They were all chasing the pattern forward — what it meant, where it led, how to stop it.

Ossie was the only one in the room who already knew how it ended. Because he had helped write the first verse of it, in the summer of 1974, with a trowel and a bag of mortar and a lie in his ears.

* * *

His name then had been Oswald Priddy, apprentice, nineteen years old, and he had believed everything old men told him because believing was easier than the alternative and cheaper too. His master, Harlan Voss, had taken him to the chapel on a Tuesday morning when the mist hadn't lifted off the hollow yet, and the foreman of the renovation crew had met them at the gate with a face gray as wet ash.

"Sealed section," the foreman said. "Old crypt access under the east transept. There was an accident during the closure work. Nine men trapped." He'd said it flat, rehearsed, the way a man says a thing he has been made to say. "They didn't make it. The diocese wants it sealed proper before word gets out. No inquest, no fuss. You'll be paid triple."

Ossie remembered the arithmetic he'd done standing there in the mist: triple wages, and September's rent, and the motorbike he'd been saving toward since he was fifteen. He remembered thinking it was sad, the nine men, but sad things happened on jobs all the time. Scaffolds fell. Trenches collapsed. The ground took people. He was a gravedigger's boy; death was his family's trade, and trade meant you got paid and you didn't ask where the bodies came from.

What he had never let himself remember clearly — until these last weeks, until the frost started writing on windows in midsummer and the dead began calling a radio show — was the sound.

Because the door had hinges on the inside. He'd noticed that even then, nineteen and green, kneeling in the dust with his trowel loaded. Hinges on the inside meant the door was built to be opened from within. Meant whoever was behind it was expected to come out. And as he laid the first course of mortar along the jamb, cold stone against his knuckles, he had heard — faint, muffled, filtered through six feet of rock and half a century of silence — singing.

Nine voices. A hymn. Not screaming, not hammering, not the clawing you'd expect from buried men. Singing. Steady and low and terribly patient, the way you sing when you believe someone is coming, when you are singing to keep your courage banked like a fire, when you have decided that despair is a luxury you cannot afford.

"They're dead," Harlan Voss had said behind him, and his voice had been wrong, too quick, too smooth. "Gasses down there. Whole crew went in their sleep days ago. What you're hearing is the wind through the vents."

Wind through the vents. Ossie had laid mortar over that explanation the way he laid it over stone, course after course, and the singing had grown no louder and no softer, and near the end — he could hear it now, fifty years later, could hear it the way you hear a bell long after it stops ringing — near the end the hymn had changed key. Lifted. As if they had heard the scraping of the trowel and understood, and were singing louder so that God or history or whoever came after might know they had gone on singing to the very last.

He had smoothed the final joint with his thumb, the way Harlan taught him, and the stone had been cold under his skin, colder than July had any right to make it, and he had walked out of that chapel into the sunlight and taken his triple pay and bought the motorbike, and he had not slept a whole night through for eleven years afterward.

* * *

"You've gone somewhere," Wren said.

Ossie blinked. The kettle had finally boiled itself silent; the frost notes on the window had gained a sixth line. The girl was watching him from the couch with her head tilted, and there was frost on her too — fine white traceries along her forearms, the same score, the same hymn, written on living skin — and she should have been dead, was going to be dead tomorrow according to every law God and gravity had ever agreed on, and instead she was sitting in a radio station eating digestive biscuits and looking at him like she could read the ledger of his soul right down to the interest accrued.

"Aye," he said. "I've gone somewhere. Been going there a long time."

Devi appeared in the doorway with the photographs under her arm and Marguerite behind her, and the four of them filled the little back room the way a coffin fills a grave: completely, with no room left for anything else. Ossie looked at them — the producer with her careful, tired eyes; the broadcaster whose family name was carved into the trouble like initials into a tree; the girl who was a door between worlds whether she liked it or not — and he felt the old weight shift in his chest, the one he'd carried since 1974, and he understood that it was time. Past time. The frost was writing faster now. Every day another note. Whatever the song was building toward, it was nearly built, and a man did not get to stand by with a trowel in his hand twice.

"Sit down, all of you," Ossie said. "There's a thing I should have said fifty years ago, and I'm going to say it now, and I'd take it kind if you let me get to the end without stopping me."

* * *

He told it plainly, because plainness was all he had. He was not a novelist like Marguerite, not a researcher like Devi. He was a digger. So he dug.

He told them about the mist and the foreman's gray face and the triple pay. He told them about the hinges on the inside — and he saw Marguerite go still at that, saw the man's hand come up to his mouth the way it did on air when a caller said something that cut too close. He told them about the mortar, mixing it in the wheelbarrow with water from a rusted drum, the sand pale and fine, the smell of lime sharp enough to sting the eyes. He told them about the singing.

"The master said wind," Ossie said. "And I wanted it to be wind. You understand? I was nineteen and I wanted triple pay and a motorbike and I wanted the world to be a place where nine men could die in their sleep under a church and it was just sad, just the ground doing what the ground does. So I mortared that door shut with wind in my ears." His hands were shaking. He put them flat on the table again, pressed them down like he was pressing down soil. "But it weren't wind. Wind don't hold a note for four hours. Wind don't change key when it hears a trowel."

Nobody spoke. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere out in the studio, a tape machine turned idly, recording nothing, or recording everything — Ossie had learned in the past weeks that in this building you could never be sure which.

"I've heard them ever since," he went on. "Not every day. But every year, on the anniversary — late June, early July — I'd wake at night and the hymn would be there, at the edge of hearing, like it was coming up through the floor. My wife thought I had tinnitus. Doctors said the same. Fifty years of doctors." He laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it, gravel shifting. "Fifty years, and it turns out the only diagnosis was true. It's the choir. They've been singing under my floor, under this town, under everything, since the summer I sealed them in. And I told myself they were dead. I told myself it every morning for fifty years, the way you tell yourself the ground is just ground. And I wasn't sure. That's the truth of it, and I'll carry it to whatever grave I get. I wasn't sure. I heard singing and I mortared anyway, because the alternative was opening that door and finding out what I'd helped do, and finding out would have cost me everything — the money, the trade, the life I'd built on top of it. So I chose not-knowing. And not-knowing, friends, is just a slower way of killing somebody."

Marguerite's voice, when it came, was rough. "You were nineteen. They lied to you."

"They did." Ossie nodded slowly. "And I let them, because the lie was comfortable and the truth had hinges on the inside." He looked around the table, at each of them in turn. "Your grandfather's sin is written in a ledger, lad. Mine's written in mortar. Don't you go telling me mine weighs less because I was young. I've weighed it every night for half a century and it hasn't lost an ounce."

* * *

It was Wren who moved first. She slid off the couch, crossed the little room, and sat down beside him, and she took one of his great spade hands in both of hers — her fingers cool, marked with frost, impossibly alive — and held it the way you'd hold a bird with a broken wing.

"My funeral's tomorrow," she said. "Everyone keeps saying it like it's a tragedy. But you know what I figured out? Being dead isn't the worst thing. Being unfinished is. Those nine have been mid-hymn for fifty years." She squeezed his hand. "You didn't finish them, Mr. Ossie. Somebody else did the sealing. But you can help finish them now. That's worth more than fifty years of sorry."

Ossie found that his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them, because a man ought to see clearly what he weeps about.

Devi had come around the table too, and she stood behind him with her hands on his shoulders — light, steady, the grip of someone anchoring a rope — and Marguerite, after a moment, reached across and laid his palm flat on the table beside Ossie's own, close enough to touch. Four people in a small hot room in July, and frost writing its patient score on the window, and the coldest summer Ossie had known since 1974.

"I can't undo the mortar," Ossie said. "But I know that door. I know its hinge-side and its weight and the lay of the stone around it, and I know the hymn they were singing, because I've heard it in my skull for fifty years and I could whistle it drunk or dying. If we're going down there — and we are, I can see it in all your faces, so don't pretend otherwise — then you'll want me. Not despite what I did. Because of it."

"That's why we keep you around," Devi said softly. "That, and you make decent tea."

Ossie laughed then, really laughed, and it cracked something open in his chest that had been sealed longer than the door — and what came out of the crack wasn't grief, or not only grief. It was relief. Guilt shared, he was discovering, was guilt halved, and halved again, and halved again, until a man could stand upright under it and walk.

He rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and went to the window. The frost score had gained its seventh line while they talked. Seven of — he counted, lips moving, an old habit from hymnals — twelve bars. Five remaining. He did not know how many notes a bar held, or how many deaths a note cost, but he knew the tempo was increasing, and he knew, the way a gravedigger knows the frost line before winter sets, that they had less time than anyone wanted to say aloud.

"There's one more thing," Ossie said, and turned to face them. "Before we plan anything, before Devi goes digging in whatever archive holds the proof. You need to hear the rest of it. The part I've never told a living soul." He swallowed. "On the last day, when the mortar was set and Harlan paid me off — the foreman gave me something to carry out. Said it was rubble, said dump it in the quarry. It weren't rubble. It was a box. Lead-lined, small enough to carry under one arm, warm as a sleeping cat, and it hummed. Low, like a held breath. Like nine held breaths."

The room went very still. Outside, the July sun kept shining, indifferent and golden, and the frost wrote on.

"I dumped it in the quarry like he said," Ossie whispered. "Or I thought I did. But it's been fifty years, and I've had fifty years of dreams about that box, and lately—" his voice failed, steadied, "—lately I've started remembering the weight wrong. Started remembering carrying it home instead. And if that box is anywhere in this world, it's wherever my memory says it is, and my memory says—"

He stopped. Looked at Marguerite. At the name the whole town knew, the name the dead callers had been saying for weeks.

"It's under the Amberdale house," he said. "It always was. Your grandfather never let it leave his property. And I think — God forgive me, I think I'm the one who put it there."

* * *

The frost on the window finished its seventh bar as they stood there, four souls in a small room, bound now by mortar and ledger and lead and song. And beneath the hum of the refrigerator, beneath the idle turning of the tape machine, faint and patient and rising like water through stone, Ossie heard the hymn begin its eighth.
