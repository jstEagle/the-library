## 17. Through the Door Sealed From Inside

The frost came down the excavation shaft with us like a fourth companion.

I noticed it first on the ladder rungs — a rime of white blooming under Devi's gloves as she descended, spreading from her grip outward in small feathered whorls, each one shaped like a note I had learned to read against my will over these past weeks. Ossie went down ahead of me with his lantern held low, and where its light touched the shaft walls, the frost retreated and then crept back, patient as tide. Wren came last. She didn't need the lantern. The pattern on her forearms gave off a light of its own, faint as milk glass held to a window at dusk.

"Talk to me," Devi said, and I realized she'd said it twice already.

"I'm here," I told her. "I'm right behind you."

The shaft was not deep — thirty feet, maybe less — but it felt like descending through years. Every rung took something out of my legs that I did not get back. At the bottom, the crew's abandoned equipment lay where they'd left it: a generator ticking over on idle, floodlights aimed at the far wall, hard hats set down in a row with a neatness that no living workman would have managed. Two of them were still there. The other two had walked into the pit nine nights ago and been found at dawn wearing smiles and frost, and their hats had been left behind like shed skins.

Ossie stood before the door.

I had heard it described a hundred times by now — hinges on the inside, mortar laid from within, stone dressed smooth on the face that faced *out*, as if whoever built it had cared only about how it would look to the people who would never be allowed past it. But hearing is not seeing. It stood perhaps seven feet tall, set into the chapel's old foundation stones, and it was warm. You could feel it from three feet away, a heat like a body standing close in a cold room, and the frost refused to touch it. Around its frame the white patterns gathered thick as lace but stopped dead at an inch from the stone, as if the door itself were breathing them away.

"It knows we're here," Wren said quietly.

"How can you tell?"

She turned her arm toward the door. Along her skin, the notes had begun to move — slowly, one after another, sliding down from her wrist toward her elbow like beads on a string, arranging themselves into a phrase I recognized now the way you recognize your own name spoken in a dark house. The hymn. The verse the choir never finished.

"It's rehearsing," she said. "It's been rehearsing all week. And just now—" she pressed her palm flat against the air a hand's width from the stone, "—it changed key."

Ossie made a sound low in his chest, half prayer, half curse. "It never changed key in '74. Not once. Not while we worked." He wouldn't say *while I mortared it shut*. He hadn't said it since the night of his confession, and I understood why. Some sentences you can only carry so many times before they wear grooves in you.

Devi had the tape recorders running hot, three of them, slung across her body like bandoliers, their reels turning. "Salt line first," she said, because ritual gives frightened hands something to do, and we knelt together and poured a white seam across the threshold of a door that had not opened in fifty years, and the salt sat there looking absurd and necessary both at once.

Then Marguerite Amberdale, last of his line, put his hand against the door sealed from the inside, and pushed.

* * *

It moved before I finished pushing.

Not swung — *yielded*, the way a held breath yields, the way a long-held note finally lets go. There was no grinding of hinges, though hinges there must be; there was only a sound like a choir drawing a single breath, drawn out and out until it became the sound of stone remembering it could move. Warm air rolled out over us, smelling of candlewax and cut flowers and something older underneath, something like the inside of a violin, like varnish and horsehair and rosin dust.

Light came with it. Candlelight, dozens of flames' worth, soft gold spilling across the salt line and up the shaft wall, and the frost on the walls around the doorway flared bright as if insulted, then settled into stillness.

"Nobody moves until I do," I said, and hated how much my voice shook, and loved Devi for not commenting on it.

We crossed the threshold together anyway, because none of us had ever obeyed me when it mattered.

* * *

The chapel beneath the chapel was whole.

That was the thing no report had prepared us for, no ledger, no suppressed file from 1974. We had imagined ruin — collapsed timber, flooded floor, fifty years of dark doing what dark does. Instead we stepped into a room preserved the way grief preserves: perfectly, terribly, against its own nature. Nine wooden chairs in two curved rows facing a bare stone altar. Hymnals open on the seats, their pages unyellowed. A coat hung on a peg by the door — wool, brown, a man's size — with a paper cinema ticket still folded in its pocket, visible through the gap in the fabric. On the altar itself, nothing. An absence shaped exactly like a reliquary, a rectangle of clean pale wood where everything else had darkened around it, the way grass stays green under a stone.

And candles. Candles everywhere, in iron sconces and glass hurricanes and simple pools of wax on the floor, every flame upright and calm, burning without flicker, burning without shortening. I watched one for a full minute and its wick did not diminish. Fifty years of fire, banked and waiting, spending nothing.

"They're not burning," Ossie whispered. He had taken off his hat, which I realized he'd worn every day of his life since 1974, and his hair was white as the frost above us. "They're *holding*. That's different. That's someone holding a note." His voice cracked on the word. "Fifty years, holding the same note."

The warmth was everywhere and came from nowhere. It pressed against my skin like hands. Somewhere above us, muffled through fifty feet of earth and stone, July continued — traffic on the ring road, a dog barking, the ordinary machinery of a town that had sung this hymn at funerals for half a century without knowing it was singing along. Down here there was no season at all. Down here it was always the moment before the door closed.

Wren walked to the front row of chairs and stopped before the nearest hymnal. "Marguerite."

"Don't touch it."

"I'm not touching it. Look."

The page was open to the hymn — our hymn, the one the frost wrote on windows, the one the transmitter played alone the night the station died. The final verse was there in the book, printed complete, all eight lines. But someone had marked it. A pencil line ran down the margin beside the last four lines, and beside those lines, in a hand gone brown with age, someone had written a single word:

*After.*

"After what?" Devi asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"After the door," Ossie said. "That's the instruction. You sing the first part inside, and the last part *after* — after the sealing, after the sacrifice, after whatever was supposed to come next." He looked at the empty rectangle on the altar. "Nobody sang it after. Nobody was left who knew how. So the hymn's been hanging there ever since, mid-verse, like a phone call nobody hung up."

Like a call nobody hung up. Twelve years of dead voices on my show, and I had built a career on answering them badly.

The frost followed us in.

I don't know how else to describe it. It came down the shaft behind us the way weather comes over a hill — not rushing, not creeping, simply *arriving*, a change in the air made visible. It climbed the interior walls in slow ferning branches, and where it passed, the candlelight turned blue-white, and the notes appeared in it, note after note after note, filling in the score that Devi had spent three weeks mapping across the whole town. Here, underground, at the source, the pattern was nearly done. Only a handful of spaces remained blank, and each blank space sat directly above one of the nine chairs.

One blank space sat above the altar.

"That's the last note," Devi said. Her recorders turned and turned. "That's the one that opens the door from either side. That's tonight or never."

"We know," I said. "We've known since the map."

"No. You've known the *when*. Look at where it is." She pointed, and her finger was steady, and I loved her for that too. "It's over the altar. Over the place where the reliquary goes. The frost isn't counting down to a time, Marguerite. It's counting down to a *return*. The song doesn't finish because the song was never the point. The song is the receipt."

The candles all leaned at once.

There was no draft. There was no wind in a room sealed for fifty years. But every flame in the chamber bent toward us in the same instant, the way wheat bends before weather, the way a congregation turns when the doors open late, and the warmth in the room doubled and redoubled until it was not comfort anymore but pressure, a hand pressing flat against my sternum, and I understood with total clarity that awe and terror are the same sensation wearing different clothes, and that whatever had kept this room gentle for fifty years was running out of gentleness.

The hymn began.

Not from any throat. From the walls, from the frost, from the held breath of the candles — nine parts, thin and clear and unbearably patient, picking up precisely where they had left off in 1974, mid-phrase, mid-word even, as if the intervening decades had been a rest written into the score. The sound filled the chamber the way water fills a lung. My teeth ached with it. Wren sank to her knees with her arms wrapped around herself, and the notes on her skin blazed in answer, moving faster now, matching the music note for note, her body become a second score, a translation, a child's copybook of a song too large for paper.

Ossie was crying without noise. "That's them," he said. "That's the Tuesday rehearsal. They always started with the third verse on Tuesdays." Fifty years, and the man remembered the day of the week.

Devi gripped my sleeve. "The terms," she reminded me, low and fierce. "You remember the terms. Alone, it kills you. We split it. Say it back."

"Return the reliquary. Finish the verse. Share the debt." I said it like a creed, because that was what it had become. "Four voices. None of it kills."

"Say it again."

"Four voices, Devi."

The hymn reached the end of the third verse and stopped — stopped the way it must have stopped in 1974, hanging, unfinished, the silence afterward so complete I could hear the salt grains settling on the threshold behind us. In that silence, every candle flame stood perfectly upright again, and every eye of frost on the walls seemed to turn toward the empty rectangle on the altar, and toward me, and toward the canvas satchel at Devi's hip where the reliquary rode against her ribs like a second heart.

From the far side of the chamber, from the dark beyond the nine chairs where no light reached, something exhaled.

Nine breaths, kept warm for fifty years, recognizing the shape of the box that had held them.

Wren looked up at me from the floor, frost-lit, terrified, alive, and said the words that pulled us the last step forward:

"They're asking us to sit down."
