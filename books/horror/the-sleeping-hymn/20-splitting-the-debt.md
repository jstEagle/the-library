## 20. Splitting the Debt

The first thing I understood about dying was that it had a sound.

Not a dramatic sound. Not thunder or tearing stone. It was small — a thinning, like a radio signal drifting out of range, like Marguerite's voice on a bad night when the transmitter ran hot and the words came back to us frayed at the edges. I had spent twelve years listening to that voice through headphones, through glass, through the long dark hours between midnight and dawn, and I knew every texture of it. I knew when he was tired. I knew when he was lying. I knew when he was smiling at someone in the booth who couldn't be seen.

Now I was learning what it sounded like when the years were being pulled back out of him.

He stood before the empty altar with his hands loose at his sides, singing. The hymn rose out of him the way water rises through chalk — slowly, unwillingly, drawn up by something older than thirst. And with each note his body paid. I could see it happening. The gray threading into his hair spread like frost across a pane. The lines around his eyes deepened from creases into carvings. His shoulders, which had carried twelve years of borrowed strength, began to bow as if someone were setting stones on them one at a time, patiently, the way Ossie once set headstones.

"Marguerite." My voice came out wrong, too loud in that buried chapel, too human. "Stop. Please."

He didn't stop. He smiled at me — that was the worst part, the gentleness of it — and kept singing. *I am finishing it,* the smile said. *This is mine to finish.* Fifty years of family sin settling onto one man's shoulders because he was the last one left to carry it, and because some part of him had always believed he deserved the weight.

Behind me, Wren made a sound I'd never heard from her before. Not fear. Anger.

"No," she said.

* * *

Let me tell you how we got there, because the descent is already written elsewhere, in Marguerite's memory and Ossie's and even Wren's strange half-lit sight, and I don't want to repeat what they saw. I want to tell you what I saw, standing in the doorway sealed from the inside for fifty years, watching my friend spend himself note by note.

The chapel below the chapel was warm. That was the first obscenity of it. After the frost that had crawled down the walls behind us like weather following weather, after the cold that had written its hymn-score across every window in town, we expected ice. Instead: candlelight. Nine candles in nine sconces, their wax soft as the day they were lit in 1974, flames upright and unhurried, burning without consuming. The air smelled of beeswax and old paper and, underneath it, faintly, of breath — the warm animal smell of a room full of people who have just finished singing.

Nine chairs faced the altar. In them, shapes. To Marguerite they were shadows; to Ossie they were worse than shadows, they were faces he had mortared shut; to Wren they were people, clear as noon, mid-hymn for half a century. To me they were somewhere between — figures like figures seen through frosted glass, moving slightly, mouths opening and closing on a verse that never ended.

And above us, on the vaulted ceiling, the frost-score glowed faintly blue-white, all but complete. One phrase left. The final line of the final verse, hanging unfinished over the altar like a held breath.

That's why Marguerite sang. A voice given freely, the choir had shown him — not taken, not bargained for, but offered — could close the verse. And closing the verse meant paying for it, and the price was the borrowed years, the twelve years the reliquary had siphoned into his bloodline since before he was born, the longevity his grandfather had bought with nine lives in 1974 and spent quietly ever after.

Marguerite Amberdale had walked down here intending to die. He hadn't said it in words. He'd said it in the way he'd hugged Devi — me — too long at the threshold. In the way he'd asked Ossie to look after the station tapes. In the way he'd told Wren, crouching to her eye level, *you're going to be fine, kid, whatever happens,* in the tone of a man distributing his estate.

So when his voice began to fray — when the third note of the phrase came out of him aged, when I watched his hands grow old in real time, knuckles swelling, skin going translucent over blue veins — I understood that I was watching a suicide conducted as an act of music. And I discovered something in myself that surprised me, that I will carry out of this chapter and out of this story:

I refused.

* * *

"Devi." Ossie's hand closed on my arm. His grip was still strong; seventy years of graves will do that. "Devi, think. If you interrupt—"

"If I interrupt nothing," I said, "he dies."

"He's chosen—"

"He hasn't chosen anything. He's inherited. There's a difference, and it's the whole point." I pulled free of his grip and stepped forward, my boots loud on stone that hadn't heard footsteps in fifty years. "Ossie, you told us yourself. The debt isn't his alone. Your trowel laid the mortar. His grandfather stole the reliquary. This town looked away. Four of us came down here. Why does only one of us pay?"

Wren was already moving. Of course she was. She was seventeen and should have been dead for three days and had spent those three days learning exactly how much a life weighs, and she crossed that chapel floor like a girl crossing a street against the light — certain, furious, unkillable in spirit if not yet in fact.

She reached Marguerite first. She did not touch him. She stood in front of him, between him and the altar, between him and the nine waiting shapes, and she opened her mouth and sang.

Her voice cracked on the first note. She was terrified; anyone could hear it. But the note landed, and where it landed the frost-score on the ceiling brightened — and Marguerite's graying hair lightened by a shade. I saw it happen. I swear on every tape in the station archive, I saw the debt divide.

The choir stirred.

All nine shapes turned, or seemed to turn, their attention swinging toward the girl like a compass needle finding north. The hymn beneath our feet — the one rising through the floor from whatever depth held them, the one the excavation crew had heard and two men had walked into the pit following — shifted key. Warmer. Expectant.

"It works," Wren said, breaking off, breathless, staring at her own hands as if they belonged to someone braver. "It — Devi, it *works*, it splits—"

"Don't stop," I said. "But don't do it alone either."

I am not a singer. Let the record show that. Twelve years in radio and I had never once let my voice go out over the air unguarded; I was the producer, the fixer, the woman behind the glass, the one who kept other people's voices safe. Singing in front of anyone had been my private nightmare since childhood choir practice, since Mrs. Halloran put me in the back row and told me to mouth the words.

I walked to the altar and I sang anyway.

My voice came out flat and wavering and true enough. The note found its place in the score overhead — a small blue star of frost flaring among the constellation — and the pull on Marguerite eased again, the invisible ledger rebalancing, four names now where one had been. I felt the cost land in me immediately. Not pain. Withdrawal. Like something warm being siphoned out through the sternum, like standing in winter wind with your coat open on purpose. My knees ached. My vision sharpened and dimmed by turns. Somewhere behind my eyes I felt the exact weight of one quarter of twelve years — three years, maybe, three winters, three birthdays — being called due.

Three years. I could pay three years. Three years would take me from thirty-four to thirty-seven and leave me alive, leave me *here*, leave me standing beside him when the last note faded.

"Ossie," I said, and my voice shook, and I didn't care. "Get over here, you stubborn old grave-robber. You owe them fifty years of this. You can spare one verse."

* * *

He came slowly. He came the way old men come to confession, which is to say he came the way he'd wanted to come for half a century and had talked himself out of it ten thousand times.

"I can't sing," he said. "Never could. They used to say at the yard I hummed flat enough to wake the dead." He stopped. He heard what he'd said. His face did something complicated. "Well," he finished quietly. "Aye. That."

He stood at the altar beside us, this man who had mixed the mortar in 1974 believing — wanting to believe — that the choir inside was already dead, and he opened his mouth, and what came out was barely more than a rumble, a gravel-and-tar drone that no choirmaster would have tolerated for a bar.

The ceiling loved it.

The frost-score blazed. A new note ignited, then another, the final phrase filling in like a sky filling with stars, and I felt the debt redistribute again — quarters becoming thirds becoming fourths, the weight spreading across four backs until it was no longer a burden but a shared load, the kind of load people have carried together since before we had words for it. Marguerite sagged, caught himself, and turned his head. His face was older than it had been an hour ago — older by years, real years, honestly paid — but his eyes were wet and alive and looking at us with an expression I will spend the rest of my actual, mortal, unborrowed life trying to deserve.

"You absolute idiots," he whispered between notes. "You beautiful, insubordinate idiots."

"Sign-off's not till dawn," I told him. "Keep singing."

And Wren laughed — laughed in the middle of the hymn, a bright broken sound, and somehow the laugh went into the music too, wove itself under the melody like a descant, and the nine shapes in the nine chairs leaned forward.

* * *

What happened next, I can only describe as an exhale.

The choir had been holding its breath for fifty years. That sounds like metaphor. It was not. When Ossie and I opened the reliquary earlier — when we lifted the lid off that leaden box recovered from the evidence locker, the box that held not bones but breath, nine preserved exhalations kept warm so the Amberdales could keep living off them — we had felt the warmth of it. Breath doesn't stay warm for fifty years. Theirs had, because theirs had never been allowed to leave.

Now, as our four voices — one cracking, one flat, one frightened, one fraying but no longer falling — carried the final phrase toward its close, the nine shapes stood. All of them, together, the movement smooth and unhurried, the way a congregation rises. And they breathed out.

I felt it pass through me. Warmth, moving through the cold chapel like a current through wire — through Wren, who shivered and grinned; through Ossie, who wept without noise; through Marguerite, whose aging *stopped*, right there, mid-note, the frost-line in his hair halting its advance like a tide meeting a wall; through me, where it left something behind, some small settled peace I don't have vocabulary for yet. Nine exhalations, fifty years held, released at last into a room where someone was finally singing the ending.

They streamed toward the reliquary — visible now even to me, even to my unmarked, unmystical eyes: ribbons of pale gold light, curling, patient, each one shaped faintly like a song. They poured into the open box the way warmth pours back into a house when the door finally closes against winter. One ribbon paused. It hovered before Wren, close enough that her frost-marked skin flushed pink, and I understood — we all understood — that it was thanking her, or blessing her, or simply saying goodbye to the girl who was half its kind. Then it joined the others.

The reliquary glowed. Then, gently, it cooled. For the first time since 1974, the breath inside it was just breath — resting, finished, no longer fuel.

Above us, the last note of the frost-score bloomed into place.

The hymn was complete.

* * *

The silence afterward was enormous. Not dead air — I know dead air, I've engineered it, I've filled it in a panic at 3 a.m. This was different. This was the silence of a room after the final chord of a concert, before the applause, when everyone present is briefly the same person.

The nine chairs were empty. Not vanished — emptied. The candles guttered once, all together, like a nod, and settled back to their patient burning.

Marguerite sat down on the altar steps. His hair was gray — really gray now, honestly earned, perhaps fifteen years older than the man who had signed on at midnight — and his hands trembled, and he was laughing, quietly, helplessly, the laugh of a man who has set down something he forgot he was carrying.

"I thought—" He stopped, wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "I had it all planned. The terms, the trade, the clean arithmetic of it. One life for nine. Mine. Tidy." He looked up at us, and the candlelight made him look, for a moment, like every caller he'd ever taken — lonely, grateful, astonished to be heard. "You ruined the math."

"The math was always wrong," Wren said. She sat down beside him, uninvited, the way family does. "You don't get to subtract yourself. That's not how it adds up. Ask literally any of the dead people you've been taking calls from for twelve years."

Ossie lowered himself down with a grunt, joints cracking like green wood, and took off his cap, and held it against his chest the way he did at gravesides. "Fifty years," he said. "Fifty years I heard them under the ground and told myself it was water in the pipes." He shook his head slowly. "Water in the pipes. Lord."

I sat down last. The stone was cold through my clothes and I have never in my life been so glad to feel cold — honest cold, ordinary cold, cold that meant nothing except that we were underground and alive and the debt was paid and none of us had died to pay it.

Four voices. One desk, soon. I was already rewriting the show in my head — the name, the format, the sign-on. But that was for later. Above us, far above, through fifty feet of earth and the old chapel and the July morning that must be waiting there, the frost was finishing its final pattern, and the door—

The door at the top of the stairs swung wide.

Morning came down the steps like a benediction.
