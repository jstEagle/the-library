## 22. Sign-On

The clock above the mixing desk still ran four minutes fast, and Marguerite had decided he would never fix it.

Some things you kept broken on purpose. The clock was one. The scar of frost-scarring along the baseboard beneath the window was another — the maintenance men had offered to sand it out twice, and twice he'd told them to leave it, and they'd looked at him the way people look at a man who keeps a dent in his car because of who was sitting in it when the dent happened.

It was the last week of October, and the show came back on air tonight with a new name.

*The Long Night, With Friends.*

Devi had fought for it through three meetings and one almost-argument with the station manager, who wanted something punchier, something with *Midnight* in it, something that sounded like every other show between twelve and four. Devi had stood in his office with her arms crossed and said, "He spent twelve years alone at this desk pretending he wasn't. The name is the apology. Take it or take the format back." And the station manager, who had heard about the night the transmitter played a hymn by itself for forty-one minutes while the whole building sat dark, had taken it.

Four voices at the desk instead of one. That had been Wren's idea, actually, delivered in the flat, certain way she delivered everything now, the way of someone who had attended her own funeral and found it boring. "You shouldn't be alone in there," she'd said. "Not ever again. Not even for an hour."

So: four chairs. Four mugs — Ossie's was enamel, chipped, older than the station; Wren's was covered in stickers of bands that hadn't existed when the choir went behind the door; Devi's said WORLD'S OKAYEST PRODUCER in letters that were peeling. His own was plain white ceramic, and he liked it that way. Plain things could hold anything.

Marguerite arrived at eleven, as he always had, but now the parking lot wasn't empty. Now there were three other sets of headlights cutting through the cold, and the sight of them did something to his chest that he was still learning to let happen without flinching from it.

* * *

The studio smelled different since the descent. He couldn't explain it to anyone who hadn't been down there, and even among the four of them they rarely spoke of it directly. But the air had changed — thinner somehow, cleaner, like a room after all the windows have finally been opened in spring. The old smell of dust burning on the transmitter coils was still there, and the coffee, and the faint electrical sweetness of the board warming up. But underneath it, where for twelve years there had been something else — something he had never named, because naming it would have meant admitting he could smell it — there was nothing now. Just air. Just the ordinary breath of an ordinary building full of machines.

Ossie was already in his chair, feet up on an empty equipment crate, reading a newspaper he held too close to his eyes. Eighty-one years old and he'd come back to work within a month of crawling out of a hole under a chapel, because, as he put it, "I've buried enough nights. I'd like to sit through a few more awake."

"Paper say anything?" Marguerite asked, settling into his chair, putting on the headphones. They were warm. Devi had started leaving them on the radiator before sign-on, another small kindness in what had become a long chain of them, a rosary of small kindnesses he didn't know how to repay except by showing up.

"Same as always." Ossie folded the paper with the care of a man handling something fragile. "Chapel dig's been filled in. Town council voted to leave the ground consecrated and untouched. There's a plaque going up in spring." He paused. "Nine names on it. I checked."

"You checked."

"I wanted to read them out loud once." Ossie's voice was steady, the way it had been steady in the dark under the earth, steady when he'd laid his trowel on the altar like laying down a weapon after fifty years of carrying it. "Evangeline Marsh. Thomas Ives. Ruth Calloway—" He read all nine, slowly, into the quiet studio, and Marguerite listened the way you listen to a hymn you've learned not to sing along with but to know, so you'll hear it when it changes.

When Ossie finished, neither of them said anything for a moment. Then Devi came in with the log sheets and Wren came in eating cold pizza, and the moment folded itself away somewhere safe, the way good moments do.

"Board's hot," Devi said. "Phones are already lit."

Marguerite looked at the phone panel. Three lines blinking. Before, in the old life, blinking lights had been the thing he feared most in the world — each one a possible voice from six days dead, from fifty years deep, from under the ground. He had built systems against those lights. Tapes. Alibis. A wall of explanations mortared up as carefully as Ossie had ever mortared a door.

Now he looked at them and felt only what a host is supposed to feel: curiosity. Someone out there wanted to talk. Someone was awake at midnight and alone, and the light was blinking, and he could answer.

"We're on in ninety seconds," Devi said, and then, softer, bumping his shoulder with hers: "You ready?"

"I've been ready since July," he said. "I just didn't know what for."

Wren snorted. "Nobody ever does. That's the whole thing. That's literally the entire human condition, and I would know, I've seen both sides of it."

"Microphones," Devi said, grinning, "in sixty seconds, children."

* * *

The red ON AIR light came on, and Marguerite leaned in, and the familiar ritual began — but changed, the way a room changes when someone new moves in, same furniture, different light.

"It's the last week of October, friends, and it's cold enough tonight that I saw frost on my windshield before I even left the house. Which means it's time for the first broadcast of a show with a new name and an old heart. This is *The Long Night*. I'm Marguerite Amberdale, and I am not alone at this desk anymore, and I want to tell you why that matters in a minute. But first—"

He glanced at the others. Ossie gave him a thumbs-up. Wren was mouthing something at Devi that made Devi press her lips together to keep from laughing on air.

"—first, introductions, because if this show has taught me anything, it's that names matter, and saying them out loud matters, and waiting twelve years to say yours out loud is twelve years too long. On production, the person who has saved my life more times than I can count and exactly once in a way involving actual literal dirt: Devi Raghunathan."

"Once was plenty," Devi said into her mic, dry as chalk.

"On phones and general moral support, a man who dug graves for forty years and has the strongest opinions about soil quality of anyone I've ever met: Ossie Brand."

"Topsoil's a lie they tell you at garden centers," Ossie said solemnly. "That's all I'll say on air."

"And our youngest voice, who listeners may remember from a call some months back that we have never adequately explained and no longer intend to: Wren."

"Hi," Wren said. "I'm alive. It's great. Highly recommend."

And there it was — the joke that only the four of them fully understood, tossed out over the airwaves to ten thousand listeners who would hear a teenager being a teenager, and that was fine. That was more than fine. Some truths were load-bearing walls; you didn't hang everything on them. You just made sure they held.

"The reason for the name," Marguerite continued, and here he slowed down, because he'd written this part out and then thrown the notes away, because some things you had to say looking at real faces. "For twelve years I did this show alone. And I told myself I preferred it. I told myself a lot of things. And what I've learned — recently, expensively — is that the long night isn't the hours between midnight and dawn. The long night is any stretch of time you spend convinced that no one is coming, that no one can come, that whatever you're carrying you carry by yourself. That's the long night. And the only cure I know for it — and I paid retail price for this knowledge, friends, I paid more than anyone should — is other people. Not fixing you. Not even understanding you, necessarily. Just... refusing to let you pay alone."

He heard the silence on the line, the particular silence of a listening town, and he let it breathe.

"So the phones are open. And I'll tell you honestly what kind of calls we're taking, because honesty is the house policy now. We're not taking ghost stories tonight. I know. I'm sorry. If you've got a haunting, keep it warm till Halloween, we'll do a whole special. Tonight we're taking a different kind of call. The lonely kind. The three-a.m.-and-awake kind. The I-don't-know-why-I'm-calling kind. Because I spent twelve years answering the wrong question at this desk, and I'd like to spend the rest of my career answering the right one."

He nodded to Devi. She punched up line one.

* * *

The first caller was a man named Harold who hadn't slept properly since his wife died in March — not a death that called anyone back, not a death with frost on its windows, just an ordinary grief, which is to say an enormous one wearing ordinary clothes. He talked for eleven minutes about how the house sounded different, how the refrigerator hummed in a key she used to hum in, how he kept setting out two cups in the morning out of habit and then standing there holding the second one like an idiot.

"You're not an idiot," Marguerite said. "You're grieving. Those are different things, though they wear the same bathrobe."

Harold laughed — a wet, surprised laugh, the kind that comes out of people when they realize they're allowed one.

"My wife used to listen to your show," Harold said. "Every night. She said you sounded like a man keeping watch."

Marguerite closed his eyes for half a second. In his headphones, faintly, he heard Wren shift closer to her mic, ready — they all were, still, reflexively — for the voice to change, for the line to go strange, for the dead to speak through the living the way they once had.

But Harold was only Harold. Grieving, awake, holding two cups. And the line stayed warm and human and ordinary, and that ordinariness was the miracle now. Every unhaunted call was a small resurrection of the world's normalcy.

"She was right," Marguerite said. "That's exactly what I was doing. Keeping watch. But I'll tell you what I've learned about watchkeeping, Harold: it's not meant to be done solo. So here's what I want you to do tomorrow. Call your sister — you mentioned a sister in Duluth. Don't wait for a reason. 'No reason' is the best reason there is. And set out one cup, and drink your coffee, and let the house sound however it sounds. The humming isn't her absence, Harold. It's just the refrigerator. She's not in the appliances. She's wherever love goes when it's finished being a person, and I believe — I believe this with everything in me now — that it goes somewhere good."

A pause on the line. A breath.

"That's the first time anybody's said it doesn't get better," Harold said. "Everybody says it gets better. You just said it doesn't, and that's — huh. That helps more."

"It doesn't get better," Marguerite agreed gently. "It gets carried. Preferably not alone."

They took a caller who worked nights at the hospital and hadn't spoken to another adult in nine hours. A woman whose son had moved across the country and who called mostly, she admitted, to hear a voice answer. A trucker on Route 9 who wanted to request a song for nobody in particular, "just so somebody knows I passed through tonight." Wren took that one, and talked to the trucker about music for six minutes with such unguarded delight that Devi had to fade her mic briefly because she was laughing too hard to broadcast.

Between calls, Ossie read the weather. He read it like scripture, every front and pressure system, and afterward he leaned toward Marguerite's mic and added, unprompted: "First hard frost due Thursday night. Cover your tender plants. Say goodnight to the gardens proper. They earned it this year."

Only the four of them knew what it cost him to say the word *frost* on air, easy and plain, defanged. Only the four of them knew he'd practiced it in the parking lot.

* * *

At 2:40 in the morning, between callers, Devi handed Marguerite a slip of paper. A listener had emailed — not phoned, emailed, which was unusual at this hour — and the subject line read: *the window on Elm Street.*

He read it twice.

*I don't know if this reaches the right person. My daughter woke me up at midnight because the frost on her bedroom window had made a shape. She thought it looked like a musical note. I told her frost does that sometimes. But she said it was just one note, all by itself, in the middle of the glass, and it looked like it was waiting for something. She wouldn't go back to sleep until I promised to tell someone. So I'm telling someone. Sorry if this is strange. It just seemed like the kind of thing you'd want to know.*

Marguerite set the paper down. His hands were steady. He noticed that they were steady, and was grateful, and noticed that he was grateful, and understood that this was what healing actually looked like from the inside — not the absence of the old fear, but the arrival of a new steadiness alongside it, the two of them learning to share a body.

"Line four's open," Devi said quietly, off-mic, watching his face. "We don't have to."

"No," he said. "We do. That's the whole point now."

He keyed the mic. "Friends, a quick word before our next caller. Some of you may have woken tonight to find frost on your windows earlier than the weatherman promised. Some of you may find shapes in it. If you do — a curve, a line, something that looks almost like writing, almost like music — I want you to know it's nothing to fear. I know that sounds like a strange thing for a radio host to announce. I know how it sounds. But I'm asking you to trust me on this the way you'd trust a friend who's been somewhere you haven't. Whatever the frost is drawing, it isn't a warning anymore. It's finished being a warning. These days, I think it's just... saying hello. Saying thank you. Saying: you made it through the long night, and morning is coming, and here's a little cold lace on the glass to prove the night was real and you slept through it safe."

He paused, and the studio was very quiet around him, and outside the window over Devi's shoulder the October dark lay soft on the parking lot.

"If you see a shape," he said, "you don't need to send it to me. But if you want to — if it comforts you, if it makes you feel less alone at 3 a.m., which is the only job I've ever really had — send it along. I'll keep them. All of them. I'm keeping a lot of things these days that I used to throw away."

* * *

They signed off at four, the way they always had, but differently. Where once he'd ended alone — *this has been Marguerite Amberdale, goodnight, sleep if you can* — now it was a relay, each of them taking a piece.

"From all of us at the long night desk," Devi said, "thanks for keeping us company."

"Mind your topsoil," said Ossie.

"Stay alive," said Wren, and then, catching herself, "—it's a whole thing. Goodnight."

"And from me," Marguerite said, leaning into the mic for the last words, the way a man leans into a doorway at the end of a long shift, looking out at the street he's watched over, "goodnight, Amberdale. Sleep well. Nobody's calling you from anywhere you need to worry about. And if you're lonely — call us tomorrow. Or call anybody. Or knock on a neighbor's door. The night is long, but it is not endless, and you were never meant to stand it alone. That's the show. Goodnight."

The light went off. The transmitter hummed its low, faithful hum.

* * *

Devi and Wren left first, arguing cheerfully about whether Wren could get a segment where she reviewed local haunted houses "as a professional." Ossie lingered, folding his newspaper, and at the door he stopped and looked back at Marguerite with those old grave-digger's eyes that had seen everything twice.

"Thursday," Ossie said. "Hard frost, they're saying."

"I heard."

"You'll check the window?"

"I'll check the window."

Ossie nodded, satisfied, and went out into the cold, and his footsteps crunched away across the lot like a man walking home from church.

Marguerite stayed. He always stayed a little now — ten minutes, fifteen, letting the studio settle, letting the machines cool, letting himself feel the shape of the room that had nearly killed him and had, in the end, given him back his life at a discount, four ways. He washed the mugs. He straightened the chairs. He stood at the window with his coffee going cold and looked east, where the sky was thinking about getting lighter.

On Thursday, the frost came as promised.

He was home, in the old Amberdale house that no longer felt like a debt being serviced, and he woke before dawn the way he often did now — not from nightmares, but from the simple animal habit of having survived something, the body still keeping its own vigil. He padded downstairs. His breath fogged. The kitchen window was furred white, edge to edge, the last frost of autumn according to every forecast, the kind that kills the garden and starts the long honest sleep of winter.

He stood in front of it with his hands wrapped around a warm mug, and waited, without hurry, the way you wait for a friend you know is coming.

There. In the center of the glass, where the frost had thinned just enough: a single mark. One clean curve, one stem. A note — the final note, the resolving one, the one the hymn had been reaching for across fifty years and twelve and one long summer. It sat alone in the middle of all that white lace, complete, needing nothing before it and nothing after.

And beneath it, drawn smaller, in lines so fine they might have been breath itself:

*thank you*

Marguerite pressed his palm flat against the cold glass, over the note, the way you'd press a hand to a shoulder. The frost melted in a small clear circle around his warmth, and through the little window it opened he could see the sky going pale over the chapel hill, and the town waking under it, and smoke rising from chimneys, and somewhere a car starting, and somewhere a kettle, and everywhere — he believed this now, all the way down, with the faith of a man who had audited the accounts of the dead — everywhere, people beginning their days, none of them alone unless they chose to be, and most of them, thanks to something stubborn and warm and shared, choosing not to.

"Anytime," he said quietly, to the window, to the hill, to the nine, to whoever was listening. "Anytime at all."

Then he put the kettle on for four cups, because Devi would be over by eight, and Ossie never could resist a Thursday, and Wren had keys now, and the long night was over, and the morning, at last, belonged to everyone.
