## 9. The Well Refills

The knock came during third period, which was suspicious in itself, because nobody knocked at Hollowmere Consolidated School. They arrived. The building's doors had the particular gravity of mouths.

Mara was mid-sentence about the water cycle—evaporation, condensation, and the part where rain fell back down and everyone pretended that was the end of it—when the door opened and Mayor Aldous Voss stepped into his classroom as though he owned not only the room but the concept of rooms.

He did, in a sense. The plaque by the front office said the school had been endowed by the Voss family in 1908, "in perpetuity," a word Mara had always found faintly threatening when applied to real estate.

"Mr. Zhou." Voss's voice was warm the way a bank lobby is warm: heated, deliberate, and designed to make you feel that everything here is fine and also that you are being watched by cameras. "Don't let me interrupt the hydrology."

"You're not interrupting," Mara said. "We've just learned that clouds are basically lazy rivers going uphill."

A ripple of laughter from the students—the good kind, the kind that meant they'd actually been listening—and Voss smiled with them, but his eyes went around the room the way an auditor's pen goes down a column. Desk by desk. Face by face. Mara watched him do it and felt something cold settle behind his sternum, because he recognized the gesture. He'd seen Headmistress Voss do the same thing on his first day. He'd assumed it was a family habit of attentiveness.

It looked different now. It looked like counting.

Twenty-two desks occupied. Seven empty. Ilsa Brandt's desk, third row by the window, still sat scrubbed and bare, as if it had never held anything but morning light.

* * *

Voss stayed for eleven minutes. Mara knew this precisely, because documenting things had become less a professional habit than a nervous system function. His battered notebook lived in his jacket pocket now, and his hand drifted toward it the way other men's hands drifted toward cigarettes.

The mayor moved along the back wall first, examining the construction-paper food chains the students had made. A hawk eating a mouse eating grass. A shark eating a seal eating fish. Wren Aldery had drawn something more ambitious: a long vertical diagram of a lake, surface to mud, labeled in her small precise hand—*sunlight zone, twilight zone, midnight zone, the part we don't check.*

"That one," Voss said, tapping the paper above Wren's head. She didn't flinch, which impressed Mara. Most adults flinched around this man without knowing why.

"It's very thorough," Voss said to Mara. "You're a thorough teacher."

"Thoroughness is mostly anxiety with a filing system," Mara said, and got another laugh, and noticed that Voss didn't laugh this time. He just looked at Mara a moment longer than the joke deserved, filing *that*, too.

Then the mayor did the tour. He asked a boy named Pell what he was reading (*Treasure Island*, sir, there's a mutiny coming), asked a girl named Odile whether she liked mathematics (she said no with such conviction that Voss appeared genuinely pleased), and stopped beside Tomas Brandt's desk for exactly two seconds longer than he stopped anywhere else. Tomas stared straight ahead. His knuckles were white around his pencil, and Mara saw the boy mouth something—not words, just breath shaped like words—and Voss moved on.

When the bell rang, the students poured out with the usual thunder of adolescence, and Voss remained, standing at the front of the emptying room like a man waiting for a tide to go out so he could inspect what it left behind.

"I'll be brief," he said. "I wanted to welcome you properly. My sister runs the school, but the town and the school are—" he paused, choosing, "—interlocking accounts. What affects one affects the other. And I hear you keep records."

"I'm a teacher," Mara said. "Record-keeping comes with the disease."

"So I understand." Voss smiled. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "May I see your notebook?"

* * *

There are moments in a life that arrive pre-labeled, like parcels. Mara would think, later, that he actually heard the label being printed somewhere behind the world: *This is the one where it stops working.* Because until that moment, invisibility had been his superpower. In every place he'd fled before Hollowmere—the graduate program, the city school where his classroom management had been described in a review as "enthusiastic chaos"—he had survived by being unremarkable, by keeping his head down and his failures folded small. Nobody audits a substitute. Nobody counts a man who has already counted himself out.

But Mayor Voss was asking to see the notebook, and the notebook contained, among lesson plans and grocery lists and a doodle of the water cycle wearing sunglasses:

— the exact number of desks versus students, dated September 3rd.
— the fact that Ilsa Brandt's desk was cleared overnight, with the time he'd confirmed it (6:40 a.m.) and the observation that no report had been filed anywhere.
— the enrollment figures he'd pulled going back decades, and the pattern he hadn't yet written down in full because writing it down made it real: twenty-three, twenty-two, twenty-one. Minus one. Every autumn. Like clockwork. Like tithing.
— a sentence he'd crossed out twice and rewritten anyway: *Nobody asks where they go. Nobody asks at all.*

Handing that over would be like handing a wolf your diary and asking it to respect the parts about wolves.

"No," Mara heard himself say.

And then, because Voss's eyebrows rose with the mild surprise of a man who had perhaps never been told no by someone who needed him, Mara heard himself continue, smoothly, warmly, with a smile he could feel sitting wrong on his own face:

"I mean—I'd love to, but it wouldn't be fair to you. It's a mess. Half of it's me arguing with myself about cafeteria portions. There's a whole page about whether the vending machine counts as a food group." He laughed. "Let me clean it up. Transcribe the useful parts. I'll bring you a proper copy by Friday. You'll get the polished version, not the raw ore."

Silence. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the hall a janitor's mop bucket squeaked its slow, patient rounds.

"A copy," Voss said.

"A curated edition," Mara said. "Every author does it. The journal is for the writer; the book is for the public. You don't want my rough drafts, Mayor. Trust me. There's a sonnet in there about chalk dust. It doesn't scan."

Something shifted in Voss's face—not anger, nothing so legible. More like a man recalibrating an instrument that had returned an unexpected reading. He had expected the notebook handed over with the small apologetic eagerness of a new employee. Instead he'd gotten a salesman, and salesmen, whatever else they were, were people who understood what other people wanted and gave them a version of it.

"Friday, then," Voss said. "I look forward to the polished version." He extended his hand, and Mara shook it, and the mayor's grip was dry and cool and lingered half a second past courtesy. "One thing, Mr. Zhou. A piece of local wisdom, free of charge."

"Those are usually the expensive kind."

"This town keeps its books carefully," Voss said. "Everything that happens here gets entered somewhere. That's not a threat—it's a comfort, really. Nothing in Hollowmere is ever lost. Everything is accounted for." He released Mara's hand. "People find that reassuring, once they stop fighting it."

He left. His footsteps went down the hallway at an unhurried pace, and Mara stood alone among the abandoned desks listening to them fade, and realized his heart was doing arithmetic of its own.

*Friday,* he thought. *He expects a copy Friday.*

Which meant Mara had four days to produce a notebook so boring it could be read aloud as anesthesia, while the real one—his map, his evidence, his only proof that he wasn't losing his mind—needed to be somewhere no Voss would ever look.

* * *

That evening he sat in his rented room above the hardware store—he'd taken it because the landlady, Mrs. Tull, was deaf as a post and curious as a spy, and the combination struck him as survivable—and he began the forgery.

It was harder than lying. Lying was a sentence; a forged notebook was a whole autobiography. He wrote lesson plans in a slightly looser version of his own handwriting. He invented marginalia: *ask Odile about fractions again, she almost had it.* He included a complaint about the radiator, a list of supplies, a doodle of the water cycle now wearing both sunglasses and a tiny hat. He even manufactured a few dull observations about Hollowmere itself—*town very quiet, everyone friendly, pie at the diner excellent*—each line a small brick in a wall between himself and the family that had run this valley since before it had a name.

Around nine o'clock he caught himself pausing over one entry and smiling despite everything. Early September, second week, first frost:

*Ilsa Brandt absent today. Third absence this month. Asked her brother Tomas; he said she was 'staying with relatives.' Which relatives? He looked at the floor like it owed him money. Kids lie badly and honestly. Adults lie well and politely. Somewhere in between is the truth, probably sitting in an empty desk by the window.*

He'd forgotten writing that. Reading it now, in his own hand, he felt the strange vertigo of documentation—that the notebook remembered him better than he remembered himself. It was the closest thing he had to a witness.

He copied none of that into the forgery. The forgery got weather.

By midnight the fake was done: forty pages of impeccable, soul-crushing blandness. He wrapped the real notebook in a plastic bag from the hardware store downstairs, and then, feeling ridiculous and doing it anyway, wrapped that in a second bag, and considered a third.

Where? Under the mattress—no, first place anyone looks, and Mrs. Tull vacuumed with forensic enthusiasm. The school—no. Never the school. Not anymore.

He ended up, on impulse, walking out into the cold with the notebook inside his coat, down the silent main street, past the diner (dark), past the church (darker), to the town's one nod at municipal pride: a drinking fountain, a bench, and a bronze historical marker erected in 1976, which read:

**HOLLOWMERE — FOUNDED 1802 BY FAMILIES OF INDUSTRY AND FAITH. THE VOSS, ALDERY, BRANDT, AND OKAFOR LINES ENDURE. "WHAT IS GIVEN IS KEPT. WHAT IS KEPT PROVIDES."**

Mara had walked past it a dozen times without really reading it. Now he read it three times. *What is given is kept.* He thought of Ilsa Brandt's cleared desk. He thought of Tomas saying *we gave her up*—no, wait, Tomas hadn't said that yet, that was still coming, that was a conversation Mara didn't know he was going to have—but the phrase surfaced anyway, unbidden, like a bubble rising through dark water.

Behind the marker, the ground sloped down to the old town well—dry for decades, capped with an iron grate, tucked behind a hedge nobody trimmed because nobody needed to. Mara crouched, worked the grate up—it came away easier than rusted iron should have—and peered down into blackness. Cold air breathed up against his face. Not stale air. Moving air. Air with intent, if air could have intent, which it couldn't, obviously, he was tired.

He lowered the notebook on its string of plastic bags until it hung below the grate, then replaced the iron cover and scattered dead leaves across it with the guilty efficiency of a man burying a body in a sitcom.

"There," he whispered to the well. "Keep that safe. That's an order."

The well said nothing, which he appreciated. Wells, unlike mayors, didn't ask follow-up questions.

* * *

He slept badly and woke before dawn to a sound he couldn't place—a low tone under the wind, steady as a held note. He lay in bed cataloguing possibilities: furnace pipes, the hardware store's compressor, a truck idling on the far road. None quite fit. It wasn't mechanical. Mechanical sounds stuttered. This didn't. It rose and fell so slowly it might have been breathing, if something the size of the town breathed.

At school, the day started normally, which by then Mara understood was Hollowmere's most alarming setting. He took attendance—twenty-two names, twenty-two voices—and tried to teach fractions, and mostly succeeded, and only twice caught himself looking at the floor as if it might explain itself.

It was during last period that Wren Aldery raised her hand.

"Mr. Zhou?"

"Yes, Wren."

"The floor's humming again." She said it the way other children reported a broken pencil sharpener. Several heads nodded. Pell added that it hummed louder near the stairwell to the lower hall, and Odile said her dog had refused to walk past the school all week, and Tomas said nothing at all, but he had gone pale in the specific way of a boy hearing a family secret discussed in daylight.

"Humming," Mara repeated. "Like machinery?"

Wren considered this with the seriousness of a scientist deciding which lie to tell a grown-up.

"Like humming," she said finally. "The kind you do when you know a song but you're not supposed to sing it."

After the bell, Mara walked the lower hallway alone. The lights buzzed overhead. The air came up the stairwell cold enough to fog his breath, in October, indoors, and beneath his shoes—faint, patient, unmistakable—the floor was humming.

He took out his real notebook, retrieved that morning from its well, and wrote:

*Day 31. The mayor wants my records. I lied to him. First time I've lied to anyone in this town, and I'm afraid it was the first honest thing I've done here. The floor is humming. The children already knew. Tomorrow I'm going to find out how long they've known, and what else they know, and I'm starting with the girl who draws lakes all the way down to the part we don't check.*

Below his hand, through the page, through the floor, the note held—low, unhurried, and getting warmer, like something below remembering a song it had been waiting a hundred years to finish.
