## 11. A History of Subtractions

The Hollowmere town archive occupied the basement of the old meetinghouse, which was to say it occupied a damp stone room that smelled like pennies and old paper, lit by three fluorescent tubes that buzzed in a key I was beginning to suspect was B-flat. The same note as the humming under the floor. I'd been noticing that note everywhere lately. I was trying not to read anything into it, the way you try not to scratch a mosquito bite.

"Sign here," said Mrs. Pell, the archivist, sliding a ledger across her desk. Of course it was a ledger. In this town, everything was a ledger. She had the look of a woman who had spent sixty years being interrupted by people asking questions she already knew the answers to, and she resented every one of those interruptions on principle.

I signed. "I'm looking for enrollment records," I said. "School records going back as far as they go."

"How far back do you think they go?" she asked, which was not an answer, but had the texture of one.

"I honestly don't know."

"Neither does anyone else," she said, with something like satisfaction, and led me down an aisle of shelving so deep the fluorescents gave up before the end of it. "Enrollment's in the blue boxes. Census is green. Property is brown. Founding families' materials are—" she paused at a shelf of black binders, spines stamped in gold that had gone the color of old teeth, "—not for browsing."

"What are they, then?"

"For reference," she said, "by appointment, by family members, or by act of God." And then, because I must have made some face, she added, "You can look at the blue boxes all day. Nobody's ever complained about the blue boxes." This struck me as a strange thing to say about archival materials, but I let it go. I was letting a lot of things go in Hollowmere. They were piling up somewhere inside me like unread mail.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about research: it is mostly tedium punctuated by moments where your own heartbeat startles you. For two hours I sat at a scarred oak table with a pencil (pens forbidden; Mrs. Pell had confiscated my pen at the door with the reflexes of a customs agent) and worked backward through the blue boxes.

The enrollment records were meticulous. That was the first strange thing. Not just meticulous—*lovingly* meticulous. Every year, every class, every student recorded in a fine copperplate hand, with attendance figures tallied in the margins like a bookkeeper who genuinely cared about the numbers. Somebody in this town had cared about these children very much, in the way you care about inventory.

I started with the current year because you should always know where you're standing before you look behind you. Twenty-three students. My twenty-three. Then 1987: twenty-four. 1986: twenty-five. 1985: twenty-six.

I told myself it was a declining town. Towns decline. The mill closes, the young people leave, the classes shrink. It happens everywhere; there's nothing in it. I kept reading.

1970: thirty-one students. 1960: forty-two. 1950: fifty-eight. A smooth curve downward, exactly what you'd expect from a town slowly emptying out, except—

Except it wasn't smooth. When I charted it—and yes, I charted it, because I am the kind of man who charts things when he's nervous, my ex-wife used to say I could turn a grocery list into a spreadsheet—the decline wasn't gradual at all. It stepped down. One student per year, every year, like a metronome. Forty-two, forty-one, forty, thirty-nine. Never two. Never zero. Never three. One, every autumn, with the regularity of a tax.

Towns don't shrink by metronome. Towns shrink in fits and starts—a factory closes, ten families leave at once; a highway bypasses you, half the class of '63 vanishes over a single summer. Real decline is jagged. This was arithmetic.

I sat back and looked at the buzzing lights and thought, very calmly, the way you think when you're standing on ice and hear it tick: *Somebody has been subtracting one child per year from this school for longer than anyone in it has been alive.*

And the second thought, arriving uninvited, wearing its little hat of dread: *And this year, the subtraction was Ilsa Voss. And next year—*

Next year there would be a new teacher standing in my classroom, counting desks.

* * *

Mrs. Pell brought me tea around four o'clock, unprompted, which after six hours alone in a basement felt less like hospitality than surveillance.

"Find what you were looking for?" she asked.

"I'm not sure yet," I said. "The records are very thorough."

"We're thorough people." She set the cup down and looked at my notebook—my battered, bulging, everything-in-it notebook—with an expression I couldn't parse. Not disapproval. Something closer to recognition. "Mr. Zhou. Do you know what the founding families did, before they founded anything?"

"Farming?" I guessed. "Timber?"

"Counting," she said. "They were factors. Agents for shipping concerns downriver. Their whole trade was knowing exactly how many of a thing existed at any given moment—barrels, bolts of cloth, men. A count that's off by one isn't a small error, Mr. Zhou. It's theft, or it's death. Somebody lost cargo, or somebody lost a life, and either way somebody pays." She smiled, and it was a perfectly nice smile, and I felt it go through me like cold water through a coat. "You strike me as a man who understands that counts matter."

"I'm a teacher," I said. "I take attendance."

"Mm," said Mrs. Pell. "Same thing, if you do it right." And she went back up the stairs, leaving me with tea gone lukewarm and the distinct sensation of having been weighed.

* * *

I want to be honest about the state of me at that point, because honesty is the whole point of the notebook, even the parts I'd never show anyone. I was frightened, but I was also—there's no dignified way to put this—*thrilled*. Some shameful engine in me had woken up. For years I had been a man whose life refused to resolve into pattern: failed marriage, failed novel, failed tenure track, a CV that read like a series of polite rejections. And now here, in a basement that smelled like pennies, was a pattern. Clean, repeating, verifiable. One per year. I had found the seam in the wallpaper of this town, and my hands were shaking, and part of the shaking was joy.

That's the thing about people like me. We don't investigate because we're brave. We investigate because the alternative—not knowing—is worse, and because somewhere underneath, we believe that if we can just get the whole thing written down correctly, it will have to make sense. Documentation as prayer. Attendance as a kind of prayer. I'd been doing it since my first day.

So I did the thing I always do. I reached for the next box.

It was the wrong box. Or the right one. The black binders were right there at the end of the aisle, gold-stamped, and Mrs. Pell had said founding families' materials were not for browsing, and I have never in my life heard a sentence that sounded more like an invitation. I looked up the stairs. I listened. Fluorescent buzz. B-flat.

I took down the oldest binder. Its cover bore no title, only a date range: 1898–1923.

* * *

The handwriting inside was the same copperplate as the enrollment records. Same hand, or same school of hands—the founding families taught their children to write the way other families teach children to pray. The binder was organized by season. Each page covered one autumn. Each page had columns:

NAME. DATE. AMOUNT. PAID.

Amount was always the same. One. Sometimes written as a numeral, sometimes spelled out—one soul, one scholar, one of ours—and once, in a shakier hand, *one more, God forgive the arithmetic*. Paid was always a date. Payment rendered within days of the entry, usually the same week the school year began.

I flipped pages with a pencil, because you don't touch things like this with your skin, though I couldn't have told you why. Names scrolled past. Aldery. Voss. Brandt. Okafor—so the foreman's people went back further than he probably knew. Pell. All the founding names, taking turns, decade after decade, entering into the record the names of children who appeared in the blue-box enrollment rolls one year and not the next. Cross-referenced. Reconciled. The ledgers balanced against each other perfectly, across a century, and the perfection of it was the most frightening thing in the room. You could forgive a town its tragedies. You could even, at a stretch, forgive a town a ritual, born of famine or fever, clung to out of fear. But this wasn't fear. Fear is sloppy. This was bookkeeping. Somewhere beneath Hollowmere was a creditor, and these families had been servicing the debt for a hundred and twenty years, on schedule, without missing a payment, and they had raised their children to hold the pen.

I found Ilsa Voss's grandmother's name in a 1954 entry. I found Wren Aldery's great-uncle in 1961. The past wasn't behind any of them. It was a column they were all still standing in.

Then I turned a page and stopped breathing for what I would estimate was four full seconds, because I later counted them, because of course I did.

The entry was dated September 1911. The hand was firm and beautiful. The name in the NAME column was:

**ZHOU, E.**

*Elias Zhou.* Amount: one. Paid: October 2nd, 1911.

I read it eleven times. The letters didn't rearrange themselves. The ink stayed 1911-brown and patient. Elias Zhou. There had been a Zhou in Hollowmere before there had ever been a Zhou in my family's village in Guangdong, before my grandfather's grandfather had learned there was an ocean. A Chinese man in a Vermont mountain town in 1911, entered in a founding family's ledger, paid in full on the second of October.

Unless it wasn't a man. Unless Zhou meant something else in whatever private language these people counted in. Unless it was coincidence, and coincidences happen, and I am a rational man who does not believe that a hundred-year-old ledger reaches across time to touch a specific person—

But my hands knew better than my philosophy. My hands were shaking so hard I had to put the pencil down.

Because here is the detail that undid me, the one I've turned over every night since: the DATE column. September 1911. And beside the name, in the margin, in the same copperplate, a small annotation, the only annotation I'd seen in the entire binder. It said:

*Expected. Will arrive by the north road. See that the desk is ready.*

The desk. Thirty desks for twenty-three students. I had counted them on my first day and assumed sloppiness, surplus, some bureaucratic rounding error. But desks in Hollowmere weren't furniture. They were reservations.

I sat in the buzzing dark for a long time. Somewhere above me a door opened and closed—Mrs. Pell, or the wind, or the building settling—and I understood, with the calm of a man watching water rise past his ankles, that I had never chosen Hollowmere. The posting no one wanted. The bus with no return schedule. The ad I'd answered that I could never afterward find again. I had applied for a job the way a check gets written: because someone, a hundred and eleven years ago, had entered my name in a column, and the ledger does not forget, and the ledger does not forgive, and the ledger, apparently, does not care that the Zhou family had never heard of Vermont.

*Expected. Will arrive by the north road.*

I copied the page. All of it, every column, every annotation, in my notebook, in my own hand, because if this town ran on records then records were the battlefield, and I intended to be armed. My pencil scratched in the dark like something alive. When I finished, I slid the black binder back onto its shelf, aligning its spine with the others to the millimeter, because some animal part of me believed that if it noticed I'd touched it, that would be bad.

At the top of the stairs, Mrs. Pell was waiting with my coat.

"Lose track of time?" she asked.

"A little."

"The blue boxes will keep," she said. "They always do." And then, as I shrugged into my coat, gently, almost kindly: "Mind the fog on your walk home, Mr. Zhou. It's thick tonight. Easy to lose your way between here and the schoolhouse. People have been known to wander." A pause. "We'd hate to enter it wrong in the record."

I walked home fast. Behind me the archive lights clicked off, tube by tube, three notes descending, and under the streetlamps the fog came up the hill from the direction of the school, and it smelled faintly, unmistakably, of chalk.

In my room I locked the door, set the notebook on the desk, and stared at it for an hour. Tomorrow I would go to the headmistress. Tomorrow I would ask Headmistress Voss, politely, professionally, what the founding families thought they were owed, and whether accounts could be settled some other way.

But tonight, before sleep, I did the only thing that has ever steadied me. I opened the notebook to a fresh page and wrote at the top, in careful letters:

*Mara Zhou. Present.*

And below it, because the building hummed its B-flat through the floorboards and I needed to hear a voice, even mine, even only in my head: *Present. Present. Present.*

Outside, the fog pressed against the window like a reader leaning close.
