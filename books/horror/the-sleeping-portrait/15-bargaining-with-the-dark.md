## 15. Bargaining With the Dark

Mara didn't sleep so much as lie in the dark of his rented room above the pharmacy, listening to the house settle and trying to decide which of the settling sounds were houses and which were not.

He had spent the evening doing what he always did when the world stopped making sense: he made lists. The notebook lay open on his chest in the lamplight, pages soft as cloth from handling. *Facts,* he'd written at the top, because facts had a way of behaving themselves once you named them.

*Fact one:* Ilsa Brandt is gone, and no one will say her name except her brother, who begged me not to write it down, which I did anyway, because writing it down is the only prayer I know.

*Fact two:* My surname appears in a ledger from 1911. PAID, says the column. I was paid before I was born, apparently, which strikes me as an accounting error.

*Fact three:* Headmistress Voss keeps a page with my name on it, entered before I ever applied for this job. Which means either I never chose Hollowmere, or Hollowmere chose me, or — and this is the option my hand shakes over — there is no difference between those two sentences.

*Fact four:* Twenty-two students came to class today. There are twenty-three names on my roster. Tomorrow there may be twenty-one. The arithmetic has a direction, and the direction is down.

He closed the notebook. In the silence that followed he heard it again, faintly, through the floorboards and the foundation and however many feet of Hollowmere soil: a hum. Not mechanical. Not animal. It had the patient pitch of something humming to itself while it worked, the way his grandmother used to hum while she shelled peas, unhurried, certain of the bowl.

"Okay," Mara said out loud, to the ceiling, to the town, to whatever was listening with its pea-shelling patience. "Okay. Let's talk terms."

---

He dressed carefully the next morning, which was absurd and he knew it. If you're going to offer your life to a century-old death cult, you should at least wear the good sweater. It was forest green and his sister had bought it for him two birthdays ago, back when his failures were smaller and his family still called at holidays. He buttoned his coat over it like armor made of knitwear.

The walk to Voss House took him past the school. He made himself look at it. The excavation shaft at the east side was fenced now with orange plastic mesh that flapped in the wind like the world's saddest festival banner. Forty feet down, at the bottom of that hole, was a door with its bolts thrown from the inside. Somebody had locked themselves in. Or somebody had been locked in. Or — and here was the thought he kept stepping on like a loose stair — somebody had *volunteered*, long ago, and the volunteering had become a tradition, and the tradition had become a column in a ledger labeled PAID.

The school windows caught the morning sun and threw it back, ordinary and golden. Twenty-three desks inside. He counted them every day now. It was becoming a tic, like checking a lock.

Voss House stood at the top of Founders' Hill, a gray stone pile with white trim and a garden so precisely maintained it looked retouched. Gnomes, Mara noted. Actual garden gnomes, six of them, arranged in a semicircle by the path. He found this disproportionately comforting. Evil, in his experience as a reader of novels, did not own gnomes. Then he looked closer and saw that each gnome's eyes had been painted slightly too far apart, and each one faced not the street but the hill behind the house, where the ground sloped down toward the school, and he revised his theory of evil accordingly.

Headmistress Voss answered the door herself. She was small and silver-haired and wore a cardigan the color of weak tea, and she smiled at him the way a librarian smiles at someone returning a book visibly damp.

"Mr. Zhou. I wondered how long you'd sit on it."

"You knew I'd come."

"I knew you'd come eventually. You're a documenter, Mr. Zhou. Documenters can't leave a question mark in the record. It itches." She stepped aside. "Come in. I've put the kettle on. Negotiations go better with tea. That's not doctrine, just experience."

---

Her parlor was warm and cluttered in a way that seemed deliberately unthreatening, the way a spider's web is deliberately sticky. Photographs on the mantel: generations of Vosses and Alderys, all squinting slightly, all standing in front of the school, all positioned as though the building were a relative they were obliged to include. On the side table, between a doily and a dish of peppermints, lay a ledger. Old leather, brass corners, a ribbon bookmark hanging out like a tongue.

Mara sat on the edge of the settee. Mrs. Voss poured tea with the serene competence of a woman who had done ten thousand small kindnesses and perhaps four large cruelties, and who filed them all under the same heading.

"I'll come to the point," Mara said.

"Oh, I hope you won't. The point is so thin. Come at it sideways, the way you do everything else." She handed him his cup. "Sugar?"

"No, thank you. Headmistress — Ilsa Brandt. And the others. The ones from previous years. I've read the ledgers in the archive. I know what the column means. I know about the count, and the audit, and the thing beneath the school that must be given a number each season or it comes up to take one itself."

Mrs. Voss sipped her own tea and watched him over the rim with bright, interested eyes, the eyes of a woman hearing a student finally deliver the thesis she'd assigned weeks ago.

"And this year," Mara continued, and his voice held, which surprised him, "the number is me. My name's in the ledger. Entered in 1911, and confirmed — I assume — when I signed my contract. So here's my proposal." He set down the cup. His hands were steady. Someone else's hands, borrowed for the occasion, but steady. "Take me instead. All of me, one transaction, settled in full. And in exchange, the count stops. No more students. No more autumns with an empty desk. You close the ledger. You let the children grow up without knowing what their grandmothers have been paying into since before the well was a well."

The room was quiet. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below them — below the parlor floor, below the cellar, below the hill itself — the hum continued its slow work.

Mrs. Voss laughed.

It was not the laugh Mara had braced for. He had prepared himself for cruelty, for the dry cackle of a storybook witch, for mockery he could hate and therefore survive. Instead she laughed the way you laugh when a child proposes paying for the whole grocery store with a single shiny button — warmly, helplessly, with genuine delight at the shape of the misunderstanding.

"Oh," she said, pressing a hand to her cardigan. "Oh, Mr. Zhou. *One* transaction. Settled in full." She wiped the corner of her eye. "Forgive me. It's been years since anyone offered anything that sweet. You want to know the terrible thing? I believe you'd do it. I believe you'd walk down that shaft tomorrow with your little notebook in your pocket and your chin up, and you'd knock on that door and say *here I am, collect.* It's the most decent offer anyone's made in Hollowmere in living memory."

"Then accept it."

"I can't." She said it gently, the way you tell someone their flight has been cancelled. "Not because I'm cruel, Mr. Zhou, though you'll decide otherwise, and I don't blame you. I can't because you've misunderstood the instrument." She touched the old ledger on the side table, resting her fingers on it the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping dog. "You think it's a bill. A debt, payable once. But a ledger doesn't trade. A ledger *accumulates.*"

"Explain that."

"It isn't mine to explain, exactly, but I'll try, because you've earned tea and an explanation both." She folded her hands. "When your name went into that book in 1911, it wasn't a promise that you would be taken. It was a *record* that you belonged. Do you see? The entry doesn't expire. It doesn't get satisfied and crossed off. Your great-grandfather's arrangement — and yes, it was a Zhou who made it, a surveyor passing through, who took shelter one bad winter and ate our bread — his arrangement bound the name. All of it. Every person who carries it, forever, forward and backward along the line. The count doesn't want *a* Zhou, Mr. Zhou. The count wants the Zhou it was promised. And when the season comes, it takes what's nearest to hand that matches the entry."

The warmth drained out of the parlor so gradually Mara couldn't say when it went.

"So if you gave yourself," Mrs. Voss continued, almost kindly, "the ledger would note it. *Zhou, delivered, autumn.* A fine entry. A proud entry. And then next autumn the book would open again and find the same name still written there — because that's what written means, dear, it *stays* — and it would look around for another Zhou. And finding none—" she spread her small hands, apologetic, "—it would widen the definition. Names blur, near the bottom of a well. Zhou becomes Zhou-adjacent. Zhou-adjacent becomes *the man who taught the Zhour child.* You wouldn't be settling the debt. You'd be teaching it your address."

Mara sat very still. His tea had gone cold in its cup and he stared into it as though the leaves might rearrange themselves into better news.

"The students," he said. "If I go down there, does it buy them time? Even a year?"

"A year, perhaps. Perhaps a season. The count is patient but it isn't stupid — it knows a payment when it holds one. But Mr. Zhou." She leaned forward, and for a moment the headmistress fell away and something older looked out of her face, something tired and dutiful and horribly reasonable. "You would be buying time with the only coin that never runs out of denominations. Yourself today, Wren Aldery in five years, some frightened boy in ten. The ledger has been fed this way for a hundred and forty years and it has never once said *enough.* It cannot say enough. Enough isn't a word it has. It only has *more* and *next.*"

"That's insane."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "It's also arithmetic. We didn't write the rules. We inherited them, the way you inherit a house with a leak in the roof — you can curse the builder, but you still put out the buckets."

Mara stood up. His legs felt borrowed too, and less steady than his hands had been. "Then I'll change the rules."

Mrs. Voss sighed and gathered the cups onto their tray, hostess to the last. "They all say that. The young ones, when they first read the archive. They come up the hill full of fire and they say *I'll change the rules,* and we nod, and we pour more tea, and in twenty years they're the ones explaining the buckets." She walked him to the door, past the gnomes with their wrong-set eyes. At the threshold she paused.

"For what it's worth — and I suspect it's worth nothing, which makes it safe to give away — I'm sorry it's you. You're a good teacher. The children like you. Wren Aldery talks about you the way children used to talk about things before we taught them not to." Her smile returned, small and genuine and worse than any sneer. "Do keep documenting, Mr. Zhou. Whatever happens. Someone should have an accurate account."

The door closed. Mara stood on the step for a long moment, looking at the gnomes, at the hill, at the smoke rising gently from the school chimney like the town exhaling.

"Humiliation," he informed the gnomes, "is apparently part of the curriculum."

---

He walked down Founders' Hill with his collar up and his mind moving fast, faster than it had moved since the bus. Because here was the thing Mrs. Voss had given him, wrapped inside the refusal like a stone inside a pastry:

*A ledger doesn't trade. It accumulates.*

Fine. He was a teacher. He knew what you did with a system that only accumulated.

You found where it kept its totals. And you burned them.

It was a mad thought, a thought shaped like a wish, and he turned it over as he walked, testing it, the way you test a stair with your toe before committing your weight. The presence below counted. Counting required records — names, dates, entries, columns. Mrs. Voss had said it herself, hadn't she, in her reasonable voice: *the book would open again and find the same name still written there.* Written. Kept. Filed.

Everything it knew about what it was owed lived on paper.

And paper, Mara thought, stepping off the hill and onto the lane, paper has one great virtue that doors and wells and hundred-year doctrines do not.

Paper burns.

He was so occupied with the beautiful, terrible symmetry of this that he nearly walked past Tomas Brandt, who was standing at the corner by the pharmacy with his hands jammed in his jacket pockets and his face the color of old snow.

"Mara." The boy's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Mara, something's happening. The hum — everyone can hear it now. The dogs left last night, all of them, just — gone, over the ridge, Mr. Okafor saw them go. And the shaft—" Tomas swallowed. "The shaft's breathing. Warm air, out of the ground, and it smells like chalk. Like the classroom. Like erasers."

Mara looked down the street toward the school. The orange mesh around the excavation fence flapped and flapped. From somewhere beneath the town, beneath the lane, beneath his own cold feet, the hum rose — louder now, unmistakable, no longer deniable — and it had changed key. It sounded less like a woman shelling peas.

It sounded like someone clearing their throat before taking attendance.

"Tomas," Mara said quietly, "go home. Lock the door. And listen to me — whatever happens tonight, whatever you hear, do not answer to your name unless it's a voice you can see."

The boy stared at him. "Whose voice would I hear?"

Mara looked at the school, and at the hill, and at the thin gray ribbon of smoke rising from the archive roof across the square, where a hundred and forty years of names sat shelved and waiting, patient as kindling.

"Mine," he said. "Everyone's. Go on now."

And he turned toward the archive, already counting in his head — not students this time, but shelves.
