## 20. Voss Descends

Mayor Aldous Voss had performed the Founders' Audit thirty-one times, and in all those years he had never once been late.

He was late now. Not by much — four minutes, by the pocket watch his grandfather had carried down the same shaft before him — but lateness on Audit Eve was the sort of thing that got remarked upon at the winter supper, and Aldous had no intention of being remarked upon. He was a man who had built an entire public life out of being unremarkable in exactly the right ways: punctual, pleasant, forgettable at budget meetings and indispensable at funerals.

"Your tie is crooked," said his sister, Headmistress Prudence Voss, falling into step beside him at the mouth of the excavation shaft. She straightened it without asking, the way she'd been doing since he was nine years old and had ruined a funeral with a grass stain.

"It's always crooked."

"Yes," she said. "That's what makes it yours." She handed him the ledger. The year's ledger. Thin this season. One name.

Aldous did not look at the name. He never looked until he was standing in front of the door, because looking early was how you started arguing with yourself, and arguing with yourself on the ladder down forty feet of wet earth was how men like Denny Okafor ended up quitting in the middle of a paid contract.

"You're sure about the teacher?" Prudence asked quietly.

"I'm sure I asked to see his notebook and he told me he'd left it at home." Aldous adjusted his cuff. "Nobody leaves a notebook at home, Pru. Teachers sleep with their notebooks. It's the one romance they're permitted."

"He's thorough."

"He's *documenting*," Aldous said, and let the word sit there between them like something pulled out of the well. "There's a difference. Thoroughness is a virtue. Documentation is a door left open." He sighed. "After tomorrow we'll deal with him. Tonight is tonight."

The elders were already gathered at the rim of the shaft, seven of them, dressed in their good coats despite the mud, because the Audit was not labor, the Audit was *bookkeeping*, and you did not keep books in your work clothes. Old Marta Aldery stood at the front, ninety-one years old and built like a fence post that had decided to become a person. She held the lamp.

"You're late," she said.

"Four minutes."

"The well doesn't round down."

"The well doesn't have a watch, Marta."

"The well has *us*," she said, "and we have watches. Get on the ladder."

* * *

The descent took eleven minutes. It always took eleven minutes, though Aldous had timed it once, secretly, and found it took eight, and had never mentioned the discrepancy to anyone because some arithmetic you simply do not share.

The shaft was Okafor's crew's doing — blasted and shored through clay and old fill, past foundations nobody remembered pouring, down to where the ground stopped being ground and became architecture. At the bottom, the corridor began. Smooth stone, older than the town charter, older than the county, older than any document Aldous had ever signed or filed or burned. The walls sweated cold. Their breath came out white even in October.

And at the end of the corridor, the door.

It was fourteen feet of black granite carved with figures that might have been people and might have been tally marks wearing people's clothes. Across its face ran three bolts, each as long as a man's arm, thrown from the inside — which was the detail that had ended Okafor's career and very nearly ended several of the crew's sanity. A door sealed from within meant someone had gone in and chosen not to come out. Or something had come in after them and chosen the same.

"Positions," Marta said, and the elders arranged themselves in the old crescent, oldest nearest the door, youngest nearest the ladder. Aldous took his place third from the left, between his cousin Emeric and old Hollis Brandt, who was already wheezing and would be dead within two years, which was fine, because the Audit didn't require him alive much longer either way.

Prudence opened the ledger.

"We begin," she said, "with gratitude."

This was the part Aldous had always privately thought of as the customer service portion of the evening. Thirty-one years of it. You thanked the thing below for its restraint. You acknowledged the payment rendered across the seasons — names given freely, or given at least given — and you presented the current account for review. Then you read the name. Then the bolts stayed bolted, the hum stayed low and almost musical, and everyone climbed back up and had pie.

It had worked every single time. That was the whole genius of the arrangement, the thing the founding families understood and the later generations sometimes forgot: the thing below was not evil. Evil was unpredictable. The thing below was *procedural*. It wanted what it was owed, no more, and if you kept immaculate records it kept immaculate faith. Aldous respected that. He'd built a marriage on less.

"Gratitude," the elders echoed.

Marta lifted the lamp higher. The light reached the door and stopped, the way it always did, as if the granite were drinking it.

"Honored presence," Marta intoned, "keepers of the count below, we the undersigned present ourselves for the autumn audit. We bring the record of the season. We bring the payment agreed. We ask the count be accepted and the account closed."

The hum rose from beneath the floor. Low. Familiar. Aldous felt it in his molars, in the soles of his shoes, in the little bones of his ears. Somewhere above them, forty feet up, dogs were leaving town. He tried not to think about the dogs.

"Read the name," Marta said.

Prudence opened the ledger to the marked page.

And here Aldous made his mistake, small and human and four minutes late already: he looked at the page. He let himself see the name written there in Prudence's careful hand, the ink still faintly wet from this afternoon, and he felt the familiar seasonal grief arrive on schedule, right on time, unlike himself.

Then the hum changed key.

It was subtle. A half-step down, maybe less. Like a choir losing interest mid-hymn. Hollis Brandt noticed first — Hollis, who couldn't hear the supper bell anymore without his hearing trumpet, felt it in his chest and put a hand flat against the stone wall.

"That's wrong," he said. "That's lower than last year."

"Last year you said it was higher than the year before," said Emeric.

"And I was right both times! It's been climbing for a decade, one step each audit, and tonight it went *down*. You don't go down unless—" He stopped. Nobody finished the sentence for him, which was itself unusual, because finishing sentences was the founding families' chief athletic event.

"Proceed," Marta said, in the voice she used when proceeding was the only plan anyone had.

Prudence read the name.

The hum stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. The silence that followed had texture to it, weight, the specific quality of a room where someone has just turned around to look at you. Aldous had spent thirty-one autumns listening to that hum, and he realized only now, in its absence, that he had never once heard the door be quiet. Even in the years before the excavation, when the audits happened in the old sealed cellar under the school and the door was rumor more than fact, there had been the hum. It was the sound of the arrangement holding. It was the sound of *yes*.

Its absence was a different word entirely.

"Marta," Prudence said carefully, "the acknowledgment."

"There's no acknowledgment," Marta said. "I can hear that there's no acknowledgment. Give me a moment; I'm ninety-one, my moments are slower than yours."

They gave her her moment. The lamp flame leaned toward the door, though there was no draft. Aldous watched the flame lean and thought, with the strange clarity of a man whose life is about to change shape, *Denny Okafor quit over equipment arranged in a circle, and I called him superstitious, and I owe that man an apology and possibly a truck.*

"Perhaps," offered cousin Emeric, who was the family optimist in the way that a leaky roof is waterproof, "it's resting."

"It doesn't rest," said Marta.

"Perhaps it's *considering*."

"It doesn't consider. That's the entire point of it. That's why our grandmothers fed it instead of fighting it. You cannot negotiate with a thing that thinks, and you cannot outwait a thing that doesn't." Marta stepped forward, one lamp-bearing fence post against fourteen feet of granite, and raised her voice into the formal register that had not changed since 1849. "Honored presence. The count is presented. One name, given per the covenant. Acknowledge receipt."

Silence.

"Acknowledge receipt."

The silence got worse. Aldous could not have told you how silence got worse, but it managed it — thickened, deepened, acquired a listening quality so complete that he could hear his own pulse and, distantly, Hollis's wheeze, and underneath both, faintly, from somewhere far above, the school bell ringing midnight though it was barely ten.

Then the first bolt moved.

It did not slam open. Nothing so dramatic. The great iron bolt, thrown from the inside a century and a half ago, slid back two inches with a sound like a coffin lid clearing its throat, and stopped. Two inches. An installment.

Every elder took one step backward in perfect unison, which was remarkable, because none of them had agreed to do it, and Aldous would remember afterward the small shame of realizing that his body considered the ladder more important than his dignity.

"No," Marta said. Not loudly. With the weary authority of a woman who had spent nine decades telling things no — children, dogs, weather, husbands. "No. The covenant holds. The payment is made. You take the name and you stay below. That is the *arrangement*."

The second bolt drew back three inches.

"Honored presence—" Prudence began, and her voice cracked on the honorific, and Aldous heard it happen, heard his sister's certainty fail her in real time, and understood with total clarity that whatever happened next, the world they had inherited — the tidy, terrible, well-kept world of ledgers and pie and one name per autumn — had just ended.

Because here was the truth none of them said aloud, the truth the doctrine was built to avoid saying aloud: the arrangement had never been a treaty. It had been a habit. They had assumed the thing below kept their rules because their rules were correct. But a creditor who stops acknowledging payments hasn't agreed to new terms. A creditor who stops acknowledging payments has decided the debt was never the point.

Or — worse, and Aldous felt this land in him like a swallowed stone — has decided the debt was never *enough*.

The third bolt moved. And this one did not stop.

It drew back the full length of its channel, slow and even and deliberate, iron dragging through iron, and behind it Aldous heard — muffled, patient, unhurried — a sound he recognized from his own office. The sound of a page turning.

"Up," Marta said. "Everyone up. Now. Audit's concluded."

"The audit isn't concluded," Emeric said stupidly. "Nothing happened. The door didn't—"

"The door *answered*," Marta snapped. "Forty years I've done this and the door has never once answered. Up the ladder. Hollis first, he's slowest. Move."

They moved. Hollis went up wheezing, then Emeric, then the others, hands and boots on rungs slick with cold. Aldous paused at the base of the ladder — one heartbeat, no more — and looked back down the corridor.

The lamp was still burning where Marta had set it. Its light lay across the door, and in that light Aldous saw that the carved figures along the door's face — the tally-mark people, the figures he had looked at every autumn of his adult life without ever really seeing — had changed.

Not many of them. Three. Three figures near the bottom, previously smooth-worn and anonymous, now had faces. Small, distinct, unmistakable faces.

He knew two of them. Everyone in Hollowmere knew them. They had been missing students, entries in old ledgers, names given per the covenant, seasons closed, accounts settled.

The third face was newer. Sharper. Recently arrived.

It wore glasses, and it looked, Aldous thought with a lurch that had nothing to do with the climb ahead, remarkably like a man who kept meticulous notes and slept with his notebook and had told the mayor, quite politely, quite reasonably, quite *falsely*, that he'd left it at home.

"Oh," Aldous said, to no one, forty feet underground. "Oh, you *idiot*."

Above him, the ladder creaked under his sister's climbing weight. Below him, behind fourteen feet of granite that had finally, after a hundred and fifty years, begun keeping its own books, the page turned again.

Aldous climbed.
