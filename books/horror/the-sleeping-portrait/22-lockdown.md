## 22. Lockdown

The first thing Mara noticed was the silence of the radiators.

He had been at Hollowmere long enough to know the building's vocabulary. The radiators knocked like polite ghosts at 3:40, right when the last bus left. The pipes in the east wing groaned a phrase that, if you squinted your ears, sounded suspiciously like *one more year*. The front doors stuck in damp weather and sighed open in dry, and the third stair from the bottom on the main staircase had a creak so reliable that Wren Aldery used it as a doorbell.

At 4:12 on the afternoon after the audit ritual began, the building went quiet the way a person goes quiet when they're listening.

Mara stood in his classroom with a stack of quizzes he was no longer pretending to grade and listened back.

"Okay," he said to the empty room. "I hear you. What?"

Nothing answered. But the silence had texture now, the silence of a held breath, and Mara had spent enough of his life around institutions to know that a held breath in a building usually meant someone, somewhere, was deciding something.

He gathered his notebook, his copies, the duplicate ledger pages he'd sewn into manila folders labeled *PARENT COMMUNICATION — DO NOT FILE* because no one in the history of education had ever opened a folder with that title, and went out into the hallway.

The hallway was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name, which was the worst kind of wrong. Then he named it: the light. The windows at the east end, which at 4:12 in October should have been pouring amber across the linoleum in long slanted bars, were pouring nothing. Not dark — Hollowmere's autumn dark came late and gradual — but white. A flat, sourceless white, like the inside of an envelope.

He walked to the nearest window and looked out at the town of Hollowmere, and the town of Hollowmere was not there.

Fog. Not the creeping coastal kind he knew from his childhood, that came in on little cat feet and left again. This fog stood. It stood against the glass the way a crowd stands against police barriers — patiently, with enormous weight behind it, waiting for permission. He pressed his palm flat to the pane. The glass was cold, but not outdoor-cold. It was the cold of something that had never been outside at all.

Beyond the fog, where the Voss house should have been — the big white one with the widow's walk that Headmistress Voss's grandfather had reportedly built so he could *watch the road for debts coming in* — there was nothing. Where the church steeple should have been, nothing. Where Denny Okafor's excavation shaft and its circle of abandoned machinery should have been, nothing, nothing, nothing.

The fog had eaten the horizon and was working inward.

"Right," Mara said, because saying *right* out loud had become, over the past several weeks, the load-bearing wall of his sanity. "Right."

* * *

He found Wren in the library, and Wren found him, and for a moment they simply stood at opposite ends of the biography section taking stock of each other like survivors of a shipwreck confirming the manifest.

"Did you see it?" she asked.

"The fog. Yes. Did you—"

"The doors." She held up her hands, which were red at the knuckles. She'd been pulling at something. "The main doors, the fire exits, the loading dock. The fire exits won't open, Mr. Zhou. The *fire exits*. There's a bar you push. It's not complicated. It's the one thing in the whole building designed for panicked people."

"What happened when you pushed it?"

"Nothing happened. That's what happened." Her voice was steady in the way he'd learned to dread — the steadiness of a child who has had to be steadier than the adults for a long time. "The bar moves. The door doesn't. It's like the door and the frame are the same piece of wood now. And there's no gap. There's no *gap*, Mr. Zhou. A door has a gap. There's a line where the door meets the frame and light comes through. There's no line. I put my eye against it and it's just — wood. Continuous wood. Like the door grew there."

Mara thought about the stone door forty feet beneath the school, its bolts thrown from the inside, and felt a cold thought arrive and settle in like a relative: *a door has two sides, and a frame, and a gap. This building has stopped pretending otherwise.*

"The windows," he said. "The fog."

"Since the bells stopped. Did you hear the bells?" She didn't wait for him to answer. "Three o'clock. The bell rang three o'clock and then it rang again, and then it kept ringing three o'clock, eleven times, and then it stopped, and the fog was there. Like the building rang itself a warning and then closed the curtains."

"Where are the others?"

"Lower hallway. The little kids are with Ms. Ferro in the music room. Tomas is — " she hesitated, and the hesitation had a shape Mara recognized, the shape of something Tomas had done that Wren was still deciding whether to forgive — "Tomas is with them. He's being helpful. He's being *very* helpful."

They went down together. The stairwell's third step from the bottom creaked under Mara's foot, faithful as ever, and he was absurdly, disproportionately comforted by it — one thing still telling the truth.

* * *

The lower hallway was breathing.

There was no better word for it. The air moved in a slow tide, out of the classrooms and toward the far end of the hall, where the door to the boiler room stood, and then back again, out and in, out and in, on a cycle of maybe twenty seconds. The children had figured out the rhythm already, because children figure out rhythms the way water figures out downhill. Ms. Ferro had them sitting in a circle, and every time the hallway exhaled, a small boy named Pip held up a paper airplane and let the current carry it a few feet toward the boiler room door, and every time the hallway inhaled, the airplane came back, and the little kids shrieked, and honestly it was the most educational physics lesson Mara had ever witnessed.

"Mr. Zhou." Ms. Ferro stood, and her face did the thing faces in Hollowmere had learned to do: it smiled first and decided later. "We were just — the heating's acting up."

"The heating is breathing, Ms. Ferro."

"The heating is *cycling*," she said firmly, "which is normal for this time of year," and Mara understood, with a tenderness that surprised him, that she was not lying to him. She was lying to the eight-year-olds. The lie was a blanket she was tucking around them, and he could either pull it off or help her hold the corners.

He knelt down to the circle's level. "Pip. How far does the airplane go?"

"Further every time," Pip said, with the pride of a research scientist. "Yesterday it only went to the water fountain."

Yesterday. The word landed in Mara's stomach like a dropped stone. Yesterday the airplane had only gone to the water fountain. The hallway's breath was getting longer. The building was — what? Warming up. Stretching. Settling into its new job.

He stood and said, quietly, to Ms. Ferro: "We should move everyone up to my room. Second floor. Away from the far end."

"The far end is the boiler room, and the boiler room is fine—"

"The far end," Mara said, "is the door."

Ms. Ferro looked at him for a long moment, and then at the boiler room door, at the fog-white light seeping under it like spilled milk, and something in her face gave up the smile and let the decision arrive. "Music room, everyone. We're taking our instruments on a field trip. Single file. Nobody breathes on the way — I mean nobody *stops* breathing, Wren, don't be clever."

They went up. Mara took the rear, and as he passed the boiler room door he did something he would later have trouble explaining even to himself: he put his ear against it.

From the other side, faint and patient, came a sound like a very large ledger being opened. Not the sound — the *feeling* of it. A spine cracking wide. Pages settling.

He did not open the door. He was proud of that, later. There were perhaps four times in the whole affair when Mara Zhou did the wise thing, and this was the first.

* * *

By six o'clock, the school had finished introducing itself.

They learned it in stages, the way you learn anything about a person who has been lying to you for years — first the small confessions, then the large ones.

The small confession: the clocks. Every clock in the building — the wall clocks, the bell schedule, the ancient electric flip-clock in the front office that Ms. Ferro swore had been broken since 1987 — now read the same time, and the time did not change. 3:00. The hour the bells had rung eleven times. The building had taken the moment of the audit's beginning and pinned it to itself like a brooch.

The medium confession: the vending machine. Mara went to it at half past six because his hands were shaking and he needed the small dignity of a task, and found it fully stocked — which it never was; the machine had offered, in living memory, one bag of pretzels and a bottle of water with a 2021 expiration date — and every single item in it was the same: a small paper carton of milk, the kind that came with elementary school lunches, each one stamped with a different child's name. He checked. Twenty-three cartons. Twenty-three names. His own hand shook as he read them, and then he found the twenty-fourth carton at the back, and it said **MARA ZHOU** in his own handwriting, the handwriting from his notebook, the slightly cramped *M* he'd never been able to fix.

He did not drink the milk. He put the whole carton in his bag, though, because he had learned something in Hollowmere: evidence was a kind of weapon, and you kept your weapons close.

The large confession came at seven, and it came through Wren.

She had gone — against explicit instructions, with the specific speed of a twelve-year-old obeying instructions in spirit only — to check the gym, because the gym had exterior doors too, and because she said, "If the building is a machine, I want to see the parts." She came back up the stairs at a dead run and stopped in front of Mara, and she was not out of breath, which meant she'd had time to catch it, which meant she'd stood somewhere first and made herself calm, and that frightened him more than running would have.

"The gym," she said. "The floor."

"What about the floor?"

"It's not a gym floor anymore." She swallowed. "It's a page. It's — Mr. Zhou, it's a *ledger page*, the size of a gym. Ruled lines. The lines go all the way to the walls. And there's writing on it. Old writing. It's the same writing from the archive, the founder's hand, and it's — " her voice, for the first time all evening, wobbled — "it's a list. And the list has our names on it. All of ours. And there's a column."

Mara knew. He knew before he asked, and he asked anyway, because the ledger-keeper in him — the part that documented, that cross-referenced, that believed a thing written down was a thing you could fight — needed the column named.

"What's the column called?"

"*Paid*," Wren said. "And next to every name there's a little mark. Except — " she looked up at him — "except yours has two."

* * *

They gathered in Mara's classroom because it was the room with the most desks and the least windows — three, all now showing the patient white fog, all now covered with Ms. Ferro's construction paper and the children's drawings, because Ms. Ferro had decided, with the iron practicality of a woman who had taught for thirty years, that if the apocalypse was coming it could look at some crayon turkeys first.

Twenty-three children. Two teachers. Wren. Tomas, who sat by himself by the coat rack, doing a very committed impression of a boy who had not tried anything terrible an hour ago.

Mara stood at the front of his own classroom and looked at his own chalkboard, and the chalkboard was the last betrayal.

Because written across it, in his own handwriting — the cramped *M*, the unmistakable slant — was the day's attendance. Every name. Every one of his students, listed in the order he always listed them, alphabetical, with the little check marks he always made. And at the bottom, where he always wrote the date and the total, the board said:

**3:00 — 23 PRESENT — 24 EXPECTED — 1 OUTSTANDING**

He had not written it. He had not been in the room. But it was his hand, his abbreviations, his habit of underlining the total twice.

"It's keeping my records," he said aloud, to the room, and his voice cracked on the last word and he let it, because the children deserved to see that fear was allowed here, that fear could be in the room and still be in its seat. "It's — I've been writing everything down since I got here. Every absence. Every name. Every date. I thought I was building a map. I thought I was — " he laughed, and it came out wrong — "I thought the notebook was *mine*."

"It was," Wren said. She had come to stand beside him at the board, small and square-shouldered. "That's why it's his handwriting. You can't copy a person's writing unless you've been reading it. It's been reading you, Mr. Zhou. Since the first day. Maybe since before." She pointed her chin at the board, at *24 EXPECTED*. "It's not counting the students. It's counting you. We're the — " she searched for it, and found it, because she was an Aldery and accounting was in her blood — "we're the *collateral*. You're the account."

The radiator in the corner knocked. Once. Twice. And then, in a rhythm no radiator had ever kept, it knocked out the pattern Mara knew better than his own heartbeat: the little double-tap he made on his desk before taking attendance. *Attention, please. Roll call.*

The children heard it. Pip's hand crept into Ms. Ferro's. And in the silence after, from somewhere below the floor — below the boiler room, below the stone door, below everything — came a sound so soft that Mara would spend the rest of his life half-convinced he'd imagined it.

A page turning.

Then the intercom speaker above the door, dead since September, crackled once, gently, like a throat clearing.

Mara looked at it. It looked — if a speaker could be said to look — back.

"Everybody," he said quietly, moving to stand between the speaker and the children, an act of protection so useless and so human that Ms. Ferro would later cry telling her sister about it, "hands over your ears."

The speaker breathed in.

And in a voice Mara knew better than his own — because it was his own, recorded and replayed with loving accuracy, down to the little cough he always did before reading aloud — it began to recite his notebook. Entry one. September third. *Twenty-three students present. Thirty desks. Discrepancy noted.* It recited his observations, his fears, his 11 p.m. marginalia, his mistakes — and when it reached the entry where he had written the wrong date, October 4th instead of October 5th, it recited the error too, and then, kindly, patiently, in his own voice, it corrected him.
