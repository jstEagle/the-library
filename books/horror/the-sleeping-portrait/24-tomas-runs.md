## 24. Tomas Runs

Tomas Vey had always believed he was the kind of person who would do the right thing under pressure. It was a comfortable belief, the way a heavy coat is comfortable in summer—you never actually have to wear it, and so you get to keep thinking it fits.

He was wearing it now. The school had sealed itself an hour ago, and the coat did not fit at all.

The lockdown had come the way weather comes: no announcement, no warning siren, just a change in the pressure of the air. One moment the hallway doors were doors; the next they were decisions that had already been made without him. The windows showed fog where Hollowmere should have been—no rooftops, no water tower, no crooked steeple of the First Families' chapel, just a white nothing pressing against the glass like a face too close to a photograph.

And then the intercom had spoken.

It was speaking still, in a low, patient voice that used the mouths of missing students. Right now it was Ilsa's voice—Tomas's sister's voice—and it was reading Mr. Zhou's notebook aloud.

*"Entry forty-one: Wren Aldery drew the door from memory. Accuracy: total. She has never seen it."*

Tomas sat on the floor of the science classroom with his back against a cabinet full of glass beakers that rattled softly, like teeth, every time the hum swelled through the floor. Around him were eleven other students. Eleven. He kept counting them because counting was something his hands knew how to do, and because if he counted them often enough maybe the number would stay put. Numbers in Hollowmere did not like staying put.

Wren Aldery sat nearest the door. She had her knees pulled up and her arms wrapped around them, and she was watching the intercom speaker in the ceiling corner the way you'd watch a spider you'd decided not to kill yet—not afraid exactly, but respectful of what it might be carrying.

Tomas watched Wren too. He had been watching Wren for most of his life, if he was honest about it, which he tried not to be. She was Ilsa's best friend before Ilsa was given up, and after that she was the only person in town who still said Ilsa's name out loud, casually, like it wasn't contraband. That had made Tomas love her in a quiet, stubborn way that he had never once mentioned, because mentioning things in Hollowmere was how things got written down, and things that got written down belonged to somebody else.

The intercom crackled.

*"Entry fifty-three,"* said Ilsa's voice, gentle as a hand smoothing a bedsheet. *"Tomas Vey, age fifteen, reports his sister 'went to stay with cousins.' No cousins exist. Cross-reference: none."*

"Stop it," Tomas whispered.

The speaker did not stop. The speaker had never once stopped for anybody.

He looked around the room—at Priya Nair hugging her backpack, at the Okafor boy crying silently into his sleeve, at little Dev Marsh who had fallen asleep, actually asleep, which was either the bravest or the most doomed thing anyone in the room had done all night. And he looked at Wren, whose name he suddenly could not stop hearing inside his own head, loud as a bell struck in a stairwell.

Because here was the thought. Here it was, the ugly thing, arriving on schedule, the way ugly things do when the lights go out and the arithmetic starts.

The presence downstairs counted names. It collected them. It read them back. And there were twelve people in this room, and somewhere below, something was tallying heartbeats, and when it finished—if it finished—it would come up the shaft and take what the ledger said it was owed.

But ledgers could be amended. That was the whole religion of Hollowmere, wasn't it? You didn't fight the thing below. You *paid* it. You gave it a name each autumn and it stayed down where it belonged, humming its hymn through the floorboards, and everyone lived another year. His own family had done it. His grandmother had signed the page. His mother had held the pen steady while his father wept, and nobody had ever told Tomas it was wrong until Mr. Zhou arrived with his battered notebook and his terrible habit of writing everything down, including the truth.

So the rules existed. The rules had always existed. And one of the rules—the oldest rule, the load-bearing rule—was that a name could be substituted. Not traded; the ledger didn't trade. But offered. Entered. If a name was entered freely, by a living hand, in the dark, during the count—

Tomas's hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking the way you notice someone else's hands shaking, from a distance, with a strange academic interest, as though they belonged to a boy in a story who was about to do something unforgivable.

*You're not going to,* he told himself.

*I might,* he answered, honestly, because lying to himself now seemed like a luxury the room couldn't afford.

Wren shifted. Her eyes came up and found his across the dim classroom, and for one horrible second he was certain she could see the whole thought sitting behind his face like a guest who'd arrived early.

"You okay?" she mouthed.

"Yeah," he mouthed back.

That was the first lie. There would be others. There was, in fact, about to be the biggest one in the history of his short and mostly honest life, and it would take him less than twenty minutes to tell it, and he would spend the rest of whatever he had left paying for it.

* * *

The chance came at half past three in the morning, because chances in Hollowmere kept banker's hours.

Mr. Zhou had gone out into the hallway with Denny Okafor's walkie-talkie, trying to raise the foreman, who had gone below with blasting equipment and not come back. The door of the science classroom had unsealed just enough to let him through—one of the building's small cruelties, opening exactly far enough to let hope stick its head out—and then closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting.

Eleven students. Ten, with Mr. Zhou gone.

Tomas stood up.

Priya looked at him. "Where are you going?"

"Bathroom," he said. Second lie. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. Fear, it turned out, made some people stutter and some people smooth, and Tomas was a smoother, and he hated himself for it even as the words left his mouth sounding reasonable, calm, trustworthy.

The hallway was cold in a way hallways aren't. Cold like a held breath. The emergency lights gave everything a color between amber and bruise. Lockers lined the wall, and every locker was shut, and Tomas had the sudden crawling certainty that behind each metal door was a sound being politely suppressed—a breath, a name, a small wet counting.

The archive was two floors down. He knew the way because every kid in Hollowmere knew the way, the way farm kids know where the well is: not because anyone showed them, but because the town leaned toward it. The founding families' ledgers lived down there, in the old records room, behind a door with the Aldery crest on it—a door Wren had once shown him when they were nine, proudly, the way other kids showed treehouses.

He took the stairs. The hum rose through the steps to meet him, and by the third landing it wasn't a hum anymore. It was almost words. Almost his sister's voice again, saying almost his name, testing it, rolling it around like a coin in the dark.

At the archive door he stopped.

Here is what Tomas Vey expected to feel at this moment: nothing. He had told himself, on the stairs, in the cold clear voice of self-negotiation, that this wasn't really betrayal. Wren wouldn't even be hurt, probably. The thing below would take her the way it had taken Ilsa—quietly, overnight, desk cleared by morning—and she'd go to stay with cousins, and in ten years nobody would say her name, and that was just how Hollowmere worked, and it had worked that way since 1847, and who was he, fifteen years old, to redesign a system older than the lightbulb?

He had rehearsed this reasoning the way you rehearse asking someone to a dance. And like a rehearsed dance invitation, it collapsed completely the moment he was actually standing in front of the person.

Except the person wasn't Wren.

The person was the ledger.

It lay open on the archive table where Mara Zhou had left it—the original, the pre-doctrine record, the true book, the one Wren had stolen from her own family and given away at enormous cost. Its pages were soft as cloth. The ink had gone brown as old blood, which, Tomas supposed, in a few cases it was.

And there, near the front, in handwriting like fence posts, was a list of names. The founders' names. The first families. The ones who had built the door and thrown the bolts from the outside and sworn, in ink, that nothing below would ever be fed again.

Nothing below would ever be fed again.

Tomas stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame and felt the reasoning come apart in his chest like wet paper. Because this was the thing Wren had given up her family for. This was the book that proved the whole doctrine—the audits, the payments, the autumn counts, his sister's cleared desk—was a lie with a hundred-year head start. And he had walked down here, past ten sleeping children, to write a girl's name into the machinery that book said should have been buried forever.

His legs sat down without asking him. He ended up on the archive floor, back against the table leg, holding the ledger in both hands like it was hot.

"I'm sorry," he said out loud, to nobody, to everybody, to Wren most of all, who wasn't there and therefore couldn't refuse the apology, which was the only kind of apology he currently had the courage to make.

The hum changed.

It didn't get louder. It got *closer*—the way a voice gets closer when someone leans in—and Tomas understood, with the total clarity available only to people who have just done something unforgivable, that the thing below had heard him. Not the words. The shape of them. The intention, still warm, still fresh, still smelling faintly of Wren Aldery's name.

Somewhere above him, a door slammed. Then another. Then another, in sequence, descending through the building like footsteps coming down a staircase made of the whole school.

*"One,"* said the intercom, in Ilsa's voice, from everywhere. *"Two."*

It was counting its way down to him.

Tomas ran.

Not toward anything. Away—from the archive, from the ledger, from the boy who had walked down those stairs with a plan, from the plan itself, which he now wanted to kill more thoroughly than he had ever wanted anything. He ran up the stairs two at a time, and the count followed him, unhurried, *"three, four,"* each number landing on the step he'd just left, patient as interest accruing.

He burst into the science classroom. Everyone was awake now. Of course they were awake; the building was slamming its own doors like a metronome the size of a soul.

"Tomas—" Priya started.

"The ledger," he gasped. "Downstairs. The original. It's—it's just sitting there, and the count is going down toward it, and if it reads the founders' names—" He didn't know what would happen if it read the founders' names. But Mr. Zhou had gone down the shaft with that exact purpose, hadn't he? Reading the roll. Returning the debt. And if the presence got to the book first—

Wren was already on her feet. "Show me."

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." She was pulling her sleeves down over her hands, which was what she did when she was scared and refusing to file it under fear. "I stole it once. I can steal it back."

They went together, and the count went with them, *"nine, ten,"* and Tomas discovered that shame is a fuel like any other: it burns fast, it burns dirty, but it moves you.

* * *

They reached the archive as the last door slammed.

The room was freezing. Breath hung in the air like the ghost of breath. The ledger lay open on the table exactly as he'd left it—but the pages were turning. Slowly. By themselves. A page lifted, hung, and fell, and beneath it the brown ink seemed darker than it had been, fresher, as though the book were remembering how to be new.

*"Fifteen,"* said the walls.

"Wren." Tomas grabbed her arm. "Before it finishes—I need to tell you—"

"Don't."

"No. I have to." The words came up out of him like splinters, each one taking flesh with it. "I came down here to give them your name. To the thing below. I was going to write it in. I got all the way to the door and I couldn't, and I'm not telling you so you'll forgive me, I'm telling you because if I don't say it out loud it stays true, and if I say it, maybe—"

The pages stopped turning.

In the silence that followed, Wren looked at him for a long time. Her face did several things in order: surprise, hurt, a flicker of something that might have been understanding, and finally a tiredness so old it couldn't possibly have belonged to a fourteen-year-old and must have been inherited, handed down through four generations of Alderys who had all, at some point, looked at a ledger and learned what their family was.

"You couldn't," she said.

"What?"

"You couldn't do it. That's why you're telling me. The ones who can do it don't confess. My uncle never confessed anything in his life." She exhaled, and her breath fogged, and the fog drifted toward the open book like it was being read. "You're a coward, Tomas Vey. But you're the kind of coward who runs *back*. There are maybe three of us in this town."

Above them, muffled by floors and fog and fate, the intercom spoke one more time—but not in Ilsa's voice anymore. In a voice none of them knew. Old. Dry. Patient as a fence post.

*"Found,"* it said. *"Mine."*

And the ledger's pages began to turn again—faster now, faster, tearing free of their binding one by one and rising into the cold air like a flock of brown birds, streaming up through the ceiling, toward the shaft, toward the light, toward the man climbing down it with the same book's twin in his hands.

Wren snatched at the flying pages and caught one. Tomas caught another. On his, in fence-post ink, half a founder's name: **ALDER**—

"They're going to him," Wren breathed. "The names. They're going to *him*."

Tomas looked at the scrap in his fist, at the letters of the family that had built the door, and heard, very far below, a scream begin that had every name it had ever collected in it—including, he realized with a lurch, his sister's.

"Hold on to it," he said. "Whatever happens—don't let go."
