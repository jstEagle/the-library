## 27. Ledger Burners

The gymnasium had stopped pretending to be a gymnasium.

Mara noticed it the way you notice a stranger in your house—by what was missing. The basketball hoops were gone, not removed but *absent*, as if they'd never been bolted to anything. The floorboards had gone soft and pale underfoot, like paper left out in rain. Somewhere above them, where the scoreboard should have hung, there was only a rectangle of darker ceiling, the shape of something that had been taken down for repairs that would never come.

Twenty-one students sat on the bleachers in a rough crescent, and Mara stood in front of them with his battered notebook held against his chest like a shield he no longer trusted.

He had gathered them the way he'd learned to do everything in Hollowmere: by writing it down first. He'd torn eleven pages from the back of the notebook, folded each one twice, and passed them hand to hand down the hallways—which were still rearranging themselves at intervals, like a sleeper turning over—and each page said the same thing in his careful teacher's print:

*Gymnasium. Midnight. Bring nothing you love.*

Wren Aldery had added, beneath his message on hers, in her small severe handwriting: *He means it. I checked.* Which was, Mara thought, either very reassuring or deeply alarming depending on how much you knew about Wren's checking methods.

"Before we start," Mara said, "I want everyone to look at the person next to you."

They did. Twenty-one heads turned in unison, and someone—Devon Okafor's little cousin, Priya, who was twelve and had so far survived the night by refusing to be impressed by anything—snorted.

"Good," Mara said. "That's the person whose name you're going to remember better than your own by sunrise. If you get separated, if the halls move, if you end up somewhere that shouldn't exist—you say their name out loud until you find them or they find you. Names are the only currency this place respects, so spend them on each other."

"That's not how currency works," said Priya.

"It's how *this* currency works," Mara said, and got the small tired laugh he'd been fishing for. Gentle humor, he'd found, worked on frightened children the way a splint works on a broken arm—it didn't fix anything, but it kept things aligned while the real mending happened elsewhere. Or never happened. He was trying not to think about the *or never happened*.

He let the laugh die naturally. Then he held up the notebook.

"You've all seen me carrying this around since September. Some of you have made fun of me for it." He looked directly at Devon, who had the grace to look approximately sorry. "I want to tell you why I started, because it matters to what happens next."

He opened to the first page. His own handwriting from the first day of school looked almost childlike now, all that wary hope pressed flat between the lines. *Sept 3. 23 students. 30 desks. Asked Headmistress Voss about the extra desks; she said 'the school keeps its own attendance.' Thought that was a figure of speech.*

"I wrote everything down because writing things down is how you prove they happened," Mara said. "If a student disappears and nobody files a report and her desk vanishes overnight, then the only evidence she ever existed is whatever someone bothered to record. I thought I was keeping a record *against* this town." He turned the page. "I was wrong about what I was building."

He showed them the pages. The attendance tallies. The dates. The pattern he'd found in the enrollment records going back ninety years—classes shrinking by exactly one each autumn, subtraction performed with the patience of tides.

"This isn't just my notebook," he said. "It's a ledger. And here's the thing I figured out tonight, while the school was trying to eat the east wing—" Priya raised her hand. "Yes, Priya."

"The east wing is a supply closet now."

"The east wing is a supply closet now," Mara agreed solemnly. "Thank you for the update. The thing below us—the thing the founding families have been feeding names to for two hundred years—it doesn't run on hunger. Hunger is simple. Animals are hungry. You can wait out an animal." He tapped the notebook. "It runs on *arithmetic*. On records. On the difference between what was promised and what was paid. Every name in those ledgers downstairs is a debt it believes it's owed, and it can only collect a debt it can count."

Wren leaned forward on the bleacher, elbows on knees. She had ash—or what he hoped was ash—smudged along one jawline from the archive fire, and she hadn't slept, and she looked, Mara thought, more composed than any adult remaining in Hollowmere. "You're saying it's not the monster," she said. "The ledgers are the monster. It's just the collection agency."

"I'm saying the ledgers are the *cage*," Mara said. "And the lock is made of numbers. And we are standing inside a building that has become part of the bookkeeping—the doors won't open because we're entries now, we're filed, we're *in the system*." He heard his own voice go thin and steadied it. "But a system needs its records. All of them. Every copy, every duplicate, every backup the families have hidden in floorboards and Bible covers and—I'm guessing, Wren, correct me if I'm wrong—bricked-up fireplace flues."

Wren didn't confirm or deny. Her family's talent for concealment was, apparently, also a matter of doctrine.

"If every record burns before dawn," Mara said, "before the final count—if the last number goes up in smoke while it's still mid-tally—then it loses the sum. It can't finish the count. It can't collect. It doesn't know what it's owed anymore, because the proof is ash."

Silence. The good kind, the kind where he could see twenty-one minds actually working, which was the only sound a teacher ever really wanted.

Then Tomas Vane, sitting at the far end of the bleachers with his arms wrapped around himself like a man holding his own coat closed, spoke for the first time all night.

"And when it can't count," he said, "what does it do instead?"

Mara had known this question was coming. He'd rehearsed answers in the dark hallway while the walls breathed. Most of them were lies. He'd decided, looking at these faces, that they deserved the one that wasn't.

"I don't know," he said. "Probably something worse than counting. Probably loud. Probably fast." He met Tomas's eyes—Tomas, who had begged him once not to write a name down, who had tried, three nights ago, to give the presence Wren's name to save his own skin, and who had been living inside that shame like a wet coat ever since. "But here's what I do know. If we do nothing, dawn comes, the count finishes, and it takes exactly as many of us as the arithmetic says. That future has a number in it. This other future—the burning one—is the only version where the answer isn't already written down."

"So we're choosing the unknown over the known-bad," said Priya.

"When you put it that way it sounds almost reasonable," Mara admitted. "Teaching is mostly convincing children to choose the unknown over the known-bad. Long division. Adulthood. Same principle."

Another laugh, thinner this time, but real. He'd take it.

* * *

The plan, laid out on the gymnasium floor in chalk—real chalk, which Mara had pried from a supply closet that had briefly been the east wing and might be again—took the shape of a map of Hollowmere drawn from memory, which meant it was wrong in at least four places, and they corrected it together, which felt strangely like the best class he'd ever taught.

"Three fires," he said, tapping the chalk. "Not one. One fire is an accident. Two is suspicious. Three is a policy." He drew circles. "The town archive—that's half done, thanks to Wren and me. The Voss house has the family ledgers, the working copies, the ones they update every autumn. And the school itself." He paused. "The school's basement archive, behind the boiler room. Where I found the original records. Where the door is."

Nobody wanted to say *the shaft*. They'd all heard what came up it, counting.

"The school burns last," Wren said. Not a question.

"The school burns last," Mara confirmed, "because the school is the most recent entry in the ledger—we're *in* it—and I don't know yet whether burning the building while we're inside counts as erasing us or filing us permanently. So we save it for the end, and by the end, I intend for none of us to be in it."

"And the elders?" asked Devon. "Some of them aren't going to let you near their books. Mrs. Aldery sleeps with hers. I've seen it. It's under her pillow like a teddy bear."

"Some will fight us," Mara said. "Some will help. Mayor Voss is still down the shaft, or whatever the shaft is now, and half the old families' courage went down with him. Fear makes people cling to their ledgers—but so does grief, and some of those books are the only proof their people existed. We're not stealing, anyone who helps us. We're offering them the one trade this town has never made." He looked around the crescent of faces. "We're offering to let the dead stop being debts."

He watched that land. Watched it land hardest on the older students, the ones who'd grown up hearing the doctrine at dinner tables, the ones who'd been taught since birth that the counting was a mercy, a maintenance, a tax paid so the collector wouldn't visit personally. Wren's face didn't change, but her hands, folded in her lap, went white-knuckled and then deliberately, effortfully, relaxed.

"My grandmother used to say the ledgers were holy," Wren said quietly. "She said a name in the book was a name *kept*, like pressing a flower." She looked up at Mara. "I believed that right up until I read the original one. The pre-doctrine one. The founders didn't keep names in those first pages, Mr. Zhou. They *refused* to be counted. That's the whole reason there's a door down there at all. The thing below us isn't our god. It's our prisoner. And we've spent two hundred years feeding it because we forgot we were the jailers and started acting like the congregation."

"Which is why the original ledger matters," Mara said. "Why I'm taking it with me when we go down—not to feed it, not to bargain with it. To remind it." He didn't explain further. He wasn't sure he could explain further. There was an idea in him, half-formed and terrible, about reading the founders' true names aloud in the dark, and he was keeping it folded up small, the way you keep a match dry.

Tomas raised his hand. Actually raised it, like a student, and something in Mara's chest twisted at the sight.

"What happens," Tomas said, "to the ones who stay? The missing kids. Ilsa. The others down there." His voice cracked on his sister's name and he pushed through it. "If the records burn—if it forgets what it's owed—does it forget *them*?"

Mara had an answer prepared. It was honest and it was awful and he delivered it as gently as he could.

"They come home," he said, "or they don't. But either way, they stop being *payments*. Whatever they are down there, they'll be people again, not line items. That's the terrible cost, Tomas. That's the cost of the whole plan. We're not just burning the debt. We're burning the receipt. Everything this town ever bought with those names—every safe year, every quiet autumn—becomes unproven. Unpaid-for. We'll have lived two hundred years on credit and tomorrow morning the bill and the proof of payment burn together." He spread his hands. "There's no version of this where Hollowmere stays Hollowmere. There's only versions where the people in it walk out."

Priya, of course, had the last word before the vote.

"So basically," she said, "the whole town is a subscription service and we're canceling."

Mara laughed before he could stop himself—a real laugh, startled out of him, the first one in days—and the sound broke something loose in the room. Twenty-one students laughed with him, raggedly, hysterically, the way people laugh when the alternative is screaming, and he let it run its full course because he understood, finally, that the laughter wasn't a break from the fear. It was the fear, metabolized. It was proof they were still people and not entries.

"All in favor of arson," he said, when they'd settled, "raise your hand."

Twenty-one hands went up. Wren's went up last and highest.

* * *

They worked through the small hours, and it was the strangest lesson plan of his career.

He split them into teams the way he'd split classes for group projects, except the rubric was survival. Wren took four of the steadiest seniors to the Voss house, because Wren could open her own family's locks and because, as she put it with terrible calm, "if my mother points a shotgun at you, she'll hesitate pointing it at me. Probably. Fifty-five percent." Devon took the younger kids to the ridge road to prep Denny Okafor's trucks—he'd come back, Denny, guilt being a fuel more reliable than diesel—and Priya appointed herself Mara's shadow, on the grounds that "someone has to fact-check you, Mr. Zhou, you make mistakes."

"I do make mistakes," he'd agreed. "One of them is letting you out of my sight."

The presence felt them planning. He was sure of it. Twice, while he copied the last of the school's records by candlelight in the archive room—his duplicates going into backpacks, into coat linings, into the hollowed-out spine of a hymnal—every clock in the building ticked backward simultaneously, one single tick, like a throat clearing. Once, the intercom crackled and said, in a voice like chalk on slate, "*Thirty desks,*" and then, after a pause, "*twenty-two filled,*" and then nothing, and Mara had written the moment down because writing things down was how you proved they happened, and he intended, whatever else burned, to prove all of it.

By four in the morning, the teams reported back by code phrase—*the homework is done*—delivered in whispers through heating vents that no longer connected to anything sensible but had decided, temporarily, to cooperate.

Archive: half ash already, the fire having burned upward and slow, the smoke spelling numbers against the stars. Wren had seen it herself and refused to describe it further.

Voss house: the working ledgers secured. Mrs. Voss had stood in her nightgown on the porch and watched them carry the books out, and had not raised the shotgun, and had said only, "Mind the 1911 volume, the binding's weak," which Wren reported was either surrender or blessing, and possibly the same thing.

School basement: pending. Last. Dawn coming at 6:41.

At five o'clock, Mara stood in the rearranged hallway outside the gymnasium with his notebook open to a fresh page, and wrote the heading he'd been avoiding all night:

*Final attendance.*

Below it, he wrote the twenty-one names. Then, because it seemed important, because documentation was the only prayer he'd ever believed in, he walked into the gymnasium and read them aloud—one by one, slowly, looking each student in the eye as their name landed on them like a hand on the shoulder. Not a roll call. A christening. *You exist. You exist. You exist.*

When he finished, the gymnasium was very quiet, and then Priya said, "Okay. Let's go cancel the subscription."

Mara closed the notebook, and that was when the lights died—all of them, everywhere, at once—and from far below, patient as arithmetic, the counting began again.

It was 5:12. Dawn was at 6:41. And the voice rising through the floor was reciting names, and it had reached the letter *A*.
