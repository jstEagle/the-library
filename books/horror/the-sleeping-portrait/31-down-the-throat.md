## 31. Down the Throat

The shaft breathed at him.

That was the only way Mara could describe it. He stood at the rim with the original ledger wrapped in a plastic garbage bag and tucked under his arm like a loaf of bread he'd stolen from a very unfriendly bakery, and the hole in the ground exhaled—slow, warm, patient—air that smelled of chalk dust and floor wax and, faintly, of pencil shavings, which was the smell of every classroom he had ever loved.

"Nobody has to do this," Denny said for the fourth time. He was crouched by the winch, checking the harness buckles with the tenderness of a man who had decided that if he couldn't save anyone, he could at least make sure their equipment didn't fail. "I mean it. Nobody. I could rig the charges without you going down there. You could stand up here and read your names into a megaphone and we'd call it a day."

"The names have to be heard from down there," Mara said. "That's the whole point. You don't return a debt by shouting at the debtor's window. You knock on the door."

"You don't know it's a door anymore. It might be a mouth."

"Then it's a door that eats," Mara said, "and I'd rather be eaten holding the right paperwork."

Denny almost smiled. It was a terrible, exhausted almost, but it counted. "You teachers," he said. "You'd narrate your own funeral. 'And now, class, observe the deceased's poor posture.'"

"I'd at least use the whiteboard. Chalk's for people with dignity."

The wind came down off the ridge and stirred the dust around the shaft's mouth, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a pillow, the hum continued. It had not stopped since the recount began. All night it had gone on—doors slamming in sequence through the ruined school, a voice tallying heartbeats, patient and mechanical and getting no slower. Wren had timed it. Four seconds between counts. It would finish by dawn.

It was 11:40 now. Mara had checked his watch so many times the number had stopped meaning anything.

He looked at the ledger under his arm. The original. Wren's gift and Wren's burden, the pre-doctrine record, the book that showed the door had not been built to honor anything but to imprison it—and that the thing behind the door had been people once, the first families, the first refusal, names that had refused to be counted and been buried for it. Somewhere down there, in the dark, those names were still true. And a debt that was never acknowledged could never be forgiven, but Mara had realized something in the long terrible hours since the fire, something so simple it felt like cheating: nobody had ever read the founders' names *to* the thing. Not once in a hundred years. The town had only ever read names *to it*. Taken. Paid. Owed.

Nobody had ever said: *these were yours first. You were robbed. Here is the receipt.*

"You'll hold the line," Mara said. It wasn't a question. Denny had come back with blasting equipment and guilt in equal measure, and guilt was the more powerful explosive.

"I'll hold the line," Denny said. "Wren's got the second charge wired to the surface box. You give the signal—three tugs on the rope—we blow it whether you're clear or not." His jaw worked. "You understand that, right? Whether you're clear or not."

"I understand it. I've been grading on that principle all year."

Denny snorted despite himself. Then he stopped snorting, because the hum shifted, and for a moment it sounded almost like it was forming a word, and neither of them wanted to know which word.

Mara stepped into the harness.

* * *

The descent was worse than he had prepared for, and he had prepared for it by lying awake imagining it, so the bar had been high.

The rope hummed through the brake. The walls of the shaft were old stone below the newer excavation cut—ancient, fitted, deliberate—and someone, long ago, had carved them with marks that might have been letters and might have been tally strokes and were probably, Mara thought grimly, both. He counted them as he passed. It was a teacher's reflex. You counted things. You counted your students going out and coming back, you counted the books, you counted the minutes until recess. Counting was how you loved things: you paid attention in units.

He was thirty feet down when he understood that the shaft was counting him back.

It wasn't a sound. It was more like the rope pauses—the brake catching at intervals, four seconds apart, in time with the recount above. The dark was keeping time with him. He was being entered into something, line by line, foot by foot.

"Fine," Mara said aloud, and his voice fell down the shaft ahead of him like a stone. "Fine. But I'm writing in pen."

The dark did not laugh. But the air warmed, very slightly, and he chose—with the full knowledge that a man alone in a hole should not make such choices—to take it as amusement.

* * *

At forty feet he passed the door.

He knew it was the door before he saw it, because the cold arrived first, rolling out of a side gallery like a tide going the wrong way. Then his descending lamp caught it: the great carved slab, its bolts thrown from within, its surface covered in the same not-letters. It was bigger than the drawings Wren had made. It was bigger than it had any right to be, forty feet underground in a town that wasn't on enough maps.

And it was open a crack.

Not the way a door opens when something is coming through. The way a door opens when something inside is *listening*. A hand's width of perfect black, and against that black, faintly, the sound of pages.

Mara hung on the rope and looked at it, and did the thing he had done all year, the thing that had kept him visible and alive and himself: he documented. In his head, because his hands were full. *Door, ajar. Listening posture. Subject aware of visitor.* He thought: *if I survive this, that's a terrible sentence to write in a notebook, and I'm going to write it anyway.*

"I'm not here for you," he told the door. His voice sounded small and stubborn, which was accurate. "I'm here for what's under your rent."

The black crack did not widen. But the page-sound stopped, and in the silence that followed he heard something worse: breathing. Many breaths. Slow, and arranged, like a class waiting for the substitute to find his place in the lesson plan.

He kept descending.

* * *

The bottom of the shaft was not what he expected, which by now was its own kind of expectation.

He'd imagined a chamber, a pit, a thing. Instead the rope set him down in a corridor—a school corridor. Lockers. Linoleum, cracked and pale. Fluorescent lights that buzzed at half strength, the way they did in every school on earth at six in the morning. A bulletin board with construction-paper letters that spelled out, in a child's careful hand, WELCOME TO THE COUNT, and beneath it, someone had added in marker, *just kidding, it's attendance.*

Mara stood there for a long moment with his lamp shaking and his heart going like a fist on a locked door.

"This is a joke," he said. "You're doing a bit."

The corridor hummed, four seconds, four seconds, and did not deny it.

He walked. The ledger under his arm felt heavier with every step, the way a debt should. Doors lined the corridor—classroom doors, each with a small window, each dark. As he passed the first one, something moved behind the glass. He made himself stop. He made himself look.

A desk. An empty desk. On it, a name card in careful handwriting: *ILSA V.* And beneath the desk, on the floor, a neat pile of her belongings—hair ribbons, a library book, a lunchbox with a cartoon whale on it—arranged with terrible tenderness, the way you'd arrange the things of someone you expected back.

"Not yet," Mara whispered. "I'm coming back for all of you. That's the whole plan. It's not a good plan. But it's the one I've got."

The next door: *TOMAS A.* The next: *WREN A.*, and his chest clenched so hard he had to stand there and breathe through it, because Wren was up on the surface with her hand on the detonator, and the fact that the dark had already made her a room down here was either a threat or a prophecy or a seating chart, and he refused, absolutely refused, to find out which.

He walked faster.

* * *

The corridor opened into the old archive—the real one, the pre-doctrine one, the room the school had been built over and the town had been built over that. He recognized it from the ledgers' descriptions, and then he didn't, because the room had changed. The shelves were still there, the ledgers still ranked in their hundreds, but the far wall had become the shaft mouth again, stone and dark, and the air came out of it warm and chalky and *waiting*, and in front of it stood the missing ones.

Ilsa. Tomas. The others, the ones from autumns going back, going back—some of them grown now, impossibly, years of height added in a season of dark. They stood in a loose half-circle facing the shaft mouth, and they were tending it. That was the only word. They smoothed its edges. They listened to its hum the way you listen to a garden growing. Their eyes were open and their eyes were not there.

Mara's plan, such as it was, had a first step, and the first step was *don't run*. So he didn't run. He walked to the center of the room, unwrapped the ledger from its garbage bag with the ceremony the moment deserved and the rustle the moment did not, and he opened it on a lectern that had no business being there and was there anyway.

"Good evening," he said. His voice cracked on *evening* and he let it. "My name is Mara Zhou. I'm the substitute. I know. I know what that means. I know I was entered before I applied. We'll get to that. First, some housekeeping."

The hollow-eyed children turned, slowly, all together, like a weathervane finding the wind. Ilsa's mouth moved. No sound came out, but he read it, because he was a teacher and reading lips was half the job: *you came down.*

"Somebody had to," he said. "Attendance is a kind of prayer. I learned that here. I'm hoping to teach a better one."

He looked down at the ledger. The original. The true names. And his hands, which had been shaking since the rim of the shaft, went still—completely, unnaturally still, the way they went still when he stood in front of a class on the first day, because some part of him had finally understood that this was the first day, the real one, the only lesson that had ever mattered, and the room was full even if the room was wrong.

He found the page. The founding names. The ones who had refused to be counted, and been sealed in for it, and been fed on for a century by their own descendants rather than freed by them.

He took a breath, four seconds long, in time with the hum.

And he began to read the roll.
