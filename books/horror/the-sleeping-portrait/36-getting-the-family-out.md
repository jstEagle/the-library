## 36. Getting the Family Out

Wren had always thought she would know the end of Hollowmere by some great sound—a bell, a scream, the door below finally giving up its bolts for good. Instead it ended with the smell of diesel and Denny Okafor swearing at a fuel line in the blue hour before dawn.

"Third truck's got a leak," he announced to no one in particular, elbow-deep in the engine like a man performing surgery on a relative. "Third truck has *always* had a leak. I have personally replaced that line four times."

"Maybe it doesn't want to leave," said Petra Okafor, his niece, who was eleven and had decided sometime around midnight that gallows humor was her personality now.

"It's a truck, Petey."

"So was the school a building, and look how that turned out."

Wren almost laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked, more like a hiccup of pain than anything joyful, but Mara looked over from where he was counting students into the bed of the first truck—counting them *aloud*, names first, then a gentle tap on each shoulder—and he smiled at her like the laugh was worth more than the ledgers they'd burned.

There were nineteen students. She knew because she'd heard him count them eleven times since the school had spat them out onto the frost-stiff grass of the front lawn. He counted them the way other people checked a wound was still bleeding: not because the answer might change, but because the asking was the only thing keeping him upright.

"Zhou," Denny called. "We got room for maybe forty souls if we ride tight and nobody's precious about personal space. You planning on filling them?"

Mara didn't answer right away. He looked down the hill toward town, where the windows were still dark but not, Wren thought, dark in the ordinary way. Dark the way a held breath is dark.

"Some," he said finally. "Some are coming. Some aren't."

* * *

The defectors began arriving as the sky went the color of dishwater.

Wren stood by the second truck with a clipboard Mara had pressed into her hands—"You're an Aldery, you're organized, it's genetic"—and took attendance of a whole town. That was the joke of it, she thought. Her family had spent two hundred years keeping meticulous lists of who left Hollowmere permanently, and now here she was doing the same thing, except her column was labeled *SAVED* instead of *PAID*, and she intended to keep it that way.

The Harrow family came first, all six of them, carrying nothing but a suitcase of photographs and Grandmother Harrow, who refused the truck's help getting up into the bed and then accepted it anyway when her hip disagreed with her pride.

"I told Edmond in 1968 we should leave," she announced to the truck in general, once seated. "I said, Edmond, this town counts things it shouldn't. He said the ledgers were tradition." She sniffed. "Edmond stayed. Edmond can wave at us from the porch."

The Pell family came next, quieter, the father carrying his sleeping son wrapped in a quilt like a bundle of kindling. Then the two youngest Okafors from the far farm, who had walked three miles in the dark because Denny's trucks hadn't reached them yet. Then, to everyone's surprise, old Mrs. Calloway, who had taught piano in Hollowmere for fifty years and who climbed into the third truck—the leaky one—with the dignity of a woman boarding a ship she had personally paid for.

"I'm not fleeing," she told Wren firmly, through the window. "I'm *retiring*. There's a difference, and I'll thank you to note it in your little book."

"Noted, ma'am."

"And put down that I left voluntarily and in good spirits. If anyone asks."

"Who's going to ask, Mrs. Calloway?"

"The town," she said, and her eyes flicked downhill, and for a moment her good spirits looked like something she was holding up with both arms. "The town asks everything, eventually."

By the time the eastern sky had gone properly gray, Wren's clipboard held thirty-one names. Nineteen students. Twelve adults. Denny had done the arithmetic out loud twice—forty seats, thirty-one souls, nine empty—and both times Mara had flinched at the number nine like it had been thrown at him.

"We could wait," Wren said. "For the ones still deciding."

Mara looked at her. He had chalk dust still in the creases of his knuckles, she noticed. After everything—the archive fire, the shaft, the screaming in every voice it had ever collected—he still smelled faintly of chalk, like the school had gotten into his blood and refused to be fully evacuated either.

"Wren," he said gently, "the ones still deciding aren't deciding. They're bargaining. There's a difference, and—" a ghost of a smile— "I'll thank you to note it in your little book."

She noted it. Her hand shook while she did.

* * *

The Alderys came last.

Wren saw them coming up the hill road in the half-light—her father, her uncle Aldous, Aunt Marguerite, and behind them a straggle of cousins—and her heart did something complicated and painful, lifting and dropping at once, like a stone tossed and caught and tossed again.

Her father stopped at the tailgate of the first truck. He looked at the children packed in it, at Mara standing guard beside it with his notebook, at Wren with her clipboard, and his face did the thing Aldery faces did, which was become a closed ledger.

"You're going," he said. Not a question.

"Yes, Papa."

He nodded slowly. Then, in the tone he used for discussing fence lines: "Your grandmother rode in a truck like this when she was your age. Did you know that? 1931. They moved the whole family to Corvallis for a winter because the count that year had come up short and the elders wanted to balance it with a Voss child." He said it flatly, the way you'd recite rainfall totals. "Grandmother said it was the only year she ever slept well. She said the trucks smelled like manure and freedom."

"Papa—"

"I'm not going." He said it before she could. Of course he said it before she could; he'd had the whole walk up the hill to rehearse it. "Someone has to stay with the books, Wren. Someone has to keep the record straight so that someday, if anyone comes back, there's an honest account of what happened here. That was always the Aldery work. The true ledger." He touched his coat pocket, where Wren knew the family's remaining volume sat against his heart like a second heartbeat. "It seems fitting it ends with me."

"You could write it anywhere," Wren said, and hated how young her voice sounded. "You could write it in Corvallis. You could write it on the moon, Papa, the paper doesn't care where it is—"

"The paper cares," he said quietly. "You know it does. Everything in this valley cares about where it is. That was rather the whole problem."

Behind him, Uncle Aldous shifted his weight. Aunt Marguerite was crying without any noise at all, which was a skill Wren had never managed to learn. And Wren understood, suddenly and completely, the way you understand a thing you've been refusing to understand for years: they weren't staying out of loyalty to the dead. They were staying because leaving meant admitting that two hundred years of their lives had been a subscription to a monster, paid in children, auto-renewed, and no one wants to cancel a subscription that long. Staying was cheaper than that kind of honesty. Staying cost only everything.

"Come with us," she said anyway. Because you said it anyway. Because Tomas had run and tried to trade Wren's name to save himself, and had wept about it after, and she had forgiven him—not because he deserved it but because forgiveness was also a kind of bookkeeping, and someone had to keep the living accounts balanced. Because her father had carried her on his shoulders through every Founders' Audit until she was nine, telling her the humming under the ground was just the river. Because he was her papa, and the ledger didn't get to have him too.

Her father cupped her face in both hands. His palms smelled like ink and cold air.

"You look like my mother," he said. "She had exactly your expression whenever she was about to do something the family would regret and history would thank her for." He kissed her forehead. "Go count the living, Wren. We'll mind the rest."

Then he stepped back from the truck, and Aldous stepped back, and Marguerite stepped back, and Wren stood in the truck bed with her clipboard against her chest and watched her entire family become a line of figures on the hill, getting smaller, getting stiller, becoming the kind of entry she would spend the rest of her life trying not to make.

Tomas found her there. He didn't say anything—there wasn't anything, and they both knew the shape of the unsaid thing between them, and they were both too tired to carry it—but he climbed up and sat beside her and put his arm around her shoulders, heavy as a roof beam, and that was its own kind of entry in its own kind of book.

* * *

"Everyone accounted for?" Mara asked, climbing into the cab of the lead truck. In the gray light he looked ten years older than the man who'd arrived on a bus with no return schedule, and ten years lighter too, like grief and relief had met somewhere in the middle and agreed to share the load.

"Thirty-one plus drivers," Wren said. "Thirty-three."

"Thirty-three." He wrote it down. She watched him write it, and understood what he was doing, what he'd been doing since the night he tore a page out of a ledger mid-transaction: making the numbers mean something gentle. Every list he kept now was a small act of repair. Attendance as prayer, but the good kind, the kind where nobody gets fed to anything.

Denny fired the engines. The third truck coughed, leaked, and ran anyway, which felt thematically appropriate.

They rolled down past the outlying farms, past the Harrows' shuttered place, past the church whose bell hadn't rung in living memory, and then into Hollowmere itself, slow, because Denny refused to speed through a town he'd grown up in even if the town had recently tried to eat his equipment in a perfect circle.

And the town waved.

That was the part Wren would never be able to explain later, not to herself, not to anyone. The elders stood on their porches in the early light—Voss in her mayor's coat, the last of the founding families arranged along Main Street like a receiving line—and they raised their hands as the trucks passed. Not menacing. Not triumphant. Just *waving*, the way people wave at departing trains, the way families wave at weddings and funerals alike because the arm motion is the same and the heart makes of it what it can.

Headmistress Voss stood at the school steps with a book in her hands. As the first truck passed, she lifted it—not in farewell, Wren thought. More like a toast. And she was smiling, serene as a closed cover, and Wren found she couldn't look away until the road curved and took the school with it.

"They're not chasing us," Petra whispered from the truck bed, watching the rear window. "Why aren't they chasing us?"

"Because we're not what it wants," Wren said, before she could stop herself. Then, because Petra's face started to crumple: "Also because Mr. Zhou is terrifying when he holds a grudge, and the whole town knows it now."

That got a wet, surprised laugh out of the girl, and out of three or four others, and Wren decided to count it. On the clipboard, under SAVED, she added a new column, just for the morning: *LAUGHED ANYWAY*, and put down six names.

* * *

The ridge above Hollowmere was where Denny finally stopped, because the road down the far side was washed out and because, he said, "a man ought to get one good look back, it's traditional, and God knows this town respected tradition enough to kill for it."

They all got out. Thirty-three people stood in the frost and looked down at Hollowmere as the sun cleared the hills and lit it up gold and ordinary: the rooftops, the water tower, the school with its flag hanging slack. Smoke rose from a few chimneys. Somebody, somewhere down there, was putting a kettle on.

"Look," Mara said quietly, and pointed.

The windows were lighting up. One by one, house by house, street by street, as the sun touched them—or as something else moved through them—every window in Hollowmere filled with light, in sequence, orderly as a tally being taken. Warm yellow squares blinking on down the avenues like someone walking through town flipping switches, except no one was walking anywhere, and the order was too neat, and it ended at the school, where every window blazed at once and then, together, went dark.

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

"Well," said Grandmother Harrow at last, briskly, wrapping her shawl tighter. "I've seen worse send-offs. My sister's wedding, for one."

The laugh that went through the group was shaky and real and necessary, and Wren loved every single one of them for producing it—Mrs. Calloway snorting, Denny shaking his head, Tomas's arm tightening around her shoulders, Mara smiling his tired smile and reaching, absently, for a notebook he wasn't holding anymore, because they'd burned them all, and finding only air, and looking briefly lost, and then looking at his students instead, all nineteen, alive, and seeming to decide that was a notebook enough.

"Trucks!" Denny clapped his hands. "We've got a coast to reach and a fuel line that hates us. Everybody in. Count off if you want—I know somebody here likes counting."

"Nineteen students," Mara said immediately, pointing at each one, naming them, tapping shoulders. "All present."

"All present," Wren echoed, and marked it, and climbed in.

As the trucks pulled out, she looked back one last time. Her father was still on the ridge, a small dark figure beside the road, and he raised his hand—not waving, she realized. Holding it up. The way Aldery men held up a page to show the light through it, checking the record one final time.

She raised hers back, and kept it up until the road bent and the valley let go of them.

In her backpack, against the clipboard, against the spare pencils and the granola bars, something pressed softly against her spine with every bump in the road. A folded paper she hadn't put there. She'd felt it since the school lawn and hadn't looked, the way you don't look at a splinter, the way you don't look back—

But the trucks were safe now, and the sun was up, and her father's hand was a mile behind her, and Wren Aldery had spent her whole life being taught that records wanted to be read.

She reached into the bag.
