## 2. Inventory of a Missing Man

The estate did not so much decay as sulk. That had always been my read on Hollow Creek House, even as a boy, when Julian and I spent summers here inventing reasons to be anywhere else. The house didn't fall apart the way other old houses did — no dramatic collapses, no dramatic anything. It simply let things go. Paint loosened its grip in slow, offended patches. The wallpaper in the east hall peeled back at the corners like a lip curling. It was a house that had decided, sometime around the Nixon administration, that effort was beneath it, and had been phoning it in ever since.

I stood in the gravel drive for a long moment with my brother's house key — my house key now, I supposed, though I hadn't yet found a way to say the word *inherited* without my jaw tightening — and let the place look at me.

It looked back. It always had. Julian used to joke that the house kept a ledger. "Every window's an eye that's seen too much," he'd say, and then he'd laugh, because Julian laughed at everything, and I'd roll my eyes, because I rolled my eyes at everything, and that was the entire choreography of being twins.

I went in.

* * *

The front hall smelled the way it always smelled — dust, lemon oil somebody had stopped applying in 1998, and underneath it all a faint mineral coolness, like the air over a well. I set my bag down on the console table, next to a ceramic owl Julian had bought at a yard sale for reasons he never satisfactorily explained, and I did what I always did when the world went sideways.

I made a list.

This is the part where I should probably explain something about the Sterling twins, for the record, since the record seems to be the only thing holding this whole story together. Julian was the archivist. He catalogued everything — the estate's contents, the family photographs, the tax records going back four generations, once, as a Christmas gift to our father, who thanked him with the enthusiasm of a man being handed a phone book. Julian kept notebooks. Dozens of them. Grid paper, hard covers, dated on the spine in his small, ferocious handwriting. He believed — genuinely, cheerfully believed — that anything written down carefully enough could not be lost.

I was the one who photographed things. Documents, receipts, the casket lid of a stranger wearing my brother's face. Julian wrote things down so they couldn't be lost. I took pictures so they couldn't be denied. Between the two of us, we had assumed we were bulletproof against forgetting.

I want it noted that we were wrong, and that I hate being wrong in an empty house.

* * *

His study was on the second floor, at the back, overlooking the carriage house. I climbed the stairs slowly, cataloguing as I went — a habit I caught myself in and decided not to fight. Third stair loose. Banister dusty except for a hand-wide swath, polished, as if someone had run their hand along it recently, or as if the house had simply given up on that one spot first.

The study door was open. Julian never left it open. He had a whole theory about doors — that a closed door kept a room's thoughts in the room, and he liked his study thinking its own thoughts. I stood in the doorway a moment, absurdly, waiting for the room to adjust to me.

It didn't. Rooms don't. I know that. Most days I know that.

The desk was exactly as he would have left it, which is to say: perfect. Blotter squared. Pens in the cup, nibs down. A glass of water with a skin of dust on it, which meant he'd been here and hadn't come back to it — Julian rinsed his glass the way other men said their prayers. Beside the glass, his current notebook, spine labeled in that ferocious hand:

**HC ESTATE — CATALOGUE VOL. XI — 2024**

I sat down in his chair. It was still warm.

No. It wasn't. I know that too. It was a leather chair in a cold house in early October, and chairs do not stay warm for three weeks, and yet I sat there feeling like a man who has just missed a train by seconds — the kind of missing that is worse than missing by an hour, because an hour lets you blame the schedule and seconds only let you blame yourself.

I opened the notebook.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about Julian's notebooks: they were not diaries. There is not, in eleven volumes, a single sentence about a girlfriend, a hangover, or a movie he liked. They were inventories. He wrote the estate the way a cartographer writes a coastline — every object fixed, every room mapped, every anomaly flagged with a small red asterisk and a note in the margin. Reading them was like listening to him think, which is a sentence I would have given a great deal, three weeks ago, to never have to write in the past tense.

The early pages were comfort itself. Pure Julian.

*September 3. Attic, north gable. Forty-one hatboxes, contents: hats (31), newspapers (6), a wedding cake topper, unexplained (1). Flagged: why does this family own forty-one hatboxes and eleven hats? Query for Dad. Too late for Dad. Flagged for me, then.*

He'd crossed out "Dad" and rewritten it, and the small grief of that correction sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Our father had died in the spring. That was the whole reason Julian had come back to Hollow Creek at all — to catalogue the estate for probate, to put the Sterling possessions in order so the Sterling money could be divided, or donated, or burned, depending on what the lawyers said. Julian had volunteered instantly. I had stayed in the city and handled the flowers. That division of labor now struck me as the single most damning fact of my life: my brother went into the house, and I sent lilies.

I kept reading. The pages turned the way pages do, one after another, each one a small lit window, and I read them the way a drowning man reads a flotation manual — hungrily, gratefully, believing every word.

*September 9. Cellar. Wine rack (bottles: 14, drinkable: 2). Cistern access sealed, brick, original. Flagged: cistern has been dry since county water in '61, so why does the seal look maintained? Mortar too clean. Someone's been at it. Note: ask Ruth Ann next door, she remembers everything, including things she shouldn't.*

There it was — the first red asterisk. I photographed the page. Then the next. Then the next. My phone's shutter click was very loud in the study, and I found I liked the sound. It was a sound of procedure. Of a man doing something, anything, that could be checked later. Click, and the page existed twice. Click, and Julian's handwriting existed in two places at once, which felt, in that cold room, like a way of keeping him alive by redundancy. Backups. That's all grief is at first, I think — the frantic creation of backups.

*September 14. Carriage house exterior. Foundation sound. Upper loft: hay dust, mice (est. 12, optimistic). Lower level: the sealed door again. It is not on any floor plan. I have the 1902 blueprints, the 1937 renovation survey, and the 1968 insurance assessment. Three documents, three centuries of paper, and not one of them admits the room exists. Flagged: rooms that aren't on paper are either additions or secrets. This house doesn't add. Therefore —*

The sentence ended there, with a dash, and then the next entry picked up two days later as if nothing had happened. Julian did that. Left dashes like doors ajar. I used to tell him it was maddening. He used to tell me that a finished sentence was a closed door, and he didn't trust closed doors.

I'm going to remember that he said that. I'm going to remember it exactly.

* * *

The afternoon went the way afternoons do in old houses — all at once and then not at all. I worked through the notebook in order, because Julian would have wanted it read in order, and because I have never in my life skipped to the end of anything, a character flaw I was about to have surgically removed.

I photographed everything. The catalogue of the silver. The inventory of Father's study, down to the number of rubber bands in the desk drawer (nine, "pathetic, flag: buy rubber bands"). The page where Julian had sketched the staircase with measurements, and in the margin, in tiny letters, *third step loose — fix or document?* I looked up from the page and photographed the actual third step, and for one strange moment the notebook and the staircase were the same object to me, two copies of a thing that could not be lost, and I felt — I want to be honest in this record, honesty is the whole point of a record — I felt briefly, stupidly happy. He was everywhere in this house. Every page was his voice. I could sit in this chair for a week and just listen to him catalogue, and as long as the notebooks kept going, he wasn't gone, he was just in the back room, doing what he did best, putting the world in order.

That was the prayer. I didn't know it was a prayer. I thought it was research.

The sun moved. The light in the study went from gold to copper. Somewhere below me the house made its noises — the tick of cooling wood, a door settling on its hinges — and I catalogued those too, in the margin of my own mind, because the house was listening and I wanted it to know I was keeping records.

*September 21. Found the gap in the plans. The carriage house lower level is exactly the footprint of the missing room — the dimensions line up to the inch with the void between the 1937 survey's "storage" notation and the foundation wall. Someone built a room and then un-built it on paper. You can un-build a room on paper. I checked. It's terrifyingly easy. One line through a plan and a room stops existing for everyone who trusts paper.*

*Flagged: who trusts paper? Everyone. That's the trick. That's the whole trick.*

I sat with that one for a while. My phone was at forty percent. I plugged it into the wall behind the desk — Julian's charger, still there, still coiled the way he coiled things, in a loop neat enough to be exhibited — and I kept reading.

*September 26. Ruth Ann came by. Brought a pie I didn't ask for and didn't want and ate all of. She looked at the carriage house a long time and said, "Some rooms you leave shut, Julian, on account of what's in them isn't done." I asked what she meant. She said, "You count everything, boy. Make sure the count comes out even." Then she left. The pie was apple. Flag: apple. Also flag: what count?*

I laughed. It came out of me like a sneeze, sudden and aching, and echoed in the empty study, and I sat there afterward with my hand over my mouth like a man who'd sworn in church. Ruth Ann. I didn't know a Ruth Ann. There had been no Ruth Ann in my childhood summers, no pie, no neighbor — the neighboring property had been, as far as I knew, an overgrown field and a mailbox with a name I'd never read. But Julian knew her. Julian had known her for three weeks, at least. My brother had moved into this house and within days had been adopted by some pie-bearing oracle next door, and he had never mentioned her in any of our calls, and there had been calls, we'd talked twice a week, and now I went back through my memory of those calls like a man checking his receipts and found — nothing. Nothing about the carriage house. Nothing about a sealed room. Nothing about a count.

He'd left it all in the notebook. He'd left it all where paper could hold it.

Because paper was where he put the things that scared him. That was the system. That had always been the system. Julian told me the funny things out loud and wrote down the true ones, and I had read eleven volumes of his true ones in one afternoon and every single page was a hand reaching out of the past saying *look, look, I found it, I found the thing, look at what I found* — and the last pages were going to tell me what it was, and my hands were shaking, and I photographed them too, my own hands, for no reason, click.

* * *

*October 1. The room under the carriage house.*

The handwriting changed on this page. Not the letters — those were the same small, ferocious capitals — but the pressure. The pen had pressed harder, the way it did when he wrote in cold rooms, or angry ones. The ink was a different pen. He'd switched from his good fountain pen to the cheap ballpoint he kept in the junk drawer "for emergencies," and Julian considered running out of fountain ink an emergency, so the ballpoint itself was a message.

*It's not a room. I need to start there because I keep writing "room" and "room" is a word with furniture in it. This is a space. It is the size of a small bedroom and it is behind a brick wall and the wall was sealed from the inside. I want to be clear about what that means because I have read my own notes three times to make sure I wrote it right. The mortar is fingered smooth. From the far side. Someone stood in there, on purpose, in the dark, and smoothed the mortar over their own exit, and then the room was shut, and the plans were amended, and the count was made even.*

*1974. Ruth Ann says 1974 like a woman setting down something heavy. She won't say more. She says the county has the file and the county keeps its own count. I'm going to the archive Thursday.*

*The house knows I know. I know how that sounds. Write it down anyway. The house knows I know, and last night the third stair wasn't loose.*

I stopped. I looked up. Through the study door, down the hall, I could hear — nothing. The house making its nothing. But I got up, because Julian had gotten up, I could feel him getting up on that page, and I walked down the hall and put my weight on the third stair.

It held. Solid as bedrock. Solid as a stair that had been repaired by someone who wanted the house quiet, who wanted no creak to report on anyone's movements, in or out.

I stood on the third stair for a long time, in the copper light, in my brother's silence.

Then I went back and read the last page.

* * *

*October 3.*

*The archive is closed Thursdays. Of course it is. Everything in this town is closed, including, I suspect, certain files that are technically open. I'll go Friday. Ruth Ann says the harvest moon is in three weeks and she says it the way you'd say a deadline, and I laughed, and she didn't, and I am learning not to laugh at Ruth Ann.*

*Here is what I have, in order: a room off every plan. A wall sealed from the inside, smoothed by fingers. A year — 1974 — that makes Ruth Ann's face do something I have no word for. A stair repaired by no one. A count that must come out even. And a family — our family, Seb, our family, I keep trying to write around it and I can't — a family that has owned this ground for a hundred and forty years and never once, in all my cataloguing, in all the boxes and volumes and hatboxes and ledgers, never once owned a single thing it didn't take from someone with less paper.*

*I'm going down to the carriage house tonight with the good flashlight. If the room is sealed from the inside, then whatever's in there was put in on purpose, and if the plans were amended, then someone wanted it forgotten, and if the count must come out even —*

The sentence stopped.

Not with a dash. Julian's dashes were doors ajar, deliberate, an invitation to continue. This was different. This was a sentence interrupted — the pen dragged sideways at the end of the last word, a thin gray tail like a pulse flatlining, the ballpoint skidding off the page as the hand left it. As if he'd heard something. As if he'd stood up mid-thought, mid-word, mid-count.

The next line of the page was blank. The page after that was blank. All the remaining pages were blank, and they were the first blank pages Julian had ever produced in his life, and I turned them one at a time, and the sound they made was the sound the house was making, which is to say none at all.

I sat in my brother's chair in the copper light and held the notebook the way you hold a hand.

Then — because I am who I am, because I am the twin who photographs, because somewhere in the last hour the documentation had stopped being a method and become a devotion, a rosary, the only prayer I knew — I raised my phone and photographed the final page. The interrupted sentence. The flatlined tail of ink. Click.

And it was in the phone's screen, in the cold little gallery of my evidence, that I saw what I had missed on the paper: at the very bottom of the last entry, beneath the broken sentence, in handwriting that was Julian's and yet pressed lighter than Julian ever pressed, in pencil, where he had used only pen —

*come down and be counted*

I did not sleep that night. I sat at the desk with every notebook I could find, eleven volumes, and I read until the light came up gray over the carriage house, and I made a list, because lists are how I talk to God:

1. Find Ruth Ann.
2. Find the 1974 file.
3. Do not go down to the carriage house alone.

I looked at item three for a long time. Then I added, in the margin, in my own small ferocious hand:

*4. Julian — if you can hear this — hold on. I'm coming to count you out.*
