## 13. Probate of the Damned

The probate clerk's name was Doreen Fitch, and she had the eyes of a woman who had spent forty years watching people lie to her through paperwork.

Sebastian liked her immediately.

"Reopen a 1974 estate," she repeated, not looking up from her terminal. Her fingernails were painted a defiant coral. "Mister...?"

"Sterling."

"Mm." She typed something. Deleted it. Typed it again, slower, as though the keyboard might be persuaded to disagree. "The Sterling estate was settled in '74. Final disposition filed, judge's signature, done and dusted. You can't reopen dust, Mr. Sterling."

"I believe there are irregularities in the record."

"There are irregularities in *every* record. That's what makes them records." She finally looked up at him, and her expression did something complicated — recognition, then regret, then a kind of professional pity. "You're the twin. The one whose brother went missing."

"I'm the one asking for the file."

"And I'm the one telling you that file is sealed by court order, dated October of 1974, signed by Judge Ambrose Pell, who — fun fact — owned half the timber rights your family didn't burn down." She folded her hands on the counter. "Come back with a lawyer and a reason a judge will sign off on, and we'll talk. Come back without one, and I'll keep pretending you're here about a fishing license."

Sebastian leaned in slightly. "Doreen. May I call you Doreen?"

"No."

"Doreen, my brother may be alive behind a brick wall under my family's carriage house. The body I buried last week isn't his. And the paperwork that explains all of it is sitting in a sealed box in this building, probably two aisles over, probably next to the fishing licenses."

For one unguarded second, something moved behind Doreen Fitch's coral nails and forty years of counter service — something that looked almost like hope, or its exhausted cousin. Then she smoothed it away.

"Aisle six," she said quietly. "But you didn't hear that from me, and if anyone asks, you were never here, because I am *this* close to retirement and I am not losing my pension over the Sterlings."

She slid a visitor log across the counter and turned it face-down before he could sign it.

* * *

Aisle six smelled like pencil shavings and old rain. Sebastian found the box within twenty minutes — STERLING ESTATE / 1974 / SEALED PER ORDER OF PELL, J. — and discovered that whoever had sealed it had also, thoughtfully, wrapped the entire thing in packing tape, as if the contents might otherwise leak out and testify.

He was photographing the tape itself — documentation as prayer; he'd stopped being embarrassed about it somewhere around chapter three of his own life — when a voice behind him said:

"You'll want to get the corners. They always miss the corners."

The man was perhaps sixty-five, silver-haired, wearing a seersucker suit that had been fashionable during the Nixon administration and had simply refused to surrender. He held a coffee thermos like a sidearm.

"Warren Osgood," he said. "Attorney at law. Retired, mostly. You're the surviving twin."

"Sebastian Sterling."

"I know who you are. I know what you buried, too, which is more than most people in this town will admit to knowing." He unscrewed the thermos, poured coffee into the cap, and offered it. Sebastian declined. Warren drank it himself, unhurried, a man who had learned that patience was cheaper than urgency. "Your grandfather retained me in 1973. Estate planning. Wills, trusts, the usual architecture of not paying taxes. I was thirty-one and flattered."

"And then?"

"And then in October of '74 he asked me to draft a different kind of document, and I told him no, and he found someone who wouldn't." Warren set the cap down on top of the box, precisely centered. "I've been waiting fifty years for somebody from your family to walk into this aisle and start pulling thread. Frankly I expected the brother. Julian had been calling the archive every Tuesday for a month before he disappeared. Doreen mentioned it to me at the diner. She mentions everything to me at the diner. It's how she gossips while technically keeping her pension."

Sebastian's chest tightened. "What did Julian ask for?"

"The same thing you're asking for. The '74 file. He'd gotten as far as the petition — I have a copy of his draft, actually, he mailed it to me for review, which tells you either how thorough he was or how alone he felt." Warren's face did not change, but his voice dropped half a register. "Three weeks ago, Mr. Sterling. Three weeks ago your brother was doing exactly this. Standing exactly there. Photographing tape."

* * *

They sat in Warren's office above the hardware store, in a room lined with law books nobody consulted anymore, their spines sun-bleached into uniform surrender. Warren produced a manila folder from a filing cabinet that required him to move a decorative ceramic owl first.

"Before I give you anything," Warren said, "understand what you're asking me to do. I took an oath. I've kept it for fifty years, mostly by keeping my mouth shut, which is the easiest way to keep any oath. If I hand you this file, I'm not just breaking silence. I'm handing ammunition to a Sterling, and the last time this town gave the Sterlings ammunition, a child's whole existence got filed out of the record."

"Then don't give it to me," Sebastian said. "Tell me where to dig. Tell me who to petition. Give me the legal path."

"The legal path is closed. That's rather the point of sealing things." Warren tapped the folder. "Judge Pell sealed it. Judge Pell's nephew is now Judge Pell-the-Second, and he plays golf with the Hollis family, and the Hollis family's sawmill sits on land that was transferred out of your grandfather's estate in '74 at roughly one-tenth of its value, to buyers who were willing not to ask questions. The Corbins got the east acreage. The Pruitts got the water rights. Half the respectable fortunes in Hollow Creek are load-bearing walls built on that sealed box, and you want to come in with a crowbar and a petition form."

"Then what's the non-legal path?"

Warren looked at him for a long moment. Outside, someone in the hardware store below dropped a paint can, swore cheerfully, and life went on being ordinary, which suddenly seemed like an outrageous luxury.

"The non-legal path," Warren said, "is that I'm seventy-one years old, my wife passed in March, and I have been carrying this particular stone in my shoe since I was younger than you are now." He pushed the folder across the desk. "So. The non-legal path."

* * *

The original file was thinner than Sebastian expected. Evil usually was; it economized.

His grandfather's death certificate, dated August 1974 — two years before the man had actually died, a fact Sebastian had confirmed twice already and still made his skin prickle, like reading a letter addressed to a house you used to live in. A birth certificate for a child named Elias Boone, born to Maribel Boone, hired girl, residence: the Sterling property. And beneath it, a second document, typed on the same machine, same ribbon, same day: a birth certificate for a Sterling child. Same date. Same attending physician. Different name. Different line. As if the county registrar had run the boy through the typewriter twice and only one version had been allowed to survive.

There was a deed of transfer. There was a waiver of inquiry, signed by the sheriff. There was a check stub — $4,000, payable to cash, memo line blank — and beside it, in his grandfather's small, precise handwriting, a single word:

*Settled.*

And then, tucked at the back, a page Sebastian hadn't been warned about. A letter, unsent, unsigned, in his grandfather's hand:

*I did what the house required. I have written this sentence eleven times tonight and it is true each time and it does not help. The room asked for a count and I gave it one. God forgive me, I gave it the wrong one.*

Sebastian read it four times. On the fourth pass, his hands were steady enough to photograph it, and photographing it felt like the most obscene act of his life — reducing a confession to pixels so it couldn't be lost, so it couldn't be denied, so it couldn't be *sealed*. Documentation as prayer. He understood Julian completely now. You photographed the horror because if you looked at it directly, unmediated, it would look back, and it would recognize family.

"Your grandfather wrote that the night before the door was bricked," Warren said quietly. "Maribel Boone's boy drowned in the cistern in June. By October, the county had no record he'd ever existed, the Sterlings had a new heir on paper, and the carriage house had a fresh wall. I drafted none of it. But I knew. Knowing is its own signature, Mr. Sterling. I've been countersigning in my head for fifty years."

"Why give it to me now? Why not Julian?"

"Because Julian called me and asked questions." Warren closed the empty folder. "You walked into aisle six and started photographing packing tape. There's a difference between a man who wants to understand a thing and a man who intends to finish it." He stood, joints cracking like the floorboards of the estate itself. "Now take the file, make copies — real copies, three sets, one off-site, don't trust the cloud, clouds belong to people too — and then listen to me very carefully."

"I'm listening."

"Leave town." Warren said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Not forever. Just — leave. Take the copies, go to the state capital, find a journalist with nothing to lose, let the machinery work at a distance. Because here's what I know that you don't yet, boy: the families I named — the Hollises, the Corbins, the Pruitts — they don't all believe in the sleeping room. But they all believe in *each other*, and they believe in the wall, and when a Sterling starts pulling thread in public, they won't send lawyers. Lawyers leave paper trails. They'll do what their grandfathers did."

"Which was?"

Warren gathered his thermos, his owl, his dignity.

"They settled the count," he said. "And they were very careful about who was available to be counted."

* * *

Sebastian made the copies at the twenty-four-hour print shop on Route 9, where a teenager with lavender hair charged him for nine pages and rang up twelve without comment, having apparently absorbed the local policy regarding Sterlings and arithmetic. One set went into the glovebox of the rental car. One went into a padded envelope addressed to a journalist in Richmond whom Julian had corresponded with — Julian, always three weeks ahead, always mailing drafts to strangers because the family couldn't be trusted with the truth. The third set Sebastian carried against his own ribs, inside his coat, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat.

He drove back toward the estate as the sun went down, and halfway there he pulled over, because the light on the water tower said HOLLOW CREEK in letters that had lost their W years ago, and he needed to sit with what Warren had said.

Leave town. Take the evidence somewhere safe. Let institutions handle it.

It was good advice. It was correct advice. It was advice from a decent man who had spent fifty years learning exactly how far decency got you in Hollow Creek, which was to a nice office above a hardware store, keeping a stone in your shoe until someone else's brother went missing.

Sebastian looked at the passenger seat, where Julian's notebooks sat in their plastic sleeve. The last entry, mid-sentence. *The room under the carriage house takes what the ledger says it's owed, and the ledger says—*

And the ledger said Elias Boone. The ledger had always said Elias Boone. Every document in the file, every transfer, every waiver, every blank memo line — it all pointed down, under the carriage house, to a wall with fingerprints smoothed into the mortar from the far side.

Institutions had sealed the file. Institutions had buried the wrong body. Institutions had looked away in 1974 and were looking away now, politely, generationally, with golf games and pension plans.

Justice outside the law, then. Ruth Ann had said the room would accept restitution — a name returned to its rightful line — but only before the harvest moon. Four days now. Maybe five. He'd have to ask her again; time in Hollow Creek had begun behaving like the house, rearranging itself when he wasn't documenting it carefully enough.

His phone buzzed. An unknown number, local prefix. He answered, because at this point what was one more voice.

Static. Then, faintly, almost fondly, a sound he couldn't place until it placed itself: a child humming. A tuneless, patient little melody, the kind a boy might hum while doing chores, while carrying water, while waiting fifty years in a preserved room for someone to say his name correctly.

The call ended. No number displayed.

Sebastian put the car in gear and drove toward the estate, three copies of the truth riding in the dark around him, and the harvest moon already swelling somewhere behind the hills like a held breath.

Four days. He had a name to return, a fortune to renounce, a lawyer's warning to ignore, and a brother behind a wall who had begged him — no. Not yet. That conversation was coming. First, the records. First, the name, in ink, in public, in blood if the rite required it.

First, he was going to finish what Julian started, mid-sentence, three weeks ago.

The ledger said Elias Boone.

Sebastian intended to balance it.
