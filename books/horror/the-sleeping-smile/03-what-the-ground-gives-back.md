## 3. What the Ground Gives Back

The survey crew arrived on a Tuesday, which Elodie felt was typical of the house. It never did anything dramatic on a Sunday, when a person might have the spiritual resources to cope. No — Tuesdays. Wednesdays at the outside.

They were five of them, sent by the county to dig a drainage trench along the east lawn, where water had been pooling for years in a way that no amount of topsoil seemed to fix. Elodie met them at the gate with a thermos of coffee and what they hoped passed for a normal landowner's smile.

"East lawn's all yours," they said. "Mind the roses. They're spiteful."

The foreman — a broad, weathered woman named Petra Okafor who shook hands like she was testing your bones for hollows — squinted up at the house. "Place like this," she said, "you'd think it'd be on the heritage register."

"It's on several registers," Elodie said. "None of them flattering."

Petra laughed, and Elodie found themselves liking her immediately, in the way one likes anyone who laughs within the first minute of arriving at Winterbourne Hall. Most visitors took a full day to stop flinching at the acoustics.

Elodie watched from the library window as the little excavator chewed its way across the east lawn. There was something almost festive about it — men in hi-vis vests, a kettle on a folding table, someone's radio playing tinny pop through a work speaker. For one absurd hour, the hall felt like a place where things *happened*, rather than a place where things were endured and written down in ledgers.

The ledger sat open on the desk beside them. Entry forty-one: *Dug a new key for the cellar lock; it still turns slowly, as if reluctant.* Entry forty-two: *Found Odile's scarf behind the boiler. Moths. Obviously moths.* The explanations had been getting shorter. Elodie had noticed this the way you notice a friend replying to your letters with fewer words each time — not with alarm, exactly, but with a cold, patient arithmetic. Eventually there would be nothing left to write, and then what would they be? A person who lived inside a question mark.

At 2:17 in the afternoon — Elodie checked, because checking was the only prayer they had left — the excavator stopped.

Not broke down. Stopped. The kind of stop where the driver climbs down and stands very still with his hands on his hips, looking at the ground as though it has said something rude.

Elodie was out the side door before they'd decided to move.

"It's stone," the driver was saying when they arrived. He was crouched at the edge of the trench, which had opened into a neat rectangle of pale masonry maybe four feet down. "Six feet under the turf, no rubble, no fill. Just — stone. Like a floor. Except it's got a seam." He traced a line in the air. "Door-shaped."

"That'll be an old ice house," said the youngest crew member, whose name was Dev and who had spent the morning cheerfully narrating everything he found. "Victorian places always had ice houses."

"Ice houses aren't six feet under a lawn," Petra said. She'd knelt and was brushing soil away from the seam with her thumb, gentle as a nurse. "And ice houses don't have hinges."

She was right. As the crew cleared more of the soil, the shape resolved: a door, set flush into the stone, iron-banded, its hinges fused into the surrounding rock so completely that the metal and the stone might have been grown together, like a tree swallowing a fence post. And the door itself —

"It's bowed," Petra said quietly. "Outward. See?" She laid her palm flat against it. "Like something pushed. From underneath."

"Sealed from the inside," Elodie heard themselves say.

Everyone looked at them. Elodie realized they'd spoken aloud, and that their voice had come out flat and certain, the voice of someone reading from a list they didn't remember writing.

"Well," said Dev, with the determined brightness of a man refusing a thought, "someone sealed it from the inside, then. Probably. Historically. People did that. With ice houses."

"They really didn't," Petra said.

They worked until five, clearing the soil, photographing everything, arguing pleasantly about whether to call the county archaeologist or just crack the thing open with a crowbar. Petra voted archaeologist. Dev voted crowbar. The vote went to the archaeologist, who could not come until Thursday.

That night the wind came up off the hills and moved through the hall the way it always did, room by room, like a landlord inspecting the property. Elodie lay in bed with the ledger on their chest and listened to the footsteps overhead — the east wing, the empty rooms, the ones with the furniture under sheets. *Settling timbers,* entry nineteen had said. *Thermal expansion,* entry twenty-three. Tonight they didn't reach for the pen. Tonight the footsteps sounded less like walking and more like pacing. Like waiting.

Around midnight, Elodie thought they heard laughter from below the floor.

It sounded like Odile.

They wrote nothing down. That was the worst part. Not even denial has the energy forever.

* * *

The knocking started at half past three in the morning.

Elodie came down the stairs with every light in the hall blazing behind them, because if you must answer a door at 3:30 a.m. in a haunted house, you do it lit like a stage. Through the frosted glass: a shape. Small, hunched, dripping.

Petra Okafor stood on the step soaked to the skin, though it hadn't rained since Tuesday morning. Her hi-vis vest was plastered to her, mud to the knees, and her eyes had the fixed, polite blankness of someone who has walked a long way without deciding to.

"Ms. Okafor?"

She didn't answer. She was shivering, but mechanically, like a machine remembering the motion.

"Come in. God — come in, you're freezing."

Elodie got her onto the hall bench, wrapped her in the good blanket, put tea in her hands. Petra held the cup and stared at the fireplace and did not drink. Her lips were blue. Her boots left puddles shaped like small countries on the flagstones.

"The others," Elodie tried. "Dev, and the lads — are they at the site? Do you want me to drive you back to the village?"

Nothing. Petra's mouth moved once, shaping some syllable that never arrived, and closed again.

It was while kneeling to tug one of the wet boots loose that Elodie saw it: mud on Petra's hands, yes, everyone had mud — but also a fine grey powder worked deep into the creases of her knuckles and under her nails. Stone dust. The same pale dust Elodie had watched puff out of the trench that afternoon.

Except the trench was a mile from here, and Petra had gone home to the village at five o'clock. Elodie had waved to her from the window. She'd honked the van horn twice, cheerful, going away.

So how had she gotten wet? How had she gotten *here*?

"Petra," Elodie said carefully, "where did you walk from?"

Petra turned her head. Slowly, smoothly, like a door on well-oiled hinges. She looked at Elodie, and her face did something terrible: it smiled. Warmly. Gratefully. A smile of perfect, serene contentment.

And it was wrong. Not wrong the way a stranger's smile is wrong. Wrong the way—

Wrong the way the smile in the casket had been wrong. The smile Odile never wore. Elodie had spent five years telling themselves they'd imagined that too, that grief distorts, that dead faces relax into shapes the living never make. But here it was again, on a living face, in Elodie's own hallway: that same unhurried, satisfied curve, as if the mouth belonged to something that had finally been given permission to use it.

The grandfather clock struck four. On the fourth stroke, Petra blinked, and the smile drained out of her face like water out of a cracked pot, and what rushed in behind it was horror — hers, real, human, immediate. She looked down at the tea. At the blanket. At Elodie.

"Where—" Her voice came out rusted. She swallowed. Tried again. "I was in the trench. We were all in the— I turned around and the door was *open*, it was open downward, stairs, and the lights—" Her breath hitched. "I climbed. I don't remember climbing. I remember grass. Then I was walking and I couldn't— my name wouldn't—"

She stopped. Looked at her own muddy hands as if counting them.

"There were five of us," she whispered. "There were five of us, and I counted coming up, and I counted *four*."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the empty east wing, footsteps crossed the ceiling — unhurried, deliberate, pausing directly over the hall bench as if listening.

Petra looked up. So did Elodie.

"Ms. Winterbourne," Petra said, and her voice had gone very small and very steady, the way voices go when a person stops being able to afford panic, "what is under your lawn?"

Elodie opened their mouth to say *nothing*, to say *an old ice house*, to say any of the forty-two explanations waiting patiently in the ledger upstairs.

What came out instead was the truth, plain and cold as well-water:

"I don't know. But I think it knows we've found it."

Because that was the thing Elodie couldn't say aloud, the thing sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone: the hinges hadn't been fused shut all these centuries by time or rust or accident. Sealed doors are made by people who want things kept *out*. But that door bowed outward. That door had been braced, fused, grown over — from beneath.

Whatever was down there hadn't been locked in.

It had locked itself in, and waited, patient as masonry, for someone to dig.

And now the archaeologist was coming Thursday, and four men were unaccounted for, and the woman on the bench had come home wearing a smile that belonged to the house.

Elodie stood, went to the window, and pulled back the curtain.

Across the dark lawn, the drainage trench glimmered faintly — and from somewhere far below the east lawn, faint as a memory, unmistakable as a heartbeat, came the sound of Odile laughing.
