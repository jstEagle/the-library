## 5. The Borrowed Coat

Kestrel woke on the floor of his own apartment with his boots still on, and the boots were wrong.

That was the first thing. Not the floor — he'd slept worse places than hardwood, and lately the floor had become a kind of compromise between his body and his bed, which he no longer trusted. Not the light, gray and early, or the taste of iron at the back of his throat like he'd been breathing through a penny. It was the boots. His boots were size eleven, bought three years ago from a surplus store in a town whose name he'd documented in a notebook that was now buried somewhere under a landslide of other notebooks, and these boots fit like they'd been poured around someone else's feet and then handed to him as an apology.

He sat up. The room tilted in a way rooms shouldn't tilt, a slow hydraulic lean, as if the whole apartment were settling into water.

His clothes were damp. Not wet — *damp*, the specific humidity of things that have been underground for a long time and have only recently come up for air. He looked down at himself: canvas jacket, flannel shirt, cargo pants, all of them his, all of them hanging off his frame by half an inch in the shoulders and pinching at the waist, as though he had shrunk overnight, or as though the clothes remembered being worn by a slightly different man and were grieving the change.

There was mud on the cuffs. He crouched over it the way he crouched over everything — notebook out, pen uncapped, the old reflex of devotion — and the mud was wrong too. It was fine-grained, almost blue-gray, shot through with threads of something organic that might have been rotted root or might have been hair. He knew it. He knew it the way you know a face in a photograph you weren't supposed to have. That was tunnel silt from below the third gallery, from the flooded section past the collapse, the stretch of passage that had been underwater since 1974, since the reservoir's final raise drowned the lower town and everything beneath it.

He had not been there. No one had been there. The water in that section stood forty feet deep against a plug of collapsed rock, and his own survey — his beautiful, obsessive, laser-true survey — confirmed it.

*Then where did I go last night,* he wrote, and then, because the ritual demanded honesty even when honesty was unbearable: *or where did whatever wore me go?*

The recorder was still running on the nightstand. It had been running every night for six weeks now, ever since the second voice first surfaced in the recordings like a fish rising through black water. He pressed playback and listened to eight hours of his own sleeping breath, the small sounds of a body turning, and then — at 3:11 a.m., the timestamp he had come to think of as an appointment — the voice.

It didn't speak so much as settle into the room, dry and patient, the way dust settles. And this time it wasn't answering questions he hadn't asked. This time it was telling him about his childhood.

*"You used to line your shoes up by the door before bed,"* it said, in a voice like paper being unfolded very slowly. *"Left to right, toes out. Your mother thought it was charming. She called it your little soldier habit."*

Kestrel stopped the recording. Sat with his thumb on the button until the plastic creaked.

His mother had said that. *Little soldier.* He had never told anyone that. It wasn't in any notebook; he'd checked, twice, hands shaking, because if it was in a notebook then at least there was a chain of custody, a provenance, some thread connecting the phrase to his life instead of to the thing below Vesper Hollow that had apparently reached up through forty feet of drowned rock and helped itself to the inside of his head.

But it wasn't in any notebook. Which meant either the voice had taken it directly from him — from the unrecorded, unwritten, genuinely private archive of his memory — or it had given it *to* him, planted it, and now he could not be sure the memory was his at all. Maybe his mother had never called him little soldier. Maybe there had been no shoes lined up by any door. Maybe he was a man assembled last Tuesday out of borrowed details, and everything he believed about himself was just the most recent coat something else had tried on.

He played more of the tape because he couldn't not. That was the corruption in him already, he understood that now — the same hunger that had made him crawl through flooded culverts with a laser level strapped to his chest, the need to *know*, to have the record, to hold the proof. Even now, with the proof turned inside out and hunting him, he needed to hear the rest.

At 4:02 a.m., the voice laughed.

It was Danny's laugh.

His brother had been dead for nineteen years — a bad road, a wet night, a guardrail that did what guardrails do — and Kestrel had not heard that laugh since the funeral, except once, years later, in a dream so vivid he'd woken up crying and written it down and then burned the page because keeping it felt like grave robbery. But here it was on a cheap digital recorder at four in the morning, coming out of the mouth of something that lived under a drowned town: the short, startled, double-beat laugh, the one that always came right before Danny said something cruel-funny, the laugh that meant *I love you and I'm about to make fun of you.*

*"Careful,"* the voice said afterward, gentle, in a register that was trying very hard to be Danny and almost succeeding. *"You'll wear a groove in the floor, pacing like that. Mom used to say you'd dig yourself to China one day. Look at you. You dug straight down instead."*

Kestrel turned off the recorder. He went to the sink and ran the tap and put his face in the cold water and stayed there until his lungs complained, and when he came up gasping, his reflection in the dark window behind the faucet took half a second longer than he did to settle.

* * *

He spent the day doing what he always did, because ritual is what holds a person together when the person is coming apart: he documented. He photographed the mud sample against a scale card. He logged the boot discrepancy. He transcribed the 3:11 a.m. passages in his neatest hand, and the act of writing them steadied him the way it always did — ink as anchor, paper as ground — until he noticed, near the bottom of the fourth page, that he had written a question in a handwriting that was not quite his own. Slightly narrower. Slightly older. A hand that leaned left instead of right.

*What do you want returned?*

He stared at it for a long time. He did not remember writing it. He did not remember asking it. But the page was warm where the words were, faintly, like skin after a slap.

The phone rang at dusk.

He almost didn't answer. Unknown number, area code two counties over, and he was in no state to be a person on the telephone. But the explorer's superstition in him — the belief that everything that arrives uninvited is data — picked up.

"Mr. Sandoval." A woman's voice, old and dry and absolutely steady, the voice of someone who has spent decades refusing to be hurried. "Alma Reyes. You've been reading my paperwork."

"I—" He sat down without deciding to. "Yes. Ma'am. The '74 water authority filings. I'm sorry if—"

"Don't apologize. Nobody's read them in fifty years. That's rather the point of them." A pause, and he heard her breathing, slow and deliberate, like someone conserving strength for a long walk. "I dreamed your face last night."

The cold went through him all the way down to the borrowed boots.

"It was a young man's face," she said, "but it wasn't a young man looking out of it, if you understand me. There was something behind the eyes taking notes. And it was wearing my mother's way of tilting its head when it listened. My mother has been dead sixty-one years, Mr. Sandoval. Nothing living should know how she tilted her head."

"Ma'am—"

"You've been in my tunnels."

"They're not—" He stopped. Swallowed. "How did you get this number?"

"The tunnels gave it to me," Alma Reyes said, without a flicker of irony, and he realized she meant it exactly the way he would have meant it: the way you mean things when you've learned that the world keeps records in places other than county offices. "They give me things sometimes. Little pieces. They've been giving me pieces of you for weeks — your name mostly, over and over, like they're practicing it. Like they're learning to say it properly before they introduce you to somebody."

He looked down at the transcription on his desk. At the narrow, left-leaning handwriting. At the question he had asked without asking.

"What are they?" he said. "What's down there?"

A long silence. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, wearier, the dignity still intact but the fear showing through it like bone through torn cloth.

"In 1974, four men came to my house with a document," she said. "They told me it was a formality. They told me everyone in Vesper Hollow was signing. And I signed it, Mr. Sandoval, because I was twenty-four years old and they were men with briefcases and the reservoir was already filling and what does a girl from a drowning town know about what a deed can hold?" Her breath caught, released. "It wasn't land they took. Land they could have condemned and paid for, land they could have stolen fair and square like governments do. What they wrote into that paper was my *name*. They bound it into the deed of the thing that sleeps below, and they made me the lock on the door, and they told me the arrangement would end when the paperwork ended. Well." A sound that was almost a laugh, dry as the voice in his recordings. "The county is finishing the paperwork next week. Final dissolution. My grandson is bringing the papers for me to sign, and after that there will be nothing left of Vesper Hollow on any record anywhere, and the lock comes off, and the thing below will belong to whoever's name is nearest the door."

"Which will be mine," Kestrel heard himself say. "My notebooks. My survey. My recordings. I'm the only one who's documented it in fifty years."

"Yes," Alma said softly. "You've been knocking for years, Mr. Sandoval. Did you think nobody heard?"

He wanted to hang up. He wanted to drive east until the reservoir was a rumor and burn every notebook in a rest-stop trash barrel. Instead he said, "Come here. Please. Before your grandson's papers — I need to show you something in the tunnels, and I think you're the only person alive who'll recognize it."

Another silence, longer. Then, quietly, in a voice that had nothing left to protect:

"I dreamed your face, young man. And in the dream, you were standing in my mother's kitchen, wearing my father's coat, and you asked me for my name back." A pause. "I've been waiting fifty years for someone to ask me that. I'll come tomorrow. Bring a wheelchair ramp, and don't let my grandson drive — he thinks this is about money, and men who think it's about money sign too fast."

She hung up.

Kestrel sat in the dark of his apartment, in clothes that fit him wrong, in boots caked with mud from water no living person had touched, and across the room the recorder clicked on by itself — 3:11 p.m., twelve hours early, the appointment moved — and the voice began, gently, patiently, in his mother's cadence, in Danny's warmth, in a tenderness aimed at him like a weapon:

*"You don't need her, sweetheart. You've got me. You've always had me. Now come downstairs and let's talk about the deed."*

Kestrel picked up his pen. And in the narrow, left-leaning hand that was becoming more comfortable to him than his own, he began to write.
