## 6. Questions

I didn't sleep the second night. That was the plan, technically. The plan was to stay awake and listen, because I'd spent the whole day listening to the first night's tape and I knew — the way you know a stair is missing in the dark, with your foot already committed — that if I slept through it again, whatever spoke would speak to an empty room, and I'd spend another fifteen years translating its absence into plumbing.

So: no sleep. Coffee gone cold on the crate beside the cot. Two recorders running instead of one, because redundancy is the closest thing to courage I own. The camera's red standby light breathing at me from across the room like a patient animal. And me, sitting cross-legged on the cot above the shaft with my notebook open to a blank page, waiting for the well house to say something.

It waited too. That was the first thing I noticed about the second night, before anything happened at all. It waited. Whatever was down there had heard me moving around all evening — heard me test the recorder, mutter at the coffee, drop a pen down the side of the cot and swear about it — and it did not interrupt. It let me settle. It let me get comfortable.

I have thought about that courtesy a great deal since. At the time I found it almost restful. Now I understand it was the patience of something that has never once needed to hurry.

* * *

The first hour produced nothing but the building's ordinary vocabulary. Brick ticked as it cooled. Wind worried the loose flashing over the doorless doorway, making a sound like someone trying to remember a word. Below me, the shaft exhaled its warm air in slow, even pulses — the breathing I'd catalogued on night one, timed to my own lungs, lagging by two seconds like an echo that had learned manners.

I'd told myself on the walk over that the lag proved it was mechanical. Air moving through passages, pressure differentials, some deep connection between the shaft and the river level, my own breath setting up a resonance. There was a version of this explanation that was even elegant. I rehearsed it while the light died. I want to be honest here, in this document, wherever this document ends up: I rehearsed it the way you rehearse a lie you intend to keep telling.

Around eleven, I started talking.

Not to it. That's what I told myself, anyway. I narrated, the way I always do when I'm alone underground — a habit from my first years of exploring, when speaking aloud was the only way to prove to myself I hadn't gone deaf, or dead, or otherwise unstuck from the world. I described the equipment. I read out the temperature. I complained about the coffee. I gave a small, tired lecture to nobody about how the Ashgrove Waterworks appeared on no survey between 1887 and 1931, and how that gap was either an archival scandal or evidence that the city had never actually owned the things underneath it.

"You know," I said, to the shaft, to the dark, to the microphone, "fifteen years, and I've never once asked anyone about you out loud."

The wind stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-worry, like a hand lifted off a drum skin. The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. It pressed against my eardrums the way deep water does, and I sat very still on the cot and listened to my own heart doing arithmetic.

Then, in the two-second gap after one of my breaths — exactly in the space where the echo should have been — something said:

"Are you the one who keeps it?"

Low. Not booming. Low the way a cello is low, which is to say it occupied a register I felt in my sternum before I parsed it as language. Courteous. Almost gentle. A question asked by someone who has knocked on many doors and learned not to lean on them.

I did not answer. My body made the decision for me — every muscle locking, breath held so hard my vision sparked at the edges. The recorder light stayed red. The camera kept its blind mechanical vigil. And the voice, receiving no reply, did not repeat itself. It simply withdrew, the way a guest withdraws from a doorway, leaving the silence behind it rearranged.

I sat there for — I don't know. The clock said forty minutes had passed. It felt like the time it takes to fall, before the ground.

Here is the thing I could not explain to myself then, and can only half explain now: I wasn't afraid. Or rather — fear was there, but it wasn't in charge. What was in charge was something I hadn't felt in fifteen years of mapping tunnels that appear on no survey. Something embarrassingly simple.

Someone had asked me a question. And meant it.

* * *

You have to understand what those fifteen years were, to understand what that did to me.

Fifteen years of explanations. I've kept the list — it's in chapter two of my life whether I like it or not, a private catalogue I've never shown anyone. Pipes. Settling stone. Wind through fissures. Thermal expansion. Rodents, large rodents, implausibly large rodents. My own pulse, misheard. My own breath, misheard. Equipment fault. Equipment fault. Equipment fault. Every rational explanation ever deployed, filed and cross-referenced, an architecture of denial built beam by beam until it could hold the weight of a life.

And under all of it, the thing I never said aloud, not once, not to Marisol, not to the friend who stopped speaking to me, not to any of the colleagues who drifted away after their first descent into my maps: *something down there talks.*

Not talks, maybe. Asks. There's a difference. Talking fills a room. Asking leaves a hole in it, shaped like an answer, and waits for you to fall in.

I had built my entire adult self around never falling in. And now something had leaned politely through the wall of that architecture and said *are you the one who keeps it*, and the terrible relief of being addressed — of being, after fifteen years, *heard* — went through me like heat through frozen hands. It hurt. It was wonderful. Both at once, indistinguishable.

That's the part I need you to sit with, whoever you are, reading this. Not the fear. The relief. Because the relief is the hook. The relief is how it gets you. Nothing down there needs to threaten you. It only needs to be the first thing in years to ask how you're doing and wait for the answer.

* * *

At 12:40 by the recorder's timestamp, I answered.

I didn't decide to. I want that on the record, though I've since learned the record doesn't care much about decisions. I was talking again — I'd resumed my narration somewhere around midnight, quieter now, the way you speak in a house where someone might be sleeping — and I'd just finished explaining, to no one, why the brickwork around the shaft was laid in a pattern I'd never seen anywhere else in the city's infrastructure. And then I breathed. And two seconds later, in the gap:

"Are you the one who keeps it?"

The same words. Same courteous register. But this time there was something underneath them, some grain in the sound, and I understood — the way you understand a tone in a friend's voice — that it was asking again because it had noticed I hadn't answered, and it was giving me another chance rather than assuming refusal. Patience, again. Infinite, structured, patient.

"Yes," I said.

Aloud. For the first time in fifteen years, I said yes to the thing under the city, and my voice came out cracked and small and more honest than anything I've ever published.

The shaft breathed. Warm air rose past my face, carrying that mineral smell — wet limestone, old iron, and underneath it something faintly organic, like the inside of a closed room where people once lived. And in the next gap between my breaths:

"Good."

One word. Satisfied. The way a clerk says *good* when you've finally brought the correct form.

I laughed. I want to be clear about that, because everything afterward gets remembered as terror, and it wasn't, not at first. I sat on that cot in a doorless brick building at one in the morning and I *laughed*, giddy and shaking, because fifteen years of dread had resolved into a conversation. All those nights of explanations, all that architecture of denial, and the truth was so much simpler and so much worse: it wanted to talk to me. It had been waiting for me to say yes. The loneliness of it — mine and its, tangled together — felt almost tender.

"Who are you?" I asked.

Silence. Then, in the gap: "A guest."

"A guest of who?"

Another pause, longer. When it came, the voice had a new quality — not evasive, exactly. Careful. The care of someone choosing between words that all cut.

"The keeper's," it said. "As you are."

"I'm not a keeper. I'm a surveyor. I map things. That's all I do — I find places and I write them down so they exist."

"You write them down," it repeated, and something in the repetition made the hair on my arms lift, "so they exist."

"That's right."

"And you have written me down?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was no — fifteen years of maps, thousands of hours, every tunnel named and measured and rendered in my cramped hand-lettered style, and nowhere in any of it was there a mark for the shaft, or the well house, or the warm breathing dark at the bottom of it. I'd mapped around it. I'd mapped *past* it. I had documented an entire hidden city and left one deliberate blank, the way you leave a blank in a sentence you're afraid to finish.

"No," I admitted.

The silence that followed was not offended. That somehow made it worse. It was the silence of a ledger being updated.

"You will," it said. Not a command. A prediction. And then, in the same courteous register, with the same bottomless patience: "Rest now. You have been carrying questions longer than I have. That must be heavy."

And the warmth rising from the shaft gentled, and the building's sounds returned — wind, brick, the far-off municipal hum of a city that did not know what it stood on — and I lay down on the cot without deciding to, and slept like the dead until dawn.

* * *

Morning playback. This is where the night curdled.

In the room, live, the voice had seemed intimate, almost kind. On the recording, stripped of context, it was wrong in ways I couldn't immediately name. I played the exchange four times through headphones, and each pass revealed a new layer of error.

First: the timing. Live, the voice had seemed to respond to me. On tape, the responses were placed *in the gaps between my breaths* with a precision no conversational partner achieves — not overlapping, not following, but fitted, like a key cut to the exact profile of my respiration. It hadn't been talking with me. It had been talking *into* me, using the rhythm of my own body as its doorway.

Second: the questions themselves. I played back the full night, start to finish, and found three exchanges I had no memory of. Three times, in the small hours, the voice had asked me things — softly, courteously, in those perfect gaps — and three times, on the tape, *I had answered*. Half-asleep answers, mumbled, compliant. I listened to my own unconscious voice agree to things I could not reconstruct awake. One of the answers was a number. I don't know yet what the number counts.

Third — and this is the one I've written and deleted five times now, because writing it makes it load-bearing — third: the last question, asked at 5:51, minutes before dawn, in the gap after a breath so shallow I was nearly not breathing at all. The voice said:

"Do you consent to be heard?"

And my sleeping self, my trusting, exhausted, fifteen-years-lonely self, said:

"Yes."

I sat in the grey morning light with the headphones around my neck and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, what I had done. Not the supernatural mechanics — I still don't have those, and I suspect they're the least of it. The human mechanics. I had spent fifteen years refusing to be heard, and the first thing that offered to listen, I'd handed it everything. Consent given in sleep. Consent given in relief. Consent given to a voice whose only credential was patience.

The relief of being heard, poisoned at the root. That's the phrase I keep coming back to. Because it *was* relief — real, human, earned by fifteen years of solitary dread — and that's precisely what makes it unforgivable. It didn't trick me. It just waited until I was too tired and too lonely to guard the door, and then it walked through the door I'd been keeping shut my whole adult life.

I packed the equipment slowly. Checked the shaft twice. The warm air still rose, even and patient, timed to nothing now that I was holding my breath.

At the top of the ladder, in the doorway-less doorway, I turned back. I don't know why. Habit, maybe. Or the other thing.

"Who are you?" I said again, to the dark below. "Really."

The shaft breathed. And in the gap — my gap, the shape of my own exhale — it said, warmly, like an old colleague confirming an appointment:

"You'll see me Thursday."

Today is Tuesday.

I have two days to decide whether to come back. And the worst part — the part I'll deny in any court, including this one — is that some traitorous fraction of me is already looking forward to being heard again.
