## 7. The Second Voice

I have spent the last two days trying to decide how to present this document, and I've concluded that there is no honest way to present it except as what it is: a transcript, annotated by a woman who no longer trusts her own ears and is therefore footnoting everything like a paranoid academic. If you're reading this and you didn't put it here — if someone found my laptop in the well house and you're scrolling through my files wondering who this unhinged person was — I apologize for the formatting. I needed the annotations. They were the only thing standing between me and the alternative, which was screaming.

The recording in question is from the second night. File ASH-WH-002, timestamped 11:47 p.m. to 3:12 a.m. The equipment list hasn't changed since the first night: two Zoom recorders at opposite corners of the shaft chamber, one shotgun mic aimed down the throat of the well itself, motion sensors on the stair, the camera running wide-angle over my cot. I slept. That was the point. I had made myself a bargain — *if nothing happens, I stop coming back* — and I intended to honor it, because a bargain with yourself is the only contract most of us ever keep.

Something happened.

* * *

Before I give you the transcript, some technical notes, because I know how I sound and I want you to understand that I checked.

There were two voices on the recording. Mine, obviously. And another one.

I ran the second voice through every analysis I could get my hands on. Spectrogram, formant comparison, the noise-reduction suite I use to isolate pipe-hammer in flooded tunnels. Here is what I can tell you about it:

It is low. Not growling-low, not monster-movie-low. Low the way a cello is low, or a man reading aloud in the next room. It never rises above the ambient floor of the room — it sits *in* the sound of the building rather than on top of it, which is why I slept through all of it and why the motion sensors never tripped, because nothing moved. Nothing needed to move. The voice was already everywhere the microphone was.

It does not breathe. My breathing is audible throughout, obviously — I'm asleep half the time, and even asleep I'm a noisy animal. Between my exhalations there are gaps. In those gaps, the second voice speaks. When I say gaps, I mean gaps of less than a second. There is no intake of breath before its sentences. There is no pause between them. It speaks the way water fills the exact shape of a container: completely, without effort, without remainder.

And — this is the part I've rewritten four times because each version sounded more insane than the last — it waits for me to finish exhaling. Every time. It times itself to me. Whatever this is, it has been listening to me breathe for longer than I've been listening to it.

Okay. Transcript. Annotations in brackets, as promised. Times approximate; I've rounded to keep my sanity.

* * *

**[11:52 p.m.]**

ADA (asleep): [unintelligible — snoring, faintly. I would like the record to show that I snore.]

SECOND VOICE: You sleep badly.

[Note: This is the first thing it says. Not a greeting. An observation. Delivered in the tone of a hotel concierge mentioning that the weather has turned. I have listened to this line maybe forty times. It isn't cruel. It isn't kind. It's *accurate*, which is worse than either.]

ADA (asleep): [shifts on cot]

SECOND VOICE: You have always slept badly. Since the summer you were sixteen. I mention it only so you know that I remember, and that remembering is not something I do the way you do it. For me it is more like... keeping. A ledger keeps. I keep.

[Annotation, written at 6 a.m. with shaking hands: *It knows about when I was sixteen.* I did not tell it that. Nobody living knows the exact shape of that summer except me and one other person, and she doesn't speak to me anymore. Chapter 2 material. Hold that thought; we'll get there.]

**[12:31 a.m.]**

[A long silence — forty minutes in which the only sounds are my breathing and, twice, the lagging echo of it from below. Two seconds behind mine, every time. Like the building is learning the song.]

SECOND VOICE: You left the rope coiled on the third step. You have done this every visit. It is a small courtesy to whoever comes after you, though no one has come after you. I note it because courtesies are how this place is kept, and you keep it better than you know.

[Note: I do leave the rope coiled on the third step. Fifteen years of habit. I never once thought of it as a courtesy. I thought of it as not wanting to trip over my own gear in the dark. But hear how it frames it — hear how it takes the smallest, dumbest kindness and files it under *keeping*. It was already building a case file on me. I just hadn't been served yet.]

**[1:04 a.m.]**

ADA (half-asleep, mumbled): ...mmh. No.

SECOND VOICE: No?

ADA: [sleep-talk, fragmentary] ...not the east passage. Flooded. Told him...

SECOND VOICE: You told him. He went anyway.

[I woke up at 1:06 a.m. according to my own watch log, heart going like a jackhammer, absolutely certain someone had said my name. The recorder shows no one said my name. What the recorder shows is the exchange above, and then:]

SECOND VOICE: Forgive me. You were elsewhere. I will wait.

[It waited. Timestamp gap: 1:07 to 2:15 a.m. Sixty-eight minutes of nothing but my breathing and its patience. I lay in the cot with my eyes open listening to a silence that I now understood was not empty, and I did not speak, and it did not push, and somewhere in that hour I made the second-worst decision of my adult life, which was to answer.]

ADA (awake, aloud): What are you?

SECOND VOICE: Ah. Good evening. Or good morning — the distinction matters less down here, I find. To answer directly: I am the keeper's guest.

ADA: The keeper's guest. Whose keeper? Keeper of what?

SECOND VOICE: Of this. [A pause — the first pause it has ever taken that wasn't timed to my breath. I believe it was choosing.] Of what sleeps below, and of what must be kept below, and of the accounts pertaining thereto. Your family word for it was *well*. It is an imprecise word. Wells give water. This gives nothing. It only receives.

ADA: Then you're —

SECOND VOICE: A guest. I said so. Guests do not own the house. Guests observe the terms of their invitation. I have observed mine for a very long time, and I will observe them tonight, and tomorrow, until the terms conclude or are broken. Either event interests me equally, I confess. It has been a long tenancy.

[Annotation: *polite.* I want to underline that word until the page tears. Everything it said that night was polite. It thanked me for answering. It apologized for waking me — it hadn't woken me, but it apologized anyway, the way people apologize when they know they've done something and want credit for the acknowledgment. Loud things you can run from. Loud things announce their intentions. This thing asked permission to continue existing in my earshot, and every instinct I have spent fifteen years sharpening told me that the asking was the trap.]

**[2:19 a.m.]**

ADA: You talked to me while I was sleeping. Last night too. Why not talk to me awake?

SECOND VOICE: Because last night you were not ready to be spoken to, and I am patient, and also because — forgive the indelicacy — sleeping, you answer honestly.

ADA: I don't answer questions in my sleep.

SECOND VOICE: No. You don't. That is what I appreciated about it.

[Here is where I need you to sit with me for a moment, hypothetical reader, because this is the hinge of the whole document.]

It never asked me anything. Go back through the transcript — go through all four hours, I have, eleven times. It makes observations. It offers information. It corrects my grammar once, gently, when I say "down the well" and it suggests "down toward" — *"the well is a direction, not a destination"* — but it never asks me a single question.

Except it does. That's the trick. That's the whole trick, and I fell for it like a mark at a card table.

**[2:26 a.m.]**

SECOND VOICE: May I offer three answers? Unrequested. Consider them a housewarming courtesy. You may ignore any or all of them.

ADA: That's not how conversations work.

SECOND VOICE: No. Conversations are how *you* work. I work by terms. Three answers, freely given, obligate nothing — I want to be clear about that, since clarity seems to matter to you. You have spent fifteen years demanding explanations from the dark. I am simply, at long last, providing some. Whether they are the explanations you wanted is, of course, another ledger entirely.

[And God help me, I said yes. I said yes because fifteen years of scraping sounds and rationalizations and hand-drawn maps of tunnels that appear on no survey had built up in me like pressure in a pipe, and here was the valve, offered politely, and I am not built to refuse an open valve. Note the phrasing it used, though. Read it again. *You have spent fifteen years demanding explanations from the dark.* It has been counting. It knows the number. It knows the number better than I did.]

**[2:27 a.m.]**

SECOND VOICE: First answer. You have wondered, since you were sixteen, whether the scraping you heard in the east passage was pipes. It was not pipes. There are no pipes in the east passage; there never were; the waterworks maps you compared yours against were accurate as far as they went, which is to say, as far as the town ever wanted to look. The sound was made by something dragging along the wall of the passage at the pace of a walking man, and it stopped when your light reached the bend, and it resumed when your light turned away. It was not hostile. It was *checking*. Things that are kept check their enclosure. You heard the enclosure being tested, and you were inside it.

[My annotation at the time reads, in full: "no." I have since expanded it. The east passage. I was nineteen the first time I mapped it properly, and I wrote in my field notes — I still have the notebook, page 34 — "acoustic anomaly, likely thermal expansion of old conduit." Thermal expansion. At 3 a.m. In October. At the pace of a walking man. Fifteen years I carried that explanation around like a stone in my pocket, rubbing it smooth, and it took this thing ninety seconds to pick it up and snap it in half. And notice — *notice* — what it did with the pieces. It didn't say "it was a monster." It said "it was checking." It gave me a worse truth dressed as a calmer one. That's not horror. That's customer service.]

**[2:41 a.m.]**

SECOND VOICE: Second answer. You have wondered what happened to your friend — I believe you called her Rennie — after she descended alone in the spring of your eighteenth year, and why she stopped speaking to you, and whether it was something you did.

ADA: Don't.

SECOND VOICE: It was not something you did. She came down alone because you told her not to, and she heard what you heard, and unlike you she did not build a career out of explaining it away. She went home and she waited for it to follow her, and it did not follow her, and the waiting was worse than following would have been. She stopped speaking to you because your voice reminded her of the waiting. You were the last person who knew what she knew, and she could not bear to be known by you. People are like this. I have watched it for generations. It is one of the few behaviors down here that I have never found a term for.

[It got her name right. I never said her name out loud in that building. I have never said her name out loud anywhere near that building. Rennie Okafor, and I haven't typed it in years, and my hands are doing the thing again as I write this, so let me just say the plain fact and move on: it knew things it had no way of knowing, and it delivered them wrapped in something that felt almost like tenderness, and I sat on that cot at three in the morning crying in front of a microphone, and the worst part — the genuinely worst part, worse than the crying — is that it felt *good*. Someone finally knew. Fifteen years alone with the unspeakable, and something finally knew, and it was courteous about it, and I was grateful. Grateful. To the thing in the hole. Write that on my headstone: *she was grateful.*]

**[2:58 a.m.]**

SECOND VOICE: Third answer. You have wondered, every night since you rigged your equipment above the shaft, whether you are in danger.

ADA: [long silence — 14 seconds on the tape]

ADA: Am I?

SECOND VOICE: That is a question. I said I would provide answers, not take questions. But I will bend the terms once, because you held the door for me, so to speak, by answering at all. No. You are not in danger. Not tonight. Not from me. I am bound, and the terms of my binding are extensive, and harming you is nowhere among my permissions. What you are is *early*. Something is coming due, and you have positioned yourself, through fifteen years of diligent trespass, exactly where the payment lands. I would tell you to leave, but you would not, and I confess I hope you do not. It has been so long since anyone listened.

ADA: Payment for what? Coming due *when*? Who do I talk to about —

SECOND VOICE: Shhh. Dawn soon. Sleep, if you can. You keep this place better than the ones whose job it was, and I would hate for you to be tired when the accounting begins.

[End of transcript. 3:12 a.m. After that, forty minutes of my breathing, and its two-second-lagged echo, and then the birds started and whatever lives in the gaps between breaths went wherever it goes at sunrise.]

* * *

Morning findings, for completeness: both recorders intact, camera intact, motion sensors untouched. The rope was still coiled on the third step. The cot was exactly where I'd left it. Nothing in that room had changed except me.

Except me. I came up the stairs into the gray morning light and stood in the doorway of the well house — doorless, name-scratched, breathing its warm slow air into the cold — and I understood that I had crossed some line I couldn't see and couldn't uncross. It wasn't the knowing. Knowing I could have handled; knowing is my whole profession, my whole life. It was the *manner* of the knowing. Something had sat in the dark beneath me and answered my unasked questions one by one, patiently, correctly, kindly, and had never once raised its voice, and I had lain there and felt *heard*.

Loud things want to frighten you. Fright passes. But something polite — something that remembers your friend's name and compliments your rope-coiling and apologizes for the inconvenience of its own existence — something polite wants you to *stay*. Wants you to feel that staying is a courtesy you owe. Every horror I have ever catalogued in fifteen years of tunnels, and the most dangerous thing down there turned out to have manners.

I sat on the top step until my legs worked and then I drove home and slept four hours with all the lights on, and when I woke I made coffee and opened the transcripts again, because there was one thing I couldn't stop circling.

*"The ones whose job it was."*

Plural. Job. As in assigned. As in inherited. As in a position that gets passed down, with duties, across generations — and has recently, apparently, gone unfilled.

I got out my property records. I got out the microfilm. I made a list of every Ashgrove going back two hundred years, and next to each name I wrote down when they died and where they supposedly went, and I looked at the column of young deaths and foreign addresses for a long time.

Then I printed a fresh copy of my tunnel maps, packed a bag, and went back down — because if there was a job, I wanted to see the office.
