## 10. Marisol

There are three kinds of phone numbers in my life. The ones I call without thinking — the county records office, the diner that still delivers to the waterworks gate if you tip the driver enough to feel complicit. The ones I never call, which is most of them. And then there's Marisol Okonkwo's number, which I have carried through four phones and eleven years like a splinter I refused to let a doctor remove.

I dialed it on a Tuesday, at an hour I told myself was professional — mid-morning, businesslike, the kind of hour when you can pretend you're calling about archives and not about the fact that something under the city has started asking me questions in my own breathing gaps.

She answered on the second ring.

"County Historical Society, this is Marisol."

"It's Ada."

A pause long enough that I could hear her office around her: the tick of a space heater, someone's cart squeaking down a distant corridor, the particular silence of a person deciding how much of themselves to spend on this call.

"Ada Villalobos," she said finally. "Fifteen years, and you lead with 'it's Ada.'"

"I didn't want to make it weird."

"You made it weird by saying that." But there was something underneath the dryness — not warmth exactly, more like a door left unlocked. "Is this about the Ashgrove papers? Because if you're calling to tell me you've found another tunnel that doesn't exist, I'm going to need you to lead with that, actually. I've been having dreams."

My stomach did something complicated. "What kind of dreams?"

"The kind where I'm standing in front of a brick building with no door," she said, "and my grandmother is telling me to walk away, and I keep explaining to her that there's no door to walk away from, and she keeps saying *that's the point.*" A breath. "So. Is it a brick building with no door?"

I sat down on the edge of my cot, which was still set up in my apartment because I hadn't slept in my own bed since the second recording. The sheets on the cot smelled like the well house now — cold stone and warm air, a combination that shouldn't be possible and yet was the single most frightening thing I'd cataloged in fifteen years.

"Yes," I said. "It's a brick building with no door."

* * *

We met at a diner off Route 9, neutral territory, halfway between her archive and my obsession. She arrived carrying a canvas tote bag full of folders, which was so perfectly Marisol that I had to look out the window for a second to compose myself.

She looked good. That's not relevant to the horror story, but it's true, and I promised myself this account would be honest even when honesty was embarrassing. Her hair was shorter. She wore reading glasses on a chain now, which meant time had passed, which meant we had both become people who wear glasses on chains, and some traitorous part of me wanted to spend the whole lunch asking about that instead of what I actually needed to ask.

"You look terrible," she said, sitting down. "In a documented way. Like a primary source that's been handled too much."

"Fifteen years of fieldwork will do that."

"No." She ordered coffee without looking at the menu. "Fieldwork doesn't do that. Fieldwork makes people tan and smug. You look like someone who hasn't slept in their own bed. Am I right?"

I was quiet long enough that the waitress came and went.

"Ada."

"There's a building," I said. "On no map. Brick, doorless, built into the hillside above the old waterworks. Inside there's a shaft that breathes. Warm air, up from below, against the cold. It's rhythmic."

"Rhythmic how?"

"Like breathing."

Marisol took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose, and for a moment she looked less like an archivist and more like a woman hearing a family story she'd been dodging since childhood. "And you've been sleeping next to it."

"I rigged recorders. Motion sensors. A camera."

"And?" She looked up. "Ada. And?"

I turned my laptop around and slid it across the table with the transcript open. I watched her read. I know how she reads — fast first pass, then slow, then back to the beginning with her finger under the lines, the way she handles anything she doesn't trust. By the third pass her finger had stopped moving.

"*Are you the one who keeps it,*" she read aloud, very quietly. "That's the question it asked?"

"That's the first one. There are others. It answers questions I haven't asked. It calls itself 'the keeper's guest.' It's polite, Mari. That's the worst part. It says *thank you* when I describe things to it. It said my mapping was—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me it complimented you."

"It said I was thorough."

Marisol closed the laptop gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone has died. Then she opened her tote bag and pulled out a folder, and her hands were steady in the way I recognized as effortful — the steadiness of someone who has decided to be calm and is paying for it hourly.

"My grandmother grew up in the Hollow," she said. "The neighborhood they razed in '61 for the reservoir expansion. You know it."

"Everyone knows it got moved for the reservoir."

"Everyone knows what the plaque says." She laid a photograph on the table between us, yellowed, soft-cornered. A row of houses. In the background, on the hillside, a small brick structure. "This is from 1948. My grandmother kept it her whole life. When I started at the Society, she gave me a box of things and made me promise not to go looking into certain subjects until she was dead. She died in 2019. I waited two years out of respect, and then I got busy, and then I forgot, and then—" she tapped the laptop "—then you called."

"What did she say about it? About the building?"

"She called it the Well House, but she never once called it a well." Marisol's voice had gone flat, careful, the tone of a woman quoting scripture she didn't believe but couldn't afford to misquote. "She said the Ashgroves kept it. She said every family in the Hollow knew two rules: you don't take work from the Ashgroves that involves the hill, and you don't answer anything that talks to you from underground. She said her own mother had heard it once, during the famine year, calling up through the ground in a voice like your favorite person's. And her mother put a chair over the cellar door and sat on it all night and never told anyone what it said, except once, at the very end, she told my grandmother: *it wasn't threatening. That's what I couldn't forgive. It was reasonable.*"

The diner noise seemed to recede. I became aware of my own heartbeat, and then — and I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left — I became aware that I was counting the interval between its beats, the way I'd learned to count the lag between my breathing and the shaft's.

"It answers questions you haven't asked," I said. "That's what it does. That's what it's been doing to me."

"Then stop answering."

"I already did. That's the problem. The night I finally talked back was the best night of my life, Mari. Fifteen years of noises down there, fifteen years of explanations, and the first time something spoke to me directly, I felt *heard*. Do you understand what that does to a person? To a person who has spent their whole adult life being told the tunnels aren't real?" I heard my own voice climbing and forced it down. "It was kind to me. And I know — I *know* — that kindness from something like that is a hook. But knowing doesn't unhook you."

Marisol was looking at me with an expression I remembered from the year we were together — the expression she wore when a document contradicted everything the finding aid claimed. Not surprise. Recalibration.

"You loved it," she said. "The attention."

"I loved being believed."

"And now it believes in you very, very much." She gathered the photograph back up, squared its edges against the table. "Okay. Here's what I can do. The Society has the Ashgrove charitable trust filings going back to 1902. We have the microfilm. We have the Hollow relocation records, including the compensation ledgers nobody's cross-referenced because they're boring and handwritten and in three different hands. If your thing downstairs is connected to payments — and everything your transcript says screams *payments* — then the paper trail exists. Towns like this don't make deals like that without paperwork. They always think the paperwork makes it legal."

"You'll help me dig?"

"I'll help you dig." She put her glasses back on, and behind them her eyes were wet and furious, mostly furious. "But I'm telling you the terms now, so you can't retroactively claim you didn't hear them. I will not go underground. Not down a ladder, not down stairs, not into any structure whose floor is lower than the surrounding grade. My grandmother sat on a cellar door for one night and it rearranged her whole life. I have inherited enough from that family. I'm not inheriting that."

"Understood."

"And Ada — one more term." She reached across the table and took my hand, and her grip was warm and dry and real, and I realized with a lurch that no one had touched me in months except to shake it. "You sleep somewhere else tonight. Somewhere with a foundation and neighbors and at least two exits. And you call me after. Not text. Call. If you're going to be the protagonist of whatever this is, you don't get to do it alone anymore. That was the old arrangement. The old arrangement is how you ended up holding a rope you didn't know you were holding."

I stared at her. "I haven't told you about the rope."

Her face went carefully blank. "What rope?"

* * *

I told her about the rope on the walk to her car — about the coil of new hemp I'd found at the shaft's lip on my fourth visit, about how I'd assumed another explorer left it, about how it was frayed at the end in a way that suggested use, and about how I had, without deciding to, taken to coiling it neatly each time I left, the way you'd maintain shared equipment.

Marisol listened with her arms crossed, shivering slightly in the October air though it wasn't cold.

"You maintained it," she said.

"I maintained it."

"That's acceptance, Ada. In any legal framework ever devised, that's acceptance. You took custody of an instrument of descent and you cared for it." She unlocked her car but didn't get in. She stood facing me across the roof of it, and the afternoon light caught the lenses of her glasses and hid her eyes. "My grandmother had a phrase for people like us. People who touch things. She said the town was full of people who thought looking was free. *Nothing you attend to is free,* she used to say. *Attention is a signature.*"

"Then I signed fifteen years ago."

"Then we find out what you signed." She opened the door, then paused. "Come by the Society tomorrow at nine. Bring your maps — all of them, every survey, every hand-drawn passage. If we overlay them against the relocation ledgers, against the trust filings, against the property transfers..." She trailed off, and I saw the archivist fully surface, the hunger that had made me love her once, aimed now at the thing that had been eating my life. "If there's a pattern, we'll find it. Paper remembers what people are paid to forget."

She drove off. I stood in the parking lot for a while, holding my laptop against my chest like a shield, aware of two things simultaneously.

The first was that for the first time in fifteen years, I was not alone with this. Someone else had seen the transcript. Someone else had said *we*. The relief was so physical it was almost vertigo — I actually leaned against a stranger's bumper and breathed until my eyes stopped stinging.

The second thing was quieter, and I've tried to leave it out of this account twice, and both times it insisted on staying, so here it is: as Marisol's taillights disappeared toward town, my phone buzzed in my pocket. One notification. A voice memo app I don't remember opening, showing a recording I don't remember making, timestamped forty minutes ago — the exact duration of our lunch.

I played it. It was the ambient sound of the diner: dishes, chatter, the hiss of the espresso machine. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a low voice speaking in the pauses between sounds, patient and courteous, and it was saying:

*"How lovely. She remembers the rules. Ask her what her great-uncle was paid."*

I deleted the memo. Then I stood in the parking lot and made myself say it aloud, to the empty air, because I have learned that pretending not to hear is a form of answering too:

"I'm not asking her that."

The air said nothing back.

But tomorrow at nine, I would be walking into her archive carrying fifteen years of maps, and I would be carrying that sentence too, whether I wanted to or not. Some questions, I understood then, aren't asked by you. They're installed in you, like a rope you find yourself maintaining.

I got in my car and drove toward town, toward the light, toward the neighbor's foundation I'd promised to sleep inside, and I did not let myself wonder — not once, not the whole way — what a famine-year payment might have looked like, or whose name might be written in a ledger I hadn't opened yet, in handwriting I might recognize.
