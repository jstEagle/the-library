## 11. What Was Invited In

I want to say I went down on the third night because of courage. That would be a lie with good posture. I went down because the voice had said *thank you* when I said goodnight, and no one had thanked me for anything in eleven years, and I am apparently that cheap.

Let me back up.

The second recording — the one where it asked if I was the one who keeps it — I played maybe forty times. Not for evidence. Evidence was settled. I'd stopped thinking in terms of evidence somewhere around the moment I heard my own breathing answered, two seconds late, by something patient underneath me. I played it because of how it asked. There was no menace in it. That's what nobody tells you about the things under old towns: they don't growl. They *inquire*. The voice was low and unhurried and it pronounced every syllable like a man reading terms aloud to a signatory who ought to pay attention, and when I'd answered — "No. I don't know what I keep" — there had been a pause, and then, gently, almost kindly:

*Then we should discuss what you hold.*

Discuss. Like a bank appointment. Like a mediation session. I lay awake that whole second day composing rebuttals to a voice that hadn't spoken since, and around four in the afternoon I caught myself rehearsing an opening statement out loud in my kitchen, and that was when I understood the trap wasn't in the well house. The trap was that I wanted the meeting.

So: third night. I brought the rope I'd been using for fifteen years, the good static line, the harness I trust more than most people. I brought two recorders running, a backup on my chest rig, fresh batteries, and a thermos of coffee I would not drink because my hands were already steady in the wrong way, the way they get before a first descent into an unmapped section, and I've learned not to argue with that steadiness. It isn't calm. It's the body agreeing to something the mind hasn't signed yet.

The shaft breathed warm air up at me, as always. Fifteen years of explanations, and the honest one is the simplest: the hole exhaled, and the exhale was warm, and warmth underground in October means one thing only — that somewhere below, something is alive, or was, or remembers being.

I clipped in. I said, out loud, because it seemed rude not to: "I'm coming down. Don't startle me."

And from below, faint as a voice through a wall, it said: *I never have.*

* * *

The descent was longer than the depth. That's the only way I can describe it. My rangefinder put the bottom at thirty-one meters, which is nothing, which is a water tower, which is a Tuesday, but the rope paid out and paid out and the walls changed character twice — brick, then cut stone, then something older and smoother that my light didn't so much illuminate as apologize to — and by the time my boots found floor, my watch insisted nine minutes had passed and my body insisted on a number it refused to share.

The chamber at the bottom was round. Not carved-round. Worn-round, the way a stair is worn, the way a threshold is worn, the way a pew is worn after three hundred years of the same families sitting in it. The floor sloped gently toward the center, toward a low ring of stone I at first took for a wellhead, an inverted echo of the shaft above, except there was no water in it and no darkness either. It was simply a boundary. I knew that the way you know a doorway is a doorway even when the door is open air.

I stood at the edge of my light and did what I have always done: I narrated. Fifteen years of solo exploration teaches you to talk to yourself, to timestamp, to describe. "Chamber approximately eight meters across. Floor dressed stone, heavily worn. Central feature: circular depression, no water, no debris. Air temperature—" I stopped, because the air temperature was comfortable. Not cave-comfortable. Room-comfortable. Someone's-parlor-comfortable.

"You can hear me," I said.

*I hear everything you say down here,* the voice said, and now it came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, the way sound behaves in a room built for listening rather than speaking. *You have been saying things down here for fifteen years. You named forty-one passages. You apologized to a dead bird in 2014. You told your father you loved him to the empty tunnel off Delve Street because you could not say it upstairs.*

My throat closed. Because all of it was true, and all of it was private, and none of it had ever felt private down there — I had just chosen not to know that I was being overheard. That's the architecture of denial for you. It has load-bearing walls, and I had been leaning on them for half my life.

"I'm not the keeper," I said. "I need you to understand that. I'm a surveyor. I map things."

*You mapped,* it agreed. And there was something in the way it said it — not correction, not threat. Something almost like... appetite held politely in check. Or grief. At this distance I still can't tell those apart. *Come. Sit. If we are to discuss what you hold, you should not do it standing like a debtor at a door.*

"There's nowhere to sit."

*There is always somewhere to sit,* it said, and my light found, along the curved wall behind me, a bench of stone that had absolutely not been there when I'd swept the chamber with my beam ninety seconds earlier. Or it had been, and I had filed it under *rock formation*, the way I'd filed fifteen years of sounds under pipes and settling and wind. I sat down. My legs made the decision; I was just attached to them.

* * *

"Tell me what you are," I said. "Not what you're called. What you are."

A long silence. Long enough that I checked my recorder was running. Then:

*That question has been asked of me four times in three hundred years. Twice by keepers who wished to mock. Once by a child who fell in. Once by you.* A pause. *The child got the true answer. The keepers got the useful one.*

"Give me the child's answer."

Another pause, longer. When it spoke again, the voice had changed register — lower, wearier, the voice of someone who has explained themselves too many times to audiences who wanted a different explanation.

*Before your town had its name, there was a camp on this ground, and in the camp there was a sickness, and the sickness killed faster than the ground could be dug. Do you know what it is to dig graves faster than people die? No. Of course not. Your century solved that problem by forgetting people faster.* Something moved in the dark beyond my light — not approached, just *turned over*, the way a sleeper turns over. *The living could not bury the dead fast enough, and the dead were not resting, and the sickness walked. So a delegation came down — here, to this chamber, which was already old when your language was young — and they made a request.*

"To take the sickness away."

*To take the dead away. The sickness followed its dead, mostly. We are not so different from wolves in that respect.* The voice did something I had not heard it do before. It paused with difficulty, the way a person pauses when the next sentence costs them. *They could not pay. They had nothing. A starving camp has nothing. So they offered what desperate people always offer, believing it costs nothing: later. Later, they said. Remembrance. Restitution. Forever. And forever, to starving people, is a word like a horizon. It looks close. It cannot be reached.*

"And you took the deal."

*I took the dead.* Flat. Immediate. *Forty-one bodies in one night, and the walking sickness with them, and by morning the camp slept — really slept, for the first time in a season. And I waited to be remembered.* The turn-over again in the dark, slower. *Do you know what remembrance is, to something like me? It is not sentiment. It is structure. It is the shape of the agreement being renewed daily in the minds of the debtors. A contract of that kind is not paper. It is attention. Every year they were meant to come down, speak the names, acknowledge the payment, renew the terms. For one generation, they came. Their children came sometimes. Their children's children sent a man with a shovel and a bag of lime once a decade, and called it maintenance.*

"The Ashgroves."

*The Ashgroves learned early that attention could be delegated. That is the whole history of your town in one sentence: someone discovered the bill could be forwarded.* Now the voice cooled, and I heard — clearly, unmistakably — the thing beneath the courtesy. Not anger. Arithmetic. *They began paying in other coin. Money, buried where I could not spend it. Reputation, spent where I could not collect it. Occasionally—*

It stopped.

"Occasionally," I said. "People."

*Occasionally the terms required flesh,* it said, with the terrible mildness of a clause being read aloud. *I did not write the terms. I have never broken the terms. Read that sentence again in your notes, surveyor, and mark it: in three hundred years, I have taken nothing that was not delivered to me, promised to me, or defaulted to me. Whatever else you decide I am, decide that first. I am owed. I have always simply been owed.*

And here is the part I have turned over so many times it's gone smooth as river stone: I believed him. Sitting on a bench that hadn't existed, in the dark, three hundred years of somebody's arithmetic hanging in the air, I believed him — and worse, I *sympathized*. Because I know what it is to be owed something no one will acknowledge. Fifteen years of maps no archive would take. Fifteen years of saying *this exists* to people who smiled and changed the subject. There is a particular madness in holding a true account that everyone else has agreed to treat as fiction, and I recognized the texture of his voice the way you recognize a family resemblance.

He was a creditor. That was all. The oldest kind — the kind whose claim is real and whose remedy is patience, because patience is the only weapon that never rusts. Everything monstrous about him was downstream of that. The breathing in my recordings, the questions in my sleep-gaps, the bench appearing when needed: none of it was hunger. It was *collections*.

And I hated myself a little for the sympathy, because I could feel it doing what sympathy does — softening the judgment, greasing the slope. He noticed. Of course he noticed. He'd had three centuries of practice noticing what debtors feel.

*You understand,* he said, and there was something dangerously gentle in it. *Most of them never get this far. The keepers come down angry, or drunk, or both, and they read their lists and throw their lime and leave. You are the first in a hundred years to ask what I am instead of what I want.*

"What do you want?"

*What any creditor wants. Payment. Or renegotiation. Or—* and here the pause stretched so long I stood up without deciding to, *—a new signatory. The old line thins, surveyor. Edmund is the last who answers to the name, and he has been skimming the account for fifty years, and a contract starved of honest parties does not die. It goes looking.*

"Looking for what?"

*For whoever holds the rope,* he said, and I heard my own rope creak against the stone above us, though nothing had touched it. *You have held it for fifteen years. You have touched every wall and named every passage. Under the old terms, that constitutes—*

"No." I was on my feet, backing toward the wall, hand finding the rope. "No. I never agreed to anything. I didn't know the terms existed."

*Neither did the camp,* he said, almost tenderly. *They agreed anyway. That is how it has always worked. Consent arrives retroactively, or not at all.* A breath — his, or mine, or the shaft's, I genuinely could no longer tell. *But I am not unreasonable. I have never broken terms, and I do not spring them. Go home, Ada Villalobos. Sleep in your cot above the door. Think about what you hold. The account is old; it can wait a week. It has waited three hundred years.*

I climbed. Hand over hand, faster than protocol, my light shaking shadows across walls that seemed to lean in slightly as I passed, and just before the night air closed over my head, his voice rose up the shaft after me, quiet as a favor between neighbors:

*One more thing. Ask your archives who owned the quarry on Delve Street in 1847. Ask them why the price was so low. Ask who was paid away that year, and whether anyone ever said his name again.* A pause, and then, with unmistakable satisfaction, the satisfaction of a ledger balancing: *You are very good at finding what records omit, Ada Villalobos. It is why I chose you.*

I came up into the cold like a diver surfacing, and lay on the bricks gasping, and the stars overhead were indifferent and beautiful and completely uninvolved, and I remember thinking, with the last coherent thought of that night:

He said *chose*. Not *noticed*. Not *found*.

*Chose.*

And I lay there under the ordinary sky, warm air rising past me out of the open door below, and understood that I had gone down looking for a monster and met a bookkeeper instead — and that the bookkeeper thought I was his newest asset, and that the worst part, the part I'll admit to no recorder, is that some small, shameful part of me had liked being *seen*.

Sunrise was four hours off. I didn't sleep. I started a list titled *Questions for Marisol Okonkwo*, and the first line wrote itself before I could stop it:

*Who was paid away in 1847?*
