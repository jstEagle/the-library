## 12. The Last Heir

It took me eleven days to find Edmund Ashgrove, which I consider a personal failure given that he was in the phone book.

Not his own phone book — nobody has those anymore — but the modern equivalent: a care facility's website with a tasteful photo of a garden courtyard and a staff directory that included an activities coordinator who answered my email within an hour because I'd claimed to be researching "pioneer families of the county" for a historical society newsletter. Which was even true, in the way that all my best lies are true. I *was* researching pioneer families. I just hadn't mentioned that one of them appeared to be running a subscription service for something under their property, and that the current account holder was ninety-one years old and possibly the only person alive who could tell me what I'd signed up for.

The facility was called Rosewood Commons, which is the kind of name that exists specifically to make you forget you're looking at a building where people go to be managed until they stop. It sat on a hill east of town, all beige siding and wheelchair ramps, with a view of the reservoir that the brochure called "serene" and I called "the water table." Inside it smelled of coffee and industrial lavender, and a nurse with kind eyes and a clipboard asked me if I was family.

"No," I said. "I'm the new tenant."

She didn't understand that, obviously. I let her think I was a journalist, which she understood perfectly and disliked mildly, and she led me down a corridor lined with framed photos of residents at various stages of decline, all of them smiling like the photos were being taken hostage.

Edmund Ashgrove had the corner room. Of course he did.

* * *

He was sitting by the window in a wingback chair that someone had clearly carried up from a better house, doing a crossword in pen. He didn't look up when I knocked on the open door. He said, "You're late," in a voice like gravel poured slowly, and kept writing.

"I'm sorry?"

"The well house." Now he looked up, and I got my first full look at him, and I want to describe it accurately because accuracy turned out to matter later, in ways I couldn't have predicted. He was small. That was the shock of it. Ninety-one had compressed him into something birdlike, sharp-boned, with hands like knotted roots and eyes that were pale blue and absolutely awake. Everyone expects monsters to be large. Nobody warns you that some of them fit easily into a wingback chair. "I felt you take the key off the hook in March. I've been waiting for you to work up the nerve since April. You're late."

My mouth did something dry. "There's no key. The door doesn't have a lock."

"There's always a key." He went back to his crossword. "Seven letters, 'one who inherits what cannot be refused.' Starts with H."

"Heir," I said, before I could stop myself.

"Mm. And there we are." He set down the pen with the deliberation of a man laying down a card he'd been holding for several rounds. "Sit down, Ms. Villalobos. Or do you prefer the name you use on your maps? L.A.? Very anonymous. Very careful. You've been careful for fifteen years, and look how much good it's done you."

The chair across from him was a plastic visitor's chair, and sitting in it felt like sitting across from a doctor who'd already read your chart. "How do you know my name?"

"You wrote it in the well house." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, worse than anything I'd heard on tape, because it was *pleased*. "Third visit. You stood at the shaft and said your own name out loud — 'Ada Villalobos, urban explorer' — like you were introducing yourself at a party. It heard you. So did I, eventually, when it told me. We keep each other informed, the old place and I. Long-distance plan. Very reasonable rates."

I had prepared questions. I had a list, actually, written out in my notebook with the obsessive numbering I bring to everything: property records, the disappearances, the phrase *whoever holds the rope*, the voice, the questions, whether he had ever been down, whether his family had ever — but sitting in front of him, every question collapsed into one, and it came out of me before I could dress it up:

"What is it?"

His eyebrows rose, genuinely delighted, like I'd asked him about a grandchild. "What is *what*? Be specific. It hates vagueness. That's not a joke, incidentally. Vagueness in the terms is how this whole mess got so expensive."

"The thing under the well house. The voice. The—" I gestured, helplessly, at everything. "The breathing."

"Ah." He folded his root-hands in his lap. "You know, in eighty years of dealing with it, no one in my family ever asked me that question directly. My father forbade it. His father forbade it. There was a whole catechism of things you didn't ask, and 'what is it' headed the list, right above 'what happens if we stop,' which was second, and 'can we give it back,' which was third and considered very rude." He leaned forward. "So. What do you think it is?"

"I think it's owed something," I said. "And I think your family has been paying it out of other people's pockets for two hundred years."

For a moment — just a moment — the delight flickered, and underneath it I saw something older and colder, the actual animal wearing the patriarch costume. Then the delight came back, warmer than before, and I understood that my answer had pleased him more than any question could have. Confirmation. A new keeper showing aptitude.

"Close enough," he said. "You'll want details, though. You're a mapper. Details are your religion." He settled back into the wingback. "Ask me things. Properly. One at a time. I'm ninety-one; I intend to enjoy this."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened to me in that room, because honesty is the whole point of everything I've done since, and it starts here.

I hated him. From the first minute, I hated him — the crossword in pen, the corner room, the way the nurses touched his shoulder gently when they passed, the way the world had arranged itself around his comfort for nine decades while the thing under his family's floor collected its interest from people who never knew they'd opened an account. And underneath the hatred, worse, was recognition. Because when he said *you've been careful for fifteen years and look how much good it's done you*, he wasn't mocking me. He was describing himself at thirty. I could see it. The same architecture of denial, load-bearing walls and all. He'd built his version so well it had become a house he owned outright, with a view of the reservoir, and mine was still under construction in a rented body, and the difference between us was not character. It was time and inheritance. That was the horrifying part. Not that he was unlike me. That he was exactly like me, finished.

"Your family," I said. "Every generation. Someone goes abroad, someone dies young. I found the pattern in the papers."

"The abroad ones don't go abroad." He said it the way you'd correct a child on a date. "That was the polite fiction. They go *down*. Voluntarily, usually, by the end. The young deaths are the ones who fought it. My uncle Thomas fought it. 1931. He was twenty-six and he locked himself in the pantry of the big house and swore he'd starve before he'd serve, and three days later they found the pantry empty and the well house door standing open, and Thomas was never seen again, and my grandfather paid the quarterly early that year, out of pocket, no skimming, because Thomas had made it angry." He said it all evenly, a man reciting a weather report. "Fighting it costs more than feeding it. That's lesson one. There are forty lessons. I have them all memorized. Would you like them?"

"Not yet."

"'Not yet,'" he repeated, savoring it. "Oh, you're going to be fun. My children weren't fun. My children were cowards. Gerald took the money to Vancouver and sends a card at Christmas with a religious sentiment in it, as if distance counts as repentance. Carol wouldn't come to her own mother's funeral in case the ground here got ideas about her. I raised two people whose entire inheritance strategy was hoping I'd die before I could explain anything." He shook his head with genuine grievance. "Do you know what happens to a debt when the named heirs refuse it?"

"It defaults," I said quietly.

"It *defaults*," he agreed, with relish. "To whoever holds the rope. And you, Ms. Villalobos, have been holding the rope for fifteen years. Touching every wall. Naming every passage. Saying your name out loud in the dark like a fool at a party." He spread his small hands. "You thought you were exploring. It thought you were applying."

* * *

Here is what I should have done: stood up, walked out, driven home, burned my maps, moved to a city with real basements and no founding families.

Here is what I did instead: I opened my notebook, and I asked him about 1979.

Because I'm a mapper, and details are my religion, and because some part of me had already understood — sitting in that plastic chair, hating him, recognizing him — that the only way out of a debt is through the ledger. You can't fight a contract you haven't read. And Edmund Ashgrove, I realized with a lurch that felt almost like vertigo, wanted to talk. Had been waiting, delightedly, for someone to talk to. Eighty years of catechism and quarterly payments and children who fled, and finally, finally, a stranger walks in holding the rope and asking questions. He wasn't confessing. Confession implies guilt. But he was *briefing* me, the way you brief a successor, and the pleasure he took in it was bottomless.

"Nineteen seventy-nine," he said, when I asked. "Yes. That was a good year. Efficient." He examined his hands. "We'd had a bad decade. Inflation, the mill closing half-shifts, and the thing below gets restless when the town's fortunes dip — sympathy, I suppose, or appetite, they look alike from above. The quarterly payments were straining us. My father had died owing a full quarter plus penalties, and I was thirty-eight and drowning. And then there was this man. A surveyor. Hired by the county to do a water table assessment, of all things, poking around the old works with instruments." His mouth thinned. "Instruments. Near my well house. Asking my tenants questions about sounds."

"Your tenants."

"I owned the block. Slumlord, if you like, but a responsible one — I kept the boilers working. This man, Reyes — no, Delgado, Ramón Delgado — he lived over the laundromat. Decent man. Curious man. Curiosity is the disease, Ms. Villalobos, and I recognized the symptoms, because I'd caught it myself at his age, and look at me now, doing crosswords in a lovely chair." He sighed. "So I introduced him to the well house. Told him it was a local curiosity. Let him lower the rope himself. He was so pleased to be trusted."

The room was very quiet. Down the hall, someone's television murmured about the weather.

"You killed him," I said.

"I *paid* him," Edmund snapped, and for the first time his voice cracked with something real — indignation, the indignation of a bookkeeper accused of poetry. "I paid him into the account. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire moral structure of my family's stewardship, and if you can't hear it then you're not ready for the rest. I didn't touch him. I didn't harm him. I opened a door he was already scratching at, and the creditor collected what curiosity costs, and the town slept soundly, and the water table stayed where it belonged, and Ramón Delgado's widow received a settlement from a utility company for a tragic accident involving a confined space, which was even technically accurate." He straightened in his chair, composed again. "No guilt. None. I've checked, over the years, the way you check a tooth. Nothing there. What I feel is *bookkeeping anxiety*, and it's considerable, because Delgado's payment bought the town twenty good years and I skimmed the last four quarters of it to cover the mill debts, and unbalanced accounts accrue, Ms. Villalobos. They accrue and they metastasize and they end up attaching themselves to whoever holds the rope. Which is why you're here instead of Gerald."

I wrote *Delgado, Ramón, 1979* in my notebook, and my hand was steady, and I noted that too, the steadiness, filed it away with everything else. Somewhere below the town, in a furnished room styled sometime in the late seventies, a patient figure was presumably still seated. I didn't say so. Some knowledge you keep in your pocket like a knife.

"Why tell me this?" I asked. "You've spent eighty years keeping the secret. Why hand it to a stranger?"

Edmund looked at me for a long moment, and the delight drained out of his face entirely, and what remained was neither cruel nor kind — just tired, anciently tired, a fatigue with geological strata in it.

"Because I'm dying," he said. "They don't put that in the brochures here, but I read my own chart when the nurse isn't looking. Months, maybe weeks. And when I go, the account doesn't close. Accounts like this don't close. They transfer." He picked up his pen again, turned it over in his root-fingers. "Gerald won't come back. Carol would sooner burn the county down. So the debt rolls to the next holder of the rope, and the next holder of the rope is a woman who says her own name out loud in the dark and then asks the thing below what it is — which, incidentally, no keeper has ever dared, and it *answered* you, didn't it, it answered your questions, I could feel it down there practically purring—"

"It asked me questions," I said. "It asked if I was the one who keeps it."

"And did you answer?" He leaned forward, and his pale eyes had gone bright, hungry, a boy's eyes in a bird's face. "Tell me you answered. Please. Eighty years I've wondered what it does when someone answers honestly. My father lied to it his whole life. His father sang hymns at it. Cowards, all of us, right down the line. And you — you walked in and treated it like a source." He laughed, a dry rustling sound. "Did it like you? Did it *talk* to you?"

"Yes," I said.

"*What did it say?*"

And I told him. I gave him the transcript from memory — *are you the one who keeps it*, and the courteous low voice, and the three answers to questions I hadn't asked, including the one about the door and the one about the names on the wall. I watched him listen the way starving men watch food. When I finished, he sat back, and closed his eyes, and said, softly, mostly to himself:

"So it remembers the original inscription. Under our signatures. Of course it does. It *wrote* it." He opened his eyes. "You have no idea what you've started, Ms. Villalobos. No idea. You've been down there having conversations with a party to a two-hundred-year-old contract, treating it like a person, and contracts, when both parties start acting like persons, get *renegotiated*."

"Good," I said.

He stared at me. Then he laughed again, longer this time, until the nurse poked her head in with concerned eyebrows and he waved her off with imperious little fingers. "Good, she says. Oh, marvelous. Marvelous. Do you know what my grandfather would have done to a keeper who said *good*?" He wiped his eye with a knuckle. "You're going to ruin everything. I can see it. You're going to stand in front of it with your maps and your recordings and your terrible honest face and you're going to try to audit a god."

"That's the plan."

"Well." He picked up his crossword, found his place, and clicked the pen. "Then I suppose I should tell you the rest. All of it. The forty lessons. The quarterly schedule. Where the ledgers are hidden — because there are ledgers, Ms. Villalobos, two hundred years of them, in oilcloth under the floor of the springhouse, and every entry is in my family's hand, and every entry is evidence." He filled in a square without looking at it. "Come back tomorrow. And the day after. I'm dying, I'm bored, and you, my dear, are the most interesting thing to happen to this family since 1931."

I stood to leave. At the door I stopped, because there was one more question, the rude one, the third on his forbidden list, and I found I couldn't walk out without asking it.

"Mr. Ashgrove. Can it be given back?"

He didn't look up from his puzzle. But his pen stopped moving, and for a moment the small bird-shape in the wingback chair was utterly still, listening — and I realized with a cold drop in my stomach that he wasn't deciding whether to answer.

He was listening to make sure *it* wasn't answering first.

"No one's ever tried," he said at last, lightly, filling in another square. "Ask it yourself. You're the one it talks to."

I walked out past the hostage photographs and the lavender smell and the kind-eyed nurse, and out into the parking lot, where the afternoon sun lay flat and ordinary on the reservoir below, and I sat in my car with my hands on the wheel and understood, finally, the shape of the trap I'd been living in for fifteen years.

The ledgers were under the springhouse floor. Two hundred years of entries, in his family's hand, every one of them evidence — of theft, of murder dressed as payment, of a debt inflated by generations of skimming. Edmund was handing me the ammunition with the generosity of a man lighting the fuse on something he wouldn't live to see explode. Whether he meant it as a weapon or a legacy or simply the last entertainment of a dying bookkeeper, I couldn't tell.

But I knew where the springhouse was. I'd mapped it years ago and marked it *structure, purpose unknown*.

Tomorrow, I'd find out what it was for.
