## 18. Edmund's Confession

The call came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Catastrophe in my experience prefers Tuesdays — too far from the weekend's false comfort, too close to Friday's hope.

"Miss Villalobos." The voice was dry as ledger paper. "This is Fairhaven Manor. Mr. Ashgrove has requested you by name."

"I didn't know he knew my name."

"He knows a great many things," the nurse said, and there was something in her tone I'd come to recognize over the years — the flatness of someone who had decided not to think about their job too hard. "He says it's urgent. He says—" a pause, papers shuffling, "—he says 'the account is coming due and the girl should hear it from me before the auditors arrive.'"

"The auditors."

"That's what he said. He also asked for two eggs and a radio, so I wouldn't build your whole worldview around his phrasing."

I told her I'd come Thursday. She said he'd wanted Wednesday. I said Wednesday, then, because apparently I was already taking dictation from a ninety-one-year-old man I'd met exactly once, and because some part of me — the part that had spent fifteen years telling itself that scraping sound was water pipes — understood that you don't negotiate with urgency. You just show up and hate yourself later.

* * *

Fairhaven Manor sat on the good side of town, which is to say the side whose street names were all Ashgrove-adjacent. Ashgrove Boulevard became Ashgrove Lane became Ashgrove Court, a diminishing series like the man himself, and the care facility occupied what had once been the family's summer house, donated to the county in 1961 with great fanfare and, I now suspected, considerable tax advantage. The lobby smelled of potpourri and antiseptic fighting to a draw. A volunteer with kind eyes pointed me down a corridor lined with framed photographs of local history — mill openings, parade floats, a hospital wing groundbreaking featuring a man whose jawline Edmund had clearly passed on along with everything else.

His room was at the end, facing the water tower. Of course it was. The man arranged his dying the way he'd apparently arranged everything else: with sightlines.

"You came." He was propped up in bed like a monument to himself, thin as a rolled newspaper but with eyes that hadn't gotten the memo about decay. They were quick, amused, and utterly without warmth — the eyes of an accountant reviewing a column of numbers that happened to be people. "I half expected you to make me wait until spring. You have that look. Stubborn as a fence post."

"You've read my file?"

"I wrote your file, in a manner of speaking. Sit down. No — not the chair by the window. The other one. The window chair is where my daughter sits when she pretends to visit, and I find the association tiresome."

I took the other chair. Up close he smelled of eucalyptus and something older underneath, something mineral, like the air off deep stone. My skin did its own thinking about that before my brain caught up.

"You're dying," I said.

"Everyone is. In my case the paperwork is simply further along." He waved a hand at a side table, where a leather folio lay closed. "I've asked you here to perform a confession. Not the church kind — I outgrew the church around the same time I outgrew believing anyone was keeping score besides me. The legal kind. Or near enough. There's a recorder in the folio. It's been running since you walked in."

I looked at the folio. Then at him. "Why?"

"Because," Edmund Ashgrove said, settling back against his pillows with the satisfaction of a man about to enjoy a very long-awaited meal, "you have been asking it questions down there, and it has been answering them, and I would bet this facility — which is, incidentally, partly mine still, through three shells and a trust — that it hasn't told you the half of it. It never does. It's very precise, our friend below. Precision without context is how you get people signing things they don't understand. And I have watched you, Miss Villalobos, for longer than you know. You are about to sign something you do not understand."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere down the hall a television laughed at nothing.

"Start at the beginning," I said, because my voice needed something to do while the rest of me reorganized.

* * *

He began, as I suppose he had to, with his father.

"My grandfather held the account through the war years. My father through the fifties and sixties. Neither of them enjoyed it, particularly, but neither of them questioned it either, because questioning it would have meant admitting that the family fortune — the mills, the land, the bank, the boulevard, all of it — rested on a payment schedule. And men who inherit fortunes do not audit them. Auditing is for people with something to lose." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had lost everything except the ability to be smug about it. "I inherited in 1971. I was thirty-eight. My father handed me a ledger — an actual physical ledger, oilcloth-bound, older than the state of this state — and a key, and a sentence. The sentence was: 'Keep it fed and keep it sleeping.' Very biblical. Very useless. There was no instruction manual. There was only the ledger, and the ledger was a list of debts."

"What kind of debts?"

"All kinds. That's the thing no one understands, the thing I'm going to say plainly because I am ninety-one years old and past caring who repeats it." He leaned forward, and for a moment the amusement dropped away and something almost professional looked out at me — the face of a man doing his job. "The debt isn't money. Money is just one currency it accepts. It takes anything of value. Reputation. Time. Memory. Love, if you're poor enough in everything else. And people. People are the oldest denomination. The founding bargain — and yes, I know what the founding bargain was, I read the ledger cover to cover the winter I turned forty, every page, twice — the founding bargain promised remembrance and restitution in perpetuity. Remembrance, restitution. Two words. Do you know how many ways there are to fail at two words?"

"How many ways did *you* fail?"

He didn't flinch. I'll give him that. Ninety-one years old and dying and he didn't flinch; he simply opened the folio, turned the recorder toward me like a man offering a dish, and began.

"In 1974, the payments were falling behind. Not because the well demanded more — it doesn't inflate, it's not a landlord — but because I was skimming. There, I've said it. Every keeper before me skimmed a little; it's practically a family tradition, the way some families have a recipe. But I skimmed more than most, because I had ambitions. The marina development. The land acquisitions along the river. Ambition requires liquidity, and liquidity requires that the account below receive somewhat less than the full measure." He recited it the way another man might describe a golf handicap. "In 1976 the balance grew short enough that things started surfacing. Small things. Dreams, mostly, across the east side. A season of nightmares that put four families into therapy and one into the river — ruled accidental drowning, and it was, technically, she walked in herself, but she was walking *away* from something she'd seen asleep. I paid that year in reputation. Spread enough money around the right institutions that the nightmare cluster got attributed to contaminated groundwater. Which, in fairness, we did also have."

"And 1979," I said. My mouth had gone dry somewhere around the marina.

"Ah." He looked at me with something that in a better man might have been apology, and in Edmund was merely acknowledgment — the nod of one professional recognizing another's area of expertise. "1979. Yes. You know about 1979."

"A worker," I said. "An accident. In the tunnels under the old pump house."

"Dominic Reyes." He said the name easily, without ceremony, and that ease was the worst thing in the room, worse than anything below the well house, because it was the ease of long familiarity. "Thirty-one years old. A pipefitter, second generation, good hands, bad luck — or rather, good luck for me, which is the same thing stated honestly. That autumn the shortfall was severe. I'd leveraged nearly everything for the riverfront parcel, and the account was hungry, and hungry accounts start collecting on their own terms, and its terms involve neighborhoods, Miss Villalobos. Plural. Random. It doesn't discriminate when it collects for itself; discrimination requires intent, and intent was always supposed to be the keeper's contribution. That's the arrangement. It takes what it's owed. The keeper decides *who*. That's the whole terrible dignity of the office — you get to choose, so that the choosing at least happens above ground, among the living, where it can theoretically be done with mercy."

He paused, and I realized he was waiting for me to understand the shape of it before he filled it in. He'd been a teacher once, I learned later. Before the inheritance took him.

"So I chose," he said. "Reyes was contracted for maintenance work on the low gallery — the passage you've mapped, the one with the standing water. I arranged the schedule myself. I made sure the shoring inspection was signed off by a man who owed me money. I made sure the gas monitor was calibrated by a technician who didn't know what calibration meant. And I made sure Dominic Reyes went down alone, because the account prefers singles — easier to weigh, it says. Easier to hold." His voice didn't change register at all. It stayed level, dry, precise, the voice of a quarterly report. "The collapse was real. I didn't fake the collapse. I only arranged the circumstances, and then I called it an act of God, and God, whoever God is in this arrangement, did not contradict me."

The television down the hall laughed again. A cart rattled past the door. The world continued being ordinary at volume, and I sat in a vinyl chair holding the knowledge that the man in front of me had selected a human being the way you select a stock to sell — at a loss, deliberately, to cover larger obligations — and had slept afterward. Had, presumably, slept well.

"You feel nothing," I said. It wasn't a question, but he answered it like one.

"Guilt?" He considered the word like a wine he'd been served and found corked. "Guilt is what people feel when they imagine an audience. I had no audience. The one party entitled to judge the transaction accepted delivery and gave receipt. You can check the ledger — every year I settled, there's a mark. Received in full. Whatever else I was, I was *solvent*, Miss Villalobos. Forty years solvent. Do you know what the town would have looked like if I'd had a crisis of conscience in 1985? In 1993? The east side knows. The east side dreamed it." He tapped the folio. "What I feel is bookkeeping anxiety. There is a difference, and the difference matters to what comes next, so attend: guilt is backward-looking and useless. Bookkeeping anxiety is forward-looking. It says: the accounts must balance before the auditor arrives, or the estate pays penalties."

"The auditors," I said. "Your nurse mentioned—"

"My nurse mentions whatever I tell her to mention. Listen." For the first time, something moved behind the dryness — not fear, I want to be clear about that, Edmund Ashgrove did not fear anything I ever saw, but urgency, the urgency of a man watching a deadline approach that he could no longer manage personally. "I am dying on a schedule I did not choose, and the account will notice my death the way a bank notices a defaulting signature. The obligation passes. That is the design — perpetual, transferable, attached to the office rather than the man. My children refused the succession. Both of them. Smart children. Cowardly, but smart — cowardice and intelligence run together in this family like veins in marble. So the debt defaulted. And per the original terms — terms I did not write, terms older than the ledger, older than the language they were first spoken in — a defaulted obligation attaches to whoever holds the rope."

"Holds the—"

"Don't play innocent with me. You've been descending for fifteen years. You've touched every wall. You've named the passages — I know you name them, it's in your maps, your beautiful obsessive hand-drawn maps, and naming is *acceptance*, it's the oldest form of acceptance there is, older than signatures. You thought you were exploring. You were *onboarding*." He actually chuckled, a small dry sound like a drawer closing. "It's been cultivating you for years. The breathing on the recordings. The questions. It doesn't haunt, it *recruits*. And now the incumbent is dying, and it wants continuity of service, and you are the most qualified candidate it has produced in a century."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to stand; my body simply removed itself from the conversation. "I'm not accepting anything."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Not yet. But you came here today, and you'll come again, and you'll listen to everything I say, because you need to know, and needing to know is the leash. It's always been the leash. Curiosity is the rope, Miss Villalobos. You've been holding it since you were sixteen years old."

* * *

We talked for three hours. The nurse came twice and was waved off both times with a flick of his fingers, and she went, and I understood that everyone in this building had some version of the arrangement with him — small services rendered, questions not asked, comfort purchased at the price of ignorance. The whole facility was a little model of the town. Maybe the whole town was a little model of something older.

He confessed in order, chronologically, like a man walking a property line. 1982: the scholarship fund endowed with money that should have gone below, patched the following year with a professor's career — "a plagiarism scandal, entirely manufactured, I regret the mechanics, not the outcome." 1988: the hospital wing, dedicated with his father's name on it, funded by a decade of deferred payments that cost the pediatric ward two nurses' worth of luck apiece — "nothing fatal, I wasn't a monster, just attrition, misfortune distributed." 1999: the year he tried to stop paying altogether, convinced by a lawyer — "a clever fool, he thought it was a superstition, he wanted to litigate it, can you imagine, he drafted a motion" — and the resulting eight months, which he declined to detail except to say: "Ask the east side about the summer of '99. Ask them why half the street sold their houses in one season and none of them will say why. We resumed payments in October. Things quieted by Christmas."

And through all of it, the ledger. He showed me pages of it eventually — I held it, oilcloth cool and faintly warm at the center, like something recently breathed on — columns in a dozen hands across two centuries, each entry a year, a sum, a denomination. Some entries were sums of money. Some were single words. One, dated 1953, said only *a voice* — and when I asked, Edmund said, "My aunt Margaret. She sang. It liked her singing. She gave it forty years of Sundays and died at fifty-one with a voice like gravel, and she considered it a fair trade, and who am I to argue with a dead woman's accounting?"

"Why are you telling me this?" I finally asked, near the end, when the light through the window had gone amber and the corridor outside had gone quiet. "Really. Not the bookkeeping anxiety. Why confess to me specifically? You could have burned the ledger. Died silent. Let the debt sort itself out."

He was quiet for a moment — the longest silence he'd allowed all day — and when he spoke, the dryness cracked just enough to show that there had been something underneath it all along, though whether it was fear or envy or grief I couldn't tell, and I suspect neither could he.

"Because I have kept this account for forty years and I have never once been *witnessed*," he said. "Do you understand? The town doesn't know. My children don't know. My wife guessed and chose the garden club. Every payment, every choice, every name — mine alone, unwitnessed, unaudited by any living soul. And now I'm ending, and the ending is the first honest thing I'll have done since 1971, and an honest thing deserves a record." He closed the folio. "You wanted evidence your whole life, Miss Villalobos. Fifteen years of recorders in the dark. Well. Here is the evidence. The monster was never only in the well. The monster had a desk."

He turned his face to the window then, toward the water tower, and I understood I was dismissed.

At the door I stopped. "Dominic Reyes," I said. "Did he have family?"

"A daughter," Edmund said, without turning. "She'd be in her fifties now. Lives out west, I believe. He had a brother who never believed the accident report and drank himself dead over it by '86." A pause. "That one wasn't in the ledger. That one was just collateral. There's more collateral than principal, in the end. No one tells you that."

I left. In the parking lot my hands shook so badly I couldn't fit the key in the ignition for a full minute, and I sat there watching the water tower go gold and then gray as the sun went down, thinking about a daughter in her fifties who didn't know, and a brother who died not knowing, and a man upstairs who felt nothing about any of it except that the books were about to close unbalanced.

I drove home. I checked my bag out of habit — camera, notebook, the tools of a fifteen-year habit — and everything was there. Everything accounted for.

It was three days later, going through my jacket pocket for receipts, that I found the slip of paper I hadn't put there. Heavy stock, cream-colored, engraved. An address I didn't recognize, a date — next week — and beneath it, in a firm old-fashioned hand:

*Probate of the Estate of Edmund Ashgrove. All interested parties requested to attend. All obligations appurtenant shall pass to the current holder of the key.*

I don't own a key to the well house. The well house doesn't have a lock.

But my lock picks were missing from my bag, and in their place, wrapped in a twist of oilcloth that smelled of deep stone, was a single iron key, warm as a held coin.
