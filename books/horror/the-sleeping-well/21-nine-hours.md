## 21. Nine Hours

The summons came at 6:14 in the evening, which I know because every device in my apartment announced it in chorus — the recorder on my desk, the backup recorder in the closet, the voice memo app on my phone, even the ancient dictaphone I'd bought at a yard sale for five dollars and never erased. All of them turned themselves on at once and spoke with one patient, courteous voice:

*"The account is open. Come settle it."*

Then they all shut off again, politely, like a receptionist hanging up.

I sat on my kitchen floor for a while after that. Not crying, not panicking — just sitting, the way you sit when a doctor says a word you've been dreading for years and your body needs a moment to catch up to the news. Fifteen years of explanations, catalogued and cross-referenced. Two nights of recordings. One dying patriarch who'd smiled at me like I was a promotion he'd earned. And now this: an appointment.

Sunrise was nine hours away.

Here's what nobody tells you about terror: it's incredibly clarifying. My whole life I'd assumed that if the thing below ever actually called me by name, I'd fall apart. Instead I stood up, washed my coffee mug, and started making lists. Because if I was going to walk into that shaft tonight, I was not going to walk in empty-handed, and I was not going to walk in as *the keeper*. Edmund Ashgrove had held that title for sixty years and all it had made him was a man who arranged accidents and felt nothing but bookkeeping anxiety about them. I refused to die as his successor. If I was going down there, I was going down as something else. A witness, maybe. An auditor. A pain in its courteous, bottomless ass.

* * *

The list looked insane written out, so I tried not to look at it too directly. Iron stakes — four of them, from a salvage yard off Delmore Street, cold and pitted and heavier than they had any right to be. Salt, two full canisters, because folklore is folklore for a reason and I'd rather be the explorer who overpacked than the one who didn't. My camera. Three recorders. Rope — my rope, the one I'd been holding for fifteen years without knowing what holding meant.

And paper. God, so much paper.

The lawyer's letter took me two hours to draft, mostly because I kept stopping to laugh at myself. Here I was, drafting formal correspondence to an entity older than language, cc'ing no one, on letterhead I made myself. But the act of writing it mattered. I wrote out everything Edmund had confessed to me three days ago, in that care facility room that smelled of lemon disinfectant and old money: the 1979 accident he'd arranged, the payments he'd skimmed, the decades of theft dressed up as stewardship. I named dates. I named amounts. I named the worker — Daniel Reyes, thirty-four years old, a name Edmund had recited with the flat affection of a man reading last quarter's losses.

At the bottom of the letter I wrote: *"This account has been fraudulently inflated by the party responsible for maintaining it. The undersigned requests a full audit before any transfer of obligation is recognized."*

Then I signed it. Ada Villalobos. In ink. Like it meant something. Like anything meant anything.

The recorded testimony was harder. I set up a recorder facing my kitchen table and talked into it for forty minutes, naming Edmund's crimes in a steady voice, and somewhere around minute twenty-five I realized my hands were shaking — not from fear of the thing below, but because saying Daniel Reyes's name out loud, twice, deliberately, felt like the first honest work I'd done in fifteen years. All those tunnels I'd mapped. All those passages I'd named. I'd been building a career on top of a grave and calling it scholarship.

I finished the testimony anyway. Then I made copies. Paper, flash drive, cloud backup, and — because I am who I am — a microcassette hidden in my boot, because digital files can be deleted and I wanted at least one copy that would require scissors and patience to destroy.

If I was going to be trapped, blackmailed, or eaten, there was going to be a paper trail. Let the thing below explain *that* to whatever passed for its supervisors.

* * *

It was Marisol who broke the spell of my competence.

I heard her knock at 9:40 — three sharp raps, then a pause, then three more, the rhythm she'd used since grad school — and when I opened the door she was standing in the hallway with a thermos, a first-aid kit, and an expression I remembered from the year we'd dated and the twenty years since: the expression of a woman who has already won the argument and is waiting for you to notice.

"No," I said.

"I brought coffee."

"Marisol. No."

"You said sunrise is at 5:52," she said, stepping past me into the apartment like she still lived in it, which in some ways she never stopped doing. "That gives us eight hours and twelve minutes. You'll need forty minutes to get to the well house, an hour to rig the descent, and I refuse to do that math without caffeine."

"*We?*"

She set the thermos on my kitchen counter and turned around slowly, and I saw that under the defiance she was terrified. Her hands weren't quite steady. She'd told me once, years ago, that her grandmother had made her promise never to go near the Ashgrove land, and here she was with a first-aid kit and a thermos, breaking a deathbed promise to stand next to me.

"I'm not going underground," she said. "I told you. I keep my promises where it counts."

"Then—"

"But I'm not letting you drive out there alone at midnight to negotiate with something that eats keepers, either. I'll be at the surface. Top of the shaft. Holding the rope." She paused, and something flickered across her face — the same vertigo I'd felt when I realized what rope-holding meant under the old terms. "Metaphorically and literally. And if you're not back up by 4:30, I'm calling everyone. Police, press, the county archive, my grandmother's ghost if I can get a signal. Everyone."

"That's not how any of this works."

"Neither is a haunted dictaphone, Ada, and yet." She poured two cups of coffee and pushed one toward me. "You spent fifteen years down there alone because you thought solitude was safety. Look how that worked out. The debt defaulted to whoever holds the rope precisely because you made sure no one was ever standing next to you." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the word *next*. "Not anymore. I'm done being the woman you call afterward. Tonight I'm the woman who's there."

I drank my coffee because arguing with Marisol Okonkwo is like arguing with weather. And because — I'll admit it here, in the privacy of my own head, where I keep the things I don't say out loud — because some traitorous part of me had been counting the minutes until she knocked. I'd drafted that lawyer's letter alone. I'd recorded that testimony alone. I'd been preparing to die alone with excellent documentation, and it had felt exactly as grim as it sounds, and when those three knocks came I'd felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched.

Love, it turns out, is mostly logistics performed at inconvenient hours.

* * *

We drove out in her car because mine had been making a noise I'd been explaining away for six months, and I have learned my lesson about explaining things away. The city thinned out around us — strip malls, then warehouses, then the dead waterworks with its chain-link and its NO TRESPASSING signs I'd been ignoring since I was nineteen. Marisol drove with both hands on the wheel and the radio off, which is how she drives when she's frightened, and neither of us said anything for eleven miles.

At the well house, the night was doing its usual performance: cold, clear, stars in ridiculous abundance, the kind of sky that makes you feel like an insect. The brick structure sat doorless on the hillside, breathing its warm breath up out of the shaft, and I watched Marisol feel it — that warm air against the October cold — and watched her decide, visibly, not to run.

"It's breathing," she said.

"It does that."

"That's not comforting, Ada."

"It wasn't meant to be."

We rigged the descent together, and I'll say this for terror: it makes excellent teamwork. She anchored the rope while I checked the knots twice, then a third time, because I wanted the record to show that Ada Villalobos died of supernatural causes and not of an improperly tied figure-eight. We set the recorders at the shaft mouth. We set the backup recorder inside her car, pointed at nothing, running anyway, because at this point why not.

At 11:58, I stood at the edge of the shaft with my pack on my shoulders — iron, salt, paper, testimony, microcassette in my boot — and looked down into the warm dark.

"One more thing," Marisol said.

She handed me her grandmother's rosary. Old, cheap plastic beads gone soft with handling, the kind sold at church bazaars for a dollar fifty. I stared at it.

"Your grandmother warned you about this place."

"She did." Marisol's jaw was set. "And then she told me, the last time I saw her, that the worst thing you can do down there is go alone. She said the thing below respects company. Said it gets *lonely*, Ada. Said lonely things make bad deals." She closed my fingers around the beads. "So you take the company with you. And you come back and tell me everything it says. Every word. That's the price. Witness goes both ways."

I put the rosary around my neck and it hung there, cheap and warm from her pocket, absurd and holy at once.

"4:30," I said.

"4:30," she agreed. "And Ada?" I looked up. She was backlit by the stars, and her voice came out steadier than I think she felt. "Whatever it offers you — the burden, the replacement, the tidy little contract — remember that you've already got a job. You're a witness. Don't let it promote you."

I laughed, which surprised us both, and then I took hold of the rope and went down into the breathing dark, and the last thing I heard from the world above was Marisol's voice, clear and furious and loving, calling down the shaft:

"And bring my rosary back!"

* * *

The descent took longer than it should have, because I stopped at each of the furnished rooms.

They were occupied now. I'd known they would be — I'd seen them on my last descent, the seated figures, generations of the paid, patient as furniture — but knowing didn't soften it. In the second waiting room, a woman in clothing from the 1910s sat with her hands folded, and as my headlamp passed over her she lifted her face, and the expression on it wasn't hunger or menace. It was relief. The relief of someone in a waiting room who hears footsteps, who thinks *finally, someone will tell me what's happening.*

"I'm sorry," I whispered, because I didn't know what else to say to the dead, and because Marisol was right — witness goes both ways, and these were witnesses too, witnesses who'd never been released.

In the fourth room, deeper now, a man in coveralls sat apart from the others. I knew before my light found his face. I'd read the name enough times tonight.

"Daniel Reyes," I said aloud.

His head came up. He had no eyes, and still I felt him look at me.

"They know," I told him. "What he did. It's recorded. It's going into the county archive tomorrow if I live, and if I don't, someone else will file it. Your name is going to be said up there. Out loud. Where people can hear it."

He wept, silently, without eyes, and I stood in a furnished room two hundred feet underground at half past midnight, crying with a dead man, holding a dollar-fifty rosary, and it was the sanest I had felt in fifteen years.

Then I kept descending, because the account was open, and sunrise was seven hours away, and I had documents to serve.
