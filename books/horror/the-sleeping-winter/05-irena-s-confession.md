## 5. Irena's Confession

The snow had a sound, Irena thought, that no one ever wrote down. Not the wind—everyone wrote about the wind—but the snow itself, settling on the fields in the blue hour before dark. A hush with weight to it. A held breath.

She had been listening to that sound for sixty years, waiting for it to break.

Her cottage sat at the field's edge, the last house before the furrows began, and she had chosen it deliberately when she was young enough to choose anything. From her kitchen window she could see the whole white expanse of the harvest ground, flat and innocent under its blanket, and every evening she looked at it the way other people looked at graves. Because that is what it was. A grave that had never been filled properly. A grave that breathed.

Tomas had been eleven. She could still see him if she let herself—the gap in his teeth, the way he ran through the stubble in autumn with his arms out like wings, the verse he'd begged her to teach him because he wanted to sing at the winter gathering and be praised. *Sing it back to me, Irena. Sing it again so I can learn it true.*

And she, fourteen and vain and flattered by his adoration, had sung it twice.

She remembered the exact moment the second verse left her mouth. The way the air changed texture, thickened, as though the words were being swallowed rather than spoken. The way Tomas's smile froze on his face—not fear, not yet, just a kind of listening, his head tilting toward the fields as if someone far away had called his name. And then he had simply walked. Out the door, past the fence, into the stubble, and the snow had come early that year, three weeks early, and by morning there was nothing to find but his boot prints going out and none coming back, and those prints themselves filling softly, softly, until by noon even they were forgotten by the snow.

The village had grieved. The village had also understood. Old Halvard, dead now these forty years, had taken her by the shoulders in the churchyard and said, *You gave it a taste of a name, girl. Now you must spend your life making sure it never gets the rest.* And she had. God help her, she had.

Every night since, she had hummed the melody. Only the melody, never the words, never twice, trailing off each time at the same broken place where the verse ended incomplete—as if the song itself had been wounded and limped home without its final measures. She hummed it into the dark of her kitchen, facing the fields, low and steady, a fisherman's line cast into deep water. *Come back, Tomas. Come back to the sound of me.*

It never came back. But something always listened. She could feel it on the far side of the window glass some nights—a pressure, an attention, like a face pressed to the other side of ice. It had learned her humming the way a dog learns a whistle. It came to the edge of the fields and stood there, patient, hungry, waiting for her to finish what she'd started sixty years ago.

And she had begun to understand, in her old age, that this was the cruelest part: it wasn't keeping Tomas. It didn't know how to keep anything. It only knew how to want. Whatever wore her brother's absence like a coat, whatever had taken his shape into the ground, it was still waiting for the rest of the song—and she was the only one who knew how it went.

Until the folklorist came.

* * *

Lior found her cottage by following the hum.

He had spent two days transcribing in the inn's common room, and the melody had gotten into him the way melodies do—uninvited, lodging behind the sternum. He'd caught himself humming it while winding tape, and stopped, cold, remembering the singer who had whispered *it remembers whoever repeats it* and gripped his wrist hard enough to bruise. He did not know where he'd learned it. That frightened him more than anything else in Vell Hollow. Somewhere in the village, someone was humming it right now, and it had traveled through the walls and the wind and the wool of his own thoughts until it lived in him.

He followed it up the lane past the last lamppost, past the fence where the plowed road surrendered to fields, to a small stone cottage with smoke rising straight as a plumb line from its chimney. The door opened before he knocked.

Irena Voss—no relation to Marta, she would say later, or perhaps every relation, here—was small and dry as a winter apple, with eyes the pale gray of old ice and hands folded in her lap like closed books. She looked at him, then at the recorder bag on his shoulder, and something moved across her face that was not quite a smile.

"You've been humming," she said. "Since Tuesday. In the inn. Marta told me."

"I'm sorry—I didn't mean—"

"No." She stepped back from the doorway. "You didn't mean. That's how it works. Come in before the light goes."

Inside, the cottage smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs hung from beams, and one wall was covered in photographs—decades of them, sepia fading to color, and in every single one the same composition: an old woman standing at her kitchen window, looking out at the fields. Lior counted nineteen of them. Nineteen years, at least, of standing watch.

"You photograph yourself watching," he said quietly.

"I photograph myself so I'll remember why." She poured tea without asking, and her hands trembled only slightly, and he understood that the trembling was permanent, structural, like a crack run through glaze. "Sit. And take out your machine. You'll want to record this, though heaven knows what good it will do you."

He set the recorder between them on the table. She looked at it a long moment.

"Sixty years ago I sang a verse twice," she said.

The fire popped. Outside, the snow made its weighted hush against the windows.

"My brother Tomas wanted to learn the winter gathering song. He was eleven. I was fourteen and I liked being asked. So I taught him, and when he stumbled on the third measure I sang it again to show him how it went." Her voice was level, recitational, the voice of someone who had said these words to herself ten thousand times in the dark. "He walked out into the harvest fields before the second verse finished leaving my mouth. They found his tracks going out. None coming back."

Lior's hand had gone to his mouth without his permission. "Irena—I'm so sorry—"

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She leaned forward, and the firelight fell into the hollows of her face. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Everyone in this village has spent their lives not singing. Not repeating. Building a wall out of forgetting, brick by brick, verse by unspoken verse. And it works—it holds. But it holds the way a dam holds, and dams need tending, and we are dying, folklorist. Every winter there are fewer voices at the gathering. Who tends the wall when we're gone?"

"The ledger," Lior said. "In the church basement. There's a ritual—half of it, the verses torn out—"

"Torn out by men who hoped their grandchildren would have the courage they didn't." She almost smiled. "But the verses aren't gone. They're in me. Sixty years of humming the melody, hunting the words along its bones. I have most of them. Not all. The last measures—" her voice caught, the first crack in the recitation, "—the last measures are where Tomas went, and I cannot reach them alone. My memory is an old woman's memory. It frays."

"What do the verses do?"

"They finish the invitation." She said it plainly, terribly. "What's under those fields was buried singing, did Marta tell you that? Bound with a song, lulled asleep by forgetting. But a binding sung once is a promise half-kept. The founders meant to sing it complete, twice, all together, and seal it forever. They lost their nerve. They tore the pages out and taught their children silence instead, and called the silence safety." She shook her head slowly. "Silence isn't safety. Silence is just hunger with patience."

Lior thought of the photographs stacked in his rented room upstairs at the inn—the chair in every hearth, closer in each roll, measured in centimeters like a countdown. He thought of the pressure he'd felt in the church basement, the ledger's marginalia in a founder's shaking hand: *it must be fed willingly or it will feed itself.*

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "Why me?"

"Because you're the first outsider in thirty years who came to listen. Because your machine remembers things perfectly—that's what it's for, isn't it? What time erases?" Her gray eyes fixed on him, and he felt seen through, catalogued, filed. "Because it has already chosen you. Don't look surprised. We can all see the chair in your photographs now. Marta showed me. It doesn't choose carelessly, and it doesn't choose cruelly. It chooses the one who listens best."

The recorder's reels turned between them, silent, faithful, eating the room's quiet.

"I can't finish the melody alone," Irena said. "But between your recordings and my humming and the ledger's margins—between what I remember and what your machine remembers—we might reconstruct it entire. All the torn verses. The whole invitation." She reached across the table and laid her dry, trembling hand over his. Her skin was cold as the window glass. "Sixty years I've stood at that window calling my brother home with half a song. Help me finish it. Help me end it—one way or the other, end it. Before the harvest moon rises."

"And if we're wrong," Lior heard himself say. "If finishing it does the opposite of sealing it—"

"Then it does what it was always going to do, but sooner, and to a village that at least chose its ending." She withdrew her hand and looked past him, out the dark window, toward the fields. "I have hummed to that thing for sixty years, folklorist. Some nights I swear it hums back. And some nights—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—some nights it sounds like an eleven-year-old boy, still trying to learn the third measure."

The fire settled. Somewhere beyond the fields, beneath the snow, beneath the frozen furrows, something shifted in its sleep—or woke a little further—and Lior felt the melody stir behind his sternum, unfinished, patient, waiting for its ending.

He reached out and turned the recorder on.

"Start from the beginning," he said. "Sing it once. I'll remember the rest."
