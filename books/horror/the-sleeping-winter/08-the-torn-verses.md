## 8. The Torn Verses

The church basement smelled of cold stone and lamp oil, of paper gone soft as old skin. Lior had spread the founders' ledger open on a table dragged down from the vestry, its pages weighted flat with two hymnals and a candlestick, and beside it he had arranged his life's work like evidence at a trial: the reel-to-reel recorder, its spools of tape, the notebooks with their careful phonetic transcriptions, and the photographs—every one of them now showing the chair, close enough in the last roll that he could count the rings in its wood.

Irena sat across from him, her hands folded on the table as if in prayer, though her eyes never closed. She was eighty-one years old and her hands did not shake. Sixty years of humming had stilled them, he thought, or perhaps they had simply learned that trembling was a luxury the village could not afford.

"The ledger ends here," he said, turning to the final legible page. The ink was iron-gall brown, faded to the color of dried blood, and the handwriting of Aldous Crane, first warden of Vell Hollow, marched across the page in the confident script of a man who believed he was building something permanent. Then the page ended. The next leaf had been torn away—not cut, torn, in haste or in terror, the ragged edge still furred after two centuries. "Everything after the seventh verse is gone."

"Nothing is gone," Irena said. "It's only been scattered. Like seed." She unfolded her hands and laid one, palm up, on the table between them. "I have carried my part for sixty years, Lior. Every night. Every night since Tam."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way the very old tell terrible things, with the flatness of a woman who had worn the story smooth as a river stone. She was nineteen. The winter of her nineteenth year had come early and hard, and her brother Tam had a voice like water over stones, and one night, drunk on cider and his own cleverness, he had dared her to sing the harvest verse twice. Just to prove the rule was superstition. Just to prove that the founders had been frightened farmers, nothing more.

She sang it twice.

"And the snow stopped falling," she said. "Do you understand? Not eased. Stopped. Every flake hung in the air like flies in amber, and the silence was so complete I could hear my own blood. And then the fields—" Her voice caught, not with tears but with something older than tears. "The fields breathed in. The whole harvest field, under the snow, drew one long breath, and Tam walked out the door before I could catch his sleeve. He wasn't walking. He was being *poured* out, the way you pour the last of the milk, slow and even, and he never once looked back at me."

They found his boots at the field's edge in the morning, set neatly side by side, laces tucked inside. The snow held no prints. The village held no memory of him—not in their mothers' mouths, not in the parish register, where a page had been torn out in the same hurried way as the ledger's last leaf. Only Irena remembered, and so only Irena could grieve him, and grief with no grave to visit will find its own.

"So I hummed," she said. "The melody he loved, the one that stopped mid-verse, the one no archive holds because it belongs to *him*—to what took him. I hummed it for sixty years, thinking if I sang the song wrong enough, long enough, it would come back to correct me. To bring him back. To bring back anything." She looked at Lior, and her eyes were the pale blue of ice over deep water. "It never came. Until you arrived with your machine that makes songs into *things*. Things that can be played again."

* * *

They worked through the night.

The method was Irena's, and it was terrible in its simplicity. The ledger's marginalia—Crane's cramped annotations in the gutters of the pages, notes he had clearly believed no one would read—gave the architecture of the ritual: seven sung verses to bind, three torn away to complete, and a final line, half-legible, that Lior copied out and then covered with his hand as if the ink might bite: *and the singer shall be remembered by that which sleeps below.*

"Remembered," he said. "Not remembered *by the village*. Remembered by *it*."

"That's what the chair is for," Irena said.

His recordings, played backward, gave them the torn verses' bones. This was the part Lior understood least and trusted most: the thing beneath the fields did not feed on song exactly, but on *repetition*—on the same sounds arriving again, and again, and the hunger of something that had been listening for two hundred years to a village determined never to repeat itself. Played forward, his tapes were documentation. Played backward, they were an echo, and echoes were the only language the founders had ever permitted it to hear. When he ran Irena's hummed melody backward against the reel of the old woman from the east lane—her voice fracturing mid-verse, refusing, terrified—the two sounds interlocked like teeth. A verse emerged. Not composed. *Recovered.*

Irena wrote the words as she heard them, in pencil, in a hand that finally, for the first time in sixty years, was shaking.

By the third hour of the night, they had eight verses. The ninth came at dawn, assembled from the backward tape and a scrap of marginalia in which Crane had transcribed, in Greek letters, a single line he had apparently been too frightened to write in his own alphabet. Lior's academic Greek was rusty but sufficient, and when he translated it he sat back from the table as though the chair beneath him had shifted an inch.

*"It does not want a song," he read aloud. "It wants a singer. Feed it a voice and it will sleep content, for it will finally have someone to remember it by."*

* * *

The terrible clarity came to him slowly, the way cold comes into a house—not all at once but room by room, until there is no warm place left to stand.

He had believed, walking into Vell Hollow with his equipment and his confidence, that documentation was preservation, that recording a song was saving it from time. Now, reading the completed ritual in Irena's pencil script, he understood the founders' arithmetic. The thing beneath the fields was not a beast to be banished. It was a hunger to be *kept*—kept fed, kept lulled, kept company. The village's rule against repetition was not superstition. It was rationing. Every verse sung once and never twice was a single meal, portioned out across generations, and the forgetting was not cruelty but mercy: the thing below fed best on those who were remembered, so the village had learned to forget its own dead, to tear out pages, to let names go, until there was almost nothing left for it to hold.

Almost nothing. A brother's boots in the snow. A melody hummed for sixty years.

"The ritual doesn't end it," Lior said. His voice sounded strange in the basement, small against the stone. "It doesn't banish it. It gives it what it's been starving for. A keeper. A voice that belongs to it, that remembers it, that it can remember in return. Forever."

"Yes," Irena said, without flinching. "That is what it does."

"And the singer—"

"The singer takes the chair." She said it the way you say *the sun rises*. "The chair is not a summons, Lior. It is an *invitation*. It has been moving closer to you since the day you arrived because you are the first person in two hundred years who came here *willing to listen twice*. The village sings each verse once and lets it go. You recorded everything. You kept everything. You are a man who cannot bear to let a song be lost." She reached across the table and touched the reel-to-reel, gently, the way one touches a sleeping animal. "It has been listening to you listen."

He thought of the photographs. The chair at the far wall of the hearth, then by the mantel, then at the table's edge, and in the final roll of film—developed only that morning—drawn up so close to the camera that its seat filled half the frame, waiting, patient as furniture, patient as a grave. He had measured the distances in his notebook like a fool, like a scholar, charting his own approach the way you'd chart an eclipse.

"If I don't do it," he said, "it takes someone else. The barrier is already thin. My tapes weakened it—I fed it more in three weeks than the village fed it in a decade."

"Yes."

"And if the moon rises and no one sings the ninth verse—"

"Then it comes up through the fields on its own terms, and it is very hungry, and it has been waiting a very long time, and it will not stop at one singer. It will remember all of us. All at once." Irena folded the pencil pages together with the care of a woman handling a shroud. "I would go myself. I have wanted to go myself for sixty years. But the ritual requires a singer it has *chosen*, and it has chosen you, and I am too old to be worth remembering." Her voice did not break. "I am sorry. I am so sorry. I asked you here to finish a song, and instead you must finish a grave."

Lior looked down at the completed verses, nine of them, the last three in Irena's trembling pencil, and felt something he had never felt in all his years of scholarship: that he was not reading a text but being read by one. The words required a singer willing to be remembered by the thing below—willing to trade name and memory and the whole warm archive of a self for the safety of a village that would, by the ritual's own design, forget him by morning.

He had come to Vell Hollow to make certain nothing was ever lost.

It seemed the village had found a use for exactly that.

* * *

They finished as the light outside the church's high window turned the color of pewter, the last grey hour before the harvest moon would rise. Irena copied the ninth verse fair, in ink, and Lior sang it once, softly, to test the melody against her hum—and the lamp flame leaned toward him, just slightly, like a listener.

Below the floor, beneath the stone and the frozen earth, something exhaled.

Lior closed the ledger. Outside, the villagers were gathering in the lane, and the snow had begun to fall again—falling now in long, slow flakes that seemed to be listening—and far off across the harvest fields, at the edge of sight, he could see the chair.

It stood alone in the open snow, facing the church.

Waiting for him to come and sit.
