## 9. Seven Years, Eleven Families

The Harrow County Records Annex smelled like a church basement that had given up on God and taken up with dust instead. It was a squat brick building behind the courthouse, open Tuesdays and Thursdays and by appointment, staffed by a single man named Dill who looked as though he had been appointed to the position by the building itself and had never quite worked up the nerve to decline.

Wendell liked him immediately.

"You want deed transfers for Bell House," Dill said, tapping a pencil against his lower lip. "That's a popular one."

"It is?"

"No." He smiled, and Wendell revised her opinion of him upward. "But you're the third person this year to ask, and the other two didn't laugh at my joke either. Come on back."

The deed room was a narrow corridor of green metal shelving with a single window near the ceiling, the kind of window that suggested the architects had considered the possibility of light and then decided against committing to it. Wendell had spent enough of her life in rooms like this to find it comforting. Records rooms were honest places. Houses lied. People lied. But a deed transfer was a deed transfer, dated, signed, and filed in triplicate by somebody who had nothing to gain from the truth and did it anyway.

She pulled the Bell House chain and settled at the long oak table under the one lamp.

The transfers went back further than she'd expected. She'd assumed she'd find a handful of sales, the usual slow churn of property. What she found instead was a rhythm so regular it looked less like real estate and more like a pulse.

1931 to 1938: the Voss family. 1938 to 1945: the Merricks. 1945 to 1952: the Aldridges. She kept reading, and the pattern held with a precision that made the back of her neck prickle. Seven years. Every family, seven years, give or take a month. And then a transfer, and a new name, and seven more years.

Eleven transfers. Eleven families. The Okonkwos were the twelfth, and by Wendell's arithmetic they were coming up on their seventh anniversary in the spring.

"Seven-year itch," she murmured, and then wished she hadn't, because the joke landed somewhere in her chest and stayed there, cold.

She did what surveyors do, which is to say she made a table. She drew it in her field book, neat columns, her small precise handwriting, and beside each family's name she wrote the dates. Then she did the thing she'd been avoiding, which was to look for what came *after*.

The Voss family, 1931 to 1938. She checked the county census microfilms in the second room. The Voss family appeared in the 1930 census, listed at a farm address. They appeared in the 1940 census nowhere at all. No Bell House entry. No other county entry. As if eight years of a family's life had been lifted out of the record like a tooth.

The Merricks, same. The Aldridges, same. She worked through them one by one, and by the fourth family she had stopped taking notes and started just sitting very still, because the notes were all the same note.

No obituaries. She checked. Dill kept the newspaper archives on microfilm in the corner, and she scrolled through seven decades of the *Harrow Courier* — births, weddings, church suppers, a truly alarming number of pie contests — and not one death notice, not one missing-persons report, not one farewell feature for any family that had lived at Bell House. The paper covered the county like a quilt. It covered everything except the house.

People didn't leave Bell House. People didn't die at Bell House. People at Bell House were simply *filed away*.

Wendell thought about the twelfth door closing above her on her first morning, and about a nine-year-old's drawing of a tally man, and about a figure in a yellow raincoat who had said *you're late for the count* with the mild reproach of someone discussing a dentist appointment.

"Find what you were looking for?" Dill asked, appearing beside her with two cups of coffee, one of which he set in front of her without being asked. It was terrible coffee. It was the best thing she'd tasted in days.

"No," she said honestly. "I was hoping to find less."

"Ah." He nodded with the serenity of a man who had worked in records for thirty years and had long ago made peace with the fact that paper remembers what people prefer to forget. "You know, there's a box in the back that might interest you. Surveys. Old field books the county bought at an estate sale in the sixties. Nobody's asked for them since."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're a surveyor." He said it simply, the way you'd say *because it's raining*. "And because you're the first person to come in here about Bell House who didn't seem frightened of it. The other two were. Frightened people don't ask good questions."

*The other two,* Wendell thought. *The third person this year.* She filed that away in the growing drawer of things she'd examine later, when examining things was less likely to make her scream.

The survey box was cardboard, soft with age, tied with string that had once been white. Inside were a dozen field books, cloth-bound, their spines faded to the color of weak tea. Wendell lifted the top one and read the label in old copperplate: *HARROW CO. — BELL HOUSE PARCEL — 1931 — E. LOWRY, SURVEYOR.*

She opened it.

The first page was ordinary enough — boundary measurements, bearings, the chain and transit work of a professional doing a professional's job. But it was the *handwriting* that stopped her. Not the copperplate of the label, which was formal, drafted, the handwriting you put on for other people. The working hand underneath — the quick, slanted, slightly impatient cursive of a person thinking faster than they wrote — was familiar in a way that made her stomach drop.

It was her handwriting.

Not exactly. Seventy years and a different person, obviously. But the same habits. The same way of crossing sevens. The same little tick at the end of a *4*. The same tendency to start a line confidently and let it sag where the thought got complicated. Wendell had been told all her life that her handwriting was strange — too old-fashioned, her teachers said, like something out of another century — and she had always privately agreed, without ever knowing what she meant by it.

She turned pages. Lowry, whoever he had been, was good. Meticulous. The kind of surveyor who noted the weather, the hour, the mood of the client. And somewhere around the third week of the Bell House survey, the entries stopped being about the parcel.

*June 14. Measured the interior again. The front rooms give 22 ft 4 in across; the exterior wall to wall gives 21 ft 9. I have checked my chain twice and my eyes three times. The house is larger inside. I have written this in a field book that will be read by other professionals, so I will say it plainly: the house is larger inside, and I no longer believe the error is mine.*

Wendell's coffee went cold in her hand.

*June 15. Counted the doors as a check on my floor plan. Eleven. Mrs. Voss says there are eleven. At dusk I counted twelve. I did not write this down yesterday because I hoped I had imagined it. I am writing it down today because I have decided that hoping is not a method.*

Wendell laughed — a short, startled bark that echoed off the green shelving — and then pressed her hand over her mouth, because it wasn't a funny laugh. It was the laugh of recognition, the laugh you make when a stranger on a train says something you've only ever said to yourself.

*June 19. The Voss children have an imaginary companion. The little girl calls him Mr. Tally. She says he lives in the walls and keeps the numbers. I asked her, in the way one humors children, what numbers. She said, "The ones the house owes." I have decided to take the rest of my measurements in daylight.*

*June 22. I have been in this county thirty years and no one remembers the family that lived at Bell House before the Voss family. I asked old Mrs. Henn at the general store. She sold them eggs every week, she says, but she cannot tell me their name. She cannot tell me their faces. She remembers the eggs.*

The entries grew shorter as June turned to July, and stranger. Lowry had begun, Wendell realized with a professional's horror, to do what she had done: to treat the house as a survey problem. He'd measured the hallway at different hours. He'd mapped the doors. He'd tried, with the beautiful doomed stubbornness of a man with good instruments and a bad situation, to catch the house in its error, to pin it down on paper where paper could hold it.

*July 3. Eleven families by the deed book. I have read them all. Seven years apiece, like a lease with terms no one signed. I believe the house is counting, and I believe I know what it is counting toward, and I believe that the survey is not the reason I was hired. I believe I was hired because the house wanted a reader. It wanted someone to check its sums.*

*July 4. I have a plan. If the ledger wants names, it shall have names. I will enter false families into the record — I know the registrar's clerk, and he owes me money — and I will see whether the house can tell the difference between a name and a person. If it cannot, then it is only arithmetic, and arithmetic can be cheated. If it can —*

The entry stopped there. Not faded, not torn. It simply stopped, mid-sentence, the way a man stops talking when someone else enters the room.

The next page was blank. The one after that was blank. Wendell turned through ten, twenty pages of beautiful empty paper, and then, on the last leaf of the book, in handwriting that had gone shaky, small, pressed so hard into the page that it had nearly cut through:

*it knows. it knows it knows it knows. do not add names. do not subtract. the count is honest. the count has always been honest. tell whoever reads this —*

The sentence ended. The rest of the page was a smear, as though the pen had been dragged sideways, and below the smear, in a different pressure, a different angle — almost playful — two words:

*TOO LATE.*

Wendell set the book down very carefully, the way you set down a sleeping child or a live grenade.

She sat in the green quiet of the records annex for a long time. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere a pipe ticked. Dill had retreated to his desk and was pretending, courteously, not to exist.

Here was what she knew now, laid out in her mind with a surveyor's terrible clarity: the house had been doing this for at least a century. Eleven families, seven years each, collected and erased. And in 1931 a man with her handwriting and her profession and, God help her, her exact brand of stubbornness had stood where she was standing, seen the same pattern, and tried to beat it with paperwork.

He had failed. Worse than failed — the house had taken his crew, she'd bet anything on it, because the 1931 deed transfer was signed not by Lowry but by a bank trustee, and there was no E. Lowry in the 1940 census either. She checked. She had to. There was no E. Lowry anywhere.

*The house doesn't lose tenants,* she thought. *It collects them. And it collects the people who come to count.*

But Lowry had learned something before the end, and he'd spent his last ink on it: *do not add names. do not subtract. the count is honest.* Cheating was death. Which meant the only moves left on the board were the ones the house itself permitted — and a house that permitted bargaining was a house that could be negotiated with.

Wendell closed the field book and held it against her chest for a moment, the way you'd hold a letter from the dead.

"Thank you, Mr. Lowry," she said quietly. "Sorry it didn't take."

"Did you find what you needed?" Dill asked as she passed his desk.

"I found what somebody else needed," she said. "Ninety years late."

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old paper. Wendell got in her truck and sat with her hands on the wheel, and it was only when she tried to turn the key that she noticed her phone buzzing in the cupholder. A text from a number she didn't have saved.

She opened it. It was a photograph, taken from inside a house she recognized — the Bell House hallway, the one that got longer at night. The picture was slightly tilted, taken from a child's height. And in the far end of the hallway, where the wallpaper should have been, there was a small pale figure in a yellow raincoat, standing very still, looking directly into the camera.

The text beneath it was from Amara Okonkwo, and it said only:

*he's not waiting for you anymore. he's waiting for me.*
