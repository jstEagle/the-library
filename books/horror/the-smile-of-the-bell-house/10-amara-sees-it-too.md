## 10. Amara Sees It Too

Amara Okonkwo knew three things for certain about the tally man.

One: he was tall the way a lamppost is tall, not the way a daddy is tall.

Two: he smelled like pencil shavings and cold air from a freezer that had been opened just once, a long time ago.

Three: he was not allowed to touch her yet. He said so himself, every night, in his voice like paper being folded. *Not yet. Hold still.*

She did not tell her mother about him, because her mother would do the face — the worried face with the little crease between the eyebrows that looked like a bird had landed there — and then her mother would call somebody, and then everybody would talk in the kitchen in low voices while pretending to chop vegetables, and nothing would change except that Amara would have to start sleeping with the hall light on, which made it worse, because the tally man liked the light. He stood in it. He stood in it the way you stand in front of an open refrigerator at midnight, patient, unhurried, absolutely sure the door will close on its own eventually.

But the surveyor lady was different.

The surveyor lady — Wendell, though Amara thought of her as *the measuring woman* — had been coming to the house for days now with her funny folding ruler and her little camera and her clipboard, and she counted things under her breath. Doors. Windows. Stairs. Amara had heard her through the banister on Tuesday: *"…nine, ten, eleven,"* and then a pause so long Amara had peeked over the rail to see if she'd fallen asleep standing up. She hadn't. She'd been staring at the ceiling with an expression Amara recognized, because she wore it herself sometimes in the bath when the water went cold without her noticing.

It was the expression of someone who had expected twelve.

So on Thursday afternoon, when Mama said, "Amara, go see if Miss Wendell wants lemonade," Amara went, but she did not bring lemonade. She brought her notebook instead — the purple one with the unicorn on it, the one she used for Important Things, which currently contained a drawing of the tally man, two observations about the neighbor's cat, and a list of words she planned to use someday in an argument ("*nevertheless*," "*actually*," and "*hypothetically*").

She found Wendell in the back hallway, kneeling by the pantry door with her tape measure stretched out along the floorboards like a silver tongue.

"You're measuring again," Amara said.

"I'm always measuring." Wendell didn't look up. "It's my whole personality."

"My mama says that about Papa and the grill."

"Your mama sounds wise." Wendell clicked the tape back. It retracted with a sound like a small animal being slurped into a straw, and Amara flinched before she could stop herself. Wendell noticed. Wendell noticed everything; that was the thing about her. She sat back on her heels and looked at Amara properly, the way grown-ups almost never did — no smile pasted on, no *and how are we today*, no talking down. Just looking, level, like Amara might be someone worth looking at.

"Okay," Wendell said. "You've got your serious notebook out. I can tell because of how you're holding it. Both hands, like a shield."

"It's not a shield. It's evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

Amara glanced up and down the hallway. The hallway was doing its daytime thing, which was being normal — sun on the floorboards, the smell of Mama's plantain frying somewhere far away, the coat hooks holding Papa's jacket and her raincoat and Bode's tiny hoodie. Normal. But hallways were only normal in the day. That was the deal they'd made. Or the deal somebody had made for them.

"Can I show you something?" Amara asked. "You can't laugh. And you can't do the face."

"What face?"

"The face Mama does. The *oh no, my daughter needs a therapist* face."

Wendell put her hand over her heart. "I promise on my tape measure, which is the most honest thing I own."

That was a weird thing to swear on, but Amara appreciated it. Grown-ups usually swore on God or their mothers, and those were big vague things. A tape measure was specific. You could trust a person who trusted a tape measure.

"Come on," Amara said. "But walk slow. It doesn't like it when you hurry."

* * *

The hallway ran from the kitchen to the stairs — forty-one of Amara's steps, which she knew because she'd counted them forty times, once for every night she couldn't sleep, which was most nights lately. Counting was what you did when you were scared. Her papa said counting was prayer for people who liked math, and Amara liked math a lot, so she figured she was extremely prayed-up.

"Forty-one steps," she told Wendell, walking them now, heel to toe. "I measured with my feet. Feet aren't exact, but they're exact enough if you always use the same feet."

"That's genuinely good methodology," Wendell said. "Most adults never figure that out."

"At night it's more."

Wendell stopped walking. "More what?"

"Steps. The hallway gets longer." Amara kept going, because if she stopped she might not start again. "Not all the time. Only after midnight, and only if you look at the far end. If you look at the near end, it's fine. But if you look toward the stairs, it keeps going. Like the house is breathing in. Forty-one steps becomes fifty. Once it was sixty-three. I counted the whole way and I cried a little, but quietly, because the tally man likes it when you cry. He writes it down."

She heard Wendell's breath change behind her. Not the gasp Amara had braced for. Something slower. Like someone setting down something heavy.

"He writes it down," Wendell repeated.

"In his book. He has a book. Well—" Amara turned around. "He *is* kind of a book? That's the part I can't explain right. His skin has lines on it. Little marks. Like—" she flipped open her notebook to the page, the one she'd drawn at 2 a.m. last week with the hallway light leaking under her door, "—like this."

She held it out.

Wendell took the notebook with both hands, careful, the way you hold a baby bird or a live grenade. On the page was the tally man: very tall, very thin, a head like an egg that had been drawn from memory by someone trying not to look directly at it. His arms hung too long. And across his chest, his shoulders, his cheeks, his knuckles — rows and rows of small strokes. Four vertical lines and a slash through them. Four and a slash. Four and a slash. Groups of five marching over him like he'd been wrapped in counting.

Nobody spoke for a second. Down the hall, the fryer popped. Somewhere outside, Bode screamed happily at a sprinkler. The house went on being a house.

"Amara," Wendell said quietly. "When does he visit you?"

"At night. In dreams, mostly, but it's not really dreaming, because I can get up and walk around in it. He stands at the end of the hall where it's longer, and he asks me to hold still."

"Hold still."

"That's all he says. *Hold still.* Not mean. Not scary-mean. Like a photographer." Amara hugged the notebook back against her chest. "That's the worst part, actually. If he yelled, I could yell back. But he's just *patient*. Like he already knows how it ends and he's being polite about it. He says I'm on the list. He says everybody who sleeps here is on the list. He said—" and here her voice did the wobble she hated, the nine-year-old wobble, and she pushed through it because this was Important and Important Things deserved steady delivery, "—he said the count is due. He said it like it was a library book."

Wendell crouched down so they were eye to eye. Up close, Amara could see she hadn't been sleeping either. There were shadows under her eyes shaped like little moons.

"How long have you been seeing him?" Wendell asked.

"Since we moved in. Eleven months." Amara frowned. "But it got realer in the last few weeks. Before, he was more like a feeling. Now he's got a smell and everything. Pencil shavings. Do you smell it too?"

For a moment Wendell didn't answer, and Amara watched her decide something. Watched it happen behind her eyes like weather changing.

"Yes," Wendell said. "I smell it. And Amara — I need to tell you something, and I need you to be brave, and I think you are, because you walked me down this hallway carrying evidence instead of running to your mom, which is more courage than I've seen from most adults this month."

"I'm brave," Amara agreed. "Mama says stubborn, but it's the same thing with better marketing."

A surprised laugh came out of Wendell — short and cracked, like it had escaped rather than been released. "Okay. Here's the truth. When I was six years old, I lived in this house."

Amara blinked. "You did not."

"I did. Right down this hallway. And there was a friend here — well. I *thought* he was imaginary. A boy in a yellow raincoat named Pim. And I haven't thought about him in thirty years, and three days ago he was sitting on the porch waiting for me, exactly the same, still seven years old, still wearing the coat." Wendell's voice stayed level, but her hand had found the tape measure on her belt and was gripping it like a rosary. "And he told me I was late for the count."

The word went through Amara like a bell through water.

"*Count,*" she whispered.

"So when you say the tally man asks you to hold still — I believe you. Completely. Because I think I know what he's counting toward, and I think it's soon, and I think—" Wendell stopped. Started again, gentler. "I think you and I are the only two people in this house who can see it. Your parents can't. They sleep right through it. Which is safer for them, honestly, but it means it's you and me, kid. It's you and me against the arithmetic."

Amara considered this. She was nine, but she was not stupid, and she understood alliances. She'd read enough books. In books, when two people were the only ones who could see the monster, they became a team, and teams had rules.

"Rules," she said aloud. "If we're a team, we need rules. One: no lying to each other, even nice lies. Two: if either of us sees him, we say it out loud immediately, even if it's embarrassing. Three—" she pointed at Wendell's belt, "—you teach me to use the tape measure, because I want to measure the hallway myself, with real numbers, and write them down, because numbers don't get scared even when people do."

Wendell stared at her. Then she unclipped the tape measure and held it out flat on her palm like a knight offering a sword.

"Numbers don't get scared," she repeated softly. "God, I wish I'd met you thirty years ago."

They shook on it, Amara's small hand around the cold metal case, and it felt official. It felt like the beginning of a chapter.

"One more rule," Amara added. "Rule four. Whatever the plan ends up being — and I know there's a plan, because you've got plan-face, it's like constipated but sadder — you don't leave me out of it to protect me. Adults do that. It never works. In every book, the kid who gets left behind is the one who figures out the ending alone and has to do something drastic."

"Noted," Wendell said gravely. "No leaving you out. Although I reserve the right to insist you stay in bed after midnight, because that's not protection, that's just bedtime."

"That's negotiable."

"It is not."

"It's *slightly* negotiable."

* * *

They spent the rest of the afternoon measuring.

Wendell showed her how to hook the tape on the baseboard and walk it backward without kinking it, how to read the little half-marks, how to write measurements the proper way with the foot mark and the inch mark. Amara wrote everything in her notebook in her best handwriting, and the numbers behaved themselves beautifully. Numbers always did. The hallway was twenty-six feet, four inches, and did not care one bit about being haunted.

But when the sun started going down and the light in the hallway turned from gold to gray, Amara felt it happen — the shift, the pressure change, the house inhaling its first slow breath of evening. She saw Wendell feel it too. Saw her go still, head tilted, listening to something below the frequency of sound.

"It's starting," Amara said.

"Yeah." Wendell clipped the tape measure back onto her belt, then seemed to reconsider and handed it back to Amara. "Keep it tonight. Team equipment."

Amara clutched it to her chest. It was the best gift anyone had ever given her, including the Christmas she got the microscope.

"Wendell?" she said, as the measuring woman gathered her clipboard and headed for the porch, where — Amara noticed, and decided not to mention yet, filing it under Evidence — a small yellow shape was sitting on the rail like it had been sitting there for hours. "The tally man came early last night. Before midnight. He's never done that before."

Wendell stopped in the doorway. The gray light made her look older and younger at the same time.

"What did he say?"

Amara made herself say it. Rule two: say it out loud, even if it's embarrassing. Even if saying it makes it realer.

"He said, *Tell the surveyor she has eight days left.*" Amara swallowed. "And then he smiled. He's never smiled before. It had too many teeth in it, and they were all little tally marks."

Down the hall, in the kitchen, Mama called that dinner was ready, warm and ordinary and impossibly far away. Out on the porch rail, the yellow raincoat shifted, and a small voice that only Amara and Wendell could hear said:

*"Eight days. Maybe fewer. She's counting faster than I am."*
