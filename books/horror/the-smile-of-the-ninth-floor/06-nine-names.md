## 6. Nine Names

The parish archive of Hollowmere occupied what had once been the vestry of St. Aldhelm's-in-the-Marsh, a church that had lost its marsh, its congregation, and most of its roof in roughly that order. The archive itself lived in a prefabricated shed bolted to the church's north wall, and it was administered by a woman named Mrs. Tallow who greeted me with the particular warmth reserved for strangers who might die soon.

"Research," I said, and set my tape recorder on her desk before I could stop myself. It had become a tic. Lights on, doors counted, tape running. A folklorist's rosary.

"Local history?" she asked, with the flat intonation of someone who already knew the answer and disliked it.

"Folk songs. Harvest customs. The usual."

"The usual." She said it the way you'd say *the plague*. But she unlocked the cabinet anyway, because Mrs. Tallow was, above all things, a woman who believed in procedure, and procedure said that anyone who signed the visitors' book got twenty minutes alone with the parish registers. She gave me forty. I suspect she wanted to see what I'd do.

The registers were bound in cracked leather the color of old tea, and they smelled the way all archives smell: dust, glue, and the faint vanilla of decaying paper, which chemists tell me is literally the books digesting themselves. I have always found the smell comforting. That morning it smelled faintly of something else too — pond water, maybe, or the green rot at the bottom of a well — but archives are damp places, and I told myself it was the building.

I told myself a great many things that week.

* * *

Here is what I was looking for, though I didn't fully know it yet: provenance. Every song has a genealogy. You can trace a ballad the way you trace a bloodline — through marriages of melody, through bastard verses, through the small mutations that tell you where a song has been and who it passed through on the way. The harvest lullaby Mrs. Ellery had sung for me — the one she would not sing twice — had a structure I couldn't place. Nine lines. Most lullabies run four or eight; nine is an awkward number, a number with a limp. And the ninth line repeated the first note of the first line, like a door opening back onto itself.

I wanted to know when Hollowmere's singers had learned it. I wanted dates. Dates are how I keep the dark at arm's length; a thing with a date is a specimen, and a specimen cannot hurt you.

So: registers. Baptisms, marriages, burials. The three acts of a village life, recorded in ink by men whose handwriting deteriorated in direct proportion to their job satisfaction.

1974 should have been between a flourishing page of 1973 baptisms and a 1975 marriage conducted, judging by the marginalia, during a hailstorm. Between them: nothing. Not a blank page. A *missing* one. The stub of it remained in the binding, torn out close to the spine, and the tear was old — the exposed paper had yellowed to match its neighbors, which meant it hadn't been ripped out recently. Someone had done it decades ago and done it carefully, the way you'd remove a tooth.

I sat with my finger on the stub for a long moment, feeling the investigator's thrill come up in me like heat. This is the part of the work I have never been able to explain to civilians — the joy of it. The torn page isn't a disappointment. The torn page is a *door*. Nobody tears out a page of baptisms unless there is something on it worth tearing out, and whatever is worth hiding is worth finding, and whatever is worth finding will change the shape of your whole understanding. I actually smiled. I remember smiling, alone in a cold shed, touching the wound in a book, and being happy about it.

That should probably have worried me more than it did.

* * *

Pencil rubbings are frowned upon in proper archives, and I want it noted that I hesitated for nearly four seconds before taking the soft 2B from my coat pocket. Mrs. Tallow had gone to make tea, or to telephone someone, or both — those are not mutually exclusive activities in villages — and the shed was mine.

The trouble with a torn-out page is that paper remembers. Ink presses through. Whatever had been written on the missing leaf had left its ghost in the fibers of the pages on either side, raised in relief like braille, invisible to the eye but perfectly legible to a pencil laid sideways over a sheet of tracing paper and stroked gently, gently, until the pressure of a dead man's penmanship rose up out of the page.

I worked from the 1975 side first. Ghosts came up grey and reversed, mirror-writing, and I turned the sheet over to read them properly:

*Marr. banns — HOLLIS / PRYNN — 3rd reading.*

Fine. Ordinary. Then the 1973 side, and here the rubbing thickened, because the missing page had been written on heavily, and the pressure had carried deep:

Nine entries. I counted them twice, then a third time, because the number kept insisting on itself. Nine baptismal records, or fragments of them — the rubbing caught names better than dates, dates better than godparents, so what I recovered was less a census than a litany:

**Hollis. Prynne. Vane. Cottle. Marsh (of the Marshes branch). Ferrier. Odell. Sopwith. Bracken.**

Nine family names. Nine children baptized in a year whose record had been surgically removed. And beneath the ninth name, pressed so hard the pencil picked up every groove, a tenth entry — not a baptism. The letterforms were different, smaller, hurried, as if written late at night by someone who wanted it done quickly:

*Odile —*

And then the smudge. The rest of the line had been rubbed away — not torn, *rubbed*, erased with a thumb or a cloth while the ink was wet, so that only the pressure remained and even the pressure was broken. *Odile* and then a gap where a surname should be, and after the gap two words the rubbing rendered imperfectly but unmistakably:

*— taken under.*

Taken under. I wrote the phrase down in my notebook and stared at it. Taken under could mean adoption. Taken under could mean taken under wing, under care, under the protection of the parish. Taken under could mean a great many benign things if you were the sort of person who needed it to.

It could also mean what happens to a stone when you drop it in water.

I sat back. The shed was very quiet. Somewhere outside a rook was making its one opinion known, over and over, the way rooks do, and I noticed — this is the detail I keep returning to — I noticed that the sound arrived a half-beat after the rook's beak opened. Just slightly. Just enough that if you weren't the kind of person who counted things, you'd never catch it.

I decided it was distance. Sound travels slowly; the churchyard is wide. There is always a rational explanation available if you reach for it fast enough, and reaching for them fast enough is a skill I had honed over thirty years of professional haunting-adjacency.

* * *

Mrs. Tallow came back with tea I didn't drink and stood over me while I packed up, and I made the mistake of asking casually, the way you ask about weather:

"I noticed the 1974 register's incomplete. A page seems to have been removed. Any idea why?"

The teacup in her hands did not tremble, because Mrs. Tallow was not a woman given to trembling. But she stopped moving entirely, the way animals do, and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *inventory*. She was deciding something about me. Weighing me against some internal standard.

"You'll want to talk to Mr. Grieve," she said finally. "Retired schoolmaster. He taught here since before the war. If anyone remembers anything about — " and here she performed a small, precise pause, a comma inserted into the sentence like a pin — "about local matters, it's him. Number four, Chandler's Row. Tell him Tallow sent you." She set the tea down. "Though I'd think carefully about whether you need to know anything at all, Mr. Winterbourne. Songs are songs. A man can write a very good book without knowing why people sing what they sing."

"That's rather the opposite of scholarship," I said lightly.

"Yes," she said. "It is rather the opposite of surviving, too. Some things are kept in the ground because the ground holds them better than we do." Then she smiled, briskly, and became again the archivist, and showed me out, and locked the cabinet behind me with a key she took off the ring and put in her pocket, separately, as if the cabinet might otherwise follow me home.

* * *

Mr. Grieve's cottage on Chandler's Row was the neatest house I had seen in Hollowmere, which should have told me something. Everything in it was squared to the edges of everything else. His garden fence ran true. His roses were pruned with what I can only assume was a protractor. Retired schoolmasters fall into two types — the dissolving and the fortified — and Grieve had fortified.

He was perhaps eighty, dry as a chalk stick, with the eyes of a man who had spent forty years detecting contraband sweets at forty paces. He listened to my introduction — folklorist, songs, harvest customs, Mrs. Tallow sent me — with his hands folded, and when I finished he was quiet long enough that I filled the silence, which was, I understood later, exactly the technique he'd used on generations of ten-year-olds.

"You've been to the archive," he said.

"This morning."

"And you found the gap."

I admitted I had. I told him about the rubbing, too, because I wanted to watch his face, and I did watch it, and what crossed it was not surprise. It was grief — old, worn smooth, handled so often it had gone the texture of a stair rail. He looked at me the way you look at someone carrying a parcel you know contains bad news.

"Sit down," he said. "Tea?"

"No, thank you."

"Good. Don't accept things here. Small rule." He lowered himself into an armchair with the economy of a man conserving himself for something. "You rubbed the page. So you have nine names and a smudge. Let me save us both an afternoon. Nineteen seventy-four was called the wet year, though it didn't rain any more than other years. That's just what we named it afterward, the way you name a street after a battle. Things were wet that shouldn't have been. Wells. Cellars. Dreams, some said, though dreams aren't my department. And at the end of it a child went into the well behind your house — yes, I know which house you're in, everyone knows which house you're in, the whole village watched you carry your boxes in and remarked upon it, though not within your hearing — and the child did not come out, and the page was removed, and the harvest that autumn was the finest anyone could remember, and nine families have eaten well ever since."

His voice never rose. It was the flattest horror I have ever heard delivered, delivered like a timetable.

"You're telling me they drowned her," I said. "The nine families."

"I'm telling you what I told the police vicar in 1974, who thanked me, closed his notebook, and transferred to Norfolk by spring. I'm an old man, Mr. Winterbourne. I have outlived my usefulness, my wife, and my fear of most things, but not my fear of the ones that matter." He leaned forward. "Now I'm going to give you some free advice, which as a former teacher I am constitutionally unable to withhold. Stop asking about the wet year. Take your tapes and your nice book contract and go somewhere with a normal haunted bridge or a screaming skull, the kind that only wants attention. What's in Hollowmere doesn't want attention. It wants *catching up*. Do you understand the difference? A skull raps on walls. This waits behind you, patiently, for you to arrive where it already is."

I confess I laughed. It was nerves dressed as skepticism, and he recognized it instantly, because he had heard forty years of boys laughing in exactly that register.

"You think I'm being picturesque," he said. "Folklorists love a picturesque warning. It goes in chapter one." He rose, stiffly, and walked me to his door with its paint so unchipped it looked wet. On the doorstep he stopped me with one hand, light as a moth landing.

"One more thing, since you'll ignore everything else. The child's name was Odile. Not Odile Hollis or Odile Prynne or any of the nine. She was nobody's. That's why they chose her, if you want the truth of it — a foundling, a foster-child passed hand to hand, belonging to no family that could make trouble. Nine names on the page and hers smudged, because the nine were written in ink and she was written in water." His grip tightened, briefly, surprisingly strong. "But here's what your rubbing won't show you, boy. Everyone assumes the entry says *taken under* — under the ground, under the water, whatever grim thing you're imagining. Read it again when you get home. Look at the pressure marks. The full phrase, the way it was written, the way I saw it written before the page came out — "

He let go of my sleeve.

" — was *taken under the ninth line*. As in a song. As in a verse. Whoever wrote that entry believed the child was still *in* something. Still singing something. From underneath."

He closed his door. Politely. Squared to the frame.

* * *

I walked home through lanes going blue with dusk, and I did what I always do with a day's findings: I narrated them into the recorder, walking, my breath making little clouds that the microphone loved. Nine names. One smudge. The wet year. *Taken under the ninth line.* My voice sounded cheerful, professional, the voice of a man assembling a monograph. Underneath it, the rooks were settling in the elms, and I noticed again — and this time I did not decide it was distance — that each caw arrived after the bird had finished making it, as though the sound had to travel farther than the space between us, as though the whole village were standing slightly further away than it appeared.

At the house, I counted the doors. Seven. I counted them again, because the number felt wrong in my mouth. Seven. Again. Seven, seven, seven, obedient, and yet the previous tenant's notes in the kitchen drawer had ended, I now remembered, with the figure nine, underlined twice, followed by a single word in a shaking hand: *stop*.

Inside, I put the kettle on, laid my papers out, and set the rubbing flat under the lamp. And there it was, where Grieve had promised: the pressure marks continuing past the smudge, past where the ink had been wiped, faint grooves in the paper like the wake behind a swum-under thing. I angled the lamp low, the way you do with coin rubbings, and the shadows in the grooves resolved into letters, and the letters resolved into the phrase, and the phrase was exactly what the old man said it was.

*Taken under the ninth line.*

I sat looking at it for a long time. Nine families. Nine names. A lullaby with nine lines whose last line opens back onto the first, like a door. A song that must never be sung twice, because —

Because a song sung twice belongs to whoever hears it second. Mrs. Ellery's white face. Her hands flat on the table.

And beneath the ninth line, holding the whole song up from underneath, the way a drowned girl holds up a harvest: a tenth voice. Smudged. Erased. Waiting, with a patience I was only beginning to comprehend, for someone to sing the verse that would let her finish the tune.

The lamp flickered. In the hall, the bathroom tap dripped once — I heard it clearly, distinctly, the small bright *tink* of water on enamel.

Then, one second later, it dripped again.
