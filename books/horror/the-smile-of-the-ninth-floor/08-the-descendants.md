## 8. The Descendants

There is a particular kind of politeness that functions as a wall. You've felt it if you've ever been a stranger in a small place — the tea offered before you've finished asking for it, the chair pulled out at exactly the wrong angle so that sitting feels like an imposition, the smile calibrated to the millimetre: warm enough to be deniable as warmth, cool enough to be deniable as anything else. I had spent fifteen years collecting that smile in villages across three countries. I thought I knew its full vocabulary.

Hollowmere's nine families spoke it fluently. What unsettled me was not the wall itself but how identical the walls were, house to house, as if they'd all been built from the same mould, or as if behind each of them stood the same person wearing different cardigans.

I made a list. Of course I made a list; lists are how I keep the world from sloshing over the rim. Nine names from the pencil rubbing, nine households scattered along the valley road like beads on a string, and between each bead a distance I could walk in twenty minutes with my recorder in one pocket and my questions in the other.

The tape from the lullaby session still hummed under everything. I'd taken to playing it while I walked, headphones on, listening for the child's voice beneath Mrs. Aldery's cracked soprano. Some mornings I convinced myself I'd imagined it. Other mornings the hum was so clear I could almost catch the shape of words. It did not sound like a haunting. That was the terrible part. It sounded like a girl singing along because she knew the song better than anyone, because it was hers, and she was patient about getting it back.

Patience, I would learn, ran in the valley like water in chalk.

* * *

The first house belonged to the Merrivales, and I will describe it at length only once, because after it the others become a litany, and litany is precisely what they intended.

The Merrivale farmhouse sat back from the road behind a ha-ha and two sculpted hawthorns, which in a village where half the gardens grew cabbages was less landscaping than a statement of doctrine. The door was answered by a woman in her sixties with the unlined competence of someone who has never once been caught unprepared. She knew my name before I gave it. She knew why I'd come before I said it. She invited me in anyway, which told me she wanted something from the encounter, even if it was only the pleasure of watching me fail.

"Harvest songs," she repeated, pouring tea I hadn't asked for into cups that matched nothing else in the room — good cups, old ones, kept for occasions. "We're Church of England here, Mr. Winterbourne. Hymns at the proper times. I'm sure there are folk up the moor who still do all that."

"Mrs. Aldery sang me one," I said. "A lullaby. About the barley bowing."

Something happened to her face then. Not much. A tightening around the mouth, the way a drain tightens before it takes the water down. "Edith Aldery always did have notions."

"She wouldn't sing it twice. Do you know why?"

"I expect she forgot the verses. At her age." The cup she set before me rang against its saucer, one clean note, and I noticed her hand was perfectly steady and her eyes were fixed on a point slightly left of my head, as though the interesting version of this conversation were happening to someone standing just behind me.

On the mantelpiece, among photographs of grandchildren and prize marrows, hung a small oval locket on a stand, silver gone dark with handling. I looked at it perhaps two seconds too long.

"That's family," she said, before I'd asked anything. "Family things stay family."

"Of course," I said, and smiled my own calibrated smile, and drank the tea, which was excellent, because of course it was. Prosperity has a taste. It tastes like things being exactly right in every particular except one.

At the door she shook my hand and held it a moment longer than courtesy required. "You seem a reasonable man," she said. "Reasonable men find plenty to write about in Hollowmere without troubling the past. The past keeps poorly here. Damp gets into everything."

* * *

The second household was the Corrigans, third on my list but first I could reach on foot before the light went. A younger man, forty maybe, with a tractor dealership in his manner and a golf trophy on the shelf. He didn't offer tea. He offered a beer, and we sat in a kitchen so clean it hummed, and he talked about football for eleven minutes by my watch before I could land a single question.

"Nineteen seventy-four?" he said, when I finally got there. "Before my time, mate. Bad year, everyone says. Wet."

"The wet year. Yes. There was a child involved, I think."

He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the counter. Turned it back. "Kids drowned all over in those days. Wells, ponds, canals. Nobody fenced anything off. Terrible era for it." He said it smoothly, the way you recite a fact you've rehearsed until it stopped feeling like a lie and started feeling like furniture. And then, because he was younger than Mrs. Merrivale and less disciplined, he added: "Why do people keep asking? Edith Aldery last month, now you. Nobody asked for thirty years and now it's a fashion."

"Who else asked?"

"Doesn't matter." But his eyes flicked toward the window, toward the village, and I understood that it mattered enormously, that somewhere in Hollowmere a question had already been circulating, and that I was not its origin. Someone had come before me. Or something had.

On the windowsill above the sink, weighted down by a ceramic frog, lay a length of ribbon. Green silk, faded almost to grey, knotted in a way that looked deliberate and old, the knot of someone who tied things to keep them safe rather than pretty. He saw me see it. He picked up his beer and drank with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"Nice talking to you," he said.

* * *

I want to be honest about what those visits were doing to me, because honesty is the only thing I brought to Hollowmere that the place didn't already own.

By the fourth house I had stopped taking notes openly. By the fifth I had started lying about who I was — not wholesale, just shading it, "doing some research for the county archive," which had the advantage of sounding bureaucratic enough to bore people into compliance. I told myself this was technique. It was not technique. It was anger learning to dress itself.

Because here is what curdled me: none of them were cruel. That was the thing I hadn't prepared for. Cruelty I know how to catalogue; I've sat across from men who did unforgivable things and found the human machinery underneath, and cataloguing is a kind of mercy, in its way. But these nine households were not cruel. They were *comfortable*. They were prosperous in a valley where prosperity should have been impossible — the fields green while the moor browned, the roofs new-slated while the chapel leaked, the cars recent, the freezers full. And over every mantel, in every drawer, tucked into every Bible and biscuit tin, sat their little piece of the dead girl: a locket, a ribbon, a shoe, a hairbrush with three strands still in it, a christening gown folded so many years the creases had become permanent geography.

They kept them the way you keep a deed. Not as mementoes. As title.

And when I asked about 1974 — the wet year, the year the register lost a page — they performed the same play in nine different kitchens. Denial first, smooth as varnish. Then a flicker, a quarter-turn of a glass, a glance at a locked drawer. Then the pivot to hospitality, the tea, the beer, the biscuits arranged on a plate, as if kindness could be issued retroactively, as if a plate of custard creams could be entered into evidence on behalf of the living against a child at the bottom of a well.

I began to hate them. I want to record that clearly, because later events will make it look either noble or convenient, depending on who tells it, and I would rather the record show that my hatred arrived early, undignified, and personal. They had done something monstrous and then built lives on top of it the way you build a patio over bad ground, and the ground had held, and the roses came up lovely every June, and they had learned to call the smell of what was underneath simply *the country air*.

* * *

The seventh house broke the pattern, and I should have paid more attention to how.

It belonged to a widow named Mrs. Tarnow, the eldest of the nine lines, ninety-one years old, in a cottage that had let prosperity pass it by entirely — damp in the corners, a fire that smoked, cats in numbers I declined to count. She was deaf in one ear and pretended deafness in the other, selectively, and she received me the way a fortress receives a delegation: through a slit, with interest.

"You're the one asking about the song," she said, before I'd opened my mouth. Word travels fast in Hollowmere when it wants to. "Edith's song. The one you mustn't ask anyone to sing twice."

"You know the rule."

"I invented knowing it, dear. I was there when it was made." She laughed, a dry rustling sound, and poured tea from a pot that had seen more history than the parish register. On the table between us, under a glass dome meant for cakes, sat a single child's shoe. Leather, small, one lace missing. Kept dust-free. Kept *displayed*, which was somehow worse than hidden — an object of pride, or of vigil, I couldn't decide which frightened me more.

"Whose shoe?" I asked, though I knew.

"It was Odile's," she said, and the name landed in the room like a stone dropped down a shaft, and I heard it fall a long way before anything answered.

I kept very still. In my experience, the moment after an unexpected name is the most fragile material in folkloric fieldwork; breathe on it wrong and the whole structure closes.

"You say that name easily," I managed.

"I'm ninety-one. Names stop frightening you when you've buried everyone they belonged to." She looked at the shoe under its dome the way you look at a photograph of your own funeral. "The others won't tell you anything. Good families, careful families. They'll give you tea and talk about the weather until you go away, and then they'll ring each other, and tomorrow every door in the valley will be a little harder to open. So I'll save us both the afternoon." She leaned forward. Her good ear aimed at me like a dish antenna. "Ask me what you actually want to ask."

"What happened in 1974?"

"Water happened," she said. "Water and need and nine people who believed the harvest was a negotiation." She sat back. "But you don't want the story yet. You think you do. You want the story the way you want the song — once, cleanly, filed away. It doesn't work like that here. Nothing works once here. Ask me again when you understand what you're carrying in that machine." She tapped the recorder in my pocket without touching it, and her finger stopped a centimetre short, the way you'd stop short of a sleeping animal.

"Mrs. Tarnow—"

"Go home, Mr. Winterbourne. No — not home. Go to the well. Look at it properly. Count what's coming up it." She smiled, and for the first time in seven houses I saw grief instead of varnish. "And when you come back, bring the question you're afraid of. I haven't got time left for the polite ones."

* * *

I went to the well that evening, because I am obedient to exactly one authority, and it is the voice that frightens me.

The drought had tightened its grip on the valley. The stream behind the church was a scar of grey stones. Farmers were hauling tankers. The moor had gone the colour of old paper. And behind my rented house, in its circle of stones older than any parish record, the well stood brimming — brimming in August, brimming in drought, water so close to the lip that when I leaned over I could see my face in it, whole and sharp and *present*, which is more than I could say for my reflection in any mirror in that house anymore.

I dropped a pebble in. I counted.

The splash took too long to arrive. Long enough that I had time to notice the silence between, long enough to feel the well listening to the space where the sound should have been. One second, perhaps. Perhaps a little more. Everything in Hollowmere was running one second late these days, and I was beginning to suspect that the delay wasn't the ghost's limitation.

It was her patience. She was letting the present finish speaking before she answered it.

Nine houses. Nine heirlooms. One name spoken aloud in ninety-one years, and only by the one woman too old to care what the others thought. Somewhere in those polished kitchens, behind those locked drawers, the pieces of a stolen song sat waiting to be gathered, and every owner of every piece had looked at me with the same expression — not guilt, not quite, but the vigilant blankness of people guarding a well of their own.

That night, back in the house, I played the lullaby tape one more time, and beneath Mrs. Aldery's voice the humming rose to meet me, clearer than it had ever been, and for the first time I could swear the child wasn't singing along.

She was teaching.
