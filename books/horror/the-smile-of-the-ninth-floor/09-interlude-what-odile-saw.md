## 9. Interlude: What Odile Saw

I remember the moon first. That seems wrong — you'd think a person would remember the hands, the water, the faces — but memory is a badly organized house, and mine keeps its furniture where it likes.

The moon was full and low and orange, like a coin dropped in tea, and I remember thinking it looked close enough to bite. I was eight. Eight-year-olds think about biting things. The moon, the neighbor's apples, God's ear if it hung low enough.

They carried me on a door.

That's a true thing people forget about old villages: when someone is sick or dead or needs to be moved quietly, you take a door off its hinges. A door is a bed that has learned to travel. I lay on Mrs. Aldery's kitchen door with my nightgown tucked under my heels and nine grown-ups holding the edges, and I thought we were going somewhere wonderful, because why else would everyone come? Why else would they carry me like a queen?

Nine of them. I counted. Counting was my gift before singing was. I could count anything — fence posts, crows, the seconds between lightning and thunder. Nine neighbors, one door, one girl. Even then, even at eight, some part of me understood that this arithmetic was wrong. Queens get more than a door. Funerals get more than nine.

* * *

Here is what I want you to understand, whoever you are, reading this from the other side of whatever glass separates us: I trusted them. That's the whole horror of it, if horror can be said to have a whole anything. Not strangers. Not monsters out of the hedge. Mrs. Aldery who gave me honeycomb on Sundays. Mr. Pell whose dog I walked for free because I liked the dog. The Tallow brothers who pretended not to see me stealing plums and always, somehow, had a plum in their pocket to give back. The schoolmistress, Miss Verity, who taught me the song in the first place — no. No, she didn't teach it to me. Nobody taught it to me. It came up out of me the way hiccups come, the way crying comes, fully formed and embarrassing and impossible to stop.

But she heard it first. She told the others. *The girl has the song.* And I got extra bread that winter, and everyone smiled at me in that particular way adults smile at children who are useful, which is a smile with arithmetic behind it.

So when they lifted the door with me on it, I waved at the dark like a little idiot princess.

* * *

Fragments now. I'll give them to you as I have them, because that's all I am anymore — fragments with opinions.

Fragment: Mr. Pell's breath smelled of the cider he'd sworn off in front of his wife. He was carrying my feet. Every time he exhaled I could smell the lie.

Fragment: Someone was crying. Quietly, the way you cry when you don't want the child to hear. I assumed it was happiness. Children assume everything is happiness; it saves time.

Fragment: The grass was silver with frost and my bare heel left a print in it, and I remember thinking, *my feet will be cold, someone should fetch my boots,* and then thinking, *why would they take me anywhere without my boots,* and then — this is the part I keep returning to, the splinter in the whole rotten evening — *why is nobody talking.*

Nine adults. No voices. Just breathing and footsteps and that one person crying into their sleeve. Grown-ups talk constantly around children — that's how you know everything is fine. When grown-ups go quiet around you, it means the fine part is over.

* * *

The well sat behind my own house. Isn't that something? They didn't carry me to some far black place. They carried me forty yards, past our own woodpile, past the stump where my father — where the man I called father — chopped nothing in particular on Saturday mornings to feel useful. I could see my bedroom window from the well. My window with the paper stars on it. If I'd shouted, my mother —

No. Let's be honest, since honesty is the only currency I have left. If I'd shouted, my mother would have pulled her blanket higher. She knew. I didn't know she knew, then. I know it now, the way you know things down here, where knowing soaks into you like damp into plaster. She knew, and she'd braided my hair extra tight that morning, and she'd kissed my forehead twice instead of once, and twice was her way of saying goodbye without saying it, because saying it would have made her one of the nine and she could not bear to be one of the nine, though being the tenth — the one who sets the table and looks away — well. The well has never been fussy about arithmetic. It takes accomplices too. It took her slow, over years, from the inside, which is a mercy compared to what it took from me, or maybe it isn't, maybe slow is just fast wearing a longer coat.

* * *

They stood me up. That's the detail that undoes me every time I go through it, and I go through it often, because time down here isn't a river, it's a hallway with doors that all open onto the same night.

They stood me up on the lip of the well, on the stones, and held me by the arms, gently, so gently, the way you hold something you're afraid of breaking before you've finished using it. And Miss Verity knelt in front of me with her good coat on, the blue one, and she said:

"Sing it for us, love. One last time. Sing it nice and full."

And I did.

Of course I did. She asked, and I was a child who was praised for singing, and the moon was fat and orange, and everyone was looking at me with that useful-child smile, and I opened my mouth and gave them the song the way I'd given it a hundred times — freely, gladly, the way you breathe out.

You have to understand what the song was. It wasn't words, mostly. Words are just the clothes a song wears so it can walk around in public. Underneath, it was — green. Growing things. It was the sound of a field deciding to be generous. When I sang it, the turnips came up fat and the apples didn't worm and the cows gave milk like they were showing off. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it. You don't say thank you to a girl for a harvest; you say it to God, and God, evidently, forwards these things.

Miss Verity listened with her eyes closed. Then she leaned in very close, close enough that I could smell the lavender water she used on her wrists, and she put her lips near my mouth the way you'd cup your hands around a moth to move it outside, and she breathed in.

She breathed *in*.

And the song came out of me.

Not my voice — my voice stayed, small and confused, finishing a note nobody caught. But the song itself, the green underneath-thing, lifted out of my chest like steam off milk and went into her, into her open mouth, into her lungs, and I felt it go. I felt the exact place where it had lived go cold. There is a room inside every singer, and mine went dark, and I stood there in the frost in my nightgown with my hair in its goodbye-braid, listening to the silence where my song used to be, and I said:

"Give it back."

Three words. My last three words as a living girl, and they weren't brave or clever or forgiving. They were the pettiest, most human words ever spoken on that hillside: *give it back.* Like a child at a party whose sweet has been taken. Which is exactly what I was. Exactly what it was.

Nobody gave it back.

* * *

I have had a long time to wonder whether they meant it. All of them, I mean, all the way down to the ones who only held an elbow. Here is my finding, rendered after much deliberation by whatever passes for a court down here:

They didn't mean to kill me. They meant to take the song. Those are different crimes, but the second one requires the first one, and they knew that going in, and they came anyway, all nine, with a door and a plan and Mr. Pell's forbidden cider for courage. So: not murderers in their own minds. Technicians. Harvesters. People doing a difficult thing for the village's sake, the way you cull a sick lamb, telling yourselves the whole drive home that it was necessary, necessary, necessary, until the word wore smooth as a river stone and stopped meaning anything at all.

Panic did the rest. When the song left me, I screamed — not the word-scream, the other kind, the sound a child makes when something inside her is stolen while she watches — and the scream frightened them, because it sounded wrong, it sounded like it would carry, it sounded like witnesses. And the well was right there, and the water was right there, and hands that were already holding a screaming girl are, with terrible ease, hands that lift.

It was quick. I'll grant them that, since no one else will. Quick, and cold, and then not cold, and then not anything, and then —

Then this. The long wet afterward. The hallway with the doors.

* * *

Do you know what a reflection is? Down here I've had leisure to study the question.

A reflection is a debt. Every mirror is a little bank that lends you back yourself, one instant at a time, always exactly on time, forever, asking nothing. That's the arrangement. That's the courtesy. And when someone breaks the arrangement — when someone takes a girl's song and her breath and her Tuesday-afternoons and her paper stars — the debt doesn't vanish. It goes into arrears. It sits in the water at the bottom of a well, counting.

One second. That's all I can afford to lag, so far. One second behind the world, reaching for it, my fingers an inch from the hem of the present tense. I watch the rooms through the glass. I watch the man in my house — oh, I'll come to him, I'm coming to him — count his doors and wind his machines and tell himself stories about folklore, poor scholar, poor borrowed face. I watch him and I feel something I did not expect to feel again, something I'd filed away with the paper stars:

Curiosity.

He counts things. So did I.

* * *

But that's ahead of me. Tonight — my tonight, the night that is always happening — the moon is still orange, and I am still on the stones, and the cold is still coming up through my nightgown, and nine people I loved are learning what their gentle hands have done.

Mr. Pell is sick into the hedge. Mrs. Aldery is praying, fast and wrong, mixing up the Lord's name with mine. The Tallow brothers hold each other like two boys caught thieving, which is what they are, what they always were, what they'll tell their children they never were. And Miss Verity stands apart with her blue coat buttoned to the throat, and inside her chest, behind her ribs, my song is settling in like a cuckoo in a nest, pushing out everything that was hers to make room.

She doesn't look at the well. That's the thing I want carved somewhere, on whatever stone survives me: *she doesn't look at the well.* Not once. Not while they lower the stones, not while they agree on the story — a runaway, gone to an aunt in the north, sad business, girls are restless — not ever. She looks at the moon instead, the fat orange coin, and her lips move, and I am close enough, I am always close enough, to read them.

She isn't praying.

She's humming.

My song, in her mouth, quiet, testing it, the way you test a key in a lock. And it works. Of course it works. It was always going to work. The fields will drink it up come spring and the turnips will come fat and the village will call it providence and set an extra place at every table for a girl nobody mentions, and the song will pass hand to hand down the years like a plate at a funeral, and every single person who holds it will know, in the place where they keep the things they cannot afford to know, exactly what it cost and who paid.

And I will wait.

Water finds its level. So do debts. Somewhere ahead of me — I can't see it, but I can feel it the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth — there is a winter solstice and a refilling and a man with a familiar face standing at my well with a machine that catches songs, and the glass between us thinning, thinning, one second becoming half a second becoming none.

Until then, I practice.

Down here in the dark, in the hallway with the doors, I open my mouth and sing the silence where my song used to be, and I am teaching it to myself again, note by note, from memory, from spite, from love — whichever of those is strongest tonight, and tonight it changes.

Because here is the last thing I saw, before the water closed like a book: my bedroom window, high above, with the paper stars on the glass, and behind them a shape. My mother. Standing at the window. Watching the well.

Not coming down.

Watching.

And I have carried that window with me through every year of the long wet afterward, and I have decided — courts adjourned, verdict final — that when I rise, I will not rise angry at the nine. Anger is for people who still have tomorrows to spend it in.

I will rise the way a song rises. Quietly. In someone else's mouth.

And I will sing it twice.
