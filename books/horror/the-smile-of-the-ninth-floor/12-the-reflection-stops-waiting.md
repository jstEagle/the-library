## 12. The Reflection Stops Waiting

For eleven days the mirror and I had an arrangement, if you could call it that. It lagged. I moved; it followed, one second behind, like a bad telephone connection to my own face. I had learned to live with it the way you learn to live with a dripping tap — by pretending it was character rather than defect.

On the twelfth morning, the arrangement ended.

I was shaving when I noticed it: the razor in the glass met my cheek at the same moment mine did. No gap. No courteous second of delay. The reflection had caught up.

I stood there with foam on my jaw and felt something worse than fear, which is the specific loneliness of a rule changing without consultation. For days I had built a small science around that second — timed it against my watch, tested it with a metronome borrowed from the church hall, written its constancy into my notebook in the tidy hand of a man who believes the universe can be filed. One second. Always one second. A debt being paid on schedule.

Now it owed me nothing. And a creditor who stops collecting is not a creditor who has forgiven you. It's a creditor who has decided to take payment in another currency.

I finished shaving badly. In the mirror, my reflection finished shaving well.

* * *

You will want to know how I rationalized it, because I always rationalize first and feel later; it is the occupational disease of folklorists, we who spend our lives turning other people's terror into footnotes. I told myself it was fatigue. I told myself the delay hadn't vanished but merely fallen below the threshold of perception — that the mirror was still behind me by half a second, a quarter, some fraction too small for the eye to catch but large enough for the soul to notice, which is why my hands shook while my mind composed tidy little hypotheses.

The hypothesis lasted until noon.

I came downstairs to make lunch and stopped on the third step, because the kitchen table was set for two. Two plates. Two forks, aligned. Two glasses of water, filled to within a centimeter of the brim, so precisely matched that the meniscus of each caught the window light identically, like twins.

I lived alone. I had lived alone since — well, since before Hollowmere, which is all I seemed able to say about my life before Hollowmere lately, as though the town were a tide line and everything behind it washed smooth.

I touched one of the plates. It was cold. The water was cold. But the table had been cleared when I went up — I remembered clearing it, remembered stacking the single plate and cup, because tidiness is the other thing I do instead of feeling.

In the window above the sink, in the dark rectangle of the glass, my reflection stood in the kitchen doorway. I was standing on the stairs. I checked. I actually turned my head and looked back down the stairwell at myself standing there, and then looked back at the window, where the other me had not moved from the doorway, and was holding a dish towel I did not own.

He folded the towel. Set it on the counter. Looked up at me — not at the room, at *me*, at the actual point in space where I stood on the stairs — and inclined his head slightly, the way one man acknowledges another across a crowded station.

Then he was simply my reflection again, standing where I stood, holding nothing, and the delay was back, one full second, polite as a shop assistant.

I sat down at the table for two and drank both glasses of water, because leaving them there seemed somehow more dangerous than drinking them. They tasted of well water. Everything here tastes of well water now. My own taps have been running sweet and mineral-heavy for a week, though the drought has cracked the fields like old crockery and Mrs. Aldous at the shop says the reservoir is down to mud and prayers.

* * *

That afternoon I did what I always do when the world misbehaves: I took notes. I set the tape recorder running on the kitchen counter — it goes everywhere with me now, my little black witness — and I conducted experiments on myself like a man testing whether he still owns his hands.

Experiment one: I raised my right arm. The reflection raised its right arm one second later. Normal. Restored. Almost apologetic.

Experiment two: I raised my left arm and, halfway up, reversed direction. The reflection raised its arm fully, held it, then lowered it — following my original intention, not my revised one. As if it had already read the whole sentence while I was still sounding out the words.

Experiment three: I said aloud, "Can you hear me?"

The reflection said nothing, of course. Mirrors don't speak. But its mouth moved — once, briefly, shaping two syllables I couldn't parse, and the tape recorder, which I played back three times with the volume up and my ear pressed to the speaker, caught this beneath the kitchen silence: a voice like mine and not mine, saying what I have transcribed eight ways and cannot improve upon:

*"Almost."*

I packed the recorder away and did not unpack it until evening. Some instruments tell you things you'd rather the furniture keep to itself.

* * *

The doors began that night.

You should understand about me and doors. Counting them is not a quirk I picked up in Hollowmere; it is older than my career, older than my degrees, one of those compulsions I've carried so long it functions as a personality. Lights on, doors counted, tape running. Three rituals, and I had always assumed they kept the world out. It occurs to me now, writing this, that they may only ever have kept me in.

The house has nine doors. Ground floor: front, back, pantry, the cupboard under the stairs. First floor: bathroom, my bedroom, the linen closet, and the narrow door at the landing's end that opens onto nothing but a brick wall — the previous tenant's notes had counted that one too, obsessively, in a handwriting that deteriorated over forty pages from careful to clawed. Nine doors. I count them every night. Nine. It steadies me the way a rosary steadies a man who no longer believes in what the beads are for.

At ten o'clock I counted nine. At ten-thirty, passing the landing, I counted ten.

I stood in the corridor with the lamp shaking in my hand and counted again, touching each door as I named it, because touching makes it real, doesn't it, touching is the folklorist's substitute for faith. Front, back, pantry, cupboard, bath, bedroom, linen, wall-door. Eight touched. Nine counted. There was a tenth between the linen closet and the wall-door, a door I had never seen, painted the same cream as the others, with a handle worn bright by use.

It was warm. Not sun-warm. Skin-warm.

I put my palm flat against it and felt, or imagined I felt, or the mirror in the bathroom behind me definitely felt — a rhythm underneath, slow and patient, like something breathing through wood.

I did not open it. Let the record show I did not open it. Whatever courage I've assembled in this village is the kind that counts doors, not the kind that opens ones that weren't there at breakfast.

When I went back down for the lamp oil — an excuse, obviously, a man does not need lamp oil at ten-forty at night — and returned, the door was gone. The wall ran unbroken, cream paint, hairline crack, the damp stain shaped like a hand that I had long ago stopped seeing.

But the count was wrong now in the other direction. Eight doors. I walked the house twice. Eight. The pantry door had folded itself out of existence, or been folded, neatly, like a page.

The house was editing itself. And it was doing it *deliberately* — that was the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. A haunted house makes mistakes; a haunted house is a broken machine rattling through its damage. This was not breakage. Taking a door away after adding one was a correction. A demonstration. It was showing me that the number nine meant nothing to it except as a thing it could move, the way a pianist runs scales to show the fingers are warmed up.

Nine doors. Nine neighbors. Nine names in the register under the pencil rubbing, and a tenth smudged to nothing: Odile.

The house wasn't counting. The house was reminding.

* * *

I slept — if you call it sleeping — with the tape recorder beside my pillow and every light burning, and woke at four in the morning to the sound of crockery.

Not breaking. *Arranging.* The soft ceramic click of plate on wood, the ring of a fork laid down parallel to a knife, the particular hush of a chair drawn back from a table by someone considerate of a sleeper upstairs.

I lay in bed and listened the way you listen to a burglar: cataloguing, praying, bargaining. Then I got up, because I would rather see a horror than hear one, and went down with the lamp held out before me like a man approaching a dog he suspects of rabies.

The kitchen was clean. The table was set for two again — but differently now. The settings faced each other across the corner, close, intimate, the way two people sit when one of them is teaching the other something. On the smaller plate, the child's portion, someone had placed a sprig of dried rowan from the jar on my windowsill, arranged in a little bouquet, tied with thread.

And in the window, in the black glass, my reflection was sitting at the table. Sitting, when I was standing. Wearing my face with an expression I have never worn in my life — soft, attentive, waiting — the face of someone listening to a child talk about her day.

He looked up at me. He smiled with my mouth, and it was the worst thing yet, worse than the extra door, worse than "almost," because the smile was kind. It was the smile of a man who has decided to be good. Who has decided it late, and at someone else's expense, but has genuinely decided it.

He raised one finger to his lips. Then he pointed — slowly, deliberately, my own index finger tracing the gesture in the air — toward the back window, and beyond it, invisible in the dark, the well.

Then he stood, and I stood, our movements locked together again at last, one second apart, and he let me have the kitchen back.

* * *

Morning. I am writing this at the kitchen table, at the adult setting, because sitting anywhere else feels like accepting an invitation. The rowan sprig is still on the child's plate. I can't bring myself to move it. Somewhere in the last twelve days I stopped being a researcher of this place's folklore and became one of its documents, and documents don't get to rearrange the archive.

Here is what I know, laid out in the order I can bear it:

The reflection no longer waits for me. Sometimes it does, out of courtesy or strategy, but the delay is now a costume it puts on when I'm watching, like a servant straightening up when the master enters. It moves independently. It sets tables. It knows the count of doors better than I do and changes it to suit some lesson plan I haven't been shown.

It is not mocking me. That's the conclusion I resisted longest and now can't escape. Mockery needs contempt, and there is no contempt in that face. There is patience. There is the terrible gentleness of something preparing you for a task it knows you'll refuse if you see it coming whole.

Two places at the table. A child's portion. Rowan, which grows by wells to guard them, or used to, before the villagers cut theirs down — I found the stumps behind the church, seven of them in a ring, old growth felled in 1974 by the look of the rings.

It is setting a table for Odile. And it is setting a place for me beside her, and it is doing it in my own hands, with my own face, because somewhere in the walls of this house — in the wet brick behind the plaster, in the water rising through floors that should be dry in a drought — the girl who was drowned is learning to use me. Or to *be* me. I no longer know which of us is the reflection.

Last night, before I came up to write this, I stood at the back window and looked at the well. The moon was out, thin as a paring. The well cover — the rotted boards I'd noted in week one, half-collapsed inward — was whole. New wood. Pale, unweathered, fitted tight, as if the well had repaired itself for company.

And from under the new boards, faint but unmistakable, rising through the ground and up through the soles of my feet and into the bones of my ankles, came humming. A child's tune. The harmony from my tapes, the voice beneath the lullaby, the song sung once in 1974 and stolen before it could be sung twice.

She was practicing.

Today I will go back to the nine families. I will knock on their polished doors and stand in their prosperous halls and ask them, again, for what they took. Because the house has stopped waiting for me, the mirrors have stopped waiting, the well has stopped waiting — everything in Hollowmere is finished waiting except the people, and people are the only things here that still believe they have time.

One of them broke last week and gave me a locket. One barn burned for it.

Eight relics to go, and the water is already at the floorboards, and my own face is setting the table for the solstice.

I just hope, when she sits down, she leaves room for whoever I'm going to be afterward.
