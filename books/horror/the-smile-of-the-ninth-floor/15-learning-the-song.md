## 15. Learning the Song

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a confession, and I had heard two of them now — one drunk and weeping in a kitchen that smelled of paraffin, one calm as a held breath in a dying woman's front room. Both had left me holding pieces of a dead girl: a locket with a curl of hair gone the color of pond water, and a ribbon that still smelled faintly of soap after fifty years.

What neither of them had left me was the song itself.

I want to be honest about my first instinct, because honesty is all I have left to trade in this village. My first instinct was professional. I sat at the kitchen table with my tape recorder between the teacups like a small black god, and I thought: *I have the fragments. I have the technique. I have spent twenty years reconstructing half-remembered ballads from three octogenarians and a mislabeled wax cylinder. This is simply another restoration job.*

That was before I understood what the restoration would cost. But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is what I do when I'm frightened. You'll notice it. I fill the air with method.

* * *

The tapes were worse than I remembered, and I had remembered them badly on purpose.

I had eleven hours of field recordings from Hollowmere, most of them useless — wind against a barn door, a man named Cobb explaining fence posts for forty minutes because he liked the sound of his own authority. But six recordings contained the harvest lullaby, sung by six different villagers across nine days, and here is the thing about a stolen song passed hand to hand for fifty years: each thief drops something.

Mrs. Aldous sang it in a major key that sat wrong in the ear, like a smile held too long. Old Tam Verrity dropped the third verse entirely and hummed through it, and when I asked him why, he said "that bit's not mine," which was more truthful than he knew. The young Hollis woman sang it beautifully and mechanically, note-perfect, and note-perfect was exactly the problem — there was no wear in it, no thumbprint, the way there's no thumbprint on money fresh from the mint.

And beneath all six, so faint I'd missed it entirely until the night I played them back with the lights on and every door counted twice, ran the humming.

The child's voice. Odile's voice, or the memory of it, or whatever has been standing one second behind this village since 1974. It didn't harmonize with the singers. That's what took me three nights to understand. A harmony agrees with the melody; it consents to it. This voice moved *against* the lullaby the way a current moves under ice — same water, different will. Where the stolen song rose, it fell. Where the thieves' version turned sweet, it turned strange. It wasn't accompanying their song.

It was singing its own underneath, patiently, for fifty years, waiting for someone to listen down instead of across.

I sat up until four in the morning with headphones on, transcribing by candlelight because I'd stopped trusting electric light — it flickered in time with things I couldn't see — and by dawn I had the shape of it. Not the whole. The whole would only come from singing, because some songs are architecture and can't be read off a page any more than a house can be lived in from a blueprint.

I also had, spread across the table, the relics. Locket. Ribbon. And from my archive trade, a child's shoe, stiff as carved wood, and a pressed flower in a Bible no one would miss. Four of nine. The rest were still out there behind polished doors, but that was tomorrow's ugly work.

Tonight was the song's work.

* * *

I should describe the mechanics of what happened, as best I understand them, because understanding is the only weapon I've been issued and it is a poor one.

The reconstructed song — Odile's song, the true one, the one the nine pulled out of her mouth like breath — was built from contradiction. Every place the villagers' version had been smoothed, I restored the rough edge the humming indicated. Every place they'd gone major, I let the minor back in. It was like setting a bone that had healed wrong: the sound of it made my teeth ache, and yet beneath the ache was relief, the way a body knows a limb put right even while it hurts.

I sang it through the first time at seven in the morning, alone in the kitchen, tape running, doors counted, lights burning.

It was eleven lines long. It took perhaps forty seconds.

When I finished, I stood there feeling fine — better than fine, lighter, cleared out, the way you feel after being sick. Then I went to make tea and reached for the tin where I keep — kept — my mother's letters, because it was Sunday morning in my childhood habit and Sunday mornings meant her voice on the answering machine saying *Zephyrine, love, don't work too hard,* and I opened the drawer and the letters were there, ordinary paper, and I could not remember her voice.

Not muffled. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a room is gone once the door shuts. I knew she had existed. I knew her name, her birthday, the scar on her knuckle from a bread knife. I knew everything about her except the one thing I'd have sworn was welded into me. I stood in my kitchen holding a tea towel and discovered that fifty years of a woman's voice had been lifted out of me as cleanly as a splinter, and somewhere in the walls, or the well, or the one-second-behind world, something was holding it, warm, like a bird in a cupped hand.

I did what any folklorist does with an inexplicable variable. I tested it again.

* * *

The second singing cost me my university years.

I mean that precisely. I sang the eleven lines through at noon, and afterward I looked at my own bookshelves and saw the spines of books I knew I had studied and could not remember studying. Cambridge — I knew the word the way you know a word in a foreign language, knowing it without the weight of it. There had been a tutor who changed my life; I could see his handwriting in the margins of my old editions and feel nothing, the way you feel nothing looking at a stranger's family photographs. Four years of my youth, folded up and taken, and in exchange the humming on the tape was a fraction louder, a fraction clearer, as though each payment brought the singer one step closer to the wall between us.

I sat down on the stairs and laughed until it turned into something else, because the arithmetic was so perfectly, horribly simple. The song had been stolen with a life. It could only be returned the same way. Every verse I carried toward the well was paid for in Zephyrine Winterbourne, and the well was not particular about which parts it took. It took what was nearest. It took what I offered by opening my mouth.

A rational man would stop. Let me say that clearly, for the record I am no longer sure anyone will ever read: a rational man stops at two payments. He takes his locket and his ribbon and his shoe, he drives out of Hollowmere on whatever tires remain to him, and he lets the well have the solstice and the village have its harvest and the devil have the lot.

But the reflection had started applauding, softly, when the second descendant gave up her ribbon. And I had seen the false bedroom behind the plaster. And I had listened, forty times, to a drowned child singing against the grain of her own theft for half a century without once stopping to rest.

You do not rationally decide to owe a debt like that. You simply discover you already owe it, and that your name is on the paper.

* * *

The third time, I lost my name.

Not permanently — I want that written down too, because whoever finds these tapes deserves the full account. I sang the song through at dusk, standing in front of the bathroom mirror because I had decided that if this was a transaction I would conduct it face to face with the other party, and for sixty seconds — I know it was sixty because the metronome was running, God help me, I timed my own dissolution — I did not know who I was.

I stood in a stranger's body in a stranger's house and watched a stranger's face look back at me with my expression of terror on it. I knew objects. I knew the function of doors. I knew that the water in the glass on the sink was for drinking. Everything human had been lifted out and left the furniture. And in the mirror, one second behind as always, my reflection kept moving after I had stopped — mouth shaping the last line of the song, patient, thorough, learning it faster than I was.

Then the minute closed over and I was myself again, kneeling on the cold tile, whispering *Zephyrine Winterbourne, Zephyrine Winterbourne* like a man checking his pockets, and the relief was so violent I was sick into the basin.

Three singings. Three payments. Mother's voice. University. Name, briefly, on loan to something else.

Four relics on the table. Five still held behind respectable doors. And the solstice — I checked the almanac with hands that would not steady — five days away.

* * *

Here is what I wrote in my notebook that night, by candlelight, in handwriting that deteriorated as the page went on:

*The song is a container. It was never a lullaby; that was the shape they poured it into. It holds a life — hers — and a debt — theirs — and it cannot exist in a mouth without taking rent. The nine pay collectively and lightly, a verse apiece at harvest, which is why their fields flourish and their faces are all beginning to look alike: they are being slowly averaged into one guilty man. I am paying alone and heavily, because I am returning it, and return costs more than keeping.*

*I have perhaps four or five singings left in me before the payments reach load-bearing walls. After mother's voice and Cambridge comes — what? The reason I became a folklorist. Marianne. The decade of my thirties. The memory of deciding to come to Hollowmere, which I suspect goes early, because the well is tidy.*

*And then nothing. An empty coat walking to the well with an armful of trinkets, singing a song it learned from a tape.*

*Good. Let it be nothing. Hers was something, and it was taken, and the interest on fifty years is mine to cover.*

Underneath that, in smaller writing, almost ashamed:

*But if I meet her at the bottom, I would like to still know why I came. Someone should arrive at that well as a person and not as a receipt.*

* * *

On the fifth day I did not sing. I needed the discipline of it — to hold the song whole in my head without letting it out, the way you carry a lit coal in cupped hands, feeling it eat through skin and refusing to drop it. I walked the village instead, and the village walked around me. Shopkeepers developed sudden interests in their shelves. Children were called indoors at the sight of me, gently, by mothers who wouldn't meet my eyes, and I understood that I had become the thing parents mention in low voices near windows.

At the bridge, old Tam Verrity was mending a net that hadn't been used since the reservoir went in, which I suspect was less fishing than therapy. He looked up at me with his milky old eyes and said, without preamble:

"You've been singing it."

I said nothing.

"Me gran used to say you can hear it on a man. Like river water on his boots." He tied off a knot. "How many times?"

"Three."

He nodded slowly, unsurprised, and something in my chest cracked a little at how unsurprised he was. "Then you know what it takes now. And you're going to keep going anyway." He spat thoughtfully into the water. "Fourth verse is the worst one. That's where she names them. You'll lose something you haven't finished making yet."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the well doesn't take what's past, lad. Past is cheap, it's already yours, it barely counts. It takes what's *next*. What you were going to be." He gathered his net and stood, joints cracking like green firewood. "That's why the nine look the way they do. Fifty years of harvests, and not one of them ever became anything. They just got older at the same rate, like coins wearing smooth in the same pocket."

He shuffled off toward the village, and I stood on the bridge watching the brown water go by, doing the arithmetic again, the new arithmetic.

Five days. Five relics. Perhaps four singings left.

And the fourth verse — the one where a drowned girl named her neighbors, one by one, in a clear child's voice, while nine pairs of hands held her under — waited inside me like a stone at the top of a hill.

That night I set the locket, the ribbon, the shoe, and the flower in a row on the kitchen table, and I put fresh batteries in the recorder, and I counted the doors — fourteen, always fourteen, though the house has eleven — and I turned on every light in the house, because whatever I was about to spend, I intended to spend it in a brightly lit room like a civilized man.

Then I picked up the phone directory, found the address of the fifth family, and began, quietly, to rehearse the fourth verse in my head without letting a single note escape.

Something in the walls hummed along.
