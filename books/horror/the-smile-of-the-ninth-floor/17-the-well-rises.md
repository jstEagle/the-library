## 17. The Well Rises

The water came for the house on a Tuesday, which felt insultingly ordinary. Catastrophes should announce themselves with thunder, with omens, with at least the courtesy of a dark and stormy night. Instead I woke to the sound of my own slippers squelching.

I stood in the hallway in my pyjamas, listening. Under the floorboards, something moved—not rushing, not gushing, but seeping, the patient way water finds its level, the way it has always found its level since before we built floors to pretend otherwise. I pressed my palm flat against the boards. Cold. Damp. And beneath the cold, faint as a signal through static, a vibration that was almost—but not quite—a hum.

"No," I told the house conversationally. This had become a habit of late, addressing the house as one addresses a difficult colleague. "No, absolutely not."

The house did not dignify this with a reply, but when I knelt and put my ear to the gap beneath the skirting board, I heard it clearly: singing. Or the memory of singing. The reconstructed song, the one I'd been teaching myself note by note at the cost of my own past, rising up through the foundations like groundwater through chalk.

I checked the kitchen first, because kitchens are where sensible people go in crises. A thin sheet of water lay across the flagstones, maybe two millimetres deep, perfectly still, perfectly reflecting the ceiling light. No leak from the tap—I turned it, tested it, dry as a sermon. No water from the pipes. The water had simply *arrived*, the way weather arrives, the way dread arrives.

It was coming up through the stone.

* * *

By noon the bathroom mirror's false room was ankle-deep.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last professional habit I had left. In the mirror—the mirror that had spent weeks showing me a child's bedroom that existed nowhere in my house except behind wet brick—there was now water on the floor of that impossible room. It lapped at the legs of the small bed. It climbed the damp wallpaper in a tide line, darkening the faded roses an inch at a time. And floating on its surface, turning slowly, was a paper boat.

I don't know who folds a paper boat for a drowned child. I know who folded that one. My hands remembered doing it the night before, though I had no memory of deciding to—another memory paid out like rope, gone into the song. The reflection stood at the window of the false room, my shoulders, my posture, watching the boat circle.

"You could help," I said to it.

It turned its head. Not all the way. Just enough.

* * *

In the village, the taps were running wrong.

I learned this from Mrs. Aldery at the shop, who sold me bread and batteries with the strained courtesy of someone serving a man she'd been instructed to freeze out but hadn't quite the heart—or perhaps hadn't quite the order—to do it yet. Her hands shook as she made change.

"The water's singing," she said, before she could stop herself. Then, lower: "Everyone's taps. Since dawn. You can hear it if you hold a glass to the pipe. Little tune. Nobody's taught it to the pipes."

"Odile's song," I said.

"Don't say that name in here." She glanced at the door, at the street, at the lit windows across the way where curtains twitched in the universal grammar of surveillance. "Mr. Winterbourne. Three days. Whatever you're planning—and everyone knows you're planning something—you have three days."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned across the counter, and for a moment the shopkeeper's mask slipped entirely, and underneath was just a tired woman in her sixties who had grown up drinking from that well and had never once wondered why her childhood dreams smelled of pond weed. "The water came up through my cellar this morning. Through solid floor. My husband's been bailing since six and it rises faster than he bails. And it *hums*, Mr. Winterbourne. All day it hums. My granddaughter asked me what the song was, and I nearly told her. I nearly sang it back to her." Her voice cracked on the last word. "That's how it gets in. That's how it got into all of us. Somebody hears it second."

She pushed the bread across the counter like a peace offering or a bribe.

"Three days," she said again. "Then the solstice. Then the well takes what it wants, and it won't be particular about whose throat it sings out of."

* * *

That afternoon I walked out to the well itself, because I am, whatever else I've become, still a folklorist, and folklorists go and look at the thing.

The drought had held for six weeks. The fields were the colour of old string, the stream behind the church reduced to a chain of grey puddles, farmers hauling tankers twice a day and praying quietly to whichever saint handles irrigation. Everything in Hollowmere was parched except the well behind my rented house, which brimmed to within a hand's width of the lip, black and smooth as poured ink.

Now it had begun to overflow.

Not dramatically. A slow creep of water over the ancient stones, threading down through the grass, finding channels toward the house, toward the village, toward everything. I crouched at the edge and looked down into it, and the water looked back with the particular patience of water that has been waiting since 1974.

"Hum it," I said to the well. "Go on. Hum it."

And the well—God forgive me, the well obliged. A low note rose from it, felt more than heard, up through my knees, my spine, my teeth. The harvest lullaby's opening phrase, rendered in pure vibration, the way a child might hum it underwater.

I stepped back. My heel splashed. The whole field between the well and the house was gleaming now, a skin of water over the cracked earth, and in every puddle my reflection stood a half-second ahead of me—*ahead*, note the change, no longer behind—already turning toward the house, already walking, waiting for me to catch up.

* * *

Evening. The house.

The water in the hallway was calf-deep now and rising at a rate I'd started charting on the back of an envelope, because charts are comfort and I needed all the comfort available. Four centimetres an hour. At that rate, by the solstice, the ground floor would be under a metre and a half of singing water, and the false room in the mirror would be fully flooded, and I had begun to suspect—professional intuition, the kind that has kept me alive in archives and graveyards and one memorably bad night in a Romanian forest—that the two facts were the same fact.

The mirror-room's water stood at the small bed's mattress now. The paper boat had sunk. On the bed, in the reflection, someone had laid out a child's nightdress, folded with terrible care.

And the reflection of me—the one that used to lag, then stopped lagging, then started *leading*—was standing in the flooded room holding a towel, wringing it out over the water as though trying to bail the sea with a cloth. Pointless. Devoted. It worked with the grim diligence of a parent at a sickbed.

I watched it for a long time.

"That's not going to work," I said finally.

It paused. Looked up. And its face—for the first time since the delay had collapsed, since it had begun moving on its own schedule, tidying, setting tables, living its quiet parallel life—it looked *sad*. Not menacing. Sad, the way Odile's humming was sad, the way a song sung once and stolen is sad. It pressed one hand flat against the inside of the glass, and the glass was warm, and I understood with a lurch that it wasn't threatening me.

It was asking me to hurry.

* * *

I spent the evening on the phone, working the list. Seven relics gathered—lockets, ribbons, a shoe, a christening spoon, things I had traded and begged and, yes, blackmailed out of nine families' worth of inherited guilt. Two short. The council head, Merridew, who had burned his relic in the square and declared the song belonged to the living, and whose defiance had turned every mirror in the village dark as tar. And one other: young Tom Vasseur, who had slammed his door on me and whose barn had burned anyway, fire finding the guilty the way water finds the low places.

The phone lines were mostly dead—water in the exchanges, presumably, though I suspected the exchange water hummed too. I got through to exactly one number: the retired teacher, Miss Halloway, who had confessed the council's arrangement, who had given me Odile's true name, who had begged me to leave and then, when I refused, had done the braver thing and started helping.

"The whole village is flooding," she said without greeting. "Cellars, churches, the school. The water table's risen four feet in a day and there's been no rain since September. The vicar held an emergency service tonight. Halfway through, the font started humming and forty people walked out."

"Miss Halloway—"

"Merridew's called a council meeting for tomorrow night. Solstice eve. He means to formally bar you from the well. There'll be men there. He's frightened, Zephyrine, and frightened men with a failing harvest do arithmetic very quickly—one stranger versus a village full of children." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "The water reached my doorstep this evening. It sat there. It wouldn't come in. It waited until I opened the door myself, and then it came in polite as a visitor, and it went straight to the piano and the strings hummed while it passed." A shaky breath. "It knows manners. Someone taught it manners. Do you understand what I'm telling you? It isn't rising like a flood. It's rising like a *guest*."

I looked down the hallway of my own house. The water had reached the bottom stair and stopped there, smooth and black and patient, reflecting the ceiling light in a perfect coin. Waiting for me to invite it further. Waiting, like everything in Hollowmere, for permission.

"Three days," I said.

"Two nights and a day," she corrected. "And Zephyrine—if you mean to sing it at that well, you'd best have every relic in your hands, because the water won't wait for the incomplete. It'll take its payment piecemeal. From whoever's nearest." Another pause, longer. "From the village, if you fail."

After she rang off I sat a long while in the lamplight, listening to the house breathe water.

* * *

Near midnight I did the accounting, because the hour demanded it and because I have always believed fear is best handled with a ledger.

Seven relics in the biscuit tin under my bed, wrapped in a tea towel like the world's most dreadful Christmas present. One burned. One withheld. One song, three-quarters learned, each verse costing me a room of my own past—the university already gone, my mother's voice going threadbare, last week I'd lost the name of the street I grew up on and had to invent one, and the invented one had felt truer within the hour, which is its own species of horror.

Two days. One well. Nine debts, seven paid, two outstanding.

I took off my shoes, rolled up my trousers, and waded down the hallway to check the front door was locked—an old compulsion, doors counted, lights on, the rituals of a man who has always believed that order is a load-bearing wall. The water parted around my ankles with something that felt horribly like courtesy.

At the door I stopped. Because the water had reached it too, pooling against the threshold from the outside, and in its surface—stretched door-wide, black, still—was not the reflection of my hallway.

It was the well. Seen from above, looking straight down into it. And rising slowly through the black water toward the surface, toward me, pale hands first, was a small figure with wet hair spread like ink, and around her, faint and clear and unmistakable, a voice was humming the final verse.

The verse I hadn't learned yet.

The figure stopped just below the surface. Looked up. And waited—with the patience of water, with the patience of 1974, with the patience of a girl who had been singing alone for fifty years—for me to learn the rest.

The solstice was two nights away. The well was rising. And somewhere in the flooded dark of my own hallway, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, I understood that the water wasn't coming to drown me.

It was coming to teach me the ending.
