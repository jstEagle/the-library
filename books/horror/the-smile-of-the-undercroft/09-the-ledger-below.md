## 9. The Ledger Below

Tam Osgood had not slept in nine nights, and he had stopped pretending to anyone that he had. The lie of sleep was the last lie he had left that didn't cost him, and even that one had begun to rot in his mouth. So when Orsen Blackwood knocked on his door at half past ten at night — knocked the way she did everything, twice, precise, as if the world might be persuaded to behave if addressed firmly enough — he was already dressed, already sitting in the kitchen with his boots on and the lamp turned low, waiting for her the way a man waits for a verdict.

Bree was upstairs. He could hear her moving above him, the soft percussion of her bare feet on the floorboards, the particular rhythm she made when she was checking on Nell. Nell had not slept well either. None of them had, though none of them would say so aloud, because saying it aloud in Hollowmere was the same as feeding it.

He opened the door before her second knock fell.

"You've been to the records office," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen her at the parish chest three days running, her gloved hands turning the vellum leaves like a surgeon turning back skin.

"I've been to the records office," she agreed. Her breath fogged in the cold. Behind her, the village lay under a moon that seemed to have been polished rather than risen, and beyond the last rooftops, past the churchyard where the roofless church stood open to the sky like a ribcage, the bog waited. It always waited. That was the whole of Tam's life, in a sentence. The bog waited, and Tam kept it waiting.

"Then you know about the dates," she said. "October the third. Four generations. Eight of them."

"I know."

"And the ninth niche has my name on it. Cut recently. Cut *well* — whoever cut it knew stone." She looked at him the way she looked at her dying patients, he thought. Reading the face for the thing underneath the face. "Someone in this village cut my name into a wall before I'd ever set foot here. I want to know who, and I want to know why, and I want to know it tonight, Mr. Osgood, because I have spent three weeks telling myself this place is eccentric and I am done telling myself things."

Tam stood in his doorway and felt the old ledger open in his chest, the one he kept not on paper — paper could be found, paper could be read, paper could hang you — but in the body, in the weight behind his sternum that had been there since his father put the iron key in his hand at seventeen and said, *now it's yours, God forgive us both.*

He could lie to her. He had lied to keepers' questions before. He had lied to the vicar in '98, to the surveyor in '04, to his own wife, God rest her, who had died never knowing what her husband did on the first cold night of every October, walking the bog's edge with a lantern and a long iron rod, sounding the peat the way a doctor sounds a chest.

But Orsen Blackwood had her aunt's eyes. That was the thing he hadn't prepared for. Maren had had those same eyes — grey going to iron, iron going to something older — and Maren had stood in this same doorway forty years ago and asked him the same question in the same voice, and he had lied to her, and she had gone looking alone, and what she'd found had bought the village another generation at a price Tam still paid in his sleep.

He wasn't going to lie to this one. Not because he was brave. Because he was tired, and because the bell had rung last week, and because a man who hears a buried bell ring knows that the era of useful lies is over.

"Come in," he said. "There's a thermos. It's a long walk, and you'll want your hands warm for what they're going to feel."

* * *

They went out the back way, past the bakehouse where Bree's bread would be proving for the morning, its warmth breathing through the brick like something sleeping. Tam carried the lantern in his left hand and the iron rod in his right, wrapped in sacking so it wouldn't ring against anything. It was four feet of blacksmith's iron, older than his grandfather, and it had one purpose, and the purpose was terrible in its simplicity: you drove it into the bog, and you listened.

The marsh began where the pasture gave up. In daylight it was beautiful — Tam would grant it that, had always granted it that, because the thing beneath it was vain and it did no harm to flatter your jailer — a spread of rust and gold, cotton-grass blowing like a congregation of small ghosts, black water lying in the hollows perfectly still, perfectly reflecting, as if the sky had fallen and not had the strength to get up. At night it was another country. At night the stillness of the water looked less like reflection and more like attention.

Orsen walked beside him without speaking. He'd expected questions and got none, and he understood why when he saw how she moved: she was a nurse. She knew how to enter a room where something bad was happening. You went quiet, you went steady, you let the bad thing establish its own rhythm before you touched it.

The ground changed underfoot a half-mile out. He felt her feel it — her step hitched, just slightly, the way a step hitches when the world gives you back a different sound than the one you asked for.

"It's hollow," she said.

"It's hollow," Tam said. "All of it. From here to the far alders, two hundred yards of ground, and under the ground —" he stopped, because there was no way to say it that wasn't madness, and he had decided, walking out here, that he would rather be mad in front of her than a liar. "Under the ground there's a bell. The bell of Hollowmere church. The one they tell you was melted down in 1911."

Orsen said nothing. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark a curlew called, one long rising note, and Tam flinched at it before he could stop himself.

"It wasn't melted," he went on. "You don't melt a bell like that. It was cast in 1349, out of bell-metal they alloyed with —" he waved a hand, the gesture failing him, "— with things. Church records call them *relics.* The founder went mad three days after the pour and they had to chain him in the undercroft, and the bell hung in that tower for five hundred years, and every death in the parish, it rang for. Not the sexton ringing it. It rang *itself.* Light as breath. And the dying heard it before they died. Days before. Weeks. They'd lie in their beds counting backwards, and their families would say it was the fever, and the bell would ring the night the count reached zero, and everyone would nod and say, well, the bell knew."

Orsen had gone very still. "The counting," she said. "The backwards counting. I've —" and then she stopped, and Tam watched her decide, in real time, whether to spend the truth. She spent it. "I've logged four this month at the hospice. Before I came here. I logged them as coincidence. Edith Vane counted from ninety-nine and died exactly at zero and I wrote *coincidence* in the margin of the log because the alternative was —"

"Was this," Tam said. "Yes."

The bog lay before them now, the deep heart of it, where the peat went down forty feet and the water stood in pools like pupils. Tam set the lantern on a tussock and unwrapped the iron rod. In the lantern light the metal looked wet, though it hadn't been out of the sacking in a year.

"Osgoods have been keepers since 1911," he said. "My great-grandfather was the one who helped bury it. The Blackwoods rang the bell that October — rang it on purpose, rang it to call something up, something the bell was cast to be the *voice* of — and it came, or it answered, or whatever verb you want, and it took eight of them into the ground. The village buried the bell in the bog to silence it, because a bell that's ringing can call, and a bell that's buried and silent can only wait. And then the village made a bargain it's been ashamed of for a hundred and thirteen years, and my family has been the hands of that bargain ever since."

He drove the rod into the peat.

He'd meant to explain more. He'd meant to tell her about the strangers, about the ones he'd chosen, the ones he'd led out onto this ground on cold October nights with a story about rare orchids or a lost dog, about how the bog took them soft and kept them, about how the thing below accepted a stranger in place of a Blackwood and the village bought its year. But the rod went in, and Tam put his palm flat against the iron the way his father had taught him, the way his father's father had taught his father, and the sound came up through the metal into his hand and through his hand into his bones, and every explanation he had ever prepared died in his throat.

The ground rang.

Not the rod. The *ground.* The rod was only the striker; the bog itself was the bell, two hundred yards of peat and water and buried bronze sounding one low note that Tam felt in his teeth, in the roots of his teeth, in the deep bone behind his sternum where the ledger lived. Orsen's hand came out and found the rod too — he saw her do it, saw her lay her palm against the iron the way you'd take a pulse, because she was a nurse and that was the only language her hands knew — and her face changed. He watched the rationalist leave her. He watched it go the way breath goes, not all at once, in a long reluctant letting-down.

"That's not an echo," she whispered. "Echoes decay. That's — it's *sustaining.*"

"It's listening," Tam said. "You struck it. It knows someone's asking."

He pulled the rod free and the note faded and the silence that followed was worse, the way a held breath is worse than a scream. And then, before either of them could move or speak, the bell tolled.

Once.

Not through the rod. Not through the ground they'd disturbed. From everywhere. From under everything. A single stroke, deep and round and patient, rising up through forty feet of peat as if peat were air, and Tam's lantern flame stood straight up and went blue, and every bird in the marsh — the curlew, the snipe, the roosting ducks he hadn't known were there, all of them, the whole hidden congregation of the night — went silent at once. Not scattered. Not startled. *Silenced.* The way a room goes silent when the doctor comes in. The way a hospice ward goes quiet at the hour when the counting starts.

Orsen's hand was still on the iron rod. Tam saw her knuckles whiten on it, saw her lips move, and he realized with a horror that felt almost like tenderness that she was counting. Downward. Without meaning to. Her lips shaping numbers she didn't know she knew, the way the dying did, the way they all did, the way *everyone* did when the bell spoke — because that was what the bell was, that was what it had always been, not a bell but a *count,* and the counting wasn't the dying people's delusion at all. It was the bell's voice, wearing their mouths.

He put his hand over hers and lifted it off the rod.

"Don't count," he said, and his voice came out wrecked. "Whatever you do, don't count with it. It takes the counting as an answer."

She looked at him. In the lantern light her face was grey as the bog water, and her breath came fast and shallow, and when she spoke her voice was the voice of a woman standing at the edge of a vertigo she'd been denying all her life.

"How long has it been ringing?"

"Last week it rang for the first time in thirteen years. Once. Just like that." He looked out at the black pools, at the cotton-grass hanging motionless in air that had stopped moving, at the alders on the far side standing like witnesses who would not meet your eye. "It's not supposed to ring. That's the whole of it, Orsen. That's the whole of my family's job. It rings when it's counting down to a taking, and it rings once a year on the anniversary to remind us — my father called it the *toll of the lease* — but it doesn't ring in September. It doesn't ring early. It rings early when it's hungry, and it's hungry early because —"

"Because it's already called eight," Orsen said.

The words came out of her flat and certain, and Tam understood that she'd done the arithmetic days ago, in the parish records, alone, and had been carrying it the way you carry a diagnosis you haven't told the family yet.

"Eight Blackwoods," she said. "Four generations. Every one of them October the third. And every one of them — I checked the death registers against the hospice pattern — every one of them would have been counting. The bell calls the bloodline, one by one, and it counts them down, and the ledger —" her voice caught, and she steadied it with the terrible professional calm he'd seen her wear in the village, the calm that was armor and nothing else, "— the ledger below has eight names in eight niches and a ninth niche cut for me, cut *clean,* cut by someone who knew stone, and the bell is ringing early because the ninth name is due and it knows it. It's not counting down to October the third, Mr. Osgood. October the third is when the count *ends.* The count has already begun."

Tam said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't the next line of the confession, and the next line was the one he had sworn on his father's grave he would never say, and he stood on the ringing bog with his lantern going blue and his family's sin in his throat like a swallowed stone.

The bell tolled a second time.

He hadn't thought it would. Thirteen years of once-a-year tolls, and now two in one night, two in ten minutes, and the silence afterward was deeper than before, absolute, the whole marsh holding its breath, and Orsen turned to him and her iron eyes were wide and wet in the lantern light and she said, very quietly:

"It's counting *faster.*"

And Tam Osgood, keeper of Hollowmere, fourth of his name to hold the iron key, heard himself say the thing he'd walked out here to say and had hoped all the way out here he'd find the courage to withhold:

"Then there's less time than I thought. There was supposed to be a year. There was supposed to be a taking on the third and then quiet until next October, that's the shape of it, that's the *lease* — but if it's counting fast, then it's not waiting for the third, and if it's not waiting for the third, then the bargain doesn't hold, and if the bargain doesn't hold —" He stopped. His hands were shaking. He wrapped the rod in its sacking with fingers that would not obey him. "Orsen, there are things I've done to keep that bargain. Things you'll need to know before you decide anything, and you'll hate me, and you should, but you need to know them *before* the third, because —"

He stopped again. Because out across the bog, in the direction of the village, in the direction of Wrenlow End and the bakehouse and the small upstairs room where his daughter slept, a light had come on. One window, yellow and small and impossibly far away, and even at this distance Tam could see the shape of it: Bree's kitchen. And beneath his feet, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the ground was humming — not tolling now, not striking, just *humming,* continuous, patient, a note held the way you'd hold a hand over a candle flame to feel how close it was —

— and the note was getting louder.

Orsen felt it too. He saw it hit her through her boots, saw her look down at the black water standing in the pools, each pool shivering now, each reflection trembling as if the fallen sky had begun to shake.

"It's not calling me," she said. And her voice, when she understood, broke open like ice. "Tam. It's not calling toward the house. It's calling *from* it. Something in the house is — someone in the house is *answering* it."

Tam was already running. The lantern swung wild, throwing their shadows huge and broken across the peat, and behind them the bog rang hollow under every footfall like the skin of a drum, like the body of a bell, like a ledger whose pages had begun, at last, to turn.
