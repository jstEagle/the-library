## 8. What the Bell Wants

The attic stairs were where the house stopped pretending to be a house.

Elodie had been in old attics before — every appraiser had — and they all shared a grammar: the dead air, the insulation dust, the boxes of someone else's Christmas. Hollowmere's attic stairs had none of that. The air at the top was warm the way breath is warm, and it moved. Not a draft. A rhythm. In and out, slow and patient, as though the whole floor above Elodie's head were a chest that had been breathing for a hundred and thirty years and saw no reason to stop now.

They stood at the bottom of the stairs with one hand on the rail and told themselves the same thing they'd told themselves on the porch yesterday, and in the kitchen this morning, and in the nursery with its sound older than recording: *You are here to work.* Measurements. Photographs. The roofline needed documenting; the attic square footage would affect the listing price. There was a number at the top of these stairs. Numbers were safe. Numbers didn't look back.

The salt sat in their jacket pocket, a paper twist of it, absurd and comforting, like carrying a rabbit's foot into surgery.

"Square footage," they said aloud, and their voice came out flat and professional and only slightly too loud. "That's all."

The stairs creaked under them in a pattern Elodie almost recognized — three quick, one slow, like a child taking them two at a time and then pausing out of habit. They did not let themselves finish the thought of whose habit it might have been.

The attic door was open. It had been painted shut once — the paint seam was still visible, cracked and yellowed along the jamb — but it stood wide now, swinging gently inward, and there was no wind anywhere in the house.

* * *

The attic was not an attic.

Or rather, it was, technically — sloped rafters, plank flooring, a round window at each gable end letting in thin October light the color of dishwater — but the proportions were wrong in a way Elodie's professional eye kept snagging on. The room was bigger inside than the roofline allowed. They knew the exterior dimensions. They had measured them twice. This space should have been perhaps forty feet long, low-ceilinged, cramped. Instead it stretched away into a soft brown dimness, the far wall only a suggestion, and the ceiling arched overhead like the inside of a throat or a ribcage or a bell.

And there, in the center of the floor, hanging from a beam that had no business bearing its weight —

The bell.

It was smaller than Elodie expected. That was the first obscenity of it: after everything, after the charter's grand language and Ferro's gray face and the whispering walls, they had pictured something cathedral-sized, something that would take six men and a crane. This bell would fit in a bathtub. It hung from a leather strap gone black with age, green-bronze, mottled like the skin of something that lived underwater, and its surface was covered in engraving so fine and dense that from a distance it looked like texture, like corrosion, like nothing at all.

Up close, Elodie could see it was writing. Names. Thousands upon thousands of names, overlapping, layered, worn smooth in places and sharp in others, circling the bronze in bands like the growth rings of a tree. Some were deep — cut by hand, by ceremony, by weight of meaning. Others were shallow as scratches, as if whoever made them had been running out of time, or metal, or self.

And the bell was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not radiator-warm. Warm the way a held hand is warm, and when Elodie stood beneath it, close enough that their breath fogged the bronze, the warmth reached out and settled against their face like a palm cupping a cheek.

They stepped back. Their heel scraped the planks. The bell did not swing, but something about it *inclined* — a fractional tilt, a listening.

"Don't," Elodie told themselves, and meant *don't touch it*, and heard instead, in their own voice, the shape of a child being told not to touch the stove, which had never once worked.

Because here was the thing they couldn't stop noticing, the thing that made their skin crawl in a way that wasn't entirely revulsion: if you looked at the bell too long — if you let your eyes go soft the way you do when you're tired, when you stop looking *at* and start looking *into* — the curve of it resolved. The flare of the lip became a mouth. The crown became a brow. The whole bronze body gathered itself, faintly, terribly, into the suggestion of a face.

It was smiling.

Not grinning. Not leering. Smiling the way you smile at someone you've waited a long time for, someone you've set a place for at the table, someone whose coat you've kept because you knew they'd be back for it.

"Oh," Elodie said quietly. "Oh, you're worse than I thought."

* * *

They circled it. That was the professional instinct, and professionalism was the rope they were holding onto with both hands: photograph it, document it, note the condition (*bronze bell, antique, engraved, unusual patina, hangs from original leather strap — flag for specialist assessment*), and get out. The camera came up. The viewfinder framed the bell in a neat rectangle.

Through the lens, the smile was unmistakable.

Elodie lowered the camera. Their hands were steady — they noted this with distant surprise — but their mouth had gone dry as paper, and somewhere below the steadiness a voice was saying, very calmly, *you already know what this is, you've known since the height marks, since the porch, since the kitchen that smells of bread, you know and knowing has never once saved anyone in this town.*

"I'm not signing anything," they said to the bell. "I'm not speaking my name into anything. I read the file. I know what you are."

The bell said nothing, because bells don't talk. But the warmth deepened, and the attic breathed around them, and Elodie understood — with the clarity that comes in the moment before a fall — that the house had brought them up here on purpose. Every creaking stair, every unlocked door, every room that replayed its dead like a music box wound and waiting. All of it had been leading here. The house was not hiding its heart.

It was offering it.

*Don't touch it,* said the sensible voice, the fifteen-year-old voice, the voice that had catalogued rational explanations all night at the inn like a rosary: settling foundations, carbon monoxide, grief, suggestion, the simple human talent for scaring ourselves.

But another voice — older, seven years old, sitting cross-legged on a cellar floor next to a boy who wasn't real — said: *it's lonely up here.*

Elodie reached out and touched the bell.

* * *

The world went white, then went elsewhere.

A field. Wheat to the horizon, gold going amber in late-summer light, and Elodie was *in* it — not watching it, *in* it — sweat down the small of a back that wasn't theirs, hands calloused in a pattern they'd never earned, hauling the last sheaves before the storm that stood purple-black on the western sky. There was a wife on the porch, shading her eyes. There was a dog barking itself hoarse with joy. There was the smell of cut wheat and coming rain and engine oil, and underneath it all a feeling so enormous it had no name except *mine, this is mine, this harvest and this woman and this dog and this aching back, mine, mine* —

— and then the knowledge arrived, quiet as the rain arriving, that he was dying. That this was the last harvest. That the doctor's word was sitting in his breast pocket like a stone. And the man — Aldouse? No, older, older than the Aldouses, one of the first ones, a founder, a signer — the man walked his field and loved it so hard it hurt, and when the storm finally broke he stood on his porch and looked at what was his and thought: *I can't keep it. Nothing keeps. Unless —*

The bell took him. Elodie felt it happen from both sides at once — felt the man's surrender like a door opening, felt the bell receive him like a throat swallowing — and the harvest did not end. It never ended. It was in the bronze now, circling forever with the names, the wheat gold and the wife on the porch and the dog mid-bark, preserved not as memory but as *person*, digested, rendered down into feeling and poured into metal —

Elodie tore their hand away and staggered backward into a rafter, and the rafter was solid and real and splintered their shoulder blade, and they were grateful for the pain because pain was theirs, pain was proof of a body, pain was not 1893.

"That's—" They were breathing hard. The attic swam and steadied. "That's not storage. You don't *store* them."

The bell smiled its faint bronze smile.

"You eat them." The words came out hushed, horrified, and — this was the part Elodie would hate themselves for later — reverent. "You don't keep memories. You turn people *into* memories."

Their hand was still tingling. Still warm. Still reaching.

They hadn't decided to touch it again. That was the terrible part. Their arm simply rose, the way a sleepwalker's rises, drawn by the warmth, by the vast patient hunger wearing the shape of welcome — and the second flood took them before they could brace.

* * *

Water.

Green water, lake water, July water dappled with light like scattered coins, and Elodie was laughing — a girl's laugh, bright and bubbling, ten years old maybe, treading water off the wooden dock while her brother counted to a hundred on the shore. Her hair fanned out around her. The sun came down through the surface in pillars. She dove, and the world below was cool and endless and blue-green-gold, and she was happy, happier than she had ever been, this was the last summer, though she didn't know it yet, wouldn't know until the ice went thin in February and the lake took her the way lakes take children who love them too much —

— and the bell had been waiting even then, patient, ninety years patient, and when the cold closed over her it was not the lake that caught her but the bronze, and her last summer did not end. It circled. It shone. Somewhere in the engraving her name turned slowly among the thousands, and she was still diving, still laughing, still ten, forever, and Elodie could feel how *glad* she was, how unafraid, and that was the worst of it, that was the obscenity under the obscenity:

The taken ones weren't screaming.

They were *kept*. Held the way you hold a thing you love. The farmer harvested forever. The girl dove forever. The bell didn't torture them — it *treasured* them, the way a miser treasures coins, the way a collector treasures butterflies pinned through the thorax, beautiful and motionless and utterly, eternally unable to leave.

Elodie came back to themselves on their knees on the attic planks, gasping, lake water phantom-cold in their lungs, wheat dust ghost-sweet in their nose. Two lives. Two whole people, tasted in the space of a minute, and they could still feel them — the man's love for his field, the girl's joy in the water — sitting in Elodie's chest like swallowed stones, warm, indigestible, *not theirs*.

"This is the ritual," they whispered. The understanding assembled itself with horrible simplicity. "This is what the founders built. Someone speaks their name, and you — you take all of it. Everything they were. You render them down and pour them in and they live in the rings of your bronze forever, and the house stays alive, and the town stays — "

*Fed.*

The word arrived without a voice, without sound, settling into Elodie's mind the way warmth settles into a room: gentle, total, undeniable. Not a threat. An explanation. The bell had been starving for twenty-six years, ever since Margaret Aldouse stood in this attic and lost her nerve, and it was so hungry, and Elodie had come home at last, and wasn't it kind of them, wasn't it right, they had always belonged here, it was in the charter, it was in the blood —

"No." Elodie got a knee under themselves. Stood. The attic tilted and they refused to tilt with it. "No. I'm the appraiser. I'm here to put a *price* on you."

The warmth pulsed, amused, indulgent. As if prices were adorable. As if it had watched a thousand Montroses draw lines on paper and call it power.

Elodie backed toward the stairs, one step, two, keeping their eyes on the bell because turning around felt like the kind of mistake you don't survive. The smile followed them, faint and fond. At the top of the stairs they stopped, because stopping was necessary, because some part of them — the part that had invented a friend at seven, the part that had never stopped needing — wanted to say something true before they ran.

"It ate a little girl," they said. "She was happy and you ate her anyway. Whatever you offer, whatever you show me — I know what's under the smile now."

The bell's warmth flickered. Just barely. Like a lamp in a draft.

And in that flicker, in the half-second of the bell's attention turning elsewhere, Elodie heard it — faint, from below, from the floors and floors of rooms between here and the front door:

Footsteps on the stairs. Small ones. Three quick, one slow.

Coming up.

Wren's voice drifted through the attic door, mild as morning, patient as furniture:

"You touched it twice. He gets two tastes free. House rules." A pause, and then, closer, almost at the landing: "We need to talk about what happens on the third."
