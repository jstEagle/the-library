## 10. The Ritual, Fragmented

The Bellhaven Historical Society occupied the ground floor of what had once been a dry goods store, and it smelled the way all such places smell — of paper going quietly to dust, of pennies in wooden drawers, of time embalmed rather than lived. Elodie signed the guest book with a hand that did not feel entirely their own. The line above theirs was dated June 14th. No one had visited since.

"You'll want the founding materials," said the volunteer, a man named Cobb with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and the particular patience of someone who had spent decades waiting for questions no one asked. "Most people want the Aldouse disappearance. Newspaper clippings are in the second drawer." He studied Elodie for a moment over the rims. "You're not most people, though. I can always tell."

"Why can you tell?"

"Because you flinched when you walked past the bell case."

Elodie turned. Against the far wall, under glass, lay a brass door knocker shaped like an open hand, green with age, fingers curled as if it had been about to grasp something and been interrupted mid-reach. They hadn't flinched. They were almost sure they hadn't flinched.

"The charter," they said. "I need the 1893 charter. All of it. Every page, every attachment, everything the town filed and then decided to forget."

Cobb looked at them for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly, but recognition, the way a dog recognizes thunder before it arrives. He unlocked a cabinet at the back of the room and brought out a flat box wrapped in oilcloth, handling it the way one handles a sleeping animal.

"Seven families," he said, setting it down. "That's what everyone remembers. Seven families founded Bellhaven. What nobody remembers is why seven. A covenant needs witnesses, Miss Montrose. Or Mister. Forgive me." He slid the box across the table. "A covenant needs a keeper too. And keepers don't get to be remembered kindly."

* * *

The charter was written on vellum gone the color of weak tea, the ink brown and patient. Elodie read it twice at the society's scarred oak table while October light crawled across the floorboards like something cautious.

The language was legalistic, florid, nineteenth-century to its bones — but beneath the boilerplate about boundaries and mill rights ran a second current, a text within the text, the way a hymn has a melody and underneath it a drone.

*Be it known that the undersigned, being of sound mind and settled purpose, do covenant as follows: that the Town of Bellhaven shall endure, and that its memory shall not perish with its people, but shall be held forever in the Bronze Witness, cast this day from the rings of our marriages, which bind us as the bell shall bind the town.*

*And as the bell holds, so must it be fed. Memory does not persist of its own nature. It must be given.*

Elodie read that sentence four times. *It must be given.* Not taken. Given. The distinction seemed important in a way their skin understood before their mind did.

There were seven signatures. Six were names that meant nothing — Harrow, Pell, Osgood, Crane, Vane, and Aldous. The seventh stopped Elodie's breath in their throat.

*M. MONTROSE.*

Their own surname, in ink older than the electric light, older than the automobile, older than every explanation they had ever constructed. Their grandfather's father's father, or some branch of the family that had withered and dropped away, leaving only Elodie and a father who drank and a mother who left — and none of them had ever mentioned Bellhaven, had never spoken of any of this, and yet here it was, their name at the bottom of a covenant written in 1893, waiting for them the way the height marks on the doorframe had waited, freshly carved, patient as arithmetic.

"There was a Montrose," Cobb said quietly. He had drifted back without Elodie noticing. "Milo. First schoolteacher. The charter says he was the one who designed the ritual — the actual words of it. The others signed; he wrote." Cobb's throat worked. "Funny thing is, there's no record of him after 1894. No grave, no deed, no letter. Just the signature. Like he signed himself away along with everything else."

"Where are the words?" Elodie asked. "The actual words of the ritual. If Milo wrote them, where are they?"

Cobb hesitated. Then he reached into the box again and brought out a second item, smaller — a diary bound in cracked leather, its spine repaired with copper wire. A paper label, yellowed and curling, read *ALDOUSE — DO NOT CIRCULATE* in handwriting that had pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

"Margaret Aldouse copied what she could find," Cobb said. "She was the last keeper. She spent thirty years trying to understand what her family had inherited. And then in '98—" He stopped. His hands flattened on the table as if steadying it. "You know what happened in '98."

"I know the house went empty."

"I know what the police report says, if that's what you mean." Cobb took off his glasses and polished them on his sleeve, a gesture that bought him nothing. "Take the diary. Take it home, take it wherever you're staying. I've read it forty times and I've never slept right since, and I'm eighty-one years old and past caring about rules." He pushed it across the table. "But I'll tell you one thing first, because nobody told me, and I think that's why I never slept right. Whatever you find in there — whatever the ritual asks — remember that Margaret knew. She knew all of it, better than anyone ever has. And it still went wrong."

"How?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Elodie had seen in weeks. "Read to the end. She tells you how. She just doesn't know she's telling you."

* * *

The inn at night was a held breath. Mrs. Ferro had left a thermos of coffee outside Elodie's door without knocking, and beside it, folded once, a page torn from a spiral notebook in a hand that shook only slightly:

*Equinox is in three days. Thursday. You should know that before you read anything else. Count the days yourself. Don't trust the house to count them for you.*

Elodie sat on the bed with the diary open on their knees and the lamp turned high, and outside the window the maples dropped their leaves one by one onto the parking gravel, each landing soft as a footstep.

Margaret Aldouse wrote the way people write when they believe no one will ever read them — no performance, no posture, just the raw accounting of a mind working in real time. The early entries were domestic, almost boring: prices of feed, a daughter's fever, complaints about the furnace. But threaded through them, growing denser year by year, came the other subject. The house. The bell. The debt.

*March 1971. Found Harold standing in the attic at 2 a.m. He said the bell was warm. I touched it myself and it was warm, warm as a forehead, and Harold asked me who it reminded me of and I couldn't answer because it reminded me of everyone.*

*June 1974. Ruthie Ferro's sister went up the hill and didn't come down. The Ferros won't speak of it. Mrs. Ferro senior left bread and a photograph on the porch at the equinox and the bread was gone by morning and the photograph was face-down. Harold says we should move. Harold has said we should move for nine years. We both know we can't. I tried to explain it to him tonight — that you can't leave a place that has your name in its mouth — and he looked at me like I'd lost my mind, and maybe I have, but the house agrees with me, and that's worse.*

Then, beginning in the autumn of 1997, the entries changed. Shorter. Denser. Frightened in a new register — not the fear of a haunting but the fear of a deadline.

*October 1997. The children are hearing it ring. There is no wind. Daniel (9) drew a picture of the bell with a face on it and I burned the drawing and he cried and said I'd hurt it. I have begun copying Milo Montrose's papers properly, all of them, because I think we are near the end of something and I will not let my family be the ones who didn't read the instructions.*

*January 1998. I have the whole of it now. God forgive Milo Montrose for writing it and God forgive me for understanding it. It is so simple. That is the horror of it. All these years I imagined some terrible ceremony, blood and Latin, and it is nothing but a name. You stand before the bell at the turning of the season and you speak your true name — your full name, the one that is truest, the one that names what you actually are and not what you pretend to be — and you give it. Willingly. That is the whole mechanism. The bell cannot take. It can only receive. Everything it has ever gotten, it was given.*

*The house takes the rest — the memories, the years, the self — but only after the name opens the door. The name is the key we made in 1893. Milo understood that a person is mostly memory, and memory is mostly belonging, and belonging can be transferred if the owner consents. So the house endures, and the town endures inside it, and the keeper's line pays the toll.*

*Harold says he will do it. He says it every week now. He practices in the mirror. I have caught him saying his own name to his reflection like a prayer, and the reflection listens, and I am afraid of how much it seems to want to hear it.*

The final entries were March 1998. Elodie's hands had gone cold around the pages, and they made themselves slow down, made themselves read every word, because somewhere in the back of their skull a voice that sounded suspiciously like Wren was saying *you already know how this ends, you just don't know the details yet.*

*March 19th, 1998. Tomorrow is the equinox. Harold is ready. I am not. I have lied to him all winter — I told him I would stand with him, that we would give our names together, and I meant it when I said it, but I don't mean it now. I have sat with the diary open for three nights trying to make myself ready and I cannot. My name is mine. It is the only thing I have ever fully owned. Harold is braver than I am and I hate him a little for it.*

*March 20th, 1998. Equinox. Tonight. I have done a terrible thing and I am writing it down so that someone, someday, knows. When Harold spoke to the bell I put my hand over his mouth. At the last moment. He had said half his name — half, only half, "Harold Ald—" and I stopped him, because I could not bear it, because I realized in that instant that I would be alone, truly alone, and I chose myself. God help me, I chose myself.*

*But the bell had begun to receive. Half a name given cannot be taken back. The house is caught between feeding and fasting, and a thing caught between those two states does not die. It hungers. It will hunger forever now, and it will feed on whatever comes near, and it will not be able to stop, because we broke it mid-meal.*

*Harold is still in the attic. I can hear him saying his name over and over, trying to finish it, and the bell will not accept half of anything. The children are gone — I mean gone-gone, not dead-gone, there is a difference here and I understand it now and I wish to God I didn't. The house kept them. It keeps what it is given and it was given them, in a way, by all our years of letting it save our memories, our photographs, our voices in its rooms. We thought it was keeping them safe. It was keeping them.*

*I am going up now. I will finish what I started or I will fail completely, but I will not write another entry either way. To whoever finds this: the ritual is real, the price is real, and the only thing worse than giving your name is giving half of it. Do not hesitate at the door of yourself. Hesitation is how you make a monster out of a house.*

*M.A.*

The diary ended there. The last page carried a faint brown stain shaped like nothing at all, or like a hand.

Elodie sat for a long time in the lamplight, the diary closed on their knees, the inn silent around them except for the pipes ticking in the walls — except, they thought, except that pipes tick when water moves through them, and no one had run water in an hour, and the ticking had a rhythm, and the rhythm was almost speech, and they were not going to listen to it, they were absolutely not going to listen.

Three days. Mrs. Ferro had counted for them. Thursday. The equinox.

They laid the three sources side by side on the bedspread like a surgeon laying out instruments: the charter photocopied at the society, Cobb's key-ring flashlight still in their pocket from holding it over the vellum; Margaret's diary; and the note from Mrs. Ferro, which was more than a warning, though Elodie hadn't understood that until now. *Count the days yourself.* Ferro had been paying tithe on this thing for forty years. Ferro knew the calendar the way farmers know frost.

The pieces fit with a horrible neatness. The founders built the bell to hold the town's memory, and memory must be fed, and the food is a name — a true name, wholly spoken, willingly surrendered, at the turning of the season. The keeper's line provides. The Montroses had signed first and vanished first. The Aldouses had kept until 1998, when hesitation had broken the mechanism mid-swallow and left Hollowmere starving for twenty-six years. And now the house had summoned the last Montrose home to appraise it for sale, of all things, as if the ledger itself had sent an invoice.

And Wren. Wren who waited on porches. Wren who answered questions with questions. Wren who had been a boy of ten for twelve unchanging years. The taken children, Margaret had written. *The house kept them.* Kept for what? Kept for whom? Kept, perhaps, the way a shopkeeper keeps a display — arranged, lit, presented to whoever walks in. Elodie thought of Wren's smile on the porch swing, tender and terrible, and understood for the first time that the smile might not have been Wren's choice either.

They got up and stood at the window. Across the dark fields, on the hill, Hollowmere House showed no lights, but it showed something — a faint paleness at the attic gable, like a face pressed to glass from inside, or like bronze catching moonlight, or like nothing, like exhaustion, like a trick of eyes that had read too long by lamplight.

Three days. And the ritual required a resident — that word again, *resident*, not victim, not sacrifice, resident, someone who belonged — to stand before the bell and open the door of themselves and pour out everything they were.

Elodie looked at their hands in the window glass, ghost-pale, doubled by the reflection. Whose name would they even give? They tried to say their own full name aloud, the one on their passport, the one on the appraisal contract in the car — and found their mouth hesitating around it, the syllables suddenly strange, like a word in a language they used to speak.

Behind them, very softly, the pipes ticked three times.

Thursday. Two days after tomorrow. The clock had started, and it sounded, Elodie thought as they finally lay down without turning off the lamp, exactly like footsteps coming up the stairs of a house they hadn't sold yet.

They dreamed, that night, of a doorframe with fresh-carved marks on it, and a voice reading measurements aloud in the dark — and the voice was their own, and it was counting down.
