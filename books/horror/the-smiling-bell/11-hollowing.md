## 11. Hollowing

Elodie woke the way a diver surfaces — too fast, lungs first, the world arriving in pieces. Gray light through unfamiliar curtains. The inn's ceiling, water-stained into shapes like continents. The smell of Mrs. Ferro's coffee drifting up through the floorboards, cinnamon and something burnt.

They lay still and did the morning inventory, the ritual they'd performed every day since they were nineteen years old: name, city, occupation, phone, keys.

Name: Elodie Montrose.

City: —

The gap opened under them like a trapdoor.

Not *Boston*. Not *Chicago*, not any of the cities they had lived in during those four years that were supposed to have happened. They could feel the shape of the missing thing — four years wide, dormitory-shaped, lecture-hall-shaped — but when they reached into it there was nothing to hold. It was like trying to remember a room they'd only ever seen in a photograph someone else had taken.

*My college years,* Elodie thought, sitting up too quickly, and the thought came out wrong, came out in a voice that wasn't quite theirs:

*I was happy then.*

Were they? They tried to find the evidence. A roommate's face. A final exam. A hangover, a first kiss, a thesis defended badly in April. Nothing. The years existed as a summary, a paragraph in their own biography, and paragraphs could be written by anyone.

Elodie got up, and their legs felt borrowed.

* * *

In the bathroom mirror, they brushed their teeth and watched themselves do it, and it took three full minutes before they saw what was wrong.

The reflection lagged.

Half a second — no more. When Elodie lifted the toothbrush, the reflection lifted it a beat late, a film running one frame behind the projector. When Elodie stopped moving and held perfectly still, staring, the reflection went on moving for just a moment longer. Finishing the gesture. Settling.

Elodie put down the toothbrush very slowly.

The reflection put its toothbrush down very slowly, and then — this was the part that made the cold start at the base of the skull and pool downward — it looked at them. Not with them. At them. A half-second of eye contact across a gap that should not have been able to exist, because a mirror is not a window, a mirror is not a door, a mirror is a surface, physics says so, physics has always said so—

*It's tired,* said a voice in Elodie's head, reasonable, soothing, their own voice wearing its most competent tone. *You're sleep-deprived. You've been sleeping in a haunted house's zip code for four days. The visual cortex does strange things under stress. There are papers on this.*

There were papers on this. They were almost sure there were papers on this. But the certainty had the same texture as the memory of college: a summary, a citation without a study, a shelf with no books on it.

Elodie leaned close to the glass. Their breath fogged it. The reflection's breath fogged it half a second later, a little ghost exhaling after the living one, and in that half-second delay Elodie saw — or thought they saw, or needed to see — the reflection's mouth move. Shaping words. Two syllables.

It might have been *help me.*

It might have been *too late.*

* * *

Downstairs, Mrs. Ferro was wiping down the front desk with the grim thoroughness of a woman scrubbing at something no cloth would ever reach. She looked up when Elodie came down, and her eyes did the thing they'd started doing on the second day — flicking to Elodie's face, then away, then back, like someone checking a patient for symptoms.

"You look pale," she said.

"I didn't sleep."

"Nobody sleeps well in Bellhaven." She poured coffee without being asked, slid it across. "That's not a complaint about the beds."

Elodie wrapped both hands around the mug because their fingers wouldn't stop trembling and they didn't want her to see. "Mrs. Ferro. Can I ask you something strange?"

"I'd be disappointed if you didn't."

"When you were young — do you remember your college years? Clearly? Like, all of them?"

Ferro's rag stopped moving. For a long moment she looked at Elodie with an expression Elodie couldn't parse — pity, maybe, or recognition, or the particular grief of someone watching a road they've already driven.

"Eat something," she said finally. "And don't go back to that house today."

"I have to finish the appraisal—"

"The house will still be there tomorrow." She said it flatly, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "That's the problem. It's always still there tomorrow."

* * *

But Elodie went back. Of course they went back. Because the alternative was sitting in the inn's yellow parlor cataloguing everything they couldn't remember, and the house, whatever else it was, at least let them move while they fell apart.

They told themselves it was the appraisal. The Henderson firm wanted the report by Friday. Depreciation schedules didn't write themselves. The lies had gotten comfortable; they came out now with almost no friction at all, smooth as river stones.

Hollowmere stood in the gray noon light looking, for once, almost ordinary. Almost just a house. The porch swing hung motionless. The windows reflected clouds.

Elodie made it as far as the foyer before the floor spoke.

Not creaked — *spoke.* The boards under their feet shifted in a rhythm they recognized from somewhere deep, a pattern of pressure and release, and it took them four full seconds to place it: it was the rhythm of their own breathing. The house was breathing with them. Or they were breathing with the house. The distinction had stopped meaning anything.

*You're losing it,* they told themselves, and the thought arrived in first person, which was new, which was wrong, which was — 

*I'm fine.*

Who said that?

Elodie stood in the foyer of Hollowmere House and listened to two voices live inside their skull, both claiming to be them, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling that one of them had always lived here.

* * *

Wren was sitting on the stairs.

He wasn't hiding anymore. That was the worst of it — he'd stopped pretending to appear and disappear, stopped the theatrical business of being found only in mirrors and peripheries. He sat on the seventh step, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, a boy of about ten in clothes thirty years out of fashion, and he watched Elodie come in the way you'd watch weather arrive.

"You're smaller," Elodie said. Their voice came out hoarse. "Than yesterday."

"I'm exactly the size you need me to be." Wren tilted his head. "You're the one who's getting smaller."

"Don't." Elodie gripped the strap of their bag. "Don't do the cryptic thing. I woke up this morning and I can't remember college. Four years, Wren. I can't remember my roommate's face. I can't remember—" Their voice cracked and they hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it. "What is happening to me?"

Wren unfolded himself and came down the stairs step by step, and each step made no sound at all, which Elodie's hindbrain registered as a fresh horror filed neatly beside the others.

"Sit down," he said gently.

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit down."

Elodie sat down. On the bottom stair, bag clutched to their chest like ballast, in the house they had fled at fifteen and returned to at twenty-seven to measure for sale.

Wren sat beside them. Close enough that if Elodie turned their hand, their knuckles would brush his shoulder. He smelled, faintly, of pencil shavings and autumn — the exact smell of being seven years old and safe, and Elodie despised how much their body relaxed at it.

"Do you know what a ledger is?" Wren asked.

"A record. Debts and payments."

"Mm." He picked at a splinter on the stair rail, and the splinter came away like it had been waiting for him. "Everyone thinks a house holds furniture. Chairs, tables, boxes in the attic. But a house really holds *belonging.* Everything anyone ever left here on purpose or by accident. Every Christmas morning. Every slammed door. Every secret told into a pillow because the walls seemed kind that night." He looked over. "Your walls were very kind, Elodie. You told them everything."

"I told you everything," Elodie corrected. "You were my friend. I invented you."

"You invented me," Wren agreed, mild as milk. "Out of what, though? Children invent friends out of nothing. Out of air. You built me out of everything you didn't want to keep. Every feeling too big for your body, you handed to me and I held it, and you got to be light enough to survive your father's good days and bad ones." His voice never changed temperature. That was the terrible trick of him — he could say anything in the voice of a boy explaining a card trick. "I was your denial, Elodie. I was where you put it. And the house —"

"—the house feeds on what you refuse to claim," Elodie whispered.

The words came out of them in Wren's cadence, in Wren's gentle rhythm, and they realized they'd heard them before — in dreams, in the whispering of the cellar walls at fifteen, in the half-second lag of their own reflection. The sentence had been waiting inside them like a seed.

Wren smiled, and it was the old smile, the seven-year-old smile, the one that used to make the dark cellar feel like a fort.

"Now you're getting it," he said. "Every year you didn't come back, you left more of yourself here unclaimed. Unclaimed things don't disappear, Elodie. They just stop being yours. And the house keeps them. It keeps them warm. It keeps them *ready.*" He spread his small hands. "You think you lost four years of college overnight? You lost them twelve years ago, a little at a time, every time you decided not to think about Bellhaven. Every time you chose the explanation over the memory. The house has just finished counting."

"That's not — memory doesn't work like that. Gaps in memory are normal, trauma causes—"

"Say your mother's voice."

Elodie opened their mouth.

Nothing came. Not silence exactly — worse than silence. The knowledge that there had been a voice, warm or sharp or singing, low or high, some specific human sound that had sung them to sleep and called them in from the yard — and underneath the knowledge, a hole shaped exactly like her. They reached for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and the reaching itself hurt, a muscular ache behind the eyes.

"Say your old address," Wren said softly. "The one before Hollowmere. Before you were seven."

A street name. Any street name. A number. A color of door.

"Stop," Elodie said. "Stop it."

"What did you want to be? When you were eight? Nine? Ten? Before you decided wanting things was dangerous?"

"*Stop.*"

Elodie was standing. They didn't remember standing. Their hands were shaking and their breath was coming fast and the foyer was breathing with them again, slow and patient, and Wren remained seated on the stairs looking up at them with an expression that was not triumph, which somehow made it infinitely worse. It was tenderness. It was the tenderness you'd give someone at a bedside.

"I'm not doing this to you," he said. "I need you to hear that. I'm not taking anything. I have never once taken anything. I *held* things. That was my whole job, for twelve years, and before you, for other children, for a hundred years." He rose, slowly, a boy-sized figure casting a shadow too long for the light. "The house isn't stealing from you, Elodie. It's collecting. There's a difference. Stealing takes what you want to keep. Collecting takes what you've already thrown away."

"I haven't thrown away my *life.*"

"You threw away the parts that hurt. That's most of it. That's nearly all of anybody." Wren walked past them toward the kitchen doorway, and paused there, silhouetted against the gray light. "You want to know the cruelest thing the house ever learned? It learned that people will surrender anything rather than feel one true thing all the way through. You've been paying it in installments your whole life. Denial is a currency here. It spends beautifully."

* * *

*I am sitting on the stairs of a dead house talking to a dead boy and the scariest part is that his voice is the kindest one I've heard in years.*

*I can't remember my mother's voice. Write that down. Put it in the report. Condition of property: one owner, partially repossessed.*

*If I can't remember college, what else is gone? What did I trade? Was there a wedding? Will there be? Is there a version of me in a city somewhere right now wondering why she feels hollow, thinking it's the job, thinking it's the season, thinking it's anything but a house in Vermont holding her missing pieces in rooms kept warm—*

*My hands are mine. Check. My name is mine. Check. My name is—*

*Check again.*

* * *

Elodie found themselves in the kitchen without deciding to go there. The room was warm. It was always warm. On the counter, impossibly, sat two glasses of milk and a plate of cookies cut in the shape of stars, and Elodie laughed — a short, broken sound, the laugh of someone finding a landmine charming.

"He used to leave these," they said aloud, to no one. "After Dad's bad nights. Milk and cookies in the kitchen at 2 a.m., and he'd swear he didn't know how they got there, and I'd swear I believed him."

The house hummed around them, pleased.

"No," Elodie said, louder. "No, you don't get to be sweet. You don't get to feed me. I know what you're doing. You're making yourself easy to belong to."

The hum didn't change. That was the horror of it — no guilt, no shift, no flinch. Just warmth, and the smell of bread no one had baked since 1954, and the patient arithmetic of a ledger that knew, better than they did, exactly how much of them was still on deposit.

Their phone buzzed. Mrs. Ferro: *Did you go back?*

Elodie typed: *Yes. I'm okay.*

Then stared at the message, because they had no idea whether it was true, and because the thumbs that typed it had moved with a fluency that felt slightly ahead of them, half a second ahead, a reflection leading instead of lagging.

A third text arrived, unsent by anyone:

*Three days until equinox.*

Elodie looked up sharply, but the kitchen was empty except for the cookies and the warmth and the long shadows climbing the walls like slow water. The message sat on the screen, gray-bubbled, from no contact, from no number.

From the house. From the bell. From whatever kept the books.

Elodie set the phone face-down on the counter with great care, the way you set down something that might be listening — knowing, even as they did it, that everything here was listening, that listening was the house's oldest profession — and walked to the hallway mirror to check the lag again.

The reflection was already looking at them when they arrived.

It hadn't waited. It hadn't followed. It had simply been there, facing the hall, watching the empty staircase, and now it turned — smoothly, unhurried, half a second early — and met Elodie's eyes with an expression of quiet, terrible recognition.

Like a keeper checking on a deposit.

Behind Elodie, at the top of the stairs, small and patient as furniture, Wren said:

"There's something you should read. Margaret Aldouse left a diary. It's in the parlor, under the loose board." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped its gentleness for the first time, gone thin and urgent, a boy's voice scared of the dark he lived in. "She tried what you're going to try. You need to read what happens to people who hesitate."
