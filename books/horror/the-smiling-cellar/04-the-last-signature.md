## 4. The Last Signature

The count reached forty-one at sunset.

Jude knew this because they had stopped pretending to do anything else. The paperwork for the new wing sat untouched on the desk in the night office, the sister's solicitor's letters sat unopened in the drawer where Jude had shoved them, and Jude themselves sat in the green vinyl chair beside Edith's bed with a cup of tea going cold, listening.

Edith counted the way she did everything now—slowly, with great dignity, as though the numbers were guests she was seeing off one at a time. *Forty. Thirty-nine.* Her hands lay folded on the coverlet like two small birds that had finally agreed to stop arguing. Her eyes were closed, but Jude had learned over these long weeks that closed eyes meant nothing. Edith saw plenty.

"You've gone quiet," Edith said, between thirty-six and thirty-five. "Thinking again. I can hear it. It sounds like a kettle deciding."

"I'm thinking about tea," Jude lied gently.

"Mm. And I'm the Queen of Portugal." A pause. *Thirty-four.* "You're thinking about the door."

Jude didn't answer, which was its own answer. Below them—two floors down, past the laundry, past the old kitchens nobody used—the cellar door stood open. It had unbolted itself sometime around four o'clock that afternoon, with a sound Sister Amity described as "a very polite gunshot," and no one had gone near it since. The excavation crew had packed up their tools and left without being asked. Even Maren—especially Maren—had found urgent business elsewhere, her solicitor's phone calls suddenly about a flat in Leeds instead of a sale in Marrowfield.

*Thirty-two.*

"The ledger's still on your lap," Edith said. She hadn't opened her eyes. "I can smell the old paper. Like churches and pennies."

"It is."

"Read me the part again. The part you keep reading when you think I'm asleep."

Jude looked down at the founders' ledger, open across their knees to the page they had worn soft with handling. The ink had gone brown as dried blood, but the words were clear enough, written in a hand that trembled only once:

*A house is not walls. A house is a promise, kept aloud, by blood or by love, before witnesses living and otherwise. Let the heir speak the names. Let the dead be claimed. What is named cannot be lost.*

Below it, the ritual stopped mid-sentence. Whoever had been writing had set down the pen and never picked it up. There was a stain on the corner of the page—tea, or something older—and beneath the unfinished line, in different ink, smaller, almost shy, someone had written: *we were afraid.*

Everyone was always afraid, Jude thought. That was the whole history of Marrowfield House in six words.

*Twenty-nine.*

"Read it," Edith said. Not a request.

So Jude read it. Aloud, in the lamplight, while the rain came soft against the windows and the whole house seemed to lean in. When they finished, Edith was quiet for a long moment, and then she laughed—a dry, delighted sound, like pages turning.

"They wanted an heir," she said. "*Blood*, the ledger says. Blood of Coldharbour. And all along it was you, sitting right there, hiding in plain sight, making yourself so small and useful nobody thought to look." *Twenty-seven.* "You're very good at not being looked at, Jude Coldharbour. Twenty years of practice. You could teach a masterclass. I'd attend. I'd bring biscuits."

"That's not—" Jude started.

"It is exactly what you did." Edith opened her eyes then, and they were clear, terribly clear, the lucidity that came at the end like a candle flaring before it went out. "I watched you do it for years. Slip along the corridors. Agree with everyone. Be no one's particular person so you could be everyone's. You thought it was peace." *Twenty-five.* "It was a locked door, love. And you bolted it from the inside."

The words landed the way true things land—heavy, and cold at first, and then warm, because they were true. Jude thought of all the years of practiced lies, the soothing nonsense offered at bedsides, the careful professional distance, the way they had learned to be furniture with a pulse. They had called it survival. They had even, some nights, called it contentment.

And underneath it all, the whole time, something below the floorboards had been counting with the dying. Knocking. Scratching. Patient as winter. Not explanations. Invitations.

*Twenty-three.*

"What happens if I don't go down?" Jude asked. It was the question they'd been carrying for three days, and Edith was the only person left to ask.

Edith considered this with the seriousness she gave to crossword clues. "Then the papers get signed. My sister-in-law's lot will have the house by spring. They'll put up flats with names like 'The Old Rectory Mews' even though it was never a rectory, and the dead under the floor will go on counting toward a door no one opens, forever, which sounds to me like the definition of hell." *Twenty-one.* "Or you go down there, you finish what those frightened old founders couldn't, and you say the thing out loud."

"And if I say it wrong?"

"Then you'll have said it," Edith said. "Which is more than most people manage."

* *

Nineteen. Eighteen. Seventeen.

Jude made the tea fresh—there were standards—and they drank it together, Edith taking small sips between numbers, complaining that Jude still let it steep too long, and Jude promising to do better, and both of them knowing neither thing would ever change now. It was, Jude thought later, the best conversation of their life, and it consisted mostly of tea criticism and counting.

At twelve, Edith asked Jude to open the window a crack, because she liked to hear the rain properly. At nine, she asked whether the night nurse had remembered her blanket from home, and Jude fetched it, the crocheted one with the hole in the corner that Edith refused to let anyone mend. At seven, she said, "Don't look so stricken. Everyone does this. I'm just doing it out loud, like a civilized person."

At five, she took Jude's hand. Her grip was still strong. Nurses' hands, or teachers'—hands that had spent a lifetime holding things steady.

"Four," she said. "Now listen. Whatever's down there—it isn't a monster. Monsters don't wait. Monsters don't knock politely for sixty years. Whatever it is, it's lonely, and it's been keeping your seat warm." Three. "Go and be somebody's family, Jude. You're overdue."

Two.

Jude stood. Their legs felt like borrowed equipment. In the doorway they turned back, and Edith smiled at them—the same smile, Jude realized with a jolt, that had appeared in the dust below the floorboards, patient and knowing and fond.

"One," said Edith Coldharbour's neighbor of eleven years, and closed her eyes as though settling in for a nap, satisfied.

Jude went down.

* *

The stairwell smelled of rain and brick and, faintly, of tea. Of course it did. Everything at Marrowfield eventually smelled of tea; it was the house's native atmosphere.

The door stood open at the bottom, black and patient, the bolts lying on the floor like shed teeth. Beyond it, darkness—but not empty darkness. Jude had spent twenty years learning the acoustics of rooms, and this room was full. Full the way a church is full even when empty. Something in there had been waiting a very long time, and it was not breathing, because it had no need of breath, and it was watching, because it had always been watching, kindly, the way family watches through windows when you come up the drive.

Jude stood on the threshold with the ledger under their arm and did the bravest thing they had ever done, which was nothing. They waited. They let the silence happen. They let themselves be seen—not the invisible nurse, not the careful neutral presence, but Jude, whole and terrified and willing.

Then they spoke.

"I know what the ledger says," they said, and their voice echoed strangely, as though the room repeated it in many voices, some high, some low, some very old. "It says blood. It says heir. It says Coldharbour, and I am one—I checked, I checked until my eyes hurt, the founders' line runs straight down to me, they built this place around me like a trap." A breath. "But the ledger also says *by blood or by love*. That's written there. Same page. Same hand. They just got scared before they could choose."

The dark did not withdraw. If anything it leaned closer, attentive, the way Edith leaned closer when a story got good.

"So here's my signature," Jude said. "Not mine. Hers. Edith Wren, who counted like a civilized person. And Nora Pell, who sang to her tomatoes. And Mr. Aldous, who hid his brandy in the hymnal. Every name in every admission book in this house, back to the first—every soul who ever lay in these beds and counted backwards toward a number no one reaches. I name them. All of them. Not residents. Not patients. *Kin.* Family, given freely, witnessed by the living and the otherwise. The bloodline requirement is broken. It ends with me, and it ends tonight, because love doesn't need a pedigree."

They opened the ledger to the unfinished page, took out the pen they'd brought—Edith's own, the good one with the dented cap—and wrote beneath *we were afraid*:

*We are not afraid anymore. Named and kept. — J.C., last heir, first kin.*

The silence that followed was enormous.

Then, softly, all through the dark, the counting stopped. Not faded—stopped, the way a held note stops, complete. And Jude heard, unmistakably, from everywhere and nowhere, a sound they had heard at a hundred bedsides and never from below the floor: a sigh of release, long and slow, like a house exhaling after decades of holding its breath. Somewhere in the deep, bricks settled comfortably. Somewhere, a smile.

The cold went out of the air. What remained was warmth, and the smell of rain, and—faintly, absurdly, wonderfully—the smell of fresh tea.

* *

Jude climbed the stairs slowly, and met the dawn coming gray and gentle through the east windows. Upstairs, Edith had gone with the last number, peaceful, her face turned toward the window and the rain, the crocheted blanket pulled up to her chin. On the bedside table, someone had arranged her things with unusual care, and beside the cold tea sat the sister-in-law's contract, delivered by courier the evening before and forgotten.

Jude picked it up, carried it to the washroom, and fed it page by page into the sink, where the ink ran in blue rivers and the paper went soft as skin. Unsigned. Unsignable. The house was not for sale. The house had never really been for sale; it had been waiting for someone to claim it back.

Maren would rage. The solicitors would write letters. Let them. Jude stood at the window of the night office as the light strengthened, and for the first time in twenty years they did not make themselves small. They were visible. They were known. The dead below the floorboards knew their name and had, apparently, always liked it.

On the ward, the new admissions would arrive by the weekend, frightened, counting nothing yet. Jude would sit with them. Would learn their jokes, their grievances, their people. Would be somebody's particular person, starting now.

And late that evening, doing one last round, Jude paused outside Edith's empty room and heard—from below, from everywhere, gentle as a page turning—one final sound. Not knocking. Not counting.

A smile, if a smile could be heard. Fond, and patient, and staying.

Jude smiled back at the floorboards, said, "Goodnight, family," out loud, and went to put the kettle on.
