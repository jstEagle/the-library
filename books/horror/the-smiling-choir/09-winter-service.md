## 9. Winter Service

The solstice arrived the way a debt collector arrives: without knocking.

Lena had not slept. She had not meant to. For six nights she had kept the fire fed and the mirrors covered and the last verse folded inside her like a letter she was afraid to open, and now the shortest day of the year bled its grey light through the frost-ferns on the kitchen window, and the ferns had changed overnight. They were no longer ferns. They were notation. Every pane in the house had become a page of sheet music, and the music was the hymn, and the hymn ended — she could see it now, could read it the way you read a language you never learned but somehow always knew — in her own handwriting.

That was the worst of it. The frost wrote in her handwriting. As if she had composed it herself. As if some part of her, some patient buried part, had been signing her name to this arrangement for twenty years.

She made coffee she didn't drink. She checked the recorder out of habit more than hope; the second voice had gone quiet two nights ago, after the last verse settled into her, after it had said — in Milo's cadence, in Milo's tired, wry, apologetic cadence — *that's all of it, Len. That's the whole ugly song.* Since then the tapes held only her own breathing, and even that sounded like someone else's.

At 3:41 in the afternoon, with the sun already drowning behind the western hills, the choir began to sing.

It did not come from the church. It came from everywhere — from the hedgerows, from the well, from the ground itself, from the spaces between the walls where insulation should have been. The harmony seeped through the plaster like cold seeps through stone, and Lena understood with a lurch that the walls were not keeping it out. They had never been keeping anything out. They were resonating. The whole house was an instrument, and Hollowmere had been playing it all along, and now it was being played *for* her.

She pressed her palms over her ears and the hymn came up through her arms instead. She went down to the cellar and the hymn was in the cellar, folded into the smell of cold earth and old apples. She climbed into the attic among her mother's boxed dresses and the hymn was there too, patient, gorgeous, forty voices braided into one voice that was not asking anymore. It had spent two hundred years asking. Now it was simply arriving.

*Come and be counted,* the harmony said, without words, without mouths, in the language of intervals. *Come and be counted. Come and be counted.*

Lena looked at her wrist. The frost-pattern there had completed itself sometime in the night, the final barline drawn neat as a signature, and the skin beneath it was cold — not frostbitten cold, not injured cold, but *belonging* cold, the cold of a thing that has found its place in a larger design.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on it, and the crack was the first honest sound she had made in days.

She would not wait in the house to be collected. Whatever the choir wanted, whatever the choosing meant, she would not be found in an armchair with her hands folded, humming along without knowing it. She had watched Milo's ghost spend twenty years trapped mid-hymn. She would not be caught mid-anything.

She put on her coat. She took the recorder — why, she couldn't have said; some instinct about witnesses, about evidence, about the difference between a story and a scream — and she walked out the front door into the blue-black cold.

* * *

The village was empty of everything except song.

Doors stood open. Ovens stood open, their warmth spilling uselessly onto doorsteps. A dog sat motionless in a yard, its breath hanging in the air in perfect unbroken columns, as if even its breathing had been conscripted. Every window in Hollowmere glowed with candlelight, and behind every pane a family stood facing outward, toward the churchyard, toward her, and none of their mouths moved, and all of them were singing.

The frost had written the hymn down the length of the high street. It ran along the gutters, up the lampposts, across the frozen ruts in the road — bar after bar after bar, two hundred years of verses, and Lena walked on top of the notation like a woman walking on a bridge over something deep.

The churchyard gates stood open. Of course they did.

The whole village was there, or the whole village's shapes were — forty in the choir loft and the rest arranged in the yard in concentric rings, faces upturned, eyes reflecting nothing. The reverend stood at the head of the open grave — *her* grave, the one with her name on the stone, the one that had been waiting, dug and dressed, since before she arrived — and when he saw her come through the lychgate he did not smile. He simply raised one hand, the way a conductor raises a hand, and the hymn shifted key.

Lena's feet wanted to keep time. That was the trap of it, the elegance of it: the melody was *kind*. It made room for her. She could feel the spaces in the harmony shaped exactly like her voice, gaps left open for twenty years, held open, held the way you hold a chair at a table for someone who is late. All she had to do was sing along. All she had ever had to do was sing along.

She stopped at the edge of the rings and made herself stand still, and made herself listen past the beauty to the structure underneath, and what she found there was hunger. Old hunger. Patient hunger. A hunger that had eaten one voice every winter since before anyone now living was born, and called the eating *survival*, and called the survival *grace*.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out small in all that harmony, and the harmony bent around it, absorbed it, made it almost a note. "You knew which twin. You knew from the moment I got off the bus. You weren't confused. You were *deciding*."

The reverend's mouth moved this time. "The song chooses," he said, in the tone of a man reciting a weather report. "We only keep the books."

"Then keep this one." She stepped forward, into the innermost ring, and the cold of the ground came up through her boots and into the pattern on her wrist, and somewhere behind her sternum the last verse turned over like something waking. "Keep the books on *this*."

She opened her mouth to sing.

* * *

The first note came out wrong, and she let it be wrong.

That was the whole discipline of it — the thing the sleep-voice had tried to tell her in the cellar-dark of the recordings, the thing she had only understood at three in the morning with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum: the last verse could not be sung *with* the choir. It could not even be sung *well*. The moment she harmonized — the moment she let the melody carry her, let her voice find its comfortable slot in the chord — she was theirs. The verse had to be sung alone, off-balance, cracking, ugly. It was not a verse that fit. It was a verse that *ended*.

So she sang it alone.

Her voice broke on the second line and she kept going. The frost on the gravestones shivered. The choir's harmony wavered like heat-haze, forty perfect voices suddenly unable to find the key she was standing in, because there was no key, because the verse was built out of the space between keys, the silence between notes, the small mortal imperfection that two hundred years of hymn had been smoothing away.

*Don't harmonize,* she told herself, and the thought had Milo's cadence now, had Milo's tired wry tenderness, and she realized with a grief that nearly stopped her throat that she had been carrying him with her into every line. *Don't harmonize, Len. You never could anyway. You always sang it your own wrong way and I loved you for it.*

The third line. The fourth. Her breath made ghosts in the air and the ghosts hung there in shapes like notation, and the frost on the high street — she could see it from here, could see the whole written length of it — stopped moving. Stopped spreading. A barline drawn across the lane like a scar, and beyond it the pattern simply ended, unfinished, abandoned, the way a hand stops writing when the writer dies.

The choir faltered.

It was not dramatic. That was what made it terrible. The forty voices did not scream or shatter; they simply lost one another, the way a family loses one another, one small drift at a time, and the harmony that had seeped through walls like cold now thinned into separate, ordinary, human voices, and some of those voices were weeping, and some were saying names — *Edith, Thomas, my girl, my girl* — names that had not been spoken in Hollowmere for a hundred years, names the hymn had been holding in its mouth like stones.

The reverend's raised hand trembled. "Stop her," he said, but softly, to no one, and no one moved, because there was nothing left in them that belonged to him.

Lena sang the final line.

Her voice cracked straight down the middle of it, and through the crack — through the ugly, unharmonized, mortal crack — a second voice rose out of her mouth. Milo's voice. Not the sleep-voice, not the thing that had worn his cadence like a borrowed coat, but *Milo*, whole and brief and unmistakable, one exhalation of him, the sound of a boy who had once deferred a debt twenty years because he loved his sister too much to let it land.

It said her name. Just that. *Lena.*

And then it went quiet.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of a room after a long illness ends — enormous, relieved, grieving. The frost on her wrist faded from the outside in, bar by bar, like a tide going out, and the last thing to go was the final barline, drawn in her own handwriting, unsigned now, cancelled.

Lena stood in the churchyard with her hands at her sides and her throat raw and the recorder still running in her coat pocket, and she felt the place inside her where the second voice had lived for twenty years go dark and still — not empty, not yet, but quiet, the way a house is quiet when the last guest has finally, finally gone home.

The choir did not sing again that night. The village did not move. Snow began to fall, unhurried, unwritten, falling on the open grave and the closed eyes and the woman standing alone at the center of it all, and somewhere far off — or perhaps not far off at all, perhaps only in the chamber behind her ribs — a single note faded out, and did not return.

Lena turned toward the lychgate. Behind her, she heard the first soft sound of thaw: a drip from the church eaves, falling into the snow.

It fell in rhythm.

It fell, she thought, almost in tune.
