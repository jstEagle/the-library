## 1. Something With Fingerprints

The call came in on a Tuesday, which Juniper Hartwell considered a small mercy. Tuesdays were for wasps. Wasps had rules. You knew where you stood with a wasp, which was usually at the end of a long pole with a can of foam on it.

"Scratching," said Mrs. Pettifer down the phone, in the tone of someone reporting a leaky gutter. "In the chapel walls. Behind the panelling, I'd say. My Harold says it's rats."

"And what do you say, Mrs. Pettifer?"

A pause on the line, long enough that Juniper could hear the kettle ticking toward boil in her own kitchen. "I say it's too regular for rats," she said at last. "Rats are frantic. This is... patient."

Juniper wrote *patient* in the logbook without thinking about it, and then looked at the word for a moment before closing the book.

Seven years they'd been Hollowmere's pest controller — the only one within forty miles, which was less a career than a geological fact. The village sat at the bottom of its valley like a coin at the bottom of a well, and everything that lived in the valley eventually came down to it, and everything that lived in the village eventually came to Juniper with a jar or a phone number or, once, a shoebox containing something they still declined to describe in the invoices.

It was good work, mostly. Quiet work. The kind of work where you could hum while you worked and nobody minded, because half of Hollowmere was deaf and the other half was polite.

They loaded the van. Gloves, torch, the good ladder, the boroscope camera on its little flexible snake of a neck. A tin of shortbread from the Co-op two villages over, because Mrs. Pettifer would offer tea and Mrs. Pettifer's tea was a punishment the village had collectively agreed never to discuss.

*Patient scratching,* thought Juniper, pulling out onto the lane. *That's a new one.*

Hollowmere in October was all wet stone and woodsmoke, the hills folded around it like hands cupped to keep a match alight. The chapel stood at the top of Church Lane behind a yew tree so old it predated the churchyard wall and possibly the concept of churchyards. Its flint face was dark with damp. Its one bell tower — squat, capped with slate, the louvres boarded over — rose above the roofline like a held breath.

Juniper had been in that tower exactly once, seven years ago, to clear a pigeon nest from the louvre frame. They remembered the job being unremarkable. They remembered the ladder, the dust, the smell of old lime. What they did not remember — though this seemed like the sort of thing a person ought to remember — was how they'd gotten back down.

Probably tired, they decided, as they did every time the thought surfaced. Probably just tired.

* * *

Mrs. Pettifer met them at the lychgate, wrapped in a coat that appeared to be made entirely of other coats.

"You came," she said, as if Juniper were a rare bird.

"You called."

"Yes, but people don't, usually. Not for the chapel." She said it lightly, the way you'd say *not for the tide*, and then she smiled and led the way up the path, talking about her knees, the price of oil, the scandal of the Hendersons' hedge, in the easy looping way she talked about everything, and by the time they reached the porch door Juniper had almost forgotten the word *patient*.

Almost.

Inside, the chapel smelled of candle wax and cold stone and, underneath both, something faintly organic — not rot exactly, more the smell of a room that had recently been full of people and wasn't anymore. The pews were bare. The hymn boards showed last Sunday's numbers: 214, 87, 402. Juniper had no idea whether those were real hymns. In Hollowmere, they'd learned, it was rude to check.

"Behind the panelling," Mrs. Pettifer said, pointing along the north wall, "from about there to about there. Mostly after dark. Though yesterday it started at four in the afternoon, bold as brass, right through the intercessions."

"Did it now," said Juniper, kneeling.

The panelling was Victorian oak, dark and dignified, fitted together with the confidence of an empire. Juniper ran their knuckles along it section by section, ear close, listening the way they listened to every wall in the valley — with the patient, professional attention of someone who has made a life out of taking sounds seriously.

There. Faint. Below the third panel from the east window.

Not gnawing. Not the dry scritch-scritch of mice dragging nesting material, nor the wet busy scrabbling of rats in a cavity. It was slower. Rhythmic. Almost like —

Like tapping. Like someone knocking politely, at intervals, on the inside of a door.

"Huh," said Juniper, and got out the boroscope.

The camera snake went in through a gap behind the skirting board, and Juniper watched the little screen as it threaded through darkness: plaster, brick, a dead spider wearing the expression of someone who'd seen things. Then a void — an old service gap between the chapel wall and the tower's base, running upward into blackness.

And on the screen, catching the camera's light, something pale and curved, low in the cavity near the floor.

"Bones?" asked Mrs. Pettifer, peering over their shoulder with the enthusiasm of a woman who watched a lot of television.

"Pigeon, probably," said Juniper, who genuinely believed this for another eleven seconds.

Because then the light swung up, following the cavity, and the tapping came again — three slow knocks, from somewhere above — and the whole length of the wall answered it. Not with sound. With movement. A shiver ran up through the panelling, panel by panel, like a hand drawn up a spine, and every hair on Juniper's arms stood up and pointed at the ceiling.

"That'll be the wind in the flue," said Mrs. Pettifer comfortably.

"There's no flue on this wall, Mrs. Pettifer."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "There isn't." And she smiled at them — a wide, pleasant smile — and went off to put the kettle on.

Juniper knelt there a moment longer in the empty chapel, listening to the silence where the tapping had been, and told themselves, as they had told themselves for seven years, that there was a perfectly good explanation and they would find it and write it in the logbook and invoice accordingly.

Then they went to look at the belfry door.

* * *

Everyone knew the belfry had been sealed since before the war. It was one of those facts villages keep the way trees keep rings — not discussed, just grown around. The bell itself had gone in 1911; parish records said melted down, though Juniper had never heard what for, and asking produced in villagers a curious blankness, like asking them the name of a cloud. The tower above the ringing chamber was shut up, the door painted over so many times it had become part of the wall, and the boarding over the louvres outside meant no bird had been in or out in living memory.

Which was why the grease stopped Juniper's heart for a full second.

The door was oak, iron-strapped, painted a brown so deep it was nearly black. And across its lower half, pressed into the paint as if into butter, were handprints.

Five of them. Splayed fingers, broad palms, each print larger than Juniper's whole hand spread wide — larger than any hand Juniper had ever seen on a human being. They were greasy, darkly glistening, as though whoever had made them had dipped their hands in tallow first. And they were on the *inside* of the door.

Juniper knew this the way you know your own front door: by the hinge side, by the grain, by seven years of walking past. These prints faced outward into the ringing chamber, but the pressure ridges, the drag-marks where heavy palms had slid slightly downward before lifting — they belonged to hands pushing *against* the door. From within.

The door was locked. Juniper checked, twice, with the little key the churchwardens kept in the vestry biscuit tin (next to the actual biscuits, because Hollowmere had priorities). The lock turned stiffly but truly. The door had not been opened. The paint over the hinges was unbroken, continuous, decades deep.

The prints had come through the paint. Or the paint had come up around the prints, the way water comes up around a drowning thing.

Juniper crouched down and did what they always did: took photographs, measured, noted. Palm width, thirty-one centimetres. Finger length, absurd. Grease sample scraped into a jar, labelled in careful block capitals: CHAPEL BELFRY DOOR — SUBSTANCE UNKNOWN — SMELLS LIKE CHURCH CANDLES AND MEAT.

It was while writing *meat* that the dream arrived, uninvited, the way it always did when Juniper got too close to something.

They'd had it since childhood — since before childhood, since whatever came before the adoption records began, in that grey country at the start of their memory where documents should have been. In the dream they were small, four years old maybe, standing in a stone room with a high ceiling, and above them hung a shape too large to see all at once, and the shape was warm, and the warmth had a rhythm like breathing, and small-Juniper was pressing both hands flat against a wooden door to hold it shut. Their hands were slick. When they woke, their palms always smelled faintly of candles.

Every few years the dream came, and every few years Juniper mentioned it to a doctor or a partner or nobody, and everyone agreed dreams were nonsense, and Juniper agreed too, because agreeing was easier than noticing that in the dream, the hands holding the door were far too big for a four-year-old child.

They were looking at those hands now. Thirty-one centimetres of palm, pressed into paint, from the inside.

The tapping started again — three slow knocks, directly overhead, beyond the sealed door, in a tower that contained nothing but air and boarded windows and a hole where a bell used to be.

Juniper stood very still in the ringing chamber, in the smell of candles and meat, and felt seven years of rational explanations shift quietly on their foundations, the way a house shifts when something large walks past outside.

"Rats," they said aloud, to the empty room, in a voice that didn't believe it.

The tower said nothing. But the silence had a quality Juniper recognized from the logbook, from seven years of entries — the quality of a sound that has stopped because it knows it's been heard.

* * *

Mrs. Pettifer was waiting in the porch with tea that could have stripped varnish, and shortbread Juniper had brought themselves, and a smile.

"Well?" she said. "Rats?"

"Something," said Juniper. "I'll need to come back with the big ladder. Get into the tower properly."

Something crossed Mrs. Pettifer's face then — quick, like a fish under ice — and was gone before Juniper could name it. "Oh, I wouldn't bother with the tower," she said lightly. "Nothing up there. Nothing at all. It's the walls you want, dear. Walls and floors. That's where things live."

She patted Juniper's arm with a hand that was very cold for October, and smiled her wide pleasant smile, and Juniper drove home with the windows down despite the cold, trying to breathe the meat-smell out of their nose.

That night they opened the logbook and wrote the entry. *Chapel, north wall, tapping, regular, patient. Belfry door: prints, interior face, oversized, greasy. Recommend further inspection.*

Then, below it, in smaller writing, the way you write something you're not sure you want to read later:

*Palm width 31 cm. Dream hands.*

They sat with that a while. Outside, down the valley, Hollowmere's streetlamps came on one by one, and the chapel stood black against the last of the sky, and if the tower had anything to say about it, it kept it to itself until morning.

At 3:14 a.m., Juniper woke with their palms pressed flat against the bedroom wall and no memory of getting there, and the wall beneath their hands was warm, and breathing, almost, in a slow patient rhythm, and somewhere far below — down the lane, down the hill, down through the wet stone — something knocked three times, gently, like a friend at a door.

Like someone keeping their place.
