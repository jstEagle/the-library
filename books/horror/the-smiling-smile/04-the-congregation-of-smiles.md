## 4. The Congregation of Smiles

The notice went up on the green the morning after the bell rang, which was itself a small miracle of village bureaucracy, because in Hollowmere a miracle usually took a fortnight and three committee meetings to become a notice.

**PARISH ASSEMBLY — THURSDAY, 7 PM — CHAPEL HALL**
*Subject: The Ringing. All households to attend. Tea provided.*

Juniper read it twice, coffee going cold in their hand. *Tea provided.* As if the subject line were a leaky gutter or the state of the footbridge. As if you could discuss a bell that had been molten slag for a hundred and thirteen years over digestives and a nice pot of Darjeeling.

They spent Thursday afternoon doing what they always did when Hollowmere frightened them, which was work. There was a wasp nest under the eaves of Fenwick's barn that needed smoking out, and Juniper smoked it out with the particular gratitude of a person who is relieved, for once, to be dealing with something that had the decency to be an actual insect.

"Odd business, that ringing," Fenwick said, watching Juniper lower the nest into the sack. He said it the way he might say *odd weather*. "My Bess heard her mother calling from the belfry. Been dead eleven years, her mother."

"What did you hear?" Juniper asked, because they couldn't help themselves.

Fenwick considered. "The milk churn," he said. "The one I lost down the well when I was a lad. Heard it rolling about up there like it wanted fetching." He smiled then, easy and wide, and something about the width of it made Juniper look away and busy themselves with the sack strings.

By seven, the chapel hall was full.

Juniper took a seat at the back, near the door, which was not cowardice but professionalism — pest controllers learn early that you want an exit mapped before the swarm starts. The hall smelled of damp wool and lemon polish and, underneath it, faintly, of hot metal, like a coin held too long in a fist. Someone had arranged folding chairs in rows. Someone had laid out tea things on a trestle table. Someone had put out a plate of Jammy Dodgers, and Juniper fixed their eyes on that plate for a long moment because it was the only thing in the room behaving normally.

Vicar Ambrose stood at the front beside a flip chart. A flip chart. Juniper felt a hysterical bubble rise in their chest — seven years in this village and they were finally going to see a flip chart, and it was going to be about the impossible bell.

"Right," said Ambrose, tapping his papers. "Thank you all for coming. Now. Tuesday evening's... event." He paused, and the pause had a strange quality, like a congregation waiting for an amen. "I've asked everyone to write down what they heard. Not to compare notes just yet — we'll do this properly. Mr. Quill has agreed to collect the slips."

Quill, the postmaster, moved along the rows with a biscuit tin, collecting folded papers. Juniper watched him go and noticed, in the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn, that nobody was talking. In seven years of parish assemblies — and there had been many, mostly about parking and once, memorably, about whether the allotment hedge constituted aggression — nobody had ever been quiet. Hollowmere argued the way other villages breathed.

Tonight they sat with their hands in their laps, and they smiled.

That was the first thing. They were all smiling. Not laughing, not chatting-with-a-smile-on, just smiling, steadily, at nothing in particular, the way people smile in photographs taken by someone who has said *hold it* and never said *done*.

Juniper told themselves it was nerves. A village rattled by a strange sound would look strange. Fair enough. They crossed their arms and settled in to watch the meeting like a man watching weather roll in, telling himself he'd seen worse.

Then Mrs. Petch turned to whisper something to her neighbor, and Juniper saw her teeth.

It shouldn't have mattered. Teeth are teeth. But Mrs. Petch was seventy-eight and had eaten a lifetime of toffees, and her teeth should have been a crooked, gappy, human disaster. Instead they were even. White. Set in a smile of exactly the same width as the smile on old Mr. Colley two rows down, whose own teeth were even and white, though Colley had been a pipe-smoker since the Coronation. Same width. Same curve. Same degree of lift at the corners, as if every mouth in the room had been hung by the same careful hand.

Juniper looked along the rows. Over forty — every single one of them over forty — wore the smile. It wasn't a rictus; it didn't look *wrong*, exactly, that was the horrible part. It looked right. It looked like the correct answer. The younger villagers — the Hendersons' boy, the schoolteacher, young Tam Oakes who worked the garage — sat fidgeting and glancing about like normal frightened people, and every so often one of them would catch the smile of an elder and go quiet and still, and after a while they'd stop fidgeting too.

*Everyone here is listening,* Juniper thought. *Not to the meeting.*

To something under the meeting.

Ambrose cleared his throat. "Now, the results are interesting," he said, unfolding slips of paper. "Mrs. Petch heard — let me see — 'her sister, singing.' Mr. Colley heard 'the harvest cart, on the road.' Mrs. Tallow—" he glanced at the slip and, oddly, put it face-down without reading it aloud, "—declines to share. And our own Juniper Hartwell writes only, 'the bell.'"

A ripple went through the room. Not laughter. Something more like satisfaction, moving through the rows like wind through wheat, and every one of those identical smiles widened by exactly the same fraction of an inch.

"The bell," Ambrose repeated gently. "Yes. Well. We know the bell was melted down in 1911. It's in the records. So whatever we each heard, it wasn't — strictly speaking — a bell." He smiled his own smile, and Juniper's stomach dropped, because Ambrose was fifty-three and his smile fit the pattern perfectly. "I'd like us all to agree that the matter is noted, and that we'll keep our ears open, and that there's no cause for alarm. Hollowmere has stood a long time. It knows how to keep its own counsel."

He said *keep its own counsel* the way you'd say a grace before meat, and the room murmured agreement, and the murmur was harmonized. That was the second thing Juniper noticed, and it was worse than the smiles. Two hundred voices saying *aye, aye, quite right* fell together into a chord — low, warm, consonant — the way a choir falls together, the way trained voices fall together, and no village hall in England had ever done that by accident.

Juniper's hands had gone cold around their paper cup of tea. They hadn't drunk any of it. They weren't sure they'd ever drink anything served in this room again.

* * *

There was a break for tea, because even eldritch dread must yield to scheduling. People rose and shuffled toward the trestle table, and Juniper stayed in their chair, running through explanations the way they'd run through them for seven years. Dry rot. Gas leaks. Mass hysteria — yes, mass hysteria, that was a real documented phenomenon, whole schools of fish turning at once, no reason a village couldn't—

"You're counting my neighbors' teeth."

Juniper nearly spilled the tea. Mrs. Tallow had appeared beside them without crossing any of the space in between, or so it seemed; she had a way of arriving like that, small and dry as a pressed flower. She lowered herself into the next chair with the care of someone assembling herself from parts.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Good. Means your eyes still work." She looked out at the hall, at the smiling elders clustered around the biscuits, passing the plate hand to hand. "Don't stare, though. They feel it when you stare. Like a draught."

"Mrs. Tallow." Juniper kept their voice low. "What happened Tuesday? What did everyone hear?"

"What did *you* hear?"

"The bell. Just — the bell. One note. Low. It went through my floorboards and up through the chair and into my back teeth." Juniper hesitated. "And underneath it, I think — humming. Like something humming along with itself."

Mrs. Tallow nodded slowly, and for a moment her face did something the others' faces didn't: it moved unevenly, grief pulling one corner down, and Juniper could have wept with relief at the sheer lopsided humanity of it. Then she smoothed it away, deliberately, the way you'd smooth a bedsheet.

"Aye. That'd be it finding its note again." She picked a bit of fluff off her sleeve. "You're new here. Seven years new, which is new enough. So I'll tell you the one thing worth knowing and then I'll tell you to forget it, and we'll both pretend I never spoke, same as I've done for seventy years."

The hall noise seemed to recede. The tea in Juniper's cup had gone mirror-still.

"There's a song," Mrs. Tallow said. "Older than the chapel. Four verses. The founding families used to sing it to the bell — that's what the bell was *for*, though the records won't say so. First verse is names. Second verse is thanks. Third verse is asking." Her jaw tightened. "Fourth verse is answering. And it isn't sung by the living."

"That's — Mrs. Tallow, that's a folk rhyme, that's—"

"It's a bill," she said, "that's been getting paid in this village since before your grandmother was born, and the reason Hollowmere's fields come up fat and its dead stay put and its griefs stay audible instead of howling in the streets. Everything costs. You knew that when you took this job. Rats cost. Wasps cost." She turned and looked at Juniper fully then, and her eyes were pale and clear and terribly tired. "You've been here seven years and you've heard the noises and you've written them down in your little book with your little explanations, chewing in the walls, humming under the floors, and you've slept fine. Do you know why you've slept fine?"

Juniper didn't answer.

"Because you hum," she said. "In your van. Doing your rounds. Little tunes, half-remembered, no words. Everyone hums. It's harmless — *nearly* harmless. But you've been keeping time for it, love. Seven years of keeping time. And now it's rung, and now it's noticed you keep good time, and tonight every soul in this room is smiling at you because you're the first fresh ear this village has grown in forty years."

Juniper laughed. It came out thin and stupid. "You're saying the village is — what? Bait?"

"I'm saying it's an audience," Mrs. Tallow said. "And audiences get hungry."

Across the hall, Vicar Ambrose raised his voice pleasantly — "If we could take our seats, there's just the one more item" — and the congregation turned, all together, all at once, two hundred heads rotating with the smooth synchrony of a flock, and every smile caught the light.

Mrs. Tallow's hand shot out and gripped Juniper's wrist. Her fingers were bird-boned and strong as wire traps, and cold — cold as bell-metal left out overnight. She leaned close, and her breath smelled of peppermint and something older, something scorched.

"Don't learn the last verse," she whispered.

"I don't even know the *first*—"

"*Don't.*" The grip tightened. "However it comes to you. However pretty it sounds. However much it feels like coming home." Her eyes flicked to the hall, to the smiles, then back. "Some songs you can walk away from mid-line. I'm living proof. But the last verse doesn't want your voice, child. It wants your *name*. And once it has learned yours off you, there's no walking anywhere ever again."

She released his wrist. Four small marks bloomed white and then pink on Juniper's skin, precise as fingerprints.

At the front, Ambrose tapped his papers. "Last item," he said warmly. "A vote of thanks. To our Juniper Hartwell, seven years of faithful service to this parish. I think we'd all like Juniper to stand up and be recognized."

The hall turned toward them. Every smile, aimed like lamplight.

Juniper stood, because refusing would have been stranger than standing, and heard themselves say, "Happy to help," in a voice that sounded borrowed, and the congregation answered — all of them, together, in perfect unison and perfect harmony:

"*Welcome home.*"

Only Mrs. Tallow didn't say it. Only Mrs. Tallow's lips pressed white and shut, and her hand found Juniper's wrist again beneath the fold of their coat, and held on, hard, all through the singing that started up afterward — an old harvest song, Ambrose said, just a bit of tradition, nothing to worry over — while two hundred gentle voices rolled through four lines and stopped, deliberately, at the end of the third.

Nobody sang a fourth verse. Nobody explained why. The silence where it should have been sat in the hall like an open grave, and every elder in Hollowmere smiled into it, waiting, patient as masonry, for someone to fill it.

Outside, later, in the dark by the van, Juniper flexed their bruised wrist and made a decision that felt less like courage than like falling: tomorrow they would drive to the county archive, and they would find out what really happened in 1911.

Behind them, faint across the green, the chapel belfry hummed one low note — tuning itself, Juniper thought, for Sunday.
