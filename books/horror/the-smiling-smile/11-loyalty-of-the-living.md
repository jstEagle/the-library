## 11. Loyalty of the Living

The vicarage smelled of toast and beeswax, which was the first thing that made Juniper want to scream.

They had come straight from the belfry — from the wet cavity shaped like a bell, from the smiling man who had said *we kept your place* in a voice like a hymn played underwater — and their hands had not stopped shaking since. They'd washed them twice at the chapel tap. The shaking wasn't fear anymore. It was the specific tremor of a person who has spent seven years being professionally reasonable and has just run out of reasonable.

Vicar Ambrose Pell answered the door in his cardigan, tea towel over one shoulder, looking for all the world like a man about to offer them a scone.

"Miss Hartwell," he said warmly. "Or is it Mx? I'm never sure, and I refuse to guess wrong on a Tuesday."

"Juniper's fine."

"Juniper's fine," he repeated, delighted, as though it were a small joke they shared. "Come in, come in. You look like you've seen something dreadful."

"I have."

"Well." He stepped aside to let them pass. "Toast first, or after?"

* * *

The kitchen was yellow. Everything in it was yellow — curtains, crockery, a ceramic hen on the windowsill holding wooden eggs. It was the kind of kitchen designed by someone who believed cheerfulness was a moral discipline, and Juniper sat at its scrubbed table with their cold hands around a hot mug and understood, with terrible clarity, that this man was going to be kind to them right up until the moment it mattered.

"Vicar. Last night I went into your belfry."

"Did you?" He buttered his toast with the serenity of a lake. "I do hope you didn't disturb anything."

"There's no bell up there."

"No, there isn't. Melted down in 1911. Terrible waste, everyone agrees." He took a bite, chewed, swallowed. "Lovely marmalade, would you like some? My sister sends it from Keswick."

"There's a *hole* up there. Shaped like a bell. Wet. And there's a man inside it, wearing a face off a coin, and he told me—" Juniper's voice cracked and they hated it. "He told me they kept my place."

Ambrose Pell set down his knife. He folded his tea towel. He looked at Juniper with an expression of such gentle, unhurried compassion that for one absurd second Juniper wanted to lean across the table and be comforted like a child.

"Oh, Juniper," he said softly. "So it's finally come round to you."

* * *

The elders arrived within the hour, as if summoned — and perhaps they were; perhaps the village had ears in its walls along with everything else. They came into the yellow kitchen one by one and took their seats without being asked: Mr. Coleridge from the farm shop, thin as a fence post; Mrs. Danby from the post office, her hands folded like a closed book; old Mr. Wren, who ran the allotments and had once, memorably, thrown Juniper out of his greenhouse for laughing too loudly near his marrows.

Six of them. All over sixty. All smiling — not the wide fixed smile of the congregation, but something smaller and worse. The smile people wear when a beloved grandchild says something foolish.

"Juniper," said Mrs. Danby, "we hear you've been asking questions."

"I've been asking questions for seven years," Juniper said. "You've all been very patient about it. Rats in the chapel, wasps under the floorboards, moles in the churchyard — I catalogued every noise you ever paid me to explain away. And now I know what the noises are, and I need help. Real help. People who'll stand with me. Because there is something in that tower and it knows my name, and it has known my name since before I knew it myself, and I am asking you — I am *begging* you—"

Their voice went. Just for a moment. In the silence, the ceramic hen on the windowsill seemed to watch them with its painted eyes.

"—help me close it," Juniper finished, barely above a whisper.

Nobody moved. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a blackbird sang four notes and stopped, as if it had been corrected.

"Would you like more tea?" said Vicar Pell.

* * *

"You have to understand," Mrs. Danby began, in the tone of a woman opening a well-loved book to a favorite page, "what Hollowmere was like before."

"Before the bell came back?"

"Before we learned to listen properly." She smiled at Mr. Coleridge, who nodded encouragement. "When I was a girl, we lost people the way everywhere loses people. To the city, mostly. My brother Tom went to Manchester when he was seventeen and I got two letters and then nothing, ever again. No grave. No voice. Nothing to visit. Do you know what that does to a family, Juniper? Grief with nowhere to put itself. It goes rotten in the house."

"And now?"

"Now," she said, and her face did something complicated and tender, "when my mother died, I heard her humming in the garden wall for a fortnight. Her own tune. The one she hummed when she was shelling peas. I sat against that wall every evening and listened until she was ready to go quiet. Can you tell me that's a bad thing?"

"It's not—" Juniper pressed their palms flat against the table. "It's not *her*. You know that. Whatever's making that sound, it isn't your mother. It's wearing her the way—"

"The way a bell wears its note?" said Vicar Pell mildly. "Yes. Probably. And yet."

"And yet," echoed Mr. Coleridge, and there was no mockery in it. That was the horror. There was no mockery anywhere in the room. Six elderly people looked at Juniper with perfect, patient love, and every one of them would let the thing in the tower eat a child rather than give up the sound of their dead.

"The harvests don't fail here," said old Mr. Wren, speaking for the first time, his voice like gravel poured slowly. "Never have. Not in the hungry years, not in the war. Blight skips us. Fever skips us. My father said it plain: a village that feeds its bell eats well." He looked at Juniper over his knuckles. "Your generation thinks hunger is a story. I buried two sisters before I was ten. I remember what a story it used to be."

"So children," Juniper said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "Children are the price. Eleven of them in 1911. Me, whenever I was— whatever I was. That's the arrangement. Prosperity in exchange for—"

"For names," said Vicar Pell, gently correcting, as if Juniper had miscounted change. "For songs. For a place in the harmony. Nobody dies of it, Juniper. That's the thing nobody understands from outside. The children aren't lost. They're *kept*. Nearby. Audible. On a still night you can hear them in the flint, singing rounds. I've spoken with my father every Sunday for eleven years. He was a difficult man alive — we quarrelled for a decade over the farm — and now he tells me he's proud of me, and whether it's truly him or truly the bell, I will tell you honestly, I cannot tell the difference. And neither, I think, could he."

The yellow kitchen swam. Juniper gripped the table edge.

"He's dead," they said. "Whatever talks to you, it isn't him. It's a mouth. A mouth that learned your griefs because you fed them to it, year after year, generation after generation, and now it can imitate love so well that you've built it a *parish*—"

"Careful," said Mrs. Danby, and for just a flicker — half a heartbeat — the smile slipped and something older looked out from behind her eyes, something tired and frightened and complicit. Then the smile returned, warm as toast. "Careful, dear. It's easy to call a thing a monster when you've only just met it. We've lived beside it two hundred years. We know its manners."

* * *

They talked for three hours. That was the worst part, afterward — how reasonable it all sounded, how patiently they laid it out, the way you'd explain crop rotation to a keen amateur.

The bell kept the fields rich. The bell kept the young from leaving — why would they go, when the dead stayed near and the living lacked for nothing? The bell kept grief *audible*, and audible grief, said Vicar Pell, pouring a third cup of tea Juniper hadn't asked for, is grief that heals. Every village in England buries its dead and shuts the lid. Hollowmere leaves the lid open a crack. Is that so monstrous?

"Yes," Juniper said. "When the lid opens onto a mouth."

Mr. Coleridge laughed — a real laugh, kind and crinkly-eyed. "Oh, you're your mother's child, right enough."

Juniper went very still. "What did you say?"

But Mrs. Danby was already patting their hand, and the moment folded itself away like linen. "You're upset. Of course you are. Finding out your whole life has a cellar under it — anyone would be upset. But you mustn't fight it, dear. Fighting it only makes it take more. Ask Mrs. Tallow." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "Seventy years fighting, that woman. Look how much it cost her."

"What did it cost her?"

"She'd tell you herself, if she thought it would do any good." Mrs. Danby gathered the cups, stacking them with small domestic chimes. "She won't. She believes ignorance is armor. Sweet, stubborn woman. But armor rusts, Juniper. And the song always finds the throat eventually."

"Then teach me to fight it properly," Juniper said, standing, chair legs shrieking on flagstones. "Give me the records. Give me the names. Tell me who founded the bell, who cast it, what was promised and to whom. If the village won't help me close it, then at least get out of my way while I learn how."

The six elders exchanged glances — and it was the glance that broke something in Juniper's chest. Not conspiracy. Not menace. It was the glance of parents deciding, together, kindly, that a child may not stay up past bedtime. The glance of people who loved them.

And underneath it, unmistakable, the same width of smile. The same teeth.

"We'll never be in your way, Juniper," said Vicar Pell, walking them to the door, hand light between their shoulder blades, steering with pure affection. "That's what you don't understand yet. Nothing in Hollowmere will ever be in your way. The road bends toward the chapel. The wind carries the tune uphill. Even your feet—" he opened the door onto the evening, onto the green, onto the distant chapel standing dark and patient against a bruised sky, "—even your feet know the way home. We're not your enemies, child. We're your congregation. And congregations don't fight the bell."

He squeezed their shoulder.

"They sing along."

* * *

Juniper walked back across the green with the vicar's kindness still sitting on their shoulder like a hand that wouldn't wipe off.

The lamps were coming on. Behind lit windows, families were sitting down to supper, and through the glass Juniper could see them — talking, passing plates, laughing — and every few minutes, all together, without any signal, each household would fall silent for exactly four seconds and smile toward the chapel before carrying on.

Prosperous. Warm. Fed.

The true monster, Juniper thought, stopping in the middle of the green, wasn't in the belfry. The thing in the belfry was only a mouth. Mouths don't feed themselves. Two hundred years of loving hands had fed it — hands that baked bread and sent marmalade from Keswick and folded tea towels and called it loyalty, called it memory, called it *home*.

Complicity, wearing a cardigan. Hunger, wearing kindness.

There was one door left. One person in Hollowmere who had spent seventy years refusing to sing, refusing to teach, hoarding her ignorance like armor — and whose name had come up tonight in a room full of smiles like a warning flare.

Mrs. Tallow's cottage stood at the far end of Bell Lane, of course. Everything in Hollowmere stood at the far end of Bell Lane; the lane was the village's spine and it curved, inevitably, past the chapel.

Juniper turned toward it. Behind them, faint and sweet, from the direction of the tower, someone began to hum — and every window on the green opened its curtain a little wider, as if the houses themselves were leaning in to listen.

At the end of the lane, a single candle burned in Mrs. Tallow's window. As Juniper watched, it guttered — and for one instant the flame bent sideways, toward the chapel, like a listener turning its head.

Then it steadied, and Juniper walked on, faster now, because somewhere behind them the humming had found words, and the words were in a child's voice, and the child was singing their name.
