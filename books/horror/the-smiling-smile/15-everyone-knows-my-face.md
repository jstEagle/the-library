## 15. Everyone Knows My Face

The first sign that something had gone wrong with the world was Mrs. Fenwick calling me by a name that wasn't mine.

She did it on a Tuesday morning, in the post office, while weighing a parcel of someone's knitting with the gravity of a woman defusing a bomb. I'd come in for mouse bait and stamps, the two great pillars of my profession, and she'd looked up at me over her spectacles and said, warm as a fresh loaf:

"You're up early, Elowen."

I stood there with my coins already counted out and felt the name go through me like a draught under a door. Elowen. It wasn't a name I knew. It wasn't a name I didn't know, either — that was the horrible part. It sat in my ear the way a key sits in a lock it was cut for, and somewhere behind my ribs something turned over and tried to open.

"Sorry?" I said.

"Your bait," she said, pushing it across the counter without taking my money. "No charge, love. You're family, after all."

"I'm not — Mrs. Fenwick, I'm the pest controller. Juniper Hartwell. I pay for things. That's the entire arrangement."

She smiled at me. And here I have to be careful how I describe it, because I have spent seven years describing smiles in Hollowmere, and I know them the way a locksmith knows locks. There was the polite smile. There was the village-meeting smile. There was the smile people gave me when I told them the scratching in their walls was squirrels, which was a smile that said *we both know that's not true, but isn't it kind of you to try.*

This was none of those. This was a smile with recognition in it, deep and settled, the smile you give someone you've known since they were in nappies. It fit her face perfectly. That was the worst thing. It fit like it had always been there.

"Of course you are, Elowen," she said, and went back to her knitting parcel.

I took the mouse bait. I want that on the record. I stood in the doorway for a good ten seconds weighing the option of leaving it on the counter, and then my hands — my own hands, allegedly — put it in my pocket, and I walked out into the morning like a man who has just realized the floor of his house has always been a lid.

* * *

By Thursday, half the village was doing it.

Mr. Cobb at the forge — a man who had not spoken to me in seven years except to complain about the price of my services — nodded as I passed and said, "Elowen. Your dad's gate hinge is done, if you want it seen to." I stopped in the lane so abruptly that a chicken nearly walked into my boot. Mr. Cobb had never mentioned my father. I did not have a father, as far as my paperwork was concerned; I had a county adoption file and a signature that was mostly a scribble.

"Mr. Cobb," I said carefully, "my dad's been dead a long time. If he existed."

He looked at me with something that was almost pity. "Course he has, love. That's why the hinge wants seeing to." And he went back to his forge as though he'd said something comforting.

At the shop, Mrs. Aldery refused my money for bread, for tea, for the tin of sardines I bought purely to have something to pay for. She wrapped it all in paper and said, "It's on the house, Elowen, it always was," and when I put coins on the counter anyway she slid them back with two fingers, gently, like she was returning something I'd dropped. Like I was a child playing at shopping. Like I was four.

The pub stopped charging me too. I sat in the corner with an unearned pint and watched the room, and the room watched me back with warm, unhurried eyes, and I understood that I had entered the strangest phase of my professional career: I was being welcomed. Enthusiastically. By everyone. And every welcome felt like a hand closing.

Because here's the thing about Hollowmere I should have understood sooner — and perhaps did understand, and buried it under invoices and bait receipts the way you bury anything you can't afford to look at. This village does not do anything halfway. When Hollowmere loves you, it loves you the way ivy loves a wall. Thoroughly. Permanently. From every side at once.

* * *

I tried to fight it with paperwork, which is my natural instinct in all crises. I went to the vicar — Vicar Loomis, a soft, grey, decent-seeming man whose smile I had been carefully not examining for years — and I put my adoption file on his desk and said, "My name is Juniper Hartwell. Juniper. Hartwell. I have papers."

He looked at the papers. He looked at me. He smiled that smile, the village one, the one with the same width and the same teeth as everyone else's, and he said, "Those are lovely, dear. Very official. But we've always called you Elowen."

"We? Who is *we*?"

"The village," he said, as if this were self-evident, as if the village were a person who had been waiting a very long time to say it. "We knew you'd come back. We kept your place."

*Kept your place.* I was out of his study before I realized I'd moved, walking fast down the lane with my collar up, and the horrible thing — one of the horrible things, the list is long — was that a small voice inside me, a voice I did not recognize as mine, was saying *that's nice, isn't it? They kept your place. They waited.*

I bit the inside of my cheek until I tasted copper, and the voice shut up.

* * *

The gifts started on Friday.

I came home to find things on my doorstep. A jar of honey. A knitted scarf in my — in *Elowen's* — favorite color, though I couldn't have told you what that was until I saw the scarf and felt the rightness of it settle over me like a hand. A loaf of bread still warm. A small wooden bird, carved by someone patient, painted with wings that were almost but not quite the shape of a bell.

There was no note on any of it. Notes would have implied I was a stranger, and I was not a stranger, and everyone knew it, and soon — I could feel it happening, like a tide coming in over my boots — so would I.

I carried it all inside and set it on the table and looked at it for a long time. The honey. The scarf. The bird. And I thought about Mrs. Tallow, who had gripped my wrist at the village meeting and whispered *don't learn the last verse*, who had been the only face in that room wearing a real human expression, and who had not come to the door when I'd knocked that morning. I'd told myself she was resting. Old women rest. It's one of their chief occupations.

But the honey on my table was the kind she kept bees for. I knew that jar. I'd bought it from her, once, in my first year, and she'd told me the recipe for the meadow, which she claimed was a secret and then told me anyway because I was the only person in Hollowmere who listened to her stories all the way to the end.

I put the scarf around my neck. I don't know why. It was cold, and it was soft, and it was *mine*, apparently, it had always been mine, and for about four minutes it was the most comforting thing that had ever happened to me.

Then I took it off and put it in the fire, and stood there watching it burn, and hated myself for how long I hesitated first.

* * *

That was the day the children started singing.

I want to be fair to the children. Children are not monsters; I have inspected enough of their treehouses and rescued enough of their cats to say so with professional authority. Children are just small people, and small people repeat what they're taught, the way walls repeat what's fed into them.

But at eleven o'clock that night, standing under my window in the lane, in the dark, in a village where children were normally in bed by nine because there was nothing else to do and nowhere else to go, four of them stood in a row and sang the third verse.

I didn't know it was the third verse. That's the thing I have to keep telling myself, gripping it like a rope: I did not know that. I recognized the tune the way you recognize a face in a crowd — instantly, completely, with your whole body — but the words were new to me, and I stood at my window and I *did not sing along*, and that not-singing was the hardest physical labor I have ever done, harder than any floor I've pried up, harder than biting through my own lip at noon while the bell pulled me down the lane.

The verse was short. It was about a door. I will not write the words here. I have a rule now, a rule I made with blood still in my mouth: nothing that song says goes on paper, because paper remembers, and things that remember can be asked to repeat themselves.

When they finished, the children didn't run off laughing the way children should. They just stood there in the lane for a moment, four small shapes, and then — I heard it clearly, and I will hear it clearly until I die — one of them said, in a voice too young for it:

"Goodnight, Elowen."

And another said: "You came back."

And a third, the smallest, right at the edge of hearing, said: "You can stop pretending now."

I did not sleep. I sat up with the lights on and all the doors I owned locked, which I knew was theater — I had seen what the walls of this village consider doors — and I held my father's pocketknife, which I had found in the chapel foundation dirt, which I had not told a soul about, and which had felt like a stranger's tool until that night, when it fit my hand like it had been carved for it.

Everything in Hollowmere fits me. That is the trap. That has always been the trap. The village has spent a hundred and eleven years carving a hollow in the shape of a child, and now the child has walked back into the hollow, and the hollow is closing around her like a hand around a bell.

* * *

Saturday morning I did the only sensible thing left. I packed the van.

I want to say I did it calmly. I did not do it calmly. I threw things. I put my traps in without cleaning them, which for me is the equivalent of tearing my shirt and putting ash on my head. I got the van loaded by nine, and I sat in the driver's seat with the engine running, and I looked down the lane toward the green, and I told myself: drive. Just drive. Mrs. Tallow's warnings, the photograph in the archive, the teeth in the chapel wall, the warm child-shaped hollow under the floor — all of it, all of it said the same thing, in a dozen voices: *leave, leave, leave.*

And then Mr. Cobb came around the corner of the lane carrying my gate.

Not a gate like my gate. My gate. The sagging, green-painted, hinge-rusted gate from the house I'd rented for seven years, which I had complained about to no one, because there was no one to complain to. He carried it on his shoulder like a coffin beam, and behind him came two other men with a post and a bag of concrete, and they set it down at the end of my drive, and Mr. Cobb straightened up, sweating and pleased with himself, and said:

"There you are, Elowen. Good as new. A place isn't a place without a gate."

And I sat in my van with my hands on the wheel, and the engine idled, and the gate stood there at the end of the drive, freshly painted, swinging gently in a wind I couldn't feel, and I understood — with the calm, cold clarity that comes at the exact center of a nightmare — that the gate was not to keep people out.

It was so that when I finally tried to leave, everyone in the village could say, honestly, warmly, with the best intentions in the world:

*But you had a gate. You could have gone any time.*

I turned off the engine. I don't fully remember deciding to. That's the part that frightens me most, writing it down — no, I'm not writing it down, I promised myself — that's the part that frightens me most, knowing it: my hand turned the key the way a hand turns a key, and I got out of the van, and I thanked Mr. Cobb, and I heard my own voice say "thank you, Elowen's very grateful," and I went inside and unpacked the traps and wiped them down and put them back on their shelf, each one, in order.

The gate stays open, I told myself. Gates can stay open. I'll keep it open.

But I knew, standing in my kitchen with the honey I hadn't thrown away and the bread I hadn't burned and the wooden bird watching me from the windowsill with its almost-bell wings, that I was not going to leave. Not because the village wouldn't let me.

Because Mrs. Tallow was missing, and the knocking under the chapel had stopped, and somewhere in this smiling, generous, suffocating village there was a last verse I had to finish learning — and a woman who had traded seventy years of her life to keep it from my ears, and who was, I was suddenly, terribly certain, at that very moment paying for the rest of it.

I put on my coat. Not the scarf. The coat.

And as I stepped out into the lane, every person I passed — the postman, the Alderys, old Mr. Petch with his dog — turned to me and smiled the same smile, the same width, the same teeth, and said, in a kind of gentle, patient chorus:

"Morning, Elowen."

And from the direction of the chapel, faint and clear, though the bell had been melted down before any of them were born, came a single ring — the sound of a place being kept.
