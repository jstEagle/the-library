## 22. The Song I Don't Remember

The van started on the third try, which Juniper chose to interpret as encouragement rather than complaint.

"Come on, old girl," they said, patting the dashboard the way they'd patted a thousand skittish animals out of a thousand walls and attics. "We're going somewhere with streetlights."

The road out of Hollowmere was narrow and green and utterly unremarkable, which felt like a small miracle in itself. Juniper drove with the windows down. The morning smelled of cut grass and diesel and, faintly, of nothing at all — no honey-sweetness, no bell-metal, no old pennies. Just a village behind them getting smaller in the mirrors, and a day ahead getting larger.

They'd packed in under an hour. There hadn't been much to pack. A toolbox with everything accounted for — the good torch, the borescope with its cracked screen, the little claw grabber they'd named Gerald years ago for reasons neither they nor Gerald could remember. Two changes of clothes. A kettle. The adoption file, which they had read one final time at the kitchen table of the rented cottage, then closed, then set on the counter, then — after a long moment in which the light through the window had done something patient and forgiving — left there. It wasn't theirs to carry anymore. It had never really been theirs at all.

The strange part was how light they felt. Not hollow — they'd checked, carefully, the way you check a tooth with your tongue — but light. Like a wall had come down and it turned out the wall had been load-bearing only for grief.

They hummed as they drove.

They didn't notice at first. It was just something the body did on a long straight road, the way a dog's leg twitches in sleep. Four notes up, two notes down, a little turn at the end like a sentence finishing itself. It was a pretty tune. It was a tune that felt, in the way certain tunes do, like it had been waiting for them.

Juniper hummed it through twice, three times, and somewhere in the middle of the fourth repetition, on the two-note descent, their hands went cold on the wheel.

They stopped.

The silence in the van was enormous. The engine ticked. A pheasant exploded out of the hedgerow in a fury of bad decisions and was gone.

"I don't know that song," Juniper said aloud, to the van, to the road, to whoever was listening. "I don't know that song."

And that was true. They searched for it the way you search for a word on the tip of your tongue — reaching down into themselves, into seven years of pest-control callouses and county-road miles, into whatever childhood had survived the swallowing — and found nothing. No church. No churchyard. No mother's voice. The song was simply *there*, the way a scar is there, the way the knowledge of how to ride a bicycle is there, printed somewhere below memory, below name, in the place where the body keeps the things it refuses to give up.

They had given the bell a name, and the bell had taken it. They had given it seven years of Juniper Hartwell, carefully assembled, and it had swallowed that too, and good riddance, mostly. But a song, apparently, was not a name. A song was not a possession at all. A song was something you *were*, once it got in deep enough, and you could no more hand it over than you could hand over the shape of your own hands.

"Right," said Juniper, because their mother — some mother, some voice — had apparently raised them to talk to machinery. "We don't hum it. Noted. Filed. Moving on."

They did not hum it again.

They hummed three other songs instead, deliberately, badly — a pop song from the radio, half-remembered; the jingle from a pest-control supplier's advert, which they knew in its entirety and was not proud of; and, as a final act of defiance, "Happy Birthday," despite it being nobody's birthday. The van did not object. The road did not object. Somewhere behind them, Hollowmere went on being a village.

* * *

They stopped at a petrol station forty minutes out, because the van ran on fuel and Juniper, it turned out, still ran on bacon rolls.

The man behind the counter had a face like a crumpled paper bag and the manner of someone who had been mildly disappointed since roughly 1987. He took Juniper's money, gave them change, and did not smile at them in any particular width, with any particular teeth, in any way that suggested a congregation.

"Nice morning," Juniper offered.

"It's alright," the man said, with the deep suspicion of a man who had never once trusted a morning, and Juniper could have kissed him.

They ate the bacon roll sitting on the van's bumper in the forecourt, watching lorries go by. A woman filled her car two pumps down, arguing cheerfully with a small child about the non-negotiability of seatbelts. A dog leaned out of a passing window with its whole face in the wind, wearing the expression of profound idiot joy that dogs have patented and humans can only rent.

Ordinary. All of it ordinary. Juniper finished the roll, licked salt off their thumb, and found they were crying — not the dramatic kind, just a slow leak, the kind you'd blame on wind if anyone asked.

Nobody asked. That was the gift of it.

They were crying, they worked out somewhere around the second minute, not for what they'd lost. They couldn't mourn what they couldn't remember; that door had been paid for and closed. They were crying for the woman across the green — the one who was not quite Mrs. Tallow, who had nodded to them that morning with a face that was finally, entirely, only her own. She had traded a wedding day. She had traded her son's face. And when the bell went silent and the debt came due in reverse, when all the taken things came flooding home, she had received seventy years of memory in a single morning, every one of them, all at once, at the age of seventy-nine.

Juniper had gone to her house before packing. They'd meant to say goodbye properly, to say *thank you*, to say the enormous unpayable thing.

She'd been in the garden in a cardigan, kneeling at a flowerbed, and she'd looked up at Juniper with wet eyes and said, "I had a son." Not a question. A discovery. She'd said it the way you'd say *it's raining* — astonished, factual, delighted and devastated at once. "I had a son, and he had a gap in his teeth, and he was frightened of cows, and I'd *forgotten* him, and now I've got all of him back and he's been gone fifty years and I don't know whether to laugh or—" and then she had laughed, and then she had cried, and then she had gripped Juniper's hand with a strength that belied the cardigan and said, "You go on, love. You go on and don't you dare look back at this place for anything but the view."

So Juniper was crying for her, a little. And for the eleven children in the photograph, who had finally, after a hundred years, been permitted to simply be children — remembered by a village that wept for them that morning without knowing why, and would spend the rest of its days building them a memorial without being able to explain to the county council why it mattered so much. And a little, if Juniper was honest, for themselves — for the child who had run through the frost with a hand over their mouth, and who had at last, at the very end, been allowed to stop running.

They wiped their face with the heel of their hand, got back in the van, and drove.

* * *

At the top of the long hill — the one where, according to a road sign Juniper had never read before this week, drivers should use a low gear — Juniper did the thing they had promised the not-quite-Mrs.-Tallow they wouldn't.

They looked back.

Hollowmere sat in its valley like a spoonful of sugar left on a table: small, white-washed, harmless. Smoke rose from chimneys in ordinary threads. The green was the green. And the chapel stood at the head of it, grey stone, squat tower, a building that had stood there for four hundred years and would stand there for four hundred more, and it was — this was the thing, this was the thing that made Juniper grip the wheel and laugh out loud, a single startled bark of a laugh — it was *simply a chapel*.

That was all. Their eyes went to it expecting the other thing, the wet cavity, the mouth, the vastness wearing a building like a coat, and found only stone and slate and a weathervane turning idly in the wind. It had never been anything else. Or it had always been something else, and now wasn't, and the past had obligingly rearranged itself around the fact. Records would say the bell had been melted in 1911 and never replaced, and the records would be right, because the bell had been melted in 1911, in every way that mattered, and what had worn its shape since had finally, at long last, been given nothing left to sing.

A chapel. Just a chapel. Juniper looked at it for a long ten seconds, memorizing its harmlessness, and then faced front and drove down the hill with the engine in a low gear like the sign asked, because they were a professional, and some habits outlast even a name.

* * *

The trouble started, if you could call it trouble, at the county line.

Juniper stopped at the first town with a registry office, because a person with no name and no past runs into practical difficulties at borders, banks, and job interviews, and Juniper intended to remain, at minimum, employed. The plan was simple: declare themselves, get papers, become someone new. Someone invented. Someone with a clean page.

The clerk was a kind, tired woman named Doreen, and she looked at Juniper over her glasses and said, "Name?"

And Juniper opened their mouth, and found the space where *Juniper Hartwell* had lived for seven years, and found it swept clean. Not empty, exactly. More like a room after the furniture's gone — the shape of things still faintly on the wallpaper, a rectangle of less-faded paint where a picture used to hang. They knew they'd had a name. They could feel the absence of it the way you feel a missing tooth.

"Give me a moment," Juniper said.

"Take your time, love. There's a queue but it's all pensioners."

Juniper stood there in the draughty hall, in front of a noticeboard about recycling, and waited for a name to arrive. And what arrived, unbidden, warm as a stone from a sunny windowsill, was not a name at all.

It was the tune.

Four notes up, two notes down, the little turn at the end. And this time, humming it — they'd given up on not humming it; it was clearly going to do what it liked — they felt something loosen, and a word arrived with it. One word. A name-shaped word, small and worn smooth, the way a token gets worn smooth by seventy years in a pocket.

They couldn't say it. They tried, and their mouth went around the shape of it and produced nothing, the way a bell with no clapper produces nothing. But they could feel it. They knew it the way you know a face in a dream. It was a child's name. It was *their* child's name, the one given before the frost and the hand over the mouth, the one the bell had never gotten, the one that had been carried out of Hollowmere hidden somewhere no bell could reach — not in memory, not in papers, but in the song itself, in the fourth verse, in the place where the ending should be.

The founder's daughter had taught them the verse in fragments, in sleep, and warned them that hearing it whole meant singing it whole. And Juniper had woken with most of it and a tuneless gap at the end, and had thought the gap was loss.

It hadn't been loss. It had been a hiding place. The last verse had no words Juniper could sing because its words *were* the name, and the name had been folded into the silence where the song stopped, and the bell, greedy and vast and finally finished, had taken everything offered to it except the one thing never offered. You can't swallow what isn't given. That had been the whole trick, all along, two hundred years of tricks, and a four-year-old running through frost had known it without knowing it: you keep the last verse in the not-singing of it.

Juniper thanked Doreen, said they'd come back another day, and walked out into the high street feeling like the last piece of a jigsaw that had been in the wrong box for thirty years and had just been found under the sofa.

They'd need a name eventually. They'd pick one. Something with no history, something they chose themselves, which was more than most people got. But not today. Today the nameless thing in the driver's seat had one more piece of business.

* * *

Because here was the thing they'd been not-thinking about since the county line, the thing sitting in the glovebox.

Juniper had packed the van in under an hour, and everything in it was accounted for — the tools, the clothes, the kettle, the abandoned adoption file left on the counter. Everything except the glovebox. They hadn't opened the glovebox. They had, in fact, been carefully, professionally, seven-years-of-rationalizing-ly *not* opening the glovebox, in the finest tradition of every noise behind every wall they'd ever explained away.

They pulled into a layby. Beech trees. A view of absolutely nothing. Perfect.

They opened the glovebox.

Inside, on top of the insurance documents for a person who no longer existed, lay a single brass token.

It was warm. That was the first thing — not sun-warm, not engine-warm, but warm the way a hand is warm, warm the way the child-sized hollow under the chapel floor had been warm. It was the size of an old penny, and it was worn smooth, and it was engraved.

Juniper turned it over in the light and read the name, and their breath went out of them all at once, slow, like air leaving a bell.

They almost recognized it.

That was the only honest way to put it. The letters were familiar the way the tune was familiar — known somewhere below knowing, worn into them by a voice that had taught a four-verse song to a child in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but here, in brass, in the body, in the place where the body keeps what it refuses to give up. It was the name from the tuneless gap. It was the name their mouth couldn't say. And it was in Juniper's glovebox, warm, which meant someone had put it there, which meant that somewhere between the chapel and the county line, a woman in a cardigan who was not quite Mrs. Tallow — or who was exactly Mrs. Tallow, or who was whatever Mrs. Tallow was becoming now that she had everything back — had opened a van door and left a gift.

Or it meant something else. Juniper had spent seven years learning that every explanation is a door, and behind every door is another room, and some rooms have teeth. But this room, they decided, turning the token over, feeling its small warmth against their palm, this room they were going to let be what it looked like. A keepsake. A *remember me*. The one thing in the world that still knew who they had been before they were anyone.

Some verses are learned so deeply they survive even forgetting. The song had taken the name Juniper Hartwell and been welcome to it. But the first name — the true one, the child one, the one sung into brass and silence — that one had been paid for, hidden, carried, and delivered. It was theirs. It had always been theirs. It had simply been waiting, patient as a seed, in the one place no bell could reach.

Juniper closed their hand around it.

"Right," they said to the token, to the beech trees, to the enormous ordinary day. "Not today. But someday. When I've got a name to sign under it."

They put the token in their breast pocket, over the heart, where warm things belong, and started the van — first try, this time, which they chose to interpret as encouragement — and pulled back onto the road.

And as the layby fell away behind them, without meaning to, without deciding to, Juniper hummed.

Four notes up. Two notes down. And then — for the first time, in a voice that shook only a little — the little turn at the end, the sentence finishing itself, the gap where the ending should be, filled at last with a single word they could not say aloud but could, finally, hear.

They didn't know what the song was. They never would.

They knew it was theirs.
