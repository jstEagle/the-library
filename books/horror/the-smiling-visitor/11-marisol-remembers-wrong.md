## 11. Marisol Remembers Wrong

The kitchen smelled of cinnamon and something underneath it — something mineral, like water left standing in a copper pipe too long. Kestrel noticed the second smell before they noticed anything else about the room: the way the curtains were drawn against the afternoon, the way three chairs sat at the table though only two of them would be used, the way Marisol had set out the good plates, the ones with the blue rim that had only ever come down from the cabinet for funerals and christenings.

Kestrel stood in the doorway with their hand still on the frame, as if the wood could anchor them to some version of this house where dinner was just dinner.

"You came." Marisol turned from the stove, and her smile arrived a half-second before the rest of her face seemed to decide on it. "I wasn't sure you would."

*You came.* Not *you're home.* Not *Wren.* Something in Kestrel's chest unclenched by a single degree. Since the funeral — since the casket, since the face, since Odile Voss had pressed a photocopied ledger into their hands two nights ago with the warning *don't let her sing for you* — every word out of their mother's mouth had been a coin they couldn't stop turning over, looking for the false edge.

"I almost didn't," Kestrel said.

"But you did." Marisol lifted the lid off a pot and steam rolled up past her face, and for one ordinary moment she was only a woman cooking for her child, small and gray-streaked and tired around the eyes. Then the steam cleared and she was smiling again, that same even width, held a beat too long, like a note sustained past the end of the measure. "Sit. It's almost ready. Wren always said my pozole tasted better out of these plates."

Kestrel's stomach dropped through the floorboards.

"Wren never ate pozole," they said. Their voice came out flat, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice they already knew was thin. "Wren hated hominy. You used to make them a separate bowl of just broth and chicken because they'd cry otherwise. They were six. You called it the Wren Bowl."

Marisol stirred the pot. The wooden spoon made slow circles. Outside, a bird called once and did not call again.

"You're thinking of someone else," she said pleasantly.

"I'm thinking of my twin."

"You're thinking of stories." She turned, and the lamplight above the stove caught her teeth, and Kestrel looked away before they could confirm what they'd half-seen at the funeral reception — that her canines were too long, or too many, or simply arranged in a way that suggested a mouth built for something other than chewing. "Families collect stories the way attics collect dust. Some of them are true. Some of them are just what we needed to believe." She gestured with the spoon toward the table. "Sit, mijo. Mija. Sit, whatever you are today."

The words landed wrong in a way Kestrel couldn't immediately name, and then could, and wished they couldn't. *Whatever you are today.* As if gender were the strange thing in this kitchen. As if the strangeness were a garment Kestrel had put on, rather than the house itself wearing a costume of home.

They sat. The third chair stayed empty between them, angled slightly toward the window that faced the back field.

* * *

Dinner proceeded like a play performed by an actor who had memorized the lines but not the pauses. Marisol told stories. That was mostly what she did — ladled pozole into the good bowls and told stories about Wren, each one polished smooth and slightly wrong, like river stones.

"—and when Wren broke their arm falling out of the pecan tree, they laughed the whole way to the hospital—"

Wren hadn't broken their arm. Wren had broken their wrist, and they hadn't laughed; they'd gone silent in that particular Wren way, white-lipped and furious at their own body, and had refused painkillers on principle at nine years old because they'd read somewhere that doctors lied.

"—and Wren wanted to be a marine biologist, did I ever tell you? They loved the ocean—"

Wren had been terrified of deep water. Had taken baths instead of showers until eleven. Had once cried at a school film about whales, not from wonder but from a dread so pure it embarrassed them, and Kestrel had held their hand under the desk and sworn never to tell.

Every story was a portrait painted from a photograph hung upside down. The features were right. Everything else was inverted. And Kestrel understood, sitting there with their spoon moving mechanically through the bowl, that this was the point — that these weren't mistakes but replacements, a new Wren being installed over the old one story by story, and if Kestrel heard enough of them, if they stayed long enough, the corrections might start to feel like memory. Might start to overwrite.

"Mom." Kestrel set down the spoon. "Why is there a third chair?"

Marisol didn't look up. "Habit."

"It's facing the field."

"Habit," she said again, and smiled, and under the table Kestrel felt rather than heard a low vibration come up through the floor, faint as a truck passing miles away, except no road ran within four miles of the house.

Kestrel pushed back their chair. "I need to ask you something."

"Of course." Marisol folded her hands in her lap, and her whole body went still in a way bodies aren't supposed to go still — no swallow, no blink adjustment, no micro-shift of weight. Just stillness, total and patient, like a pond waiting for a stone.

"The lullaby," Kestrel said. "The one you sang us. The Quintana song. Odile Voss says there's a verse missing. The last one. Cut out of the songbook before either of us could read."

Something moved behind Marisol's eyes. Kestrel had spent thirty years reading those eyes — had catalogued every weather they could produce, grief and fury and the rare bright squall of laughter — and what moved behind them now was none of those. It was more like watching a lock consider a key.

"Odile Voss," Marisol repeated softly. "She always did keep other people's accounts."

"She keeps a ledger. Of trades. Names given, names taken. Every Quintana generation. And every entry ends mid-verse, Mom. Mid-verse, like whoever wrote it couldn't finish the song. Like finishing it does something."

"And you think I know how it finishes."

"I think you know more than you've sung."

For a long moment the only sound was the refrigerator humming its one dumb note, and beneath it, so far down Kestrel might have imagined it, the other hum — the floor's hum, the field's hum, the capped iron mouth in the dark humming along.

Then Marisol began to laugh. It was warm. It was genuine. That was the worst part — it rang true as a bell, and Kestrel felt their whole body lean toward it the way a cold hand leans toward a stove, because it was their mother's laugh, the real one, the one from before everything, and some traitorous part of them wanted to crawl inside it and stay.

"Oh, my love," Marisol said. "You want the whole song."

"I want my brother back."

"You want the whole song," she repeated, gently, as if Kestrel hadn't spoken, "and you think it's a door. Everyone thinks it's a door." She rose, unhurried, and carried the bowls to the sink, and stood with her back to Kestrel while the water ran. "It's not a door. It's a mouth. But—" she shut off the water, and the silence afterward had texture, "—you're my child. And children should know their family songs. All of them."

She turned around. She dried her hands on the dish towel, fold over fold, precise as ritual. And then Marisol Quintana began to sing.

* * *

Kestrel had heard the lullaby ten thousand times. In the dark of shared bedrooms, over fevers, on long drives when the radio died, hummed absently while Marisol shelled peas. It lived in Kestrel's body the way their own heartbeat did — five verses, descending like stairs, each one lower and slower than the last, ending on a note so soft it was less a sound than a settling, the musical equivalent of a hand smoothing a blanket over sleeping shoulders. *Down where the water dreams its silver dream / sleep, little lanterns, sleep.*

Marisol sang it flawlessly.

That was the first horror: that it was flawless. Not the worn, human version Kestrel remembered, with its bent notes and swallowed syllables, but perfect — pitch-true, rhythm-locked, every verse landing exactly where it belonged, as if she'd rehearsed it against a tuning fork for years. Her voice didn't sound like her mother's voice. It sounded like her mother's voice played back through a machine that had studied it.

And her eyes had gone somewhere else. Glazed, fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, on the wall behind the wall. Her hands rested at her sides, palms forward, fingers loose. She swayed very slightly, a metronome of one.

Verse one. Verse two. Kestrel's skin crawled upward, vertebra by vertebra, because the song was right and the singing was wrong, and the combination was worse than either alone. This was the song that meant safety. It had meant safety for a hundred years of Quintanas. Hearing it now was like hearing a stranger recite your own prayers back to you in your own voice.

Verse three. Verse four. The kitchen light seemed to dim without changing, the way light behaves in dreams. The hum beneath the floor grew a fraction louder, and Kestrel realized with a lurch that it had found the melody — that the house itself was harmonizing, low and vast, a congregation of dirt and stone joining in under the floorboards.

Verse five. The settling note. The blanket-smoothing close.

Marisol kept singing.

There was no pause. No breath. The fifth verse ended and the sixth began as if it had always been there, waiting inside the fifth like a seed inside fruit, and Kestrel's mind skidded against it — *that's not the song, that's not the song* — but their ears had no choice. The words came clear and sweet and in Marisol's voice:

*Down where the water counts what it is owed,*
*one name to hold the dark, one name to go,*
*sing, little lantern, sing and do not stop,*
*the well is patient and the well knows—*

She stopped.

Not trailed off. Stopped, mid-word, mid-syllable — *knows* cut clean at the s — and stood smiling in the sudden silence with her glazed eyes and her palms forward, and Kestrel understood in one white-hot flash that the cutting was deliberate, that this too was rehearsal, that she had practiced stopping there the way she had practiced everything else, because the verse could not be finished here, in a kitchen, over pozole, for free.

"Mom," Kestrel whispered.

Marisol blinked. The glaze drained out of her eyes like water down a drain, and she was present again, warm again, looking at her child with mild maternal concern. "What is it? You've gone pale."

"You — the song. You kept going."

"Did I?" She tilted her head. "Silly of me. Old songs have more verses than we remember. That's what makes them old." She picked up the dish towel and began folding it again, fold over fold. "You shouldn't repeat any of that, by the way. What you just heard. Songs are like seeds, mija. They grow whether you mean to plant them or not."

Her teeth were wrong in the lamplight. Kestrel was sure of it now — not fangs, nothing so honest as fangs, just an arrangement, a crowding, too many teeth for the available jaw, packed the way things get packed when something larger has been folded down small to fit inside a familiar shape. Or rehearsed into. That was the thought arriving now, cold and complete, the thought that would follow Kestrel up the stairs and into every night after:

*She isn't possessed. Possessed would be easier. Possessed would mean there was a her to save underneath. She's rehearsed. She learned this. She chose it, verse by verse, year by year, the way you learn an instrument — and she's been conducting longer than I've been alive.*

"How long?" Kestrel's voice cracked on the second word. "How long have you been singing for it?"

Marisol smiled the long, even smile. "Since before you were born, little lantern."

The nickname went through Kestrel like ice water. It was Wren's name for them — no one else's, never Marisol's, a secret currency minted between twins in the dark of shared bedrooms — and hearing it in their mother's mouth was theft performed so casually it took a moment to feel the wound.

"That's not yours," Kestrel said. "That's Wren's word. For me. You don't get to—"

"He gave it to me," Marisol said pleasantly. "He gives me such lovely things, down there. He's very generous with his memories. It passes the time." She unfolded the towel and draped it over the oven rail, smoothing it flat with both hands, twice. "He asked me to tell you something, actually. He said you'd come asking about the song eventually — he knows you, you see, better than you know yourself, he says — and he wanted me to tell you: *come to the field at dusk, and bring the recorder, and I'll teach you the rest myself.*"

The kitchen tilted. Kestrel gripped the table's edge.

"He also said," Marisol continued, gathering the third chair and returning it, gently, to the corner where it lived, "to tell you not to bother with Odile Voss's rules. He said rules are just songs people were too frightened to finish." She set the chair down. Straightened it. Looked up, and her smile widened by a degree that the muscles of a human face should not permit. "He sounds hoarse, my darling. He's been singing such a long time. Won't you come take your turn?"

Kestrel ran.

They didn't remember deciding to run — one moment they were gripping the table and the next the night air was slapping their face and the back field grass was whipping their shins and behind them the kitchen window glowed amber and, rising through the open frame, faint and flawless and doubled beneath itself, Marisol's voice began the lullaby again from the top. Two voices. Perfectly aligned, perfectly hers, and one of them coming from a register no throat owns — deeper, wetter, patient as groundwater — harmonizing a half-beat behind like a shadow that had finally learned to keep up.

Kestrel ran all the way to the fence line before their legs gave out, and stood in the dark with their hands on their knees, gasping, the melody lodged behind their teeth like a fishhook.

They did not sing it. God help them, shaking, sobbing, with the whole song open in their head like a door swinging on its hinge — they did not sing it.

But lying awake hours later, wired to the recorder, listening to the house settle, Kestrel finally understood the shape of the trap, all of it, the whole architecture: the funeral, the face, the obituary, the stories upside-down, the third chair facing the field. Marisol wasn't the well's victim. She was its choir director. She had raised the bait, tended it, tuned it — and every kindness she'd shown since Kestrel stepped off the bus had been a rehearsal for tonight.

On the recorder, in the morning, there would be a new voice. Not Wren's. Not the chorus. A voice like Marisol's, but younger, warmer, wearing Wren's cadence like a borrowed coat — and it would say, patiently, kindly, in the dark of Kestrel's own bedroom:

*You ran before I finished the verse. Shall I sing you the rest?*
