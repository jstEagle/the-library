## 12. Refilling

Odile Voss had kept the ledger for thirty-one years, and in all that time it had never once lied to her.

She woke before dawn on the last Tuesday of June with her grandmother's word sitting on her tongue like a stone: *refilling*. It was not a dream, exactly. It was older than dreaming — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron at the back of the throat, the particular heaviness in the floorboards that meant water was moving somewhere it shouldn't. Her grandmother had called it *the turn*. Her great-grandmother had called it nothing at all, because by the time she'd have named it, she'd already been standing at the cap with her skirts hitched and her mouth open, listening.

Odile lit the lamp in her kitchen and set the ledger on the table where the light fell cleanest. The book was older than the house around it, bound in hide that had gone the color of wet slate, its spine stitched with sinew that no tanner in the valley could identify anymore. She turned to the current page — the Quintana entry, the one she'd opened the night Kestrel Quintana first came to her door looking like a question that hadn't been asked yet — and read it through once, the way you'd check a knot before trusting your weight to it.

*Quintana, Marisol. Debt assumed, fever year. One name given against one name owed. The song sung to settle accounts:*

And then the verse began, inked in her grandmother's careful hand, four lines of it, and then nothing. Every entry ended mid-verse. That was the design. The well-keepers recorded the terms but never the tune; the melody lived only in mouths, and mouths were mortal, and what the well wanted it had to wait a generation to get. Odile had always found a grim comfort in that. Paper couldn't sing. Paper couldn't be tricked into singing. Whatever else the valley took from her family across two hundred years, it had never taken the words off the page.

The page was blank.

Not smudged. Not faded. Blank — the way a page is blank when the ink has been *drunk out of it*, drawn down through the fibers and away, leaving the paper itself faintly damp, faintly cold, smelling of deep stone and old rain. Odile pressed her thumb flat against the center of the page and held it there until her arm shook. Cold as a well bucket in August. She turned back. The page before it — the Aldous entry, 1911, her own great-aunt's trade — blank. The page before that. Blank. She went backward through the book faster and faster, leafing like a gambler, and every page was the same soft empty cream, two hundred years of names and terms dissolved into nothing, until she reached the flyleaf, where her grandmother had written in a hand like barbed wire:

*The well keeps its own accounts now.*

Odile sat very still for a long time. Outside, the sky had gone the gray of dishwater, and the birds had started.

* * *

She heard them before she saw them — a sound like a crowd murmuring in a church, low and circular — and stepped onto her porch with her coffee going cold in her hand. The starlings came up from the back field in a slow spiral, dozens of them, then hundreds, turning over the Quintana land in a column so wide and so patient it looked less like flight than like something being stirred. They did not scatter when a truck rattled past on the county road. They did not cry out. They turned, and turned, and turned, wings catching the early light in little silver flashes, and Odile thought: *they're counting something.* She didn't let herself think what.

By seven, the first calls came.

Petra Nilsen from the co-op, voice tight: the tap in her kitchen had run black for ten seconds that morning, thick as crude oil, smelling of the deep places, before clearing to a brown trickle. Harlan Deets from the far side of the valley: his stock troughs wouldn't hold clear water, the horses refusing to drink, standing at the fence lines with their ears pinned toward the Quintana field. The school secretary, apologetic, asking if Odile knew anything about why the artesian well behind the gymnasium — dry since before the war — had begun, overnight, to seep. Not gush. Seep. A slow dark weeping out of ground that had been giving nothing for seventy years.

"They're all connected," Odile said, and heard how it sounded, an old woman stating the weather. "It's all one water."

She said it because it was true, and because saying it aloud was the last ritual she had left that still worked. Her family had been keepers of a thing they were never allowed to explain, and now the explanation was happening whether anyone consented or not. The well under the Quintana field wasn't a well. It was a mouth, and the mouth had been held shut for years by a single exhausted voice singing below it, and the voice was failing, and everything the water touched — every aquifer, every spring, every buried vein of it running under the valley like a second bloodstream — was beginning to rise to meet it.

One family's debt, coming due as a whole valley's flood.

* * *

She drove out to the homestead just after noon, taking the back way past the church, past the diner where the specials board still advertised a fish fry for a man who was dead three weeks and wearing somebody else's face in a casket. The town had gone quiet in a way Odile recognized from her grandmother's descriptions of the fever year — not peaceful quiet, but *held* quiet, the hush of people unconsciously waiting for a sound they dreaded. Nobody was watering their lawns. Nobody had to be told not to. You don't put more water into a thing that's already rising.

Kestrel met her at the door, and Odile saw immediately that the nights had gotten into them. There was a rawness around the eyes, a tremor in the hands they kept folding into each other, and — this was new — a strip of clean cloth lying on the hall table beside a spool of thread, cut to length, waiting. Odile didn't ask. Some questions you already knew the answers to, and the knowing was bad enough.

"The recorder," Kestrel said, before she was fully through the door. "You need to hear it. I need someone else to hear it, because if it's just me, I'm going to start believing I made it up, and I don't think I made it up, and I need—" They stopped. Breathed. "I need a witness."

They played it in the kitchen, the little machine sitting between them on the table like a third guest.

It started as it always did: the small sounds of Kestrel sleeping, breath and rustle, the house ticking. Then Wren's voice, hoarse now, worn down to almost nothing, answering the question Kestrel had asked aloud before bed — *how much longer can you keep singing?* — with an answer that made Odile close her eyes.

*"I don't know. It's getting louder down here. Like the room is filling up with other people."*

Then the humming began.

Odile had heard the lullaby hummed before, in fragments, in warnings. This was different. This was *many*. Dozens of voices layered beneath Wren's single thread, men and women and children, none of them distinct, all of them perfectly together, holding one long unbroken tone underneath the melody like a floor holding up a dancer. And threaded through it, unmistakable, unmistakably *hers*, Marisol's voice, clear and rehearsed and smiling even in sound, singing the verses Odile's grandmother had forbidden her family to ever write down.

"It started two nights ago," Kestrel whispered. "There were three voices. Last night there were— I counted. I counted forty-one."

"Stop counting," Odile said. "Counting is how it gets you to participate." But her hands weren't steady either, because she understood what forty-one voices meant. The well wasn't just refilling with water. It was refilling with *singers*. Everyone the valley had ever lost to it, everyone traded and drowned and settled, all of them pulled up out of wherever the well kept them, massing below the cap like a congregation gathering before a service. And a congregation needs a lead voice. A congregation waits for someone to begin.

Wren's voice came again through the little speaker, thin as paper:

*"Kes. If you can hear this. Don't come to the field. Whatever happens, whatever you hear calling you — the water knows your name now. It learned it from me. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, I said it in my sleep."*

The recording ran another minute in that terrible layered hum, and then stopped.

Kestrel sat with their face in their hands. Odile looked at the window, where the light had gone strange and greenish, storm-colored without a cloud, and at the glass, where a fine tremor was running through the surface of Kestrel's water glass — not from traffic, not from any truck on any road. From below. Rhythmic. Patient. Like a pulse.

"How long," Kestrel said, "do we have?"

Odile thought about the blank pages. About the birds. About her grandmother, who had died with her lips sewn shut by her own instruction, in a house with every drain plugged, every basin filled with salt, because she believed the end would come as a thirst that arrived from underneath.

"The solstice," she said. "That's what the ledger always pointed to, when it bothered pointing at all. Nine days." She stood, and her knees cracked like the old house around her. "But the ledger is gone, Kestrel. Two hundred years of terms, drunk down to blank paper. Which means the well isn't waiting for the terms anymore. It's writing new ones."

* * *

She stayed until dusk, drawing maps of the valley's water on the back of a feed-store calendar — every spring, every sump, every forgotten hand-dug well, marking which ones had reported black water and which would follow. The pattern was ugly and simple: a slow circle tightening around the Quintana field, mile by mile, day by day. The well was calling its water home. Everything the valley drank was being summoned back to the mouth.

On her way out, pausing at her truck, she felt it — a change in the air pressure, subtle as a door opening in a far room. The spiral of birds overhead had tightened. Below the field, beneath forty feet of summer earth, something vast shifted its weight, and the sound that reached her wasn't a sound at all but a feeling, a subterranean swallow, and every hair on her arms rose.

From the open kitchen window behind her, faintly, she heard the recorder switch itself on.

Neither of them had touched it.

A needle of static, a breath, and then — not Wren, not Marisol, not any of the forty-one — a voice Odile had never heard and recognized instantly anyway, because it sounded exactly like the inside of a smile: warm, wide, patient, and addressed, with terrible courtesy, to her.

*"Keeper. Your book was overdue."*

Odile got in the truck. She did not run — running was singing's first cousin, running invited pursuit — but her hands shook the whole drive home, and when she pulled into her own yard the porch light was on though she'd left it off, and on her kitchen table, square and neat as a place setting, lay a single page of fresh cream-colored paper, damp at the edges, cold as a well bucket, with one line written on it in ink that gleamed like water in the lamplight:

*Nine days is what the old terms allowed. The new terms are shorter.*
