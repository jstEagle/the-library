## 3. Hollowmere Welcomes You Home

The sign at the edge of town still read WELCOME TO HOLLOWMERE, POP. 1,204, though the paint had gone the color of old teeth and someone had added, in careful white letters beneath it: *We're glad you're home.*

Orsen didn't remember that line being there when they were a child. They didn't remember the sign being repainted at all. But then, they'd only ever come in summer, in daylight, with their grandmother's hand around theirs, and grandmothers have a way of steering children past the things worth noticing.

They drove in from the county road with the attic chair's warmth still ghosting through their memory like a fever that hadn't broken. They had told themselves, all the way down Route 9, that they would not bring it up with anyone. That they would pack the house, sell it, and be gone before the first snow. A week of work. Ten days, tops.

The town had other plans.

* * *

Mrs. Aldous was on her porch before Orsen's car had fully stopped, a casserole dish balanced on one forearm like a shield or an offering. She was eighty if she was a day, and she moved toward the car with the brisk certainty of someone who had been waiting.

"Orsen Delacroix." She said the name the way you'd say a password. "You look just like your grandmother. Around the eyes."

"Thank you," Orsen said, getting out. "I'm sorry—do I know you?"

"You will." She pressed the dish into their hands. It was warm. Everything in this town seemed to run warm—the pavement, the porch rails, the hands of strangers. "Chicken and dumplings. Your grandmother's recipe. I made it the way she made it for the Founding Supper, back when we were girls."

"The what?"

Mrs. Aldous smiled. It reached her eyes. That was somehow worse. "You'll see. There's talk of holding one again, now that Edith has passed. A welcome supper. For you." She patted Orsen's arm once, twice, three times, as if counting them. "You mustn't lift a finger while you're here. The house is yours to settle, but the town is yours too. Whatever you need. Anything at all."

She walked back to her porch without waiting for a reply, and Orsen stood in the middle of Vane Road holding a warm dish and feeling, distinctly, like something being fed.

* * *

By evening, the porch of the Delacroix house looked like a funeral buffet that had gotten out of hand. Casseroles. Pies. A loaf of bread still steaming. Jars of preserves labeled in handwriting Orsen almost recognized. Every neighbor within four streets had come by, and every one of them knew Orsen's name—not *Delacroix*, not *Edith's grandchild*, but *Orsen*, first name, spoken softly, the way you speak to someone you've been told about.

"I heard you work with the dying," said Mr. Pruitt from the hardware store, helping carry boxes that did not need carrying. "That must be such holy work. Being there at the end."

"It's a job," Orsen said.

"Nothing's just a job," he said warmly, and set down a box of winter coats that had been in the attic, in a locked trunk, that no one had asked him to move.

A woman named Sela brought firewood and stacked it to the eaves, enough for a winter three times as long as any Hollowmere had seen in decades. When Orsen thanked her, she said, "Oh, you'll want it. Solstice comes early some years." And she laughed, and the laugh was genuine, and Orsen stood in the yard listening to it fade and could not make themselves laugh along.

Everyone offered help. No one accepted refusal. When Orsen said they preferred to pack alone, the town nodded sympathetically—and kept coming. By the third day, Orsen had stopped keeping track of who had visited, because the answer was: everyone, always, in rotation, as though the whole town were taking shifts.

It should have felt like love. That was the terrible part. Orsen had spent seven years in hospice wards watching people die unheld, unwitnessed, and some starved animal in their chest wanted to lie down inside all this warmth and never leave. When Mrs. Aldous took their hand at the mailbox and said, *You're one of us, dear. You always were,* something in Orsen nearly believed her.

Nearly. Because underneath the casseroles ran a current, and Orsen had spent seven years learning to feel currents under calm surfaces. The dying taught you that. Smiles are the last thing to go.

* * *

The first diary page came on the fourth morning, folded once and slipped through the mail slot with no envelope.

Orsen found it among the flyers and a card from the funeral home, and unfolded it on the kitchen table. The paper was old, soft as cloth, the ink browned. The handwriting was small and slanted and urgent.

*June 3rd, 1961.*

*Mother says I must stop writing about the Supper but I cannot. Tonight was the Founding Supper at the Hall and I counted the chairs. Nine chairs, eight founders, and nobody will say why. When I asked Papa he laughed and said I count everything twice, which is true, but he did not answer me. Nobody answers. They smile so you won't count the chairs at the table.*

*I saw my sister looking at the ninth chair too. Ruth sees it. Ruth always sees.*

Orsen read it three times. Then went to the mailbox. Empty. Went back inside. Read it again.

The second page came the next morning.

*June 5th, 1961.*

*Ruth won't eat at the Supper tonight. She says she will not sit at a table where one seat is kept warm for something with no face. Mother cried. Father did not cry. Father carried her chair out to the shed and burned it, which frightened me more than anything else, because why burn a chair unless the chair matters?*

*The Guest was quiet all winter. Everyone says that means it is pleased. I think it means it is patient, and those are different words for the same hunger.*

Orsen stood at the kitchen window with the pages in both hands, watching the street. Across the road, Mrs. Aldous waved from her porch. Orsen waved back. Their own smile felt like something worn over the face, a mask handed to them at the town limits.

*Vane,* they thought suddenly. *Ruth Vane.* And then, colder: the woman two doors down, ancient and half-blind, who had not come with a casserole, who had not come at all, whom everyone spoke of in the softened voice reserved for the very old and the very forgotten. *Old Miss Vane keeps to herself now. Sad thing, what happened to her sister. Nobody rightly remembers what.*

*Nobody rightly remembers.* The phrase turned over in Orsen's mind like a stone showing its underside.

The third page came on the fifth morning, and this time Orsen was waiting at the window and saw nothing—no figure, no car, just the page already lying in the box when they opened it at dawn, damp with night air though it hadn't rained.

*December 19th, 1961.*

*Two days until solstice. Ruth is gone. Not dead—I would know, I would feel it—but gone the way a word goes when everyone stops saying it. Her room is a sewing room. Her photograph is a landscape. At church yesterday I said her name aloud and the whole congregation turned to look at me with such kindness that I understood, finally, what the smiles are for.*

*They smile so you won't count the chairs at the table. They smile so you won't count the sisters at the table either.*

*If anyone finds these pages: the heir is chosen, not born. The Guest does not take the willing. It takes the one who hears. Run before the snow. Do not attend the Supper. Do not let them feed you. And if you hear counting—anywhere, from anyone—count yourself away from it.*

Orsen lowered the pages. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Seven years of deathbeds teaches your body to go still when the news is bad.

Outside, the street was bright and ordinary and warm. Somewhere a screen door banged. Somewhere children were laughing. Down the block, Mrs. Aldous was sweeping her walk in slow, contented strokes, and she paused as she swept, and lifted her head, and looked directly across the road at Orsen standing in the window.

She smiled.

And Orsen, who had spent a lifetime telling themselves that patterns were coincidences and voices were symptoms and warmth was just warmth, finally admitted the truth they had been circling since the attic:

The town wasn't welcoming them home.

The town was setting the table.

* * *

That evening, Orsen walked two doors down to the Vane house. The paint was peeling, the garden gone to seed, the windows dark except one. Orsen knocked, and waited, and knocked again, and was turning to leave when the door opened onto darkness and a voice like dry leaves.

"I wondered how long it would take you," said Miss Vane. "Come in before they see you on my step. And Orsen—" the old woman's hand closed around their wrist, cold and strong, "—bring the pages. You'll want to know what happened to my sister. Because it's going to happen to you, if you stay past the solstice."

Behind her, deep in the dark house, something that might have been a clock began to tick—or perhaps to count.
