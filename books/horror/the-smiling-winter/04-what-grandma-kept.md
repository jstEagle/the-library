## 4. What Grandma Kept

The attic stairs were the only thing in the house that still frightened Orsen, and they had been frightening them since before Orsen had words for fear.

They were narrow, unpainted, and steep as a ladder laid at a coward's angle, and the door at the top had been locked for as long as Orsen could remember. When they were six and asked why, Grandma Ada had said, *Because that's where I keep the things that aren't finished.* Orsen had thought it was a joke. Grandmothers said things like that. It was only now, standing in the upstairs hall with the key ring from her nightstand in their hand, that Orsen understood it might have been the truest sentence anyone ever spoke to them.

The chair was up there. They'd carried it down two days ago, or rather, they'd found it already down—sitting at the foot of the attic stairs, warm as a body, patient as one too—and then they'd put it back. Not because they wanted to. Because leaving it downstairs felt like setting a place at the table.

Orsen climbed.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The door swung inward onto darkness that smelled of cedar and dust and something underneath both, faint and organic, like the inside of a violin. Orsen reached for the pull-chain and the bulb came on with a buzz, throwing a cone of yellow light across a room that should have been full of boxes and wasn't.

It was full of boxes. But they were arranged. Neat rows, labeled in Ada's small precise hand: WINTER CLOTHES. HOLIDAY. ORSEN — KEEP. That last one sat alone on a shelf at eye height, close to the ladder, easy to reach. Easy for a child to reach, Orsen thought. If a child had ever been brought up here.

They hadn't. In thirty-one years, Orsen had never once stood in this attic. Every summer visit, every Christmas, every funeral-adjacent holiday, the door had stayed locked and Ada had kept her keys on a chain around her neck, and Orsen had never once wondered about it, because children don't wonder about locked doors, and adults don't wonder about grandmothers.

That was the thing Orsen was learning, day by day, in this house that creaked like a ship: grief was just the beginning of what you didn't know about the people you loved.

They started with the box marked ORSEN — KEEP, because of course they did. Inside: every drawing they'd ever mailed her, flattened and preserved. A baby tooth in an envelope. A cassette tape labeled *O., age 7, singing* that Orsen had no memory of recording. And beneath all of it, wrapped in a tea towel, a photograph Orsen had never seen.

It showed the house, younger, its paint unpeeled. On the porch stood a woman Orsen almost recognized—Ada, forty years old maybe, holding the hand of a girl of perhaps ten. The girl had Orsen's face. Not similar. Identical. Same narrow jaw, same watchful eyes, the look of someone standing slightly outside a room even while photographed inside it.

On the back, in Ada's hand: *M., age 10. She heard them too.*

Orsen sat down on a trunk because their legs suggested it.

*M.* There was no M in the family that Orsen knew of. No aunt, no cousin, no name in any story Ada had told across thirty years of summers. And yet here she was, wearing Orsen's face like a borrowed coat, and here was Ada's handwriting saying *she heard them too* with the flat calm of someone noting a grocery list.

Heard who? Orsen almost wrote it off—the hospice had taught them a hundred ways a grieving mind could bend—but the photographs downstairs were still there, still real, still holding that chair in every frame going back to 1911, closer in each decade like something walking toward the camera one winter at a time. You couldn't rationalize a chair into being. Orsen had tried. They'd spent two nights trying, with the methodical desperation of a man who has spent seven years explaining away the dying counting backwards, and the chair had outlasted every explanation the way stone outlasts rain.

So: *she heard them too.* Orsen put the photograph in their jacket pocket, against their heart, which was stupid and also felt necessary, and went looking for whatever else Ada hadn't finished.

* * *

The journal was under the floorboard beneath the window, which was the kind of hiding place you chose when you expected someone to search and wanted them to find it anyway.

It was a ledger, actually—one of those old cloth-bound accounting books, its spine cracked white—and the first page was dated sixty years ago, almost to the week. Ada's handwriting again, but younger, harder, pressed so deep into the paper it had nearly cut through.

*They asked me tonight if I would take Edith's place when my time comes. I said yes before I understood what yes meant. Aldous says it is an honor. Aldous says many things in a voice like warm bread.*

*Aldous Merrick,* Orsen thought. The name arrived with a face attached—a broad, silver-haired man who had shaken their hand at the funeral with a grip like a manacle and said, *You're home now,* in a tone that had not been a greeting but a verdict.

Orsen read.

The early entries were practical, almost boring. Inventories. *Flour stores good. The Vane girls helped with the salting.* Then, threaded through them, entries in a different register entirely, as though Ada were keeping two books in one:

*Held the door tonight. It is not difficult. You sit in the chair and you do not look behind you and you count your own heartbeat until dawn. What comes through the crack is not a voice. It is more like remembering something you never learned. I wrote my mother's maiden name on the sill afterward and did not know why.*

*Edith's sister came by today asking after Edith. I told her Edith moved away. This is technically true. Everything the town agrees on is technically true.*

*The Guest was restless in January. I sang to it. I do not know the song. My mouth knew it.*

*Guest.* The word sat wrong on the page, domestic and polite, a word for a dinner party. Orsen turned pages faster. Sixty years of entries, thinning out toward the end, Ada's hand growing shakier, and through all of it the same refrain surfacing like a body refusing to stay sunk:

*Every sixty years the door must be held. An heir must sit. Willing, Aldous insists. It must be willing or it does not hold. He has never explained why willingness matters. I think the Guest can tell the difference. I think that is the most frightening thing about it—that it knows the difference between a gift and a theft.*

And then, dated thirty years ago, the entry that stopped Orsen's breath:

*The child came for the summer. O. Six years old and already listening to rooms the way other children listen to music. I watched them stand in the hallway for ten minutes, head tilted, and when I asked what they heard they said "the house is counting" and laughed and ran outside. They laughed, Aldous. They are not afraid of it. Do you understand what that means? We searched twenty years for an heir who wouldn't break. She was in my daughter's belly the whole time.*

*I have begun the stories. Every heir must be prepared. It is not cruelty. It is the opposite of cruelty. A child raised knowing would flinch. A child raised loving will come home.*

Orsen read that paragraph four times.

*Every childhood visit, every story.* The summers. The walks along the creek where Ada pointed out which houses were oldest and which families had lived in them longest. The bedtime tales about the hard first winter, how the founders had survived it, how Hollowmere had never once lost a soul to cold—not in a hundred and eighty years, not once, while towns all around them buried their frozen dead every February. Orsen had loved those stories. Had built whole parts of themselves out of them. Had chosen a career sitting with the dying because Ada used to say, *Somebody ought to keep the door open for people going through it,* and Orsen had thought it was a figure of speech.

It had been a job description.

They set the journal down. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; seven years of hospice work taught you steadiness the way soldiers teach you sleep—anywhere, anytime, on command. But underneath the steadiness something was moving, large and slow, like ice shifting on a lake.

She had groomed them. There was no softer word. Thirty years of engineered affection, calibrated stories, a childhood tuned like an instrument so that one day the instrument could be played. *A child raised loving will come home.* And Orsen had come home, hadn't they, walked straight back to this house the moment the funeral ended, packed nothing, planned nothing, stayed.

But—

And this was the part Orsen kept circling, the part that made betrayal feel like a coat worn inside out—they remembered being happy. All of it. The creek, the stories, the smell of Ada's kitchen. None of it was fake. Whatever Ada had been building toward, the love itself had been load-bearing and real. Orsen had spent seven years watching people die and learning the difference between a lie and a kindness, and Ada had managed, impossibly, to be both at once.

*You sit in the chair and you do not look behind you.*

Orsen looked up at the chair by the ladder. It hadn't followed them up here. It had simply been here when they arrived, the way it had simply been in the photographs, the way everything in Hollowmere simply *was*, pre-existing any explanation offered for it. From this angle, in the bare bulb light, Orsen could see the seat was worn smooth in the center—as if by decades of sitting—and yet the wood was warm. Still warm. Days later. Warm the way a lap is warm.

"I'm not her," Orsen said aloud, to the chair, to the attic, to whoever was keeping the books now. Their voice sounded very small among the boxes. "I'm not Edith. I'm not M. Whoever she was."

The house did not answer. Houses don't. But somewhere below them, in the walls, in the space between the studs where insulation goes, Orsen heard—or imagined, or failed to imagine, the distinction had stopped mattering—a sound like someone settling in to listen.

They read until the light through the attic window went from gray to blue to black. The last entries were the worst, because they were the most ordinary. Grocery lists. A complaint about her knees. And then, three weeks before she died, in a hand gone thin as thread:

*Aldous came again. He says the sixty years are nearly up and asks if I have spoken to O. I lied and said O. never hears anything. First lie I have told the town in sixty years. I am out of practice and it felt wonderful.*

*If you are reading this, O.—and you will be, because I left it where you would find it, because I could not tell you and could not not tell you—I am sorry. Not for preparing you. For not warning you. There is a difference and I only have time to love you, not explain myself.*

*One more thing. The chair does not want a Keeper. It wants company. Remember that when the time comes. Remember that it is lonely. Everyone in this town has forgotten that it is lonely, and forgetting is the one thing we did to it that cannot be forgiven.*

*Count forwards, sweetheart. Never let them teach you to count backwards.*

Orsen closed the journal and held it against their chest and sat in the dark attic for a long time, breathing the dust, feeling the warmth of the chair reach toward them like a hand.

Downstairs, the front doorbell rang.

Once. Polite. Patient.

Through the floorboards, muffled and warm as bread, came Mrs. Vane's voice from the porch—except Orsen had watched Mrs. Vane's funeral procession pass the house yesterday morning, had stood on this very porch and watched them lower her into the ground.

"Orsen, dear," the voice called pleasantly. "I've left something in your mailbox. You'll want to read it before supper."
