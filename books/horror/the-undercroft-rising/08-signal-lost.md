## 8. Signal Lost

The water came up to her knees and then it came up to her waist and then she was counting the alcoves wrong.

Forty-one. She had counted forty-one on the way down — forty-one alcoves like radio booths, each one humming with a voice that wasn't quite a voice, more a memory of broadcast, the shape sound leaves in a room after the room has forgotten it. But now, standing chest-deep in black water with her crowbar gone from her hand and her recorder gone from her pocket, she counted thirty-nine. Then forty-three. Then the numbers stopped behaving altogether, sliding through her fingers like the silt that had turned the floodwater to warm broth.

"Hello?" she said, and heard it twice: once above the water, thin and human, and once below it, slowed and thickened, as if something down there were learning the word by chewing it.

The flashlight was still working. That felt like an insult. Everything else could be lost — the crowbar, the recorder, the hours, apparently — but the flashlight held its little cone of yellow certainty against the dark, and in that cone she saw her own reflection broken across the water's surface, and the reflection was not moving when she moved.

She told herself: current. Refraction. Physics.

She told herself a lot of things, down there. She remembers telling them. What she does not remember — this is the part she returns to, turning it over like a stone looking for the soft underside — is leaving.

* * *

There is a gap.

She wants to be honest about the gap, because honesty is all she has left, and even the honesty has holes in it. There is a gap between the moment she stood in the flooded Undercroft watching her reflection disobey her, and the moment she woke in her own bed with morning coming through the blinds in gray stripes and her alarm clock reading 11:47 a.m., which was impossible, because she had gone down at night, and you don't sleep through a descent into a municipal bathhouse basement, you don't wake from that, you either come back or you don't.

Her bed was dry. Her clothes were folded over the chair — folded, not crumpled, folded with a care she did not possess at three in the morning or ever — and they were damp at the hems, and there was a fine gray grit in the folds, the color of old concrete ground to powder.

Silt. Under her fingernails too, when she looked. Silt packed into the crescents beneath every nail, ten little gray moons, evidence of hands that had been digging, or gripping, or reaching for something under water while the rest of Ulyana Halloran slept in her bed like a good girl, like a woman who had never been anywhere at all.

She sat on the edge of the mattress for a long time. The apartment made its small familiar noises — the radiator ticking, the neighbor's television murmuring through the wall, the refrigerator's dying hum — and each noise sounded rehearsed. Each noise sounded like it was being performed for her benefit, by a city that had been briefed.

"You went down," she said aloud. "You went down and you came back."

But the voice that said it — her voice, coming out of her mouth — had a cadence she didn't recognize. A patience in it. A wetness, almost, though that couldn't be right, voices aren't wet, except hers now was, slightly, the way a recording is wet, the way tape hiss is wet, the way a signal carried through water would be.

* * *

She did what she always did when reality got slippery: she checked the recordings.

The sleep app had been running, because it always ran, because some part of her still needed proof of what happened in the dark even now that she knew exactly what happened in the dark. She scrolled back through the nights. Last night's file was there, timestamped 2:14 a.m. to 6:03 a.m., six hours of her sleeping in her bed, if the app was to be believed, and why wouldn't it be, apps don't lie, apps just record.

Except the file was nine hours long. It began at 9:40 p.m. — before she'd left for the Undercroft, before she'd packed the crowbar and the recorder and the flashlight, before any of it — and it ran until 6:03, seamless, unbroken, one continuous night of Ulyana Halloran asleep in her bed.

She played it at first on fast-forward, listening for the second voice, the wet patient one, the one that answered her questions in her cadence. And it was there, of course. It was always there. But tonight — last night, the impossible seamless night — the two voices had changed places somewhere around hour four, and she had to play it three times to be sure, and even then she wasn't sure, because the exchange was so gradual it might have been happening inside her own ears rather than inside the recording.

At hour two, the second voice says: *Come down.*

At hour five, the voice saying *come down* is hers.

Not similar. Not an imitation. Hers — the exact grain of it, the slight flattening of her vowels that listeners wrote in about, the way she swallowed the ends of sentences. Somewhere between hour two and hour five of a night she spent both asleep and underground, the voices traded coats, and neither one seems to have minded.

She listened to the last twenty minutes at normal speed, sitting very still on her kitchen floor with the phone pressed to her ear like a teenager, like a widow, like someone waiting for a call that matters.

In the final minutes of the recording, the voice that is hers says, softly, to no one, to the dark bedroom, to whatever is lying in the bed:

*"Which one of us do you think they'll miss?"*

And then a laugh. Her laugh. Except she has never laughed like that — low, unhurried, delighted, the laugh of something that has been keeping a joke for fifty years and finally gets to tell it.

* * *

She called in sick. The word *sick* tasted wrong in her mouth; she hadn't missed a shift in three years, not since the calls started, not since the dead began phoning in during the graveyard slot with their obituaries and their studio descriptions and their terrible, gentle patience. Ashgrave After Dark was hers, four hours a night, and she had clung to it the way drowning people cling to things that are actually pulling them under.

So of course she listened to last night's broadcast. Of course she did. She pulled it up on the station archive at 3 p.m., alone in her apartment, and pressed play, and there she was — her voice, warm and low, doing the intro, taking the first caller, doing everything exactly as she always did it.

Except.

The cadence. God, the cadence. Once you hear it you can't stop hearing it. Every sentence ended on a falling note that she never used, a settling note, a note like water finding its level. The pauses were too long and too comfortable, the silences of someone who knows the listener will wait. And when she read the weather — the actual weather, mundane and gray, rain ending by evening — she read it the way you'd read a eulogy, or an invitation.

A caller came on at 1:20 a.m. Some insomniac named Doreen, one of the regulars. And Doreen said, hesitant, sweet: "Honey, are you okay? You sound different tonight."

And the voice that was hers and was not hers said: *"I'm more okay than I have ever been, Doreen. I'm finally all in one place."*

Doreen laughed nervously. The voice laughed with her — that low delighted laugh from the end of the sleep recording — and then it said, almost tenderly: *"Sleep well. All of you."*

All of you.

Ulyana paused the archive and sat with her head between her knees, breathing the way they teach you in the videos, in through the nose, out through the mouth, except every breath smelled faintly of standing water and old concrete, a smell that was not in her apartment, that could not be in her apartment, that was nevertheless in her apartment, in her hair, in her mouth, under her nails.

* * *

Here is what she can reconstruct, and she lays it out like evidence, like a case file about a stranger:

She entered the Undercroft at approximately 12:30 a.m. She knows this because she texted herself a timestamp out of some instinct she didn't understand at the time and understands completely now — a message to the future, a note pinned inside the drowning man's coat. The text is still on her phone: *12:31. Going down. Forty-one alcoves. If I don't come back—* and then nothing, because she'd never finished typing it, or because something had finished it for her and deleted the rest.

She stood in the water among the alcoves. She heard the voices being kept there, archived, maintained. Her recorder played her mother's confession back at her before her mother spoke it — she remembers that, remembers the awful courtesy of it, the thing below letting her preview her own inheritance.

Then: the reflection that didn't move. Then: counting wrong. Then: the gap.

Nine hours of audio say she never left her bed. Gray moons of silt under her nails say she never left the water. Both cannot be true. Both are true. She has become a place where contradictory records coexist, and she recognizes, with a calm that frightens her more than panic would, that this is exactly what the Undercroft is. An archive. A place where things are kept past their endings.

Maybe she went down and something came back wearing her night. Maybe she never left and something went down for her. Maybe the distinction stopped mattering at hour four, when the voices traded coats, and what's sitting in her kitchen drinking cold coffee is simply *the broadcast now*, the signal, continuing, whichever body happens to be nearest the transmitter.

She looks at her hands. She turns them over slowly, palms up, the way you'd check a coin for the year.

Under the nails: silt. Still there. She hasn't washed it out. She realizes she doesn't want to. It's the only thing she has from the night, the only artifact, the only proof of which version was real — and some deep, wet, patient part of her whispers that it isn't proof of anything except that her hands were where her hands were, and that hands, like voices, can be borrowed.

The coffee has gone cold without her noticing. On the counter, her phone lights up. A reminder she doesn't remember setting, timestamped for this exact minute, in her own handwriting converted to type:

**7 p.m. — ASHGRAVE AFTER DARK. Don't be late. It's waiting for airtime.**

She stares at it for a long moment. Then she gets up, and fills a glass of water she won't drink, and begins, methodically, folding her clothes for the night — folding them with a care she does not possess — while somewhere beneath the city, forty-one voices hum in their booths, and one of them, newly returned, practices her name until it sounds like someone else's.
