## 2. Twenty-Six Desks

The enrollment list said nineteen.

I counted twice, because counting twice is what I do, because a number written down is a promise and a number counted is a truth, and I had come to Cold Spring believing the two could be made to agree. Nineteen names in Mrs. Halloway's neat, sloped hand—nineteen children between the ages of eight and thirteen, the whole of Cold Spring's schoolable population gathered into one room under one teacher, which was me, as of the first Monday of September.

The room held twenty-six desks.

I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song—not all at once, but as a small cold wire pulled through the morning. The children sat in the front rows, clustered toward the blackboard and the coal stove the way plants lean toward light, and behind them the room stretched back into shadow, desk after desk, some of them pushed against the far wall as if tidied away, as if stored rather than set. Nineteen occupied. Seven empty.

I told myself it was simple arithmetic of the sort every rural school knows: desks outlast children. A town shrinks, the furniture remains. Cold Spring had the look of a town that had been larger once—second floors shuttered on Main Street, a church with ivy eating its bell tower, a bandstand rotting gently into the square like a drowned thing surfacing. Of course there were extra desks. Of course.

But I am an archivist before I am anything else, and archivists are people who have learned that *of course* is the most expensive phrase in the language.

So while the children copied their penmanship—*diligence, diligence, diligence*, a word I chose for its long patient tail of letters—I walked the back rows with my notebook open, and I looked.

* * *

The first desk told me nothing. Pine, scarred, ink-stained in the way of a hundred years of restless hands. The second told me nothing. The third had a wad of ancient gum fossilized beneath its lip, gray as a slug, and I noted that too, because noting is breathing to me.

It was the fourth desk that stopped my pen.

Someone had carved initials into the wood—not the idle gouges of a bored student but letters cut deep and deliberate, with a blade or a nail, the strokes even, the serifs careful. A craftsman's letters, or a mourner's. *H.M.* And beside them, in smaller characters, a date: *May 1897.*

I ran my thumb over the grooves. The wood inside the cuts was dark, almost black, and smooth as river stone, as though the letters had been rubbed by fingers for a very long time. Or as though the wood itself had aged around them, which was impossible, because the desk could not be older than the school, and the school, according to the plaque by the door, had been built in 1901.

The desk was older than the room it stood in. The desk was older than the school. I stood there with my thumb in the groove of a dead child's initials and felt the first true cold of Cold Spring, and it did not come from the fog outside.

*H.M. May 1897.*

I copied it into my notebook. Date, initials, position of desk—third row, second from the wall. Then I moved to the fifth.

*E.T. — November 1903.*

The sixth: *R.C. — June 1911.*

I worked down the rows while my students' pens scratched in the front of the room, nineteen small hearts beating under nineteen small collars, and I found them one by one, seven desks, seven sets of initials, seven dates, each carved in that same careful, grieving hand. The last was the most recent: *D.A. — October 1981.* Only four years ago. A child who would be a young adult now, if a young adult existed somewhere with those initials, somewhere outside Cold Spring.

The spacing between the dates was not regular. Eleven years, then eight, then fourteen, then—my pen hovered—then a gap of twenty-three years, then six, then three, then four. I wrote the intervals in the margin and stared at them. Not a cycle, then. Not a schedule. Something worse than a schedule: a thing that came when it came, and was waited for, and was fed.

I did not write *fed*. I wrote *occurred*. There is a discipline to fear; I have always kept it the way other people keep a garden. But my hand knew things my mind was not ready to say, and the word *occurred* came out shaky, the *r* doubled over itself like a man flinching.

"Miss Voss?"

I turned. The class had stopped writing. Nineteen faces watched me from the lamplit front of the room, and I saw that I had been in the dark back rows for some time, and that the fog had thickened against the windows until the glass looked frosted from the inside, and that none of the children had looked back here. Not once. Not one of them had turned their head toward the seven desks. They faced forward with the fixed politeness of people riding past an accident.

"It's all right," I said. "Continue your penmanship. *Diligence.*"

They bent to their work as one.

* * *

At lunch I took my notebook to the school's records room, a closet off the principal's office that smelled of cold paper and mouse, and I asked Mrs. Halloway for the old enrollment registers.

"Old ones?" She smiled. She smiles very easily, Mrs. Halloway; it is like watching a door open onto a lit room and being unable to see anyone inside. "Goodness, those go back to the founders. Whatever are you teaching them that requires the 1890s?"

"History," I said. "Local history. Children write best about what they can touch."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true. She considered me for a moment with that easy smile, and then she handed me a brass key on a loop of string, and the smile did not waver, and I understood that I had been permitted something, and that the permitting was itself a message I could not yet read.

The registers were bound in gray cloth, one per decade, their spines soft as old bread. I carried four to the window and began, with the small pleasure that never fails me, the pleasure of the list, the index, the counted thing—I began to reconcile.

Nineteen children currently enrolled. I wrote their names. Then I turned back, decade by decade, and built a second list: every child who had ever sat in that classroom, as far as the registers reached. The penmanship of the clerks changed over the century—copperplate giving way to a cramped pencil, then to ballpoint, then, in the last register, to Mrs. Halloway's sloped hand—but the format never changed. Name, age, date of entry.

I was an hour into it when I understood what I was doing, and my hand went still above the page.

Every child whose initials were carved into those seven desks was absent from the registers.

Not withdrawn—there were no withdrawal codes, no *moved away*, no *transferred to county school*. The entries simply stopped. A name would appear in September, appear again in October, appear in November, and then—nothing. No line, no note, no closing entry. As if the clerk had looked away from the page for a moment and when he looked back the child was a thing that had never been written down, and the page had healed over the absence like water.

I checked the dates against my desk list until the light went gray. H.M., May 1897—*Harriet Mowbray*, entered 1893, last recorded entry November 1896. E.T., November 1903—*Edmund Thackery*. R.C., June 1911—*Ruth Callow*. All of them. Seven names, seven entries that stopped mid-breath, seven children the ledger had digested without leaving a bone.

And here was the thing that made me close the register and hold it against my chest for a moment, the way you'd hold a door shut: the ink of the final entries was not faded. Every other line in those books had faded to the brown of tea, but the last written line of each vanished child was as black and wet-looking as the day it was set down. As if the ink refused to age. As if the entries were not past but *present*—ongoing, unfinished, waiting for a clerk to come back and complete them.

I copied everything. Dates, names, intervals, the strange black lines. My pen moved fast and my handwriting, usually so disciplined, slanted downhill like something running.

* * *

The fog came in early that afternoon, earlier than the previous days, pressing at the windows like a face. By three o'clock the light in the classroom had gone the color of dishwater, and I lit the lamps, and the children's shadows swung enormous against the walls, and still they did not look back at the seven desks.

I dismissed them at four. They left in a quiet, orderly stream, coats buttoned to the chin, and each one, without exception and without appearing to coordinate it, glanced at me as they passed—a quick, assessing look, the look of people checking whether a thing they expect to happen is happening. The last one out was a girl I had noticed before, small and sharp-faced, with dark hair cut blunt across her brow and eyes the gray of the lake in November. Wren Aldous, said the register. The only child in the room who had looked at the back rows that morning, I realized now. Only her. She had looked, and then she had looked at me, and she had not looked since.

She did not leave with the others.

I heard her come back up the aisle while I was erasing the board, a small deliberate tread, and I turned, and she was standing in the doorway of the lamplight with her coat on and her schoolbag gripped in both hands like a shield, and she was looking at me with an expression I had never received from a nine-year-old before. It was pity. It was pity, and under it, something like apology.

"You counted them," she said.

It was not a question. I set down the eraser. "Counted what, Wren?"

"The desks." She came one step closer, and her voice dropped, though there was no one left in the building to hear, and perhaps that was exactly why she dropped it—because she believed otherwise. "You counted them, and you wrote it down. In your book. I saw you."

"Twenty-six desks for nineteen students," I said. "Old desks. A town gets smaller, the furniture—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard, and for a moment she was not a child at all; she was an old woman standing in a small body, a sentry, a keeper of a gate. "Don't do the thing where you say the ordinary reason. Everyone does the ordinary reason. My mother does the ordinary reason. The ordinary reason is how it keeps working."

The lamps hissed. Outside, the fog had swallowed the schoolyard whole; the flagpole, the fence, the road, all of it gone, as though the school were a ship and the world had drowned.

"Then tell me the other reason," I said.

She looked at the seven desks for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed—it had the cadence of something recited, learned young and repeated often, a catechism passed hand to hand in whispers.

"They're not extra," she said. "They're *theirs*. The school keeps them. You can't move them—I saw Mr. Pruitt and two other men try, the year I was in first grade. They got them as far as the doorway and then the doorway wouldn't—" She stopped, restarted. "The desks wouldn't go through. They went back where they are, by themselves, after. Nobody talks about it. But everybody knows the desks are waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

She looked up at me then, and I saw that her eyes were wet and that she was furious about it, jaw tight, holding the tears the way she held the schoolbag.

"For the rest of them," she said. "For whoever's next. That's why we sit in front. That's why nobody turns around. If you don't look at them, they don't—" Her breath caught. "Tomas used to sit in the back. Did you notice? Before. He liked the back. He said it was quiet back there."

*Tomas.* The name snagged in me like a fishhook. I had a Tomas on my enrollment list—had had one, I realized, and could not, even now, summon his face with any certainty, only the fact of the name and a vague impression of a cowlick and a gap-toothed grin that might have been a photograph I invented. The absence where his face should have been made a small cold sound inside me, like ice settling on a lake.

"Wren," I said, carefully. "Where is Tomas now?"

Her chin trembled once and steadied. "You know where," she whispered. "You counted the desks. There's twenty-six now. There were twenty-six when you came. There were twenty-five when Tomas was here."

The arithmetic went through me like a nail through paper. I thought of the black, unfaded ink. I thought of the clerk looking away, and the page healing over. I thought: *the register is not a record. The register is the ritual. The writing is the feeding.*

"You have to go home, Miss Voss." She was backing toward the door now, and the fog beyond it seemed to lean in to receive her. "You have to go home before dark. That's the other rule. The desks are for the day, but the dark is for—" She shook her head hard, refusing the sentence its ending. "Just don't stay past dark. Not in the school. Not anywhere in Cold Spring. Go home, lock your door, and whatever hums, don't hum back."

"Hums," I repeated. "What hums?"

But she was already gone, out into the white, her small footsteps fading on the boardwalk faster than a child should run, and I stood alone in the lamplit room with twenty-six desks and my open notebook, and I wrote down everything she had said, every word, because writing it down is how I keep the world from dissolving, because a thing recorded can be fought.

Then I sat down at my own desk—the teacher's desk, the twenty-seventh, the one that faced them all—and I looked at the seven carved names in the lamplight, and I noticed for the first time what I should have noticed hours ago, what the archivist in me had been refusing to see since the moment my thumb found that first groove.

The dates were not random. I had been wrong. I laid my notebook open beside the last carved desk and checked the intervals again, and there it was, plain as a confession: the gaps were shrinking. Eleven years, then eight, then six, then three, then four. The thing that came, came when it came—but it was coming *faster*. Whatever Cold Spring fed, whatever the desks waited for, it was growing impatient. It was growing *hungry*.

And the last date carved into the last desk was October 1981.

Four years ago. And the intervals, shrinking. Three years, two, one. The next date, if the pattern held, was due now. This autumn. This fog season. This classroom, with its nineteen children sitting very carefully in the front rows, not looking back.

I blew out the lamps, locked the schoolroom door with hands that were not quite steady, and stepped out into a fog so thick it seemed to have weight, to press on my shoulders like a wet coat. Somewhere out in the white, faint and far and impossible to place, something was humming.

I did not hum back.

I walked home fast, and I did not look behind me, and all the way down the empty street my notebook thumped against my ribs like a second heart, and I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only after the point at which it could do any good, that I had not come to Cold Spring to teach children penmanship.

I had come to keep the record. And the record, I now understood, was not finished with me.
