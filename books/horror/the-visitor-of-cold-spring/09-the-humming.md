## 9. The Humming

The hum came to Odile on a Thursday, in the last hour of the school day, while she was writing *conscientious* on the board in her careful archivist's hand.

She had been teaching penmanship to eleven children—eleven, always eleven now, though the roster in her desk said twelve and the amended roster in the office said eleven and she had stopped arguing with paper in Cold Spring—and the radiator beneath the windows had been ticking its ordinary tick, and the fog had pressed its ordinary gray face against the glass, and everything was exactly as wrong as it had been for weeks, which is to say: unremarkably, unbearably wrong.

Then the radiator stopped ticking.

Not the heat—the heat continued, thin and mineral-smelling as ever—but the tick, the small metallic heartbeat of the building, ceased between one syllable and the next, and in the silence that opened beneath it Odile heard the hum.

It was not a sound, exactly. It was the memory of a sound, the way a bruise is the memory of a blow. It came up through the floorboards, through the soles of her shoes, through the arches of her feet, and it was pitched so low that she understood at once why no one else heard it: it was below hearing the way the lake was below the town, the way the ledger was below the school, the way everything true in Cold Spring was buried beneath something ordinary.

*Hmmmmmmmm.*

The children kept writing. Their pencils moved in their small synchronized rhythms, and Odile watched Wren—Wren, whose left hand had developed a tremor she tried to hide by pressing her wrist flat against the desk—watched Wren go still, her pencil hovering, her head tilting by a degree, like a bird that has heard something under the snow.

Then Wren looked up and met Odile's eyes across the room, and Odile saw in the girl's face the thing she had been dreading since the ledger: recognition. *You hear it too. Now you're one of us.*

Odile wrote *conscientious* a second time, though no one had asked, and her hand did not shake, and she was proud of that, and ashamed that she was proud.

"Feet flat," she said, and her voice came out steady. "Spine straight. We finish the line."

The hum did not stop. But it changed, and this was worse: it *noticed* her. She felt it turn, the way you feel someone's attention on the back of your neck in an empty room. Somewhere below the floorboards, below the boiler room, below the coal-dark foundations of a building that had stood on this ground since before the fog had a name, something that had been humming to itself for a hundred and twenty-seven years had paused, mid-note, and listened.

And then, so softly she could have imagined it, it resumed—but warmer. Welcoming.

Like a host who has heard the front door open.

* * *

She did not go down that night. She wanted the record to show that she had not gone down that night.

She went home instead, to the rented house on Larkspur Lane with its sagging porch where Pim sat—Pim, who sat there every evening now, in every weather, small and patient and unchanged, its button eyes catching the porch light and holding it without reflection. She had stopped trying to make it speak. She had started, instead, keeping it company, which she understood was a different thing and possibly a worse one.

She sat on the steps with her notebook on her knees and wrote by the light of the kitchen window:

*Oct. 23. The hum is audible to me now. Wren heard it first—she described it weeks ago as "under the floorboards," and I filed it under childish metaphor, which may be the last mistake of my professional life. It is not a metaphor. It is a frequency. It is a summons. It is, I think, an invitation, and I am apparently on the guest list.*

*Question: why now? Why me? Hypothesis: because I read the ledger. Because I put my hands on the founders' names and read them aloud in the dark like a fool reading a menu in a restaurant that serves her. Hypothesis: because I was always going to. Hypothesis, the one I don't want to write: because it has been waiting for someone who could hear it, and hearing is a kind of consent.*

She looked up at Pim.

It had not moved. It never moved when she watched it; movement happened only in the periphery, in the fog, in the corner of the eye, in the space between blinks. But tonight its head was tilted—tilted toward the school, toward the east, toward the direction of the hum—and one small hand, the felt one with the worn thumb where nine-year-old Odile had worried it smooth through two years of bad nights, was raised.

Pointing.

"No," Odile said.

The hand did not lower.

"I said no. Whatever's down there, whatever you want me to—no. I don't take directions from things that sit on porches."

Pim's head tilted the other way. And though it had no mouth that moved, though its face was the smooth unfinished face of a thing a child had imagined without quite knowing what faces needed, Odile felt—distinctly, unmistakably—its meaning arrive in her the way meaning arrives in dreams: not as words but as certainty.

*Not a summons. A task.*

"Those aren't different things," she said, and her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and she wrote the crack down later because she wrote everything down, because writing was the only prayer she had ever believed in.

* * *

She lasted four days. She was not proud of this either, but she noted it, because the record mattered: four days of teaching eleven children penmanship and arithmetic and the state capitals while the hum rose beneath them like water in a well, four days of sleeping with her notebook open on her chest, four days of Pim on the porch with its pointing hand, until on the fourth night the fog came down so thick that the streetlamps burned as gray coins suspended in nothing, and Odile stood at her window and watched the school—three blocks away, invisible, but she could feel it the way you feel a tooth—she could feel it humming, and she understood that she was not going to be able to not go.

She dressed warmly. This seemed important, absurdly important, a superstition of the flesh: wool socks, two sweaters, her father's old scarf. She filled a thermos of coffee she would not drink. She took the notebook, two fountain pens, a box of matches, and the flashlight she'd bought the week Tomas disappeared and never used, because using the flashlight would have meant admitting the dark was worth preparing for.

Pim was on the porch.

"You're not coming," Odile said.

Pim stood. It had never stood before. It stood the way furniture would stand, if furniture loved you—slowly, jointlessly, with the terrible patience of objects—and it fell into step beside her in the fog, three feet to her left, exactly where it had walked beside her at nine years old, on the lane behind her mother's house, in the year she did not think about, in the year she had built it out of loneliness and grief and whatever raw material children's imaginations are made of, and it walked beside her now, and she did not have the strength to tell it to leave, because the truth—the truth she had not yet written down, the truth waiting in her notebook like a knife in a drawer—was that she did not want it to leave.

The streets were empty. Cold Spring emptied early these days; the shopkeepers had stopped meeting her eyes, and now they had stopped lighting their back windows, and the town at night was a mouth with the lights gone out behind its teeth. She walked east on Founders Street, past the church with its white steeple dissolving upward into fog, past the war memorial with its list of names that she had checked, twice, against the ledger, and found wanting.

The school rose out of the fog like the idea of a school. Its windows were dark. Its door was unlocked, because it was always unlocked, because Cold Spring had never in its history locked a door against anything that mattered.

Inside, the smell of chalk and floor wax and, beneath it, faintly, the lake. Odile's flashlight beam moved down the corridor, over the trophy case with its yellowed photographs of children's faces, over the drinking fountain, over the door of her own classroom with its paper pumpkin still taped up from October. The hum was louder here. Not louder—*closer*. It had stopped being a sound in the floor and become a sound in the walls, in the plaster, in the bones of the building, and it had a rhythm now that she could almost parse, almost resolve into language, the way you can almost resolve a voice through a wall into words.

She followed it to the basement door.

She had been down once before, to find the ledger, and she had told herself she would never go down again, and here she was, and the record would show it, and the record was all she had. The stairs went down into dark that her flashlight pushed back grudgingly, a beam like a held breath. The boiler room door stood open on its black rectangle. She did not go in. The hum was not in the boiler room. The hum was behind the far wall—the north wall, the one that backed onto the old foundation, the part of the school built in 1897 and never renovated because, she now understood, you do not renovate a wound.

She put her palm against the wall.

The plaster was cold. Not stone-cold, not winter-cold—*lake*-cold, the cold of water that has never once been warm, and through her hand the hum entered her body entire, and she gasped, and she did not pull her hand away, because in the hum, now that it was in her, she could hear—she could almost hear—

*Words.*

Someone speaking. A man's voice, worn down by a century of vibration into a thread, but a voice, a human voice, saying something, saying it over and over and over, saying it the way a record says its last groove, and the words were—

"—and the bargain shall hold so long as the offering is made freely, and the town shall know warmth, and the town shall know—"

It stopped. It stopped the way it had always stopped, she understood suddenly, the way it had stopped in 1897, mid-sentence, mid-word, because the man who was speaking it had *fled*, had turned and run with his candle guttering, and the sentence had hung in the air of this room for a hundred and twenty-seven years, unfinished, and everything—every child, every ledger entry, every renewal, every generation of postponed horror—everything was the echo of an unfinished sentence.

Odile stood with her hand on the wall and her breath smoking in the cold and she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *It's a door. It's a door that was never closed. It's a door that can't close because someone ran before he finished closing it.*

And behind her, in the dark of the basement, small and patient and unchanged, something said:

"There."

Not Pim's voice. Not a child's voice. A voice like the hum given a throat, a voice she had heard once before, at nine years old, through a floorboard, through a wall, through the thin place where her brother had gone down and something else had come up wearing the shape of her own invented friend.

She turned. The flashlight found Pim, standing by the boiler room door, and Pim's arm was raised, and Pim was pointing—not at the wall, not at the hum, but at the baseboard, at a seam in the plaster low near the floor, a seam she would never have seen, a seam shaped like nothing, like a crack, like the outline of a door that had been plastered over with the rest of 1897.

"Open it," the voice said, from Pim's unmoving mouth. "Finish it."

"You finish it," Odile whispered. "You're the one who—"

"We cannot," Pim said, and for the first time in twenty years, in two lifetimes, in all the fog-bound years of Cold Spring, the small figure's shoulders bent, and it looked, for one unbearable moment, not like a monster and not like a friend but like a prisoner at a window, watching someone walk past who might hold the key. "Only the one who was loved. Only the one who loved. Only—"

The hum surged, and the words drowned in it, and the seam in the baseboard breathed—actually breathed, the plaster swelling and subsiding like the flank of a sleeping animal—and Odile Voss, who wrote everything down, stood in the cold dark beneath the school with her flashlight shaking in her hand, and understood that the record she had been keeping all this time was not a record at all.

It was a draft.

The sentence was waiting to be finished. And somewhere in her notebooks, in her careful archivist's hand, in the pages she had filled since the day she arrived—the answer, or the words, or the price—was already written.

Pim pointed one last time, at the wall, at the seam, at her.

Then the hum faded to a thread, the cold receded like a tide, and the basement was only a basement, and Odile was alone with the unfinished sentence and the sound of her own heart, saying what it had been saying since the night she arrived in Cold Spring:

*You know what it wants. You have always known. Go home and read what you wrote.*

She climbed the stairs. Behind her, faint as breath on glass, the hum followed her up—patient, warm, and waiting.
