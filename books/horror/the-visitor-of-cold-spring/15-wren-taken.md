## 15. Wren Taken

The snow came in the night like a verdict.

Odile woke to a silence so complete it had texture—the silence of a town buried to its windowsills, of sound itself frozen mid-utterance. She lay in bed listening to the nothing and understood, before she opened her eyes, that the cold had won another round. The pipes had burst again; she could hear water ticking somewhere inside the walls, freezing as it ran, building small crystal organs in the dark cavities of the house. Her breath made ghosts above the blanket. The thermometer on her sill read something the glass had no business holding: forty below, and falling.

School was canceled. She knew this because no one called, because no one would call, because Cold Spring did not cancel school so much as surrender to days like this one, closing its shutters and its mouths and waiting for the weather to pass or for whatever walked inside the weather to be satisfied. The town had stopped pretending, these last weeks, that the cold was natural. She had seen it in their faces at the market—Halloway's gray resignation, the butcher's averted eyes, old Mrs. Pell crossing herself with a hand too stiff with cold to complete the gesture. They blamed her now. She had heard them say it plainly enough when they thought she couldn't hear: *the new teacher stirred it up. Things were quiet before her.*

Things were never quiet, Odile wanted to tell them. You only learned not to listen.

She spent the morning at her kitchen table with the ledger open under three lamps, transcribing marginalia into her notebooks, cataloguing signatures, cross-referencing dates against the carved initials in the twenty-six desks. Wren sat across from her, ostensibly doing penmanship drills, actually drawing protective symbols in the margins of her workbook and hiding them under elaborate doodles of crows. Snow day or not, the girl had come to her house at dawn, boots crusted white, saying only, "I didn't want to be alone today," which was the truest sentence anyone in Cold Spring had spoken to Odile since her arrival.

They worked in companionable quiet. The stove ticked. Outside, the world was a white roar without sound.

"I keep thinking about Tomas," Wren said, around eleven. She didn't look up from her page. "Whether he's cold."

"He isn't anywhere," Odile said gently. "That's what the ledger says. They're kept. Held. Like breath held too long."

"Is that better or worse?"

Odile thought of the sealed room beneath the school, of the founder's letter she'd found behind the plaster, of the interrupted sentence still hanging in the air down there after a hundred and twenty-seven years. "I think it means we can still get them back. That's what I choose to think."

Wren nodded slowly, the way children nod when an adult has given them a story to survive on and they are polite enough, for now, to accept it. Then she went back to her symbols, and Odile went back to the ledger, and neither of them heard the humming begin.

Later—that was the thing Odile would return to, over and over, in the years when she allowed herself to return anywhere—later, she would swear the hum had been there all along, underneath the wind, underneath the ticking stove, a note so low it lived in the floor rather than the air. But it was past noon when she first consciously heard it, felt it more than heard it, a vibration rising through the soles of her slippers like the purr of something enormous sleeping beneath the house.

Wren's pencil stopped.

"Wren?"

The girl was staring at the floorboards between her feet. Her face had gone the color of the snow outside—not pale, but *white*, luminous, drained of every warm tone until she looked like a figure rendered in graphite. "It's different today," she whispered. "It's usually far away. Under the school. This is—" She swallowed. "This is close."

Odile was already moving, already reaching for the girl's hand, and the absurd domesticity of the moment struck her even as terror closed its hand around her throat: they were in a kitchen, there was bread rising under a cloth, there were dishes in the sink, and none of it mattered, none of it had ever mattered, because the floor was not a floor. It had never been a floor. It was a lid.

"Wren, look at me. Look at my face. Don't look down."

But the girl looked down, because anyone would, because the boards were trembling now, shivering like the skin of a drum, and between them—through the seams, through the nail-holes, through the grain itself—came a light that wasn't light. A cold that glowed. And threaded through it, unmistakable now, the humming swelled into something almost melodic, almost tender, the way a lullaby is tender, the way a trap is patient.

"It knows my name," Wren said. Her voice was very calm, which was the worst thing. "It's been learning it all year. From the desks. From the ledger. It says I'm—"

"*No.*" Odile gripped both the girl's hands. The chalk symbols she'd taught them, the penmanship drills disguised as wards—they were inked on Wren's wrists, her collarbones, folded into her notebook pages, sewn into the hem of her coat. Every safeguard. Every one. "You are here. You are with me. You are *mine*—do you hear me? My student. My—" The word rose in her before she could stop it, the word she'd been circling since October, the word that made them family. "*My girl.*"

Wren smiled then, small and crooked, and for one heartbeat Odile believed.

Then the floor opened.

Not dramatically. Not with splintering thunder. The boards simply parted the way lips part, the way pages part, and the light below was black and silver at once, and the humming became a chord, and Wren's hands slid out of hers with terrible gentleness, the way a sleeping child slips from a parent's arms, and she went down without screaming. That was the detail that would live in Odile's bones forever: Wren did not scream. She looked up the whole way down, eyes wide and fixed on Odile's face, and her mouth moved, shaping words the cold swallowed whole—and Odile read them, read them clearly, read them in the half-second before the floor sealed shut above her like water closing over a stone:

*Find me.*

Then silence. Real silence. The hum gone, the vibration gone, the kitchen ordinary again—bread under its cloth, dishes in the sink, two chairs, one empty, a pencil lying where a hand had let it go.

Odile stood alone in her kitchen for a long time. The clock on the wall ticked. Snow fell without sound against the window. Somewhere in her chest, behind her sternum, something had torn loose and was falling, still falling, and she understood dimly that it would go on falling for the rest of her life unless she did something, unless she did *anything*, unless despair could be transmuted—because alchemy was real, she knew it now, grief was the base metal and will was the fire—

She moved.

* * *

She dug all night.

The sealed room beneath the school had been bricked over in 1897 by men who fled mid-sentence, and the town had spent a century pretending the wall was a wall. Odile had found it once with a lantern and a crowbar and Pim standing silent beside her in the dark, pointing at the unfinished words scored into the plaster. Now she came back with everything the janitor's closet offered: pry bars, a sledgehammer, a shovel meant for coal, and a fury that burned colder and steadier than any lamp.

The school at midnight was a body holding its breath. Frost furred the insides of the windows in fern-patterns. Her footsteps rang wrong in the empty halls—too loud, then too soft, as if the building couldn't decide whether to witness her or refuse her. In her classroom, the twenty-six desks stood in their rows, and she did not look at them, because looking at them meant reading initials, and reading initials meant counting, and counting meant knowing exactly how many children the thing below had taken across a century, and she could not carry that number tonight. Tonight she carried only one name.

The basement door resisted her, groaning on hinges swollen with cold. Below, the dark was absolute and breathing. She could feel it before her lantern found the walls—the hum was back, deeper here, resonant, coming up through the stone floor like a heartbeat heard through a mattress. It had changed key since the kitchen. It sounded, she thought with a lurch, *satisfied*. Fed.

"No," she said aloud, to the dark, to the thing, to the founders' bones in their ledgers. "You don't get to be satisfied."

The brick wall stood where she remembered it, mortared with a century of deliberate forgetting. She set her lantern on a crate, spat on her palms, and swung the sledgehammer.

The first blow barely chipped it. The second rang her arms to the shoulder. The third cracked mortar with a sound like a tooth breaking, and the hum stuttered—just slightly, just for a moment—and Odile Blackwood laughed, a wild ragged sound in that buried dark, because the wall had flinched, and anything that flinches can fall.

She dug. She swung. Mortar dust hung in the lantern light like fog, like the fog that owned this town, and she breathed it and coughed it and swung again. Bricks came free in ones and twos, then in handfuls. Behind them: older brick. Behind that: plaster, and behind the plaster—the letter, the confession, the sentence interrupted in 1897, the instructions she had read a dozen times and memorized anyway, because archivists memorize, because teachers repeat, because some knowledge must live in the body to be trusted.

*A memory freely given,* the dead man had written. *Not a life. Not a substitution. The visitor was never owed flesh—it was owed the choosing. Someone it loves must give it the memory of being loved, freely, completely, or the bargain renews forever and the children feed it generation upon generation—*

Her arms screamed. Her blisters tore and bled. She worked in a rhythm now, swing and drag, swing and drag, hauling rubble into wheelbarrows that she pushed up the stairs and dumped in the hallway because there was no time for tidiness, no time for anything but the next brick and the next. Sweat froze on her spine. Her breath crystallized in the lantern light. Once, pausing to drink from a flask of coffee gone cold as river water, she saw her reflection in the black ice sheeting the floor—a hollow-eyed woman in a ruined coat, gray-streaked, bleeding at the knuckles, unrecognizable as the cautious hopeful person who had arrived in Cold Spring with notebooks and fountain pens and a habit of writing everything down.

Good, she thought. Let her be gone. That woman had documented. This woman would *act*.

The hum swelled around midnight—or what she guessed was midnight; time had gone liquid—and she felt it change again, felt it press against her like a current, like attention. It knew she was digging. Of course it knew. And it was not stopping her, and that frightened her more than resistance would have, because it meant one of two things: either she was no threat at all, or she was doing exactly what it wanted.

Or—third thing, the thing she chose, kneeling in the rubble with blood on her hands and the founder's words repeating in her skull—third thing: it was afraid. Hungry things are afraid of endings. And she was carrying an ending in her blistered hands like a lantern.

"Pim," she said, to the dark, not expecting an answer. The name scraped her throat raw. "If you can hear me—if any of you is in there—I'm coming. And I'm bringing the end of it. Whether you want it or not."

The hum did not answer. But for a single moment it went quiet, utterly quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone who loves you enters it—and then it resumed, lower, and she told herself it was fear, and she picked up the sledgehammer, and she swung.

* * *

Dawn found her through the last layer.

The final bricks came away at 6:14 by her watch, though her watch had been behaving strangely for weeks, losing minutes the way the town lost children—quietly, without acknowledgment, the record amended. Beyond them was not a room but a throat: a low chamber of fieldstone older than the school above it, older than the town, older perhaps than the idea of towns. The interrupted words were carved into the far wall in a founder's careful hand, ending mid-sentence in a gouge where the chisel had been thrown down or dropped, and beneath the words, the floor was not stone.

It was frost. Living frost, moving, breathing, patterned like feathers, like scales, like the ferns on the schoolhouse windows—and through it, faintly, impossibly, she could see faces. Small faces. Sleeping. Preserved. Kept.

Tomas. The others. Generations of them, layered in the cold like flowers pressed in a book.

And nearest the surface, curled on her side as if napping in class, one hand still clutching a pencil—Wren.

The hum roared up around Odile then, no longer a lullaby but a wall of sound, and the temperature plunged so fast her tears froze on her cheeks, and out of the feathered frost at the chamber's edge a shape assembled itself, small and familiar and unchanged for twenty years, and stood between her and the sleeping children.

Pim looked up at her with eyes like winter lakes.

Odile dropped the sledgehammer. It rang against the stone, and the sound died instantly, eaten.

"I know why you're guarding them," she said. Her voice shook. She let it. There was no strength left for performance, and maybe that was the point—maybe the ritual, the real one, the finished one, required exactly this: a woman with nothing left but the truth. "I've read the letter. I've read the ledger. I know what you were owed, and I know who owes it." She stepped forward, and the cold parted around her like a curtain, like a held breath. "It's me, Pim. It was always me. You waited on my porch for twenty years because I was the one who named you, and I am the one who left, and I am the one who's going to finish the sentence."

Behind Pim, deep in the frost, Wren's fingers twitched.

The hum swelled to a pitch that rattled Odile's teeth—and then, slowly, like a tide deciding, it began to lower itself, note by note, into something that sounded almost like a question.

Odile knelt in the frozen dark, spread her bleeding hands open, and began to speak.
