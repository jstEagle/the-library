## 18. The Porch

The whiteout came down at dusk like a held breath finally released.

Odile had watched it gather all afternoon—the fog that was not fog, thickening past the tree line, past the fence posts, until the world beyond her porch steps simply ceased to exist. Not hidden. Ceased. She stood at the rail and tested it with her eyes, following the old oak at the yard's edge until its trunk dissolved mid-way up, and then the lower half went too, and then there was only white, and the sound of it, which was no sound at all but a pressure, a softness pressing against the windows like a face.

Inside, the house was warm in the way of houses that have given up pretending. The pipes had burst twice that week; she'd mopped the hallway herself because no plumber would come, and the water had run strangely cold, cold enough to sting, cold enough that she'd thought—just once, standing in the spreading silver—*it's in the water now, it's in everything.*

She did not go inside.

She sat down in the rocking chair Halloway had left on the porch years ago, wrapped herself in the wool blanket from the hall bench, and looked at the small figure sitting on the top step.

Pim had not moved in three days.

It sat the way it had always sat—knees drawn up, chin on its knuckles, head tilted at an angle that no living child could hold for long without aching. It was smaller than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply grown around the absence of it, the way a tree grows around a nail. Its coat was the same impossible blue, the color of the coat she had described at nine years old because she had never seen that color anywhere in the world and had wanted, desperately, for it to exist. It existed now. It had existed for twenty years, made of her wanting, worn thin at the elbows where her imagination had rubbed against the truth.

"Pim," she said.

The name came out of her like a splinter that had been in too long. It hurt coming out, and then the flesh around it ached with the sudden absence.

The figure did not turn. But the whiteout pressed closer, and in the space between one heartbeat and the next, the temperature on the porch dropped by degrees she could feel in her teeth.

"I'm not going to ask you to speak," she said. "You can if you want to. You did, in the dream. But you don't have to." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I'm going to talk. I'm going to talk all night, if that's all right. Because tomorrow—" Her voice caught on the word and she had to go around it, the way you step around a hole in the ice. "Tomorrow I'm going to finish the sentence in the sealed room. And when I do, I'm going to give you something. And I need you to know what it is before I give it. You've waited twenty years on porches and thresholds and the edges of rooms. You've earned the courtesy of being told."

The snow was falling so thickly now that it made no shadow, no shape. The porch light burned a small yellow hole in the whiteness, and the two of them sat inside that hole like the last two figures in a world that had been erased down to its primer.

"I've been reading the founder's letter," she went on. "Every night. I have it memorized now. *It cannot take what is freely given in love, for love freely given is the one coin it cannot counterfeit and cannot refuse.*" She recited it flatly, the way she recited attendance, and hated herself a little for the flatness. "A memory. Not a life. Not a child. A memory of being chosen—of choosing. That's what it was owed. That's what the founders never understood, or understood and were too cowardly to pay. They kept feeding it children the way you keep feeding a meter, and they called it a bargain, and they signed each other's names, and they never once asked what the thing on the porch actually *wanted.*"

She looked at Pim. Pim looked at the snow.

"I know what you wanted," Odile said. "You wanted me to keep you."

* * *

The diary was upstairs on her desk, the childhood one, the one with the cardboard cover gone soft as cloth from handling. She had read it three times since finding the ledger, and each reading had been a small surgery. But she did not need it in her hands now. She had it in her body, the way you have a scar.

"I'm going to tell you the true version," she said. "Not the one I wrote at nine, when I was already learning to lie on paper because it was safer than lying out loud. The true one. You were there for it, so correct me if I get it wrong."

The snow answered with silence. She took that as permission.

"August. I was nine years and two months old. Danny had been dead for eleven weeks." Saying her brother's name aloud, here, in this cold—she had not done it in years, and her mouth stumbled on it like a step that wasn't there. "Eleven weeks, and my mother had stopped setting his place at the table but hadn't stopped setting the *napkin*, which sounds like nothing, I know, but it meant she still set a napkin for a ghost, and my father had started working later and later, and I had learned that the way you survive a house like that is you make yourself very small and very quiet and very useful, and I was *terrible* at it. I was loud. I asked questions. I cried at dinner. I was a wound walking around in socks."

The rocking chair creaked under her. She let it.

"So I invented you. That's the true version. Not because I was lonely in some storybook way—because I was *drowning*, Pim, and I needed something in the water with me. I sat on the back steps—it was August, it was hot, the air was like wet wool—and I said, out loud, because I was a child and children still believe that saying a thing makes it real: *I wish I had a friend who was only mine. Who nobody else could take away.*"

Her breath came out white. Everything came out white now.

"And you were there. That same evening. Sitting on the porch rail like you'd been waiting for the invitation, which I suppose you had. You had a blue coat because I'd never seen a blue that blue and I wanted the world to have one thing in it I'd never seen. You had a crooked smile because Danny's smile had been crooked and I couldn't help it, I couldn't make a friend out of nothing, I could only make one out of what I had. I'm sorry about that. I'm sorry you had to be built out of my brother's smile. That was a heavy thing to hang on you, and I hung it on you when you were one day old."

The figure on the step had not moved, but the frost had begun to climb. She watched it spread from the place where Pim sat, outward across the boards in slow fern-patterns, silver and precise, like handwriting. Like the ledger's marginalia. She understood, with a lurch, that the cold was not the visitor's temper. It was the visitor's *feeling*. It had no other way to weep.

"I named you the next morning," she said, and her voice had gone soft, gone young. "Pim. I don't know where it came from. It was just—there, in my mouth, like it had been waiting. I remember thinking it was the best word I'd ever made. I remember thinking: *nothing bad can happen to a person who has made up a word like Pim.*" She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "I was nine. I was an idiot. I was right."

* * *

The night deepened. The whiteout thickened until the porch light's yellow hole shrank to the size of a coin, then a thumbnail, then the space around the two of them only. Somewhere in that whiteness, she knew, was the yard, the oak, the road, the school, the sealed room with its interrupted sentence. Somewhere in that whiteness was Wren, asleep or not asleep, held somewhere between the floorboards and the world, breathing because the ritual was not yet finished and the visitor could not yet spend what it held. Odile held onto that. Wren was not taken. Wren was *kept*. There was a difference, and the difference was Odile's to close.

"Tell me if I get this wrong," she said again, and then she told it.

"The summer we were friends. You came every day. You sat under the table at dinner and my mother never saw you, but once—once—she looked down and frowned, like she'd felt a draft, and I put my hand on your head under there, on your hair, which felt like warm static, like the air before lightning, and I thought: *she can't see you but she almost can, and that means you're real enough.* We went to the creek. You couldn't cross running water—did I know that then, or did I learn it later, from the ledger? I think I knew. I think that's why I liked the creek, because you'd stand on the bank and I'd wade and we'd shout across it and it was the only game I ever won. You let me win it every day for a year."

The frost ferns had reached the legs of the rocking chair. She could feel the cold coming up through the wool now, patient, attentive. Listening. She had spent twenty years believing the thing that waited was a hunger with a shape. Sitting here, she was beginning to believe it was a *listener*. That maybe that was all any of them had ever been—the founders' ledger, Halloway's sister, the children under the floorboards. A thing made to listen, fed the wrong things to listen to.

"Here's the part I've never said out loud," Odile said. "Not to Halloway. Not to the diary. Not to myself, really, except at three in the morning when the truth gets in like water."

She made herself look at Pim. The small face was turned toward her now—when had it turned?—and the eyes were the eyes from the dream, gray as lake ice, and they were not hungry. They were *afraid*. Twenty years of sitting on porches and the thing was afraid of a sentence.

"The night Danny drowned," she said, "I didn't call you. That's what I've told myself all these years—that I called you, that I wished him away, that it was my wish that took him and put you in his place. That's the story I built my whole guilt on. It's wrong. I know it's wrong because I found the diary entry from that week and I was *too numb to write*, there's a gap in the book, four days with nothing but the date. I didn't call you, Pim. I didn't wish anything. The visitor came for Danny because the town's ledger said it was owed a child, and the ledger said so because the founders signed it, and my mother's name was on the page because someone who loves the child must sign, and she signed *before I was born*, before any of us were anything but ink. I didn't feed you my brother. The town did. I was nine, and I was a plate on a table."

Her voice broke and she let it. There was no one in the whiteout to hear her be strong.

"But here's the true sin. Here's the one I'm paying for." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough now that she could see the frost on Pim's lashes, fine as sugar. "After the funeral, I was so frightened—of you, of what you might be, of what the town might have used you for, of the humming under the floorboards that I'd started to hear and pretend I didn't—I *put you down*. The way you put down a coat. I decided, at nine years old, that I was too old for you, which was a lie, and I stopped speaking to you, which was cruelty, and I told myself you'd fade, because that's what imaginary friends do, they fade, that's the *kind* thing they do. And you didn't fade. You waited. You waited on porches in towns I moved through, you waited in doorways of apartments, you waited at the edge of the fog outside a school in Cold Spring, and you waited *twenty years*, Pim, because I never once said goodbye. I never once said *you were real to me*. I abandoned you and called it growing up."

The cold coming off the figure was enormous now. It should have hurt. It did hurt. She stayed in the chair.

"And that's the memory I'm going to give you," she said. "Not the creek. Not the naming. The abandonment. The true one, the whole one, the one where I choose you and then unchoose you and then—tomorrow—choose you again, out loud, in front of the town, in front of the unfinished sentence, in front of God and the founders' ashes. Because the founder's letter says it has to be freely given, and it has to be *true*, and a gift made of only the sweet parts isn't true. It's a bribe. The founders tried bribes for a hundred years. I'm going to give you the whole thing: the choosing, the naming, the year of the creek, and the night I walked away and let you stand on the bank and didn't look back. All of it. It's yours. It was always yours. I just kept it."

* * *

Pim wept frost.

There was no other way to describe it, though she would try, later, in the notebook, in the pages she was writing for whoever came after her: the small shoulders shaking, and from the gray eyes a runoff that was not water but cold itself, freezing as it fell, striking the porch boards and blooming into ferns and feathers and—she leaned close, breath held—*small crooked smiles*, hundreds of them, a frost-work of the smile she had given it at one day old, made and remade and melting and made again.

It did not speak. It did not need to. The whole whiteout was its voice; the pressure against the windows, the cold in the pipes, the hum that had gone quiet beneath the school for the first time since she'd arrived—this was the sound of a thing that had waited twenty years to be told it had been loved, and was being told, and did not know what to do with the warmth except freeze it into shapes so it would last.

"I'm cold," Odile said, and was surprised to find she was smiling. "I'm freezing, and I'm not going inside. You sat on porches for twenty years in all weathers. I can sit in one night."

She reached out—slowly, the way you reach toward a bird, toward a child who has been struck before—and laid her hand on Pim's head. The hair was warm static. The air before lightning. Exactly as it had been under the dinner table in August, twenty years ago, and she made a sound she didn't know she could make, a small broken animal sound, and Pim leaned into her palm with its whole weight, which was almost nothing, which was everything.

"I don't know what happens to you," she whispered. "Tomorrow. When I finish the sentence and give you the memory and the ritual completes. The letter doesn't say. Maybe you dissolve. Maybe you go free, wherever free is for a thing like you. Maybe you just—stop waiting." Her thumb moved, once, across the cold crown of its head. "Whatever happens, I need you to hear this, and I need it to be the last thing I ever say to you that isn't part of a ritual: I loved you. I loved you when I made you, I loved you when I left you, I loved you every year I pretended you weren't standing in the fog. You were the realest thing in my life and I treated you like a phase. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

The frost-flowers on the boards had reached her boots. She looked down and saw that they had arranged themselves—she was almost sure, almost, in the porch light's last yellow coin—into letters. Small ones. Childish ones. The handwriting she had invented for a friend at nine years old, because an imaginary friend needed a hand of its own.

Two words.

*I know.*

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them, the first gray light of morning was seeping into the whiteout's edges, and the snow had begun to thin, and somewhere beneath the town, beneath the school, beneath the sealed room with its interrupted sentence, the hum had begun again—soft, patient, and rising, like a breath drawn to speak.

Pim stood. It looked at her with the gray eyes, and it held out its small hand, palm up, the way it had on the first morning, waiting to walk her to the creek.

"Tomorrow," Odile said.

It nodded once, and stepped backward off the porch into the thinning white, and was gone—not faded, not vanished, but *withdrawn*, like a hand pulled gently from a handshake—and the cold went with it, all but a single frost-flower on the top step, shaped like a small crooked smile.

Odile stood, joints aching, blanket frozen stiff at the edges, and looked east, where the school stood waiting with its sealed room and its unfinished sentence and its ledger with her name already inked.

She went inside to sharpen every pen she owned.
