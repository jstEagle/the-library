## 20. Amnesia of the Valley

I woke to sunlight, which in Harrow Glen is the sort of thing that makes a man check his pulse.

For four months the light through my window had come filtered through fog, through frost, through the grey wool of a sky that seemed to be holding its breath. This morning it came through clean glass — clean, and bare. No names written in ice. No letters crawling across the pane like spiders learning to spell. Just the ordinary, stupid, glorious fact of a winter sun doing its best over a valley that had, by every appearance, been forgiven.

I lay in bed a long while and took inventory, because that is what I do. Counting is my rosary. Doors, chairs, breaths. So I counted the sounds of the village waking: a cart on the frozen lane, a rooster with delusions of punctuality, a woman calling a child in for breakfast in a voice with no grief in it at all.

That last one was the strangest. I lay there and listened to it and felt the shape of the wrongness before I could name it. A voice with no grief in it. In Harrow Glen, every voice had carried grief the way wool carries lanolin — soaked in, invisible, but there in everything the hands touched. This morning the voices were light. Light, and lighter than light.

They had paid for it, of course. That was the transaction. I had watched it happen, or the tail end of it, down in the dark under the school, and what I had watched had cost more than I think any of us who stood in that circle will ever fully itemize. The valley had bought its morning with its own memory. It woke lighter because it had been emptied. It grieved nothing because it no longer knew there was anything to grieve.

I got up. My mirror — the same mirror that had lagged behind me all winter, that had shown me my own face a heartbeat late like a bad actor missing his cue — obeyed me now. I raised my hand; it raised its hand. I frowned; it frowned, promptly, dutifully, a perfect servant again. I should have been relieved. Instead I stood there a moment feeling the way you feel when a dog that growled at you all winter suddenly wags its tail. You don't trust the peace. You wonder what it knows that you don't.

Downstairs, the schoolhouse was quiet in the wrong way. Not empty — the boiler was ticking, Odell's old mop leaned in its corner where he'd left it, and I had not had the heart to move it yet — but quiet the way a room is quiet after an argument that everyone has agreed to pretend never happened.

I put the kettle on. I waited for Wren.

* * *

She came down at half past seven, as she always did, hair braided badly and coat buttoned wrong, and she was carrying her brother's name the way she carried everything — clenched, secret, close to the ribs.

"Everyone's acting strange," she said, by way of good morning.

"Define strange."

"Happy." She said it like a diagnosis. "Mrs. Coleridge smiled at me on the lane. Mrs. Coleridge has never smiled at anything that wasn't a pie. And she asked after my family. She asked—" Wren stopped, and I watched her arrive at it, the thing she'd been carrying since she woke. "She asked how my mother was doing. She didn't say his name. She always used to say his name. She'd say *give my love to your mother, and tell Tammy to stay out of her garden.* She said it every time. She didn't say it."

"No," I said. "She won't. Not ever again."

Wren sat down at the staff room table, which was my table now, insofar as anything in Harrow Glen belonged to anyone, and put her chin in her hands. "It's like he was never born. Not died. *Born.* There's a hole where he should be and nobody's even looking at the hole. Except me. And you."

"And Marguerite," I said.

"Marguerite doesn't count. Marguerite looks like she remembers on purpose, like it's a job she took." Wren turned her cup in its saucer, three turns, four. "Do you think that's what it's like for everyone else? Just — nothing? Not even an ache?"

"I think the ache was the price," I said. "And the valley paid it all at once, without being asked. Which is why we're the only two people in Harrow Glen who know what was bought."

"Three," Wren said. "Don't forget the headmaster's dog. It keeps sitting by the school gate waiting for him." She looked up. "Even the dog remembers, and it doesn't even know what it's remembering. It just knows someone's missing."

I didn't have anything better than silence to offer her, so I offered that, and she accepted it, because Wren Halloway at eleven years old had already learned the lesson most adults never manage — that silence, honestly kept, is worth more than comfort dishonestly given.

* * *

The school opened at nine, and the children came, and this was the hardest part, harder than the circle, harder than the dark.

Twenty-one of them. I stood at my desk with the register in front of me and I made myself look at each face as it came through the door, and I made myself not count the gaps, and I failed, because counting is what I do. There were gaps. Of course there were gaps. The taken children had come back to us the night before — I had seen them emerge from the ground with my own eyes, changed and blinking and nameless, and Marguerite had led them, one by one, to their own front doors, and knocked, and said the only thing that could be said, which was nothing. *You left this at my house,* she'd said, as if returning a borrowed coat. And the parents had gathered them in, bewildered and weeping with a joy that had no history, and the valley had absorbed them like water into sand, and by morning it was as if they had always been there. As if they had never left.

Because in a sense they never had. That was the trick of it. The village could not remember them leaving, so the village remembered nothing at all — no absence, no winter of watching windows, no names in the frost. The ledger of Harrow Glen had been torn out and rewritten in a hand that left no gaps, because gaps are what memory is made of, and memory was the one thing the ritual would not leave behind.

"Sir?" A small boy in the front row — Pell, I thought, Pell Fenwick — was looking at me with the particular patience of a child who has asked a question twice. "Are we doing arithmetic or are you doing *that thing where you stare at the wall*?"

"Arithmetic," I said. "Both of those things, ideally."

A laugh went around the room, easy and unshadowed, and I opened the register, and there it was — the school's own book, the official one, the one the headmaster had kept. The names of the enrolled. And where the vanished children's names should have been there was nothing. Not crossed out. Not inked over. Nothing, as though the page had always held exactly the names it held now, as though paper itself had conspired in the forgetting. Twenty-one names where there had been twenty-two enrolled in September, and the arithmetic of it — the *count* of it — simply declined to be wrong.

Except. I turned the page, and my breath caught, and I was glad Wren was in the classroom and not the staff room, because what I felt then was not a thing I'd have wanted her to see on my face.

There was one name that had not been erased. One line in the register, in the headmaster's small precise hand, that remained: *Aldous Crane, Headmaster.* The man himself was gone — I'd checked his office at dawn, out of something between duty and dread. The chair was there. The coat on its hook was there. The drawer with his private ledger of payments — the accounts of a man who had sold one child a winter for forty years and called it stewardship — that was there too, and I had locked it in the chapel with the rest of the evidence, because the valley would never bring him to any justice it could remember, and I could not bear for there to be *no* record. But the man was gone, taken down under the frost with the thing that had fed him, and the village had woken with a headmaster-shaped hole in it and no idea that holes could exist.

And yet his name stayed in the register. The frost had taken the children's names and left his. I sat with that a moment, turning it over, and I confess the conclusion I reached was not a comforting one. The ritual had been honest. That was its whole nature — we had completed it honestly, or Marguerite had, and honesty was the price and the key both. And an honest ritual, I think, does not erase. It only takes what is freely given. The children had not given their names; the names had been *taken* from them, stolen across decades, and so the ritual, in its terrible bookkeeping, had returned the theft to the thieves — dissolved the stolen names back into the dark they came from. But Crane had given himself. He had come down into that chamber demanding his due, and the thing beneath had taken him *freely given*, whole and willing and corrupt, and what is freely given, an honest ritual keeps.

His name remained because his name was *paid*. The children's names remained absent because they had been *refunded*.

I don't know if that's true. I only know it was the accounting my mind insisted on, and my mind is a counting-house that has never once closed.

* * *

At midday I walked the village. I told myself it was to fetch chalk, which was a lie so thin it was nearly transparent, and I went anyway.

Harrow Glen in sunlight is a handsome lie of a place. The crooked lanes, the slate roofs dripping, the smoke rising straight from forty chimneys — it looked like the frontispiece of a book about a village where nothing bad has ever happened, and the horror of it was that now, for everyone in it, that was simply true. I passed Mrs. Coleridge's garden and she was out in it, cutting the last blackened roses off the bush, and she called to me — called to *me*, the outsider she'd helped stone with glares all winter — and asked did I not think it a fine morning, and wasn't it strange how light she felt, like she'd put down something heavy and couldn't recall what.

"Perhaps you were carrying it a long time," I said.

"Years, by the feel of it," she said, and laughed, and went back to her roses, grieving nothing, missing nothing, a woman amputated by magic and healed so smoothly she'd never know she'd had another arm.

At the well, two men were talking about the solstice. "Odd, that," one said. "I could swear we always do something, that night. A bonfire, or a carol, or something. Can't for the life of me remember what."

"Never did anything," the other said comfortably. "It's the longest night. You go to bed early, is all."

And the first man nodded, satisfied, and the conversation moved on to drainage, and I stood there with my hands in my pockets feeling like the last speaker of a dead language. The longest night. They had gone to bed early. Under them, in the dark under the frost-line, something had finished being fed and had gone, or slept, or *settled* — I did not know which, and I suspect I will not know for a long time, and that not-knowing is itself part of the price we paid. We bought the valley's peace with our certainty. I have decided it was a fair trade, most days. Most days.

The dog was still at the school gate. Crane's dog, a grey-muzzled thing with a herding breed's patience and none of its joy, sitting in the frost-rimed grass facing the doors. It looked at me as I passed, and it did not wag, and it did not growl. It simply looked, with the flat patient certainty of an animal that knows someone is coming back, and I had to keep walking, because I could not tell it what I knew, which was that it was the only creature in Harrow Glen — except three, and a register, and a locked drawer — that remembered Aldous Crane at all.

I stopped at the edge of the green, where Odell's cottage stood. His memory-books had been burned in the lane by men whose names I still knew and whose faces I still could not look at; the valley had turned on him in its fear, and he had died — or very nearly died, the distinction had blurred by the end — with his name written on a scrap of paper in his breast pocket, forty years waiting, freely given. The cottage door stood open. Someone had been in; the valley tidies itself, now, the way a body heals. The books that survived the fire had been stacked neatly inside, and on top of the stack someone — Marguerite, I'd have bet the school's entire chalk budget — had placed a single fresh notebook, and a pen, and a jar of ink.

A message in a language of objects. *Someone should keep writing. It might as well be you.*

I took the notebook home.

* * *

Evening came early, as it does in a valley in January, and I sat in my lodgings above the empty headmaster's office with the lamp lit and the new notebook open, and I wrote the first names into it in my best hand, because some things deserve your best hand.

*Tomas Vell. Wren's brother — Tammy, she called him, Tammy Halloway.* And the others, the ones Odell had remembered and Marguerite had confirmed and the old ledger had listed in its unsigned entries: name after name, decade after decade, going back to the founders themselves. I wrote them all. It took until nearly midnight. When I finished, I read them aloud, quietly, all of them, because a record that is never spoken is only paper, and I had made a promise to myself in the dark under the school that these names would live *somewhere*, in *someone*, even if the somewhere was one tired man in one cold room reading to no one.

Wren was asleep in the next room — she'd stayed over, and neither of us had discussed it, and neither of us needed to; found family formalizes itself in exactly these unspoken arrangements. But her door was ajar, and when I finished reading the last name I heard the small sound she made, and I knew she'd been listening, and I knew that was the whole point. One day it will be her notebook. That is the arrangement, though we've never said it. The register passes down. The valley forgets; we do not.

I closed the book. I looked at the mirror.

It behaved perfectly, damn it. I raised the lamp; it raised the lamp. But as I turned down the wick I caught — or I thought I caught, and I have learned to trust the thought — the reflection hold a half-moment after I stopped moving. Not lagging. Not late, the way it had been all winter, when the world's rules were coming loose. Something else. Something more like *attention*. Like a thing on the other side of the glass that had finished its long hunger and taken up a new trade, and had chosen, of all the trades available to it, the trade of *watching*.

I looked at it for a long moment. It looked back, promptly, obediently, and not quite only.

"Goodnight," I said.

And I swear on Odell Prue's forty-year scrap of paper that before the lamp died, the glass brightened — just slightly, just briefly — the way a face does when it is smiled at, and smiles back.
