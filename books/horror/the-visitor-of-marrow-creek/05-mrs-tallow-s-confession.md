## 5. Mrs. Tallow's Confession

Prudence Tallow had sold houses for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above the others: never be in the room when the buyer falls out of love with the property. Let them discover the damp stain on their own, in their own time, after the ink has dried. Let them name it themselves. A house is like a spouse in that way — the flaws must be chosen, not assigned.

She had broken that rule only once before, in 1998, when the Fenwick boy bought the mill cottage and she had been honest with him about the well at the back. He had thanked her. He had lasted nine days.

So she knew what she was doing, driving up the lane to Hollowell House at half past six in the evening with a basket of seed cake and a bottle of sloe gin she had no intention of drinking, when what she actually carried was a confession thirty years in the keeping.

The house looked at her car the way it always did — the way a dog looks at a stranger's hand, deciding. The windows along the east face had gone amber with the last of the light, and for a moment, passing beneath them, she felt the old familiar sensation of being counted. Not watched. Counted. As though somewhere inside the walls a pencil was moving down a list, and her name had a small tick beside it, and the tick was older than her marriage, older than her mother, older than the war.

She parked at an angle she pretended was casual. You did not park facing the front door of Hollowell House. Everyone in Marrow Creek knew that, the way everyone knew not to say the house's name after dark and not to answer if a voice from the churchyard called you by your maiden name. The knowledge was not written anywhere. It was in the water.

The surveyor's car was still there — that neat grey thing, spotless even after two weeks of lane dust, because of course he kept it spotless. Ada Vane. She had read his file the way she read all their files, and his had been the saddest and the most promising both. A man who measured things because measurements could not lie to you, and who had arranged his whole life so that nothing in it required him to be measured back.

*He'll do,* the elders had said, and she had hated them for saying it, and she had agreed with them, and both of those things were still true.

She knocked. The knocker was iron, shaped like a bell with the clapper removed — a little joke of the house's, or of history's, she had never decided which — and before her knuckles had finished the second strike, the door opened.

He stood there in his shirtsleeves with a tape measure still hooked over his belt like a sidearm, and behind him the hall stretched back into its usual dishonest gloom.

"Mrs. Tallow." His voice was flat, courteous, and cold as a spirit level. "I was going to call you tomorrow."

"Were you." She lifted the basket. "I've brought cake. May I come in, Mr. Vane? I think it's time we had a proper conversation, you and I."

Something moved behind his eyes — not hope, exactly. She had seen hope in buyers' faces for thirty years and this was not it. It was more like the moment a man who has been holding a door shut against a gale feels the wind change direction. He stepped aside.

The hall did what the hall always did. It let her in.

* * *

She had expected him to rage, and he did not, and that was somehow worse. He sat across the kitchen table from her — the modern kitchen, the 1987 one, the one with the false ceiling and the safe wiring — and he listened with his hands folded and his face arranged into the expression of a man taking notes even though he had brought no notebook, and when she had finished he was quiet for so long that she poured herself a sloe gin she hadn't meant to drink just to have something to do with her mouth.

"Say the last part again," he said finally. "The part about the others."

She set down the glass. Her hands were not steady. Thirty-one years of professional composure, and it came apart over seed cake in a kitchen that smelled faintly of iron.

"There were four before you. That I know of. There may have been more — the records before 1960 are..." She stopped. *The records before 1960 are in the house,* is what she had been about to say, and she was not going to say that, not tonight, not with the walls listening the way they listen when they're hungry. "The county archive has gaps."

"Names."

"Mr. Hollis, 1961. A Mr. Prentice, 1972. A young woman — Miss Averil Dunn — in 1988. And Mr. Cathcart, 2003. All of them chartered surveyors. All of them engaged by the Tallow agency to conduct a full structural appraisal of Hollowell House."

"And all four appraisals are where?"

She looked at him. He looked back. He was not a stupid man; she had known that from the first phone call, when he'd asked questions about load-bearing walls that no one asks about a house they intend to sell. He had known something was wrong from the beginning, and he had come anyway, because the fee was triple and because — she understood this now, understood it with a tenderness that made her want to weep — because no one had ever wanted him anywhere that badly before.

"There are no appraisals," she said. "That's the point, Mr. Vane. There were never meant to be any."

* * *

She told it to him the way you tell a thing you've rehearsed in the dark for years, in the flat voice of a woman reciting a shopping list, because if she let the voice bend it would break.

The village had been paying the tithe since 1911. Not money — money was the least of it, though there was money too, a fund, an account her grandfather had administered and her father after him and now the parish council, quietly, line-itemed as *maintenance of historic properties*. The real tithe was the other kind. Teeth from the dead — that was the old way, before the war; you buried your dead with one tooth missing and the house collected. Hair, cut at the solstice. Years. Memory. Her own mother had given the house the memory of her wedding day, voluntarily, gladly, because the alternative was the house reaching out and taking something you hadn't chosen to give, and the house's reach, when it was hungry, was long.

"And then in 1974," Ada said quietly, "the bell-keeper."

"Tom Crane. Yes." She wrapped her hands around the glass. "He was the one who fed it. The keeper's the one who carries the ledger down to the well each quarter and reads the names. And in the spring of '74 he went down with the ledger and came up without it. Took the heart out of the well and walked out of the village before anyone knew. They found his coat on the churchyard wall. Nothing else. Never. And the house has been starving for fifty years, Mr. Vane, and a starving house doesn't wait for the solstice. A starving house reaches."

"The pub landlord's brother," Ada said. "The one who went missing in March."

"Wasn't the brother." She said it and watched him absorb it. "It was the landlord himself. We said brother because — " She made a small, hopeless gesture. "Because that's what villages do. Because the vicar said it would cause less trouble. Because we are *ashamed*, and shame in Marrow Creek is a structural material, same as the flint in the church walls. We built half this village out of not-saying."

Ada was silent. Then: "And the surveyors. Hollis. Prentice. Dunn. Cathcart."

"The house needs to be appraised." Prudence heard the words come out of her mouth and hated how sane they sounded, how reasonable, how much like an estate agent's patter. "That's the only way I can explain it that won't have you calling me mad, and I'd rather you thought me mad than a liar, but I'll say it plainly anyway. The house is a thing that measures. It was built out of a bargain, and the bargain is measurement — what a thing is worth, what a person is worth, what a soul weighs against a harvest. For fifty years it has had nothing to measure. Its ledger is gone. Its bell is gone. It's been — " she searched for the word — "*famished*. And when it's famished it takes. But when it's given a surveyor — a man or woman whose whole life is the honest weighing of things — it doesn't take. It *reads* them. It goes through them like a ledger. And while it's reading, it's quiet. It's fed. Not on the person. On the measuring. Do you understand? You've been here two weeks, Mr. Vane. When did the house last move a door on you? When did it last ring the bell?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She had watched the village for fourteen days the way she watched everything — closely, from behind net curtains — and she knew that since the grey car had come up the lane, the bell had rung only at dawn, politely, like a clock. The doors had stayed where they were put. The cold spot in the east bedroom had warmed.

"Mr. Hollis stayed eleven years," she said. "He moved into the lodge and did his survey over and over, room by room, year by year, and the house let him, and he was — I knew him, I was a girl — he was *happy*. He said it was the only house that ever told him the truth about itself. Prentice stayed four years and then walked into the well on his own, smiling, and we don't talk about that. Averil Dunn stayed three months and left at a run and we've never found where she went, and the house sulked for a season and took the Fenn children's dog and old Mrs. Petch's memory of her son's face. Mr. Cathcart — " Prudence's voice did break then, just slightly, and she let it. "Mr. Cathcart was the last one before you. He lasted two years. And in the nineteen years since, the house has been getting hungrier, and the elders drew up the roster, and then your letter came, and you have the fee of a man who doesn't ask twice about triple rates, and a name that — "

She stopped.

Ada's eyes had come up. "A name that what, Mrs. Tallow."

*Careful now,* she told herself. *Not all of it. Not the ledger page. Not tonight.* "A name that came up in the old records," she said, which was true, and not the truth, and the house in the walls would know the difference and forgive her for it or not, that was between the house and her. "The Crane family. The bell-keepers. There were Vanes in the parish register going back to 1850. It's in the records. I can show you the records."

"You hired me," Ada said slowly, "to be read."

"I hired you to keep the village alive through one more harvest," Prudence said, and her voice came out harder than she intended, harder than thirty-one years of softening hard things had trained it to be. "Yes. Yes, Mr. Vane. The parish council voted. Nine to two. I was the two. I argued — God knows I argued, I stood in that vestry and called them every name a woman can call men who do arithmetic on a human being, and then I went home and I slept the whole night through, because the arithmetic is *right*, and that is the filthiest thing I have ever known about myself. Nine to two. And I was the two, and I drove to the county office and I pulled your file myself, and I read about your father, and your debts, and the flat in Leeds you've lived in eleven years without putting a picture on the wall, and I sat in my car outside the office and I cried, and then I went in and I sent you the letter."

The kitchen was very quiet. The house was listening. She could feel it in the floorboards, that faint attentive hum, like a radio tuned between stations.

"Say the rest," Ada said.

"There's no rest."

"There's always rest. You're an estate agent. You've never once in your life stopped at the bad news." And there — the ghost of something in his face that wasn't quite a smile. "You said every surveyor before me stayed, or didn't. That's not the whole sentence. Say the whole sentence."

Prudence Tallow looked down into her sloe gin, at the dark red of it, at her own warped reflection, and gave him the whole sentence.

"Every surveyor before you stayed, or didn't," she said, "and the ones who stayed, the house kept. And the ones who didn't — the house kept too. Hollis is in the east wing. I've seen him. He's not — he isn't suffering. He's *filed*. Like a document. Like something the house found too useful to let go. And Cathcart is in the well, and Prentice is in the well, and Averil Dunn — " she made herself finish — "Averil Dunn is in the wallpaper in the morning room, Mr. Vane, and on foggy mornings the pattern moves, and I have stood in that room and I have *apologised to her*, out loud, for what we did, and the pattern goes still when I do, and that is the most terrible thing I know, that she can still hear me."

* * *

He was on his feet. She hadn't seen him stand; one moment he was folded at the table and the next he was upright with his hand flat on the worktop, and the house hummed under his palm like something leaning into a scratch.

"Get out of my house," he said — and then stopped, and she watched him hear what he'd said. *My house.* Two weeks, and the possessive had grown in him like ivy.

"It's not — " Prudence began, and then thought better of it, because it was, a little, already, and lying to him now would be a cruelty on top of the betrayal.

"The elders," Ada said. His voice was level again, which frightened her more than shouting. "The roster. You mentioned a roster."

"I said too much."

"You said the word *roster*, Mrs. Tallow, and you've been a professional liar for thirty years and you're very bad at it when you're frightened, which tells me you haven't had to lie to save yourself in a long time, and I'd like to know what you're frightened of." He came around the table. He didn't loom; he wasn't built to loom; he simply stood at a surveyor's precise distance and waited, and it was worse than looming.

She could have lied. Nine to two. She had earned the right to keep one thing back.

But she was so tired. She had been tired since 1974, since the morning her father came back from the churchyard without his coat and wouldn't say what he'd seen on the wall, and she had been tired every harvest since, every autumn when the fund was paid and the names were read and the village held its breath and called it *maintenance*.

"The harvest moon," she said. "Nineteen days. If the heart isn't back in the well by moonrise, the house stops reading and starts taking, and it takes in order, and the order is written down, and it's been written down since before your letter was sent." She met his eyes. "Your name is first on it, Mr. Vane. It was first on it before you ever came. The house asked for you. Do you understand what I'm telling you? It *chose* you. Out of every surveyor in England, it reached into the ledger's old pages and it chose the blood of the man who robbed it. The elders think that's the house wanting revenge." She picked up her basket. Her legs were shaking and she made them work. "I think it's the house wanting *family*."

At the kitchen door she stopped, because there was one more thing, the thing she had carried under the confession like a stone in a shoe, and she set it down between them now because it was the only thing she had to give him that wasn't a lie.

"There's a woman lives past the church, in the cottage with the iron gate. Edith Crane. Tom Crane's daughter." Prudence did not look back at him. "She has her father's letters. She's kept them fifty years and she's kept them from us, and she hates this village, and she's right to. If there's a way out of this that isn't the roster, Mr. Vane — it isn't in my agency files, and it isn't in the vestry, and it isn't in me. It's in her."

She walked out through the hall, past the stairs, past the door to the morning room, and she did not look at the morning room door, and the house let her go, the way it always let her go, because she was not worth keeping — and for thirty-one years that had been her safety, and walking down the lane in the blue dark with the seed cake untouched, Prudence Tallow understood for the first time that her safety had always been the same thing as her worthlessness, and that the man she had fed to the house had been wanted — chosen, asked for, *named* — and that she would have traded every clean sale of her long career to be wanted like that, even once, even by a thing with teeth.

Behind her, in the lit kitchen window, Ada Vane stood without moving.

And in the churchyard at the edge of the village, in the cottage with the iron gate, a lamp that had been dark for fifty years came on.
