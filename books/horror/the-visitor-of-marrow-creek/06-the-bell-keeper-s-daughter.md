## 6. The Bell-Keeper's Daughter

The name came to him the way most useful things came to Ada Vane: through paperwork, and against the explicit wishes of everyone involved.

Mrs. Tallow had given him a list of "local contacts" after their conversation in her office — a list so carefully useless it might have been composed by a committee of people trying to hide something, which, he now understood, it had been. The vicar (deaf, conveniently). The historical society (met quarterly, next meeting in November). A retired schoolmistress who had "known the house as a girl" and whose number rang through to a fax machine in a dentist's surgery. He worked through all of them in two days of increasingly terse phone calls, and got nothing but the particular silence of people who had rehearsed saying nothing.

It was the fourth entry on the list that mattered, though Mrs. Tallow had almost certainly not intended it to be. *E. Crane — see re: boundary dispute, 1987.* Just a line in an old file, a name attached to a parcel of land at the eastern edge of the parish. But Crane. He'd seen the name before, inked into the cellar ledger's final entries, in a hand that grew shakier with each page: *T. Crane, bell-keeper.* And then 1974, and nothing.

The parish register gave him the rest. Thomas Crane, bell-keeper of Marrow Creek, married 1951; one daughter, Edith, born 1953; wife deceased 1969. No record of death for Thomas Crane. No record of anything. A man who simply stopped appearing in the paper trail, which in Ada's experience meant one of three things: emigration, prison, or a hole in the world shaped exactly like a man.

He found her cottage on the Thursday, at the end of a lane that the Ordnance Survey map insisted did not exist. That should have warned him off. It didn't. Lately everything in Marrow Creek should have warned him off, and none of it had, and he was beginning to suspect that was the point of him.

The lane ran between hawthorn hedges gone feral and leggy, and it climbed, gently, toward a low stone cottage with a slate roof furred green with moss. Smoke stood up from the chimney in a perfectly straight column, unbothered by any wind, and Ada looked at that column longer than he meant to. There was wind. He could feel it on his face. The smoke simply declined to acknowledge it.

A garden surrounded the cottage, and it was the garden that told him who lived there before he knocked. It was not a gardener's garden. It was a surveyor's garden — or the ghost of one. Rows of vegetables laid out with geometric severity, each bed exactly the width of the others, each path exactly the width of the paths beside it, stakes driven plumb and true. Someone had once measured this ground and made it obey. Weeds had crept in over the years, softening the lines, but the bones of it were still legible, and reading them felt like reading his own handwriting.

He knocked. Nothing. He knocked again, and heard, from somewhere inside, the deliberate sound of someone deciding whether to answer.

"I know about the ledger," he said to the door. It wasn't strictly true, but it was close enough to true, and doors in this village seemed to respond to proximity rather than accuracy. "The tithe ledger. In the cellar of Hollowell House."

The bolts went back one at a time — three of them, he counted, because counting was what he did instead of breathing — and the door opened on a woman in her seventies with a face like weathered oak and eyes that assessed him the way he assessed buildings: load-bearing elements first.

"You're the surveyor," she said. Not a question.

"Ada Vane."

"I know." She stepped back from the doorway without inviting him in, which he understood to be an invitation of a very specific and conditional kind. "You'd better come in before the lane does. It doesn't like being walked on twice in one day."

* * *

The inside of the cottage was warm and dim and smelled of woodsmoke and beeswax, and it was, he noted with a small cold shock, full of measurements. Not instruments, exactly — though there were calipers on the mantel and a steel rule hanging by the fire like a poker — but measurements as objects. Framed on the walls were survey maps, some of them old enough that the parish boundaries were drawn in iron-gall ink gone brown as dried blood. On a shelf sat a row of glass jars, each holding a length of knotted string, each knot labeled in faded copperplate: *doorway, front stair, well-shaft, east window.* Measured things, kept. Preserved against change.

His own flat held much the same collection, though his jars held pencil stubs and his knots were unlabeled. He stood in her sitting room feeling less like a visitor than like a reflection arriving late to its own mirror.

"You measured things," he said. Stupidly. It came out of him anyway.

"My father measured things," Edith Crane said. She lowered herself into a chair by the fire with the economy of someone whose joints had negotiated a strict treaty with her will. "I just never learned to stop. Sit down, Mr. Vane. You've the look of a man who stands when he's frightened, and I've no intention of talking to a frightened man standing up. It makes them pace."

He sat. The chair received him with a creak of honest carpentry.

"The ledger," she said. "Tell me what you saw."

He told her. He described the cellar as precisely as he could — the brick, the damp, the shelf — and then the book itself: the vellum, the columns, the names going back past living memory. Payments rendered in teeth. In hair. In years. One woman in 1938 who had paid with the memory of her sister's face, and the ledger recorded it in a hand that shook with something that was either grief or satisfaction. He described the way the entries stopped mid-sentence in 1974, the pen-stroke trailing off the page like a rope cut loose.

"And the last page," Edith said, when he finished. "The fresh ink."

He hadn't mentioned the last page. He watched her watch him realize this, and something passed across her face that was not quite surprise — more the weary confirmation of a hypothesis decades old.

"What's written there?" she asked.

"My name," Ada said. "In ink that wasn't dry. Dated—" he stopped. "Dated this year. This month. Before I ever set foot in the county."

Edith Crane closed her eyes. When she opened them again they had gone somewhere else, somewhere fifty years down a road he couldn't see, and when she spoke it was half to him and half to the fire.

"He used to say the house didn't keep accounts. He said that was the whole horror of it, and the whole mercy. It *was* the account. Every wall a column, every room a page. The ledger in the cellar was just its handwriting practice — the thing it wrote so it could remember what it had taken. But the memory itself, the sum total, the heart of all those years of names and teeth and Sundays—" she opened her hands, palms up, empty. "That lived in the bell-well. Under the house. My father called it the ledger heart. Bell-shaped. Because that's what it was. What was left of the bell after 1911, when they melted her down and sang her voice into the mortar. They poured what wouldn't melt into a mold, and what came out of the mold was the house's memory, cast solid, and they put it down the well where it could keep the books."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, the straight column of smoke went on refusing the wind.

"In 1974," Ada said, "he took it out."

"In 1974," Edith said, "he took it out, and he carried it out of Marrow Creek under his coat on a night in October, and he walked until dawn, and he never told me where he put it, and he never came back for me, and I have spent fifty-one years not knowing whether that makes him the bravest man this parish ever produced or the cruelest." Her voice stayed level throughout, which somehow made it worse. Level voices, Ada knew from long professional experience, were load-bearing. You did not lean on them unless you were prepared to hold up your end.

"Why?" he asked. "Why would a bell-keeper steal from the house he served?"

"Because he read the ledger," she said simply. "All the way through. Backwards, from 1974 to 1911. He said you had to read it backwards to see it properly — forwards it's just payments, one name after another, poor souls giving up bits of themselves because the alternative was worse. But backwards—" her hands tightened on the arms of her chair— "backwards you can watch it getting hungrier. The first year, the house took a coin from every household and was satisfied. By the twenties it wanted hair. By the forties, teeth. By the sixties it wanted memories, and then it wanted *years*, Mr. Vane, actual years off the ends of lives, and my father sat in that cellar with the lamp turned low and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said that within twenty more years the house would want whole people. Not pieces. People. And the village would pay, because the village has always paid, because paying is easier than leaving and leaving is easier than asking what the house is actually *for*."

"So he took the heart to starve it."

"He took the heart to starve it," she agreed. "And it starved. For a while. You can't eat a village if you can't remember how many mouths it has. But hunger doesn't die, Mr. Vane. Hunger waits. And a starving thing gets clever, and patient, and it learns to reach." She looked at him then, directly, and her gaze had the terrible steadiness of someone delivering a survey report on a building she already knows is condemned. "It reached for my father first. He died in January of '75, in a lodging house in Leeds, of nothing anyone could name. The doctor wrote *cardiac arrest*, but my father's heart was sound as a church roof; I have the letters from his own physician to prove it. It reached across a hundred miles and stopped him, because he was carrying its memory inside his coat and it wanted to know where he'd put it down. It couldn't find it. So it stopped him instead, and it has spent fifty years reaching further, and wider, and now—" she gestured at him, at all of him, with one blunt finger— "it has reached for you. Before you ever arrived. Your name in wet ink. Tell me, surveyor: when they hired you, did anyone ask how you came to be offered the commission?"

He thought of Mrs. Tallow's eagerness. Of the letter that had appeared in his postbox with the parish crest on it, addressed in a hand he didn't recognize, offering terms too good to refuse. He had assumed luck. He had built a career on assuming luck.

"No," he said.

"No," Edith echoed. "Because you weren't hired. You were *called*. The house has been writing to its own ledger, and the ledger says you're due."

* * *

He stayed another hour, though neither of them pretended it was hospitality. She fed him bread and cheese and strong tea because, she said, frightened men think better fed, and while he ate she talked, and what she talked about — haltingly, reluctantly, as though each word cost a small fee — was her father.

Not the bell-keeper. The man.

"He used to measure me," she said. "Every birthday. Against the doorframe of the kitchen, with a carpenter's pencil, the same pencil every year, and he'd write the date and the height in his little copperplate hand. Eleven marks on that doorframe by the time I was eleven. And then in '74 he stopped, because by then he'd read the ledger backwards and he'd stopped trusting doorframes, stopped trusting *doors*, and I hated him for it. Do you understand? I was eleven years old and my father suddenly wouldn't touch the walls of our own house, wouldn't let me hang pictures, moved my bed away from the north wall, and I thought he'd gone mad, or cruel, or both. I spent forty years angry at a madman. And then you come up my lane talking about a ledger with my family's name in it, and I have to sit here and do the arithmetic all over again, and the arithmetic says he was sane. He was the only sane man in the parish, and I let him walk out of it alone because I was eleven and he wouldn't tell me why."

She said it without weeping. Ada recognized the technique because it was his own: grief managed like a budget, expenditures controlled, nothing wasted on display. But her hands, wrapped around her cooling tea, were white at the knuckles, and he understood that the level voice was bearing weight it had borne alone for half a century.

"He never wrote anything down?" Ada asked. "About where he took it?"

"He wrote plenty. None of it useful. Letters to me, over the years — posted from Leeds, from York, from places he never explained. Full of instructions that sounded like madness. *Count your doors. Never answer a knock after the bell rings. If the walls learn your name, move.* I burned most of them when I was young and furious. Kept a few because burning them felt like burning him." She rose, joints protesting, and went to a bureau in the corner, and stood before it with her hand on the drawer without opening it. "And before you ask — no. There's nothing in them about the heart. He was too careful for that. He knew the house could read. He told me once, the last time I saw him, standing at that gate out there—" she nodded toward the window and the severe geometry of the garden— "he said, *Edie, whatever happens, don't let them make you the keeper. Keepers are just the house's hands. Be the thing that holds the key instead.* And then he walked down the lane and the smoke from our chimney bent after him like it was pointing, and I went inside and sulked, because I was eleven."

She did not open the drawer. Ada did not ask her to. Something in the set of her shoulders said that the drawer was a border crossing, and that she was not ready to show her papers.

But as he stood to leave, she caught his sleeve with a grip far stronger than her frame advertised, and pulled him close, and her voice dropped to the register people use in churches and cellars.

"One thing more, surveyor, and you'll listen. The heart isn't just memory. Memory is what it's *made* of, but what it *does* is keep count. It's the house's arithmetic. While it's down that well, the house knows exactly what it's owed and takes it precise. With the heart gone, the house has been guessing for fifty years — and a hungry thing that guesses always guesses high. Whatever bargain the village thinks it's still keeping, whatever tithes they've paid since '74, the house doesn't know it's been paid. It only knows it's starving. Do you understand what that means?"

"That it can't be appeased," Ada said slowly. "Only ended. Or fed."

"It means," Edith Crane said, releasing his sleeve, "that when it finally reaches far enough to take what it wants, there will be no ledger to mark the payment, and no keeper to close the account, and it will not stop. Not at one landlord. Not at ten villagers. It will take the parish entire and then go looking, because the last thing it remembers clearly is being *full*." She stepped back. "Find the heart, Mr. Vane. My father carried it out of here to save a village that let him carry it alone. The least his blood can do is finish the job."

"His blood," Ada repeated. The words went through him like a nail finding a stud behind plaster. "Why did you say it that way?"

Edith Crane looked at him for a long moment, and in her face he watched a decision being weighed — some knowledge held behind the locked drawer of her expression, some inheritance she was measuring out in installments.

"Because the ledger keeps names," she said at last, "and names are blood, in that book. Go home, surveyor. Count your doors. And come back tomorrow — come back before dark — because there's a question you should have asked me first, and I'd rather you asked it sitting down."

* * *

The lane down from the cottage was longer going back, the way lanes are, and the hedges leaned closer, and Ada walked it doing what he always did when the world misbehaved: counting. Hedge posts. His own footsteps. The distant, impossible tolling that had begun again from the direction of Hollowell House, though the tower held no bell, ringing slow and patient as a pulse.

*Count your doors,* the old woman in the pub had said, pressing the iron nail into his palm. *Count your doors.*

He was still turning Edith's parting words over — *a question you should have asked me first* — when he reached the foot of the lane and looked back once, the way he always looked back at buildings, checking the profile against the plan.

At her kitchen window, framed in the glass, Edith Crane stood watching him go. And beside her, faint as breath on a pane, stood the outline of a second figure — tall, stooped, in the shape of a man who had once measured a daughter against a doorframe every birthday, with a carpenter's pencil, in a careful copperplate hand.

The figure raised one hand. Not waving. Measuring — thumb extended, sighting along it, taking Ada's height from fifty years away.

Ada Vane walked back to the inn without running, which cost him more than running would have, and lay awake under the low beams doing the only arithmetic left to him. A bell-keeper named Crane. A great-uncle he had never known existed, in a family tree he had kept pruned to a single bare branch — himself. Names are blood, in that book.

Tomorrow, before dark, he would go back and ask the question he should have asked first.

He already knew, in the place where surveyors keep the drawings they never submit, what the answer would be.
