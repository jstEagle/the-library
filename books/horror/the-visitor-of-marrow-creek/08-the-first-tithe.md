## 8. The First Tithe

Edith Crane had spent fifty years learning not to hear the bell.

It was a discipline, like any other. You began with the small things — you learned to keep the wireless on after dark, to hum while you washed the dishes, to fill the silence of your little house on Creek Lane with anything at all that had a voice of its own. Then you learned the harder things. You learned not to count the seconds between the wind and the sound that followed it. You learned that when a neighbour said *did you hear something*, the correct answer was *no*, always no, and you learned to say it with your face arranged into the particular blankness that Marrow Creek wore like a uniform. Her father had called it *keeping the quiet*, and he had taught it to her at the kitchen table when she was nine years old, his hands steady on her shoulders, his voice low and kind and terrible: *Edie. Whatever rings, whatever calls, whatever knocks — you keep the quiet. Promise me.* She had promised. She was the only one left who had.

So when the bell rang on the last Tuesday of September — one clean note rolling up out of Hollowell's dark windows and across the fields at half past two in the morning — Edith heard it the way a deaf woman hears a lorry through the floorboards: not with her ears but with her whole body, a shudder in the bed frame, in her teeth, in the water glass on the nightstand trembling itself into rings.

She lay still. She kept the quiet.

And then she heard the pub door go.

The Anchor was two lanes over, close enough that on still nights she could hear Tom Wicker calling last orders, the scrape of his stools, the low argument of the darts board. She had heard that pub breathe all her life. She knew its sounds the way she knew her own heartbeat. So when its door opened at half past two in the morning — when she heard the bell over the bar give one flat, startled clang, as if someone had walked into it in the dark — she sat up before she could stop herself.

Through the window, in the blue-grey nothing of the hour, she saw Tom Wicker cross the square.

He was in his shirtsleeves. No coat, though the night had teeth in it. He walked the way a man walks when he's late for something that matters — not hurrying, exactly, but *aimed*, his head up, his face turned toward the fields north of the village where the corn had come in thin and grey this year, the way it always came in thin and grey now. The harvest moon was four nights off, a swollen yellow coin low over the hills, and it lit him well enough that Edith could see the shine of sweat on his bald head.

She had known Tom Wicker since he was a boy who stole apples from her father's tree. She had pulled his tooth once, in the pub, with a length of thread and a slammed door, and he'd bought her a whisky after and called her a butcher and a saint in the same breath. He was the closest thing Marrow Creek had to a mayor, a priest, a spine. He was sixty-one years old and he was walking into the fields in his shirtsleeves and Edith Crane lay in her bed with her hand over her own mouth and did nothing.

That was the first thing. Remember that. She did nothing.

* * *

The bell rang again at dawn. Or — no. She would spend the rest of her life being precise about this, because precision was all she had left after that morning: the bell did not ring at dawn. The bell rang *once*, at half past two, and the silence after it was what rang, all through the grey morning, in every ear in the village.

By eight o'clock the square was full.

They came out of their houses the way they always did on the bad days, moving slowly, not looking at one another, gathering without gathering, the way iron filings find a magnet. Edith stood at her gate with her cardigan buttoned wrong and watched them. Nobody spoke. Mrs. Tallow stood at the edge of the crowd in her good coat, her face the colour of candle wax, her hands folded so tightly that her knuckles had gone the white of peeled bone. Old Sam Petchey was there, and the Hollis sisters, and young Danny Wicker — Tom's boy, home from the city for the harvest, his jaw working like he was chewing on something too big to swallow.

They were all looking north. At the fields.

The corn stood in its thin grey rows, and the mist lay on it, and somewhere out in that mist was Tom Wicker's coatlessness, his bare head, his sixty-one years. The search party formed itself the way the crowd had — without anyone asking, without anyone deciding — and Edith watched them go out into the rows with their sticks and their dogs and their phones held up uselessly, and she watched the corn close behind them, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in sideways: *we knew. We all knew, the moment the bell rang, and we let him walk out the door.*

Because that was the thing about the tithe. It had never once, in all the long hungry years, taken anyone who fought it. The house called, and someone answered — always someone who had been named in the ledger, always someone whose family had paid longest, always someone who had drunk too much the night before or quarrelled with his wife or lain awake listening to the silence until the silence got inside him and turned him around like a weathervane. The house called, and someone answered, and the rest of the village stood in the square and let them go, and afterward they said *he'd been poorly* or *he always was a wanderer* or *these fields are dangerous at night*, and they held a service, and they lowered the stone, and they kept the quiet.

Edith had watched it done to the Hollis boy in 1988. She had watched it done to old Mrs. Petchey's sister in 1996. She had watched it, and said nothing, and kept the quiet, and told herself that her father's theft — the ledger heart, the bell-shaped core of the house's memory, gone these fifty years into wherever he had hidden it — was not her fault, because she had been a child, because she had not chosen, because the sin was his and the silence was theirs and she was only the daughter of the one and the neighbour of the other.

Standing at her gate in the grey morning, watching the corn swallow the searchers, she understood for the first time that there was no *only*. There was no outside the bargain. There was only the village, and the house, and the quiet they all kept together, like a hymn.

* * *

They found his boots at noon.

Not his body. His boots, standing upright in the corn at the field's dead centre, laces tied, toes pointing north, as neat as if he'd stepped out of them on his way to somewhere. And beside the boots, laid flat on the trampled earth, the little brass bell from over the Anchor's bar — the one that had hung there since before the war, the one that rang when the door opened, the one that had given its one flat startled clang at half past two in the morning.

It was cracked down the middle. Split clean through, like an egg.

Danny Wicker carried the boots back into the village himself, held out in front of him at arm's length, and when he reached the square he stopped in front of the crowd and looked at them — at all of them, at Edith, at Mrs. Tallow, at the elders in their good coats — and he said, in a voice that cracked in the middle the way the bell had cracked:

"He walked past my window. I heard the door. I *heard* him. I thought —" and then he couldn't finish, because there was no thought that could be finished, no sentence that could hold what he had thought and what he had done with the thinking, which was nothing, which had been to roll over and pull the pillow over his head and keep the quiet.

Nobody answered him. That was the shame of it. That was the thing Edith would carry now alongside all the other things: not that the village had failed to save Tom Wicker, but that when his son stood in front of them with his father's boots in his arms and his heart coming apart in his face, not one of them — not the elders, not the landlord's own oldest friends, not Edith Crane who had pulled his tooth and been called a saint — not one of them could look at him.

They looked at the ground. They looked at the hills. They looked anywhere but at the boy, because to look at him was to be seen, and to be seen was to be *known*, and Marrow Creek had spent a hundred years perfecting the art of not being known.

The service was held on the Thursday. The vicar — what remained of the parish could still produce a vicar, a tired young man from two counties over who didn't know what he'd been sent to — spoke the words over an empty grave with the boots in it, and the village sang the hymn with its eyes down, and Edith stood at the back and did not sing. She watched their mouths move around the words and she thought: *you are singing to a hole in the ground. You are singing the same song you sang for the Hollis boy and for Mrs. Petchey's sister and you will sing it again in four days when the moon comes up and the ledger is still hungry, and you will keep singing it until there is no one left to sing.*

And then she went home, and took down the loose brick at the back of the pantry, and looked at the tin box her father had left her.

She had not opened it since 1974.

* * *

The surveyor was still at the Anchor — or what was left of the Anchor, its door chained now, its windows dark, a bunch of browning flowers nobody would admit to leaving wired to the handle. Edith found him at the inn instead, in the little parlour with its dead fire and its taxidermied trout, sitting at the table with his papers spread out in front of him in rows so straight they looked printed. Ada Vane. The man the village had hired, or summoned, or *grown* — she still didn't know which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. He looked up when she came in, and she saw him do the thing she had seen him do in the pub the first night: the small involuntary straightening, the surveyor's instinct, measuring her before she'd said a word.

"Mrs. Crane," he said. "I was sorry to hear about Mr. Wicker."

"Were you." It came out harder than she'd meant. She sat down across from him without being asked, because if she asked she might not sit, and if she didn't sit she might not stay, and she had come here to stay. "You weren't here for him, Mr. Vane. None of us were. That's rather the point."

He didn't answer that. He had the look of a man who had learned that most sentences are better left unpicked. His hands were flat on the table, and she noticed — the way she noticed everything now, her father's noticing come back to her like a debt — that his right thumb was pressed against the table's edge as if checking it for level.

"I knew your father," he said at last. "Or — no. That's not true. I know *of* him. The ledger knows of him."

The word went through her like cold water. *Ledger.* She had not said it to anyone in fifty years. She had not let the word past her teeth since the night in 1974 when her father had come home with his hands burned and his eyes wild and a canvas satchel that clinked, and had knelt in front of her at this same kitchen-table age she'd been, and had said: *Edie. Whatever happens, whatever they ask — the heart is ours now. Ours to keep from it. Promise me you'll keep the quiet.*

"Then you know what it is," she said. "The ledger. The tithe. What the house is."

"I know what it writes." He turned a page toward her, and she made herself look: names, columns, entries in a hand that was not any human hand, payments recorded in teeth and hair and years. "I know what it's begun to write again. And I know that in four nights the moon comes up, and the elders have a list, and I've seen the list."

She had known about the list. Of course she had. She had watched them draw it up in the vestry two nights ago, through the window, the elders bent over the table like surgeons, Mrs. Tallow standing apart with her face in her hands. She had seen the first name. She had gone home and sat in the dark and told herself it wasn't her business, that the list was the village's sin and not hers, that she had kept her father's promise for fifty years and she would keep it to her grave.

But Tom Wicker's boots were in a hole in the ground, and Danny Wicker's face was in her chest where the stone was, and the hymn was still ringing in her ears, and she was sixty-eight years old and tired, so tired, of being the only one in Marrow Creek who knew what the quiet cost.

"My father stole something from that house in 1974," she said. "You've read the ledger. You know the year it stops."

"I know."

"He stole its heart. Not a figure of speech — its heart. A bell, or the memory of one, the core of it, the thing it thinks with and hungers with. He took it out of the bell-well and he carried it away and the house has been starving ever since, and a starving house eats." Her voice was steady. Fifty years of practice. "He did it to save the village. That's the joke of it. He thought if the house couldn't remember what it was owed, it couldn't collect. He was wrong. It just got crueler. And then he died, and left it to me, and I have kept the quiet for fifty years, Mr. Vane, and last Tuesday I lay in my bed and listened to Tom Wicker walk out of his own pub in his shirtsleeves, and I did *nothing*, and I am done."

She put her hand flat on the table, between his straight rows of paper.

"There are letters. My father's letters — he wrote them the last year of his life, to me, or to himself, or to the house, I've never decided which. They say how the heart can go back. They say who can take it back. And they say—" she stopped. Swallowed. The stone in her chest turned over. "They say a name, Mr. Vane. A name I have not said aloud in fifty years. A name I think you may already have seen written somewhere."

The fire in the grate had gone out hours ago, but the room had gone colder than the room, colder than the night, as if something in the walls had leaned in to listen. Ada Vane sat very still. His thumb was still on the table's edge, checking for level, and Edith thought: *he already knows. He has known since he read his own name in fresh ink. He came here to measure a house and the house has been measuring him since before he was born.*

"Tomorrow," she said, standing, because her legs wanted to be standing, wanted to be moving, wanted to be anywhere but in that room with the listening walls. "Come to Creek Lane tomorrow. Bring nothing. Tell no one. And Mr. Vane—" she stopped at the door, and did not turn around, because if she turned around she would see his face and she would lose the last of her nerve.

"Whatever my father's letters tell you to do — and they will tell you, they were always going to tell you, that's what they were *for* — you remember Tom Wicker's boots. Laces tied. Toes pointing north. That's what the house leaves when it's finished with a man. And in four nights, if that heart isn't back in its well, the house is going to finish with *you*."

She walked home through the dark streets of Marrow Creek, past the chained door of the Anchor, past the square where the crowd had stood and looked at the ground, and behind her, high in the black windows of Hollowell House, something that was not a bell gave one soft, satisfied note — as if it had heard every word, and was pleased.

And Edith Crane, who had kept the quiet for fifty years, kept walking, and did not look back, and began — for the first time since she was nine years old — to break her promise.
