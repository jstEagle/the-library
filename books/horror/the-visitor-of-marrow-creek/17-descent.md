## 17. Descent

The quilt-map had shown him a door behind the cellar's coal chute, and the door was there, exactly where Edith Crane's father had sewn it into cloth fifty years ago: low, black, without hinges on its outer face, breathing a cold that smelled of wet stone and old iron. Ada stood before it for a long time with his surveyor's lamp in one hand and the iron nail from the old woman in his pocket, touching it through the fabric like a talisman he no longer quite believed in.

He had prepared the way a meticulous man prepares. He had eaten nothing since noon so his stomach would not betray him. He had written two letters — one to no one, because there was no one; one to Edith, telling her where he'd gone and what he meant to do, in case the house kept him — and left them under the lamp in the inn room that was no longer entirely sure it belonged to the inn. He had checked his instruments out of habit: tape measure, plumb line, notebook, pencil sharpened twice. He almost laughed at himself. He was going down to give away the only thing he'd ever owned outright, and he was bringing a tape measure.

But then, that was the point of him, wasn't it. That was what the house had chosen him for. A man who measured things. A man who believed that if you could get the numbers right, the world would hold still.

He opened the door.

* * *

Steps went down. Not stairs — steps cut into living rock, or into something that pretended at rock the way the house pretended at architecture. Each one was slightly concave, worn as if by centuries of feet, though the ledger said the well had only been open since 1911. Unless the house was older than its own records. Unless the house had been hungry long before the village gave it a voice to hunger with.

Ada counted as he descended. It steadied him. Twenty steps to the first landing, where the air changed and the whispering began.

At first he thought it was water. A sound like a stream moving underground, syllabic, patient. Then he understood it was voices — many voices, layered, none of them distinct, all of them saying his name in the tone people used when they were offering him something.

*Ada.* Not Vane. Just Ada, the way his mother had said it, the way no one had said it in thirty years.

He kept walking. The walls here were soft-looking, like clay pressed with fingerprints, and set into them, at the height of a man's heart, were objects. He raised the lamp.

Teeth. Dozens of them, human molars mostly, pressed into the wall in rows like coins in mortar. Below them, a stratum of hair — braided lengths of it, brown and grey and one astonishing copper, woven into the stone as if the stone had drunk it. Below the hair, wedding rings, still on their bands sometimes, two rings fused together where the fingers had been taken whole.

"Names," he said aloud, because saying it made it data, and data was a wall he knew how to stand behind. "Payments in kind."

The walls rustled. Somewhere below, something turned over in its sleep.

* * *

The second landing held the names.

They were carved, not written — gouged into the rock in hands that had shaken, some of them, and in hands that had been calm, which was worse. He found TALLOW among them, three generations deep, the oldest carved in a schoolroom slant. He found CRANE, and beside it, smaller, newer: EDITH, and beneath her name a payment recorded in the same gouged shorthand — *one brother, aged nine, fever, taken kindly.*

Ada stood very still. Edith had never mentioned a brother. She had spoken of her father the way you speak of weather that ruined a harvest — flatly, finally. But here was the ledger of it, in the house's own hand: the house remembered what the village had agreed to forget.

*I can give him back,* whispered the wall, close to his ear, though the wall was six feet away. *I keep everything I am given. Nothing is lost in me. I could let you hear his voice. I could let you see his face at nine years old, before the fever, before we took him kindly. Wouldn't you like to be the one who brings her that? Wouldn't she open her door to you then?*

"No," Ada said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "You don't give back. You only lend the memory of having taken."

The whisper paused. Then, with something like respect: *You have read ledgers before.*

"I write them."

*We know,* said the walls, all of them, softly, warmly, horribly. *That is why we chose you.*

* * *

He counted again going down. Forty-one steps to the third landing, and here the tithes changed character, and Ada understood he was reading the house's history downward through its payments, the way a geologist reads time in cut earth.

The upper strata were desperate things: teeth, hair, rings, the small hoarded valuables of poor people paying rent on their own continued existence. The middle strata were stranger — a child's shoe, pressed flat and perfect; a glass eye that caught his lamplight and winked it back; a set of dentures grinning up out of the stone; a hymnal, swollen with damp, its spine cracked at the page for *Abide With Me*. The lower strata, the oldest, nearest 1911 —

The lower strata were music.

Not objects. Grooves. The walls were scored with fine parallel lines, thousands of them, running horizontally around the shaft like the grooves of an enormous record, and when his lamp passed over them they hummed, faintly, each band a different pitch. He put his palm against one, gently, the way he would touch a client's wall to check for damp.

The groove sang into his bones.

It was a village singing. He couldn't make out words but he could make out the shape of them — a congregation's sound, unaccompanied, ragged and earnest, dozens of voices giving something away. Giving their voices away. The casting of the bell, 1911: the parish too poor to buy bronze enough, so they had sung into the metal while it poured, every man woman and child contributing breath and pitch, and the bell had come out of the mold already knowing them, already loving them, the way a thing loves what it is made of.

And when the bell was melted down — no. When the bell was *given*, the records said melted, but the grooves said given, the villagers had walked the voice across the churchyard and sung it into the foundations of Hollowell House, and the house had swallowed it, and the house had become the bell, and the bell had needed feeding ever after, because a voice is a hunger that never closes.

Ada took his hand off the wall. His palm was ringing. He could feel the note fading in the meat of his thumb like a struck tuning fork.

"That's why the tower rings," he said quietly. "There's no bell up there. There never has to be. You are the bell. The tower is just where you used to keep your mouth."

The shaft was silent for a moment. Then, from far below, up through the strata of teeth and hair and names and song, came a sound he recognized from the photographs — the rope moving through the empty ring, the soft wooden knock of a beam swinging free. The house was nodding. Or beckoning.

He went down.

* * *

The offers got better as he descended. That was the design of it, he realized — the same design as any ledger, any mortgage, any appraisal. The deeper into debt, the sweeter the terms.

On the fourth landing the walls offered him his mother's kitchen, 1989, the smell of onions in oil, the radio playing the shipping forecast, and his mother alive at the counter with her back to him, humming, and the offer was simply: *stay. This is real. We kept it. Nothing is lost in us.* And God help him, he stopped. He stood on the step with the lamp trembling in his fist and he wanted it with a want that had no dignity in it at all, the naked want of a man who had spent forty years making himself small and portable and easy to leave.

"It isn't real," he said. "She's dead. You're showing me a receipt."

*We are showing you what we hold in trust,* the walls said, gentle as Mrs. Tallow closing a sale. *Everything you refused, we accepted on your behalf. Every door you didn't walk through, we kept open. You think you have lived alone, Ada Vane. You have lived in arrears. All those belongings you declined — they accrued. They waited. They are waiting still, and the interest alone—*

"Stop."

*—would make you rich. Belonging compounds, Ada. Didn't you know? Every year you stayed apart, the part of you that wanted in grew larger. We can feel it from here. It's the largest thing about you.*

His hand found the iron nail in his pocket. He didn't draw it. He just held it, the way you hold a fact.

"You're describing hunger," he said. "I've read your ledger. Everything you take, you call keeping. Everything you starve for, you call love. I appraise property for a living. I know what a distressed asset looks like." He lifted the lamp and made himself look at the walls, at the teeth, at the fused rings, at the grooves full of song. "And I know what happens to a buyer who falls in love with the house."

The whispers did not stop, but they changed key — lower, less sweet, and underneath them now he could hear the other sound again, the true one, the one the sweetness was laid over like icing on a well: a vast slow emptiness, fifty years of it, a belly that had learned to eat itself. Pity rose in him before terror could stop it, and he hated the pity, because pity was a door, and doors were how this thing fed.

He went down faster.

* * *

The last flight was narrow, single-file, cut steep as a chimney's throat, and here the walls pressed close and the tithes became recent. He passed a wristwatch still ticking — the landlord's, he thought, still ticking, still counting toward moonrise for a man who would never wind it. He passed a pair of spectacles folded neatly into the stone. He passed, pressed into the clay at eye level, a surveyor's brass plumb-bob, green with age, and stenciled on the wall above it in careful professional lettering:

HOLLOWELL SURVEY — J. MERCER, OCT. 1961

and beneath it, in a different hand, shakier:

R. ASHWORTH, APRIL 1979

and beneath that, fresher, the paint barely dry:

D. OKAFOR, 2016

Three men. Three lamps, maybe, hung somewhere in these walls. Three sets of measurements that refused to stay the same. Ada stood in front of the plumb-bob for a moment and felt the full weight of what Mrs. Tallow had confessed — *every surveyor before him stayed, or didn't* — and understood at last that the profession had been the trap all along. The house didn't want appraisers. The house wanted readers. Men who would come down into its body and read it, stratum by stratum, and understand it, and be understood by it, and stay.

*I will show you the whole of yourself,* the walls whispered, close now, intimate, the voice inside his own skull wearing his own timbre. *Every room. Every locked door. You have spent your life measuring other people's houses so you would never have to live in your own. Come. Be surveyed. Be known. No one has ever known you, Ada. No one has ever wanted the inventory. We want the inventory. We have wanted it since before you were born.*

And the terrible clarity came, then, standing on the last step with the dark breathing around him: that it was working. That some sealed room in him, the room he had papered over with precision and distance and the clean grammar of reports, was leaning toward that voice like a plant toward a window. That the loneliness he had called a discipline was, from the outside, exactly what the house said it was — an arrears, a debt accruing — and that this thing below him was the first creditor ever to name the sum correctly.

That was the clarity, and it was terrible, and he carried it down with him like a lamp of its own: *the house is right about me. That doesn't mean I owe it.*

* * *

The steps ended.

He came out into a round chamber, high-vaulted, dry as a bone in a desert, and his lamplight climbed walls that were smooth here — finished, deliberate, shaped by hands or by longing into something almost beautiful, ribbed like the inside of a bell. In the center of the floor stood a pedestal of black stone, waist-high, and set into its top was a depression in the shape of a bell: a cradle, a socket, a negative space worn smooth by decades of absence.

The cradle was empty.

Of course it was empty. Edith had the heart, hidden fifty years in the melted stump of the bell, and she was coming, if the roads held and her nerve did, with the thing wrapped in her father's quilt. But the emptiness of it, the sheer sculpted *want* of it, hit Ada harder than any whisper on the stair. The socket was polished bright. Fifty years of starving, and the house had kept the cradle clean. Kept it ready. Kept it, in the only language the house had, *loved*.

Around the chamber's rim, at the height of his eyes, ran a final inscription, carved in the same schoolroom slant as the names above, in a hand he now recognized from Edith's descriptions: her father's. The bell-keeper had come down here, once, before the theft — or after; the letters hadn't said — and he had left a message for whoever followed.

Ada raised the lamp and read it, and the words went through him like the note of a tuning fork goes through bone:

*TO THE ONE OF MY BLOOD WHO COMES DOWN: IT KNOWS YOUR NAME ALREADY. DECIDE WHAT IT MAY KEEP BEFORE YOU LET IT SPEAK TO YOU.*

Below, faint, as if added later, in a woman's hand he also somehow knew:

*He means you, Ada. Decide quickly. — E.C.*

And then, from the throat of the shaft above him, came the sound of the harvest moon clearing the ridge — a pressure more than a light, a tide turning in the stone — and every groove in every wall began at once to sing, and the empty cradle began, softly, to ring.
