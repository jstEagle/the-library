## 7. Nightfall on Old Chapel Road

The sun went down at four minutes past three.

Sable knew this because she was watching it happen, standing at the west window of the drawing room with her phone in her hand, watching the light fail like a tide going out too fast. The forecast had said sunset at 4:47. She had checked twice. But the sun slid down behind the ridge as though something on the other side of the world had pulled a cord, and the shadows of the yew trees came across the lawn like fingers closing.

Her breath fogged the glass. Behind her, in the reflection, the room was already darker than the room should have been.

She turned around. The lamps she'd set up that morning—the two work lights on their tripods, the anglepoise on the mantel—were all burning steadily. That wasn't what frightened her. What frightened her was that between one blink and the next, she could not remember plugging them in.

*You've been here three days,* she told herself. *Of course you remember.*

She didn't, though. She remembered setting them up the way you remember a photograph of yourself doing something: accurately, and from the outside.

The clipboard hung by the front door where it had hung since 1979, and its pages rustled without wind.

At 3:20, she heard the front door lock.

Not slam. Not creak. *Lock*—the deep, deliberate sound of a bolt she had never seen being thrown home somewhere inside the wood, followed by a second bolt, and a third, each one further down the door than the last, as if the house were buttoning itself up for the night. She crossed the hall and put her hand flat against the oak. It was warm. Not sun-warm; there had been no sun for an hour. Warm the way a flank is warm.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud had become, over three days, the only proof she had that her voice still belonged to her. "Okay."

The house did not answer. The house never answered directly. It only rearranged itself around whatever she said, the way water closes over a stone.

She got her bag from the drawing room. Inside it: the laser measure, the notebook, the surveyor's notes photocopied onto loose sheets, a torch, a flask of coffee gone cold hours ago, and the iron poker she'd taken from the fireplace that morning with no particular plan except that iron felt like a thing worth holding. Her hands were steady. She noted this with clinical interest, the way she'd once noted her own pulse in a hospital corridor—*elevated but regular*—as if the woman having the experience were someone she was appraising.

That was the trick of it, she was beginning to understand. That was how the house worked. It didn't frighten you into running. Running was loud, and loud things echoed, and echoes were how it learned your voice.

Instead it made you calm. It made you *interested*. It walked you through yourself the way a good auctioneer walks a buyer through a lot, pointing out provenance, condition, the small flaws that make a piece valuable—and by the time you understood you were the lot, you'd already agreed on a price.

The hallway lamp flickered once. Then the door at the far end of the hall—the one to the morning room, which she had locked herself, which she had *keyed* herself—swung open with the slow courtesy of a footman.

Sable stood in the hall for a long moment, listening to her own breathing.

Then she picked up her bag and went through the door, because the alternative was standing in the hall until the house grew tired of asking, and she had read enough of the 1979 notes to know what happened when it grew tired of asking.

*It wants to be read to,* the surveyor had written, in handwriting that stopped being his halfway down the page. *It wants to be told what it is.*

Fine, she thought. Then let's take inventory.

* * *

The house walked her.

There was no better word for it, and she had tried several. The rooms opened in sequence ahead of her and closed behind her, gently, the way a museum attendant locks each gallery after the visitor passes. Doors she hadn't touched swung wide before she reached them. Stairs appeared where walls had been. Twice she looked back and the corridor she'd come down was simply not there anymore—just wallpaper, unbroken, patterned with a repeating motif of small grey birds that she was almost certain had been fleurs-de-lis yesterday.

And in every room, the house showed her things.

In the morning room, the dust lay on the floor in long pale drifts, and in the dust were shapes. Not footprints exactly. Impressions—the way a body leaves an impression in a bed it has just left. A curve of shoulder. The hollow of a hip. One impression near the window was small, child-sized, curled on its side as if asleep, and Sable stepped around it very carefully, the way you step around a sleeping animal.

In the study, the desk drawers were open, and inside them lay objects arranged with terrible tenderness: a man's spectacles folded closed. A woman's glove, single, fingers relaxed. A pocket watch, stopped at 11:52. None of them dusty. All of them waiting, the way things wait when they are not abandoned but *kept*.

"Who are they?" she asked, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

The lamp above the desk brightened fractionally, the way a face brightens when it's pleased you asked.

"No," Sable said. "Don't. Don't do that."

But the house was already moving her on, up the back stairs, past the nursery where the wallpaper birds had flown into a corner and huddled there, and she understood, with a lurch, that the tour was not random. This was not haunting. Haunting was accidental, the residue of tragedy. This was *curation*. The house was showing her its collection, and she was beginning to suspect why.

Because you don't show a collection to a stranger. You show it to an heir.

* * *

The attic stairs were narrower than the rest, and colder, and the air smelled of soot and old rain and, beneath both, faintly and impossibly, of woodsmoke from a fire seventy years cold.

Her torch beam found the dust first.

The attic ran the whole length of the house, low-beamed, floored in boards gone silver with age. The dust lay thick as snowfall, undisturbed—or so it seemed, until she saw that the undisturbed parts were themselves arranged. Paths swept clean, borders of dust left standing like hedgerows. Someone, or something, had gardened this dust for a century.

And there, in the middle of the floor, under the one window whose glass had never been replaced because the crack in it had never been allowed to spread or heal—

Half a shoeprint.

A woman's shoe, narrow, low-heeled, the style old-fashioned even in photographs from the era. The print was pressed into the dust facing the window, mid-stride, the heel lifted, the ball of the foot dug in as if the wearer had been running. And it ended. Halfway through the stride. As if the woman had been lifted out of her own shoe, out of her own footprint, out of the world.

Around it, the dust had drifted and settled and drifted again for a hundred years, and the print remained perfect. Preserved. *Displayed.* A little rope barrier of cobweb strung between two nails, so fine she might have imagined it.

Sable knelt beside it. Her knees found the boards and stayed there.

She didn't need the photograph in her bag. She didn't need Mrs. Aldery's careful, guilty recitation of names and dates. She knew the shoe. There was a photograph on her mother's sideboard, sepia and cracked, of a young woman on a village street in 1911, caught mid-laugh, one hand raised against the camera—and in the photograph, cropped at the ankle, the same narrow shoe, the same low heel.

Edith Montrose. Who fled Wrenfield House in the spring of 1911. Who got away. Who lived to be ninety-one and never once spoke of this village, and who held baby Sable in her arms in a flat in Leeds and gripped her too tightly whenever the pipes knocked, and stared at the wall with an expression Sable had spent twelve years pretending she hadn't recognized.

"She got away," Sable whispered. "She got *out*."

The attic was silent. The torch beam trembled slightly, and she hated it for trembling.

Then, slowly, the dust around the half-print began to move—not blown, not shaken, but *sifting*, grains rising and settling, rising and settling, until the shape completed itself. The missing half of the stride filled in. The trailing foot pressed its ghost into the dust. Two full prints now, mid-flight toward the window, and above them, fainter than breath, the impression of a hem, a hand, a shoulder turning back—

Turning back. Toward the house. Toward whatever was behind her in the dark of the stairwell.

Toward the thing she had run from, or toward the thing she had run *back for*, and the house held the moment the way it held everything: forever, and lovingly, and as evidence.

"You kept it," Sable said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "You kept her. All these years—you kept the moment she almost—"

The beams overhead groaned, a long settling sound, and it was not settling. It was the sound a throat makes before speech, if houses had throats, and Sable sat down fully in the dust, in a century of preserved grief, and laughed—a short, broken sound with nothing in it of humor.

Because she finally understood the tour. The spectacles. The glove. The sleeping child-shape in the dust. The house hadn't been showing off.

It had been showing her the *terms*. Every generation, a stranger. Every stranger, a life measured, catalogued, kept. And Edith Montrose had run, and the house had lost her, and it had waited a hundred years with a half-finished footprint on the floor of its attic like an unpaid invoice pinned to a wall.

The debt had come due. It hadn't come for Edith's granddaughter out of malice.

It had come to *complete the collection*.

The torch beam steadied. Sable wiped her face with the back of her wrist, leaving a streak of attic dust across her cheekbone like war paint, and reached into her bag.

Her fingers found the photocopied pages. The surveyor's notes. *It wants to be told what it is.*

Below her, through the floorboards, through the whole descending skeleton of Wrenfield House, she heard it: doors opening, one after another, floor by floor, all the way down to the hall—all the doors in the house opening at once, like a held breath released.

Like an invitation.

Like a *summons*.

Sable stood up in the dark, holding the papers against her chest, and began to walk down toward it.
