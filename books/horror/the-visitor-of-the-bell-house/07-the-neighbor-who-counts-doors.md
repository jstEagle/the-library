## 7. The Neighbor Who Counts Doors

Edith Marsh had lived across the lane from Bell House for sixty-one years, and in all that time she had never once seen a Pemberton leave it whole.

She was ninety now, or near enough that she'd stopped correcting people who guessed. Her hands had gone to paper at the knuckles, translucent as vellum, and her eyes needed two kinds of glasses—one for reading, one for looking at things she did not wish to see clearly. She kept both on a ribbon around her neck, and she wore them like a penance, because seeing was the family trade and the family curse both.

The Marshes had come to Hollow Creek in 1847 with the Bells, two families and one covenant signed in a hand that shook only once—on the last line, where Abiah Bell wrote *and the debt shall walk down through the daughters*. Edith's great-great-grandmother Miriam had signed beneath him. The Bells provided the lock. The Marshes provided the key. And for a hundred and seventy years, the Marsh women had done what keys do: they had turned when asked, and said nothing, and grown old watching doors they could not close.

Edith counted doors. It was not a habit; it was an office. Every morning she stood at her window with her tea going cold and counted the front of Bell House—seven doors, always seven, though the house had no reason to have seven and every architectural excuse to have four—and if the count came back wrong, she knew someone inside was walking the stations. She had counted wrong twice in her life. Once in 1971, when Tamsin and Rosalind's mother had gone into the house to settle her own mother's estate, and once this week, when the count came back eleven.

Eleven doors. Edith had set down her tea and put on both pairs of glasses and counted again, and the house had looked back at her through its extra doorways like a face wearing too many eyes.

*Someone is walking,* she thought. *And walking late.*

* * *

She had watched the girl arrive three days ago—the one who wasn't Rosalind. That was the trouble with twins; you learned to tell them apart by their griefs, not their faces. Rosalind Pemberton had walked like a person carrying something breakable and refusing to look down at it. This one—Tamsin—walked like a person who had already been broken and reassembled slightly wrong, all her seams showing if you knew where to look. Edith knew where to look. She'd been looking at Pembertons since before either twin was born.

The funeral had been held at the church, which was correct and also useless. The town had turned out in its gray wool and its gray faces, and they had spoken of Rosalind in the past tense with the particular fluency of people reciting from a script they didn't know they were reading. *Such a shame. So young. Came back to the old place and her heart just gave out.* Edith had sat in the third pew and listened to the lie being polished smooth by forty tongues, and she had felt the old complicity settle over her shoulders like a coat she could never take off, because it was tailored to her family and there was no other tailor in the world.

That was how it worked. That was the part Abiah Bell had understood, and written down, and been ashamed of, and done anyway: the ritual did not kill its walker. It ate her. It consumed the memory of her out of every mind that ever held it, until the town remembered a funeral for a woman whose name slid off the tongue like rain off glass. They would bury an empty plot and weep without knowing why. They would say *such a shame* about a shape where a person used to be. The house took the memory as payment, and the town's forgetting was the receipt, and everyone signed it gladly because the alternative was remembering what the house actually was.

Edith remembered. That was the key's other function. A lock needs one thing outside itself that knows it is a lock, or else it isn't a lock at all—it's just a box. So the Marshes remembered, generation after generation, each woman handed the truth like a hot coal passed down a line of hands, each one burned and none allowed to drop it.

* * *

On the fourth evening, Edith put on her good shawl and crossed the lane.

The porch light of Bell House was burning the color of candlewax, which was wrong—electric lights don't flicker like that, don't lean toward whoever stands beneath them—and she saw the girl come out onto the boards before Edith had even knocked. As if the house had announced her. As if the house wanted her seen.

"You're the neighbor," the girl said. Her voice was scraped thin. Behind her, through the doorway, Edith glimpsed a hallway that should have run north-south running instead into darkness, and she made herself look away. You didn't stare at Bell House's insides. Staring was how it got your address.

"Edith Marsh." She held out a hand gloved in black cotton. "Your grandmother and I played cribbage. Your mother brought me rhubarb every June until the year she stopped coming to the lane at all."

Tamsin's face did something complicated. "Stopped coming?"

"In 1994." Edith said it plainly. Plainness was the only mercy she had left to give. "After her own mother finished the stations. My dear, I haven't got the luxury of circling. May I sit? My knees have opinions."

They sat on the porch steps, the two of them, in the last copper light, and Edith studied the girl while pretending to study the garden. There was a bruise-shaped shadow under Tamsin's collarbone, visible above her neckline—a mark Edith recognized, because her mother had carried one too, after her turn at the well house. The confession-station. It left its signature.

"You've been to the well," Edith said.

Tamsin went still. "How—"

"Because I've been to the well. Because my mother went, and her mother, and a Pemberton woman in every generation back to Miriam Marsh signing her name under a shaking man's." Edith folded her hands. "I'm going to tell you what nobody told Rosalind until it was too late to help her, and what nobody will ever tell you again after tonight, because I am the last one left who can. Do you understand? When I die, the key dies with me, and the house will have the town all to itself."

The light leaned closer. Edith ignored it.

"The ritual doesn't want blood," she said. "It never wanted blood. Blood is just what it tells you it wants, because blood frightens you properly and fear seasons the memory. What it wants is the walker herself—not her body, not her life. Her *being-remembered*. The Pemberton who completes the stations doesn't die. She walks out of the cellar chapel on her own feet, and she waves goodbye, and she goes home, and within a season there is not one soul in Hollow Creek—or Boston, or wherever she fled to—who can recall her face. Her letters go unread because no one remembers writing back. Her photographs show a smudge. Her own children—" Edith's voice caught, and she let it, because hiding the cost was the sin she was trying to confess. "Her own children grow up with a gap in the family Bible and a feeling, on certain evenings, of having lost something they cannot name. That is the payment. Not death. Erasure. Death would be kinder, and the house has never once been kind."

* * *

Tamsin had begun to shake. Edith watched her do it and did not reach out, because some truths you had to hold yourself or they didn't take root.

"Rosalind knew," Tamsin whispered. "Didn't she. At the end."

"She knew before she came back. That's why she came back." Edith looked out at the darkening garden, where the hedges stood too straight, like witnesses sworn in. "She found my door in March. Knocked at nine in the evening, soaked through, carrying a folder of papers she'd stolen from the county archive. She'd figured out the pattern—all those Pemberton women who 'moved away' in the records and appear nowhere after. She sat where you're sitting and she said, *Miss Marsh, I want to break it.*"

"And you told her—"

"I told her the truth, which is that it cannot be broken from inside. The stations are the lock turning. Walk them and be eaten. Refuse them and the house finds another way to feed—it takes the town's memories piecemeal, a year here, a face there, until Hollow Creek forgets itself entirely and becomes a place that isn't anywhere. We tried refusal once, in 1932. My aunt's generation. Six months later half the town couldn't remember the war, and a child went missing off the schoolyard, and the house gave her back smiling, and my aunt walked the stations the following spring." Edith's jaw tightened. "I told Rosalind there was one hope, and it was a mad one: that the rite might be turned against itself. Fed the wrong payment. Given something it hadn't asked for and couldn't refuse without unmaking its own terms. But I didn't know how. I was ninety years of guessing, and she was twenty-eight and desperate, and she thanked me for the tea and went back across the lane."

Edith closed her eyes. When she opened them, the porch light had gone out, politely, as if the house were giving them privacy. That frightened her more than anything yet.

"She tried it," Tamsin said. "At the chapel. Whatever she tried—she tried it, and it failed, and it killed her."

"No." Edith reached out then, finally, and took the girl's cold hand in her papery one. "Child, listen to me. Listen, because this is the last thing I know and I need it to land somewhere besides my own grave. Rosalind did not fail. Rosalind *prepared*. Everything she did in those last months—the research hidden where only a twin would find it, the way she started the rite alone, the way she died mid-station with the seal unfinished—none of it was failure. It was a door left open. She made sure the rite would wait, incomplete, hungry, holding its place—for you. For the only person alive who loved her enough to pay the price differently."

The wind moved through the hedge, and for a moment Edith would have sworn the hedges bowed, all together, like a congregation.

"The house thinks it has a Pemberton mid-rite," Edith said softly. "It thinks it has caught another daughter in the turning of the lock. But it has never, in a hundred and seventy years, faced a Pemberton who came to the last station knowing exactly what it eats—and carrying something it didn't order." She squeezed Tamsin's hand hard, harder than her old bones should have allowed. "Rosalind left you the weapon, girl. I only ever held the map. Now go inside, and count your doors, and when the house shows you the cellar chapel—because it will, soon, it's nearly ready to feed—remember that a lock can be ruined by the right key turned the wrong way."

Tamsin stood. At the doorway she paused, silhouetted against that hallway that ran the wrong direction, and asked the question Edith had been dreading since she crossed the lane:

"What happened to your family's key, Miss Marsh? If you're the last one—what happens to the remembering when you die?"

Edith smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth as a river stone.

"It passes to whoever the house fears most," she said. "I felt it leave me an hour ago. It's standing on my porch."

And behind Tamsin, deep in the rearranged dark of Bell House, something began—very faintly, very patiently—to count back.
