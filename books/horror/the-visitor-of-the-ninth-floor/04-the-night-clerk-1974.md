## 4. The Night Clerk, 1974

The Aldrich Hotel had a ninth floor the way a man has a second shadow: everyone agreed it was there, and no one could point to it twice.

Iris Delacroix learned this her first week on nights. She was twenty-three, new gloves, new name-tag, and she had the front desk from eleven until seven while the day clerk, a heavy man named Osgood who smelled of cough drops and contempt, slept off his second life in a rooming house across the street. The elevator panel in the Aldrich lobby had buttons for one through eight. Below eight, a basement. Above eight, nothing — just the brass plate of the panel, blank and warm to the touch, warmer than brass had any right to be at three in the morning in a building where the radiators clanked like a chain gang.

Yet the ledger had a ninth floor.

She found it on her third night, running her finger down the register the way Osgood had taught her — *every name, every hour, no exceptions, girl, the Aldrich keeps its own books* — and there they were, entries in a hand that was not hers and not Osgood's and not any hand she could name, slanted and patient, ink the brown of dried blood:

*Room 901 — Guest arrived. No departure expected.*

*Room 904 — Guest arrived. No departure expected.*

*Room 912 — Guest arrived. No departure expected.*

She asked Osgood about it over coffee in the morning, and he looked at her for a long moment with an expression she would spend years trying to name, and finally said, "You'll want to stop reading past eight, Iris. For your own handwriting's sake." And he took his coffee and his cough drops into the gray morning, and she stood in the lobby with the radiator clanking and the blank brass plate of the elevator panel warm under her fingertips, and she did not stop reading past eight.

* * *

The guests of the ninth floor checked in between two and four in the morning. Iris never saw them arrive. She saw only the aftermath: the pen laid down on the counter at an angle, the ledger open to the ninth-floor page, the ink still wet. She never saw them leave, because they never left. But she heard them.

The elevator ran at night with no one in it. She'd hear the cables singing in the shaft, that low hymn of friction, and the indicator needle — which only went to eight — would strain against its stop like a dog against a chain, trembling at the top of the dial, and then the cables would sing again, going down, going down, going somewhere below the basement, below the footings, below the frost line, below the city, into whatever the Aldrich had been built on top of in 1921 when the men with the money decided that corner of the street needed a hotel and not a hole.

She asked the day porter, a quiet man named Ferro who'd worked the building thirty years. Ferro polished the lobby brass and did not look up. "There's no ninth floor," he said.

"The ledger says—"

"There's no ninth floor," Ferro said again, gentler, the way you talk to someone standing too close to a ledge, and he polished the brass, and the brass was warm, and Iris went back to the desk and read past eight.

* * *

In February the snow came down for nine days without stopping, and the city went quiet in that particular way snow quiets a city — not silent but *muffled*, as if the whole world had been wrapped in cotton and set aside to wait. The Aldrich was half empty. The bar closed at ten. And at 2:14 in the morning, Iris sat behind the desk with a cup of coffee gone cold and watched the elevator needle strain against its ceiling, and for the first time she heard the guests speak.

Not words. Not at first. A sound like a choir heard through a wall — many voices, patient, unhurried, the sound of people who have all the time that has ever been made. It came up through the floor registers, through the warm brass of the desk, through the fillings in her teeth. And underneath the choir, so faint she could have told herself she imagined it, something else: a scratching. A pen on paper. Writing, and writing, and writing.

She looked down at the ledger.

The ninth-floor page had filled. The slanted patient hand had written to the bottom margin, and now — she watched it happen, she would swear on her mother's grave for the rest of her life, she *watched* — a new line began above the old ones, written from the top of the page downward in letters that grew clearer as she watched, as though the ink were remembering itself:

*Room 913 — Guest arrived. No departure expected.*

And beneath the room number, in the column where a clerk records the guest's name, the hand had written:

*IRIS DELACROIX.*

* * *

Here is what Iris Delacroix did, at 2:19 in the morning on the ninth of February, 1974, alone in the lobby of the Aldrich Hotel while the snow fell and the choir sang through the floor: she laughed.

Because it was a mistake. It had to be a mistake. Some guest — some drunk on eight, some insomniac in 807 — had written in the ledger as a joke, or the day clerk had, or Osgood with his cough drops and his cruelty. Names went in ledgers; that was what ledgers were *for*. But her name in the ninth-floor register, in that patient brown ink, in a hand she now recognized with a lurch as the same hand that had written every ninth-floor entry since before she was born — no. A joke. A cruel one. The kind of joke a building like the Aldrich played on the girls it hired for the night desk because no one else would work the hours.

She took the eraser from the desk drawer. The good one, the rubber one Osgood hoarded. She erased her name.

The ink came up clean. She almost cried with relief.

Then she looked at the page, and the page looked back, and where her name had been the hand had already written it again, in letters deeper than before, as though the page had merely been waiting to see whether she would try.

*IRIS DELACROIX. No departure expected.*

* * *

She did not sleep that day, or the next. She went to the city library and pulled the Aldrich's file — permits, blueprints, a newspaper clipping from 1921 about the groundbreaking. The blueprints showed floors one through eight and a basement. In the margin of the basement sheet, in a draftsman's cramped pencil, someone had written: *sub-level continued per owner's instruction. not for record.* The clipping said the owner, one A. Aldrich, had died before the hotel opened. It did not say where he was buried. It did not say *whether.*

She went back to the hotel at dusk and stood in the lobby while the day shift trickled out and the lights came on one by one, and she understood, in the way you understand a thing all at once, the way ice understands it is a lake — the ninth floor was not above her. It had never been above her. The elevator needle strained at the top of the dial because there was no further up to go, and the thing below had nowhere else to put its guests, so it put them *up*, the way water puts a drowned man. The ninth floor was a direction the building lied about. The guests had checked in through the ledger, and the ledger was the door, and the door had just written her name.

* * *

She tried, in the weeks that followed, everything a frightened and clever woman could try. She burned the page; the ledger was whole the next night. She wrote *CANCELLED* across her entry; the word sank into the paper like a stone into snow. She quit — walked out at 7 a.m. with her gloves and her name-tag left on the counter — and made it four blocks before she stopped, in the falling snow, because she had realized she could not remember her mother's face. She could remember her mother's *cooking*, her mother's voice, her mother's hands — but the face was a white room in her memory, and in the white room, in slanted patient letters, was written: *IRIS DELACROIX, ROOM 913, NO DEPARTURE EXPECTED.*

Your name is what holds you to the world. She understood that now. The thing below collected names the way the Aldrich collected rent, and it had taken hers out of the world's book and written it in its own, and as long as it was written there she was not a woman who worked at a hotel. She was a guest. And guests of the ninth floor did not check out.

So she did the only thing left. She stole herself back.

On the last night of February, while the choir sang through the floor registers and the elevator needle trembled against its ceiling, Iris Delacroix took a razor blade from the maintenance cart and cut the ninth-floor page out of the ledger — her page, her name, the whole leaf — and she folded it into eighths, and she hid it where the thing below could not reach without hands: inside the housing of the hotel's old transmitter, the one the Aldrich used for its in-house radio channel, the one being sold that very month to a struggling AM station across town that needed equipment and asked no questions.

She wrapped the page in wax paper. She sealed it in the transmitter's heart, among the coils and the solder, and she wrote her confession — everything, the ledger, the choir, the needle straining at the ceiling — and she hid that too, in the founding papers of the station that would buy the machine.

Then she went back to the desk and waited to be collected.

The elevator sang. The needle trembled. The choir came up through the floor, patient, unhurried, and beneath it the scratching of a pen that had nothing left to write, because its page was gone — and Iris Delacroix sat behind the desk of the Aldrich Hotel with her hands flat on the counter and her name hidden inside a radio, and she felt the thing below *notice*, for the first time in fifty-three years, that it had been robbed.

The lobby went cold. Frost climbed the inside of the windows in patterns too regular to be natural — spirals, lattices, letters — and the brass plate of the elevator panel, blank all these years, began to warm under no one's hand.

* * *

Fifty years later, in a studio that smelled of burnt coffee and old carpet, Quentin Sandoval pulled his ledger of the dead across the desk and read the names aloud, one by one, the way a clerk reads a register: the woman who thanked him for her song, the man who wanted his brother told, the child who only breathed. Nine names. Nine callers. And beside each one, in his own handwriting now, the same note, because he could no longer pretend otherwise:

*No departure expected.*

The frost on the studio window had grown a new pattern overnight. He hadn't noticed it until the dawn light caught it — a lattice of spirals, too regular to be natural, and at its center, in letters of ice written from the top of the glass downward:

*ROOM 913 — GUEST ARRIVED.*

And beneath it, in a hand that was not his:

*QUENTIN SANDOVAL.*
