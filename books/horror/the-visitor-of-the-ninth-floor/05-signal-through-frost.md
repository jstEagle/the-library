## 5. Signal Through Frost

The change came on a Tuesday, at 3:11 in the morning, and Quentin knew it before the caller said a word.

For eleven nights the calls had come one at a time. A man named Gerald Okafor who wanted his daughter to know the garage key was under the loose brick. A woman who'd been dead since 1988 asking him to tell her sister she'd made it home okay after all. Each voice distinct, each grief small and specific and human, each one landing in Quentin's chest like a stone dropped into a well he hadn't known he was carrying.

But tonight, when the board lit up—line one, then line two, then every line at once, all of them glowing that soft amber that meant someone was holding—he picked up and heard not one voice but many, layered like sheets of ice forming over a lake, speaking in perfect unison:

*"We can see your window, Quentin."*

He sat very still. The ON AIR sign hummed its red hum above the studio door. Outside the glass, the city lay under its third week of snow, streetlights turning the flakes to drifting embers, and somewhere down in that white silence, Marisol Vega's excavation crew was sleeping in trailers beside a hole in the earth where the Aldrich Hotel used to stand.

"Who is this?" he asked, though he knew. He was starting to know the way you know a song from two notes.

*"You've been so faithful,"* they said, all of them together, men and women and something older underneath both, a bass note like a building settling. *"Every message written down. Every name spelled correctly. You keep us, Quentin. No one has kept us in fifty years."*

"I write what you tell me," he said. His own voice sounded thin against theirs, a single instrument against an orchestra. "That's all."

*"It isn't all. It's everything."* A pause, and in the pause he could hear them breathing—not lungs, exactly, but something rhythmic and vast, like air moving through ductwork in a building with no furnace. *"Look outside."*

He looked. The studio window faced the parking lot, and beyond it the low gray wall of the station's back lot, and beyond that nothing but snow. But the snow was wrong tonight. It had stopped falling an hour ago, yet across the empty lot the surface was moving, rising in fine white filaments, arranging itself into lines too straight for wind, curves too deliberate for drift. From this angle he couldn't read it. He understood, with a cold that started in his teeth, that it wasn't meant to be read from here.

It was meant to be read from above. Or from below.

"Tell me what you want," Quentin said.

*"Come find out,"* said the voices, and for just a moment—one flicker, like a frame of film spliced wrong—they were not in unison anymore. One voice came forward out of the chorus, dry and tired and almost amused, a woman's voice he'd been hearing in fragments for days: *"...told him about the ledger, didn't I, poor boy, he never listens..."* And then the unison closed over her like water and they said, gently, warmly, terribly: *"Bring the ledger page, Quentin. You already know where it lives."*

He didn't. That was the worst part. They kept saying it as if he should, as if the knowledge were a room in his own house he'd simply never opened, and some traitorous part of him kept reaching for doorknobs that weren't there.

The call ended. All the lines went dark at once, and the studio was so quiet he could hear the snow settling on the roof, a sound like pages turning.

* * *

Marisol called at dawn.

"You need to see this," she said. No hello. Her voice had the flatness of someone running on no sleep and pure adrenaline. "And you need to see it before the sun gets high, because it's—" A breath. "It's fading. Like breath on glass."

The site was ringed in chain-link and orange mesh, floodlights still burning pale against the morning. Quentin showed the guard the station ID he'd laminated himself years ago for exactly this kind of errand, and the guard, a heavyset man who wouldn't look at the pit, waved him through without checking it.

Marisol met him at the edge of the excavation. She looked like she'd aged a decade in four days. Behind her, the Aldrich's bones jutted from the earth—foundation walls, a stub of tiled corridor, and there, half-exposed at the deepest point, the door. Even from thirty feet up he could see the bolts, eight of them, every one turned from the inside.

"It came back overnight," Marisol said. "The frost. We salted the whole site Tuesday. Melted everything. And then I come in this morning and—" She gestured helplessly at the ground around the pit, the frozen mud, the spoil piles, the flat pan of the old parking structure roof where the crew staged their equipment.

Quentin walked out onto it alone.

Up close the frost was fine work, delicate as lace, and it covered nearly a hundred square feet in continuous script. Not letters, at first glance—more like weather, like the ferning patterns frost makes on a windowpane—but the longer he stood there the more the shapes resolved, the way stars resolve into constellations once someone shows you the lines. He crouched. He traced the air above a curling white flourish with one finger, not touching.

His name.

Not just his name. His name, and beneath it, in characters three feet tall rendered in ice crystals on frozen concrete: **1310 AM — QUENTIN NAKAMURA — EVERY NIGHT — WE ARE LISTENING**.

The frequency was correct. The schedule was correct. The word *listening* had been written twice, underlined by a vein of frost that ran the length of the staging area and disappeared over the edge of the pit, descending toward the door like a root growing toward water.

"Radio," Marisol said behind him. He hadn't heard her follow. "It wrote radio. Why would something under a demolished hotel in 1974 know my—know your—" She stopped. Recalculated. "Your frequency."

Quentin stared at the script and felt the answer assemble itself in his gut with the slow certainty of a verdict. The transmitter. Their transmitter—the station's old tube-driven Harris unit, older than the building, older than anyone currently employed there, hauled out of storage whenever the main board failed. He'd been broadcasting through it every night for six years. He'd pressed his palm against its warm housing a thousand times, the way you'd rest a hand on a horse's neck.

Something had been inside it the whole time. Listening. Learning his voice. Learning his listeners' names.

"We're done here," Marisol said quietly. "I pulled my crew yesterday. City says the site's cursed, union says haunted, my insurance company says act of God. Nobody will go near that door." She looked at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce. "You shouldn't either."

"They called me by name," Quentin said.

"So did my brother, once. From the bottom of Lake Michigan." Her jaw tightened. "Dead things saying your name isn't a gift, Nakamura. It's bait."

He looked at the frost a moment longer—at the careful, patient, loving handwriting of it—and felt the terrible split inside himself widen: the part of him that knew she was right, and the part of him that had spent forty-one years being nobody's anything, and had finally, finally been claimed.

* * *

His manager pulled the show Thursday.

Denny Kowalczyk was not a cruel man; he was a frightened one, which was worse to watch. He came into the studio at midnight with a printout of ratings and a face like a man delivering a eulogy.

"Numbers are insane, Q. Insane. We've got listeners in counties we don't broadcast to. We've got a listener in Marquette who swears she hears you *before* you speak—calls in to finish your sentences. Engineering found frost on the *inside* of the tower guy-wires. Inside. In July temperatures." He set the printout down. "Corporate wants the overnight slot dark until further notice. Automated programming. Music and weather."

"The callers—"

"There are no callers, Q." Denny's voice cracked on it. "There are no callers, because everyone you've talked to for two weeks has been dead, and I know that, and you know that, and the only reason neither of us has said it out loud is because saying it makes it real." He rubbed his face. "I got a call today. From my mother. My mother died in 2019, Q, and she told me she was proud of me, and I sat in my car in the dealership lot and cried for twenty minutes, and then I drove here and pulled your show, because if I don't stop it, it doesn't stop."

"Denny—"

"Go home, Quentin." Softly now. Almost kindly. "Please. Whatever this is, it knows your name. Don't let it learn anything else."

He went home. He sat in his apartment with the lights off, watching the snow through the window, holding the ledger—the actual paper ledger, transcribed in his own careful hand, forty-one names, forty-one messages, each one verified against obituaries and county records because accuracy was the only prayer he knew how to offer. Forty-one people the world had finished with, who had chosen *him*, of all the voices on the dial, to carry their last words.

At 2:47 a.m., the automated programming played a smooth-jazz block, and the dead did not call, because the dead could not reach a show that was not on the air.

Quentin lasted ninety minutes.

* * *

The pirate transmitter lived in the trunk of his car.

He'd built it at thirty, during his brief and humiliating flirtation with community radio activism—a 40-watt kit rig, soldered crooked, range of maybe six miles on a good night. It had been in the trunk so long the case had a permanent dent shaped like his jack. He carried it up three flights back to the studio, past Denny's dark office, past the automated rack cycling its jazz, and wired it into the station's antenna feed with hands that shook only a little.

If they fired him tomorrow, fine. If the FCC fined him into oblivion, fine. He had spent his whole life waiting for permission—from his father, from program directors, from a world that looked through him like glass—and he was done. The dead had asked for him by name. Every night, they listened. No living audience had ever loved him that consistently, and he was not going to abandon the only congregation that had ever shown up.

At 3:00 a.m., the ON AIR light glowed red, powered by a transmitter older than sin and dented like his heart.

"This is Quentin Nakamura," he said, "on a signal the station doesn't know it's giving you, and if you're hearing this, then I guess we both know why. Ledger's open. Lines are open. Talk to me."

The board lit up—all twelve lines, instantly, amber and warm.

And the voices came through in unison, but gentler now, almost tender, a choir that had gotten what it wanted: *"Good boy. Good faithful Quentin. Now listen carefully, because we're only going to say this once, and then you're going to bring it to us."*

The frost crept up the inside of the studio window as they spoke, writing in long patient lines, and this time he could read it from where he sat, because this time it was meant for him alone:

**THE NAME IS IN THE MACHINE. IT HAS ALWAYS BEEN IN THE MACHINE.**

Quentin looked at the old Harris transmitter humming in the corner of the engineering room, its housing warm as a sleeping animal, and understood that he had never once broadcast alone.
