## 6. Marisol's Bargain

The frost came back on Thursday, and this time it spelled a name.

Marisol Vega stood at the edge of the excavation pit with her coffee going cold in her fist and watched the ice climb the shoring walls in feathered whorls, each one perfect, each one identical, arranged in rows so regular they might have been printed. Not frost, she thought. Wallpaper. The pit had become a room, and the room was decorated for someone.

Her crew wouldn't come back until the city cleared them. OSHA had red-tagged the site Tuesday, after the door. After the bolts. She'd spent two days in hearings, saying *the bolts were turned from the inside, I know how that sounds, look at the photographs,* while men in ties looked at her the way you look at a woman who has confused grief with geology. The door itself sat at the bottom of the pit now, half-shadowed under the tarp they'd thrown over it, and even under the tarp Marisol could feel it the way you feel a tooth that's about to go.

*RAFAEL*, said the frost.

She dropped the coffee. It shattered on frozen ground, and the brown splash steamed, and she stood very still because if she moved she was afraid the name would move too, would slither, would prove itself a trick of light and cold and a tired woman's eyes. It didn't slither. It just sat there in the ice, in letters a meter tall, patient as a headstone.

Her brother had been dead for eleven years. Lake Michigan, March, a dare that wasn't even a good dare—Rafael had never been a risk-taker, that was the thing nobody understood, he'd gone onto the ice because his friends were laughing and Rafael would have done anything, anything at all, to be inside a circle of laughter instead of outside it. They'd found him in April when the lake gave him back. He was nineteen. He'd been singing in the church choir since he was seven, and at the funeral the whole choir sang, and Marisol had stood in the back and thought: *that's the last time. That's the last time I will ever hear that voice in a room.*

The frost crackled. Not melting. Growing.

She should call her supervisor. She should call the police, the university, the priest her mother still lit candles to. She should walk away and let the city fill the pit with concrete and pour it slow.

Instead Marisol climbed down.

* * *

The ladder rungs were sheathed in ice that wasn't slippery. That was the detail her mind kept snagging on, later, when she tried to make the morning make sense: the frost gripped her boots like a hand, steadying her, courteous, and the cold coming off the pit floor wasn't the dead cold of January but something warmer underneath it, something breathing. The tarp had frozen to the door. She peeled it back and the door stood there in its niche of old foundation stone, oak gone black with a century of river damp, iron bolts—six of them—thrown hard from the inside, their heads punched outward through the wood like knuckles through a wall.

Below the door, in the frost, in that too-regular script: *OPEN IT AND I WILL SING FOR YOU.*

Marisol laughed. It came out of her like something breaking.

"No," she said. Her breath hung in the air and didn't disperse. "No, you don't get to—Rafael couldn't carry a tune in the shower until he was twelve, he practiced in the garage, my mother made him—" She stopped. Her voice was doing something embarrassing. "You don't know that. Nobody knows that."

The frost moved. It flowed like ink in water, letters dissolving and reforming, and when it settled it read: *HE PRACTICED "PANIS ANGELICUS." HE WAS FLAT ON THE ASCENSION. HIS SISTER TOLD HIM IT WAS PERFECT.*

Marisol sat down on the frozen ground. She didn't decide to. Her legs simply ended their employment.

She had told him it was perfect. She had stood in the garage doorway with a Popsicle in her hand, fourteen years old, and Rafael had been red-faced and furious at himself, and she'd said *it was perfect, Rafa,* because that was what sisters did, and he'd smiled at her with that whole-body smile, and she had never once, in eleven years, been able to decide whether that lie had been kindness or the first small theft in a life that would end in a bigger one.

"You're not him," she said to the frost. It was important to say it out loud. "Whatever you are. You're not him."

*I AM THE DOOR HE IS BEHIND,* the frost wrote. *I KEEP WHAT IS GIVEN. I RETURN WHAT IS ASKED. ASK.*

The cold breathed. Somewhere under the ground, under the old hotel's bones, under the door with its six turned bolts, something shifted its weight, and Marisol felt it in her sternum the way you feel bass at a concert, and then—faint, threaded through the ice, through the ground, through the fill dirt and the gravel and the century of buried city—

Someone was singing.

*Panis angelicus.* The ascension. Flat, just slightly, just the way Rafael had always been flat, the way that made the choir director wince and made Marisol's chest hurt, and the voice came up through the frost like water through cloth and it was *him*, it was *Rafa*, it was her brother's voice at nineteen with all its clumsy unbroken beauty, singing the song he'd practiced in the garage for a girl with a Popsicle, and Marisol Vega, who had supervised the demolition of forty buildings and never cried at any of them, put her face in her frozen gloves and wept.

The song ended. The silence after it was the worst thing she had ever heard.

*ASK,* said the frost.

Her hand was already on the first bolt. She didn't remember reaching for it. The iron was warm. That was wrong, that was so wrong, iron in a frozen pit in December should bite the skin, and the warmth of it felt like a hand guiding hers, felt like *permission*, felt like the door wanted this, had wanted this for fifty years, and Rafael's voice started up again—something else now, something from when they were small, the song their mother sang while she cooked, *De Colores*, and Marisol made a sound she didn't recognize as her own voice—

"—and you're listening to the night desk, this is Quentin, and if you're out there, Marisol, don't touch the door."

The radio. She'd left the site radio on the folding table at the pit's edge, tuned to 820 AM out of habit, out of eleven years of night shifts, and Quentin's voice came down the ladder shaft thin and crackling and *alive*, and it was the most human sound in the world.

"Marisol. I know you're there. The frost told me. It's been spelling your name on my windshield for an hour. Listen to me—what you're hearing is real. That's the trap. It's not a lie, it's *real*, and that's why you can't open it."

"You don't know what it's singing," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. She didn't care.

"It's singing my callers," Quentin said. "I've got a ledger full of them, Marisol. Forty-one names. People who died last week, calling my show, asking me for things. Small things. A song. An apology. And every one of them is a guest of the Aldrich Hotel, ninth floor, 1974, and every one of them is *kept*." A pause, and she heard him breathe, heard the studio's dead air behind him, the hum of a transmitter that had been humming since before she was born. "Iris was the night clerk. She's been trying to tell me. The thing under the door took her name and it takes *everyone's*, and what it offers you in exchange is a voice, a room, a place where you belong forever. Forever, Marisol. That's not a gift. That's the bolt."

"His voice," she whispered. "I haven't heard his voice in eleven years. Do you know what that's—every night I dream about the funeral and the choir and I wake up and I can't—"

"I know." And he did know; she could hear it, the specific loneliness in his voice, a man who had spent years talking into the dark to no one. "I know exactly what it's like to be that hungry. That's why it picked you. That's why it's spelling your name in the ice. It doesn't offer things to people who are fine. Marisol, listen to me. If you open that door, you don't get Rafael back. You get to *visit* him. On the ninth floor. For the rest of your life, and then after it, and then after that. You'll be a guest. You'll never check out. That's what the bolts mean. That's what they've meant since 1974."

The bolt was warm under her hand. *De Colores* swelled up through the ground, and Rafael's voice cracked on the high part the way it always cracked, and Marisol sobbed once, hard, like something tearing.

"Rafa," she said to the frost. "Rafa, mijo, if you can hear me—"

The singing stopped.

The frost went very still. And then, slowly, in letters that seemed almost gentle: *HE CANNOT HEAR YOU. I HEAR FOR HIM. I SING FOR HIM. OPEN THE DOOR AND YOU WILL NEVER HAVE TO MISS HIM AGAIN.*

"That's not him," Quentin said in her ear. "Marisol. That's not him. The real Rafael—the real one, the one who cracked on the high note—he'd never let you pay that price. You told him he was perfect once. Be perfect now. Walk away."

*De Colores* started again. And this time, Marisol heard it—the seam in it, the place where the recording looped, the same crack on the same note at the same interval, perfect, mechanical, a thing *kept* and *played* and *kept* and *played*, and the love in it was real but the love was not *his*, it was the door's love, the door's hunger wearing her brother's throat like a coat.

She took her hand off the bolt.

"No," she said. Her voice didn't shake. She was almost surprised. "You don't sing for him. You sing *as* him, and that's theft, and I know theft when I hear it. He's mine. He's *ours*. And he's dead, and the dead stay dead, and that's not cruelty, that's the only mercy there is."

The frost screamed.

There was no other word for it. The ice on the walls shattered into powder and reformed, letters boiling over each other, *GIVEN, GIVEN, IT WAS GIVEN, ALL OF IT WAS GIVEN*—and the singing curdled into a sound like a choir drowned mid-hymn, and the warmth drained out of the bolt so fast it burned her palm with cold, and Marisol scrambled back across the pit floor as the door shuddered on its hinges, six turned bolts groaning, wanting out, wanting *her*—

On the folding table at the pit's edge, the site radio made a sound like a struck bell.

Then static. Then nothing. Across the city, on 820 AM, the night desk went silent mid-word, and every radio tuned to Quentin Nakamura's voice filled with a hiss that, if you listened closely, if you were lonely enough to listen closely, sounded almost like singing.

* * *

Marisol climbed out of the pit on legs that didn't feel like hers and stood at the edge with her burned hand cradled against her chest. The frost on the shoring walls was already dying, going gray, going ordinary, the name *RAFAEL* slumping into shapeless bloom.

The radio was dead. She'd heard Quentin's voice cut off mid-sentence—*the transmitter's in the old Aldrich foundation, that's what Iris—* and then the bell, and then the dark.

She looked down into the pit. Under the tarp, the door was quiet. But the frost at its threshold had begun, very slowly, very patiently, to spell a new word.

It was spelling: *HE IS COMING DOWN.*

Marisol pulled out her phone and called the one number in the city she knew would still be listening.
