## 2. The Three Heirs

### Margot

The solicitor's office smelled of lemon polish and old paper, and Margot Fenwick-Hale sat with her spine at a perfect right angle because slouching was for people whose lives hadn't been carefully arranged. She had taken the morning off from the accountancy firm — a half-day, marked in her calendar as *Personal — Estate*, which was the only honest way she knew to describe anything.

Her brother and sister were already there, which annoyed her. She'd planned to arrive first. Arriving first was a small victory she collected the way other people collected spoons.

Dev was sprawled in a chair by the window like something the tide had left behind, his jacket doing that thing where it looked expensive and slept-in simultaneously. Cass stood at the bookshelf pretending to read spines, which meant she was nervous, which meant she'd been nervous since the train.

"Margot," Dev said, without turning around. "You smell like a bank."

"Ironically, banks don't have a smell. That's how you know they've won." She hung her coat on the stand, straightened it twice. "You look terrible."

"I'm in mourning."

"You're wearing trainers to a will reading."

"Mourning," he repeated, with dignity.

Cass turned from the bookshelf. "Are we going to do this properly? Because I'd like to get through it and catch the four o'clock back."

"You live two hours away," Margot said. "You always live two hours away. It's practically your address."

Something flickered across Cass's face — hurt, quickly filed away — and Margot felt the familiar click of having said the wrong thing, followed by the equally familiar refusal to unsay it. This was how they communicated: three siblings orbiting a shared childhood like planets around a dead star, all of them still held in its gravity and none of them willing to admit it.

Mr. Aldous, the solicitor, came in then, a small man with kind eyes and the air of someone who had read many wills and been surprised by none of them. He spread papers. He cleared his throat.

"The estate of Edith Vane Fenwick—"

Margot frowned. "That's not Grandmother's name."

"It is her full legal name," Mr. Aldous said mildly. "Fenwick was her married name; Vane was her own. She insisted upon both in the document. She was quite particular."

Edith Vane. Margot turned the name over. In forty-one years of life she had never once heard her grandmother called anything but Mrs. Fenwick, or Gran, or — in the one phone call Margot remembered clearly — nothing at all, just the long silence of an old woman listening to a granddaughter who couldn't think of anything to say.

The will itself was short. The house — Fenwick House, all of it, the grey stone pile on the hill with its eleven chimneys and its garden gone to bramble — passed to the three of them jointly. None could sell without the agreement of all. None could alter the structure. There were, Mr. Aldous noted, certain covenants attached, dating from 1911, which he described as "unusual but not unenforceable."

"What covenants?" Margot asked, because she was an accountant and unusual was a word that cost money.

Mr. Aldous adjusted his glasses. "Maintenance of the lower floor. And — I'll read this as written — 'the tenant below is to be paid her rent in song.'"

The room went very quiet. Outside, a pigeon did something idiotic on the windowsill.

"That's a joke," Dev said. "She put a joke in her will. Good for her. Ninety-four years old and still —"

"She was not a humorous woman," Margot said.

"No," Cass said quietly. "She wasn't."

And then nobody said anything, because each of them was thinking the same thing, though none of them would discover that until later: *I heard it too.*

* * *

### Dev

Dev had heard the humming when he was nine.

He'd never told anyone, because telling anyone would have required admitting he'd been awake past bedtime, lying in the blue dark of the guest room at Fenwick House, listening to the sound come up through the floorboards. Not through the walls. Through the *floor*. As if the house had a throat below it and the throat was humming.

It wasn't a tune he knew. That was the strange part — he'd spent thirty years as a semi-failed musician, learned every melody that mattered to anyone, and that humming had never matched anything. It went up where songs go down. It stopped just before it should end, every time, like a person pausing on a stair because they've forgotten why they climbed it.

He'd told himself it was pipes. Pipes hummed. Everyone knew that.

Except pipes didn't stop when you started listening. He'd tested it, even at nine. He'd lain still and breathed slow and the humming continued, low and patient, and then — this was the part he'd buried so deep he only found it again now, sitting in a solicitor's office smelling of lemon polish — it had stopped. All at once. The way a conversation stops when someone realizes they're being overheard.

"So," Dev said now, because silence was worse, "anyone else want to admit they heard the ghost pipes as a kid, or is that just me?"

Margot gave him the look. The one that said *not now, Devlin.* She'd been giving him that look since 1989.

"Humming," Cass said. Not a question. Her hands had gone still in her lap.

"In the house. At night. Coming up through the floors." He shrugged, aiming for casual and landing somewhere near defensive. "I figured it was the heating. But the heating doesn't know when you're awake."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about his family — he made jokes the way other people threw flares, and usually somebody at least pretended to see them.

"I thought it was Gran," Cass said slowly. "I used to think she sang herself to sleep. Down in her room." A pause. "Her room was on the ground floor, wasn't it."

"The east wing," Margot said automatically. She knew the house's geography the way she knew tax brackets — completely and without affection. "All the bedrooms were above the service corridor. Which runs along the north side. Below which is—" She stopped.

"The cellar," Dev said.

"The undercroft," Margot corrected, and then looked faintly disturbed at herself for knowing the word. "There's a door off the kitchen passage. It was always locked. Gran kept it locked. She kept the key—" Margot's brow creased. "I don't know where she kept the key. I don't think anyone ever saw it."

"A locked door under the bedrooms," Dev said, "and a will that says the tenant below gets paid in song. And we all heard humming." He spread his hands. "Great funeral, everyone. Really feeling the closure."

"We are not saying there's a ghost," Margot said, in the voice she used for closing meetings.

"Of course not," Dev agreed. "We're saying our grandmother paid rent to a hole in the ground. Totally different thing."

* * *

### Cass

Cass had not wanted to come back to Fenwick House, and had not known until this week how much she hadn't wanted to, because avoidance works best when you don't examine it.

She was the youngest and the one who'd stayed longest — summers with Gran while Margot did revision and Dev did whatever Dev did, which was mostly disappear into the orchard with a guitar and a girl. Cass had done the dishes. Cass had dried the good china. Cass had walked the long dim corridor to the linen cupboard, past the door that was always locked, and she had never once, in all those years, sung in the house.

She didn't remember being told not to. That was what unsettled her now, sitting in the solicitor's office with her sisters' voices washing over her. She could not find the memory of the rule — no scolding, no warning, no *not indoors, Cassandra* — and yet the rule lived in her body like a scar with no story. Even now, at thirty-six, she caught herself humming in the shower and stopping, some animal instinct pulling the sound back in.

"Why didn't we ever sing there?" she said.

Margot and Dev both looked at her.

"I mean it. Think about it. Kids sing constantly. We sang everywhere — school, car, bath. But in that house?" She shook her head. "I can't remember a single time. Can either of you?"

Dev opened his mouth, closed it. Margot's perfectly organized face did something rare, which was to admit confusion.

"There was a hymn at the funeral," Cass said. "Yesterday. In the church. And when we got to the second verse I —" She pressed her lips together. "My voice just stopped. Like something closed. I thought I was crying harder than I realized."

"You weren't the only one quiet," Dev said. "I thought the organ drowned us out."

"The organ stopped for the last verse too," Margot said. "I noticed. I notice things like that." She hesitated, and then, because she was Margot and honesty for her came out sideways: "The organist seemed embarrassed about it. Kept pumping the pedal. Nothing came."

Mr. Aldous reappeared with tea nobody had ordered, and the interruption broke the spell the way interruptions do — imperfectly, leaving threads hanging. He set down the tray and, with the delicacy of a man delivering a final blow wrapped in courtesy, produced a smaller envelope.

"One further item. Your grandmother left individual letters. She asked that you read them at the house. She was specific." He set three envelopes on the table, each addressed in a hand so steady it looked printed. "She also asked me to convey one message verbally, which I confess I found odd."

They waited.

"She said: 'Tell them the singing was never for me. Tell them to ask the house who it was for.'" Mr. Aldous folded his hands. "I assumed senility, frankly. But she signed the will twice, lucid as a judge, and she made me repeat it back to her word for word."

Cass picked up her envelope. The paper was cold, though the office was warm, though it had been sitting in a drawer for who knew how long. On the back, beneath her name, in that same printed-steady hand, someone had written a single line:

*Do not finish the song.*

* * *

### Margot

They drove up together in Dev's ridiculous car, because Margot's sensible hatchback couldn't fit three adults and their luggage and because Cass, unexpectedly, had said *let's not separate tonight,* in a voice that brooked no accounting.

The house appeared at the top of the lane the way it always had: all at once, grey against the grey sky, eleven chimneys standing like fingers curled around something it was keeping.

Margot had prepared a list. She was good with lists. *Contact insurers. Drain the system before winter. Obtain valuation.* Practical steps for an impractical inheritance. Three signatures to sell, and she already had hers ready.

But as they crunched up the gravel, and the front door — unlocked, waiting, as if someone had left it open for them — swung inward at Dev's touch, and the hall exhaled its familiar smell of beeswax and cold stone, Margot found her list had gone strangely blank.

From somewhere below the floorboards — faint, patient, stopping just short of its ending — came the humming.

None of them said a word. None of them needed to. They stood in their grandmother's hallway, three heirs to a debt none of them understood, and listened to the tenant below begin, once more, to count.
