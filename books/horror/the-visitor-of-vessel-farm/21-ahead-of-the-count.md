## 21. Ahead of the Count

The truck wouldn't start until Odile stopped looking at the house.

Wren understood this without being told. She sat in the passenger seat with the ledger on her lap, wrapped in a dish towel like something newborn, and watched her grandmother turn in her seat three times — toward the porch, toward the east hall windows, toward the field where Marcus stood with his hands over his face — and each time she turned back, the engine turned over and died.

"Stop it," Wren said. Not cruelly. Just tired. "You can look at it when we're gone."

Odile's hands were shaking on the wheel. Her knuckles had gone the color of the dawn coming up gray behind them. "I'm not doing anything."

"You're saying goodbye," Wren said. "It hears that."

Her grandmother looked at her then — really looked, the way she hadn't since Wren was small enough to be carried in from the garden — and whatever she saw in Wren's face made her turn forward and grip the wheel and say nothing at all.

The engine caught on the fourth try.

They rolled down the long drive with the headlights off because Noor had told them to, in his last instructions, delivered in that flat hospice-nurse voice he'd used for everything: *no lights until you clear the property line, no stopping, no looking into any water you pass, and don't say my name out loud until you're past the county road.* He had written it on the back of a medication schedule, of all things, in ballpoint pen, pressing hard enough to tear the paper in places. As if he'd known they would need it to survive him.

As if he'd been practicing for years — all those dying patients, all those final nights — for the one departure he would make himself.

*Don't say my name out loud.*

Wren held the ledger tighter and did not cry. There would be time for crying later, somewhere the mirrors couldn't reach, and she suspected — though she couldn't have said how she knew — that grief spent near standing water didn't stay where you put it.

* * *

Marcus drove.

Nobody had discussed it. He had simply gotten into the driver's seat of the second truck — the one loaded with what remained of their lives: two suitcases apiece, Odile's mother's jewelry box, the family Bible nobody had opened in forty years — and started the engine before anyone could vote on anything. The Vessels were done voting. Voting was how they'd ended up feeding strangers to a well for ninety years.

So Marcus led and Odile followed, and Wren rode between trucks in spirit, watching both sets of taillights through the rear window of her grandmother's cab, and the farm shrank behind them the way a fever breaks — not gradually but all at once, a threshold crossed somewhere in the dark where the fields gave way to the county road.

Except the water.

The water came with them.

It lined the road like witnesses. Every low place in every ditch, every flooded tire rut, every cattle tank and culvert pool — all of it brimming, black, unnaturally full after nine days of rain, and all of it wrong. Wren saw the first one at the property line: a puddle wide as a kitchen floor, holding the sky perfectly except for one detail. It held no truck. It held no headlights. It held no faces leaning out of windows to look at it.

She tested it at the next one. A stock pond beyond a sagging fence, broad enough to hold the whole eastern sky, and the sky floated there in silver and charcoal — clouds, the last stars, the thinning dark — and beneath the sky, nothing. Where the far bank should have been reflected, there was only depth. Where the truck should have been, only more water. It was like looking at a photograph of a room with the people cut out with scissors.

"It's not showing us," she said.

"I know." Odile's voice was gravel. "Keep your eyes forward."

"No, I mean—" Wren turned in her seat. "It's not refusing. Look at the sky in it. It's not broken, it's just... empty of us. Like we already left. Like it already let us go."

Her grandmother was quiet for a long moment. The wipers ticked. Somewhere behind them, Marcus's truck held its position, faithful as a heartbeat.

"That's what he bought," Odile said finally. "That's what your nurse bought us. Everything still works. We're just not in it anymore."

Wren thought about that the whole way to the highway. *Your nurse.* Not *Noor*. Not even *Mr. Sandoval*. Because to say his name was to spend him, maybe. Or because Odile Vessel had spent ninety years learning never to name the people her family owed, and some habits outlive their reasons.

Wren decided she would say his name every day for the rest of her life, starting now, silently, like a prayer with the volume off.

*Noor Sandoval. Noor Sandoval. Noor.*

The puddles kept passing. The sky kept floating in them, unaccompanied.

* * *

They stopped once, at a gas station outside Harlow, because Marcus's hands had begun to shake so badly he'd drifted onto the shoulder twice, and because the gas gauge on Odile's truck had gone to empty even though she'd filled the tank the evening before — the farm taking its last tithe, or maybe just the world resuming its ordinary arithmetic now that the extraordinary debt was paid.

The station was one of the old kinds, cinderblock and buzzing fluorescents, its bathroom door propped open with a mop bucket. And in the bathroom, above the sink, a mirror.

Wren stood in front of it for a long time.

A girl looked back. Sixteen, hollow-eyed, hair unwashed, wearing a flannel shirt that had belonged to an uncle who'd died before she was born. Behind the girl, the bathroom: chipped tile, a flickering tube light, a paper towel dispenser hanging crooked. All of it present. All of it *now*. The mirror moved when she moved, answered when she answered, kept perfect time.

One second late? One second early? She raised her hand and lowered it and could not tell, and understood suddenly that she might never be able to tell again — that the lag had been a language and she'd learned it fluently and now there was no one left to speak it to.

"You'll check every mirror for the rest of your life," Odile said from the doorway. Not unkindly. Just factually, the way she said everything now, as if feeling had been drained out of her along with the farm. "My mother did. My grandfather did. You get faster at it. Eventually you stop seeing anything but yourself, and then one day you stop checking, and then one day after that you catch yourself checking again, and that's just what a life is."

"Did they ever see him?" Wren asked. "In the mirrors. After."

Odile was quiet.

"Grandma. Did they ever see Elias Marr?"

"I don't know what my grandfather saw," Odile said. "He stopped sleeping in rooms with glass in them. He stopped letting us take photographs. He died in 1968 with his face to the wall, and the last thing he said was *the interest is paid, the principal is not*, and none of us knew what he meant until your nurse dug up the county records." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "But my mother — my mother swore that in her last year, when the dementia had taken almost everything, she'd stand at windows and talk to someone in the glass. Polite conversation. Laughing sometimes. And once I asked her who she was talking to, and she said, *the young man who keeps the well*, and she smiled like he was a neighbor come to borrow sugar."

The fluorescent light buzzed. Down the hall, Marcus fed quarters into a vending machine that ate them.

"Do you think it was him?" Wren asked. "Elias. In the glass."

"I think," Odile said slowly, "that we spent ninety years assuming the thing in the reflections wanted blood. And I think a drowned man who waited ninety years for someone to say his name out loud probably wanted something else entirely." She looked at Wren, and for the first time since the well, her eyes weren't dead. They were terrified in the living way, the human way, the way eyes look when they've realized the worst thing is over and now everything after has to be *lived*. "I think we never once wondered what *he* wanted. That's the real crime. That was always the real crime."

Wren looked back at the mirror. The girl looked back. Just the girl. Just the tile and the flickering light.

But as they walked out, Wren caught it — or thought she caught it — in the corner of the glass, at the very edge of reflection where the door frame blurred: a shape like a tall man standing very straight, the way Noor stood when he was charting vitals at 3 a.m., patient as furniture.

By the time she turned fully, the doorway held only the mop bucket and the dark.

She didn't tell Odile. Some things you keep the way you keep seeds — not because you doubt they'll grow, but because they're yours to plant.

* * *

They drove until the sun came up.

Not because they needed to put distance between themselves and the farm — though they did, though Marcus kept glancing at the rearview like a man expecting the horizon itself to follow — but because none of them could say the word *stop* yet. Stopping meant deciding. Deciding meant admitting the night was over and the rest of their lives had started, and none of the three of them had any practice at lives that weren't organized around a count.

At sunrise they pulled into a diner off Route 9 with a gravel lot and a hand-painted sign advertising pie, and they sat in a booth by the window because Odile refused booths against walls — no reflections behind her, ever again — and a waitress with tired kind eyes took their order like they were any family, which was the most miraculous thing that had happened all night.

Marcus ordered coffee and didn't drink it. He held the cup in both hands and stared into it, and Wren knew he was checking the surface, and she knew what he found: coffee, steam, the ceiling lights swimming. Nothing else. His shoulders came down half an inch, and then another half inch, and by the time the food came he was crying quietly into his eggs, and neither woman acknowledged it, because acknowledging it would have required deciding whether to forgive him, and forgiveness was a crop that needed a longer season than one morning.

"He tried to drug me," Wren said, to her pancakes.

"I know."

"And drive me off the property so the well would take you instead."

"I know," Odile said again.

"Are we going to talk about it?"

"When we're somewhere with a door that locks," her grandmother said. "When we've slept. When I'm not one bad sentence away from driving back to that farm and kneeling at that well until it takes me too." She cut her toast into precise squares she didn't eat. "Justice can wait eight hours. It's waited ninety years."

But Wren had the ledger open on her knees under the table, hidden by the tablecloth's drape, and she was reading it the way she'd read it all night whenever the trucks hit a smooth stretch of road and her hands stopped shaking enough to hold a page.

Every visitor since 1931. Every stranger the farm had taken in and used up and fed to the count. Names in faded ink, crossed out in red — and beside each name, in a different hand each generation, the year the farm prospered. The good harvests. The calves that survived. The loans repaid. Ninety years of prosperity purchased at one life per generation, and every entry balanced to the penny, because whatever lived in the reflections was not a monster. Monsters are chaotic. This had been *bookkeeping*. This had been a debt accruing interest, and the horror wasn't the thing in the well — the horror was the neat handwriting of her own ancestors, entering each payment in the ledger like a line item.

And on the last written page, in ink still sharp: **WREN VESSEL**, uncrossed, waiting.

Below it, in Noor's handwriting — she'd watched him write it at the kitchen table two nights ago, the pen pressing hard enough to tear the page — a single line:

**NOOR SANDOVAL — PAID IN FULL. VOLUNTARY.**

Voluntary. He'd specified voluntary. As if the ledger might dispute the terms. As if he wanted the record to show that no one had pushed him, that the hospice nurse who had spent twenty years sitting beside the dying had simply decided, one final time, to go first.

She closed the book. Under the tablecloth, in the dimness, she pressed her palm flat against its cover the way you'd press a hand against a grave.

"Marr," she said aloud, testing it. "Elias Marr."

Across the booth, Odile flinched. But nothing happened. The coffee steamed. The waitress refilled it. Outside the window, morning traffic passed on Route 9, ordinary and oblivious, and no water anywhere reflected anything but the sun.

"We find the sister's family first," Wren said. "Before anything else. Before we find an apartment, before Marcus finds a job, before any of us pretends to be normal. We find whoever descended from Ada Marr, and we give them this ledger, and we tell them everything." She slid the book onto the table, into the light, where her grandmother had to look at it. "He had a name for ninety years and nobody said it. That ends today."

Odile looked at the ledger for a long moment. Then she reached across the table and put her hand on top of Wren's, on top of the book, and her grip was trembling and strong at the same time.

"Ada Marr married a schoolteacher out of Cedar Falls," she said. "My mother mentioned it once — the family kept track, God knows why, guilt keeps better records than love. There'd be grandchildren. Maybe great-grandchildren." She exhaled. "Cedar Falls is four hours north."

"Then we drive four hours north," Wren said.

Marcus lifted his head from his ruined breakfast. His eyes were red and wrecked and, for the first time in Wren's memory, unafraid. "I'll drive," he said.

* * *

They got back on the road with the sun fully up and the fog burning off the fields, and somewhere around mile marker forty Wren finally slept — a real sleep, dreamless, the first since the counting began — and woke when the truck slowed.

She came awake all at once, hand flying to the ledger, heart hammering the old rhythm: *ten, nine, eight* —

But Odile had only pulled over at a scenic overlook, a gravel pull-off above a river valley, because Marcus's truck had flashed its lights. Because Marcus needed to stop. Because Marcus had seen something.

Wren got out. The air was cold and clean and smelled like nothing at all, which after Vessel Farm — which had always smelled of loam and iron and the mineral breath of deep water — felt like being born.

"What is it?" she called.

Marcus stood at the overlook rail, staring down at the river below. It wound through the valley in slow silver curves, pooling wide in the bends, and the morning sun lay on it in long gold bars.

"Wren," he said. His voice was strange. "Come look at this."

She went. She leaned on the rail beside her uncle and looked down at the water, and the water looked back, and—

The river reflected everything. The sky, the clouds, the gold bars of sun, the trees on the far bank, the rail, and the three of them — Marcus, Odile, and Wren herself, small and pale and upright, three figures at a rail, all of them present, all of them accounted for, all of them *there*.

Ordinary reflection. Perfect, boring, honest reflection. The kind every person on earth saw every day without thinking anything of it.

Wren started laughing. She couldn't help it. It came up out of her like water from the well, rising and rising, and it turned halfway through into sobbing, and she gripped the rail and cried and laughed and cried while her uncle put his arm around her shoulders, awkwardly, the first time he'd touched her in years, and below them the river went on reflecting the three surviving Vessels as if they were anyone, as if they were no one, as if they were free.

* * *

They reached Cedar Falls at dusk.

The town was small and brick-fronted and lit yellow at the edges, and the public library was closing in twenty minutes, and inside it, behind a desk stacked with returned books, a librarian with reading glasses on a chain looked up as the bell over the door rang.

"Help you?"

Wren set the ledger on the counter. Untwrapped the dish towel. Opened it to the first page, 1931, and turned it around to face the woman.

"I'm looking for the descendants of Ada Marr," she said. "Her brother was Elias Marr. He disappeared in June of 1931, and everyone was told he ran away." Her voice held. She'd rehearsed it in the truck, all four hours, and it held. "He didn't run away. And I need to find his family, because somebody owes them the truth, and it should have been my family, ninety years ago."

The librarian looked at the ledger. At the names. At the red crossings-out. Then up at Wren — at her face, at whatever was written there — and took off her glasses.

"Halvorsens," she said quietly. "Ada Marr married into the Halvorsen family. There's a granddaughter still living on Delaney Street. Ruth Halvorsen." She paused. "She's been coming in here every Thursday for thirty years, asking us to pull newspapers from 1931. Missing persons. She says her grandmother died waiting for a brother who never came home, and she wants to know what happened to him." The librarian's eyes moved back to the ledger, to the first crossed-out name, and her voice dropped. "Young lady, what exactly is it you're carrying?"

Wren looked down at the open book. At Elias Marr's name, ink-browned, crossed out in red ninety years ago by a hand that believed the debt was settled.

And beneath it — beneath Noor's line, *PAID IN FULL, VOLUNTARY* — fresh writing had appeared. Ink still wet. Handwriting she recognized, steady and unhurried, the hand of a man who charted vitals at 3 a.m.:

**ELIAS MARR — RELEASED.**

**TELL RUTH.**

Wren closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone has finally fallen asleep.

"Yeah," she said, to the librarian, to the town, to the dusk coming down outside. "I think I'm carrying exactly what she's been waiting for."
