## 22. Epilogue: One Second Early

The hospice in Cedar Hollow had fourteen beds and a policy of open windows, which Wren had requested on her first day and the director had granted with a shrug, as if fresh air were a small enough thing to give away. It was nothing like Vessel Farm. The floors were linoleum instead of pine gone soft with age. The walls were painted the color of weak tea. There were no mirrors in the patient rooms — that had been her one condition during the interview, delivered flatly, and the director had written it down without asking why. *No reflective surfaces in resident rooms.* She'd let them keep the bathroom mirrors, chrome-framed and institutional, because she was trying, in her careful way, to be a person who did not arrange her life around fear.

She was nineteen now. She had a state certification, a rented room above a hardware store, and a habit of arriving at work twenty minutes early so she could stand in the parking lot and watch the sun come up over the feed store across the road. In another state, no one knew what a Vessel was. When people asked about her family she said they'd sold the farm and scattered, which was true, in the way that most comfortable lies are true.

Odile lived four hours north with a cousin nobody had known existed until the money ran out. She called on Sundays. She never asked if Wren was safe. She asked if Wren was *well*, and there was a difference between those words that only the two of them could hear, and every week Wren answered *I'm well*, and every week it was mostly true.

Marcus had gone further. Montana, maybe. He sent a card at Christmas with a mountain on it and no note, just his name signed small in the corner like he was afraid of taking up space even on paper. Wren kept it in the drawer with the ledger.

The ledger traveled with her. It lived wrapped in a pillowcase at the bottom of her suitcase, and she told herself she would send it soon — she had an address now, a great-granddaughter of Ada Marr living outside Boise, a woman named Ruth who answered Wren's first letter in eleven days with handwriting that shook but didn't hesitate: *We always knew he didn't run. Thank you for saying it out loud where someone could hear.* The ledger would go by certified mail before spring. Wren just wanted to copy out the names first, all of them, the strangers in their neat red crossings, so that somewhere besides a cellar and her own memory there would be a list of who had been taken and when. A witness of witnesses. Noor would have understood that. Noor had been the kind of man who wrote things down.

She thought about him every day, but not the way you'd think. Not as a wound. More as a room she could still walk into — the east hall, the lamplight, his voice steady while hers shook. *Then we say it anyway,* he'd told her once, about the truth, back when saying it seemed impossible. He had spent his whole life beside dying people and then chosen to go first, and Wren had decided, in the months since, that this was not tragedy. It was arithmetic. He had simply moved himself from one column to the other, and the books had balanced, and she was alive to resent it and be grateful for it in the same breath, which is what love looks like when it's finished.

* * *

Mrs. Halloran was in bed nine, and she was dying the way some old people do — slowly, conversationally, with frequent complaints about the food. She was eighty-seven, a retired schoolteacher, sharp as a tack with a rusting handle. Wren liked her enormously. Mrs. Halloran liked Wren too, in the blunt way of the very old, and had announced in her second week: "You're running from something, girl. I taught forty years. I know a fugitive posture when I see one." Wren had laughed, and changed the subject, and never forgotten it.

On a Tuesday in late February, at 3:07 in the morning, Mrs. Halloran began to count.

Wren was doing rounds with a penlight held low, and she heard it before she saw it — a voice, thin and level, coming from behind the half-open door. She stopped in the hallway with her hand on the frame and felt the floor tilt under her, the way the world tilts when a sound from childhood comes back exact.

"Ten," said Mrs. Halloran.

Wren made herself look through the gap. The nightlight glowed amber. The old woman lay with her hands folded on the blanket, eyes closed or nearly, mouth moving with terrible calm. Her monitor traced its little green mountains, steady, unbothered.

"Nine."

Every muscle in Wren's body said *run*. Every memory said it louder. She stood in the doorway of a hospice in a state where nobody knew her name, and she was sixteen again, lying awake while the floorboards under her bed counted in voices that weren't voices, and the house was deciding, and everyone in it was pretending not to hear.

But this wasn't the farm. This was linoleum and weak tea and a woman who complained about the mashed potatoes. And Wren had spent three months learning to be a person who walked toward the sound instead of away from it.

She went in.

"Eight."

"Mrs. Halloran." She kept her voice low and even, the way Noor's voice had been. "It's Wren. I'm here."

"Seven." The old woman's eyes opened. They found Wren in the dark without searching for her, and there was something in them that stopped Wren's breath — not fear, not vacancy. Recognition. The look of someone greeting a guest they've been expecting.

"Six."

"Can you hear me? You're counting. Do you know you're counting?"

"Five." A pause. The green line climbed and fell, climbed and fell. Then, softer, almost amused: "I've been hearing it for days, dear. Under the radiator. I thought it was the pipes." Her hand slid free of the blanket and caught Wren's wrist — light as paper, strong as a hook. "You hear it too. Don't you."

Wren's throat closed. She nodded.

"Four." Mrs. Halloran smiled. It was not the smile of a woman being taken anywhere. It was the smile of a woman who has finally gotten an answer to a question she'd been asking all her life. "I'm not frightened," she said. "You should know that. Whatever it is, it isn't cruel. It's *patient*. There's a difference. Cruel things rush you."

"Three."

"What does it want?" Wren whispered. She had asked the question her whole life and no one had ever answered it — not Odile with her abstentions, not the ledger with its red ink, not the well with its rising black water.

Mrs. Halloran turned her head on the pillow and looked past Wren, at the dark window, and her face went soft in a way that undid her entirely — the schoolteacher vanishing, leaving just a girl of some other decade, waiting.

"Two," she said, tenderly, like a lullaby. "He's almost done."

"*Who?*" But Wren already knew. She knew the way you know your own name.

"One."

The monitor sang its long note. Wren didn't move. She stood holding the papery hand and watched the green line flatten and waited for the grief to hit, and it didn't, because Mrs. Halloran wasn't finished. Her lips were still moving. Her eyes were still open, fixed on the window, on something reflected there that Wren couldn't see — or wouldn't let herself see.

And then the old woman smiled — wide, unguarded, radiant, the smile of a homecoming — and said, clearly, gratefully, pronouncing every syllable the way you pronounce a name you have waited your whole life to say correctly:

"Noor Sandoval."

Not *Sando-val* the way the intake forms had mangled it for three months. *San-do-VÁL.* The way he must have introduced himself a thousand times, patiently correcting, kindly. The way Wren herself said it.

"He says—" Mrs. Halloran's voice was going quiet now, receding like a tide, but she was smiling, smiling, "—he says *it's all right. Tell her the count goes both ways.*"

Her hand went slack in Wren's.

The monitor held its flat green horizon. Outside, the wind moved through the open window — she'd left it cracked, per policy, per her own stubborn rule — and the curtain lifted and settled, lifted and settled, like breathing.

Wren sat down in the chair beside the bed and stayed there a long time. She did not cry, exactly. Something larger than crying moved through her and came out the other side as stillness. *The count goes both ways.* Ten, nine, eight, down into the dark — and then, upward, one, two, three, releasing. She had heard it happen. She had stood at the rim of the well and watched the water close and heard the numbers turn around. He had gone down as zero and come out the other side as a beginning.

Some debts become gifts. She hadn't believed it until now. Now a dying woman she'd known for six weeks had died smiling, attended, unafraid — and the last word out of her mouth had been a thank-you.

* * *

At dawn, Wren filled out the paperwork with steady hands. Time of death, 3:11 a.m. Peaceful. She wrote *peaceful* and meant it more than she had ever meant anything on a form.

In the staff bathroom, washing her face before her shift ended, she glanced up out of habit — out of the old, tired habit of checking — and the mirror showed her the room one second ahead: her own hand reaching for the paper towels before her arm moved to reach for it. Just a flicker. Just a courtesy.

She stood very still and watched her reflection watch her, and for the first time in her life the lag didn't frighten her. Because she understood now what it meant. It meant the reflections were keeping time with something patient, something that had learned, at last, how to arrive gently. Somewhere — down a dry well that wasn't dry anymore, in a county far away, in every dark window and still pond and polished surface where the light pooled wrong — a room existed one second early, and in it, a man was waiting without impatience, doing the work he had always done, sitting beside whoever came next, saying the thing he had said to her in the east hall with the lamp burning low:

*Then we say it anyway.*

Wren dried her hands. She went and got the ledger from her suitcase, copied the last name onto her list, and addressed the box to Boise in careful block letters.

Before sealing it, she added one page of her own, at the back, in the space where no entry had ever been written. Not a debt. Not a crossing-out. Just a record, the way Noor would have kept it:

*For the visitor of Vessel Farm, who went first so the rest of us could follow — not into the well, but out of it.*

*The count goes both ways.*

She taped the box shut, carried it out into the cold bright morning, and mailed it.
