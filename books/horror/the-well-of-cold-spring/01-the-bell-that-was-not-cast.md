## 1. The Bell That Was Not Cast

The tape recorder's reels turned with a sound like a slow heartbeat, and Isandro Okonkwo had learned over three years to find that rhythm comforting. He sat in Marta Voss's parlor with his notebook open on his knee, the lamp turned low because she had asked him to—*songs come easier in the dark, love, like moths*—and waited for her to begin.

Marta was eighty-one and sat in a wingback chair that had molded itself to her shape the way riverbanks mold to water. Her hands lay folded on a shawl the color of wet slate. Outside her window, Cold Spring went about its evening: woodsmoke rising straight in the windless air, a dog barking twice and thinking better of it, the mountains going blue and then black behind the church—or where the church had been. Only its foundation remained now, a rectangle of mossy stone at the village's heart, abandoned since 1911 when the parish had dissolved and the congregation had scattered into the surrounding farms like seed from a burst pod.

"You'll only want it once," Marta said. It was not a question. She said it every time, before every song, and every time Isandro gave the same answer.

"Once is all I need. I'm not here to take anything from you, Mrs. Voss. Only to keep it."

"Keeping." She smiled without warmth, an expression he had catalogued among the villagers' small strangenesses—the way they spoke of keeping things, keeping songs, keeping days, as though everything in Cold Spring were held rather than lived. "That's what you folklorists call it."

She began to sing.

It was a lullaby, or it wore the shape of one. The melody moved in a narrow range, almost monotone, the way water sounds when it moves beneath ice—and the words were in a dialect so old that half of them existed nowhere else in the region. Isandro had heard fragments of it before, hummed by other elders who always stopped when they noticed what they were doing. *Hush now, hush, the spring runs cold / hush now, hush, before you're told.* The verses descended as they went, each one lower than the last, until by the fifth Marta was singing in a register that seemed less like a woman's voice than the memory of one.

And there was the wrongness. Isandro felt it before he could name it, the way you feel a stair that isn't there. The song did not repeat. Lullabies repeat; that is their whole architecture, the rocking loop that carries a child down into sleep. But this one never returned to its beginning. Each verse was new, and each verse was shorter, and Isandro understood with a lurch that if the pattern held, the song would end not with a final note but with silence—a silence shaped exactly like the space where a child used to be.

He wrote that down. His pen scratched. Marta sang on, eyes closed, hands tightening on the shawl.

On the sixth verse, the words stopped being words. On the seventh, they stopped being anything he could have transcribed if he'd had a hundred years. And then Marta opened her eyes, looked directly at him across the darkened room, and said, in her ordinary cracked speaking voice:

"There. Now you've heard it whole, and I'll not sing it again, and don't ask me why, because you know why. You've known since your first winter here."

"I don't," Isandro said gently. "That's the truth of it, Marta. I write down what people give me, but the meanings—" He shrugged, smiling his careful outsider's smile. "Meanings stay home."

Something moved through her face then—not anger, not fear. Something older than either. Pity, maybe, or its grim cousin, recognition. "You're your mother's son," she said quietly. "She used that same voice when she didn't want to be argued with."

Isandro's pen stopped. Three years in Cold Spring, and no one had ever spoken of his mother unprompted. He had told them, early on, that she'd grown up here—that he'd come partly for her sake, to walk the streets she'd walked before she left for the city and never came back. They had received this information the way villagers receive weather reports: acknowledged, filed, unremarked upon.

"Marta—"

"Hush now." She raised one hand, and the gesture was so precisely the gesture of the song's first line that gooseflesh climbed his arms. "Listen."

"To what?"

She didn't answer. And in the silence after his question, Isandro heard it.

A bell.

One toll, deep and bronze and impossibly round, rolling up out of the ground itself—from the direction of the old church foundation, or from somewhere beneath it. The sound passed through the floorboards, through the soles of his shoes, through the bones of his feet, and he felt it more than heard it, felt it in his teeth, in the fluid of his spine. Then it faded, and the night closed back over the place where it had been, smooth as water over a dropped stone.

"That's not possible," he said. His voice sounded young and stupid in the dark room.

"No," Marta agreed. "It isn't."

"The bell was melted down in 1911. There are photographs. There's a parish record—I've read it forty times. The bronze was sold to a foundry in—" He stopped himself. He was doing it again. He had been doing it for three years: reaching for documents the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood, building his little raft of citations and dates while the water rose around him. The well water that ran cold in August. The songs no one would sing twice. The way conversations in the village shop went quiet when he entered, not hostile, just—careful, the way one is careful near a sleeping animal.

"It rang at midnight," he said instead. He looked at his watch. "Marta. It's not even ten."

"It knows what time it wants," Marta said. She had gone gray. Not pale—gray, the color of ash and old dishwater, the color of something that has been burned and not entirely consumed. Her hands had begun to tremble against the shawl. "It hasn't rung since the year your mother left. Fifty years, and now it rings the same autumn you come asking about harvest songs." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "You fool boy. You sweet, blind fool. Did you think three years was study? Did you think we let you stay because we liked your questions?"

Isandro stood. His knees felt borrowed. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying go home. Lock your door. Don't answer it tonight, whatever knocks, however it sounds like someone you love." She was gathering herself upright, one hand pressed to the chair's arm, suddenly ancient in a way she hadn't been five minutes ago. "And tomorrow, you go to the parish office and you read the ledger from 1974. The harvest ledger. Read the names. All of them. Both of them."

"Both of—"

"*Go.*"

He went. The night outside was preternaturally still, the kind of stillness that is not absence of wind but presence of attention. Every window on the lane was dark, but he could feel the village behind the glass, awake, listening, the way he had always vaguely felt it and always explained away—small communities are watchful, insular, suspicious of outsiders. Rationalization. He saw the word now for what it was: not analysis but shelter. A roof he had built over his own head and called scholarship.

At the corner by the dried-up fountain, old Henrik Lund stood in the dark without a lantern, and Isandro, desperate for any voice but his own thoughts, said: "Did you hear it? The bell?"

Henrik turned his head slowly. In the starlight his face was mild and empty as an unbaked loaf. "There's no bell," he said. "There hasn't been a bell since before my father's time. You should get some sleep, Mr. Okonkwo. Harvest moon's coming, and you'll want your rest."

He walked on. Behind him, faint and patient, from under the earth, the bell tolled a second time.

Isandro stood in the middle of the empty lane with his tape recorder heavy in his bag, holding a recording of a song that ended in a shaped silence, and understood—with the cold, total clarity of a man watching ice form across a well—that whatever he had been documenting for three years had not been folklore.

It had been instructions. And he had just finished reading them aloud.
