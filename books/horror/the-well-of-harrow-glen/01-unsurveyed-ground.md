## 1. Unsurveyed Ground

The satnav gave up three miles out, and Elodie Eastwick took it as a first warning.

She had been warned before, in other places, by other failures of technology—the dead zones and dropped signals that always seemed to cluster around the sites she was hired to survey. It was the nature of the work. Nobody contracted a LiDAR specialist because a location was behaving normally. But Harrow Glen's refusal was different in texture. The road simply ended on her screen, dissolving into unrendered grey, as though the cartographers at Google had looked at the glen once, long ago, and decided against finishing the job.

She drove the last miles by hand, following a lane that narrowed between hedgerows grown tall and leggy, their shadows striping the tarmac like bars. October light came down through them in slats. Her hire car's suspension complained over ruts; in the passenger footwell, her equipment cases shifted and knocked against each other with a sound like knuckles on a door.

"Nothing personal," she told them. "Nobody's home."

Talking to her kit was an old habit, one of the small superstitions of a profession that ran on precision and patience. She was thirty-four years old, she had mapped catacombs in Malta and a drowned village under a Welsh reservoir, and she did not believe in anything that couldn't be rendered as a point cloud. That was the whole of her creed, and she recited it to herself now as the lane bent down into the glen and the village appeared below her—grey slate roofs, a scatter of chimneys, and above them all the square stone tower of a church, plain as a thumb pressed into dough.

Harrow Glen sat in its hollow like something that had been set down carefully and then left alone for a very long time.

She parked outside what she took to be the only shop—a low building with a green door and a hand-painted sign reading QUILL'S, the letters faded to the colour of old celery—and carried her contract folder inside.

* * *

Martha Quill was behind the counter when the bell above the door rang, and she looked up from a ledger of her own—an actual paper ledger, columns ruled in blue, filled with a small upright hand—and studied the newcomer for a moment before speaking.

"You'll be the surveyor."

"Elodie Eastwick." She set the folder on the counter. "I've got a commission through the County Heritage Board. Anomalies near the churchyard. Ground movement, subsidence risk, that sort of thing." She said it lightly, the way she always did, because the truth—that someone had paid a considerable sum for her to scan ground that appeared on no map whatsoever—tended to invite questions she'd been paid not to answer.

Martha Quill was perhaps sixty, with iron-grey hair pinned back and forearms like a woman who had carried crates all her life. Her eyes went to the folder, then to Elodie's face, and stayed there a beat too long.

"Eastwick," she said.

"That's right."

Something moved behind the shopkeeper's expression—not hostility, nothing so useful as hostility. It was closer to recognition, and recognition of a thing one had hoped never to see again.

"There's bread just come in," Martha said, "if you're needing provisions. And the cottage on Church Lane is open. Key's under the loose brick by the step. Mrs. Aldous kept it till spring; it wants airing but it's sound."

Elodie blinked. "I haven't booked anything."

"No need. It's arranged." Martha opened her ledger again, found a line, and wrote something in it. Elodie caught only the motion of the pen, deliberate as a signature. "You'll want to start early, I expect. Light's better in the mornings, down in the glen."

It was the most words anyone had spoken to her since she'd turned off the main road, and every one of them had been correct, helpful, and somehow wrong, like a recording of kindness played at slightly the wrong speed.

"Thank you," Elodie said, and took the bread.

* * *

The cottage smelled of cold ash and lavender bags gone to dust. She unpacked with the efficiency of long practice: scanner units racked and charging, laptop humming, the tripod by the door ready for morning. Through the bedroom window she could see the church tower against the last of the light, and she noticed—it was the kind of detail her eye collected automatically—that there was no bell in the belfry. The louvred openings stood dark and empty, and yet the tower had a look of expectancy about it, the way an empty chair does.

She made tea, spread the county records across the kitchen table, and reminded herself why she was here.

The file was thin, which was itself remarkable. Harrow Glen appeared in the Domesday Book under a name she couldn't quite pronounce; it appeared in tithe maps, enclosure awards, the 1888 Ordnance Survey. And then, gradually, it stopped appearing properly. The modern OS Explorer showed the village and the church and the lane in, but the surrounding ground was marked with a hatching the legend identified only as *unimproved pasture*. No field boundaries. No footpaths, though she could see worn tracks in the satellite imagery, pale threads converging on the churchyard from six directions like spokes without a wheel.

And beneath it—according to the brief that had brought her here—there were voids. A mining consultant working a quarry proposal twelve miles east had run a seismic line across the glen almost as an afterthought and gotten returns that made no sense: cavities, extensive and interconnected, at depths between eight and forty metres, directly beneath the village and radiating outward. Not mine workings. Not karst. Something older than either category allowed for, and older than any record.

The Heritage Board wanted it documented. Someone at the Board, she suspected, wanted it documented quietly, by a freelancer, with a report that could be filed and forgotten.

"Fine by me," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen. Strange places paid better than sensible ones.

She slept badly, which she attributed to the unfamiliar silence. Villages weren't silent, really—they had dogs, boilers, wind in wires—but Harrow Glen's silence had weight, like held breath. Once, near dawn, she thought she heard a sound from the direction of the church: a single deep tone, felt more than heard, the way you feel a lorry pass two streets away. She lay listening for it to repeat. It didn't. She filed it under pipes.

* * *

Morning came up white and still, mist pooled in the glen like milk in a bowl. By seven she had the scanner set up in the churchyard, tripod legs sunk into turf soft with dew, among graves so old their inscriptions had been eaten back to blankness. The church itself was locked; she hadn't expected otherwise. She worked outward from the tower in a grid, letting the LiDAR build its ghost-architecture of the ground while she walked the lanes with the mobile unit, shooting the cottages, the wellhead she'd been told stood behind the schoolhouse (though there was no schoolhouse anymore), the sunken track at the glen's eastern lip where the seismic anomaly seemed to surface.

By noon the raw data was in. By two, processed. She sat in the cottage kitchen with the point cloud rotating slowly on her screen, drinking tea gone cold, and forgot entirely to drink it.

The tunnels were beautiful. That was her first honest reaction, professional pride swamping everything else. They were also impossible.

They began beneath the church—not near it, *beneath*, the shaft of the old well dropping clean through bedrock into a chamber—and spread from there in a geometry she had never seen outside of termite mounds and one cave system in Slovenia. Galleries spiralled outward in descending coils. Side passages branched with a regularity that wasn't natural and wasn't human either, or wasn't human in any way she recognised; human excavations followed seams and strata, but these passages ignored the geology entirely, cutting through limestone where limestone should have defeated them, vaulting spaces that had no business standing. The deepest returns put voids at sixty metres, below the water table, below anything.

She cross-checked against every record she had. The county archives listed no mines. No chalk pits beyond a shallow marl dig abandoned in 1840. No crypts beyond the ordinary. The 1911 parish inventory mentioned a bell, a font, a well "of great age, bricked at the mouth." Nothing about this. Nothing anywhere about this.

Her scanner didn't hallucinate. She calibrated it twice, ran the churchyard grid again, got the same cathedral of emptiness underneath, exact to the centimetre.

"Hello, you," she murmured, zooming into the chamber beneath the well shaft. The walls of it resolved into fine detail even through forty metres of rock, and she frowned, leaning closer, because the render showed the surfaces scored—regularly, densely, deliberately—with thousands upon thousands of small marks. Tally marks, her mind supplied, and then rejected the word as too small. There were more marks than there were days in the history of the village above them.

She was still staring when someone knocked at the cottage door.

He was an old man, tall and stooped, in a waxed jacket gone soft with age, and she placed him after a moment: he'd been one of the figures watching her from a garden gate that morning, along with two others, none of whom had spoken or waved. His face was weathered into folds, and his eyes were pale, and kind, and that was the worst part, she thought later. They were genuinely kind.

"Miss Eastwick." He held out a covered dish. "Mrs. Pargeter sent down a pie. You'll be wanting your supper, working the way you do."

"That's—thank you. Very kind." She took it. He didn't leave. He stood on the step with his hands folded, looking past her shoulder toward the laptop screen, though from the angle he could have seen nothing but glow.

"You'll be mapping the ground under us," he said. Not a question.

"I'm documenting anomalies for the Heritage Board. Subsidence assessment, mostly—"

"Aye." He nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd known for decades. "You'll find it, then. What's down there." His voice was mild, conversational, the voice of a man discussing drainage. "We've always known it was there. We don't go looking at it, is all. Looking wakes it up a bit, you understand. Like stirring a fire."

"I'm not sure I do, Mr.—?"

"Tice. Abel Tice." He smiled, and the smile reached his eyes, and that was worse. "You seem a capable young woman. Capable people stay too long, sometimes. They get interested." He glanced up at the sky, at the flat white ceiling of cloud. "Season's turning. First frost won't be long. If I were you, I'd finish your pictures and go home before it comes. Before the season turns proper."

"And if I don't?"

The kindness in his face didn't waver at all. That was the terrible thing; it never wavered, not once.

"Oh, we'd help you," he said. "Everyone would. Everyone helps, here. That's rather the trouble with us." He touched his cap. "Enjoy the pie, miss. Mind how you go."

He walked away down Church Lane without hurrying, and Elodie stood in the doorway holding a pie she suddenly had no appetite for, watching the mist thicken in the glen, feeling—for the first time in eleven years of crawling through other people's darknesses—the distinct and unreasonable certainty that the ground beneath her feet was not empty, and had noticed her arrival.

Behind her, on the laptop screen, the point cloud turned slowly in the dark, sixty metres down, patient as arithmetic.

That night she dreamed of counting.
