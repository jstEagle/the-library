## 3. Ledger of Names

The county records office occupied the ground floor of a converted wool warehouse in Ashcombe, eleven miles and one indifferent bus ride from Harrow Glen. Elodie had called ahead, been told the archive was open to researchers by appointment, and arrived at nine sharp with her laptop, a scanner she'd have no permission to use, and the particular hunger of a woman who had spent two nights not sleeping.

The bell was still in her head. That was the truth of it. She'd lain awake cataloguing rational explanations—wind through the tower's louvres, a recording, some agricultural machine with a bronze note—and discarded each one by morning light. What remained was simpler and worse: a full peal, six changes, rung by hands that did not exist, heard by an entire village that had agreed, without discussion, to say nothing.

The archivist was a small man named Pellowe with reading glasses on a chain and the air of someone who had waited his whole career for a difficult question.

"Harrow Glen," he repeated, when she gave him the parish name. "Now there's a parish that doesn't like being looked at."

"It appears on my survey data," Elodie said. "Tunnels under the churchyard. They're on no map I can find."

"Mm." He said it the way Martha Quill had said *the count wants you presentable*—no, he hadn't said that; that had been Martha, two days ago, over bread Elodie hadn't asked for. Pellowe said it the way men say things they've decided not to elaborate on. "Parish registers would be held locally. But the quarter sessions, tithe apportionments, enclosure awards—we hold those. And there's the Harrow deposit."

"The Harrow deposit?"

"Manorial papers. The Glen was a closed manor until 1921—never sold, never subdivided, passed through one family who kept their own books rather than trusting the county." He was already moving down the stacks, trailing one finger along shelf edges. "Most of it is rents and harvest accounts. Dull as ditchwater. But there are oddities."

He set three grey archival boxes on a table beneath the window and left her alone with them, which struck her later as either trust or courtesy or the practiced retreat of a man who knew better than to watch.

The first box was indeed dull. Rent rolls from the 1700s, each name followed by its payment in kind—wheat, wool, a goose at Michaelmas. She transcribed mechanically, letting the rhythm of it settle her nerves, and it was nearly an hour before she noticed what the rhythm was hiding.

Every entry, every year, every family, carried a second notation after the rent. A small symbol—a circle bisected by a vertical line—and beside it, in a different hand, a number.

She counted back through decades. The numbers changed annually but never randomly. For any given family, the figure rose and fell within a narrow band, generation after generation, as though tracking something inherited and measured. The Fennimore line sat at seven, then eight, then six across sixty years of entries. The Aldous family held steady at four for so long that when it finally shifted to five, in 1833, the clerk had underlined it twice.

Elodie sat back. The window threw pale light across the page, and she became aware of the silence of the building around her—the particular silence of a place where paper remembers everything and tells only those who ask correctly.

The second box held the manor court rolls, and these were stranger still. Between the routine presentments—stray cattle, unmended fences, a miller fined for false weights—ran a recurring item, entered at intervals of roughly twenty-five years, always in the same formula:

*Item: the well beneath St. Brannoc's is counted, and the covenant renewed, the under-shaft found whole, the tally true. Those bound do renew.*

No fine attached. No names listed beyond the formula. Just that, entered between a dispute about a hedge and the appointment of a hayward, as though counting a well were as ordinary as mending a fence. Twenty-five years. A generation. She thought of the villagers' faces—Martha's careful blankness, the vicar's denial—and felt the first cold thread of understanding go through her: this wasn't superstition layered over the village like moss. It was load-bearing. It had been built into the structure of the place before anyone living could remember otherwise.

The third box was smaller, and the papers inside were later—twentieth century, typed in places, handwritten in others. And here, near the bottom, wrapped in a folder whose string had been tied and retied so many times the knot had gone hard, she found the ledger.

It was not a county document. It was a parish book, hand-bound, its cover scuffed black leather gone soft as cloth at the corners. The title page read, in copperplate faded to the colour of weak tea: *A True Register of the Families Bound to the Well of Harrow Glen, Being the Covenant Renewed Each Generation, to the Glory of God and the Quiet of the Parish.*

Below it, a second line in a different, older hand: *And let none enter whose name is not written, nor depart whose name is written.*

Her mouth had gone dry. She turned pages with the flat care of someone handling something that might be listening—which was absurd, she told herself, which was exactly the kind of thinking the village wanted from outsiders—but her hands were not quite steady, and she noticed that too.

The ledger was organised by family. Each household had its page or pages: surname at the head, then entries in descending generations, births and deaths and marriages noted in various hands across two centuries. At the foot of each page ran a column of figures matching the mysterious numbers in the rent rolls. And against certain entries, small annotations in red-brown ink that might once have been brighter:

*Aldous — paid in full, 1791.*
*Fennimore — short by one, carried forward.*
*Tredwell — substituted, accepted.*
*Prynn — departed, debt carried.*

That last phrase stopped her. *Departed, debt carried.* She turned pages looking for it again and found it four more times across two hundred years, always attached to a family that vanished from the parish rolls shortly after—emigrated, died out, dissolved. The debt, whatever it was, did not lapse with distance. It travelled. It waited.

She told herself she was searching the index out of professional thoroughness. She was aware, even as she did it, that this was a lie, and that some part of her had known since the bell rang why she had come to this village at all—why the contract had seemed like fate, why she'd ignored the flat unfriendliness, why she had not left when Martha kindly told her to.

E was not a common initial in the index. There was one entry.

**Eastwick.**

One page. One entry. Dated 1948.

*Eastwick, Margaret Rose, admitted to the covenant upon marriage to Tredwell, Josiah (deceased 1947). Bound in right of husband's line, continued in own name per agreement of the parish.*

Beneath it, in the same careful hand, the annotation:

*Departed, debt carried.*

Elodie read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves. Margaret Rose Eastwick. Her grandmother—the grandmother who had been a single closed door in the family's history, spoken of twice in Elodie's childhood, both times in her father's flattest voice: *my mother left when I was six; we didn't follow.* No photographs. No grave visited. A woman who had walked out of one life and into another and taken the reasons with her.

1948. Married into the village, widowed within a year, and gone. And the ledger—this book that had tracked families for centuries with the patience of geology—had marked her departure not as an ending but as a deferral. *Debt carried.* Carried where? Carried by whom?

Carried down, came the answer, unbidden, in a voice that sounded like her own. Carried through the bloodline, to a son raised elsewhere, to a granddaughter who grew up in cities believing herself unconnected to any soil deeper than pavement. To a woman who chose, of all the parishes in England, to survey this one.

She pressed her palms flat against the table. The wood was cool and real. Outside, a delivery lorry changed gear on the high street; somewhere a phone rang and was answered. The ordinary world went on being ordinary, and she clung to that while the vertigo worked through her—not fear exactly, though fear was in it, but the sick gravitational lurch of discovering that the ground you stand on has been moving your whole life. She had not come to Harrow Glen. She had been coming to Harrow Glen since 1948. Every choice she'd made—the degree, the contracts, the restless need to be underground, to map what refused mapping—had been walking her here along lines drawn before her birth, in a book kept by people she'd never met, for a debt incurred by a woman whose face she did not know.

Inheritance as ambush. She'd have laughed if her throat weren't so tight.

There was more on the page. Below the annotation, in ink so fresh it might have been days old, a final line:

*Eastwick — returned. Present in parish. Count outstanding.*

The date beside it was last Tuesday. The day she'd arrived.

Elodie closed the ledger very gently, as though it were sleeping, and sat for a long moment watching dust turn in the window light. Then she took out her phone and photographed every page—the Eastwick page twice—and was reaching to rewrap the folder when Pellowe appeared at the end of the stacks, silent as furniture.

"You found the register, then," he said. Not a question.

"You knew it was there."

"I knew where it was kept." He came closer, folded his hands. His voice dropped, and it was not unkind—that was somehow the worst part, the kindness again, everywhere in this country kindness arriving like a pallbearer's courtesy. "Miss Eastwick. I'll tell you what I'd tell my own daughter. The families in that book renewed it because the alternative was worse, and they stayed because leaving didn't help. You're not the first to find their name in it. But you may be the first in a long while who could still walk away."

"I'm not planning to walk away."

"No," he said, with the mild certainty of a man reading a weather report. "I didn't think so. Your grandmother said much the same, in 1948, standing about where you're standing now. Right before she did something the parish has been paying for ever since."

The room went very quiet. Even the dust seemed to stop turning.

"What did she do?"

Pellowe looked at the ledger, then at her, and something in his face—the first honest thing she'd seen all week—was pity.

"That," he said, "is in the vicarage safe. And I'd ask yourself whether knowing is a mercy or just another way of being counted."

* * *

She walked back to the bus stop through streets that had begun, subtly, to watch her. Not hostility—nothing so useful as hostility. A woman watering her step paused, trowel dripping. Two men outside the garage stopped talking mid-sentence and resumed it only when she'd passed, their voices pitched low and even, like people continuing a conversation they'd rehearsed. A child on a bicycle stood at the corner and looked at her until she rounded the bend.

They know, she thought. They've always known. Not that I'm a stranger—they know I'm not that. They know what I am. The last of a line. A debt with legs.

At the bus stop, the timetable behind cracked glass showed the next service in forty minutes. Across the road, the church tower of Harrow Glen rose above the trees, empty, swept clean, waiting for a bell that had melted seventy years before anyone poured its successor's bronze.

She thought of her father's flat voice. *We didn't follow.* Of course they hadn't. Following meant arriving. Arriving meant being written in.

Her phone buzzed. An email from her brother Daniel—subject line: *weird letter.* She opened it with fingers gone cold despite the mild afternoon.

*Ell — got something in the post today, no return address. Handwriting I don't recognise. It says: "Tell your sister the count keeps what it counts. Come before the dark of the moon." Mum's side? Do you know anything about this? Should I be worried?*

Elodie looked up. The glen lay green and innocent in the light, smoke rising straight from a chimney, a dog barking somewhere with perfect ordinariness.

The dark of the moon was in nineteen days.

She typed a reply—*Don't come. I'll explain everything. Don't come*—and watched the message sit unsent in the dead zone, the little spinner turning and turning, patient as a tally mark, adding itself to the count.
