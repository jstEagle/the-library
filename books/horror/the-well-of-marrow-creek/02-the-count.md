## 2. The Count

The house had eleven doors, and Rosalind Lockridge knew this the way other men knew their own phone numbers.

He'd known it since he was six years old, standing in the hallway of his grandfather's house on Delmar Street while old Amos held a flashlight under his chin and said, *Count with me, boy. Doors first. Then windows. Then locks. In that order. Always that order.* The ritual had a rhythm to it, a liturgy: doors, windows, locks. You didn't skip. You didn't shuffle. Skipping was how things got in.

He was forty-three now and he still did it every night before bed, and every night it took exactly four minutes, and every night the number came out right—eleven doors, nine windows, seven locks—and every night he told himself the same lie he'd been telling himself since childhood: *this is just a quirk. A habit. Lots of people have habits.*

Lots of people did not check the back door twice.

But tonight, three days after the woman on the radio had described her own drowning in a voice like water running over stones, Rosalind stood at the top of the stairs at 1:14 a.m. and began.

"Eleven," he murmured, touching each doorframe as he passed. Bedroom door. Bathroom door. Wren's door—he paused there, hand flat against the wood, listening. She was asleep; he could hear the small wet sound of her breathing through the gap at the bottom, the one she kept insisting she'd outgrow and never did. Closet door. Hall closet. His office. Two more upstairs, the ones that led nowhere useful—a linen space and a crawl access nobody had opened since they moved in. Then down the stairs, careful on the fourth step because it had always been the loud one even after he'd fixed it twice. Front door. Back door. The cellar door, which was the one that mattered most, though he'd never have been able to tell you why without lying.

Eleven. Correct.

Windows next. Nine of them, and he checked the latches with two fingers, a flicking motion his grandfather had taught him—*you don't pull, you flick, pulling wakes them up*—and Rosalind had never asked who *them* was, because asking questions during the count broke the count, and breaking the count meant starting over, and starting over meant the thing you were counting against had time to move.

Seven locks. Deadbolts, chain, the little brass slide on the cellar door that his grandfather had installed in 1961 and that Rosalind had never once considered removing, even when the home inspector called it "a charming antique" and offered him two hundred dollars for it on the spot.

Then—the part he hated, the part that made him feel insane even doing it—he went back and checked the back door again.

It was locked. Of course it was locked. He had locked it ninety seconds ago. But his hand found the deadbolt anyway and turned it, feeling the bolt seat into the frame with that heavy, satisfying *chunk*, and only then did his shoulders come down from around his ears.

"You know I can hear you."

Wren's voice, from the top of the stairs. Twelve years old, sleep-roughened, too observant for anyone's good.

"Go back to bed, bird."

"You're doing it twice." She came down two steps, barefoot, hair a dark storm around her face. "The back door. You always do it twice."

"I'm making sure."

"You made sure already." She sat down on the step, chin on her knees, watching him with those gray eyes that were her mother's and missed nothing, which was the worst inheritance he could think of. "Grandpa used to do that. Dad said so. Counting stuff at night."

Rosalind's hand was still on the deadbolt. He made himself let go. Made his voice stay light, the radio voice, the one that could carry a caller through a panic attack or a suicide scare without cracking. "Your grandpa was a careful man."

"He was weird," Wren said, without malice. "Dad says the whole family's weird about doors." She yawned enormously, theatrical, twelve years old again instead of whatever she'd been a second ago—some older, sharper creature wearing his daughter's face. "Why do you count, Dad?"

Because my grandfather taught me that counting keeps the tally honest. Because somewhere in the marrow of this family there is an arithmetic we've been running for a hundred years and I don't know what it's for. Because last week a dead woman called my show and knew the color of the ribbon in her hair when they pulled her out of the creek, and I can't stop thinking that if the dead can find their way to a microphone, something else can find its way to a door.

"Because it helps me sleep," he said.

It was the worst lie he'd ever told her, and she knew it, and he knew she knew it, and neither of them said anything else. She went back up the stairs. Her door closed softly. He counted it anyway—eleven—and went to bed with the lamp on, which he hadn't done since he was nine.

* * *

Wren Lockridge did not go to sleep.

She lay in the dark listening to her father's footsteps do their slow circuit of the house, and she thought about the word *weird*, and how Dad said it like a shield, and how Grandpa Amos had died in this same town before she was born with all the doors in his house locked and the coroner's report saying *natural causes* but her dad going quiet for a whole year after, the kind of quiet that has weight.

When the footsteps stopped—when she heard his bed creak and then nothing—she got up.

Not to snoop. That's what she told herself. She just wanted water. The kitchen glass, the one with the chipped rim she liked. And the office was on the way. Technically.

Her father's office smelled like coffee and old paper and the lemon polish he used on the desk his grandfather had left him, a huge dark thing with drawers that stuck in humid weather. Wren pulled open the middle drawer looking for the good pens—the ones that wrote smooth, not the scratchy promotional ones from the hardware store—and her fingers hit paper instead.

A folded sheet, soft at the creases, like it had been opened and refolded many times. And beneath it, a smaller piece, newer, torn from a legal pad.

She shouldn't have. She knew she shouldn't have. She unfolded both.

The legal pad page was a list of names. Written in her father's handwriting, small and neat, the handwriting he used for the show log. Fourteen names. Thirteen of them had lines drawn through them—single strokes, clean, deliberate, the kind of crossing-out you do when you're finished with something, not the angry scribble you do when you're mad.

Three names were not crossed out.

*Merritt Calloway.*

*Dana Okafor.*

*Rosalind James Lockridge.*

His own name. On a list. With dead people—or she assumed they were dead people, because she recognized some of the crossed-out ones from the headstones she'd seen on her walk to school, the old section of the cemetery by the fence where the ground dipped and pooled after rain. Names like Harlan Voss. Names like Edith Tiller. Names that belonged to photographs from the historical society window downtown, sepia faces in high collars.

And below the list, in the same neat hand, a single line:

*Count must balance.*

Wren stood very still in the dark office, holding her father's secrets in both hands, and felt something shift in her chest—not fear exactly, not yet, but the cold settling-in of a thing becoming real. Because she knew something he didn't know she knew. She'd known for weeks.

There was a well behind the fence line, past the blackberries, the old stone-rimmed one that was supposed to be dry as dust, that had been dry her whole life, that Dad had told her a hundred times never to play near, which had always seemed like a strange rule for a hole in the ground with nothing in it.

Lately, when the wind came off the ridge in the evening, she could hear it humming.

Low. Patient. Almost like singing, if singing could be done underwater. Like something down there clearing its throat after a long sleep, remembering how.

She put the papers back exactly as she'd found them—fold along the old crease, legal pad beneath, drawer shut until it caught—and she stood in the doorway a moment looking at the desk where her father sat every night with his headphones on, talking to the town, keeping everyone's secrets but his own.

Thirteen crossed out. Three remaining.

She counted them twice, the way he counted doors.

Then she went back to bed, and lay awake, and listened to the hum rise faintly through the floorboards, patient as arithmetic, waiting for the number to come due.
