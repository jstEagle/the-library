## 16. Backlash

The scarecrow wore his coat.

Rosalind saw it from the kitchen window at 6:40 in the morning, coffee halfway to his mouth, and for one long second he thought someone had left a body on his lawn. Then the details assembled themselves: the flannel shirt stuffed with straw, the jeans with the knees blown out, and on top of it all—unmistakable, even from thirty yards—the brown corduroy jacket he'd worn on air every night for six years. His jacket. The one with the burn hole on the left cuff from a candle he'd knocked over during a blackout broadcast in 2019.

It was staked to the ground between the porch and the fence line, arms outstretched, head slumped forward like a man asleep in a chair. And where the face should have been there was nothing but burlap, stitched once across the middle with red thread—a mouth, sewn shut.

Wren was still upstairs. He went out alone, barefoot on cold grass, and stood in front of the thing trying to decide what he felt. Fear, obviously. But underneath the fear, something worse: recognition. He knew that posture. He'd spent forty-one years perfecting it—the slump of a man doing everything he could not to be noticed. Someone had studied him closely enough to build him out of straw.

The jacket smelled like smoke before he touched it. Not fresh smoke. Old smoke, days of it, soaked into the corduroy. He lifted one sleeve and the whole arm came away in his hand, crumbling into charred cloth and gray straw. The fire hadn't happened yet. The jacket had been burned somewhere else, carefully, and dressed onto the frame afterward. Whoever made this wanted him to find it already ruined. Already mourned.

There was a note pinned to the chest with a rusted nail. A single sheet of lined paper, the kind Wren used for math homework, written in block capitals:

**YOU TALK TO THE DEAD. PRETTY SOON THEY'LL TALK TO YOU.**

He stood there until his feet went numb, holding the burned sleeve, listening to the town wake up around him—the Hendersons' sprinkler, a school bus groaning on Corliss Road, someone's dog losing its mind two yards over. Normal sounds. The sounds of a place that had decided, overnight, that he was no longer part of it.

Behind the fence, the well hummed.

* * *

He didn't tell Wren about the note. He told her the scarecrow was a prank, which was technically true, and she looked at him the way she'd been looking at him since the night he'd found her list of names—like she was keeping score of his lies now, tallying them somewhere behind her eyes.

"You're wearing the other jacket tonight," she said. It wasn't a question.

"It's cold."

"Your other jacket doesn't have the hole in the cuff." She poured her cereal with the deliberate precision of a surgeon. "Somebody took your jacket, Dad. Out of your closet. While we were sleeping."

He had no answer for that, so he drank his coffee, and she let him have the silence, which was its own kind of punishment. Twelve years old and she'd already learned that the cruelest thing you could do to a man who lied badly was give him room to keep doing it.

At the station, Delia met him in the parking lot with her phone held out like a summons.

"You need to see this before you go inside."

The Marrow Creek community board—once a place to sell used snowblowers—had turned feral over the past week. He scrolled through it standing in the cold: screenshots of his show, clips of the roll call, threads titled WHO IS THIS GHOUL and HAS ANYONE CHECKED IF HE'S EVEN ALIVE. Someone had posted a photo of his house taken from the road, a red circle drawn around Wren's bedroom window. Someone else had dug up the obituary of Rosalind's mother and posted it under the heading THE FAMILY BUSINESS.

"They're calling it grief pornography," Delia said. "That's the phrase. Councilwoman Brandt used it at the meeting last night. There were people there, Roz. Forty, fifty people. They want you off the air."

"The families I've helped—"

"Are half of them. The other half are the ones who heard things last week they can't unhear." She pulled the phone back. "A woman called the station yesterday asking if we were running a scam. Said her brother died in March and nobody's called her from him, so how come the Kowalskis get messages and she gets nothing? She wanted to know what the rate was."

"What did you tell her?"

"That grief isn't a vending machine." Delia's voice cracked on the last word, and he realized she'd been crying in her car before he got there. "Roz, I've been here nineteen years. I believe you. God help me, I believe every word. But there's a man outside the building right now in a pickup truck, and he's been there since five a.m., and he just sits there watching the door. And I keep thinking about the scarecrow."

News traveled fast in a town this size. Faster than shame.

* * *

The council meeting was at seven, in the same wood-paneled room where they approved crosswalk paint and argued about the fireworks budget. Rosalind went because Voss called him at four and said, in a voice stripped down to bare wire: "Be there. Don't say anything unless you have to. And don't wear the coat."

The room was full. That was the first shock—not hostility, exactly, but fullness. He'd spent his whole life in rooms that emptied when he entered, and now every folding chair was taken and people stood along the walls, and all of it, every eye, was aimed at him. Visibility settled over him like wet wool. He understood, sitting in the second row with his hands flat on his knees, why his grandfather had chosen the ledger over the microphone. Invisibility wasn't cowardice. It was armor. And he'd handed it over name by name, night by night, on air, gladly.

Councilwoman Brandt ran the meeting the way she ran everything, with the brisk efficiency of a woman who believed procedure could hold back the dark. She read the complaint into the record: unauthorized solicitation of grieving persons, exploitation of recent deaths, creation of public alarm, operation of an unlicensed—"she paused, searching for the word"—*service*.

"Grief pornography," said a man in the back. Halvorsen. Rosalind knew his face from the hardware store. His father had died in January; his mother had called the station twice last week and hung up both times before Rosalind picked up.

"Mr. Lockridge," Brandt said. "Do you have anything to say?"

He had a great many things to say. He had three weeks of dead voices in his ears and a ledger in a historical society basement with his family's name inked into it generations before he was born. He had a scarecrow in his garage wearing his skin's memory. What he said was: "I read names. That's all. People call, and I read the names of their dead, and sometimes the dead answer, and I pass along what they say. I haven't charged anyone anything. I haven't invented anything. You can check every message against the families who received them."

"And when they don't check out?" This from Councilman Pruitt, who'd refused, three nights ago, to come to the gym rehearsal, who'd sat in his truck outside and honked the horn until the crowd dispersed. "When some grieving widow gets a message from something pretending to be her husband? Who answers for that? Because I'll tell you what I think is talking on that radio, Lockridge. I don't think it's anybody's husband."

The room murmured. Somewhere behind him a chair scraped.

"I think," Pruitt went on, warming, standing up now, "that this town has always had a sickness in it, and most of us had the good sense to leave it buried, and this man has gone and dug it up and put it on the FM band. My kids can hear it in the car. My mother won't sleep with her window shut anymore. You want to talk about harm? Talk about that."

"Your mother," Rosalind said quietly, "is on my list for Thursday."

The murmur stopped dead.

Pruitt's face went through several colors. "You leave my mother out of—"

"She called me Tuesday. Eleven-forty p.m. She wants you to know the ring is in the blue jewelry box, not the green one, and she knows you sold the green one in February, and she isn't angry, she just wants you to stop lying to your wife about the money." He heard his own voice go flat and steady, the broadcast voice, the one that didn't shake. "That's what I do, Mr. Pruitt. I deliver mail. If you don't like the postage, take it up with the sender."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Half the room looked at Pruitt with something like pity. The other half looked at Rosalind the way you look at a man handling a live wire—fascinated, and backing toward the door.

Brandt banged her gavel twice. "We're not adjudicating individual messages. The question is whether the station continues this programming. I'll entertain a motion."

And then Sheriff Voss stood up.

Rosalind had expected thinness from him—a procedural objection, a jurisdictional shrug, the minimum a man could offer and still look himself in the mirror. What he got was Voss walking to the front of the room in his uniform, hat under his arm, and saying, in a voice pitched to carry to the back wall:

"For the record: no laws are being broken. The FCC has no jurisdiction over content of this kind, the station's license is current, and Mr. Lockridge has committed no offense I'm aware of or likely to be." A pause. "I'd also remind the council that the county sheriff's office does not take direction from the town board on matters of speech."

"That's it?" someone called. "That's the defense?"

Voss looked out at the room for a long moment, and Rosalind watched him weigh something—watched the scales move behind his eyes, the same scales Rosalind had been staring into for weeks now, loyalty on one pan and truth on the other, and the truth getting heavier every single night.

"That's the defense I can give you," Voss said finally. "It's the one that keeps me employed. Take it or leave it."

* * *

Afterward, in the parking lot, under the sodium lights with moths ticking against them, Voss fell into step beside him without being asked.

"That was stupid," Voss said. "What you did in there. With Pruitt's mother."

"It was true."

"It was true and it was stupid and those are not mutually exclusive." He stopped at the edge of the light, where the dark started. "You know what happens now. You embarrassed a proud man in public using information he can't explain to his wife. Men like that don't write letters. Men like that buy gasoline."

"I know." He touched the sleeve of the substitute jacket, the one without the hole. "I got the preview this morning."

Voss followed his gaze down to the parking lot's edge, where the pickup truck sat with its lights off. Different truck than the one at the station. Same posture. Engine ticking as it cooled, or maybe idling low—you couldn't tell anymore, in this town, what was waiting and what was just running.

"Delia says there was a man at the station too."

"There's a man everywhere now," Voss said. "That's the price of the ticket, Lockridge. You wanted to be seen. Congratulations." He put his hat back on, and for a second, under the brim, his face was just a tired man's face, fifty-eight years old, carrying something heavy in the dark. "My mother called me again last night. First time since September. She wanted to know why I haven't come to the well yet. Why I'm still standing on the shore." He adjusted the brim so it shadowed his eyes. "I didn't have an answer for her either."

Then he walked to his cruiser and drove away, and the pickup truck's headlights came on a moment later, and it rolled slowly past Rosalind—slow enough to be a message, slow enough that he could see the driver's silhouette and nothing more—and turned toward Route 9.

* * *

Wren was awake when he got home. He knew it before he opened the door, because the porch light was off—he always left it on—and because the house had that particular held-breath quality of a place where a child is listening through a floorboard.

She was sitting at the kitchen table in her pajamas with the good flashlight, the one with the red filter, reading his grandfather's journal. His copy. The transcription he'd made and hidden in the freezer behind the bag of frozen peas, because he was apparently a man who hid things in freezers now, and because twelve-year-olds checked desk drawers but never freezers.

"We need to talk about the arithmetic," she said.

"Wren—"

"No, listen." She turned the page with the care of someone handling scripture. "Odette's numbers. Every name read aloud feeds it. Right? That's what she figured out. So the council is right, sort of. You're making it stronger." She looked up, and the red light made her face strange and old. "But here's what they don't get, Dad. Here's what none of them get. It was getting stronger anyway. The water was coming back before you ever read a name. Grandpa's journal says the refill takes a hundred years whether anybody talks or not. The only question—the *only* question—is whether it comes back fat and dumb and unfed, or whether it comes back having been *witnessed*." She closed the journal. "You're not feeding it. You're seasoning it. There's a difference."

He stared at his daughter, twelve years old, sitting in red light at one in the morning, having independently derived the theology of Marrow Creek while he'd been getting threatened in a municipal building.

"Somebody burned me in effigy today," he said. It came out smaller than he meant it to.

"I know. I helped carry it to the garage." She shrugged at his expression. "I'm not scared of a scarecrow, Dad. It had your coat but it didn't have your *hands*. Whoever made it has never actually watched you. Your hands always shake." She slid the journal across the table to him. "Whoever it is, they only know the version of you that sits still and talks. They don't know the one who counts doors."

Outside, far off, toward the fence line, the humming changed key.

They both heard it. Neither moved. And in the dark window above the sink—for one clear second before Wren reached over and flipped on the kitchen light, killing the reflection entirely—Rosalind saw the glass lag behind them both, and in that lag, in that borrowed second, the reflection of the kitchen showed a figure standing behind his chair that was not Wren, and was not him, and had his grandfather's shoulders.

The light came on. The window became a window.

"Thursday," Wren said, gathering the journal against her chest like a shield. "How many names left?"

"Eleven families. Then mine."

"Then yours," she agreed, and headed for the stairs, and paused on the third step without turning around. "Dad? The note on the scarecrow. The one you think I don't know about." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle. "*Pretty soon they'll talk to you.* They already do. That's the whole point. Whoever wrote it thinks that's a threat."

She climbed the rest of the stairs, and her door—her door, the one that had been shut and counted and locked every night of her life—stayed open behind her.

Rosalind stood alone in the kitchen, in the sudden terrible visibility of the overhead light, and understood that whatever came for him now would come in the open, in the daylight, in front of everyone—and that the only thing left to decide was whether he'd be looking at it, or looking away.
