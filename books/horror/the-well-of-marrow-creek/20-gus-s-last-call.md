## 20. Gus's Last Call

Gus Tiller died on a Tuesday, the way he'd promised he would—quietly, in his own bed, with the blinds drawn against the well's side of the street.

Rosalind learned about it at six-forty in the evening, when the county's automated obituary feed pinged the station's news inbox. *Harold August "Gus" Tiller, 91, passed peacefully at home, surrounded by family. A lifelong resident of Marrow Creek, Gus worked thirty-eight years at the feed store and was a fixture of the First Methodist pancake breakfasts. He is survived by—*

Rosalind stopped reading. The obituary said *survived by* and then listed no one. The feed store was gone. The pancake breakfasts were a memory older than most of the people who'd eaten them. Gus had spent his last decade in a house that smelled of dust and WD-40, watching the Lockridge property through his kitchen window, waiting for something he'd hoped would never come and had come anyway.

*Survived by no one,* the obituary didn't say. But that was the shape of it.

Rosalind sat in the studio's blue light and read the notice three times. On the board, the phone lines glowed their patient green. He had two hours before he went live. He thought about calling the house. He thought about driving past it, slow, the way people do when they don't know what else to do for the dead. He did neither. He made coffee he didn't drink and watched the light on the ON AIR sign cycle from dark to amber to red, and when it went red at ten o'clock, he leaned into the microphone and said the thing he'd been turning over all evening.

"Gus Tiller died today. Some of you knew him. Some of you bought feed from him, or got your chainsaw sharpened at his kitchen table because he never trusted the shop on Route 9 to do it right. I'm not going to do a tribute. Gus would've hated a tribute. He'd have called it *lip service* and spit in the general direction of anyone who offered it." A breath. "But if he calls in tonight, I'll take the call. That's all I've got. That's the whole tribute."

The board lit up almost immediately. He let it ring.

* * *

The first callers were the living, and they were angry in the tired, rehearsed way Marrow Creek anger had settled into over the past month. A man named Denny Kowalczyk wanted to know when Rosalind was going to stop "running a séance with a broadcast license." A woman from the Methodist church read a statement about dignity in grief. Rosalind took them all, thanked them, and moved on, because that was the job now—being the town's lightning rod, standing in the open where the strikes could find him.

At 11:52, the board lit again, and this time the caller ID field was blank. Not blocked. *Blank*, the way it always was, a string of nothing where a number should be, as if the phone company itself had decided not to look too closely.

He took a breath and opened the line. "You're on with Rosalind. Who's this?"

Static, first. The particular static he'd come to know—warmer than dead air, almost textured, like wind moving through a field of dry corn. Then:

"Roz."

Gus's voice. Thin and papery, the way it had been at the end, but *present* in a way it hadn't been for months. Rosalind closed his eyes. Behind the studio glass, the empty chairs sat in their empty rows. The VU meter danced with every syllable, faithful as a dog.

"Hey, Gus." His voice came out level. He'd gotten good at that—the steadiness was a skill now, like board operation, like reading a cold open. "I heard the news. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be quick. I don't know how long I get." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "They tell you anything about that? How long you get?"

"No. Nobody tells me anything. I just open the line."

"Figures." The static breathed. "Roz, I called for a reason and I'm not going to dance around it, because dancing was never my thing and I've got maybe four minutes of whatever this is. There's a man at the bottom of the well behind your house. Been there since 1974. My son. Danny."

Rosalind's hand found the edge of the desk and held it. "I know, Gus."

"You know the *story*. Everybody knows the story, or the version they tell at the Legion. Drunk kid, fell in, couldn't climb out, drowned before anyone missed him. That's the version that lets everybody sleep." The voice hardened, and for a moment it was the voice of a man who'd sharpened chainsaws for thirty years and didn't suffer fools at his kitchen table. "It's a lie, Roz. I put him down there."

The studio seemed to contract around the words. Rosalind checked the board—line two open, dead; line three, dead. Just the one voice. Just the one confession, going out over 400 watts to every insomniac and night-shift driver and grieving widow in the creek valley.

"Tell me," Rosalind said quietly.

"August of '74. The well had been dry since '51—your grandfather's doing, though I didn't know that then, nobody knew that then, we just knew it was a hole in the ground and holes in the ground don't need watching. But that summer things had been wrong. Livestock spooked. Dogs wouldn't go down the back lots. My wife started hearing humming at night, same as your girl hears now, I'd bet money on it." A pause, and the static filled it like a held breath. "I didn't hear humming. I heard *asking*. That's what it does, Roz, when it's waking up. It doesn't hum for everybody. For some of us it asks. And I answered."

"What did it ask for?"

"It didn't use words. That's the hell of it. You just wake up one morning knowing what it wants, the way you know you're hungry, and you can't point to where the knowing came from. It wanted a Tiller. And I—" The voice caught, and the static surged, and Rosalind leaned toward the meter as if proximity could steady the signal. "I walked Danny out to the fence line on a Saturday night. Told him I'd lost a wrench down there, needed him to shinny down and fetch it. He was nineteen. He laughed at me. Said, *Dad, you lost a wrench in a dry well?* And he went down. And when he got to the bottom, I took the ladder."

Silence in the studio. Somewhere in the building, the HVAC cycled on, a mechanical heartbeat.

"You took the ladder," Rosalind repeated.

"I pulled it up and I sat on the grass and I listened to my son holler for two hours, and then I listened to him stop hollering, and the well filled up from the bottom like a glass under a tap—black water, cold as January—and the humming stopped. And I went home and I kissed my wife and I never told a living soul for fifty-one years." The voice had gone very quiet. "The count paused, Roz. That's what I told you before. One Tiller bought the whole town a generation of quiet. That was the arrangement. That was the *trade*. And I have regretted it every single day since, and I did it anyway, because it was quiet, and quiet is the most seductive thing there is. You know that. You lived invisible your whole life. Quiet is a drug."

Rosalind's mouth was dry. "Gus—why are you telling me this now? On air?"

"Because you're doing the thing I should've done in 1974. You're saying names out loud. You're making this town *look* at what it buried." A dry rasp of breath. "And because I'm dead now, and dead men don't get to keep secrets. That's the one mercy in all this mess, Roz. The dead get to be honest. It's the living who can't afford it."

* * *

The board was lighting up. Rosalind could see it in his peripheral vision—line two, line three, line four, callers stacking up like cars at a crossing. He let them ring. Some things you don't interrupt.

"Gus," he said, "your obituary says you're survived by no one."

"Obit's right. My wife went in '89. Danny went down the hole. I'm the last of a line that ended because of me." A pause. "That's why I called. Not to confess. Confession's cheap, Roz—I've been confessing to myself every night for half a century and it never fixed a damn thing. I called for a *delivery*."

"A delivery."

"You pass things along. That's what you do now. The drowned girl, the man from Route 9, all of them—you carry their words to the living. So carry mine. But not to the living." The static shifted, and for a moment Rosalind thought he heard, underneath it, a faint sound like water moving in a deep place. "Carry mine *down*. Tell my son the debt wasn't his fault."

Rosalind's throat tightened. "Gus—"

"He's been down there fifty-one years thinking it was his. That's what the well does—it doesn't just take you, it makes you *carry* it. Danny went down thinking he'd failed somehow, that he'd done something wrong, that his daddy was angry at him. Nineteen years old and his last thought on this earth was that he'd disappointed his old man over a wrench." The voice broke, and this time Rosalind heard it clearly, the grief of a man who'd had fifty-one years to feel it and no one to feel it with. "You tell him his daddy wasn't angry. You tell him his daddy was the one who should've been ashamed, and was, every day, and that the debt was mine, all mine, and he doesn't owe that hole one single thing. You tell him he can put it down. Do you hear me, Roz? You tell my boy he can *put it down*."

"I'll tell him," Rosalind said. "I promise."

"You don't know how to get it down there."

"I'll figure it out. The water shows things. Wren saw—" He stopped. He hadn't told anyone about what Wren had seen at the fence, not even Wren, not directly. But Gus made a low sound, like he'd expected it.

"The water remembers everything it's ever taken. It'll remember Danny. You put your words in the water, it'll carry them to him. That's how it works both ways, see. That's the trick nobody figured. The well's a *mouth*, but every mouth has two directions." A wheeze that might have been laughter. "Fifty-one years and I only figured that out dead. Typical."

"Gus. One more thing." Rosalind looked at the ON AIR light, red and steady. "You told me not to upset the arrangement. You begged me. Three weeks ago, in your kitchen, you said the count paused and to leave it alone. What changed?"

The static held for a long moment. When Gus answered, his voice had lost its edge and become something older and softer, the voice of a man at the very end of a very long road.

"You did, Roz. You went and said the drowned girl's name on air and her sister finally got to stop wondering. I listened to that broadcast in my chair and I cried like a baby, first time since Danny. And I understood—I finally understood—that the quiet I bought wasn't peace. It was just a long room with the door shut. Peace is when the truth's out and everybody's still standing. I never had peace. I had *quiet*. And I'm done defending it." A breath. "The arrangement's over. Let the count come due. Just—do it right. Do it with everybody watching. That's the only way it counts. That's the only way it *ever* counted."

"Gus—"

"Time's up, Roz. I can feel it going, like a signal fading. One more thing and then I'm gone." The static was thickening now, the voice coming from further away with every word. "The well's going to offer you something. Down there, at the end of it. It offered me something in '74 and I took it and it cost me my boy. When it offers, and it will—remember that anything it gives you easy, it's giving you to keep. And anything it keeps, it keeps *easy*."

"What did it offer you, Gus?"

"Quiet," Gus said. "It offered me quiet. And God forgive me, I said yes."

The line went dead—not the warm static of the dead, but true dead air, the flat nothing of an open channel. The caller ID field stayed blank a moment longer, then cleared, as if the phone company had finished not looking.

Rosalind sat in the red light with his hand still on the board.

* * *

He didn't go to break. He didn't play the station ID. He sat for a full ninety seconds of dead air, which every radio man knows is an eternity, a geological age, the sound of a career ending—and then he leaned in and told the town what they'd just heard.

"That was Gus Tiller," he said. "Gus died today, at ninety-one, in his bed, and an hour ago he called this show from wherever the dead call from, and he confessed to a murder. His own son. Danny Tiller, nineteen years old, went into the dry well behind my house in August of 1974 because his father lured him down there and took the ladder away. The town called it an accident for fifty-one years. It wasn't. And Gus spent his whole life paying for it in the only currency he had left, which was silence, and tonight he decided silence was spent." Rosalind's voice held. His hands, below the desk, were shaking hard enough to rattle the desk itself. "Some of you are going to call the council. Some of you are going to call it a hoax, a stunt, grief pornography—yeah, I've read the letters. Call whatever you want. But there are families in this town who've heard from their dead on this frequency, and you know it's not a stunt, and Gus knew it too, and Gus chose to spend his one night of honesty telling the truth about the worst thing he ever did."

He paused. The board was fully lit now, all six lines, blinking.

"So here's what happens next. Tomorrow night, same time, I'm going down to the well behind my house, and I'm going to put a message in the water for Danny Tiller. His father's message. *The debt wasn't your fault, Danny. Put it down.* And if the water carries it the way Gus says it does, then somewhere at the bottom of that hole, a nineteen-year-old boy who's been carrying his father's sin for half a century is going to finally hear that he can let go of it." Rosalind looked at the red light. "That's not a séance. That's not pornography. That's a delivery. And I'm going to keep making them, every name, every message, until this town has heard everything it buried—because a dead man just told me the truth about the arrangement, and the arrangement is over."

He reached for the fader. Then stopped.

"One more thing. Gus's obituary says he's survived by no one. That's wrong. He's survived by everyone in this town who ever slept easy on the quiet he bought. Which is all of us. Which means we all owe him. And starting tomorrow night, we start paying up."

He faded the mic. The ON AIR light dropped to amber, then dark. In the glass, his reflection sat perfectly still, perfectly synchronized, and for one merciful moment Rosalind couldn't help checking—*one second, two, three*—before his own face finally, faithfully, moved.

* * *

He didn't sleep. At 2 a.m. he sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open to the Tiller page—Odette had photographed every page for him—and read the entry from August 1974. *One (1) Tiller, male, nineteen. Received: quiet, one generation.* In the payment column, in faded brown ink that Rosalind made himself not think too hard about: *paid in full.*

Below it, the next line. The reserved line. *LOCKRIDGE.* Blank, waiting, the leather around it darker than anywhere else on the page, as if the book itself had been gripping that spot for a hundred and twenty years.

Rosalind got up, went to the back door, and looked out at the fence line. The well wasn't visible from here—you needed to be past the fence, in the trees—but he could feel it the way you feel a storm over the horizon, a pressure in the ears, a hum just under hearing. Wren's door was closed. She was asleep, or pretending to be, and he'd made her a promise months ago that he'd stop checking on her twice a night, and he'd broken it every night since.

Tomorrow night he'd go down to the water with Gus's message. He'd speak it into the black surface and watch it carry, and he'd find out whether the dead man's trick worked—whether a mouth really does have two directions. And if it did, if Danny Tiller heard his father's voice after fifty-one years, then Rosalind would know something else too. Something that made his chest go tight and cold.

He'd know the well could *give things back*.

And if it could give things back—words, messages, maybe more—then the ledger's arithmetic was wrong, or incomplete, or lying. And a well that could return what it took was a well that could be *negotiated with*.

The hum behind the fence shifted, very slightly, like something turning over in its sleep.

Rosalind stood at the door a long time, listening, and wrote one line in the margin of the ledger, small, in pencil, where the leather couldn't see it:

*It kept Danny. Maybe Danny knows the way out.*
