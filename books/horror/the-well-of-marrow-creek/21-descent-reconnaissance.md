## 21. Descent Reconnaissance

Dale Voss had been sheriff of Marrow Creek for eleven years, and in that time he'd learned that the town kept two sets of books. The official ones lived in the courthouse basement—deeds, arrests, tax rolls, the tidy fiction of a place that functioned. The other set lived everywhere else. In the way people at the Gas-N-Go went quiet when he walked in. In the way no one had ever once asked him, in eleven years, what happened to the Harlan boy who vanished off Route 9 in 2016. In the way his own mother's voice had come through his kitchen phone last month, three weeks after they buried her, asking him to tell his brother she was sorry about the garden.

He'd chosen silence. He told himself it was prudence. He told himself it was loyalty to a town that couldn't survive its own history being read aloud. Mostly he told himself nothing at all, because thinking about it too long felt like standing at the edge of something with his heels hanging over.

Now Rosalind Lockridge stood in Voss's garage at nine on a Tuesday night, holding a hardware-store action camera and a hundred feet of paracord, and said, "I need to see what's down there."

Voss looked at the well cap in the bed of his truck. He'd taken it off the Tiller property line six years ago when the county flagged it as a hazard—a round slab of poured concrete, older than that, maybe, with an iron ring set in it like a handle. He'd meant to haul it to the dump. It had sat in his truck bed ever since, through two oil changes and one flood, and every time he reached for it his hands found something else to do.

"You understand," Voss said, "that if we do this, we're not curious anymore. Curious is a thing you can walk back."

"I stopped being able to walk things back around the time my bathroom mirror started taking requests." Rosalind set the camera on the workbench. His hands were steady. Voss noticed that the way you notice a dog not barking. "The journal says there's a chamber under the waterline. Says the walls have names on them. Every name ever counted."

"Every name," Voss repeated.

"Since 1849."

Voss did the arithmetic without wanting to. Marrow Creek's dead, going back a hundred and seventy years. Not just the fed ones—the ledger families, the wellkeepers—but everyone the town had agreed not to look at. The drownings ruled accidental. The disappearances filed as runaways. The infants buried without markers behind the church because the minister in those days charged extra for stones and nobody wanted to ask why.

"That's not a wall," Voss said. "That's a census."

"Yeah," Rosalind said. "I know."

* * *

They drove out to the Lockridge property separately, which was cowardice dressed up as procedure, and both of them knew it. Voss took the county truck so the mileage would look like something. Rosalind followed in his Honda with Wren's booster seat still clipped in the back, and Voss watched it in his mirror the whole way and thought about his own daughter in Flagstaff, eleven hundred miles away, and how far suddenly didn't feel like far enough.

The well sat forty feet behind the house, past the fence line, in a bowl of ground where the grass grew wrong—too green, too uniform, like carpet laid over something. Voss had seen aerial photos of the parcel during a boundary dispute in 2019. From above, the bowl read as a circle so perfect it looked drawn. He'd mentioned it to nobody.

The water was up. That was the first thing. When Rosalind had found the well refilled—chapter of its own, Voss gathered; the man told it like a confession now, everything came out of him like a confession—it had been maybe four feet down. Tonight Voss leaned over the stone lip with a flashlight and the beam hit black water eight feet below the rim, close enough that he could smell it: cold and mineral and underneath that something organic, something like the inside of a mouth.

"It rose a foot since Sunday," Rosalind said. "I've been marking the stones."

"Why are we doing this at night?"

"Because I don't want anyone seeing us do it." A beat. "And because I think it sleeps lighter during the day. Don't ask me how I know that."

Voss didn't ask. There were things you learned on this job that had no report format. He'd stopped trying to write them down years ago.

They rigged the camera to a steel fishing weight with zip ties and duct tape, then to the paracord, then Voss tied marker knots every ten feet because he wanted data, wanted numbers, wanted anything in this situation that behaved like the world was supposed to behave. Rosalind held the light over the rim while Voss paid out line.

"Ten feet," Voss said. The cord went slack-taut against the stone, humming faintly.

"Fifteen."

At twenty feet the knot touched water and Voss felt it through the line—not a splash, exactly. A kind of attention. Like the water had put a hand on the rope.

"Keep going," Rosalind said quietly.

Twenty-five. Thirty. The line kept feeding, which it shouldn't have, because Rosalind had measured the shaft depth from the old survey maps at thirty-one feet to bedrock. At thirty-five the cord went slack in a new way—the weight settling onto a floor—and Voss stood there holding a rope that ran through eight feet of standing water into open air, and felt the last load-bearing piece of his skepticism shear off and drop.

"Camera should be running," Rosalind said. "Give it a minute. Then bring it up slow."

* * *

They watched the footage in Rosalind's kitchen, laptop propped against the fruit bowl, both men standing because sitting felt too casual for what they were about to see. Wren was at a sleepover. Rosalind had lied to her about why. Voss recognized the lie's shape because he'd used it himself, on his own kid, back before she left: *Nothing's wrong, baby, go have fun.* The specific tenderness of a parent protecting a child from a thing the child may already know about.

The first thirty seconds were useless—black water, flashlight scatter, bubbles. Then the camera broke the surface going down and the picture inverted, water climbing the frame like a ceiling closing, and then—

Air. Dry air, below the waterline. The image steadied and showed them a floor of packed dark earth, and walls of native stone rising out of frame, and the flashlight's cone swinging across them as the camera rotated gently on its tether.

"There's a room down there," Voss said. His voice came out flat, procedural, the voice he used at bad scenes. "Through the water. There's a dry room under the water."

"Keep watching."

The camera swung. The wall filled the frame.

Names were carved into it. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They covered the stone in columns, in the tight regular hand of someone—or something—that had been doing this a very long time. Some were shallow, weathered soft, barely legible: names from the founding decades, Voss guessed, the Harmons and the Creels and the Odums, families whose descendants still ran the feed store and sat on the council. Others were cut deep and clean, edges sharp as the day they were made. And the carving wasn't random. It was organized. By generation. By family. The whole wall was a ledger written in stone, and the stone had been keeping books longer than any paper in the historical society's archive.

"Odette's ledger goes back to 1849," Rosalind murmured. "That wall goes back further. Look at the bottom rows—they're older than the town charter."

"How can you tell?"

"The letterforms. My grandfather collected old documents. I grew up reading colonial script." Rosalind's finger hovered near the screen but didn't touch it, the way you don't touch a snake even a dead one. "Some of these aren't English. Some of them might not be any language we'd recognize."

Voss made himself look. He was a lawman; looking was the job. But some part of him, the part that had spent eleven years not asking questions, screamed at him to close the laptop, drive home, pour whatever was in the good bottle, and let the town keep its second set of books until the end of his natural life.

Because here was the thing that broke him, the detail that turned archaeology into horror: the newest carvings weren't old.

The camera drifted along the wall and the names got fresher. The Harlan boy from Route 9, 2016—Voss had worked that case, had closed it as a runaway, had signed the paperwork himself. Cut deep, edges bright. The retiree with the heart attack, dead six weeks. The drowned woman whose sister had called the station sobbing. And near the bottom of the newest column, in stone so freshly cut it still looked wet:

VOSS, MARGARET ELLEN.

His mother. Her full name, spelled out in granite, carved sometime in the last month by no tool any human had carried down a dry chamber beneath eight feet of black water.

Voss stepped back from the laptop like it had bitten him. His hip caught a chair. He didn't sit in it.

"She never—" His voice failed and restarted. "She died in her sleep. St. Mary's hospice. There was nothing—she wasn't *fed* to anything. She was eighty-one years old and her heart gave out."

"Then why is her name on the wall?" Rosalind asked, and it wasn't cruel, it was worse than cruel, it was gentle. "Dale. Why is your mother's name on the wall?"

Voss stared at the frozen frame. His mother's name in stone. Below it, two more lines already begun, half-finished letters like a sentence interrupted mid-word. He leaned closer despite every instinct he owned and read them, and the kitchen went very cold around him.

One was GILLIS, RANDALL—the councilman who'd led the vote to censure the radio station.

The other was LOCKRIDGE, ROSALIND JAMES.

And below that, smaller, shallower, like a pencil draft waiting for ink: LOCKRIDGE, W—

Rosalind saw it too. Voss watched the color leave the man's face in real time, watched him mouth the letter, the dash, the unfinished word that only needed three more letters and a daughter's lifetime to complete.

"It's counting forward," Rosalind said. "It's not a record. It's a schedule."

* * *

They sat at the kitchen table afterward with the laptop closed and the whiskey out, though neither of them drank much. Voss turned his glass in circles. Outside, the wind moved through the fence-line trees, and beneath the wind—if you weren't careful, if you let yourself hear it—came a sound like humming, low and patient, coming up out of the ground.

"My mother called me," Voss said. "Three weeks after the funeral. I never told anyone. I thought I was losing my mind, and honestly, losing my mind was the better option, because the alternative was that this town sits on top of something that keeps its dead on file." He drained the glass. "I chose silence, Rosalind. Eleven years of it. I told myself I was protecting people."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen the wall." Voss set the glass down with a click that sounded final, a gavel, a lock turning. "My mother's name is on it. Your daughter's name is started on it. Randall Gillis is on it and as far as I know the man ate steak last night." He rubbed his eyes. "You said the journal describes a rite. Something the wellkeeper does. Tell me again. All of it. Leave nothing out because it sounds crazy, because we're way past crazy, we're in the part of the map where crazy is the local currency."

Rosalind told him. The rite. The willing entry. The true names spoken aloud. The century of quiet purchased with one keeper. No one comes back.

Voss listened the way he'd listened to confessions his whole career—completely, without blinking, cataloguing everything. When it was done he was quiet for a long moment.

"One keeper closes it for a hundred years," he said finally. "But the wall doesn't show a hundred-year gap. It shows continuous carving. Generation after generation. Which means either the rite's never actually been performed—" 

"—or it's been performed and it never worked," Rosalind finished.

"Or," Voss said, and the thought arrived whole and terrible, the way the worst ones do, "it works exactly the way it's supposed to, and a hundred years is just the length of the payment plan."

The humming outside swelled, very slightly, and settled. Like something below turning over in its sleep. Like something below that had heard them, and was in no hurry at all.

Voss stood, took his keys off the table, and paused at the door.

"We need Odette," he said. "Tonight. Whatever's in that oldest column of hers—the part she hasn't shown you yet—I want it translated before Friday. Because I've read a lot of contracts in my life, Lockridge, and every single one of them has fine print, and the fine print is always where they get you."

Rosalind was already reaching for his phone. "How do you know there's a part she hasn't shown me?"

"Because," Voss said, opening the door onto the dark and the humming, "when I described the wall to myself just now, I realized her face did the same thing mine did when you first asked about the well. Nobody looks that guilty about a stranger's family history."
