## 28. Rehearsal

The gymnasium smells like floor wax and old basketball games, like every school gym in every town that ever had to gather somewhere. Folding chairs in rows. A podium borrowed from the auditorium. Someone's grandmother has set up a card table with coffee urns and a plate of grocery-store cookies, because even at the end of the world, people bring cookies.

Wren sits in the third row with her father on one side and Sheriff Voss on the other, which is a strange feeling, being flanked by the two people in Marrow Creek who know the most and have said the least about it for the longest time.

There are maybe two hundred people here. Wren counts them without meaning to—counting is a thing she does now, a thing she caught from her father like a cold—and she gets to two hundred and eleven before Odette Marsh taps the microphone and the feedback squeal makes everyone shut up.

"Thank you all for coming," Odette says. She looks smaller than she did at the historical society, or maybe just more tired. Her hair is pinned up in a way that suggests she did it at four in the morning. "I know most of you don't want to be here. I know some of you think this is nonsense, and some of you think it's dangerous, and some of you are only here because your neighbors came and you didn't want to be the family that didn't show." A ripple of uncomfortable laughter moves through the rows. "All of those reasons are fine. The well doesn't care why you're here. It cares what you say."

She holds up the oldest ledger, the one with the column nobody could read until last week, when Odette finally cracked it sitting in her car outside the diner at two in the morning, weeping over a translation she'd been circling for thirty years.

"For a century and a half," Odette says, "the well was fed in secret. One keeper at a time, confessing alone into a hole in the ground, thinking that was the price. That's why it never worked. That's why the count kept rolling forward, generation after generation, name after name. Confession without witness isn't payment. It's just talking to yourself." She sets the ledger down on the podium. "Tonight we practice. Tomorrow night we do it for real, at the water, all of us listening to each other. Tonight, if it works, we'll know."

"How will we know?" someone calls from the back. Wren recognizes the voice—Mr. Aldous from the hardware store, who has been openly hostile about the whole thing since the first town meeting.

Odette doesn't smile, but something in her face almost does. "Bring a spoon," she says. "Or a phone. Anything that shows you back. If the lag closes, you'll see it close. You won't need me to tell you."

* * *

They go alphabetically, because Odette insisted that anything else would turn into an argument about who suffered most, and arguments are just another way of staying quiet.

The Tackers go first. There are nine of them left, spread across three generations, and they come up to the podium together like they're posing for a photograph none of them wants to be in. The oldest one, a woman named Dorothea with a walker and a voice like dry leaves, reads from a paper in a shaking hand.

"In 1911, Elias Tacker drowned a man named Corbett Hale in the creek and told the town he'd run off to Ohio. The family knew. We kept his farm. We paid the well in bushels of corn every harvest so it would stay quiet, and we let everyone believe Corbett Hale abandoned his wife." She stops. Her granddaughter puts a hand on her arm. "His wife waited eleven years. She died waiting. We knew where he was the whole time."

Nobody claps. Nobody is supposed to clap. But there's a sound that goes through the room anyway, a low collective exhale, like the whole gym breathing out at once.

The next Tacker reads another entry. Then another. By the fourth one, Dorothea's granddaughter takes over, and by the sixth, a Tacker boy who can't be older than ten is reading crimes committed before his grandparents were born, stumbling over words like *drowned* and *buried* and *lied*, and Wren watches his mother's hands grip the edge of the pew-back in front of her, knuckles white, not stopping him.

* * *

It goes on for two hours.

The Vances admit to a fire in 1923 that killed a family of five, blamed on a lightning strike that never happened. The Merricks admit to starving their hired men through three winters and burying them under the orchard. The Coles admit to a baby, and nobody asks whose, and the silence around that entry is the loudest thing in the building.

Some readers cry. Some read flat and fast, like ripping off a bandage. One old man laughs halfway through his family's section—a horrible, startled laugh—and then apologizes and keeps going, and Wren understands somehow that the laugh is grief wearing its work clothes.

Her father holds her hand through most of it. His palm is damp. Every time a name goes into the air, she feels his fingers tighten slightly, a small involuntary flinch, like each confession costs him something physical. He's doing math behind his eyes; she can tell. He's counting down toward Thursday, toward LOCKRIDGE, toward the line that's been reserved since before either of them existed.

When the Tillers' turn comes, Gus isn't there—he died last week, peacefully, and called the show afterward to clear his son's name—but his daughter-in-law stands up alone and reads the Tiller entries in a voice that cracks exactly once: "In 1974, Daniel Tiller went into the well so the rest of us wouldn't have to. We let him believe it was his idea."

Wren's father closes his eyes at that one. She squeezes his hand hard enough to hurt, and he squeezes back.

* * *

It's nearly eleven when Odette checks her list and says, "That's everyone who confirmed. Does anyone else—" and that's when Mr. Aldous stands up.

"I've sat here all night listening to people dig up dirt that should've stayed buried," he says, loud, from the back. "My family's got nothing in any ledger. We were farmers. We worked. We didn't feed anything down a hole."

The room goes still in a particular way. Wren has learned to recognize that stillness. It's the stillness of people who know something the speaker doesn't.

Odette flips carefully through the oldest ledger, running her finger down a column. "Aldous," she reads. "1904. Two entries. 1931. One entry. 1958—" 

"We were *farmers*."

"You were farmers who paid in teeth," Odette says quietly. "Your grandfather pulled his own molars with pliers in the spring of '58 and put them in a cloth sack, and your father carried that sack to the well, and the water level dropped six inches the next morning, and your family called it a good year." She looks up. "I'm sorry, Mr. Aldous. Everyone in this room is on a page. That's rather the point."

Mr. Aldous sits down slowly, like a man whose legs have been repossessed. And then, to Wren's lasting surprise, he stands back up, walks to the front of the gym past two hundred people watching him, takes the paper Odette offers him, and reads his family's crimes out loud in a voice that shakes so badly the microphone barely catches it. When he finishes, he stays standing there a moment, looking at nothing, and then walks back to his seat, and three different people touch his shoulder as he passes.

* * *

Afterward, Odette asks everyone to take out whatever reflective surface they brought.

Wren pulls her phone from her pocket, screen dark. Her father has a spoon in his jacket pocket—he's carried one for days now, testing it against windows, against the truck's rearview mirror, against the black water in the well itself. Around the gym, phones light up, compact mirrors flip open, somebody holds up a framed photograph under glass.

"Look at yourselves," Odette says. "Then look again in one minute."

Wren raises her phone. In the dark screen she sees a girl with her mother's chin and her father's tired eyes, standing in a gymnasium full of guilty descendants, and the image is hers, exactly hers, moving when she moves, the way it's supposed to, except that nothing in Marrow Creek has moved the way it's supposed to in weeks. She's gotten used to the lag. Used to seeing herself arrive late to her own life, half a second behind, sometimes two, sometimes—last Tuesday, in the bathroom at school—one full second and a half while figures stood in the delay behind her shoulder, patient as furniture.

Around her, people are staring at their spoons and screens with the same wary concentration. Waiting to catch themselves lying.

"One minute," Odette says, and looks at her watch, and the minute passes in a silence so complete Wren can hear the coffee urn gurgling.

"Move," Odette says. "Everyone. Slowly."

Two hundred people raise their arms, tilt their heads, wave their hands at themselves.

And Wren's reflection moves *with* her.

Not a beat late. Not half a second behind, arriving like an echo. With her. Instantly, perfectly, the way reflections have worked everywhere else in the world for all of history. She lifts her phone higher and her reflection lifts it higher in the same instant, and she turns her head and her reflection turns in the same instant, and she is so unprepared for how right it feels that her eyes fill up before she knows she's crying.

Around her, the same discovery is rippling outward. A woman near the front drops her compact mirror and picks it up and stares into it and starts sobbing into her sleeve. Mr. Aldous holds his phone at arm's length, turning his head side to side, side to side, like a man checking whether a broken bone still hurts. Somebody whispers "oh my God" and somebody else whispers "it worked" and somebody else says, louder, "did it *work*?" and Odette raises both hands.

"It held for ninety seconds," she calls out, checking her watch. "Ninety seconds of sync, town-wide, starting at approximately the moment the last name was read. Then—" she pauses, and Wren watches her decide to say the true thing instead of the kind one, "—then it started slipping again. We're at maybe a quarter-second lag now, and growing. What you felt was real. It was also temporary. Because tonight was practice. Tomorrow night, at the water, with every name spoken true and witnessed by everyone owed to—that's the real payment. Tonight just proved the arithmetic works."

The hope in the room doesn't die, exactly. But Wren feels it change shape, tighten, become something with edges. Hope with a deadline.

* * *

They drive home through streets that feel different—not better, exactly, but *watched kindly*, if such a thing exists. Streetlights they pass flicker on ahead of them instead of after. Her father keeps glancing at the rearview mirror, and every time, Wren sees his shoulders drop a little when his reflection keeps pace.

At the house, they stand in the kitchen together, neither one wanting to go to bed, both knowing tomorrow is Thursday Eve, the last ordinary day either of them might get.

"Did you feel it?" her father asks. "When the names were being read? Not the sadness. The other thing."

Wren thinks about it. "Like the house settling," she says. "But the house was the whole town."

He nods slowly. "That's what your great-great-grandfather never understood. He thought the well wanted feeding. It wanted *listening*." He rubs his eyes with the heel of his hand. "One more night of sleep, kid. Then we do it for real."

They count the doors together before bed—all eleven, twice, out of habit—and this time, when Wren says "eleven" the second time, her voice doesn't shake.

She's almost asleep when she hears it.

The humming.

It used to come from the well, faint, behind the fence line, a sound she'd convinced herself was wind in the pump housing. But this humming is coming from inside the house. From the hallway mirror, she realizes, heart slamming awake—the hallway mirror that has lagged for weeks, that showed her figures standing in the delay, that her father covered with a bedsheet two days ago.

The sheet is on the floor.

And the humming is rising, getting louder, getting *closer*, and underneath it now she can hear something new, something that makes her blood go cold and her feet hit the floor:

Underneath the hum, faint but unmistakable, the sound of water rising.

Fast.
