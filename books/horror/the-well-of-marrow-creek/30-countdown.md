## 30. Countdown

Odette Marsh had spent forty years among dead paper, and she knew the smell of a thing that was almost finished. Books had it. Ledgers had it. The well had it now, and she could smell it from the fence line before she saw the water.

She had come at dawn with her calipers, her notebooks, and a thermos of coffee she would never drink, and she had stood at the lip of the old Lockridge well and watched it rise.

Not trickle. Not seep. *Rise.*

A foot an hour, steady as a metronome. At six a.m. the black water had been eleven feet down. By seven it was ten. By eight, nine. She had measured it with a weighted string and a carpenter's pencil, marking each hour on the stone lip in her small archival hand, and the marks marched up the rim like a tide chart for a sea that had no business existing in the middle of Marrow Creek.

She had spent her whole life believing that archives were the opposite of the present tense. That paper was a way of stopping time, pinning it flat, keeping it. She had built her career on that belief. She had built her whole quiet, careful, unlived life on it.

The well was unpinning everything.

At nine she drove to the station.

* * *

Rosalind Lockridge looked like a man who had stopped sleeping on purpose. He was in the break room, not the studio, and when she walked in he was standing at the counter with a mug of coffee he hadn't touched, staring at the microwave's reflective door. Odette had seen that stare before, in the archive, on the faces of people who found their own family names in the wrong column. The stare of a man reading a sentence he'd been in his whole life without knowing it.

"Odette." He turned. The microwave door went dark behind him. "Tell me you found something."

"I found the arithmetic." She set her notebook on the counter between them, opened it to the page she'd ruled at four that morning, unable to stay in bed. "You want the bad news first or the worse news?"

"Odette."

"Fine. The water rises a foot an hour. I measured from six to nine. Consistent to within an inch. The well's original depth, per the 1897 survey—which I have, because of course I have—is forty-two feet to the chamber floor. The waterline was at thirty-one feet when I started this morning." She turned the notebook so he could read her own handwriting, because she needed him to see it, needed it to exist outside her head. "At a foot an hour, it reaches the rim Thursday at approximately eleven p.m. Full refill. Overflow by Friday midnight."

"Overflow into what? It's a hole in the ground."

"It's a hole in the ground that was dry for sixty years, Rosalind. Dry wells don't refill themselves. Dry wells don't refill *on schedule*." She heard her voice go thin and pulled it back. She was the archivist. She dealt in what was recorded, not what was felt. "The 1897 ledger has a marginal note in Elias Lockridge's hand. Your grandfather's grandfather. I translated it Tuesday night. Would you like to hear it?"

Rosalind's jaw worked. "Say it."

"*'When the water comes back, the count comes due. The wellkeeper speaks at the water's edge, in the dark, and every name is said, and every debt is named, and the town that fed it stands witness. Do it alone and it takes you anyway. Do it witnessed and the count closes. Do nothing and it closes itself, and takes the town for interest.'*"

The break room's refrigerator hummed. Somewhere down the hall a phone rang twice and stopped.

"That's why the town has to be there," Rosalind said slowly. "Not for theater. It's structural."

"It's contractual. This thing has rules the way a bank has rules, and the rules are the only mercy in it. It *wants* to be witnessed. It's been waiting a century for an audience." Odette closed the notebook. "Which brings me to the worse news."

He laughed once, without humor. "There's worse."

"The rite has to happen *before* the water reaches the rim. Not at full refill—at the water's edge. The marginal note says *'at the water's edge, in the dark.'* Once it overflows, the well isn't at the water's edge anymore. The water's edge is everywhere. We lose the venue."

"Thursday night," Rosalind said. "Before eleven."

"Thursday night. Which gives you—" she checked her watch, an old habit, though she no longer trusted any clock in this town and trusted her own pulse least of all— "a little over two days to get half of Marrow Creek to stand in your backyard and confess to feeding a well."

Rosalind set down his untouched coffee. "Voss broadcast his confession Monday. Half the town already knows the shape of it. The Hardings came by with their grandmother's letters. The Pell family dug up a tin box from their root cellar with teeth in it, Odette. *Teeth.* They brought it to my door like a casserole."

"People want to be done with it," Odette said. "That's the danger. They'll come Thursday night hoping to watch you go into the water and consider the account settled. You need them to understand that watching is not passive. Witness is a verb. If they come as spectators, the rite fails, and the well takes its interest out of all of us."

"How do you make people understand that in two days?"

"You do what you've been doing since the drowned woman first called you." She looked at him, this man she'd known for twenty years as a voice on the radio and a name on a census page, and felt something she almost never let herself feel about a living person: respect. "You say it out loud. On air. Tonight. Every hour. The time, the place, and what witness means. You've turned your show into a court, Rosalind. Now turn it into a summons."

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the archive, and the archive spent the afternoon arguing with her.

That was how it felt, anyway. She'd been pulling records all week—deeds, censuses, church registers, the water company's abandoned survey files—and the pattern kept reassembling itself faster than she could document it. Every time she thought she'd found the well's origin, an older document surfaced with an older name for it. The 1897 ledger called it the well. A land petition from 1861 called it *the spring that counts*. A fragment of a sermon, hand-copied, undated, called it *the Listener under Marrow Creek* and warned that it had been old when the town was new.

The town hadn't built the well. The town had built itself *around* it.

She sat in the reading room with the ledgers fanned out in front of her—the Lockridge ledger, the Tiller ledger, the Harding ledger that had appeared on her desk Monday morning with no note, the Pell tin photographed and catalogued—and she did the thing she was actually for. She made an index.

Not of names. She was done with names; the names were the well's currency and she refused to handle them anymore than the rite required. She indexed *payments*. Bushels of wheat, 1897 through 1904. A milk cow, 1911. Teeth—she made herself write it—teeth, various donors, 1930s, depression years, when bushels got thin. A wedding ring, 1953. A boy, 1974. Tiller. Gus Tiller's son, entered in the Tiller ledger in a shaking hand, and next to it, in different ink, added later: *count paused*.

Paused. Not closed. Paused, like a broadcast, like a held breath, like a clock with its second hand pinned. And now the hand was moving again, and it was moving fast, and Odette had spent the morning measuring exactly how fast.

At four o'clock she heard the front door of the archive open and close, and heard footsteps she knew, and looked up to find Sheriff Voss standing in the reading room doorway with his hat in his hands, which he only did when he was about to say something he'd rather not say.

"Odette."

"Sheriff."

"I need to see the ledger math myself. Rosalind called me. Thursday, eleven p.m., the rim." He came in and sat down across from her without being asked, which was also new. Two weeks ago Dale Voss had sat in her reading room and told her, in his sheriff's voice, that the dead-caller stories were a nuisance and a public order problem. Now he sat down like a man at a bedside. "I want to know how sure you are."

She turned the notebook around. She walked him through it the way she'd walked Rosalind through it, the measurements, the marginal note, the venue clause. Voss read the way she'd seen engineers read—looking for the error, hoping for it.

"And if we're wrong about the timing," he said. "If it's slower."

"Then we get a longer fuse on the same bomb. Sheriff, I've been wrong about things in my life, but I have never once been wrong about an arithmetic I did twice." She tapped the column of waterline measurements. "It rose eleven inches between six and seven, twelve between seven and eight, twelve between eight and nine. It's not just rising. It's *accelerating*. By Thursday afternoon it may be eighteen inches an hour. My eleven p.m. estimate is conservative."

Voss was quiet a moment. "My mother called me last month," he said, to the ledger rather than to her. "You know that."

"I know that."

"She told me where she hid the insurance papers. Told me to forgive my brother. Told me—" his voice caught and he mastered it, a man closing a drawer— "told me she was proud of me, which she never said once while she was alive. I've spent a month deciding that call was a hoax. A cruel one. Grief does strange things." He looked up. "It wasn't a hoax, was it."

"No," Odette said gently. "It wasn't a hoax. It was a down payment. Everything that thing does is a down payment. The calls, the messages, the mercy—it buys attention. And attention is what it eats."

"Then why help us close it? If witness feeds it—"

"Because there's a difference between being *seen* and being *witnessed*." She'd been turning this over for two days, since she'd cracked the oldest column, and she said it now carefully, because it was the whole hinge. "The well feeds on acknowledgment the way a fire feeds on air. But a fire in a stove heats a house, and a fire in a house burns it down. The rite is the stove. Every name spoken alone, in secret, in fear—that's the well eating. Every name spoken *to the people owed*, in the open, together—that's the well being spent. Witnessed truth closes accounts. Secret truth opens them. That's the whole grammar of it, Sheriff. That's what a hundred years of ledgers say."

"And Thursday, if it works—"

"Then Friday the well is a hole in the ground again. Dry. Quiet. For a century, if Elias Lockridge's arithmetic holds."

"And if it doesn't work?"

Odette looked at the window, where the afternoon was going the color of old paper, and did not lie to him. "Then Friday, Marrow Creek learns what interest means."

* * *

She went back to the well at dusk, because she was the only one who thought to bring a camera, and because somebody had to document it. That was her whole life's answer to horror: write it down, and it cannot pretend it didn't happen.

The water was at nineteen feet. She could see it from the fence line now, a black coin of surface catching the last grey light, and she stood at the stone lip and dropped her weighted string and marked the hour. While she waited for the next hour to prove her, she did something she had not planned to do. She leaned over the rim and spoke to it.

"I know what you are," she said. Her voice sounded small and correct, an archivist's voice, and she hated how small it sounded. "I've read your whole file. Every entry. I know you were old before the town, and I know you count, and I know you're rising on schedule because schedule is all you understand." The water did not move. The water did not have to move. She felt it listening the way you feel a room listening when someone has just said your name in the next one. "But here's what's in *my* file, and you should know it before Thursday. I have spent forty years keeping the record of this town, and I have never once looked away from the worst page. Not one. So whatever happens at your water's edge, understand that you will be witnessed by a woman who has already seen everything you have, in ink, and survived it. I am not afraid of the record. I *am* the record."

The water rose. She marked it. Twelve inches, on the hour, to the half-inch.

On her way back to her car she passed Wren Lockridge standing at the back fence in the dark, just standing there, a twelve-year-old girl looking at a well the way other children looked at the ocean. Odette stopped. For a moment neither of them said anything, two keepers of records, one on paper and one not yet.

"Miss Marsh," Wren said. "Is it true it'll be full by Friday?"

"It'll reach the rim Thursday night. That's why we do it Thursday."

"Then we'd better not be late." Wren said it matter-of-factly, the way she'd say *then we'd better do our homework*, and turned and went inside, and Odette stood in the dark feeling the ground tilt under a hundred years of her assumptions. The child wasn't afraid the way the adults were afraid. The child was afraid the way people are afraid when they've already decided.

* * *

At ten that night, alone in the archive with the lamps down, Odette did the final calculation and wrote it into the record in her best hand, the hand she used for permanent things.

*Waterline, 10 p.m.: fourteen feet. Rate: twelve to fourteen inches per hour, accelerating. Projected rim contact: Thursday, between 10:40 and 11:15 p.m. Rite must commence no later than 10 p.m., at the water's edge, in darkness, with all names spoken and all debts named, before the assembled witness of the town. There is no second date. There is no extension. The well has never once in a hundred and twenty-seven years of recorded history missed a deadline, and it will not miss this one.*

She signed it. She dated it. Then she picked up the phone and called the station, and when Rosalind's voice came through—the overnight voice, the steady voice, though she could hear the seam in it now, the place where the composure had been sewn over the dread—she told him the number.

"Ten p.m. Thursday," she said. "Everything in place by ten. I'll bring the ledgers. All of them. Voss is arranging the crowd. You—" she paused, because the next part was the part she'd been avoiding, the part where the archivist asked a man to do the one thing no archive could hold. "You need to be at the water's edge, Rosalind. Not near it. At it. The note is specific. And I need you to understand what I measured tonight, standing at that rim."

"Tell me."

"The water didn't just rise. It rose *to meet the schedule*. When I spoke to it, it didn't react. But when I finished, when I turned to mark the hour—" she looked at her notebook, at the numbers, wanting them to be wrong one more time— "the surface came up twelve inches in four minutes. Then it settled back to the hour rate. Like a held breath. Like something that heard its name in the file."

The line was quiet. Down the hall of the station, faintly, Odette could hear the ON AIR light buzz.

"Thursday at ten," Rosalind said at last. "Every name. Every debt. The whole town watching."

"The whole town *witnessing*," Odette corrected. "There's a difference, and Thursday night, the difference is the only thing standing between Marrow Creek and the water."

She hung up and sat in the lamplight with her ledgers, and outside the archive window, far across the dark fields, she could just see the faint shine of the well, filling, filling, patient as paper, keeping its count.
