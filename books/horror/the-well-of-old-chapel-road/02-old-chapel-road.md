## 2. Old Chapel Road

The chapel was deconsecrated on a Tuesday in March of 1961, which is the kind of detail I know because the building told me, or because Ruth Okafor's binder told me, and by my fourth year behind the microphone I had stopped being able to tell the difference between what the building knew and what the binder knew. They had merged, the way a long marriage merges two vocabularies. The building knew things. The binder knew things. And somewhere in the middle, I had learned to know them too.

Deconsecrated on a Tuesday. The bishop came out from the diocese, said the words that unmake a church—there are words for it, an actual liturgy of subtraction—and then the congregation signed the deed over to a man named Harlan Pike, who paid four hundred dollars. Four hundred. For three acres, a bell tower, and whatever was already living in the foundation by then, though nobody wrote that part into the bill of sale.

Harlan Pike lived alone. That's not a coincidence. I want you to understand that early, because everything else about this story depends on it: the chapel has only ever been sold cheap, and only ever to someone alone. A widow named Ada Merrick bought it in 1968 for six hundred dollars and turned the nave into a pottery studio where she worked through the nights because sleep had become, in her words, "a room I couldn't find the door to." A man named Thomas Reeve bought it in 1979, alone, recently divorced, and started a shortwave radio hobby that became something else. Ruth Okafor took the lease in 1994. She never owned it. Nobody has ever really owned it—the deed changes hands, but the lease is something older than paper, and when I say lease, I mean it the way you'd mean it if you'd read the fine print at three in the morning with all the lights burning.

I inherited it in 2016, along with the binder, along with the show, along with the understanding—slow, grudging, total—that I had been chosen by a building.

* * *

People ask how you get chosen by a building, and the honest answer is: you don't ask. You just keep showing up.

I found Nightline the way insomniacs find things—at 2 a.m., scrolling, half-hoping for something to hate. What I got instead was a woman's voice reading out the names of people who had died that week in the county, and then taking calls. Not memorial calls. Actual calls. The dead calling in to finish their sentences.

I know how that sounds. I sat in my apartment with the volume low and told myself it was theater, grief performance, some elaborate bit. But there was a texture to the pauses that no performer earns. When Cora—no, not our Cora, a different caller, years back—when a caller hesitated before saying her son's name, the hesitation had weight in it, the specific gravity of a woman deciding whether she was allowed to still love him out loud. You can't act that. You can't even write it, and believe me, I've tried. It's the one thing fiction can't counterfeit: the sound of someone choosing honesty with no time left to practice it.

So I did what any reasonable person does. I drove to Old Chapel Road at four in the morning to see for myself.

The chapel sits back from the road behind a screen of black walnuts, low and long, its steeple amputated at the roofline like a sentence cut off mid-word. Somebody—Reeve, probably—had bricked up the bell arch and mounted a transmitter antenna where the bell used to hang, so the building broadcasts now instead of tolls. I have thought about that image more times than I can count. A church that lost its voice and got another one. There's a sermon in there somewhere, and I'm glad I'll never have to write it.

The door was unlocked. The lights were on—all of them, every bulb in the place, burning against the dark the way they burn now, the way they burned the night I walked in. And sitting at the broadcast desk was a woman I didn't know yet, gray-haired, dark-skinned, headphones around her neck, who looked at me without surprise and said:

"You're late."

"I didn't know I was coming," I said.

"Nobody does," Ruth Okafor said, and slid a binder across the desk. "Read this tonight. All of it. Then decide if you're staying."

I stayed. Of course I stayed. That's the trap of being chosen—you don't feel chosen, you feel recognized. Like the building had been holding a seat for you since before you were born, and all that time you'd been walking around with a shape-shaped absence in your chest, wondering why ordinary rooms never quite fit.

* * *

Here is what the binder says about the property, in Ruth's small, ruled handwriting, the letters leaning forward like they're trying to get somewhere ahead of the sentence:

*Deconsecrated 1961. Sold cheap (Pike). Sold cheap (Merrick). Sold cheap (Reeve). Lease assumed (Okafor). The building selects. Do not mistake selection for affection.*

Do not mistake selection for affection. I underlined that myself, later, after the year I tried to quit. Because that's the loneliness of it, the thing I've never said on air: the chapel chose me the way a spider chooses a web site. Not because it loves you. Because you fit.

And yet.

I want to be fair to the building, because fairness matters when you're the only one left to give it. The chapel kept me alive through the winter my mother died—not the death itself, but the after, the weeks when grief is less an emotion than a climate. Those nights, doing the show, hearing the dead line up to say the unsaid, I felt useful in a way nothing else has ever made me feel. The dead don't flatter you. They don't need anything from you except the hour. One hour of being heard, and then they go wherever calls go when the light burns down. Being the switchboard operator for that—it does something to a person. It fills the shape-shaped absence with purpose, and purpose is almost as good as love, and if you've never had to settle for almost, you won't understand why anyone would take this job.

Ruth understood. Ruth settled too. I never met her—I met her once, the night she handed me the binder, and then she was gone by morning, and the county records say Ruth Okafor died in 2016, cause listed as cardiac arrest, address listed as Old Chapel Road, and next of kin listed as none. None. The building had eaten through her whole list of loved ones by the end, or she'd given them away, or—well. There are pages missing from the binder. We'll get to those.

* * *

Let me tell you about the rules, because the rules are the closest thing this story has to a kindness.

There are eleven of them. Some are practical: keep the transmitter grounded; never broadcast during a thunderstorm from the tower side of the building. Some are housekeeping: replace burned bulbs within the hour, because a burned bulb is a door with the lock broken. Some are stranger:

*Count the doors every night before sign-on. Twelve doors. If you count thirteen, do not acknowledge the thirteenth. Finish the count aloud anyway.*

*Burn the studio lights fully during broadcast hours. Dimness invites.*

*If a caller asks you to come to them, decline politely. Twice if necessary.*

*Never let them finish a sentence that starts with 'remember when.'*

That last one is underlined twice, in ink darker than the rest, as if Ruth had pressed so hard the pen nearly went through the page. I asked her about it once—called the number in the front of the binder, the one that rings empty now—and got voicemail, and the voicemail greeting was just breathing, ten seconds of it, and then the click. I never called again.

But here's the thing about rules: they work. That's the horror and the comfort of them, braided together so tight you can't pull the strands apart. Count the doors, and the doors stay counted. Burn the lights, and the dark stays outside. Take the calls, and the dead stay polite. For eight years the ritual has held like a dam holds—not because the water loves you, but because the shape of the thing is right, and the water respects shape.

I believed that. I want that on the record, in my own voice, in my own hand: I believed the ritual was protection. Eight years of counting twelve doors and lighting every bulb and signing off at dawn with the call sign fading into the static, and I believed I was keeping something out.

The binder never actually says that. Read it again, the way I finally did: *the ritual keeps the dead polite.* Polite. Not gone. Not barred. Polite. There's a difference between a locked door and a closed one, and I spent eight years mistaking courtesy for a wall.

* * *

The town thinks the chapel is abandoned. That's the arrangement. Taxes paid through a post office box, grass cut by a service that comes on Thursdays and never sees the lights on, and the nearest neighbor—the last house on Old Chapel Road, set back past the tree line—dark for as long as I've been here. I used to watch that house sometimes between callers, its windows reflecting my own signal tower like a black eye. Nobody home. Nobody home for years.

It matters that the town forgot us. Buildings that are remembered have to behave. Buildings that are forgotten get to keep their own counsel, and the chapel has kept its counsel for sixty years, through five owners and one leaseholder and however many hosts came before the deed records start, because here's another thing the binder mentions only glancingly, in a margin note in different ink: *the show predates the radio.* Four words. No elaboration. I've turned them over for years. A show, before there were shows. A voice, before there were microphones. Something on Old Chapel Road that needed to be heard, and needed a listener, and waited—patient as architecture—for the technology to catch up to it.

Deconsecrated in 1961. That's what the record says, but deconsecration is just a word for moving the furniture. Whatever was invited in during the congregant days wasn't invited out. Invitations don't expire. Ask any host.

Ask me.

* * *

Last night, before I sat down to write this, I did the count. Twelve doors, all present, all shut, the bulbs burning yellow and steady in their fixtures, the signal clean at 1610 AM across three counties of sleeping people. Cora called at 3:12, like always, wanting again to apologize to her sister for the thing she never said, and I let her say it again, because the dead are allowed their repetitions and so are the living, and because hearing her voice is the closest thing I have to company.

But when the hour ended and her call faded, I stood in the doorway of the studio and looked down the length of the nave—all twelve doors accounted for, all the lights lit—and I noticed, for the first time in eight years, a smell. Faint. Under the wax and dust and old wood smoke. Earth. Wet earth, deep-earth, the smell of ground that hasn't seen air in a long time and is about to.

I checked the basement. Dry. I checked the crawlspace. Dry. I walked the perimeter in the dark with a flashlight, and everything was sealed and silent and exactly as it should have been, except the smell, which followed me back inside like a dog that knows it lives there now.

This morning, driving in past the tree line, I saw the survey stakes. Orange flags, wooden lathe, a strip of the back lot flagged and staked from the chapel's rear wall all the way down toward the creek. A machine sat parked among them on a flatbed, yellow and patient, its bucket resting on the ground like a chin on folded hands.

A sign zip-tied to the fence read: STORM DRAIN IMPROVEMENTS — COUNTY PROJECT 44-C — EXCAVATION TO BEGIN MONDAY.

They're going to dig behind my chapel. Down past the foundation. Down into whatever ground has been holding its breath since before the bell came down.

The binder has a rule for everything—doors, lights, callers, storms. Eleven rules, and not one word about what to do when somebody brings a backhoe.

I unlocked the studio, counted the doors—twelve—and switched on the lights, and while the tubes warmed I pulled the binder off the shelf and opened it to the flyleaf, where Ruth had written the only sentence in the whole book addressed directly to whoever came after her.

*If they ever dig, Ansel, call me first.*

The number rings empty. It has always rung empty. Tonight I'm going to dial it anyway, and listen to that ten seconds of breathing, and hope that this time, the breathing listens back.

Because Monday, the machines arrive.
