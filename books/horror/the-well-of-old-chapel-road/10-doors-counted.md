## 10. Doors Counted

The first bulb died at 11:52 p.m., and Ansel knew it the way you know a tooth has cracked—not by seeing it, but by feeling the absence where something solid used to be.

She was mid-broadcast, taking a call from a man named Hollis who had died on a Tuesday and spent his hour trying to remember whether he'd ever told his daughter he was proud of her. Ansel had her ritual down to muscle memory: console lights burning, twelve doors counted at the top of every hour, the small brass lamp on the board kept lit no matter what. The chapel had been a studio for sixty years, and in all that time the building had held its shape around her like cupped hands.

Then the sconce above the mixing desk popped—a sound like a knuckle cracking—and went dark.

"Keep talking, Hollis," she said, and reached for the binder.

The rule was simple: *count the doors while the lights hold.* Twelve doors in a building with twelve doors. She'd counted them ten thousand times. Front door, vestry door, the two doors to the old Sunday school rooms, the door to the stairwell, the door to the boiler room, the four doors along the back hall that led nowhere anymore but still needed counting, the door of the confessional they'd left standing because removing it cost more than ignoring it, and the little door in the floor of the storage closet that covered the crawlspace under the sanctuary—twelve, always twelve, and as long as she counted them aloud each hour, the dead stayed polite and the living stayed asleep and nothing in the walls learned her name.

Except one of them already had.

"—and I just want her to know," Hollis was saying, "that I looked it up. After. You can look things up after, did you know that? I watched her graduate through somebody's phone."

"I know," Ansel said. "Tell me what you saw."

Above her, in the rafters where the old sanctuary lamps hung dead and decorative, another bulb burned out—the work light she'd rigged for the stairwell. The dark came down one flight and settled against the studio ceiling like a hand testing a lid.

* * *

At 12:40 the third light failed: the hallway sconce outside the booth. Ansel heard it go—glass ticking as it cooled—and felt the building's geometry shift, though nothing had moved. That was the trick of it, the thing the binder never quite said outright: the lights weren't illumination. They were *doors*. Each bulb marked a threshold the way a stitch marks a seam, and when a light died, the seam opened, and whatever worked its way up from below could pass through the opening without ever touching wood or iron.

The door beneath the chapel had hinges on the inside. Denny's crew had found it three weeks ago, oak and iron, sealed from within by hands that must have belonged to someone who then stayed. Since then the show's signal had been degrading nightly—calls arriving warped, Cora's voice arriving underwater—and Ansel had doubled the ritual, counting doors every half hour, burning every spare bulb she owned.

It wasn't enough. It had never been enough. The ritual wasn't protection; it was containment, and containment only works on things that respect containers.

She counted anyway. Front door. Vestry. Two Sunday school doors. Stairwell. Boiler room. Four back-hall doors. Confessional. Crawlspace hatch. Twelve.

Her voice sounded fine on air. She made sure of that. Hollis didn't need to hear her counting.

* * *

At 1:15 the board flickered and the ON AIR sign guttered like a candle in a draft. Ansel switched to the backup transmitter, an analog beast older than she was, and the show held—but the fourth light went with it, the one over the water heater closet, and she heard something in the wall behind her take a slow breath.

Not wind. Buildings don't breathe. This one had started to.

She put Hollis gently into his last five minutes—he'd found the memory he wanted, the pride, delivered sideways through a graduation video—and signed him off with the words she gave them all: *"You were heard. Go easy."*

Then the line blinked. Incoming call, no caller ID, which meant nothing on this show except everything.

"Cora," Ansel said, before the woman could speak.

Static, and then Cora's voice, thin and wrong, like a radio playing two stations at once. "Ansel. Listen to me. Don't count out loud after two."

"What?"

"It hears the numbers. It's using your count to find the doors. Every number you say, it checks." A pause, and under the pause, something else breathing on the line. "I'm not supposed to tell you this. There are rules for us too, and I'm breaking all of them right now, and I need you to understand that I know what it costs."

Ansel's mouth went dry. "Cora. What does it cost?"

"Everything I have left. Which isn't much. Which is why I'm spending it now, tonight, while there's still a me to spend." The static swelled. Somewhere beneath it, faintly, a second voice was saying Ansel's name in her mother's cadence, patient as tide. "Stop counting. Burn the lights and don't count. Counting is how it maps you. Counting is how it mapped *her*."

"Mapped who?"

But the line dissolved into a sound like a well exhaling, and then dial tone, pure and impossible, and Ansel sat alone in a building that was going dark one threshold at a time.

* * *

She stopped counting.

That was harder than it sounds. The ritual lived in her hands and throat; six decades of hosts had counted their way through ten thousand nights, and the silence where the numbers should be felt like holding her breath underwater. But she trusted Cora. Cora, who had called every week since her death to apologize for a sentence she'd never finished in life, who had never once asked for anything, who was breaking the rules of the dead itself to warn her.

So instead she walked the building with a flashlight and a box of bulbs, replacing what she could, and where she couldn't—two fixtures had simply refused new light, drinking current and giving back darkness—she stood in front of those doors and said nothing at all, and felt them listening.

By 3 a.m. seven lights remained. By 4, five. The building above her was closing like a fist, and somewhere below, behind a door with hinges on the inside, something was climbing through the architecture the way water climbs through brick—patiently, finding every gap the dying lights left open.

At 4:41 the line blinked again.

She almost didn't answer. Then she did, because that was the job, because the show was the pump and the pump didn't stop until sunrise, and because some stubborn, stupid part of her believed the dead deserved their hour even on the night the building turned on her.

Static. Breathing. And then her mother's voice, warm and certain and forty years dead:

*"Remember when—"*

Ansel ripped the headset off and threw it across the booth.

The binder lay open on the desk to the rule she'd underlined twice herself, months ago, without knowing why: *never let them finish a sentence that starts with 'remember when.'* Below it, in her predecessor Ruth Okafor's cramped hand, a line she'd read a hundred times and never understood until this moment:

*The lights aren't for the dead. They're for you. As long as you can see the doors, you can choose which ones stay shut.*

Five lights left. Four doors she couldn't see. And the sun was still an hour and nineteen minutes away.

Ansel picked up the headset, put it back on, and opened the microphone.

"To anyone still listening," she said, to the dead and to the thing wearing her mother's voice and to whatever climbed toward her through the dark, "the lines are open. And I am not signing anything."

From the stairwell, unhurried, came the sound of a thirteenth door beginning to open.
