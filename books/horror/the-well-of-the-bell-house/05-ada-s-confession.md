## 5. Ada's Confession

The change came on a Tuesday, in the hour when the light through the lace curtains turned the colour of weak tea.

Dominic had learned the rhythms of the house by then—the way the floorboards announced themselves differently at each end of the day, the particular creak of the third stair that meant nothing and the deeper groan of the joists over the cellar that meant the same nothing. He had learned Ada's rhythms too: the shallow tide of her breathing, the way her fingers moved against the counterpane like a pianist rehearsing a piece she could no longer name. And always, underneath everything, the counting.

*Nineteen. Eighteen. Seventeen.*

She did it in her sleep mostly, but sometimes awake, her eyes fixed on some middle distance where he could not follow. The numbers came slow and even as a metronome, and Dominic had stopped trying to interrupt them after the second week, because interrupting only made her distressed, and because—though he would never have written this in his notes—interrupting felt *rude*, the way it would be rude to talk over someone praying.

On that Tuesday afternoon he was changing the water in her carafe when she said, clearly, in a voice he had not heard from her before:

"You'll want to know why."

He set the carafe down too quickly and water slopped over his knuckles, cold as well-water in November. Ada Voss was propped against her pillows, and her eyes were open, and they were *looking at him*—not through him, not past him at the wall or the window or whatever country the dying visit while their bodies keep house. Looking at him the way a person looks at another person.

"Ada," he said carefully. "How are you feeling?"

"Lucid." She smiled, and it was a young woman's smile briefly visiting an old face, like light through a crack in a barn door. "It comes and goes now. Like weather. Today the weather is fine." She patted the edge of the bed. "Sit down, nurse. You've been standing at that door for three weeks like a man waiting for a bus."

He sat. Her hand, when it closed over his, was light as paper but warm—warmer than he expected, warmer than the room.

"You'll want to know why we count," she said.

*We.* Not *I*. Not *the patients*. We.

"I assumed it was neurological," Dominic said. "Terminal lucidity presents in strange ways. Repetitive vocalisation is documented. There's a whole literature on—"

"Dominic." His name in her mouth stopped him like a hand on his chest. "You're a good man and you read too much. I don't mean why do our mouths do it. I mean why does the *bell* ask us to."

Outside, October was pressing its face against the glass. The garden had gone to rust and bone; the church tower stood above the hedgerow, empty-headed, its louvred openings dark as eye sockets. Dominic had walked past it every morning for nineteen days and every morning it had seemed to lean a little further into his path.

"The bell doesn't exist," he said. "It was melted down in 1911. There's no bell, Ada. Whatever the counting is, it isn't a summons."

"Mm." She looked at their hands together on the coverlet. "That's what my husband said, God rest him, right up until the night it rang for him. He'd been a schoolmaster. Very fond of facts." Her thumb moved once across Dominic's knuckles. "Facts are lovely things. They're just not the whole of the animal."

* * *

She told it slowly, the way old people tell things—not for effect but because the telling itself is a kind of walking, and they have learned not to hurry across certain ground.

"There were seven of them," she said. "Founders, we call them, though they weren't founders of anything so grand as a town. They were the ones who stayed when everyone else with sense went down to the coast. Seven families, seven names, and one of them—" she paused, and something flickered behind her eyes like a bird crossing a window, there and gone, "—one of them didn't finish what he started. But that's not my story today."

She shifted against her pillows, and Dominic found himself adjusting them without thinking, the nurse's reflexes running beneath the listener's stillness.

"They built the church in 1843, and they cast the bell themselves, here in the village, in a pit behind the smithy. You can still find bits of slag in the brook if you know where to look. And when it was done—when it hung in the tower and they pulled the rope for the first time—they made it ring not for a service but for a *binding*. That's the word they used. Binding."

"Binding what?" Dominic asked, though part of him already knew, the way you know the shape of a thing in a dark room before your fingers confirm it.

"The dead," Ada said simply. "To the soil. To Bell House itself." She met his eyes. "You look at me like I'm senile, love. Indulge me. I'm having one of my fine days and I intend to spend it honestly."

"I'm listening," he said. And he was. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would turn over later at 3 a.m. staring at the ceiling: how completely, how hungrily he listened, this man who had built a life out of not believing in anything that couldn't be weighed.

"It was a mercy," Ada said. "You have to understand that first, or you'll understand nothing. Do you know what it was like, dying out here, two hundred years ago? A bad winter, a bad fever, and no doctor within a day's ride. People died alone in rooms like this one while the wind took the roof-tiles off. Husbands died while wives were fetching water. Children died in the night and weren't found till morning. And the dead—" her voice dropped, not dramatically, but the way a voice drops when it's carrying something heavy downhill, "—the dead went somewhere none of us can follow, and those left behind sat by the bed holding a hand that had already let go, and they were *alone*. Both of them alone. The dying and the living, each in their own country, with no road between."

She was quiet a moment. In the silence Dominic became aware of the house around them—the tick of the cooling stove, a rook somewhere on the chimney, the vast patient quiet of the empty tower beyond the hedge.

"So the seven of them rang the bell," she said, "and bound the village's dead to its soil. Not their souls—souls go where souls go, that's between them and God. But the *leaving*. The moment of leaving. When you die inside the parish bounds, the bell rings for you, and you don't go alone. Someone walks you to the gate. Something holds your hand on the far side of it. No one in Bell House has ever had to die alone, Dominic. Not in a hundred and eighty years. Not one."

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "Ada, that's a story. A comfort-story. Villages make them up all the time; it's how communities process mortality. The bell rings because of thermal contraction in the stonework, or memory, or—"

"Because of what?"

"Collective suggestion. Grief makes people hear—"

"Grief makes people hear true things," Ada said, "if they'll only stop explaining long enough to listen." She said it without sharpness. That was somehow worse. "You've heard it yourself. Don't lie to an old woman on her fine day."

And he couldn't. That was the bottom of it. He had heard it—three nights ago, at zero, standing in the churchyard with frost coming up through his shoes, he had heard a bell toll from a tower that held nothing but scorch marks and a rope worn smooth as river stone. He had stood there with his rationalism in his hands like a broken tool and had not been able to put it down and had not been able to pick it up either.

"Yes," he said. "I've heard it."

Something in her face eased, and he realised she had been waiting for that admission the way you wait for a fever to break.

"Then you know it's real, and knowing that, you need to know what it *is*. Because it's not a haunting, love. Hauntings are accidents. This is a promise. The oldest promise in the village, older than any of the families in it now, and every soul born under this parish line is born inside the promise whether they know it or not." Her grip tightened on his hand, paper-light and immovable. "You included."

"I'm not from here," Dominic said. "I'm agency staff. I'm from—"

"You're here now." She smiled again, that brief young smile. "The bell doesn't much care where you were *from*."

* * *

The light was going. Tea-colour deepening to amber, amber to ash. Somewhere below, the stove ticked and settled. Dominic should have been doing his rounds—checking the morphine stock, logging her obs, the small liturgy of care that structured his days—but he did not move, and Ada did not release his hand, and neither of them acknowledged that the ordinary world was being quietly set aside.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "Now?"

"Because I'm running out of fine days." She said it the way she might have reported the weather, which was its own kind of horror. "And because you've been asking questions. At the Harrow's. Of Reverend Hale. Of poor Mrs. Tallow's boy about his mother." Her eyes were suddenly very direct. "You think we don't see? Love, this village has watched its own for two hundred years. We watch strangers twice as close, and kinder, usually. You've been asking *why*, and nobody will tell you, and I know how that feels—I remember being young and asking why and getting smiles. So I'm telling you. While I still have the weather for it."

"What happens when you reach zero?" The question was out of him before he could weigh it. "In the counting. What happens at zero?"

Ada was quiet for a long time. Outside, the last light lay along the hedgerow like a blade laid down.

"At zero," she said finally, "the bell rings. And someone comes for you. And you go with them, and it doesn't hurt, and you are not afraid, because you are not *alone*." Her thumb stroked his knuckles again, back and forth, a rhythm almost like the counting. "My mother reached zero in this very room, in 1968, and I was holding her hand, and the bell rang, and her face—" the old voice caught, steadied, "—her face went *glad*, Dominic. Like a child seeing the sea for the first time. Whatever walks you to the gate, it is kind. I have to believe that. I have believed it for eighty-one years, and I am not going to stop believing it now, at the end, because a clever young man with a clipboard tells me my mother's gladness was hypoxia."

"No," Dominic said softly. "No, I wouldn't say that."

But he thought it. He thought it loudly, helplessly, the reflex of a lifetime: *terminal euphoria, endorphin surge, the brain's last kindness to itself.* And underneath the thought, quieter and growing, another voice that sounded uncomfortably like his own at zero-hour in the churchyard: *but you heard it. You heard it and you knew it was calling a name, and some part of you waited to hear whose.*

"Will you stay with me?" Ada asked. It was such a small question, so nakedly human, that it bypassed every defence he had. "When I get low again. When the numbers start. Will you hold my hand through the counting? Not to stop it. Just so I'm not counting alone."

"Of course," he said. "Of course I will."

"Good lad." She settled back into her pillows, and he watched the lucidity begin to ebb in her—not all at once but like a tide going out over sand, the bright attention dimming, the pupils drifting toward that middle distance where the dying keep their appointments. "There's more you should know," she murmured. "About the seventh founder. About what happens if the bell is ever rung *wrong*. But I'm tired now, and the light's gone, and—" her brow creased, faintly, like a woman hearing music from another room, "—and it's nearly time."

"Time for what, Ada?"

Her lips began to move. He leaned close to catch it, and the word, when it came, was soft as ash falling:

"Nineteen."

* * *

He stayed. He broke every rule in the agency handbook about shift boundaries and personal hours, and he stayed, sitting in the wing-backed chair beside her bed with her hand in both of his, while the evening thickened around the house like ink dropped in water.

At eight o'clock she counted eighteen. At nine, seventeen. The numbers came slower now, spaced out like footsteps growing weary, and between them she slept or seemed to sleep, her breathing a thread of sound in the darkened room. Dominic did not turn on the lamp. He did not check his phone. He sat in the dark holding an old woman's hand and listened to her count down the distance between herself and whatever waited at zero, and he found—to his lasting, unquiet surprise—that he was not bored, and not frightened, and not sceptical.

He was *keeping vigil*. The phrase arrived in his mind unbidden, archaic, smelling of candlewax and cold stone, and he did not send it away.

At eleven, twelve. At midnight, eleven. Once, near one o'clock, her eyes opened and found his in the dark, and she said, "You're still here," with wonder in it, as though he had done something remarkable rather than something any nurse in any century would call basic decency, and he said, "I'm still here," and her eyes closed again, satisfied.

At two, eight. At three, six. The house creaked and settled around them. Through the window the church tower stood black against a sky rinsed with stars, and Dominic caught himself watching it the way you watch a door you expect someone to come through.

At four, four. At five, three.

"Two," Ada whispered, near dawn, and her hand tightened on his with sudden fierce strength, and her eyes opened wide and clear and fixed on the ceiling above her, on something ascending or descending, and she said—

"One."

And the room held its breath.

She did not say zero. Not then. Her lips shaped it—Dominic saw the small round of it, the silent O—and then her gaze slid sideways, found his face, and something moved through her expression that he had no clinical term for and has never found one since. Recognition, maybe. Or warning. Or the particular tenderness of someone who knows exactly how the story ends and is sorry to be leaving you in the middle of it.

"Not yet," she breathed. "Not quite yet. There's still the telling to do. There's still—" and her eyes drifted shut, and her grip loosened, and the number she had refused went back down inside her like a coin dropped in a well.

Dominic sat motionless until the birds started. Then, with hands that were not entirely steady, he wrote in the log: *Patient lucid 16:40–19:00. Discussed family history. Slept normally thereafter. Counting pattern interrupted at one; resumed at nineteen following morning.*

He looked at the sentence for a long time. *Interrupted at one.* As if the counting were a thing with preferences. As if it could be declined.

Below the entry, without deciding to, he added a second line, smaller, in pencil, the kind of note a man writes when he is no longer sure who he is writing for:

*She stopped before zero. She wanted to finish telling me something. Something about the seventh founder. Something about ringing the bell wrong.*

Outside, the sun came up over Bell House, gilding the empty tower, and from somewhere in the walls—or the floors, or the soil itself, or the hundred and eighty years of kept promises packed beneath the churchyard like silt—Dominic would have sworn he heard, faint and patient, the beginning of a count.

*Nineteen,* said the house. *Nineteen. Nineteen.*

Waiting for her to catch up.
