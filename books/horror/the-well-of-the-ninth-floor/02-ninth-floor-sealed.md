## 2. Ninth Floor, Sealed

The house announced itself before they reached the gate.

Zephyrine had expected decay — that was what houses did when their families abandoned them — but Maison Marchetti stood at the end of its long gravel drive like a held breath. Four stories of grey limestone rising out of morning fog, windows dark and patient, a slate roof the color of a drowned man's lips. It was not ruined. It was *waiting*, and there was a difference, and the difference made the skin of his arms prickle under his coat.

"It's huge," Odile said from beside him, her breath fogging. She had her backpack slung over one shoulder and her phone in her hand, and she was filming the house in a slow pan, narrating under her breath for whatever audience existed on the other side of the screen. "Dad. It's literally a castle."

"It's a house."

"It's four floors of house with a tower thing." She lowered the phone and squinted up. "Five? Is that five?"

He counted without meaning to. The central block rose in even tiers — ground floor, then three above it — and above those, set back and narrower, two more storeys climbed into the fog, capped by a small cupola whose glass was black as a pupil. Eight levels of living space, if you counted generously. He'd read the survey. He'd read everything the solicitor had sent, twice, because reading was what he did instead of feeling.

"Eight," he said. "The listing says eight."

"Who needs eight floors?"

"People who never throw anything away." He hefted the keys from his pocket — an iron ring heavy enough to sag his coat, seven keys and one that didn't match any lock he could identify, a stubby blackened thing like a tooth pulled from something's jaw. "Come on. We'll take the ground floor tonight and figure out the rest tomorrow."

The front door opened onto a hall that smelled of cold stone and, beneath it, faintly, of river water. That was the detail he would remember later: not dust, not mildew, but the clean mineral smell of a riverbed at low water, as if the house had been built over a stream and had spent a century drinking it. The floor tiles were black and white checkerboard worn soft at the center, and the staircase swept upward in a curve wide enough for a funeral procession.

There were portraits on the walls. Marchettis, all of them — he knew by the jawline, which was his own jawline repeated down two centuries like a signature practiced too many times. A woman with ink-black hair and a mouth set against smiling. A man in a magistrate's collar. Children, three of them, posed on a garden bench, none of them looking at the painter. All of them looking slightly off to the left, toward something outside the frame.

"Dad." Odile had stopped halfway down the hall. "Was your family rich or something?"

"My family was something," Zephyrine said. "Rich is unclear."

"You never talk about them."

"There was nothing to talk about. My father left when I was six. My mother didn't speak to anyone with the name Marchetti after that. This grandmother—" he gestured at the nearest portrait, an old woman with eyes the color of wet slate, "—I met her once, maybe. I don't remember it."

Odile looked at the old woman's portrait for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, in the voice she used when she was trying not to sound scared, "She looks like she's looking at us."

Zephyrine looked. The painted eyes were fixed slightly off to the left. Toward the stairs.

*It's the light,* he told himself. *It's always the light.* He had built a life out of telling himself things like that, and mostly the telling worked, the way a dam works — perfectly, right up until it doesn't.

* * *

They claimed the second floor that first night — bedrooms with shutters that groaned like animals when he wrestled them closed, beds under dust sheets that came off in grey avalanches. Odile chose the corner room with the window overlooking the drive. He took the room across the hall, and lay awake listening to the house settle around them, ticking and sighing, a body cooling.

In the morning he began the inventory.

This was how he grieved; he had learned it the hard way, over the worst eighteen months of his life, cataloguing his wife's closet into keep-donate-burn while Odile slept down the hall. Lists were load-bearing. If every object had a place, then absence had a shape, and a shaped absence could be carried. So he walked the house floor by floor with a legal pad, writing: *drawing room, water damage, NE corner. Library, intact, books water-swollen. Kitchen, cast iron range, functional?*

The house had eight floors and he catalogued seven.

It wasn't until the third day that he understood why. He stood at the top of the seventh-floor landing with his legal pad and looked at the wall where the eighth flight of stairs should have begun, and saw brickwork.

Not old brickwork. Not the crumbled, patched masonry of the rest of the house. This was a single plane of brick, laid flush and neat, filling the stairwell arch from floor to ceiling, mortared smooth, and — this was the part that stopped him with his pen still raised — *painted*. Someone had painted the bricks the same cream color as the hallway walls, carefully, cutting in along the edges with a fine brush, so that at a glance the wall looked like a wall, like it had always been a wall, like no one had ever stood where he was standing and looked up at a staircase going somewhere.

Except someone had. Because the banister continued.

The oak rail ran up alongside the bricked arch and simply ended at the wall, cut clean, its newel post still polished where a hundred years of hands had gripped it. Below the paint, faintly, he could see the ghost-lines of the steps behind the brick, like bones showing through skin.

He wrote in his legal pad: *8th floor sealed. Ask solicitor.*

He did not write: *why does my skin crawl.*

* * *

"Solicitor Delorme," Zephyrine said, holding his phone against his ear with his shoulder, pacing the drawing room that evening. "The deed mentions eight floors. There's a stairwell on the top landing that's been bricked over."

A pause. Long enough that he checked whether the call had dropped.

"Monsieur Marchetti," Delorme said finally, and his voice had the careful flatness of a man stepping around something on the floor, "the ninth level is not part of the property conveyed."

"The ninth— it's inside the footprint of the building. It's on the survey."

"The survey describes eight floors." Another pause. "The brickwork is original to the 1911 renovations. It is structural. I would advise against disturbing it."

"Structural," Zephyrine repeated. "You bricked up a staircase and called it structural."

"I advise many things, monsieur. Whether they are followed is between the client and—" Delorme stopped himself. Zephyrine heard him breathe. "Is your daughter with you?"

The question arrived sideways, out of nowhere, and landed wrong. "Why?"

"Only — the house is large. Large houses have places children find." A rustle of paper, a throat cleared. "Keep her on the lower floors, monsieur. For the first weeks. Old houses settle. Sounds carry strangely. There is no sense frightening her."

"We're not frightened."

"No," Delorme said, and there was something in the word that Zephyrine would spend the next several days taking apart, the way you worry a splinter. "Of course not. Good evening, monsieur."

The line went dead. Zephyrine stood in the drawing room with the last of the daylight going blue in the windows, and noticed that he had been standing exactly where the painted woman's eyes pointed, three floors up and through two walls, and he moved out of the spot, and hated himself for moving.

* * *

It was Odile who found the humming. Of course it was. Children are sonar; they ping the dark places adults have agreed to ignore.

He heard her before he understood what he was hearing — her voice drifting down the back stairwell on the fourth evening, thin and tuneless, and he thought at first she was singing to herself, some fragment of a song from her phone, and then he realized the melody wasn't hers. It was older than anything she listened to. It had the shape of a lullaby that had been sung past the point where anyone remembered the words, only the rise and fall of it, the rocking rhythm, a cradle-song with the cradle gone.

He climbed the back stairs. She was standing on the seventh-floor landing, facing the bricked-up arch, her ear nearly touching the painted bricks, her eyes half-closed.

"Odile."

She didn't startle. That was the first wrong thing. She turned slowly, surfacing, like someone coming up from deep water. "Hm?"

"What are you doing?"

"Listening." She said it as though it were obvious, as though he were the strange one. "There's someone humming."

He looked at the wall. Cream paint, flush mortar, the ghost of steps behind it. He stepped closer — closer than he wanted to — and put his own ear against the brick.

Cold. Mortar grit against his cheekbone. And underneath, if he strained—

Nothing. Wind in the chimney flues, maybe. The house's ordinary circulatory sounds. His own pulse in his ear, loud because he was pressing his head against solid masonry like an idiot.

"I don't hear anything," he said.

"It's a lady." Odile touched the brick with two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "She hums when the sun goes down. Not before. Only after." She frowned, working at something. "She doesn't know the words either."

"Odile." He crouched to her height, took her shoulders. Her skin was warm, her pupils normal, her breath unremarkable — he ran the checklist automatically, the parent's triage, fever-no, disorientation-no, and found nothing wrong except everything. "There's no one in the walls. It's pipes. Old houses make noise — water in the pipes, wind, expansion. The metal contracts at night and it can sound almost melodic. I promise you."

She looked at him with the particular patience teenagers reserve for adults explaining weather to fish. "Okay," she said.

But that night, lying awake in his room across the hall from hers, he heard the door open at some hour he refused to check on the clock, and heard her bare feet cross the landing, and heard nothing else for a long time. When he finally got up — telling himself he was getting water, telling himself the kitchen was downstairs, telling himself all the way up two flights — he found her back on the seventh-floor landing, sitting cross-legged before the bricked arch like a child before a fire, humming.

Two voices now. Hers, and something underneath it, half a tone off, matching her note for note.

He stood in the dark and did not hear it. He wants that recorded somewhere, entered into evidence: he stood three meters away in the dark of his ancestral home and he did not hear it, or the part of him that heard it refused to sign the paperwork, and either way he said, softly, "Odile. Bed."

She came without protest. At her door she paused and said, drowsy, "She says she remembers you."

"She's mistaken," Zephyrine said. "I've never been here."

"Not here-here," Odile said, already half asleep, letting him steer her. "Before. She said you used to count along."

He tucked her in. He checked the window latch. He stood in the doorway watching her breathe until the watching became its own kind of prayer, and then he went back to his room and sat on the edge of the bed with the blackened key from the iron ring in his palm, the one that matched no lock in the house, and he told himself:

Pipes. Wind. A lonely kid in a big house making a friend out of architecture. Suggestion. Suggestion is powerful; he'd read the studies; hospice nurses knew better than anyone how a whispered number could become a dying man's whole cosmology.

He told himself all of it, thoroughly, in order, the way he catalogued rooms.

And then, because he was honest in the bone-deep way that had made grief unbearable for him and fiction possible, he admitted the crack, just to himself, in the dark, in the smallest voice he owned:

He hadn't heard the humming.

But he had recognized the melody.

Somewhere, in a life he had sealed and painted over as carefully as any stairwell, someone had sung that rise and fall over his crib — and the knowing of it had come up through the floor of him like cold water, instant and total, before his mind could draft a single rational word.

The house had eight floors. The survey said so. The solicitor said so.

So who, Zephyrine asked the ceiling, had needed a ninth one badly enough to bury it alive?

Down the hall, through two walls, faint as a pulse, Odile sighed in her sleep — and beneath the sigh, half a tone low, something kept humming, patient as mortar, waiting for the sun to go down again.
