## 7. The Cousin Who Stayed

The man was standing in the kitchen when Zephyrine came down for water at three in the morning, and the terrible thing—the thing Zephyrine would return to again and again in the weeks that followed—was not that a stranger stood in his house, but that the stranger looked like him.

Not like him exactly. Like him run through some process of subtraction. The same long jaw, the same deep-set eyes, the same dark hair going iron at the temples, but on this man every feature had been worn down as if by water, until what remained was less a face than the memory of one. He stood with his hands folded in front of him, patient as furniture, and he did not startle when Zephyrine switched on the light.

"You'd be Zephyrine," the man said. His voice was dry, papery, the voice of someone who had not used it much. "I watched you arrive Tuesday. I thought I'd give you a few days to settle."

Zephyrine's hand found the heavy brass candlestick on the counter without his deciding to reach for it. "The door was locked."

"Yes," the man agreed, as though this confirmed something pleasant. "It usually is."

"Who are you?"

"Ambrose." He said it simply, and then, when Zephyrine's grip tightened on the candlestick: "Ambrose Marchetti. Your cousin. Though I suppose your father never mentioned me. He wouldn't have. None of them who left ever mention us."

*Us.* Zephyrine kept the candlestick raised. Outside, the wind moved through the plane trees along the drive, and beneath it—very faintly, or perhaps only in the house's old bones—he thought he heard that humming Odile spoke of, rising through the floors like heat through a chimney.

"There is no *us*," Zephyrine said. "My family is me and my daughter. Everyone else is dead or never existed. My father talked about Vellacourt the way people talk about a war they deserted from. There was no one left to mention."

"That's what they're told to say." Ambrose unfolded his hands at last and laid them flat on the table, palms down, an oddly formal gesture, almost liturgical. "Sit down, cousin. You look like a man who hasn't slept since the bell rang. Neither have I. Neither has anyone in this village. We might as well be tired together."

"I'm calling the police."

"With what signal? The nearest gendarme is forty minutes away in Saint-Aubin, and Michel Ferrand hasn't driven his route since the bell came back, because Michel is ninety-one years old and last week his hands stopped shaking for the first time in thirty years." Ambrose's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "You've noticed, haven't you. How well everyone looks. How the oldest people in Vellacourt move like they're fifty. You noticed it at the tavern, I'd wager, and told yourself it was good country air."

Zephyrine had noticed. He had filed it away with the wax earplug pressed into Odile's hand by a woman whose eyes had been kind and terrified in equal measure, with the hospice patient counting backwards in his dead grandmother's voice, with the melted bell tolling over a village of silent counters. He had filed all of it under *coincidence, folklore, grief doing strange things to a rational mind*, and the file had been growing heavier every day, and he had begun to suspect it would not close much longer.

He sat down. Not because he trusted the man—because his legs made the decision for him.

*

Ambrose made tea without asking, moving through the kitchen with the unerring familiarity of someone who had lived in this house far longer than Zephyrine had. He knew which cupboard held the cups. He knew the stove needed its left burner coaxed twice before it caught. Watching him, Zephyrine felt the strange vertigo of seeing his own inheritance handled by someone else's muscle memory.

"My grandmother owned this house," Zephyrine said. "Yvonne Marchetti. She died in June. I never met her. My father left here at nineteen and never came back, never wrote, never—" He stopped. "Why would she leave it to me? I'm nobody to this place. I was never even chosen, whatever that means. That's what the ledger said. Odile found it in the attic. Nine names a century, death dates, the word *paid*. And my name at the bottom, uncrossed. Hers underneath it, fresh ink."

Ambrose set the teapot down very carefully, as if the porcelain might bruise.

"She left it to you because you were the one who got away," he said. "And the Well has a long memory for the ones who get away."

"The Well."

"You've seen the ninth floor."

"It's bricked up. It's a stairwell to nowhere. This house has eight floors."

"This house has nine," Ambrose said, "and the ninth is the only one that matters. Sit. Drink your tea. I'm going to tell you a story, and you're going to disbelieve it, and that's fine—that's healthy, even. Disbelief is a luxury. Enjoy it while you still can afford it."

*

He told it plainly, without theater, which somehow made it worse. Ambrose had the flat delivery of a man reciting a train timetable, and Zephyrine understood, with a lurch, that this was because to Ambrose it *was* a timetable. A schedule. A thing that ran whether or not anyone believed in it.

"In 1743," Ambrose began, "Vellacourt was dying. Not figuratively. The blight had taken the wheat three years running, the fever had taken a third of the children, and the wells—the real wells, the water wells—had gone bitter. Forty families. They were packing their carts to leave when Yves Marchetti, who built this house, went up to the top floor with a pickaxe and broke through his own ceiling, because he'd been dreaming about a shaft that went down instead of up, and dreams like that don't come to a man twice.

"He found the Well. Nobody knows how deep it goes. Nobody has ever dropped a stone and heard it land. But Yves leaned over the edge and heard something breathe, and the breathing sounded like a village being alive—like bread baking, like cattle lowing, like rain on wheat—and when he climbed back down, the blight broke that same autumn. The fever lifted. The wells turned sweet.

"But the Well doesn't give. It lends. And it charges interest."

Ambrose looked at his own hands, thin as winter branches.

"One Marchetti per generation. That was Yves's bargain, struck on behalf of a family that hadn't been asked. One of ours, given down into the shaft, once every twenty-five or thirty years, and in exchange the village lives. Not comfortably—well enough. The crops come in. The fevers pass over. And the people of Vellacourt live long past any decent span, so long as the payments keep arriving. Michel Ferrand's father saw Napoleon's soldiers march through and lived to watch television. Old Marguerite at the tavern buried four husbands and outlived them all by decades. That's not country air, cousin. That's borrowed time, drawn against our account."

Zephyrine's tea had gone cold in his hands. He became aware of it distantly, the way you become aware of weather in another country.

"People die here," he said finally. "People must die. The hospice—"

"The hospice takes the ones whose time was always their own. Outsiders, mostly, and the childless, and those the Well has no claim on. For them, the bell tolls and they count down from nine, because everything here runs on the Well's arithmetic, even the deaths that belong to us." Ambrose's voice didn't waver. "But a Marchetti doesn't get a hospice bed. A Marchetti gets the ninth floor."

"And if the payment doesn't come?"

"Then the Well collects elsewhere. Slowly, at first. A bad harvest. A sick child. Then faster. In 1911, when the family tried to refuse—there was a branch of Marchettis then who'd decided the whole thing was superstition—they fed the church bell down the shaft instead, thinking metal would satisfy it, thinking a symbol would do. The village lost eleven people that winter, and the bell has been ringing from below ever since, whenever the account comes due. It rings and everyone counts, because everyone knows what the counting means. It means the Well is reminding us the meter is running."

Zephyrine set the cup down. His hand was steady, which surprised him; inside, something was moving with the slow, grinding patience of a glacier calving.

"You're telling me my family has been feeding people to a hole in the floor for two hundred and eighty years."

"I'm telling you that everyone you've ever met who seemed lucky was standing downwind of this house." Ambrose leaned forward, and in the kitchen light Zephyrine could see, for the first time, the fine tremor in him—not fear, but exhaustion, the tremor of a man holding up something enormously heavy for a very long time. "You think your father left because he hated this place? He left because it was his turn to be chosen, and he ran, and the Well let him go because it had already marked the next one. His younger brother. Your uncle Tomas. Do you remember Tomas?"

Zephyrine did not answer. He remembered a photograph, one photograph, smuggled out of Vellacourt in his father's suitcase: a boy of perhaps sixteen, squinting into sunlight, with Zephyrine's own jaw and Zephyrine's own eyes. His father had never once spoken of him. When Zephyrine, at eight, had asked who the boy was, his father had looked at the photograph for a long moment and put it face-down in the drawer, and had drunk more than usual that night, and the next, and the next, in the slow descending spiral that ended when Zephyrine was twelve.

"Tomas paid in 1968," Ambrose said quietly. "He walked up nine flights and down one shaft, and the village got another thirty years, and your father got a lifetime of knowing what his cowardice cost. And now the account has come due again, and there are only three Marchettis left in the world: me, you, and the girl."

The clock in the hall ticked. Somewhere above them—above the attic, above the roof, or perhaps below the cellar, below the earth, the direction didn't matter anymore—something breathed.

"Why are you telling me this?" Zephyrine asked. "If the covenant needs a payment, why warn me? Why not just—"

"Because I was supposed to pay this generation," Ambrose said. "1974. I was twenty-two, and it was my name in the ledger, and I stood at the top of those stairs for six hours, and then I ran. I got as far as Lyon. I lasted eleven days." He laughed, a small dry sound with no humor in it anywhere. "The Well doesn't chase, cousin. It waits. I came back on my own, because the dying started—slowly, everywhere I went, strangers counting backwards from nine in my presence, wilting like cut flowers. So I came home and offered myself, and the Well refused me."

"Refused you."

"I'm spoiled goods. Marked as a runner. It keeps me alive at the threshold as a warning—a man who cannot die and cannot live, aging one day for every year, held just this side of the grave for fifty years so that every Marchetti after me can see what refusal buys." Ambrose spread his arms slightly, presenting himself: the gauntness, the worn-away face, the papery voice. "This is what I am. I am the receipt. I am what the Well keeps pinned to the wall so no one else gets ideas."

Zephyrine stared at him. Pity rose in him, unwelcome and enormous, and beneath the pity something colder: the understanding that he was looking at his own future, the shape waiting for him at the end of every road except one.

"So the choices are what Odile's ledger says," he said. "Her name in wet ink. Or mine uncrossed. Or we run, and you're what's waiting for us at the end of the road."

"Or you find a third option," Ambrose said. "Two hundred and eighty years, and no one ever has. But you should know what you're deciding between, and you should know it soon. Because the bell has already rung, and the counting has already started, and yesterday morning I heard it pause."

"Pause?"

Ambrose met his eyes, and for the first time the flat timetable-voice faltered.

"It paused at four," he said. "And it hasn't moved since. It's waiting to see what you'll do, Zephyrine. The whole village is waiting. The Well is waiting. And things that wait like that—" he glanced upward, toward the sealed floor, toward whatever breathed beneath it— "things that wait like that are not waiting to be merciful."

Above them, faint and unmistakable, the humming through the bricks changed key.

And Zephyrine, who had heard nothing through that stairwell for five days, heard it too.
