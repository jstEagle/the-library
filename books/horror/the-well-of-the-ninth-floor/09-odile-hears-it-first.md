## 9. Odile Hears It First

The humming through the bricks had changed.

Odile noticed it on her third night in the house, lying awake under a quilt that smelled of cedar and someone else's long-ago life. For two nights it had been a hum, tuneless and patient, like a refrigerator in an empty kitchen. But tonight it had shape. Tonight it was almost a melody, and melodies were worse, because melodies meant intention. You didn't hum by accident. You hummed because you wanted something to listen.

She pressed her ear against the cold plaster of the stairwell wall, the one at the end of the third-floor corridor where the stairs climbed up into brick and stopped, as if the house had been amputated mid-thought. Her father said there was nothing behind it. He'd said it in his doctor voice, the flat, kind voice he used when he was telling himself something harder than he was telling you. There's no ninth floor, Odile. The house just ends. Old houses do that.

But old houses didn't hum.

*Seven,* said the melody, without words, without sound exactly — more like the memory of a word, the groove a word leaves behind. *Seven.*

Odile pulled back from the wall so fast she knocked her elbow against the banister. The pain was ordinary and welcome. She stood in the dark corridor breathing hard, listening to her own heart, waiting for the number to come again.

It didn't. The hum resumed its patient, tuneless drone, and after a while — she couldn't have said how long — she went back to bed and slept without dreaming, which should have been a mercy.

* * *

In the morning she told herself she'd imagined it. She was good at that. Fourteen years of practice: imagined the footsteps in their old apartment in Lyon, imagined the smell of her mother's perfume in the coat closet eight months after the funeral, imagined the phone ringing with her mother's voice on the other end of the line saying nothing at all, just breathing, just being there the way the dead are never there. Grief made sounds, her father explained once, gently, the way you explain weather to a child. Grief fills empty rooms. It doesn't mean the rooms are full.

So she ate breakfast across from him in the vast kitchen with its dead fireplace and its windows fogged with river damp, and she watched him pretend to read a letter from the solicitor, and she said nothing about seven.

"You slept okay?" he asked, not looking up.

"Fine," she said. "You?"

He hesitated a fraction too long. "Fine."

They were both liars before nine a.m., which felt, in its way, like family tradition.

That day she explored. That was the deal she'd made with herself — explore everything, catalogue everything, be the scientist her father thought he'd raised. The house gave itself up room by room: the ballroom with its sheeted chandeliers like hanged ghosts, the library where the books were arranged by height instead of subject, the nursery on the sixth floor whose crib still held a folded blanket gone yellow with age. On the seventh floor she found a door painted shut, and on the eighth, rooms furnished for people who clearly expected to return and never did — a pipe rack, a half-finished embroidery, a chess game mid-match, a black bishop tipped on its side as though it had simply fainted.

None of it frightened her, exactly. What frightened her was how well she knew it all already. How her feet found the corridors without hesitation. How she turned left at the landing on the fifth floor before she consciously decided to, and stood there afterward feeling like a glove that had found its hand.

The Marchetti blood, her father would have said. Family instinct. Nothing more.

At dinner — canned soup, because neither of them had learned to cook and the village shop closed at noon and reopened, allegedly, whenever the keeper felt like it — her father asked about her day and she told him about the chess game, the tipped bishop, and he smiled the small smile he kept for her discoveries, and for one warm moment they were just two people in a big cold house, and it was almost good.

Then, over the dishes, she caught herself tapping.

Her right index finger against the edge of the sink. A slow, even rhythm. Tap. Tap. Tap. Not fidgeting — she knew her own fidgets, this wasn't one — but keeping time. Like a metronome set to something slower than a resting heart.

She stopped. The silence afterward felt louder than the tapping.

"Odile?"

"Nothing," she said. "Tired. I'll go up early."

She kissed his cheek — bristle and smoke and the faint hospital smell that clung to him even now, even here, soap and antiseptic ghosting under everything — and climbed the four flights to her room, counting the landings without meaning to.

One. Two. Three. Four.

She stopped on the fourth landing, hand on the rail, and made herself not think about what came next in any sequence that started like that.

* * *

She woke at some black hour with her mouth tasting of coins.

Not blood — coins. Old copper, riverbed silt, the taste of holding money in your teeth. She lay still in the strange bed beneath the sloped attic ceiling and listened to the house breathe around her, timbers ticking as they cooled, wind worrying the shutters, and underneath it all, faint as breath on glass, the hum.

Except it wasn't the hum anymore.

It was counting.

*Five,* said the wall. *Four.*

Odile sat up. Her body felt wrong — heavy and light at once, like she was wearing someone else's weight over her own. Her hands, she saw dimly in the moonlight through the dormer window, were moving. Both of them now, fingers curling and uncurling in her lap, keeping the rhythm. Five. Four. Each number a flexion, a small precise contraction, as though her hands had been given a job and she hadn't been consulted.

*Three,* said the wall, and her fingers answered.

"No," she whispered, and her voice sounded far away, borrowed, a girl's voice heard through water.

She got up. She didn't decide to get up; there was simply a moment when she was in bed and then a moment when she was standing, barefoot on cold boards, and the transition between the two had happened without her, the way you don't remember falling asleep. Her legs carried her out of the room, down the attic stairs, down through the sleeping floors of the house, and she watched from somewhere behind her own eyes as her feet took the turns — left at five, left at four — faster than thought, certain as water finding its level.

The corridor on the third floor. The bricked-up stairwell, pale as a scar in the moonlight.

*Two,* said the bricks, and Odile raised her hand and laid her palm flat against the plaster, and it was warm. Warm as skin. Warm as a forehead with fever in it. Beneath her palm, faintly, she felt movement — not vibration exactly, not the hum, but a pressure, rhythmic, patient, like something enormous turning over in its sleep on the other side of the world.

And her mouth opened, and her voice — her own voice, thin and clear and fourteen years old — began to count.

"One," she said.

The word fell into the silence of the corridor and rang there like a coin dropped down a shaft. She heard it fall and fall and never land.

"One," she said again, and her fingers curled shut, and somewhere very far below the floorboards, deep past the cellars, past the earth, past anything with a bottom, something exhaled — long and satisfied and grateful — and the warmth under her palm pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat accepting a gift.

Then nothing. The wall went cold as stone under her hand. The taste of coins drained out of her mouth. And Odile stood alone in the moonlit corridor, shivering, with no memory of how she'd come to be there, holding her own wrist with her other hand as if to keep it from doing anything else.

* * *

Footsteps on the stairs. Fast ones.

"Dad—" she started, and then his light hit her, and she saw his face, and whatever she'd been about to say died in her throat.

Zephyrine Marchetti stood at the top of the third-floor stairs in his rumpled shirt, hair wild, the flashlight shaking in his grip — actually shaking, she could see the circle of light trembling on the wall — and he was looking at her the way you look at something you love that has stopped being entirely itself. She knew that look. She'd worn it herself, at thirteen, at the funeral, looking at the coffin that held her mother and also somehow didn't, that held her mother's name and her mother's face and none of her mother at all.

"Papa." Her voice cracked on it. "I don't— I was asleep, I don't know how I got here."

He crossed the corridor in three strides and took her by the shoulders, and his hands were ice. "Say that again."

"I was asleep. I woke up and I was standing here. Papa, my hand is freezing, why is the wall—"

"What were you doing?" His eyes went past her shoulder to the bricked-up archway, and she watched him touch the plaster where her palm had been, and watch his face change when he felt what she'd felt — or its absence, the ordinary cold of ordinary stone, because of course it was cold now, of course it was only brick, of course.

"I don't remember," she said, and it was true, and the truth of it terrified her more than anything the night had done. "Dad, I swear, I don't remember anything. I woke up and I was here."

"Were you talking?" His voice had gone into its clinical register, the triage voice, the voice that meant he was frightened enough to need a procedure. "Did you say anything? Words? Numbers?"

"Why numbers?" she asked, and watched the question land on him like a blow.

He didn't answer. He took her hand instead — pressed both of his around it, chafing warmth back into her fingers — and led her back along the corridor, and she let him, because her legs still didn't feel entirely hers and because his terror, absurdly, steadied her. Her father, who did not believe in ghosts or bells or wells or anything that couldn't be charted, was afraid. Which meant either he knew something he hadn't told her, or he was starting to believe, and she honestly couldn't decide which was worse.

In her room he sat on the edge of her bed while she burrowed under the quilt, and he did the thing he used to do when she was small and feverish: he counted her breaths aloud, softly, one to ten and around again, until her eyes grew heavy. It was the first time she'd heard him count to anything in years. He'd stopped counting things after the funeral. She'd never asked why.

"Papa," she murmured, nearly under. "What happens at zero?"

His breath caught — an audible hitch, a skipped stitch — and for a long moment the only sound was the house settling around them, tick and groan, tick and groan.

"Nothing happens at zero," he said finally. "Zero isn't a place. Go to sleep, Odile."

But he stayed sitting there until dawn, she could tell by the quality of the light when she surfaced later, and when she woke fully he was gone, and on the nightstand beside her glass of water someone had left the wax earplug the old woman in the village had pressed into her hand outside the tavern — the one she'd pocketed and forgotten, the one she'd never brought upstairs.

Except it was here. Sitting on the nightstand. Placed there carefully, deliberately, like a suggestion.

Or like a warning.

Downstairs, faintly, she could hear her father on the telephone to the solicitor, asking in a voice stripped of all its calm whether anyone had ever reported — and then the line of questioning dissolved into the murmur of the receiver, and Odile lay in her bed in the house with the missing floor, and slowly, without deciding to, her right index finger began to tap against the quilt.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

Keeping time for something that hadn't finished counting yet.
