## 21. The House With Eight Floors

The ringing didn't stop so much as it was swallowed.

One moment the bell was tolling through Odile's skull — nine strokes, each one landing somewhere behind her eyes like a fist against a door — and then the ninth stroke simply never finished. It opened its mouth and closed it again without a sound, and what remained afterward was not silence but an absence shaped like sound, the way a pulled tooth leaves a hole your tongue keeps returning to.

She woke on the floor of her father's room with her cheek pressed to cold boards and her ears full of cotton-wool quiet. Zephyrine was beside her, still as furniture, and for one long, spinning second she thought he hadn't survived it either — that the Well had taken them both and this was the room where they'd be kept. Then his chest rose. Then his hand found hers without him waking, the way he used to find her hand in cinemas when the film got loud, and she understood that whatever had happened down there in the dark, some part of him had come back holding onto her.

"Papa," she said, and heard how wrong her own voice sounded — flattened, distant, like someone speaking from the far end of a corridor. She said it again, louder. "Papa."

His eyes opened. They were different. That was the first thing she noticed and the last thing she would ever stop noticing: the gray had gone out of them, or rather all the colors had gone out of them, leaving something pale and reflective, like water over stone. He looked at her the way you look at a room after a fire — checking, cataloguing, grateful and grieving at once.

"Odile." He sat up too fast and winced, pressing his palms to his ears. "Can you hear me?"

"Barely." She signed it first, without thinking — she'd learned a little sign language from a video series two years ago, a thing she'd abandoned after three weeks, and now her hands remembered it before her voice did. *Barely. You?*

He shook his head. He cupped his ear and made a small tilting gesture with his other hand: *half.* Half-deaf, both of them, the left ears gone quiet as if the Well had taken its toll asymmetrically, carelessly, the way a child tears a page.

They sat together on the floor while the light came up through the shutters, neither of them moving, because moving would mean deciding what came next and neither of them knew yet whether there was a next. Outside, Vellacourt was making a sound she had no name for. Not screaming. Not weeping. Something drier than that — a rustling, a settling, the sound a house makes when it cools at night, stretched across an entire village.

* * *

They went down through the house because going up was no longer possible and going nowhere was unbearable.

Odile counted the floors as they descended, an old habit, a tic she'd developed in the first week here when everything about the Marchetti house felt designed to be miscounted. Eight flights down to the ground floor. She stood in the entrance hall with her hand on the newel post and looked back up the stairwell, and the stairwell looked back, and it was only eight.

She climbed four steps to be sure. Eight landings above her, eight ceilings stacked like the rings of a felled tree. Where the bricked-up archway had been — where Ambrose had torn the bricks out with his bare hands, where the ninth floor had waited with its single room and its shaft that breathed — there was now wall. Not new brick. Old brick, smoke-darkened, mortared by hands long dead, load-bearing, structural, *original*. A hairline crack ran through it the way cracks run through anything old, meaning nothing, promising nothing.

"Papa," she said, and pointed.

Zephyrine came and stood beside her and looked for a long time. His face did something complicated — relief and horror fighting for the same muscles — and then he reached out and pressed his palm flat against the brick, the way you press a hand against a horse's neck to calm it, or yourself.

"It's rewriting," he said. His voice came out half-swallowed; she watched his lips more than she listened. "It's not just gone. It's *never been*."

"The neighbors will say—" she started, and stopped, because she could hear the sentence finishing itself in a voice that wasn't quite hers. *The house has always had eight floors. The Marchettis were always a little strange. The girl always had white in her hair.* The village was already aging into its own story, and stories are patient, and stories do not need to be true.

Her temples itched. She touched them and her fingers came away with two silver strands, fine as spider silk. There had been more last week. Whole wings of gray. Now just these two, like the Well had taken most of its loan back but kept a receipt.

"We're leaving today," Zephyrine said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite a decision. It was the tone of a man reading a verdict aloud to convince himself it was real.

* * *

Ambrose's door was open.

They hadn't been able to make themselves look at the shaft — the stairwell to the ninth floor had sealed itself over in the night, brick by patient brick, as if the house were healing around a wound — but they both knew what was behind the wall now, and who. Odile had dreamed of him. In the dream he was standing at the mouth of the well in the gray hour before dawn, and he had turned around and smiled at her with his gaunt, ruined face, and he had said, very clearly, in a voice that carried despite her deafened ear:

*Tell him it doesn't want a body. Tell him it wanted the one who ran twice. And tell him—*

But the dream had ended there, the way dreams end when they're being polite, and she had woken with the unfinished sentence sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. She wondered if her father had dreamed it too. She wondered if he'd gotten further into the sentence than she had.

They passed Ambrose's room on the way down. The bed was empty and neatly made, which was somehow worse than blood, worse than ruin. On the pillow lay the wax earplug the old woman had given her outside the tavern, weeks ago, in another life. It had been in her pocket. She checked. Her pocket was empty. Someone — something — had delivered it back, and placed it where a mourner would place flowers.

Odile took it. She didn't know why. Some things you keep not because they mean comfort but because they mean *witness*, and someone had to witness Ambrose Marchetti, who had run once and paid for fifty years and run again, upward, into the dark, and whose name would appear on no ledger now because there was no ledger anymore. Her father had burned it. She had watched the pages curl and blacken, and she had seen — she was almost sure she had seen — the ink lift off the burning paper like insects taking flight.

* * *

Vellacourt was dying the way fruit rots: all at once, everywhere, gently.

They walked down the main street in the early light and the village aged around them in real time. It wasn't violent. That was the terrible part. An old man sat on a bench outside the bakery and between one glance and the next his hands became a dead man's hands, liver-spotted and knuckled, resting on knees that had gone thin as kindling. The bakery's windows had fogged with dust that hadn't been there yesterday. Ivy moved along a garden wall with the slow speed of time-lapse, swallowing a gate, a bicycle, a child's rubber boot left out since summer.

No one screamed. No one even spoke. The villagers stood in their doorways and watched Zephyrine and Odile pass, and their faces were the faces of people watching a train leave a station they will never travel from again. There was fury in some of them — she saw it, banked and hopeless, in the tavern keeper's eyes as he gripped his doorframe with fingers that were becoming twigs — but mostly there was something worse than fury. Recognition. As if they had always known it would end this way, and had simply hoped to die before finding out.

A woman stepped into the road ahead of them. Odile flinched; her father's arm came up. But the woman only held out her hands, palms up, empty, and her mouth moved around words that Odile's half-deaf ears couldn't gather. Zephyrine shook his head slowly. The woman looked at him for a long moment — and Odile saw it happen, saw the understanding move across her face like weather — and then she stepped aside, and bowed her head, and let them pass.

"They lived on borrowed time," Zephyrine said quietly, when they were clear. "Ninety years of it. I don't know how you apologize for taking back a loan."

"You don't," Odile said. "You just walk."

He looked at her sideways, and something passed between them that had no words, partly because words were hard now, and partly because some things said aloud become smaller.

At the edge of the village, where the road bent toward the low hills and the world beyond, Odile turned around one last time. Vellacourt lay in its valley like a photograph left in the sun — roofs sagging inward, gardens gone to seed and then to dust, the church tower leaning at an angle that would take years to fall and would fall within the hour. The whole place was exhaling. Nine generations of stolen time, released in a single morning, and the morning didn't even have the decency to be dramatic about it. Crows had already arrived. Crows always know.

And beneath it all — faint, at the very threshold of her working ear, or perhaps only in the place where her ear used to report to her — she heard the bell toll once. Softly. Cracked, the way a cracked bell sounds, all its music leaking out the wound. Not nine times. Once. Like a man clearing his throat. Or like something below the world turning over in its sleep, and beginning, very slowly, to dream of counting again.

* * *

They walked for three days.

Odile kept a journal because her father told her to — "write everything down before it rewrites you" — and because the act of writing was the only thing that made the last month feel like something that had happened to her rather than something she had dreamed in a fever. She wrote about the ledger. About the counting. About Ambrose and the priest and the bell of 1911 and the taste of coins and riverbed on the ninth floor. She wrote until her hand cramped, and then she wrote about small things instead: the bread the tavern keeper's wife had given them that first week, warm, wrapped in cloth; the exact gray-green of the light through the attic window; the way her father had cried once, silently, facing a wall, thinking she was asleep.

On the third evening they reached a town with lit windows and cars and a boy on a skateboard, and the ordinary world received them without ceremony, which is the kindest thing the ordinary world knows how to do.

In a cheap hotel with a radiator that clanked, Zephyrine called a taxi company, then a train station, shouting politely into the phone because he still couldn't gauge his own volume. Odile sat on the bed and watched him and felt the strangest grief of her life, which was grief for something that had been taken away that she had never wanted and would somehow miss anyway. For six weeks she had known exactly what the world was: a skin stretched over a well, and everyone she met a potential counter, counting down. It had been horrible. It had also been *true*, in a way nothing had ever been true before, and now the truth had been unmade along with the monster, and she was left holding a reality that felt provisional, rented, like the house with eight floors.

"Papa," she said. "The house. When we sell it. What will the surveyor say?"

Zephyrine lowered the phone. He considered the question with the seriousness he gave all her questions, which was the thing she loved most about him and the thing that had saved them both, maybe — a man who took his daughter seriously in a village that took nothing seriously except a hole in the ground.

"He'll say it's a fine old property," he said. "Eight floors. Good bones. A strange draft in the east wing that no one can source." He paused. "He'll say the cellar smells of coins."

"Don't," she said.

"I'm sorry." He sat down beside her. The mattress dipped. In the mirror across the room she watched the two of them, the half-deaf man and the prematurely graying girl, and she thought: *we look like survivors in a film, and in films survivors look free.*

"Papa. Are we free?"

He didn't answer right away. He reached into his coat pocket — the same coat he'd worn since Vellacourt, which she suspected he would wear until it rotted off him — and took out the wax earplug. Ambrose's. The one from the pillow. He turned it over in his fingers.

"I don't know," he said finally. "I think freedom might be like hearing. You don't get it all back. You get enough to live on, and you spend the rest of your life noticing the difference."

Outside, far off, a church bell rang the hour. Six strokes, clear and ordinary, for an evening that meant nothing to anyone.

Odile counted them anyway. She couldn't help it. One, two, three, four, five, six — and then she caught herself waiting for seven, for eight, for nine, her breath held, her fingers curled, the old arithmetic rising in her throat like sickness —

— and the bell stopped at six, because bells stop at six, because bells have always stopped at six, and the night outside the window was just a night, and the town was just a town, and somewhere below the world there was nothing at all.

Nothing at all.

Probably.

She got out her journal, and under the day's date, in small careful letters, she wrote the sentence she would carry for the rest of her life, the sentence that was both a closing and a door left very slightly ajar:

*It's over. I think it's over. But tonight, in the hotel bathroom, the tap dripped nine times before it stopped — and I stayed awake listening, in case it started again.*
