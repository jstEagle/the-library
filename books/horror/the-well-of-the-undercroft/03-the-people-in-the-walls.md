## 3. The People in the Walls

The bread was the first clue.

Sable had been standing in the east parlor with her laser measure for eleven minutes, trying to get a reading on a wall that kept returning numbers like a child guessing at arithmetic — 14.2 feet, then 9.8, then 22.1, as though the wall were shrugging — when she caught the smell. Yeast and woodsmoke and something faintly sweet, maybe honey. It drifted down through the floorboards like an apology.

Houses that had been empty for nine years did not smell of baking.

She stood very still, the way you do when you realize the forest has gone quiet around you. The laser measure dangled from her hand, blinking its little red eye at nothing. Somewhere above her — or below her; the acoustics of Hollowmere House had already stopped making promises — came the sound of a chair scraping, quickly, the way furniture moves when someone doesn't want to be heard moving it.

"Okay," Sable said out loud, to the house, to herself, to whatever was listening. "Okay."

She had spent the morning telling herself reasonable things about Wren. Sleep deprivation. Grief wearing a costume. A very committed squatter with excellent makeup skills and access to thirty-year-old photographs. Reasonable things were how she survived; she stacked them like sandbags. But sandbags don't hold back bread smells.

She followed the scent into the kitchen, and the kitchen was wrong in a way she hadn't catalogued yet: warm. The bank's file said no utilities. The bank's file said the gas had been cut in 2016. And yet the old cast-iron range sat there radiating heat like a sleeping animal, and on the counter beside it sat a loaf under a cloth, and beside the loaf sat three cups, and one of them was still steaming.

Three cups. She'd counted Wren. That made two.

Sable Thornbury had appraised two hundred and six properties in her career. She had found squatters twice, raccoons once, and in a farmhouse outside Corben's Mill, a fully operational moonshine distillery run by a retired schoolteacher. She had never once found tea waiting for her.

"I know you're here," she said, hating how thin her voice came out. "I'm not — I'm with the bank. I'm just doing an assessment. I don't care who's living here, I'm not reporting anyone, I just need measurements—"

"You need to stop measuring," said a voice behind her, low and dry as paper. "That's the whole problem with you people. You measure."

* * *

Odile Vasseur was seventy-one years old and moved through Hollowmere's corridors the way water moves through its own bed. She was small, silver-haired, dressed in layers of cardigans that had clearly been mended by someone who loved her badly but sincerely. She did not look frightened of Sable. She looked tired of being right.

They faced each other across the kitchen island, and Odile said, "You're Marianne's girl."

Sable's spine went cold. "My mother died when I was nineteen."

"I know what she cost this house." Odile said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Sit down, child. You look like you're going to fall over, and I've swept up enough of this family off these floors."

"I'm not family."

"No," Odile agreed, pouring tea into the third cup without asking. "You're worse. You're the one they sent because nobody else would come."

"That's not — the bank assigned me randomly—"

"Mm." Odile slid the cup across. "Drink. It's chamomile. It won't help, but it's warm, and warmth is a kind of argument against this place."

Sable drank. It was absurdly good tea. Her hands steadied by degrees she resented.

"The man on the porch," Sable said carefully. "Wren. He's real."

"He's what's left," Odile said. "Of a great many things."

And then, from the direction of the pantry — a door Sable had assumed led to a pantry — came a whisper, urgent and young: *"Odie. Odie, is she one of them? Does she have the clipboard?"*

"It's fine, Ruthie," Odile called, not raising her voice, in the tone of a woman who had said *it's fine, Ruthie* several thousand times. "Come meet her properly or go back to bed."

A pause. Then the pantry door opened — revealing not shelves but a corridor, narrow, whitewashed, lit by a string of salvaged fairy lights — and two children stepped out.

Not children. That was Sable's second correction of the day. They were young adults, maybe twenty-two and twenty-four, but they carried themselves like creatures raised in small spaces: shoulders drawn in, eyes doing constant quiet inventory of exits. The boy was tall and stooped, all wrists and wariness. The girl was smaller, sharp-faced, and she looked at Sable's clipboard the way other people looked at snakes.

"This is Tam and Ruthie," Odile said. "Born here. Both of them. Which makes them the only two people alive who've never been lied to about this house."

"Hi," said Tam, with the desperate politeness of someone who had read about greetings in books.

Ruthie said nothing. She was staring at Sable's left hand, where her grandmother's ring sat — the ring Sable wore without thinking, the way you wear a scar.

"You're a Thornbury," Ruthie said finally. "An actual one." She turned to Odile, and her voice cracked with something between fury and fear. "You said if we waited long enough, one would come back. You didn't say I'd have to *like* it."

* * *

They fed her before they told her anything. Sable would understand later that this was policy, not kindness — that Odile believed no one could hear the truth of Hollowmere on an empty stomach without breaking, and that bread specifically mattered, though she wouldn't say why until much later, and when she did say it, Sable would wish she hadn't.

They ate in the corridor itself, cross-legged on a pallet bed, because — Tam explained, blushing — the rooms felt different when more than two people were in them. "Bigger," he said. "Hungry-bigger."

"The house is a well," Odile began, when the bread was half gone and the tea was cold. "I don't mean that prettily. I mean there is a shaft beneath the undercroft, older than the foundation, older than the county, and everything above it — the walls, the floors, the rooms that won't hold their size — is built over it the way a lid is built over a pot. And the well is not empty, and it is not asleep, and it eats."

"Eats what?" Sable asked, because someone had to.

"People. Slowly, mostly. It starts with the singing."

And there it was — the thing Wren had hummed on the porch, the tune she'd known at six years old and lost at seven, rising in her memory now like a body in dark water. Sable set down her cup very carefully.

"There's a lullaby," she said.

"There's a lullaby," Odile confirmed. "Every household that's owned this land has known some version of it. Mothers sang it to babies. Babies grew up and sang it to their babies. Nobody remembers learning it. That's the first trick — it feels like yours. Like a thing you've always known, the way you know your own name." Her mouth went thin. "It is not yours. You are its."

"And the verses?"

"Six that anyone living knows. My mother knew a seventh fragment and swore there were eight. The song isn't finished, do you understand? It's been unfinished for two hundred years, and the well wants its ending the way a throat wants swallowing. Every generation, someone hears further into it than the last. Someone walks down. And whoever loves them follows, because the song sounds like home, and home is the cruelest word there is."

Tam had gone pale. Ruthie was looking at the floor, jaw tight, hands fisted in her skirt.

"My parents," Tam said quietly, to his bread, "walked down when I was four. Both of them. Same night. I remember the door being open and the stairs being warm." He shrugged, a terrible practiced shrug. "Odie found me in the linen cupboard. Ruthie was already living in the walls by then. Her folks went down before mine."

"We don't say *went down,*" Ruthie snapped. "We say *taken.*"

"They walked, Ruthie."

"*Taken.*"

The silence after that had teeth. Sable sat in it, holding her cooling tea, and understood that she was watching a family argue about the shape of its own wound, and that this argument was load-bearing.

"Why are you still here?" she asked. "All of you. Why not leave?"

Odile laughed — short, bitter, genuine. "Child. Have you tried leaving?"

"I drove here this morning."

"And your car is currently—" Odile tilted her head toward the window, toward the drive Sable had come up at nine a.m., "—where?"

Sable looked. Through the kitchen window, the gravel drive stretched away into trees that had not been there at nine a.m. Trees that were, she realized with a lurch, simply *more trees than there should be*, packed close, patient, arranged like an audience.

"The property line moves," Odile said. "Or rather, it doesn't move, and everything else does. People have walked out of here for days, Sable Thornbury. They arrive back at the front porch, eventually. Tired. Hungrier. And the house always leaves the porch light on for them, because it is *glad.*"

"Wren got out," Sable said. "Thirty years ago. He got out and stayed out."

Something passed across Odile's face — pity, Sable thought, which was unbearable coming from a woman trapped in a haunted house.

"Did he," Odile said softly. "Did he get out. Or did the house put him somewhere it could keep him, and call it outside?"

Sable didn't answer. Her tea had gone completely cold, and she drank it anyway.

* * *

Later — much later, after Odile had shown her the seam in the parlor wainscoting that opened onto the servant corridors, after Ruthie had grudgingly demonstrated the pulley system that brought water up from a spring the house apparently permitted them to use ("it lets us live," Ruthie said flatly. "Think about why"), after Tam had shyly shown her the shelf where he kept forty-one books he'd read eleven times each — Sable lay on a pallet in the corridor with the fairy lights off, listening to the house breathe.

Because it did breathe. She could feel it now that she knew to feel it. A slow swell and settle in the walls, like lungs. Like patience.

In the dark, Ruthie's voice came from the next alcove, barely audible.

"You really didn't know? About any of it?"

"I thought this place was just a house," Sable said. "A bad investment with good bones."

A long pause. Then, reluctantly, almost kindly: "Nobody who grew up here thinks it's just a house. Your mother sang to you. Didn't she. That song. You just forgot."

Sable lay still. And underneath the breathing of the house, underneath the distant tick of cooling timber, she heard it — faint, from far below, patient as tide:

the melody.

Only the first three notes. Over and over. The way you'd tap a glass to see if anyone was home.

"Ruthie," Sable whispered. "How many verses do you know?"

"All six," Ruthie said. "Odie made us learn them so we'd recognize the trap." Another pause, longer. "But Sable — there's something else. Tam's been humming in his sleep again. And last night, I swear on Odie's life—"

The house breathed.

"—he hummed a note I've never heard before. A seventh note."
