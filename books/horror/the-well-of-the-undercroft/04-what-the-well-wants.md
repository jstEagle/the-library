## 4. What the Well Wants

Odile had been born in Hollowmere House in the spring of 1911, which made her either ninety-four years old or eleven, depending on which of her bodies you cared to count. She preferred not to count. Counting led to arithmetic, and arithmetic led to the question of how long she had actually lived in the walls, and that question had teeth.

She watched the newcomer through the gap behind the linen press — a habit she'd kept from girlhood, when watching through gaps was how you learned whether your father was coming home sober. The woman, Sable Thornbury, stood in the nursery with her clipboard held like a shield, measuring a room that refused to be measured.

"Forty-one feet," Sable muttered, for the third time. "The house is thirty-eight feet wide."

"It's showing off," Odile murmured.

Beside her, Ruthie snorted and clapped a hand over her own mouth. Tam, sprawled on the corridor floor with his boots off, grinned up at them both. "She's going to figure it out by supper."

"She'll figure it out and leave," Odile said. "That's what they do. They figure it out, and then they run, and the house hums a little louder until the next one comes." She smoothed her skirt — a real skirt, salvaged from a trunk in the east attic, because if she was going to be a ghost-adjacent woman living in a wall, she would at least be a *dressed* one — and made her decision. "Bring out the bread."

"The good bread?" Tam asked.

"There is only bread, Tam."

* * *

They did it the way they always did it: badly, but with conviction. Ruthie knocked twice on the inside of the pantry wall — their old signal, three knocks meant danger, two meant food, one meant Tam had done something foolish again — and waited while Sable's footsteps went still.

Then Odile opened the little door.

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it for decades, in fact, standing before the tarnished mirror in the servant's passage, practicing an expression she hoped read as *harmless neighbor* rather than *woman who has lived in your walls since the Coolidge administration*. What came out instead was:

"Don't scream. We have bread."

Sable screamed anyway, which Odile felt was fair, and dropped her clipboard, which was less fair, because the clipboard had all their measurements on it and now those measurements were wrong twice over.

* * *

An hour later they sat in the sealed morning room — the one room the house didn't watch closely, for reasons Odile had never understood and had stopped questioning around the same time she stopped questioning everything else — and shared the bread. Sable sat with her back against the door, which Odile noted with approval. Sensible instincts. The wrong door, but sensible instincts.

"Start from the beginning," Sable said. "And I want the version without the part where you're imaginary."

"Oh, we're all quite real," Odile said. "That's rather the tragedy of it." She tore her piece of bread in half, though she wasn't hungry; hunger had become mostly ceremonial down here. "Very well. My father built this house in 1898. He built it *around* something. That's the part no deed will tell you. There was already a well here — older than the county, older than the road, older, I suspect, than the idea of counties. A throat of dark water under the undercroft. And wells like that don't want to be filled. They want to be *sung to*."

"Sung to," Sable repeated flatly.

"You've heard it already. Don't lie to me, dear, it wastes both our evenings. The lullaby. In the walls, in the pipes, in the space between your ears at three in the morning. Every family that has owned this house has heard it. And every generation, one member hears the whole song — every verse but the last — and walks down the cellar stairs willingly. Smiling, usually. That's the worst part. My father went smiling. My mother followed him within the month, and my brother Henri after her, and I would have gone too except—" She stopped. Adjusted her skirt. "Except I was eight, and Ruthie's grandmother pulled me into the wall by my ankle as I was reaching for the cellar door, and I have been here ever since, being saved repeatedly against my will."

"That doesn't sound like being saved," Sable said.

"No," Odile agreed. "It sounds like duty. Which is worse."

Tam raised his hand, because he'd been raised in a wall and had never learned when raising hands was inappropriate. "Explain the last verse part. You always lose people there."

Odile sighed. This was the part she hated, and she hated it in the specific way of a woman who has explained something forty times and watched it fail forty times. "The song has six verses written down. My father wrote them in his ledger — he thought he could master it, catalog it, put it in a drawer with the rest of his disappointments. Six verses. But the song isn't finished. It wants seven. And the seventh verse — the last verse, the one that completes it — has never once been written down. Do you know why?"

"Because it changes?" Sable guessed.

"Because no singer survives to teach it." Odile let that sit in the quiet of the morning room. "Every person who has walked down those stairs has heard the final verse. It's offered to them at the well's edge, in the voices of everyone they've ever loved. And they sing it. Of course they sing it. It's beautiful. It's the most beautiful thing you will ever hear, and it is sung *to you*, in your mother's voice, and it asks only one thing: sing along. Finish it. Complete the song, and the well closes its throat, and everyone rests. That's the promise. And every generation someone believes the promise, and sings, and goes down, and the ones who love them follow, and the verse dies with them at the bottom of the water. Lost again. Waiting again."

"And you think I should finish it," Sable said. Not a question. Her voice had gone very careful, the way voices do when they're holding something heavy.

"I think someone must," Odile said. "Yes."

Ruthie set down her bread. "Mother—"

"I know what I'm saying, Ruthie." Odile kept her eyes on Sable. "Sixty years I've watched this house eat families. I watched my own go down that stairwell one by one, smiling, and I have spent my entire life in the dark listening to it hum, and I am telling you that I am *tired*. Someone has to finish the song. Someone has to walk down there, hear the last verse, and end it — properly, finally, so that Tam can see a street, and Ruthie can grow up past eleven, and I can stop counting years because there won't be any left to count."

"And the person who finishes it?" Sable asked quietly. "What happens to them?"

Odile looked at her hands. They were good hands; they had baked a thousand loaves in a kitchen that didn't exist anymore. "No one knows," she said. "That's the honest answer, and I'll thank you not to look at me like that. No one knows, because no one has come back. But I'll tell you what I believe, since you've shared our bread and bread obligates us both. I believe the song is a debt. I believe the well has been owed a completion for a hundred and twenty-seven years, and it takes a singer each generation as interest. Pay the principal, and the account closes. Whoever pays it — whatever happens to them — they'd be buying freedom for everyone in these walls. Everyone who comes after. That's not a sacrifice, dear. That's an inheritance. And you—" she leaned forward, and her voice gentled into something almost apologetic, "—you're the first one in sixty years the house has *noticed*. It measured itself for you today. Rooms grew to fit you. That's not hospitality. That's a house setting its table."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far below — or perhaps not below at all, perhaps in the walls themselves, in the joists, in the grain of the wood — something began, very faintly, to hum.

Wren's tune. Sable's face did something complicated and young.

"You know it," Odile said softly. "You knew it before you ever came back. Didn't you? It's been following you for thirty years. Little songs in hotel radiators. Melodies in supermarket freezers. It never lets its singers go, Sable. It just waits for them to come home."

"That's enough," said a voice from the doorway, and there was Wren — small, unchanged, furious in the way only the imaginary can be furious, which is completely and without consequence. "You don't get to recruit her. You don't get to dress it up in words like *inheritance*. I've heard you practice this speech, Odile. Through the wall. For weeks."

"And you've done nothing to stop me," Odile said, "because some part of you knows I'm right."

"Some part of me knows you're *scared*," Wren shot back. "Those aren't the same thing, however many decades you've had to confuse them."

The humming swelled, just slightly, pleased by the discord. Odile felt it in her fillings, in her knuckles, in the old break in her ankle where Ruthie's grandmother had grabbed her. The house liked arguments. Arguments were seasoning.

Sable stood. She picked up her clipboard from where it had fallen, hugged it to her chest like a breastplate, and looked around the morning room at the four of them — the woman from 1911, the siblings born in the dark, the friend who shouldn't exist — and Odile watched her arrive at the crossroads with the terrible clarity of someone who has not yet learned that clarity is often just fear wearing glasses.

"Teach me the six verses," Sable said.

"*No*," said Wren.

"Yes," said Odile, and hated how quickly it came out, and hated more that underneath the hate was relief, sweet and shameful as stolen sugar. "First thing tomorrow. Ruthie has the best memory for it. And Sable — one rule above all others, and if you break it, everything I've said tonight is moot. Whatever you hear down there, whoever sings it to you, however much it sounds like home—"

"I know," Sable said. "Never sing along."

"Never sing along," Odile echoed, and beneath the floorboards, patient as interest accruing, the well hummed the first four notes of a song it had been waiting a century to finish.
