## 7. The Undercroft

They were all very loud about how it was a terrible idea, which Sable found oddly touching. In her experience, people only argued that hard when they'd already lost the argument in their heads.

"You can't go down there," Odile said, for the fourth time, wringing a dishcloth as though it had personally betrayed her. "The stairwell doesn't behave. It's not a place, it's a — a negotiation."

"Then I'll negotiate," Sable said. She was packing a bag with the grim competence of someone who had once moved house three times in two years: flashlight, chalk, a coil of rope, four granola bars she suspected she'd need more than the rope.

"With *what*? Manners?"

"With whatever's down there. Tam's down there, Odile. He's sixteen and he's never seen a sky that wasn't through a window, and he walked into the dark because it sang to him in his sleep. Somebody has to go get him before he gets comfortable." She zipped the bag. "That somebody is me, because I'm the one with the clipboard, and the clipboard makes me feel invincible."

Ruthie had gone very quiet by the window, which was worse than the crying had been. She'd cried for a full day, great gulping sobs that the walls had seemed to drink up greedily, and then she'd stopped all at once and started watching the cellar door the way you watch a dog that's bitten someone. Now she crossed the room and pressed something into Sable's hand: a button, brass, worn smooth, with a thread still through the eye of it.

"It was his," Ruthie said. "Off his coat. He won't go far from it. He's a magpie, our Tam. He'll want it back."

Sable closed her fist around it. It was still warm, somehow, or she decided it was, which amounted to the same thing.

"Stay with her," she said to Wren.

Wren, who had been sitting on the stairs looking smaller than a person who hadn't aged in thirty years should look, shook their head. "I'm coming."

"You're barely — you've been fading all week. Every time the house hums, you go grayer."

"Then I'll be quick." Wren stood, and there was the old stubbornness, the six-year-old's stubbornness, the kind that had once insisted a blanket fort could withstand anything. "Sable. You invented me to be brave in the dark. Let me do the job."

Sable wanted to argue. She was, professionally, an excellent arguer. But the house had started humming again that morning — a low, patient note in the floorboards, like a stomach thinking about food — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she did not want to be unaccompanied down there. Not by anyone. Not even by herself.

"Fine," she said. "But you hold my hand on the stairs. Non-negotiable. I've read about this house."

"You haven't read about this house. You're appraising it."

"I've read about houses *like* this house."

* * *

The cellar door was oak, and it was warm.

That was the detail that nearly turned her around — not the dark beyond it, not the smell of wet stone and something older than wet stone, but the simple fact that the door felt like a sleeping animal's flank. Sable put her palm flat against it and felt, or imagined she felt, a slow pulse underneath.

"It knows you're coming," Wren said softly.

"Good. I want it to know I'm coming with a flashlight and a bad attitude."

She opened the door.

The stairwell was narrower than it should have been and went down farther than the house had depth, which was, she was learning, simply how Hollowmere did arithmetic. Her flashlight beam hit stone steps, then more stone steps, then a darkness that swallowed the light the way sand swallows water. The walls were close enough to touch on both sides. She touched them. It seemed polite.

Behind her, the cellar door swung shut with a sound like a held breath.

"Okay," she said, mostly to hear a voice. "Okay. Stairwell. Let's talk about the stairwell."

"Odile told you," Wren said. "It lengthens when you're alone. It shortens when — " Wren hesitated.

"When accompanied," Sable said. "When loved, was the word she used, and I saw you both wince, so let's not pretend it's a mystery. The house runs on love the way my landlord's building runs on a boiler held together with hope."

She started down. She counted steps, because counting was a professional habit and because numbers were a rope you could throw yourself. Forty steps. Sixty. The air got colder and wetter, and the humming in the walls got warmer, and these two facts refused to coexist, and Sable decided not to think about either.

At a hundred steps she stopped and did something she'd been turning over since the porch: she talked to Wren. Not about the house. About nothing. About the summer she was seven and they'd dug a hole behind the garden shed and called it a mine and filled it with bottle caps. About the way Wren had pronounced "archaeologist" — "arky-ologist" — with such authority that Sable had used the wrong word for a decade. About the time her mother had found them talking to the empty air and hadn't laughed, hadn't scolded, had just looked at Sable with an expression Sable had spent thirty years failing to identify and now, descending into a haunted stairwell, recognized as fear.

"You remember the bottle caps," Wren said, wonder in their voice, as though Sable had recited scripture.

"Of course I remember. I remember everything about you. That's the whole problem with me."

The stairwell shivered.

It was not dramatic. No groan, no lurch. The steps simply *agreed* with each other more. Sable's counting, which had begun to feel like counting stars, suddenly felt like counting stairs — the next landing arrived when it should, the turn came where her body expected it. The dark ahead seemed closer, friendlier, less like a mouth and more like a hallway.

"It's working," Wren whispered. "Keep talking."

"About what?"

"Anything. Everything. The house is listening for whether anyone would miss you."

Sable's throat went tight. She thought about saying something brave and instead said the true thing: "I missed *you*. Thirty years, and I told myself I'd invented you because I was lonely, and then I got un-lonely, or I told myself I did, and I still — " Her voice caught on the wet stone air. "I still set two places at the table in my head. Every night. For forty years of nights. I thought that was a quirk. It turns out it was a *door*."

The stairwell shortened again, so sharply she nearly stumbled. Steps she hadn't climbed yet arrived under her feet like favors. The walls drew back half an inch, respectful.

"Don't say things like that here," Wren said, but their voice was shaking in a way that wasn't fear.

"Why not?"

"Because it's listening. And because it's true, and true things are *loud* down here."

* * *

The undercroft opened like a held note.

Sable had expected a cellar. She got a cathedral. The flashlight couldn't find the ceiling; the beam simply gave up, its light thinning to nothing overhead like smoke. The floor was stone worn smooth in a wide circle, polished by — she made herself not think about what had polished it. And at the center, sunk into the floor with no rim, no railing, no courtesy, was the well.

It was not a hole. It was a *throat*. Dark water sat far below, so far that her beam couldn't find the surface, only a blackness that seemed to sit slightly closer than blackness should. The air above it moved. Not a draft. A breathing.

Sable stood at the edge and did the thing you must never do in a story like this.

She spoke.

"Tam?"

"Tam?" said the well.

It was her own voice. Exactly her own voice — the flat professional vowels, the slight crack she hated. But it came back *wrong*, came back a half-second early, came back before she'd finished, so that for one vertiginous moment she was speaking in unison with herself, and herself was winning.

"Tam?" she tried again, carefully, one word at a time.

"Tam?" the well agreed, ahead of her, patient as a mirror that had learned ambition.

"It echoes," Wren breathed. "But it's — Sable, it's *ahead* of you. It's not repeating. It's *predicting*."

"Then it already knows what I'm going to say."

"Then don't say it."

But Sable had spent her whole life believing that the way through a locked thing was to understand it, and she was not going to stop now, at the bottom of the world, with a brass button in her pocket and her oldest friend going gray beside her. She crouched at the well's edge — close enough to feel a faint cool breath on her face, like the house exhaling through its one open window — and she said, distinctly:

"My name is Sable Thornbury. I'm here for Tam."

The well said, "My name is Sable Thornbury. I'm here for Tam." And then it said, in her voice, in the pause where she hadn't yet decided to speak: "He's waiting."

Her blood went cold and then hot. "He's *there*?"

"He's there," the well said, ahead of her again, and this time the echo had a smile in it, and the smile was hers, borrowed, worn like a coat. "He's been waiting. He's so tired, Sable. He wants his sister. He wants the button. He wants — " and here the well's voice slid, smooth as oil finding a crack, into a melody. Three notes. Four. The first bar of the lullaby, hummed in Sable's own voice, and Sable felt her lips part, felt the tune *fit* somewhere under her ribs the way a key fits a lock that's been in the door your whole life —

"*Don't*," Wren said, and gripped her hand, and the grip was cold and small and absolutely real.

Sable shut her mouth. Her teeth ached with the unsung notes.

"It's going to offer me the song," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Down here, at the edge. That's the design. That's what Odile said — nobody's pushed. Everybody's *invited*."

"Yes."

"Then we don't listen. We shop." She stood up, brushed off her knees, and raised her voice at the dark water with the brisk cheer of a woman reading terms and conditions aloud in a bank manager's office. "Tam! If you can hear me — and I know you can, because everything in this house can hear everything, it's the nosiest building in England — I've got your button. Brass. Coat thread. Ruthie's been sleeping in your coat, by the way, which is disgusting and I respect it. Come get it."

The silence that followed was enormous.

And then, from somewhere past the well, from a darkness her flashlight couldn't argue with, a boy's voice — thin, cracked, sixteen years old and older than that — said:

"You came *down*?"

"Against unanimous professional advice," Sable said. "It's sort of my whole thing."

A sound that was half laugh, half sob. Footsteps, or the memory of footsteps. And the well began, softly, in her own voice, a half-second ahead of her heart, to hum.

Sable took Wren's hand tighter, and stepped around the throat of the house, toward the sound of a boy who had never seen the sky — and the humming stopped.

Because something down there had realized, all at once, that she had brought a second voice into the dark.
