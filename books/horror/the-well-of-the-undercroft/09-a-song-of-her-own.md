## 9. A Song of Her Own

The well had Sable's mother's voice now, and it was using it cruelly well.

*Come down, little bird,* it sang, in the warm alto that had once coaxed her out of nightmares. *Come down where the water remembers. Finish what the family started. Finish what the family—*

"Stop it," Sable whispered. Her throat ached. The worst part — the part she would never tell anyone, not Odile, not Ruthie, not even Wren — was that some small, starving corner of her wanted to. The melody was a hand at the small of her back. The final verse was right there, hovering just beyond hearing, the way a sneeze hovers, the way a name on the tip of the tongue hovers, and all she had to do was lean.

The water leaned with her.

Below the ledge where she crouched, the well was not water exactly. It was a throat of black glass that reflected nothing — not the lantern, not her face, not even the dark. It only reflected sound. When she breathed, it breathed back a half-second early. When her heart stuttered, it stuttered first. It had been rehearsing her for thirty years.

*Almost,* said her mother's voice, gentle as Sunday. *Almost, sweetheart. You always did hate leaving a song unfinished.*

"That's the whole trick, isn't it," Sable said aloud, and her voice cracked on the last word, and the well drank the crack like cream.

* * *

Above her, at the top of the stairwell that had shortened for her going down, Odile stood with both hands pressed flat against the cellar door, holding it open against a pressure that had no business pushing on wood.

"She's at the water," Odile said. Her voice was very steady, the way a rope is steady while it holds a falling thing. "I can feel the house listening. It's listening through my teeth."

"Then we go get her." Ruthie was already moving, lantern swinging, her brother's absence hanging off her shoulders like a coat too heavy to take off. "We go get her and we get Tam and we—"

"Ruthie." Odile caught her arm. "If we go down there singing nothing, we're just more verses. That's how it works. That's how it has *always* worked. It calls, and we answer, and answering is the whole of the trap."

"Then what do we do?"

From the shadows of the sealed corridor, a small voice said: "We sing something else."

Wren stood in the doorway of the hidden passage, and she looked terrible. The house had been humming through the walls for three days, and each hum had taken something out of her — color first, then weight, then the particular brightness around the eyes that made her look like Wren and not like a memory of Wren. She was translucent at the edges now, like paper held up to a window.

"It can't digest what it didn't ask for," Wren said. "It's a well. Wells receive. It's been receiving the same song for two hundred years, the same verses, the same devotion, the same *yes*. It doesn't know how to swallow a no."

"It's never been offered one," Odile said slowly.

"It's never been offered *anything else*." Wren's hands were shaking. She clasped them together to make it stop, the way she used to when they were six and the thunder was loud. "Sable used to make up songs. Terrible ones. About the dog next door. About vegetables. She'd sing them at me until I begged for mercy." A ghost of a smile crossed the fading face. "I never begged. I loved them."

* * *

At the well's edge, Tam was standing in the black water to his knees, and he was smiling.

He looked, Sable thought with a lurch, like a man coming home. He had never had a home. He had been born in a wall, in a house that ate families, and the well was offering him the only belonging he'd ever been shown: the sweet, sinking certainty of finishing something.

"Tam." She said it quietly, because loud things fed it. "Tam, look at me."

"I hear the end of it," he said wonderingly. "Sable, I hear the *end*. It's like — it's like the last piece of a puzzle. It's like putting your cold hands in warm water. It's like—"

"Tam, it's like nothing. It's a hole in the ground that learned to sing. It doesn't want you. It wants your *yes*."

His foot lifted. The water made no ripple. The well echoed his heartbeat, slightly ahead of it, and Sable understood with terrible clarity that it was not waiting for him to decide. It was deciding for him, one borrowed beat at a time.

So she did the only thing left. The graceless thing. The human thing.

She opened her mouth and sang.

* * *

It was not a good song. It was not a song at all, really. It was a mortgage.

*"I've got a thirty-year fixed rate and a leaky kitchen sink,"* Sable sang, off-key, desperate, loud, *"and the appraisal's due Friday and the boiler smells like — something — and I don't even WANT this house, you overgrown cistern, I want a small apartment with bad water pressure and a cat—"*

The well shuddered.

It was a small shudder at first, a hiccup in the echo, the sound of a throat encountering a bone it could not swallow. The water recoiled from Tam's knees. His foot came down on stone instead of song.

*"And I burn the toast,"* she pressed on, her voice cracking, her eyes stinging, *"every single morning, because I'm thinking about other things, and the smoke alarm goes off and the neighbors hate me, and I stay anyway. I STAY. That's the verse. That's the whole verse. Nobody wrote it down because nobody had to — you just get up and you burn the toast again and you stay—"*

*"You can't,"* said the well, in her mother's voice, and for the first time in two hundred years, the voice sounded afraid. *"It has to be the verse. It has to be the—*

"It has to be nothing," said Wren, from the stair.

* * *

She came down the stairs like weather — small and fading and absolutely unstoppable, with Ruthie behind her and Odile behind Ruthie, and all three of them singing, if you could call it that.

Wren sang about a dog named Biscuit who was afraid of the vacuum. Ruthie sang about the bread she'd learned to bake in the walls, and how the oven ran hot on the left side. Odile, weeping without any shame at all, sang about her mother's garden and the roses that never grew and how she'd hated them and missed them anyway. None of it rhymed. Half of it didn't scan. Ruthie lost her place twice and made up words both times.

The well could not swallow it.

The black water began to churn, and where it churned it made sound, and the sound it made was every voice it had ever taken, trying to harmonize with a song about burnt toast, and failing, and failing, and the failing was the loudest thing Sable had ever heard.

"Tam!" Ruthie lunged and caught her brother's wrist. "Tam, come *on*, we're going somewhere with an oven that runs even—"

He came. He came stumbling and weeping and looking back at the water with a homesickness that broke Sable's heart, because the well had shown him a home and it had been a lie, and Ruthie was hauling him toward a true one that smelled of yeast and lamp oil and other people.

"UP," Odile shouted. "Everyone UP, it's coming apart—"

She was right. The house had begun to groan.

* * *

The groan was not like a building settling. It was like a building *grieving* — a long, deep, structural moan that started in the undercroft and climbed the walls like water finding its level. Plaster rained down the stairwell. The stairs themselves shuddered under their feet, and Sable felt, absurdly, the house measuring them one last time, the stair lengthening now out of spite, out of drowning panic, out of a well's last attempt to keep its verses.

"Sing!" Wren gasped. "Keep singing! It can't hold the stairs and swallow the song!"

So they sang up the stairs, all four of them, the worst choir in the history of Hollowmere. Sable sang about the bank and its forms in triplicate. Tam, voice breaking, sang about the moon, which he had seen only through a vent, and how he was going to see it from *outside*, from the actual sky, and Ruthie harmonized badly and Odile not at all because she was laughing and crying in equal measure, and Wren — Wren sang the verse about Biscuit the dog again, louder, because it was hers, because Sable had made it for her when they were six, because love that gets made up on the spot is the one thing no well can hold.

The cellar door burst outward ahead of them. They burst outward after it.

They made the front hall as the chandelier came down. They made the porch as the windows blew inward in a long glittering sigh, and the sound that came out of the house behind them was the sound of two hundred years of swallowed *yeses* finally, finally being given back — a chorus of the vanished, rising up through the flooding undercroft, through the groaning floors, through the collapsing roof, and out into the night air where nobody had ever let them go before.

Sable turned on the lawn and looked back.

Hollowmere House was folding. Not burning, not exploding — folding, like a paper thing, like a throat closing, the walls drawing in on the well at their heart as the well drowned in a song it could not digest. And in the last moment before the roof came down, Sable would have sworn — would always swear — that she saw them. Figures at the windows. A woman with a garden's worth of roses in her arms. A man holding a lantern high. Dozens of them, pale and unhurried, stepping out through walls that had stopped being walls.

The house came down in a great soft roar of dust and dark.

When the dust cleared, there was a hole in the earth where the undercroft had been, and the hole was filling with ordinary groundwater, and it was not singing.

Tam was on his knees in the wet grass, looking up. Ruthie was beside him, gripping his hand hard enough to hurt, both of them staring at the moon with their mouths open like it was the first time.

It was.

Odile sat down right where she stood, in the weeds, and laughed until she cried.

And Wren — Wren stood at Sable's side, watching the ruin of the house that had made her, and her edges had stopped fading. She was, if anything, a little more solid than she'd been in thirty years. She reached over and took Sable's hand, and her grip was warm.

"You sang so badly," Wren said, with enormous tenderness. "It was perfect."

Sable opened her mouth to answer — and stopped.

Because there, at the treeline at the edge of the overgrown lawn, stood a row of pale figures in the moonlight. Not walking toward the house. Not walking away. Just standing, in the manner of people who have been somewhere a very long time and are not at all sure what the world outside is like.

One of them raised a hand — a small, uncertain wave.

"Oh," Sable said softly. "Oh, we're not done, are we."

Wren squeezed her hand. "No," she said. "I don't think we are."
