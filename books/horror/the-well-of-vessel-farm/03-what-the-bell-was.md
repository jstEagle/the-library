## 3. What the Bell Was

The rain had stopped by morning, and Vessel Farm lay rinsed and gleaming under a sky the color of old pewter. Oscar walked to school early, taking the long way past the church, because he had begun to understand that in this village you learned things by walking past them slowly.

St. Brannoc's was a small grey building with a squat tower, and it took him three passes to see what was wrong with it. There was no bell. Not merely no bell ringing—no bell at all. The belfry's louvred windows stood open on darkness, and where the headstock should have shown through them there was only an empty oak beam, polished smooth as a banister, as if something had hung from it for centuries and then been taken away yesterday.

He asked Mrs. Halloway about it when he arrived, setting his coat on its hook in the little vestibule that smelled of chalk and coal smoke. She was arranging the infants' paint pots, her back to him, and for a moment—a half-second—he thought she did not intend to answer at all.

"There hasn't been a bell since 1911," she said pleasantly. "Melted down for the war effort."

"The war effort? In 1911?"

She turned, and her smile was as warm as ever, but it arrived a moment after her eyes did, like thunder after lightning. "The munitions drive. It was very patriotic of the parish." She handed him the attendance book. "You'll find we're not a village that dwells on the past, Mr. Vasquez. That's one of our virtues."

*One of our virtues.* He wrote it down that evening in his own notebook, because it had the ring of a sentence rehearsed.

* * *

Edith Crane lived in the last cottage before the moor road, behind a wall of fuchsia so overgrown it seemed the house was being swallowed whole. Oscar found her on the step shelling peas into a blue enamel bowl, and she looked up at him with the frank, unafraid curiosity of the very old.

"You're the new schoolmaster," she said. "Sit down, then. My legs are past standing and gossiping at once."

He sat on the bench beside her. For a while they shelled peas together, or rather she did, his hands resting idle on his knees, and the lane was so quiet he could hear the fat click of each pod splitting.

"I asked Mrs. Halloway about the church bell," he said. "She said it was melted down in 1911."

Edith's hands went still. Not dramatically—she simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and looked out across her garden at nothing.

"Did she say why?"

"The war effort."

"Hm." A long breath through the nose. "I was six years old in 1911. I remember it better than I remember last Tuesday." She set down the bowl. "It wasn't the war, young man. The war came three years after, and by then the bell was already slag. They melted it in the spring, in a pit behind the smithy, and my father carried me on his shoulders to watch, because everyone was made to watch. All of them. Every soul in the parish, and if you didn't come, they knew, and being known was—" she searched for the word, and chose it carefully— "unhealthy."

"Why would a village melt its own bell?"

"Because it rang," Edith said. "When it shouldn't have."

The wind moved through the fuchsia hedge, and somewhere far off a crow said one flat syllable and thought better of it.

"My grandmother told me this, and her mother before her told her, so take it as you take all such things. The bell was cast in 1743, and it was a good bell, a true bell. But a true bell doesn't only mark the hours. It marks *other* things. Deaths that haven't happened yet. Debts that have. And in 1911 it began to ring at midnight—not cracked, not broken, just ringing, nine strokes every night, regular as breathing, and no hand near it. Nine strokes. Do you know what else comes in nines here?"

Oscar thought of the register, the razored pages, the October gaps. "No," he said, which was a lie, and Edith looked at him with such mild, terrible kindness that he understood she knew it was.

"The founding families," she said. "Nine names on the parish roll when the well was first walled, back before anyone alive can remember properly. The bell counted them. And the village—my father's generation, my grandfather's—they sat up nights listening to it count, and they got afraid, and afraid men do stupid thorough things. So they took the bell down and melted it, thinking a thing unheard is a thing undone." Her mouth twisted. "But you can't melt a debt, Mr. Vasquez. You can only agree to pay it another way. That's what 1911 was. Not patriotism. Bookkeeping."

"What kind of agreement?"

She picked up the pea bowl again, and her hands resumed their work, steady now, almost serene. "The count," she said. "That's what my grandmother called it. One family, chosen fair and square by whatever rules the well keeps, given over whole—and in exchange, the rest of us get good harvests, healthy children, quiet graves. No fever. No blight. No bad luck worth mentioning. Nineteen-eleven to now, and I'll tell you straight: I've buried everyone I love, but I've buried them *old*, in their beds, and there's not many places in this world where you can say that." She looked at him sidelong. "It's a bargain, right enough. Just ask what's on the other side of it before you sign."

"And the bell? If it was melted—"

"It rings anyway," Edith said simply. "Not often. Only when the count turns. Midnight, across the fields, and no bell in any tower to ring it. Folk pull their curtains and don't speak of it, same as they don't speak of most things." She tipped the last of the peas into his hands, an odd small ceremony, closing the audience. "You've been asking questions at the school. Questions are fine. But mind you don't start *counting*, Mr. Vasquez. Counting's how it finds you interesting."

* * *

He walked home along the moor road with the light failing, telling himself the usual things. Old woman, old stories. Isolated communities metabolized guilt into folklore; he'd read the anthropology. The razored register pages were damp and mice. Tam Aldridge's empty desk was truancy, or worse, and the chalkboard tallies at the farm were a family's private grief arithmetic, nothing more.

The fog came up off the low fields as he walked, not creeping but *rising*, as though the land itself were exhaling, and by the time he reached the stile above the schoolhouse the world had shrunk to a grey room twenty yards wide with him alone in the middle of it.

Midnight found him awake, lamp still burning over his lesson plans, when the sound came.

At first he took it for the wind in the belfry-less tower, a resonance, an architectural trick—the way empty buildings make noises that seem meaningful. But the wind was not blowing. The fog lay on the fields like a held breath. And the tolling came again: deep, bronze-dark, patient, each stroke swelling out of nowhere and dying into nowhere, and beneath it he felt rather than heard a lower note, a hum in the floorboards, in the water pipe, in his own sternum, as if the ground itself were the bell and everything built upon it merely its clapper.

He counted without meaning to. He hated himself for counting.

Nine strokes.

Then silence, total, the silence of a held note finally released, and in it he heard—or believed he heard, and would spend the rest of his life deciding which—the faintest sound of many voices beneath the earth, not screaming, not weeping, but murmuring, the way a congregation murmurs when it waits for the service to begin.

Oscar sat until the lamp burned low. Then he took out his notebook and, beneath *one of our virtues*, wrote a single line:

*The bell counts. Something must be counted.*

And below that, because his hand moved before his caution could stop it:

*Tam Aldridge. Present.*

In the morning he would go to the cellar. He already knew that the way you know a stair is there in the dark—by the certainty of falling toward it.
