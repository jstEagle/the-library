## 6. Fog Over the Fields

The fog came in off the low fields on the third morning, and with it came the certainty that Oscar Vasquez had exactly three nights left to do something, or do nothing, forever.

He had counted the days the way the Aldridges counted them—chalk marks on a slate, one stroke per morning, the tally growing like a wound that would not close. Three strokes now. He stood at his classroom window watching the fog eat the hedgerows, and he understood, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own sentence, that the village had begun to watch him back.

Mrs. Halloway had not mentioned the ledger. That was the thing. She had caught him in the cellar with the count open in his hands, and she had spoken to him gently, reasonably, the way one speaks to a child who has found something in a drawer that was not meant for him—and then she had simply gone on teaching fractions as though the world beneath the schoolhouse were not full of waiting. No threat. No warning. Only a warmth that had acquired, overnight, the texture of a held breath.

He marked the other things instead, the small betrayals of a small place. The postman who had always raised a hand to him now looked through him. The woman at the shop counted his change without meeting his eyes. And on the lane between the school and his lodgings, he had begun to find the same three men at the same hours—never together, never speaking, just present, the way furniture is present. One leaned on the gate by the chapel. One stood at the crossroads where the bus from Ashford used to stop. One walked his dog past the schoolhouse at four o'clock every afternoon, and the dog, a placid brown thing, always stopped and stared at the well-cover behind the playground and would not be pulled away.

The bus. That was the first fact he had to accept: there would be no bus. He had checked the timetable pinned in the village shop, then walked the six miles to the crossroads at dawn to see for himself. The stop was a rusted pole with a faded sign, and beside it, on the morning the Aldridges might have stood waiting, one of the watchers was already there, smoking, patient as weather. The route was watched. Perhaps it had always been watched. Perhaps the bus company itself was part of the arithmetic—Oscar no longer found that thought paranoid. In Vessel Farm, everything was part of the arithmetic.

The roads, then. But the roads were remembered. That was the phrase Edith Crane had used, weeks ago, when he'd asked why no one ever drove out after dark. *The roads are remembered, love. They don't forget who leaves.* He had taken it for the poetry of the old. He did not take it for poetry now.

So: three nights, no bus, remembered roads, and a family of five in a farmhouse whose windows had begun, he noticed on his visits, to be watched the way the well was watched. He would have to move them himself, in a vehicle, on a night when the fog was thick enough to drown the lanterns he knew would rise along the hedgerows. And he would need help, because a man alone cannot drive a truck and watch six directions of dark at once.

There was exactly one person in Vessel Farm who might help him, and she had already, in a way, asked to.

* * *

Edith Crane's cottage sat at the end of the last lane, where the village gave up and the fields began. Oscar came to her after dark, on the second night, having spent the day teaching long division to children whose names were written in his register in his own careful hand, knowing that in three nights some of those names would be razored out of every record in the parish, and the paper would heal over the wound as if the names had never been.

She opened the door before he knocked.

"I saw you coming up the lane," she said. "Been watching you decide for two days, Mr. Vasquez. Come in before the fog gets in with you."

The cottage smelled of coal smoke and dried lavender. On the mantel stood a photograph of a man in a flat cap, squinting into a sun that had probably been real once. Edith poured tea without asking, and her hands, he noticed, were steady in the way of hands that had decided something and would not be argued out of it.

"You've been to the cellar," she said.

It was not a question, so he did not insult her with a denial. "You knew."

"I've known since the day you arrived and asked about the October pages." She sat down across from him, small and upright in her chair, her eyes wet and fierce at once. "I've been waiting for you to come here. I thought you'd come sooner. I thought maybe you'd be like the others—the ones who learn and then learn to live with it. There's been four of you in my lifetime. Teachers. They all stayed. They all taught right up to the count and then they looked the other way, and the village was kind to them for it. Kind as you like."

"And if I look the other way?"

"Then you'll live a long time here, and you'll be warm, and everyone will love you." She said it without contempt, which was worse. "And every seven years you'll hear the bell, and you'll know what the warmth is made of."

Oscar set down his tea. His mouth was dry. "The Aldridges. You know it's their turn."

"I know it's been their turn since before Tam was born. That's how it works. It's not chosen, see—that's the lie they tell themselves, that the well *chooses*, as if that made it holy. It's a rota. A rota, Mr. Vasquez. My husband's family was on it, his grandfather's generation. The Cranes went down the well in 1925, all but my husband's father, who was away at sea, which is why there was a Crane to marry me at all." Her voice did not break. It had been broken a long time ago and had healed around the break, like a tree around wire. "I've paid my tuppence into that village my whole life. I've smiled at Halloway. I've said nothing. And every October I count the fog like the Aldridges count their days, and I do nothing, and I am eighty-one years old and I am *done* doing nothing."

"The roads are watched," Oscar said. "The bus stop is watched. The lanes—"

"The lanes are remembered," she said. "But my Tom's truck is not remembered, because Tom drove it for forty years and everyone's so used to it they don't see it. It's in the barn. It starts. It holds diesel. And the back road over Crane's Hill—" she almost smiled, "—the road that's named for my family, that the village forgot to watch because the family that gave it its name went into the ground—that road comes out at the Ashford turnpike, and it's not on the count's map, because the count's map is old, and the old ones don't look up."

Oscar felt something in his chest unclench for the first time in weeks. "Edith. If they find out—"

"They won't find out. They'll find *you*, maybe, in the end. They'll find what you did. But they won't find the truck, because after you've gone I'll drive it into the flooded quarry and tell them Tom's old wreck finally died, and I'll weep at the funeral of a truck and they'll believe me, because I've been believing them for eighty years and they've got used to it." She reached over and gripped his wrist, her fingers thin and hard as roots. "Listen to me. You're going to fail if you go tonight with a plan. You'll fail if you go with courage. You'll only manage it if you go with *meanness*—with the mean, low cunning of a woman who's hidden things from these people her whole life. Go tomorrow night, not the last night. Go while they're still watching the last night, waiting for you to run on the last night. They think like the ledger thinks: everything in its page, everything in its order. So be out of order."

* * *

He spent the next day teaching.

That was the part he could not have explained to anyone from the world outside—the ordinary obscenity of it. He marked sums. He heard readers. Tam Aldridge, restored to his desk since the family's surrender had not yet been formalized, worked through a page of fractions with his tongue between his teeth, and when the bell rang for home time—for the school bell, the small hand-bell, the innocent one—Tam looked up at Oscar with an expression no nine-year-old should have had to build.

"Sir," he said, while the others clattered out. "My mum says if there's a way, we take it. She says even a bad way."

"It's a good way," Oscar heard himself say. "It's a mean, low, out-of-order way. Be at your window at half nine tomorrow night. One knock on the glass, no lamp. Bring nothing you'd stop to pick up if you dropped it."

Tam nodded, solemn as a communicant, and ran out into the fog.

* * *

The truck was older than Oscar, green once, its cab smelling of oil and dog and forty years of a dead man's tobacco. Edith had it running with three turns of the crank, and the sound of the engine in the fog-bound yard was the loudest thing Oscar had heard in Vessel Farm that was not the bell.

She pressed the keys into his hand and then, unexpectedly, a folded oilcloth packet. Inside: a torch, a flask, a road map of Kent with the Ashford turnpike inked in her dead husband's hand, and a small tin of boiled sweets.

"For the children," she said. "Tom always kept sweets for the children. He'd have wanted—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "I want you to understand what I'm giving you, Mr. Vasquez. It's not a truck. It's my guilt. I've carried it since 1953, when I stood in this yard and watched the Halloway of that day walk the Marsh family down the lane, and I closed my curtains, and I made my tea, and I lived. You take the guilt out of this village tonight, and you leave me the truck to drown and the lie to tell, and that's the bargain, and I'll keep my side of it if you keep yours."

"And your side is?"

"Get them over Crane's Hill. All of them. Don't be noble halfway, don't go back for anything, don't stop for lanterns. The lanterns aren't people, not really, not on count nights. They're the village wearing people. And if the worst comes—" her grip tightened on his hand, "—if the worst comes, you drive into the fog and you don't look at the faces. Promise me."

"I promise."

She looked at him for a long moment in the yellow light of the yard lamp, this young man from the world of timetables and registers, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod once, satisfied or resigned.

"Half nine," she said. "The fog will be at its thickest tomorrow. It always is, on the second-to-last night. They think the fog is theirs." Her mouth twisted. "It was never theirs. It's just the county breathing. Go home, Mr. Vasquez. Sleep if you can. And when you hear anything in the night—a bell, a knocking, a voice calling your name from the lane—"

"Yes?"

"Don't answer it. Nothing in Vessel Farm that calls you by name after dark is calling you kindly."

* * *

He walked back through the fog with the oilcloth packet under his coat and the keys in his fist, and the village was silent around him in the way it was always silent now—a listening silence, a held-breath silence. At the crossroads the watcher was there, cigarette glowing, and Oscar nodded to him as he passed, and the man nodded back, and neither of them broke the fiction that they were two men in a village at night, and nothing else.

At his lodgings he laid the map on his bed and traced the route with his finger until he could see it with his eyes shut: the back lane, the ford, the rise of Crane's Hill, the turnpike. Nine miles. Twenty minutes if the truck held. He packed a bag he could carry at a run. He wrote a letter of resignation, dated three days hence, and left it where Mrs. Halloway would find it only if everything failed—a small lie of paperwork, a page that would heal over the wound.

And then, at midnight, as he sat in the dark with his boots still on, the bell rang.

Not the school hand-bell. Not any bell that existed. It came across the fields from the direction of the church that had no bell, deep and bronze and patient, one toll, then another, counting something in the dark—and Oscar sat with his keys in his hand and did not answer it, and did not move, and understood that it was not ringing for the Aldridges.

It was ringing early.

It was ringing for him.

Tomorrow night, half nine, the fog, the truck, the hill. He lay down in his clothes and watched the ceiling go grey, and somewhere below the village, below the schoolhouse, below the well, something old and patient turned a page and began, gently, to read his name.
