## 8. Odile's Ledger

The ledger lived in a fireproof box under the floorboards of Odile Vann's kitchen, beneath the loose tile she'd been meaning to regrout since 1994, and she had never once told anyone it was there.

She thought about that now, standing in her own doorway with the wind worrying at the eaves and Coral Ishikawa on her porch looking like a man who hadn't slept in a week and had decided, with some dignity, to stop pretending otherwise.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you," he said. "I've been working at it."

He was holding two paper cups of coffee from the gas station by the reservoir road, which in Harrow Glen passed for hospitality. She took one because refusing would have required conversation, and conversation was what she'd spent forty years avoiding, and here she was about to break every rule she'd built her life out of.

"Come in," she said. "Before the town sees you on my porch and starts talking."

"What would they say?"

"That I've finally gone strange." She stepped aside to let him pass. "I'd rather they think I went strange without witnesses."

* * *

Her house sat at the end of Quill Street, the last before the road gave up and became gravel and then became a suggestion and then became the water. It had been a Vann house for five generations, which meant it had been a house that kept things: ledgers, letters, silences, one sealed room upstairs nobody opened, and a kitchen table scarred with rings from forty years of mugs set down carelessly by people who were no longer alive to be scolded about it.

Odile cleared a space among the tax files and seed catalogs and set the fireproof box on the wood table with both hands, the way you'd set down something sleeping.

Coral watched her work the combination lock. "Is this about Wren?"

"It's about everyone," she said. "Wren included. Eventually."

The lock gave. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp that had never once gotten through but that she checked anyway, twice a year, like visiting a grave — inside lay a ledger. Not a grand thing. A school composition book, the kind sold three for a dollar at the drugstore, its cover soft as cloth from handling. And behind it, stacked in date order, eleven more just like it.

Coral went very still. She recognized the stillness. It was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want finished.

"How many?" he asked.

"Forty years," Odile said. "Give or take. The first entry is 1983. A girl named Tamsin Reeve, seventeen, last seen walking home along the reservoir road on the fourth of January. Her boots were found on the ice, side by side, laces tied."

"Tied?"

"People leave their boots," Odile said. "That's the part nobody wants to sit with. Forty years, and not one of them fought. No scratches on the ice. No torn coats caught on fence wire. They take their boots off first, most of them. Like they're being polite."

She opened the first ledger. The handwriting inside was her own, younger, more certain of itself, each entry ruled off in careful columns: name, age, date last seen, location, weather, ice conditions, whether the family stayed in Harrow Glen afterward or left. In the margin, small annotations had accumulated over the decades like silt.

*Boots tied. Ice solid to four feet. No hole found.*

*Third disappearance within sight of the Hollis house. The Hollis mirror faces the road.*

*Frost on the INSIDE of the Kowalczyk windows. Checked twice.*

*Song heard by Mrs. Adeyemi, three nights before her husband walked out. She hummed it for me once. I have not slept properly since.*

Coral read over her shoulder. He didn't touch the pages. Good, she thought. People always wanted to touch. As if the past were a cat that might let them.

"Nobody else knows this exists?" he asked.

"The sheriff knows disappearances happen. The sheriff's office has files. What the files don't have is the pattern, because the pattern requires you to write down things like *frost on the inside of the windows* and expect to keep your job." She turned a page. "My grandmother started the practice, actually. Not the ledger — the watching. She used to say the town had a leak, and somebody ought to know where the water line was. I made it formal. I'm an archivist by training. Cataloguing is the only prayer I trust."

"And the pattern is?"

"Winter. Always deep winter, January through March, when the reservoir freezes hard enough to walk on. Water or glass — every single one of them was last seen near open water, or near a mirror, or in a house where the mirrors had started misbehaving. And—" she hesitated, then gave it to him straight, because he'd earned it by lifting a casket lid and staying in town anyway, "—and singing. Or humming. Nearly half of them had been heard humming in the week before. Same tune. Nobody could name it. Nobody could stop."

Coral set his coffee down without drinking. "The hymn. From the house. Under the boiler noise."

"You've heard it." It wasn't a question. His face answered it anyway. "Then you know it's real, and I don't have to feel foolish saying the next part."

"Say it anyway."

Odile closed the first ledger and rested her hand flat on the stack of them, all twelve, forty years of the dead filed in dollar-store notebooks.

"The founding story — you'll have heard some version from someone by now. The choir. The valley. The flooding in 1911. Five families signed the dam papers, mine first, my great-grandfather's signature at the top like he was proud of it." Her voice did something complicated around the word *proud*, and smoothed itself out. "The story says the choir drowned when the waters rose faster than promised. The story does not say what the five families did afterward, which was hire a man from out of county — a music teacher, a Mr. Ellery Voss, paid in land — to write a song. A binding hymn. Twelve verses, sung at the winter solstice, to hold the drowned choir down. To keep them rehearsing instead of roaming."

"Twelve verses," Coral said slowly. "You're sure it was twelve?"

"I'm sure the contract specified twelve, because I've read the contract, because reading contracts is also a form of prayer." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "And I'm sure that only eleven were ever performed. Voss delivered eleven and left town in February and died in April, in a town with no water deeper than a creek, of drowning in his bed. Dry drowning, the doctor called it. Lungs full of clear water. There's a phrase for that in the old notes my grandmother copied out: *the choir collects its arrears.*"

Coral had gone pale in the particular way of a man whose brother's notebooks were waiting for him in an attic. "So the song is missing its ending. Has been, since 1911."

"For a hundred and thirteen years, eleven verses have held thirty-one singers under the ice. Mostly." She tapped the ledger stack. "Mostly is the word doing all the work in that sentence. Every few winters, the binding slips. Someone hears the tune. Someone walks out onto the ice in their socks. And the town holds its breath until thaw, and then we tell ourselves it was the cold, the grief, the bad well water, anything but the true thing, because the true thing would mean the whole town has been living on top of a lid for a century, holding it down with a song that was never finished."

The stove ticked. Outside, a crow argued with another crow about something neither would concede.

"Why are you telling me this?" Coral asked. Quietly. Not hostile. Just a man checking the load-bearing walls before he leaned on them.

Odile looked at him for a long moment. He had his brother's face, mostly. But where Wren — from the photographs, from the funeral, from the way the town spoke of him — where Wren had looked like someone leaning toward things, this one leaned back. Careful. Cataloguing. An archivist's face on a stranger.

"Because your brother came to see me in November," she said. "Eleven days before he died. He sat where you're sitting and he asked me almost exactly that question — why am I telling you this — except he asked it about himself. Why was I showing him the ledgers. And I told him: because you're the first person in forty years who came here already believing, so I didn't have to spend the visit building the floor before we could stand on it." She folded her hands. "And because he asked me for everything I had, and I gave it to him, and eleven days later he was dead in the shallows with his boots tied beside him, and I have spent every day since wondering if I handed a man a map straight off a cliff."

"He went into the water?" Coral's voice cracked on the second word.

"On the twelfth of December. Between the boat ramp and the old church site, where the water's shallow enough to stand in, if you were determined to drown somewhere inconvenient." She watched him absorb it. Grief was a country; she knew the customs; you let travelers move at their own pace through it. "The coroner wrote accidental. The coroner writes accidental every time. It's practically a form letter."

Coral pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes for a moment. When he lowered them, he was, remarkably, smiling — the grim, grateful smile of a man who has finally been given real information after weeks of being handed casseroles and condolences.

"Do you know how insane I've felt?" he said. "The body in the casket. The mail. The mirrors. I started photographing the hallway like a crime scene because it was the only thing keeping me sane, and I kept thinking, either I'm losing my mind or this town is, and honestly the second option was starting to look better."

"Oh, the town lost its mind generations ago," Odile said. "We just institutionalized it. We call it tradition. We hold the Winter Festival. We sing eleven verses at the solstice service and nobody asks why the program says twelve." She stood, joints announcing themselves, and fetched a tin of shortbread from the counter — her mother's recipe, made every December whether anyone ate it or not, because some rituals survived even when their reasons rotted. "Eat. You look like you run on caffeine and denial."

"Accurate."

They ate shortbread at the scarred table, and for a few minutes neither of them said anything true, which was its own kind of rest.

* * *

It was Coral, eventually, who broke the quiet, because he was his brother's brother after all.

"The lag," he said. "In the mirrors. At the house. Have you — is that in the ledgers?"

Odile set down her mug. "How far behind is yours?"

"A second. Maybe a little more. I tested it with a dropped cup." He paused. "The reflection drops it late."

"Mm." She got up, went to the hall, and came back with a hand mirror — her mother's, silver-backed, tarnished at the rim — and laid it face-down on the table between them like a card she was choosing not to play yet. "Mine's been behaving. That's the thing about the lag. It isn't everywhere. It picks houses. It picks people." She turned the mirror over. In it, the kitchen showed: the stove, the window, the two of them seated. Present tense. Obedient. "Your family's house sits over the old lane that ran down to the church. Before the flood, the Ishikawa property backed onto the churchyard. Your grandfather bought the lot cheap in the fifties and everyone said what a bargain, and my grandmother said, *that ground remembers being holy,* and everyone said, oh, Odile's mother says that about everything."

"So the house is — what? Soaked in it?"

"Houses are just slow water," Odile said. "That's a Vann saying. My grandmother again. She was right about nearly everything and pleasant about none of it, which I believe is called a legacy."

Coral laughed — a short, surprised sound, the first she'd heard from him that wasn't shaped like politeness. "You miss her."

"Every day, in the way you miss a stone wall. Not warmly. Structurally." She gathered the ledgers back into their stack, squared their edges. "Here's what I believe, Coral, after forty years of this. Take it or leave it. The song keeps the choir contained. Mostly. Eleven verses out of twelve is a lock with most of its tumblers set. Most nights, most winters, it holds. But a lock like that doesn't fail all at once. It slips. A little more each year. The lag in your mirrors is a slip. The humming is a slip. Tamsin Reeve's boots, tied neat as church shoes — that's a slip. And slips compound." She met his eyes. "I think we are living in the last years of *mostly.*"

He was quiet for a while. Then: "Wren's notebooks. In the attic. He documented the lag for two years. It was growing. He measured it." His jaw worked. "His last entry stops mid-sentence."

"Bring them to me," Odile said. "All of them. I want to see his numbers next to mine."

"You think they'll match?"

"I think your brother was a better mathematician than I am, and I intend to steal his work shamelessly." She stood to see him out, and at the door, with the wind pushing at both of them like an impatient usher, she added the thing she'd been circling all afternoon:

"One more thing. The night before Wren died, he asked me a strange question. He asked if I'd ever heard the twelfth verse. Not read it — heard it. Sung. Anywhere, anytime, by anyone." She watched Coral's face do several things at once. "I told him no. And that was true. But I've thought about the question every day since, because it wasn't a scholar's question, Coral. It was a man planning something. And I gave him the only answer I had, and it wasn't enough, and he went out to find the verse himself, alone, in December, and the water took him politely, the way it takes everyone."

Coral stood on her step with the wind pulling his coat sideways, and for a long moment he didn't say anything at all.

Then he said, "He wouldn't have gone alone if he'd known anyone would help him."

"No," Odile agreed. "He wouldn't. That was his tragedy and it will not be yours. Come back tomorrow with the notebooks. And Coral—" she waited until he turned around on the gravel, "—if the humming starts. If you catch yourself with a tune in your mouth you can't name. You come to me that same day. Not the next day. That day."

He nodded and went, and she watched him all the way down Quill Street until the dark took him, and then she went inside and did what she had done every night for forty years.

She opened tonight's ledger and wrote the date, and the weather, and *visitor: C. Ishikawa, shown archives, disposition: grieving but functional,* and then she stopped, pen hovering, because for the first time in forty years the next column had no heading.

Finally she wrote, in the margin where the frost-notes went, in her small precise hand:

*Lag reported at the Ishikawa house: ~1 second. Watch it. If it widens, we are out of mostly.*

Under the floorboards, the empty fireproof box waited. On the hall table, her mother's mirror lay face-down, obedient, present-tense.

But as she turned off the kitchen light, Odile could have sworn — and did not write down, because writing it would make it data, and data would make it real — that the tarnished silver caught the streetlight a half-beat after the window across the room did.

One second. Maybe a little more.

She slept with the lights on, and dreamed, for the first time in decades, of her grandmother's voice saying: *count the seconds, girl. The water counts them too.*
