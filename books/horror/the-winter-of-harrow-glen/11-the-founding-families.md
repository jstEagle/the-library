## 11. The Founding Families

The county hall smelled of old paper and older coffee, and Odile Vann had been avoiding it for exactly forty years.

Not consciously, of course. She was a woman of archives; she had spent more of her life in rooms like this than in her own kitchen. But there is a difference between a room that holds other people's history and a room that holds your own, and Odile had always known, in the way you know which floorboards creak, that the second floor of the Harrow Glen county hall held hers.

She climbed the stairs anyway, Coral Ishikawa two steps behind her with a canvas bag full of Wren's notebooks, and she thought: *Well, Odile. You always said you'd face it eventually. You just assumed eventually would come after you were dead, which is the traditional arrangement.*

The records room was presided over by a woman named Mrs. Tallow, who had worked the front desk since roughly the invention of the front desk, and who greeted Odile with the particular warmth of someone who has been waiting decades for a specific person to walk through her door.

"Miss Vann," Mrs. Tallow said. "And you'd be the brother. The Ishikawa one." She looked at Coral the way people in Harrow Glen had been looking at him all week — a little too long, a little too carefully, as if checking which twin he was. "We have the reservoir commission files. Been expecting you."

"Expecting us," Odile said.

"Expecting *somebody*." Mrs. Tallow slid a key across the counter. "Second door on the left. The lights flicker, that's normal. If you hear humming, that's the radiator. It hums. It's a radiator."

She said it the way people in Harrow Glen said everything now — a half-second too firmly, the way you say *I'm fine* when you are deciding to be fine later.

* * *

The room they were given was narrow and windowless and lined with green metal cabinets, the kind that had been bolted to the floor by someone who understood that the things inside them were the only things keeping the town's past from simply walking off. Odile switched on the overhead light. It flickered, as promised. She pulled on white cotton gloves — habit, armor, costume — and opened the first cabinet marked *RESERVOIR COMMISSION, 1911–1919.*

"Start with the founding documents," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was a skill she'd been practicing since she was nine years old. "The commission papers will list the signatories. Five families. I've known that since I was a girl — it's in every local history, it's on the plaque in the square. What the plaque doesn't mention is what the commission actually *did* to get the valley flooded."

Coral set the notebooks down carefully, the way he did everything, as if the world were a thing that could still be broken further. "You've never read them?"

"I've read everything *except* them." Odile drew out the first folder. The paper inside had gone the color of weak tea. "There's a word for that, I think. In therapy. Which I never went to."

The first document was the charter itself, signed in five hands. Odile read the names aloud, because some things need to be said in the open air to be believed:

"Aldous Vann. Emeric Quist. Tobias Marsh. Silas Fenn. And Corwin Ashe." She looked up. "Vann, Quist, Marsh, Fenn, Ashe. Five names. You've seen them on the plaque. You've seen them on the street signs. Vann Road, Quist Lane, Marsh Hill, Fenn Bridge, the Ashe building that's been empty since I was a teenager."

"And your family signed first," Coral said.

"My family signed *first*," Odile confirmed. "Aldous Vann. My great-great-grandfather, if the arithmetic is kind, which it rarely is." She laid the charter flat on the table and smoothed it with one gloved finger, the way you'd touch a wound to check whether it still hurt. It did. "He was the one who brought the idea to the other four. The state wanted the reservoir. The state needed local men to convince the valley to give up its land. Aldous volunteered before the state even asked."

* * *

The morning went on in that fashion — folder after folder, and Odile narrating because Coral's hands were occupied with his camera, documenting each page the way he documented everything, and she had learned in the past week that his photographing was not a hobby but a form of prayer, the only liturgy he trusted. So she read, and he shot, and the radiator hummed, and the light flickered, and the past unspooled with the patience of something that had been waiting.

The commission's correspondence told the story in the polite language of men who knew exactly what they were doing and preferred to describe it as weather.

*The residents of the lower valley have proven resistant to reasonable offers,* Aldous Vann wrote in March of 1913. *Their attachment to the church and its congregation is an obstacle. It is my belief that attachment, being irrational, will yield to circumstances.*

"Attachment," Odile repeated. "The church. Its congregation. Ninety-one people, Coral. The parish rolls are in folder six. I counted them when I was eleven years old and then didn't count anything for a month afterward. Ninety-one. And the choir was thirty-one of them, because the lower valley church was famous for its choir. People came from three counties to hear them. My grandmother used to say the valley didn't have a church, it had a *voice*, and the church was just the building the voice lived in."

Coral lowered his camera. "What happened to them? Really. Not the plaque version."

"The plaque version says the flooding proceeded on schedule and the residents were resettled with compensation." Odile pulled folder four, the thinnest one, the one she had never opened, and set it down between them like a card she'd been holding face-down for forty years. "The compensation was paid to eleven families. The other eighty residents of the lower valley were, per the commission's own correspondence, 'unaccounted for at the time of inundation.'"

"Unaccounted for."

"Which is county language for *we closed the sluice gates on a Tuesday morning and the valley filled faster than anyone could walk uphill, and the five of us knew it would, because we'd done the mathematics ourselves, and the mathematics required the valley to fill fast.*" She said it flatly, evenly, the way she'd rehearsed it in the car on the drive over, and then her voice cracked anyway, because some sentences break no matter how carefully you carry them. "Aldous Vann did the mathematics. It's in his own hand. Right there. *Inundation to crest: eleven minutes.* He wrote it like a recipe."

The room was quiet except for the radiator, which hummed, being a radiator.

Coral sat down slowly. "The choir was in the church."

"The choir was in the church because it was the last thing standing. Everything else had been cleared or abandoned, but the congregation kept holding services right up to the end. My grandmother told me — she was a girl then, she *watched* it, from the ridge — they sang the whole time. The water came up the aisle and they kept singing. She said you could hear them for almost the whole eleven minutes." Odile took off her gloves, finger by finger, though there was nothing more to touch. "And then the commission's records stop mentioning them entirely, and the reservoir fills, and the town gets its water, and five families get their names on a plaque, and everyone agrees to call it a tragedy of circumstance."

"Except it wasn't."

"No. It was a drowning with a budget."

* * *

They broke for lunch at the diner on the square, mostly because Coral's hands had started shaking and Odile had decided, with the wisdom of long self-management, that grief and archival research require regular fuel. She ordered for both of them without asking, which he seemed to find either comforting or strange; with Coral it was often both at once.

"Tell me about your grandmother," he said, halfway through a grilled cheese he wasn't eating. "The one who watched."

Odile stirred her coffee long after it needed stirring. "Marguerite Vann. She was eight in 1919. She stood on the ridge with her father — with Aldous — and watched the valley fill, and she heard the choir, and something in her broke in a way that never set right. She spent the rest of her life trying to give it back."

"Give what back?"

"The song." Odile said it quietly, because the diner was full of townsfolk and the town had ears now, frosted or otherwise. "You know what she did, in the end. You found her work. The sealed rooms in my house. The verse written backwards." She watched him absorb it. "But what you don't know is *why* backwards. Everyone assumes it was to hide it. It wasn't. Marguerite heard the choir's hymn complete — once, from the water, through the ice, the winter after the flooding. She was the only Vann who ever heard the twelfth verse, the one that was never written down, the one the choir was singing when the water closed over them. And she understood — she wrote this, it's in her papers, I'll show you — she understood that the hymn was the binding. That the choir couldn't stop singing it, and the town couldn't stop needing it sung, and the only mercy available to anyone was to finish it. To give the song its last verse so it could finally, finally end."

"And she wrote it backwards so no one could read it aloud by accident," Coral said slowly.

"Because she tried it forwards first." Odile's coffee had gone cold and she drank it anyway, a penance with a handle. "In 1954. She stood in front of her bedroom mirror and she read the verse as written, aloud, all the way through. And the mirrors in this house have been lagging ever since. Not just mine — everyone's, everywhere, all at once. She broke something loose that day, or she woke something up, and she spent the next thirty years making sure no one could ever do what she did by accident again. She wrote it mirrored. She sealed the rooms. She told my mother never to learn it, and my mother told me never to ask, and I was a good girl, Coral, I never asked, and I have hated myself for being a good girl every single winter of my life."

The waitress refilled their coffees and retreated, having the good sense of long practice to hear nothing.

Coral was quiet for a while. Then: "Your ancestors led the drowning. Wren's notebooks led me here. And the woman who heard the verse was a Vann." He said it not as accusation — Coral did not accuse; he catalogued — but as architecture. "It's all one thread."

"It's all one thread," Odile agreed. "And I'm the knot in the middle of it. My family drowned ninety-one people and then spent four generations pretending we'd been the heroes of a flood. Marguerite was the only one who tried to pay it back, and she paid in the only coin the thing accepts, which is *almost*. Almost finishing the verse. Almost telling someone. Almost, almost, almost, and then she died, and the almost passed to me like the good china." She laughed — a real laugh, small and terrible. "Do you know what I did with my inheritance, Coral? I became the town archivist. I catalogued every disappearance, every frozen winter, every person last seen near water or glass. Forty years of it. I built a ledger so careful that no one would ever have to feel what I felt, which is that the monster in the story has your last name."

"Odile—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and her hand was steady, and she was proud of it and disgusted by it in equal measure. "Don't forgive me yet. I haven't earned it. I've known since I was nine years old what my family did, and I knew Wren was digging, and I *helped* him, and I still never once said out loud, in plain words, to another living soul: *my great-great-grandfather closed the gates on purpose and my family has been paying the interest ever since.* Forty years of ledgers, and I never wrote that sentence in any of them." She pulled her bag onto the table, took out her own ledger — the fat, gray, forty-year one — and a pen, and opened it to the first blank page.

And there, in the county diner, over cold coffee, Odile Vann wrote it down. *The Vanns led the drowning of the lower valley. Ninety-one souls. The debt is ours. I am done hiding from my own name.*

She slid it across the table so he could witness it, because a record unwitnessed was just a diary, and she had not spent forty years learning the difference for nothing.

Coral read it. Then he did the thing she had not expected, the thing that undid her more than any accusation could have: he took out his camera and photographed the page.

"Documentation is a kind of prayer," he said. "Wren taught me that. Now it's witnessed."

* * *

They went back in the afternoon and finished the folders, and it was Coral — methodical, grieving, camera-clicking Coral — who found the thing that mattered.

He had worked his way into the commission's final reports, the ones from the winter after the flooding, the ones written in the careful past tense of men closing accounts. And he stopped, and Odile heard the camera stop clicking, and she knew before she looked.

"What is it?"

"Page forty-one." He turned the folder toward her. "The commission's final assessment of the reservoir site. There's a section on the church. They note the structure was never demolished — it was too deep to reach by the methods of the day — and they note that the building 'remains intact beneath the water.'" His finger moved down the page. "And then there's this. A marginal note, in a different hand. Aldous's hand. Dated the following spring."

Odile read it. She read it twice. The radiator hummed. The light flickered, and this time she was fairly sure the flicker was only the light.

The note said: *The singing has not stopped. The men who work the north shore hear it under the ice on still nights. I have instructed them to say nothing. A debt that sings is a debt that intends to be collected. We must find what it wants before it tells us.*

"He knew," Odile said. Her voice came out very small for such a large room. "Aldous knew, the very first winter. He heard them. And instead of — of *anything*, repentance, restitution, blowing the whole thing open — he wrote a memo." She sat back. "And then what did he do with the knowledge? He passed it down. Quietly. Father to daughter to daughter. That's what Marguerite inherited — not just guilt. *Instructions.* The Vanns weren't just the drowning family, Coral. We were appointed the wardens. We've been the lock on the door for a hundred years, and nobody ever asked the prisoners if they wanted a lock."

Coral was already reaching for Wren's notebooks. "Wren mapped the hymn's structure. Eleven verses. He was certain there was a twelfth that was never written — that the binding is a song with a hole in it, and the hole is the door." He opened the last notebook to its final entry, the one that stopped mid-sentence, and laid it beside Aldous Vann's marginal note, 1919 and two years ago, the same sentence written a century apart in two different kinds of desperation.

*A debt that sings is a debt that intends to be collected.*

And beneath it, in Wren's hand, the entry that ended everything, the words he had never finished:

*The verse is scattered. Mirabel hid it where they'd never think to look — in the things they touched. I've found three pieces. The fourth is in the —*

The sentence stopped there. It had stopped there for two years, and Odile had read it a hundred times, and it had never before looked like an instruction.

"In the *what*," she said. "In the *what*, Wren."

The light overhead flickered — once, twice, three times — and in the windowless records room of the Harrow Glen county hall, where there were no mirrors and no water and no ice, both of them heard, faint and unmistakable from somewhere inside the green metal cabinets, a single voice begin to hum.

Mrs. Tallow's radiator, they told each other.

It was a radiator.

It hummed.
