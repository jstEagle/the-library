## 1. Attendance

The bus let me off at the edge of Hollowmere the way a body lets go of something it no longer wants to carry — without ceremony, without apology, and with a certain finality in the sound it made pulling away.

I stood on the shoulder of Old Chapel Road with two suitcases and a box of books, and I watched the taillights shrink into a gray morning that seemed to have been going on for years. The frost had come overnight. I could tell because every fence post, every mailbox, every bare branch along the road wore a thin white skin, like the town had been dipped in milk and left to dry. My breath came out in small ghosts. Somewhere behind me, crows were arguing about something in the dead corn.

*First frost*, my grandmother used to say, *is when the world tells you what it's been hiding.*

She said a lot of things like that. She was a Delacroix, and Delacroix women were apparently constitutionally incapable of saying anything plainly. I hadn't thought about her in months, and now here she was in my head, uninvited, on the first morning of my new life, murmuring superstitions into the cold.

The school was a twenty-minute walk from where the bus stopped, which nobody had mentioned in any of the correspondence. The job posting had said *rural district, K–8, small class sizes, housing provided*. It had not said that the housing was a cottage behind the schoolhouse itself, or that the schoolhouse was old enough to remember being a chapel, or that the road leading to it was called Old Chapel Road as though there might be another one, a newer chapel somewhere, a town that had moved on.

There wasn't. Hollowmere had not moved on. Hollowmere had settled into itself like an old man into a chair he expects to die in.

I walked. The frost crunched under my boots in a way I would come to know intimately — that particular brittle squeak of grass giving up its warmth all at once. Past the feed store with its windows papered over. Past a church whose steeple leaned like a man listening hard. Past houses with woodpiles stacked high and precise, the kind of preparation that isn't thrift but fear. A woman watering nothing watched me from her porch, and when I lifted a hand she did not lift hers back. She just looked at me the way you look at weather coming in.

The schoolhouse sat at the end of the road like a period at the end of a sentence. Gray clapboard, white trim gone yellowish, a bell tower with no bell. Behind it, orange plastic fencing marked off a rectangle of torn earth where a state crew had already begun staging equipment for the foundation repairs — the reason the district had finally hired a teacher mid-year, they'd told me. Structural work. Mandatory. Temporary disruption.

A man in a canvas coat stood at the edge of the excavation site, hands in pockets, watching the ground the way you'd watch a dog you suspected of something. He glanced at me, nodded once, and went back to staring at the dirt.

Inside, the hallway smelled of floor wax and pencil shavings and something underneath both — something mineral and cold, like the air in a root cellar. My classroom was the third door on the left. The name plate beside it read MS. DELACROIX, and someone had screwed it in recently; the screwheads still shone.

They had expected me. That should have felt welcoming. Instead it felt like the plate had been waiting, patient, for the right name to arrive.

* * *

Twenty-two students were enrolled in my class. I knew this because the roster said so, typed neatly, laminated against spills. Twenty-two names in a tidy column, and beneath them, in smaller print, a note from the district: *Class size reflects historical enrollment trends.*

At 8:47, they began to arrive.

They came in ones and twos up the walk, bundled so heavily they moved like small arctic expeditions, breath pluming, boots stamping. They hung their coats on hooks worn smooth by sixty years of other children's coats. They took their seats with the practiced quiet of children who have learned that quiet is safer than noise.

And they avoided three desks.

I didn't notice it at first. I was too busy learning faces, matching them to the list, doing the teacher's arithmetic of names and eyes. But by the time the second bell rang, I had noticed that the room's geography had a hole in it. Three desks near the window — middle row, spaced normally, nothing wrong with them — sat empty, and the children arranged themselves around that emptiness the way water arranges itself around a stone. Nobody sat near them. Nobody looked at them. One boy, passing the desks to reach the pencil sharpener, gave them a wide berth so deliberate it was almost a bow.

Nineteen children sat before me. Nineteen.

"Good morning," I said. "My name is Ms. Delacroix."

Something moved through the room at that — not a sound, exactly. A stillness. Nineteen pairs of eyes fixed on me with an attention that felt less like curiosity than appraisal. As if my name were a fact they'd been given in advance and were now confirming.

"We're going to start every day the same way," I told them. "With attendance. When I call your name, say *here*. Even if your voice is sleepy. Especially then."

I picked up the roster. And I will swear this on anything you ask me to swear on: I read the names as they were printed. All twenty-two. I read them aloud, one after another, and nineteen voices answered, and three names got nothing but the tick of the radiator and the wind worrying the window frames.

The third unanswered name was Cass Wren — no. Wait. No, that's wrong. Let me be careful here, because this is the part I've written down four times and read back five, and each time I check it, I need it to stay exactly what it was.

Cass Wren answered. Small voice, second row, dark-haired girl with chapped knuckles who said *here* like she was planting a flag. Cass Wren is how this whole account begins, in a sense, though I didn't know it yet.

The name that got no answer came last on the list. Printed clearly. I read it aloud — I heard myself read it aloud — and the silence that followed had texture. It wasn't empty. It was the silence of nineteen children deciding, simultaneously and without signal, to become furniture.

I looked up. "Is she absent?"

Nothing. A girl in the front row studied her desktop as if it contained instructions.

"Does anyone know—" I started.

The door opened.

Mr. Voss entered the way cold enters a house — through the gap you thought you'd closed. He was tall and dry and gray in the way of things kept in attics, with a cardigan buttoned to the throat and glasses that caught the window light so thoroughly I never did see his eyes that first morning. He carried a folder.

"Ms. Delacroix." His voice was mild. Mild the way a lake is mild right before the ice takes it. "A word about your roster, please."

He crossed to my desk, opened the folder, and laid a single sheet beside my laminated copy. Same header. Same district letterhead. Same date.

Different names.

"There was a printing error," he said. "The corrected version supersedes the original. You'll want to discard the old one."

I compared them. Nineteen names on his sheet. Nineteen. The three names that had gone unanswered — gone. Not struck through, not redacted. Simply never printed, as if the typesetter's hand had slipped cleanly past them, as if they had never existed anywhere but in my mouth.

"But I called roll," I said. "All twenty-two names are on my copy. I read—"

"You'll find nineteen is accurate," Mr. Voss said, "for this classroom. It has been accurate for some time." He squared the corrected roster against the desk edge, once, twice, a small precise percussion. "The district apologizes for the confusion. Errors happen. Paper is unreliable."

He held out his hand for the old roster, and here is the thing I have turned over more times than any other from that first day: I gave it to him. I stood in my own classroom on my first morning and handed a stranger the evidence of my own senses, because he had a folder and a title and a voice that made disagreement feel like a breach of manners.

He slid it into the folder without looking at it. At the door he paused.

"The children will tell you what you need to know," he said, "when they're ready. Not before. And Ms. Delacroix—" the light shifted on his glasses, and for half a second I thought I saw eyes behind them, pale and patient, "—welcome to Hollowmere. We keep good records here."

Then he was gone, and the bell rang, and nineteen children exhaled at once, a sound like snow sliding off a roof.

* * *

That night, in the cottage behind the schoolhouse, I started the notebook.

It was a habit even before Hollowmere — my grandmother's habit, inherited like the cheekbones and the superstitions. *Write down what doesn't add up*, she told me when I was nine. *Not to prove anything. Proof is for people who can leave. Write it down because a thing written down can't pretend it didn't happen.* She kept notebooks her whole life, dozens of them, in handwriting that got smaller and tighter toward the end, as if she were trying to fit everything into less and less space.

So I made tea in the cottage's unfamiliar kitchen, wrapped a blanket around my shoulders against a chill the stove couldn't seem to reach, and I wrote:

*October 14. First frost. Arrived Hollowmere.*

*Roster discrepancy: 22 names (my copy) vs. 19 (Voss's). I READ ALL 22 ALOUD. Three names unanswered. Voss says 19 has "been accurate for some time." Accurate for some time. What does that mean? How long is some time?*

*Three desks by the window. Children avoid them. Not fear exactly — avoidance. Like walking around a grave.*

*Voss took my copy. Why does a principal collect a misprinted roster?*

I stopped. Read it back. The words sat on the page looking smaller and stranger than they had in my head, and underneath them I could feel a question assembling itself, the way frost assembles on glass — slowly, silently, in patterns too regular to be accidental.

Because here was the thing I hadn't written yet. Here was the thing I'd been circling since the bus pulled away.

On the drive in — no. On the *walk* in, past the leaning church and the papered windows, I had passed the historical society. A squat stone building with a brass plaque, and in its window, arranged on velvet like relics, a display of photographs. School photographs. Rows of children in rows of desks, decade after decade, sepia fading into color. And beneath each photograph, a small card with a number.

I hadn't stopped to read the cards. But even walking past, even at a distance, in the corner of my eye, I had registered the numbers' shape. They weren't dates. They were counts.

I put down the pen. Picked it up. Wrote:

*Note to self: go back to the historical society. Read the cards.*

Outside, the wind found a loose shutter somewhere and worried it, a slow rhythmic knocking, patient as anything that knows it has all the time in the world. The stove popped. The blanket had slipped off my shoulders and I hadn't noticed, because the cold in the cottage wasn't coming from outside.

It was coming up through the floor. From below. From the direction of the schoolhouse, and whatever the schoolhouse was built on top of.

I wrote one more line before bed, and I have kept it verbatim ever since, because it was the first sentence in that notebook that felt less like documentation than like prayer:

*Something in this town is counting, and I intend to count back.*
