## 9. Twenty-Three

Cass Wren knew how to count before she knew how to tie her shoes, and Mama said that was because counting was older than shoes, older than laces, older than the first person who ever looked down at their own two feet and thought, *one, two*. Cass liked that. She liked knowing something old. Most things in Hollowmere were old — the school, the chapel, the frost that came in September and stayed like a guest who'd forgotten the way home — but being old wasn't the same as being broken, Mama always said. Old things just remembered more.

Cass was remembering numbers now.

She didn't mean to. That was the strange part, the part she couldn't figure out how to explain to anybody without sounding like Bram after his bad dreams, which she was not going to do, because Bram was seven and cried at everything and she was ten and did not.

It started on a Tuesday, in Miss Delacroix's class, with the multiplication tables. Miss Delacroix had written *7 × 3* on the board and asked who could solve it, and Cass's hand went up all by itself, like it had somewhere to be, and her mouth opened and what came out was not *twenty-one*.

What came out was: "Twenty-three."

The room got quiet in a way rooms weren't supposed to get quiet. Not teacher-quiet, where everyone waits to see if you're right. This was a different quiet, a held-breath quiet, the kind of quiet that has weight, like snow on a roof. Cass could feel it pressing down on the tops of all their heads.

Miss Delacroix smiled her careful smile — the one she used when she thought something was wrong but hadn't decided yet what kind of wrong. "That's not quite it, Cass. Try again?"

"Seven times three is twenty-one," Cass said, and her voice sounded far away to her, like it was coming up through a floor. "But twenty-three is what comes next."

"Next?" said Petey Marsh's little sister, from the back row, and then clapped both hands over her mouth like the word had bitten her.

"Next after what?" Cass asked, because honestly she didn't know. The number had just been there, sitting behind her teeth like a stone from a riverbed, smooth and cold and patient. It had been sitting there for days, maybe weeks. Maybe longer than she'd been alive. She only noticed it now because now it wanted out.

Miss Delacroix wrote *21* on the board very firmly, underlining it twice, and moved on to the next problem, and the class breathed again, all together, like one big animal. But Cass saw the way Miss Delacroix's eyes flicked to the window, and then to the three empty desks in the back row — the desks nobody ever sat in, the desks nobody would explain, the desks that were always, always clean, like somebody dusted them every night in case company came.

The empty desks made Cass think of the door.

She drew the door sometimes. She didn't decide to; her pencil just went there, the way water goes downhill. A rectangle with an arch on top, iron-colored, with hinges drawn on the *inside*, which Mr. Kowalczyk's crew had all been talking about for two weeks now, down at the diner, voices low, like the words might wake something up. Hinges on the inside meant the door opened inward. Cass had figured that out herself, doing the math of it in her head, and when she told Bram he'd gone white as milk and said, "Then whatever's behind it can push."

Bram was seven and cried at everything, but sometimes he said things that made Cass's skin feel too small for her body.

At lunch she sat with her tray of tater tots and fruit cup and didn't eat, just counted. One tot, two tots, three tots. She got to twelve and stopped, because thirteen felt like standing on the edge of something, and fourteen through twenty-two felt like walking along that edge in the dark with her arms out, and twenty-three —

Twenty-three felt like the edge ending.

She pushed the tray away. Her hands were cold. Her hands were always cold lately, even inside her mittens, even pressed against the radiator in the classroom, which Miss Delacroix kept cranked so high the windows wept condensation all day long. Cass had touched the glass once and left a handprint, and the handprint had stayed for hours, and when she came back at the end of the day it was still there, five fingers and a palm, except now there were six fingers, and she hadn't told anyone about that either.

Some things you keep in your mouth like stones. You get used to the weight. You learn to talk around them.

* * *

Mama worked doubles at the diner three nights a week, so it was Cass's job to walk Bram home and start the beans and make sure the woodstove had wood, and Cass did all of this the way she did everything, carefully, completely, like a small person running a small country. Their house sat at the bottom of Old Chapel Road where the road gave up trying to be straight and curled into the trees like a question mark. In the winter the wind came down off the ridge and found every gap in the siding and whistled through them, and Mama called it the house singing, and Cass used to like that.

That night the house wasn't singing. The house was holding its breath.

Cass noticed it while she was stirring the beans. The wind was still there — she could hear it moving through the pines, a long gray sound like the whole world exhaling — but none of it reached the house. The air around their roofline was still. Too still. Still like the three empty desks. Still like the moment after she'd said *twenty-three* and before anyone breathed.

"Bram," she called. "Come set the table."

No answer.

She found him in his room, sitting on his bed with his legs dangling, staring at the window. His breath fogged the glass in little puffs, regular as a clock.

"You're supposed to set the table," Cass said.

"I'm watching the frost," Bram said.

"It's just frost."

"No," Bram said, in the voice he used when he was being seven and certain about something, which was worse than when he cried. "It's writing."

Cass should have laughed. She should have said *you're such a baby* and dragged him to the kitchen by his sleeve. Instead she crossed the room and looked, because some part of her had already known, had known since lunchtime, since the tater tots, since the number sitting behind her teeth. Some part of her had been waiting for the frost all day the way you wait for a letter you already know is bad.

The window was furred white at the edges, feathers of ice climbing in from the corners, and in the middle, where the glass was clearest, the frost had grown letters.

Not letters exactly. Names. Cass knew they were names the way you know a face is a face, even from far away, even upside down. They were written in the fine cursive style that only grown-ups and dead people used, looping and formal, each one complete, each one followed by a date. The dates were old. The oldest ones were older than Mama. The newest one —

The newest one was today's date, and beside it, not finished yet, the frost was still growing the beginning of a word. Cass read the letters over and over until they arranged themselves into something, and the something was *Wren*.

"Cass," Bram whispered. "Is that us?"

"I don't know," Cass said, which was a lie, because she did know, she had known since seven-times-three, since the number rose in her throat like water finding its level. The frost wasn't writing their name because it was confused. It was writing their name because it was *counting*.

She took Bram's hand. His fingers were freezing. So were hers. Between the two of them they probably had one warm child's worth of heat, and it wasn't enough, and outside the sky was coming down in that particular heavy way that meant the real cold was coming, the deep cold, the cold the town talked about in the past tense like a war it had survived.

"We're having beans," Cass said, loud and normal, because that was the job of big sisters, to stand in front of the scary thing and say ordinary words at it until it got bored. "And cornbread if the baking soda isn't dead. Come on."

They ate at the table with the lamp on and the curtains drawn, and Cass made Bram play the game where you name an animal for every letter of the alphabet, and he got stuck on X like always, and she let him use *X-ray fish* like always, and everything was almost okay until the lights flickered — once, twice — and in the dark between flickers, Cass heard it.

Counting.

Not from outside. From underneath. From below the floorboards, below the crawlspace, below the dirt, a voice like many voices braided into one, patient as erosion, saying numbers in order. It was at nineteen when the lights steadied. By the time Cass had worked up the courage to put her ear against the cold kitchen floor — and she did do it, she knelt down and laid her cheek against the boards like she was listening for a train — it had reached twenty-one.

Twenty-two.

Then silence. A silence with a hole in it, shaped exactly like the next number.

Cass lay there on the kitchen floor with her heart going like a rabbit's, and she understood, in the way children understand things — completely, instantly, without any of the mercy of doubt — that the silence was waiting for her. That the missing number was hers to say. That it had been hers all along, maybe since before she was born, maybe since the day some ancestor she'd never met signed her name into a book she'd never see, trading something precious for something necessary, the way people in Hollowmere had always done.

She didn't say it.

She stood up, and she washed the bean pot, and she put Bram to bed and lay down next to him because he asked, and she stared at the ceiling and did not sleep, and she did not count sheep, and when her mind tried to sneak numbers in she made herself think about the alphabet instead, A B C D, letters were safe, letters were just shapes.

Around three in the morning, the frost on Bram's window finished the word.

Cass heard it happen — a sound like a fingernail drawing a line, delicate and endless — and she got up and looked, because by then she was past being scared into stillness; she was scared into motion, which is a different animal entirely.

The frost had written *Cassandra Wren* in full, in that beautiful dead-person handwriting, and beneath it, the date. And beneath the date, in smaller script, like a footnote, like an afterthought, like a prayer:

*the last one needed.*

Cass stood at the window a long time. Down the road, the schoolhouse sat black against the snowfield, and behind it, though she couldn't see it from here, she knew there was a pit now, and in the pit there was a door, and the door was warm. Mr. Kowalczyk's crew said it was warm. Warm in winter, warm in frost, warm as a sleeping animal, and sealed from the inside, which meant —

Which meant, Cass thought, pressing her cold fingertips to the cold glass, that whatever was in there had closed itself in. Or that someone had closed it *for* it, from within, holding the handle, holding the dark shut like you'd hold a mouth shut during a scream.

Her breath bloomed on the pane and covered the frost-writing, and for one second her name disappeared, and Cass thought: *I could breathe on it forever. I could keep my name fogged over until spring.*

But when her breath faded, the frost had written something new, lower down, small as a secret:

*twenty-three, twenty-three, come and say it, we've been keeping your seat.*

In the morning, Mama would find the windows and burn sage from the jar above the stove and call Odile's cousin in Bellhaven and get no answer, because the phone lines had gone quiet overnight, all of them, everywhere. In the morning, Miss Delacroix would stand at the board with chalk dust on her sleeve and look at Cass a beat too long, and Cass would look back, and neither of them would say the number, and the silence where it lived would grow one day deeper.

But that night, Cass climbed back into bed beside Bram and took his cold hand in her cold hand and made herself a promise, the kind children make, absolute and unbreakable: *I will never say it out loud. Whatever it costs, whatever it offers, however long it waits — I will hold that number in my mouth until my teeth turn to stone.*

Below the floorboards, in the dark under the dark, the chorus voice sighed — almost fondly, almost patiently, the way you sigh at a child who thinks she's discovered hiding — and began, softly, to count backward.
