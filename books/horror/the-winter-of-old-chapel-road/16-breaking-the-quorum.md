## 16. Breaking the Quorum

The chapel had not held a full congregation in eleven years, and it showed in the smell of the place — dust and cold wax and the sweet rot of hymnals no one had opened since the last hard winter. But on the night the town gathered, every pew was filled. Farmers in their funeral coats. Shopkeepers with their hands folded like children waiting to be scolded. The Kowalczyk crew, unwashed, unshaven, Denny in the back row with a flask he did not drink from, because drinking would have meant he needed steadying, and he refused to need anything ever again.

Above the altar, the stained-glass window of the Good Shepherd had gone strange. No one could say when. The shepherd's crook had darkened, and the glass around the lamb's throat had begun, slowly, to crack in a pattern like a keyhole.

Voss stood at the pulpit in his good suit, the one with the shiny elbows, and he did not look like a school principal anymore. He looked like a man reading from a teleprompter only he could see.

"We are gathered," he said, "to decide."

The word *decide* moved through the chapel like a draft. Shoulders hunched. A woman in the third pew began quietly to weep, the way people weep at weddings — without wanting to, without being able to stop.

"For sixty years," Voss said, "this town has been kept. Kept warm when the counties around us froze. Kept fed when the crops failed everywhere else. Kept whole. And kept is not free. You all know the terms. You have always known the terms. Your grandparents knew them, and their grandparents before them, and they did not call it a crime. They called it a covenant."

He let the word sit. Somewhere in the rafters, frost crackled along a beam, though the chapel's stove was lit and burning hot.

"The count stands at twenty-two," Voss said. "One more, and it is finished. Finished, do you understand? No more winters like '54. No more ledger. No more listening at night for the door. The children already held — they go free. Or they are released into whatever comes next, but either way, the debt is paid in full, and none of your children, none of your grandchildren, will ever be counted again. Peace. For the price of one."

He paused, and his voice went soft, and that softness was the most honest thing he had said all night.

"I know what you are. I know how tired you are. I have watched this town eat itself with guilt for sixty years, and guilt is a hunger too. Completion ends the hunger. One name, freely given, and Hollowmere sleeps for the first time since 1897."

In the back, a man stood up. Harlan Peck, whose family had farmed the north fields for four generations, whose daughter had been in Sable's homeroom before the family moved her to a cousin's house in Cedar Falls and never explained why. Harlan's face was gray with exhaustion, the exhaustion Voss had named and was now offering to buy back.

"Freely given," Harlan repeated. "Whose name, then? Yours?"

And Voss, who had an answer for everything, who had spent thirty years deflecting parent complaints and school board audits and the slow erosion of his own soul, looked down at his hands and said nothing.

That silence was the crack in the ice. Half the town saw it.

* * *

Sable had not planned to stand. She had planned the opposite — she had planned to sit in the last pew with the ledger in her satchel and let the town hang itself, and then, when the vote came, when the hands went up, she had planned to walk out into the frost and never come back, because there are some rot you cannot cut out of a body, and a town that votes for its own children is one of them.

But when Voss said *one name, freely given*, her legs stood her up on their own.

She walked down the center aisle the way she used to walk to the chalkboard in her first year of teaching, when she was twenty-two and terrified and had learned that the only cure for terror in front of a crowd is to keep moving and keep talking. The floorboards groaned under her. The frost in the rafters crackled louder, as if the building itself were leaning in.

She set the ledger on the pulpit beside Voss's Bible. It looked small there. It looked obscene.

"This is the record," she said. Her voice carried strangely in the cold air, flat and clear, the way sound carries over ice. "Sixty years of it. Mr. Voss wants you to decide. Fine. Decide with the facts in front of you. Decide with the *names* in front of you."

She opened to the first page. The leather was soft as skin.

"September the fourteenth, 1897. Abigail Marsh. Held."

Someone in the congregation made a sound like they'd been struck.

"October the second, 1897. Thomas Wren. Held." She looked up. "Yes. A Wren. This family has been paying since the beginning, and you want to charge them again."

She kept reading. She did not perform the names. She did not weep over them, though her throat wanted to close around every one. She read them the way she wrote in her documentation journal — plainly, precisely, like a prayer that does not ask for anything, only states what is true so that the truth cannot be unmade.

"Elias Crane. Held, March 1903. No return date."

"Mary Odette Bellamy. Held, November 1909."

"Joseph and Peter Kowalczyk. Held together, January 1921. Two names, one entry, as if the thing below could not tell them apart, or did not care to."

Denny's flask hit the floor. He did not pick it up. He was staring at her with his mouth open, and around him the crew had gone still as fence posts, because every one of them had heard Petey's brother calling from under the ground, and now they were hearing his name read aloud in a chapel, sixty years after it was written down, and the two sounds were the same sound.

Sable read on. The frost crept down the rafters as she read, threading the beams in white, and the candle flames leaned toward the pulpit as if they too were listening.

She reached the middle years and her voice began to change — not breaking, but *settling*, the way a judge's voice settles, the way a sentence settles. Because that was what this was. There were no courts for this. There had never been a law against it; Voss had made sure she understood that, standing in his office with his careful hands and his careful nothing. No crime had technically occurred. No body had ever been found. The missing children were, in the eyes of any state or statute, simply missing.

So this was the court. This room. This frost. This woman at the pulpit with a stolen ledger, and the testimony was the whole of the law.

"Ruth Ann Delacroix," she read, and had to stop, because it was her own name, her great-aunt, a woman whose photograph hung in her mother's hallway, smiling in a pinafore, and beneath the photograph her mother had written *lost young, God keeps her*. God had not kept her. The door had. "Held, February 1948."

She read the fifties. She read the year the town had tried to end it and lost fourteen people in one winter, and she read those fourteen names too, because they belonged in the record, because Voss would use them as an argument and they deserved better than to be an argument.

She read the sixties, the seventies. The names got closer to the present. Parents in the congregation began to recognize their neighbors' children, then their own cousins, then — in one terrible case — a name that made a woman in the fifth row stand up and say, "That's my sister. That's my *sister*, she didn't run away, they told us she ran away—" and her husband pulled her down and she fought him and the whole pew shook, and Sable did not stop reading, because stopping now would have been a mercy, and mercy was not what this testimony was for.

"Odile Wren's mother's brother," she said, turning a page. "Held, 1989. The Wrens have given this town four children across three generations, and you let them believe it was bad luck."

She reached the last written page. The most recent name. A boy from 2019, held the year before Sable was even hired, the year before the Delacroix estate filed the paperwork that would bring an excavation crew to the schoolhouse, the year before the machinery of the covenant began, patiently, to assemble her.

She closed the ledger.

"Sixty years," she said. "Twenty-two names in the Held column, and fourteen more dead in the winter you tried to stop it, and every one of them a child this town fed and taught and buried empty coffins for. Mr. Voss says one more name buys peace. He is right. It does. It bought peace in 1897 and in 1921 and in 1954, and every peace it bought came due again, because that is what this is — not a covenant. A *debt*. And you do not pay a debt by feeding it your children. You pay it by ending the creditor."

She looked out at them. Farmers and shopkeepers and the weeping woman in the third pew and Denny Kowalczyk standing now, standing very straight, and Odile Wren in the aisle with her hand on her daughter Cass's shoulder, and Bram pressed against his mother's side with his eyes closed, counting under his breath, *four, three, two* — no. Not counting. Sable made herself look away from Bram's moving lips.

"Voss asked for a decision," she said. "Here is mine. I will not consent. I am a Delacroix, and my family built this thing, and I am telling you — the last name it wants is the one it cannot take without your yes. Starve it. Let the town freeze. Let the crops fail. Freeze and starve and be *human* again, because whatever you are now, you are not that. You are a feeding trough with a zip code."

Silence. The candles had all gone out except one, and the one that remained burned without flickering, straight and white, like a held breath.

Then Harlan Peck, in the back, said quietly: "Easy to say. You'll be gone when the frost takes the cattle. You'll be gone when the babies get croup and there's no doctor within forty miles of a road that stays plowed." His voice cracked. "You don't know what hungry is. My grandmother did. She signed the same as yours did. And she said the same thing you're saying, right up until the winter of '54, right up until she watched her neighbors bury fourteen children in one month. And then she signed again."

"Harlan—" someone began.

"No." He was crying now, furious, ashamed, and the shame was the worst thing in the room. "I'm tired. We're all tired. Sixty years of being *tired*, and he's offering us the end of it, and God forgive me, half of me wants to say yes. Half of me has *always* wanted to say yes." He looked at Sable, and his eyes were the eyes of a good man standing at the edge of an abyss with his family's arms around his neck. "So don't preach at us about what we are. Tell us what you're going to *do* about it. You've got a plan, girl? You've got anything better than a frozen town and a clear conscience?"

And Sable, who had a map inked in blood in her satchel, who had tunnels and a smugglers' door and a great-grandmother waiting in the dark, said nothing at all.

Because Voss was smiling.

It was a small smile, at the corner of his mouth, and it was not triumphant. It was *relieved*. And Sable understood, with the cold certainty of a key turning, that this had been the purpose of the meeting all along. Not the vote. The *reading*. The town had needed to hear the names — the covenant had needed it, the quorum needed its witnesses — and now the ledger was spoken aloud, entered into the record of the room, and the count did not need her consent for what came next.

It only needed the town's despair.

"Thank you, Miss Delacroix," Voss said gently, into the ringing silence. "The record is complete. The congregation has heard." He turned to face them all, and his voice dropped into the register of a man reading scripture. "Then let us proceed to the matter of the twenty-third."

The frost came down off the rafters all at once, a white rain that did not melt, and every candle in the chapel went out except the one that burned before Sable, straight and white and patient.

And in the dark, from beneath the floor — from beneath the town, from beneath sixty years of buried coffins and unasked questions — came a sound like a door, very far away, beginning to open.

Sable's hand found Odile's in the dark.

"Get the children," she whispered. "Tonight. We go *down*."
