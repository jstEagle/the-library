## 8. Invisible by Practice

The trick to being forgotten, Wendell had learned by the age of seven, was to be useful in exactly the wrong amounts.

Too useful and people noticed you. Too useless and they noticed that too. But a child who set the table without being asked, who never cried at bedtime, who could be relied upon to be asleep when the caseworker called and awake when the toast burned — that child became furniture. And furniture, Wendell understood early, was safe. Nobody threw away furniture in anger. They just stopped seeing it.

She was practicing it now, forty-one years old and crouched in the pantry of Bell House with a cup of cold coffee going bitter in her hand, watching the wooden chair at the far end of the kitchen.

It hadn't moved since dawn. That didn't comfort her. Furniture that stays still is furniture you can trust. This chair had the patience of something that knew it would win.

*Later,* she told it silently. *I have to think about some things first.*

And because thinking about the chair meant thinking about being seen, and being seen meant thinking about how she'd spent her whole life learning the opposite, Wendell did what she always did with unbearable things: she made tea she didn't want and let her mind walk backward down its own tunnels.

* * *

The first house was the Delgados', in a brick row home in a city whose name Wendell had deliberately misplaced somewhere around age twelve. She'd been six. There were four other foster kids in the house and one bathroom and a mother, Mrs. Delgado, who worked doubles at a hospital and loved them all in the abstract, the way you love a photo of a beach.

Wendell figured out the system within a month. The kids who got hit were the loud ones. The kids who got sent back were the difficult ones — the bed-wetters, the biters, the ones who asked *when can I go home* with wet eyes at dinner. The kids who lasted were the ones who folded into the wallpaper.

So Wendell folded. She learned Mrs. Delgado's schedule the way other children learned prayers. She learned which floorboards announced you and stepped over them. When the older girl, Tanya, wanted the window seat, Wendell gave it up before Tanya even asked, so smoothly that Tanya never registered there'd been a contest. When the caseworker came for her quarterly visit, Wendell sat quietly with a book she wasn't reading and answered questions in the flattest voice she owned.

*"And how are things here, Wendell?"*

"Fine."

*"Anything you want to tell me?"*

"No, ma'am."

The caseworker wrote *adapts well* in her file. Wendell saw the words once, upside down across the table, and felt the first pure triumph of her life. *Adapts well.* It was a spell. She'd cast it herself.

The trouble with spells is they don't ask your permission before they become permanent.

* * *

The second house taught her refinement. The third taught her theory.

By the third house — the Okonkwos', a wide drafty place with a dog named Bishop who was the only creature alive that seemed to see her clearly — Wendell had developed what she thought of as the Rules.

Rule one: never be first. First is remembered. First gets blamed, first gets praised, both are dangerous. Be fourth. Be fifth. Let someone else break the silence at dinner; add your sentence two beats later, pitched low enough that it seems like an echo of the conversation rather than a contribution to it.

Rule two: be competent at chores, mediocre at school. Straight A's get you called on. Failing gets you conferences. A solid gray B-minus is a cloak.

Rule three — and this was the sacred one — never give anyone a reason to tell a story about you. Stories are hooks. People remember the girl who threw up on the bus, the boy who set the microwave on fire. If nothing about you is story-shaped, you slip through memory like water through a net.

Bishop, the dog, ruined Rule three constantly. He'd follow her from room to room, press his warm skull against her knee, stare at her with an attention that felt less like love than like audit. *I see you,* that stare said. *I've got your number.* Wendell fed him bacon under the table and told herself the arrangement was transactional.

When she left the Okonkwos' — aged nine, moved along for reasons nobody explained to children — Bishop stood at the fence and barked until the car was out of sight. Not angry barking. The other kind. Wendell pressed her forehead to the cool window glass and added a corollary to Rule three, written in the small private alphabet of a lonely kid:

*Sometimes being seen costs the seer more than it costs you. Try not to let anyone pay it.*

* * *

There was one exception to everything, and her name was Odile Joubert, and she was the reason any of this was survivable.

Grandma Odile entered Wendell's life the way good things often do: irregularly, without paperwork. She was a distant relative — a great-aunt, maybe, or a cousin once removed; the family tree had been pruned by estrangement until only a few stubborn branches remained — and twice a year, like a migrating bird with excellent timing, she appeared at whatever house Wendell currently inhabited with a tin of lemon drops and an hour of undivided attention.

Odile saw her. That was the terrifying thing. Not the way Bishop saw her, or even the way the caseworker pretended to. Odile looked at Wendell the way surveyors look at land — patiently, thoroughly, with respect for what might be underneath.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said once, when Wendell was eleven. They were sitting on the porch of some group home in some town, and Wendell had just deflected a question about school with a joke so smooth the other kids hadn't noticed it was a deflection. "The disappearing act."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." Odile unwrapped a lemon drop and held it out. "You know, in my family we had a saying. *Lo que no se nombra, te nombra.* What you refuse to name will name you instead." She smiled, and there was something old in the smile, something that belonged to cellars and ledgers. "One day, little bird, something is going to look at you whether you like it or not. Best decide now what it'll find."

"What does that even mean?"

"It means take the candy and stop performing for me. I'm family. You don't have to be furniture here."

Wendell took the candy. And for one hour, twice a year, all through the worst years, she practiced being a person instead of a presence. It was like exercising a muscle that had begun to atrophy — awkward, sometimes painful, always worth it.

She never told Odile that those hours were the load-bearing walls of her entire childhood. Some debts are too large to say out loud. You just try to live in a way that honors them.

* * *

Adulthood, when it came, rewarded her training. Urban exploration turned out to be the perfect profession for a woman built out of negative space. Municipal employees forgot she'd filed records requests. Property managers waved her through fences. Once, in the storm drains under a county seat whose name she'd also misplaced, a police officer shone his flashlight directly at her, said "evening," and walked on — because Wendell Joubert, in coveralls and a headlamp, radiated *authorized*, the way certain moths radiate *twig*.

She mapped tunnels that appeared on no survey. She kept meticulous notes and shared them with no one. Her apartment lease was month-to-month; her phone plan was prepaid; her emergency contact line read, for eleven years, *none*. She was, by every measurable standard, the freest woman she knew.

She was also, by every measurable standard, alone. But that was just the interest on the loan. Armor has a weight. You carry it or you don't wear it.

* * *

Now she sat in her grandmother's kitchen, in her grandmother's house, drinking tea she'd made to avoid thinking, and the armor was failing.

Because here was the problem, laid out plain: the house saw her. Not metaphorically. Not in the way a childhood home holds your shape in its doorframes. The house *saw* her — tracked her, anticipated her, arranged itself around her attention like water around a stone. Doors she needed were open before she reached them. Doors she dreaded were shut. Last night the hallway light had flickered twice when she whispered "goodnight" to an empty room, and she had heard — distinctly, unmistakably — the sound of something answering.

And the chair. God, the chair. It had been in the parlor when she arrived, patient as a court summons. Then it was in the hall. This morning, the kitchen. It never hurried. It never hid. It simply *was where she was going to be*, the way a verdict is where a trial is going to end.

Every survival strategy she owned was useless here. Rule one, never be first — the house went first, always, opening doors and dimming lamps ahead of her like a butler with terrible intentions. Rule three, never be story-shaped — she was the protagonist of a story the house had apparently been telling for a hundred and seventy years, complete with a prop chair and a supporting cast of carved names.

Even Rule four, the private one, the one about not letting anyone pay the cost of seeing her — even that was breaking. Because Marisol had started leaving notes on the kitchen counter. Little ones. *Soup's in the blue pot. Lock the back door, not just the front.* And Theo had taken to sitting on the porch steps after school, doing homework he didn't need help with, waiting for Wendell to come out so he could *not quite* ask if she was okay.

They were paying attention to her. Willingly. At cost. And instead of the old satisfaction — *adapts well, adapts well* — she felt something closer to panic, because attention, in this house, was currency, and she was starting to understand what the well below the floorboards did with currency.

* * *

The kettle clicked off. Wendell realized she'd made a second cup of tea without noticing, which was either absent-mindedness or the house being helpful again, and neither option was comforting.

She carried both cups to the table — because apparently she was setting a place for the chair now, because apparently that was her life — and sat down across from it. Up close, in honest afternoon light, it was such a plain thing. Pine, probably. Hand-planed. No ornament except the faint worn shine on the seat, the kind wood gets from being sat in by many bodies over many years.

Many bodies. Or one body, many times.

"Okay," Wendell said aloud, because talking to furniture was only insane if the furniture wasn't listening, and she was well past believing this furniture wasn't listening. "Here's the deal. I learned to be invisible before I could ride a bike. Twenty years of practice. Whole houses full of people couldn't keep track of me. So whatever you think you're doing—"

The chair creaked.

Not settling. Not thermal expansion. A single deliberate creak, the sound a chair makes when someone shifts their weight forward, interested, leaning in.

Wendell's mouth went dry. Somewhere upstairs, a door closed softly — not slammed, *closed*, the way a considerate host closes a door to give guests privacy.

And beneath her feet, so deep she felt it in the soles of her socks more than heard it, came a long slow exhalation, like the house breathing out, like something vast turning over in its sleep and murmuring a word it had been holding in its mouth for generations.

The word sounded, impossibly, like *Joubert*.

Wendell sat very still, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since chapter one of this whole nightmare: the ledger in the attic, dated three days before grandma died, unfinished mid-sentence. The names carved in the well, including hers, freshly cut. The hospice nurse's careful face when Wendell asked what Odile had talked about at the end.

*What you refuse to name will name you instead.*

"Oh," Wendell said quietly, to the chair, to the house, to the listening dark under the floorboards. "You're not haunting me. You're *claiming* me."

The chair sat there, warm as a lap, smelling faintly of lavender.

And that was when she heard the front gate squeak — Theo's gate-squeak, she knew it already, three days and he had a signature — and then his sneakers on the porch, faster than usual, and then his fist on the door, hitting it hard, a boy's frightened knock.

"Wendell?" His voice cracked through the wood. "Wendell, are you in there? I gotta tell you something. About last night. About what I saw in the window."

The chair creaked again — that lean-forward, interested creak — and Wendell understood, with the calm clarity of a woman whose whole life had trained her for exactly one skill, that whatever Theo had seen, the house had seen him seeing it.

She got up and went to open the door.
