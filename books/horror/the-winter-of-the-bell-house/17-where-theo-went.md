## 17. Where Theo Went

### Theo

Theo woke to the smell of wet stone and old pennies.

He was lying on his side on a floor of smooth, cold rock, and the first thing he thought—before he opened his eyes, before the fear arrived—was that it smelled like the inside of the church basement where they kept the rummage sale stuff. That specific smell of things nobody had wanted in a long time.

Then he opened his eyes and remembered everything at once, which was honestly the worst way to do it.

The tunnel. The chalk marks. Following Wendell's map because he'd copied it out of her notebook when she wasn't looking, because he'd wanted to help, because helping had seemed like such a good idea about six hours ago in the warm daylight world where good ideas existed.

He sat up slowly. Nothing hurt. That was the strange part. He'd fallen—he distinctly remembered falling, a long sliding tumble down a slope of loose shale that should have left him looking like he'd lost a fight with a cheese grater—but when he checked his hands in the dim light, they were clean. Unscratched. His jeans weren't even torn.

Around him, the well opened up like the inside of a bell.

He'd seen it from above, sort of, in Wendell's maps—the dry well beneath Bell House, the one she talked about in a voice she probably thought was casual but wasn't. But maps didn't prepare you for the size of it. The shaft rose above him farther than he could see, and the walls were carved, every inch of them, with names. Thousands of names. Tens of thousands. They caught the faint gray light and threw it back wrong, so the whole chamber seemed to shimmer like heat off summer asphalt except it was winter and freezing.

And there were chairs.

Seven of them, arranged in a half circle near the well's center, facing outward like an audience waiting for a show to start. In six of them sat people.

Not sitting like tired people. Sitting like furniture. Their clothes were wrong for now—wool coats with high collars, a dress with buttons all the way to the throat, suspenders, boots that hadn't been made in anyone's lifetime. Their skin was gray as the stone, and their eyes were open, and none of them were blinking, and Theo's body did the smart thing, which was to try very hard to become invisible by not moving at all.

Except then he saw her face.

The woman in the fourth chair had his mother's face.

Not exactly. Older. Grayed. But the same jaw, the same deep-set eyes, the same little crease between the brows that appeared whenever Theo forgot to take the trash out. He knew that face the way you know your own hands.

"Mom?" His voice came out small and stupid in the huge dark.

The woman didn't answer. None of them answered. They just sat, breathing—if that was breathing, slow as continental drift—and watching him with eyes like marbles left outside too long.

Theo hugged his knees and did what he always did when things got bad: he started making lists, because lists were solid, lists didn't shift around under you.

*List one: ways out.* The tunnel he'd come down was behind him somewhere, choked with rubble. There might be others. Wendell would come. Wendell always came; she'd saved him once already, pulled him out of the water when the well had seeped up out of nowhere—

The water.

He looked down. The floor was dry. Bone dry, dust lying undisturbed around the chairs like it had been there for years. Whatever had risen up to swallow him had gone back down again, or been put away, or—

Or it had gotten what it came for.

That was when he tried to think of his mother's name and found a hole where it should have been.

It wasn't panic, at first. It was more like reaching into your pocket for a key and finding the pocket sewn shut. He knew he *had* a mother. He could see her face right there in the fourth chair, for crying out loud. He knew she made pancakes shaped like lopsided stars on Sundays. He knew she hummed when she thought no one was listening, off-key, something old. But her name—the word itself, the sound of it—was just gone. Smoothed over. Like a name written in fog on a window that someone had wiped while he wasn't looking.

"Denise," he said out loud, testing. "Karen? Sandra?"

None of them felt right. All of them felt like guesses, and the horrible thing was he couldn't tell if they *were* guesses or if one of them was true and he just couldn't feel the truth of it anymore.

In the fourth chair, the woman with his mother's face moved. Just her mouth. Just barely.

She mouthed something at him, slow, patient, like a teacher sounding out a word for a struggling student. Three syllables. He watched her lips shape them over and over, and each time they dissolved before they could land.

"Don't," Theo whispered. "Don't say it. If you say it, I'll forget it faster, won't I? That's how this works."

Her mouth stopped moving. Her marble eyes stayed fixed on him, and Theo got the strangest feeling—not comfort exactly, but its distant cousin—that she wasn't guarding him. She was *keeping* him. The way you keep something precious that you're afraid of dropping.

He wrapped his arms tighter around his knees and waited for Wendell, and told himself stories about her coming, and every few minutes he tested the hole where his mother's name used to be, the way you test a loose tooth, and hated himself for it, and couldn't stop.

### Wendell

Wendell had been digging through the collapsed tunnel mouth for four hours when Marisol arrived with sandwiches and judgment.

"You haven't eaten since yesterday," Marisol said, setting the paper bag on a rock with the firmness of a woman who had administered medication to stubborn hospice patients for twenty years and was not about to be defeated by a thirty-year-old urban explorer. "You can't rescue anyone if you faint. Physics doesn't care about your guilt."

"I'm not guilty," Wendell said, hauling another slab of shale aside. "I'm focused."

"You're guilty-focused. It's a subtype." Marisol crouched beside her, picked up a rock, and began moving debris without being asked, which was the most Marisol thing Wendell had ever witnessed. "The boy snuck in following your map. You didn't push him. But you're going to dig like it was your fault anyway, so let's skip the part where I have to talk you into eating."

Wendell paused, dust on her cheekbones, hair escaping its braid in gray-streaked wisps. "When did you get wise?"

"About the same time you got predictable." Marisol handed her half a turkey sandwich. "Eat. Then keep digging. I'll pray, quietly, in Spanish, so it doesn't count as anything weird."

They dug until the light through the tunnel entrance went amber, then blue. And it was Wendell who heard it first—a sound rising out of the dark below the collapse, thin and wavering.

Singing. Badly.

"*...twinkle, twinkle,*" Theo's voice floated up through the rocks, cracked and hoarse, "*little... star... how I wonder what you... are...*" A pause. Then, smaller: "I don't remember the rest. Why don't I remember the rest? Everybody knows the rest."

Wendell's heart dropped through the floor of the world.

"Theo!" She pressed her mouth to the gap between two slabs. "Theo, it's Wendell! Are you hurt?"

A long silence. Then, shuffling, scraping, and his face appeared in the gap—pale, smudged, eyes too wide, but alive, gloriously, ordinarily alive.

"Wendell! Okay. Okay okay okay. Don't be mad."

"I'm already mad. Move back from the rocks."

"It's just—I know why I'm here, and it's bad, and also there's something else that's worse, and I need you to not freak out, because if you freak out then I'll freak out, and I've been alone with the chair people for—" he squinted, visibly calculating, "—I actually don't know how long. Time's weird down here. Like soup."

"Chair people," Wendell repeated flatly.

Marisol had gone still beside her. "The keepers," she said quietly. "From the ledger. From the cellar pages. They're *down there?*"

"They're sitting in chairs," Theo said, his voice climbing toward the register usually reserved for fire alarms. "And one of them looks like my mom, except old, and she keeps mouthing my mom's name at me except I can't—" His breath hitched. "I can't remember my mom's name, Wendell. I know her face and her pancakes and she hums off-key, but the *name* is gone, there's just a hole, and every time I try to look at the hole it gets bigger—"

"Stop." Wendell said it gently but firmly, the way you'd speak to someone standing on ice. "Stop poking at it. You're right—it gets bigger when you poke. We're getting you out. Marisol, the eastern passage, the one on my survey that runs under Harrow Street—it should connect to the well chamber at the upper gallery. If we go around instead of through the collapse—"

"If we hurry," Marisol said, already pulling her phone flashlight out.

They went around. It took forty minutes of crawling through passages Wendell had mapped in ink that now felt like prophecy, and when they finally dropped into the upper gallery of the well chamber, Wendell stopped dead, because the last time she'd stood here the floor had been trying to drown a child, and now it lay quiet as a held breath.

The seven chairs faced them. Six occupied. One empty—the plain wooden chair, the one from the photographs, the one that had followed her from room to room, and it sat here at the head of the half circle like a throne waiting for a coronation.

Theo sat huddled against the well wall, knees up. When he saw Wendell, he scrambled up and hit her at waist height, arms clamped around her, shaking.

"Hey," she said, into his hair. "Hey. I've got you."

"You came," he said, muffled. "I knew you'd come. I wrote it on a list. Lists are real even when names aren't."

"Yeah, kid. Lists are real."

Then he pulled back, looked up at her, and said, with the terrible directness of children: "The lady in the chair—my-mom-face lady—she hasn't taken any more. Since you got here. Before, it was like sipping through a straw, little bits, all night. Now it stopped. Why'd it stop?"

Wendell looked at the seated keepers. At the woman with the familiar stranger's face. And something clicked into place with an almost audible snap—the ledger entries, the unfinished line in her grandmother's hand, the way the well had flooded only when Theo, unclaimed, unconnected, had trespassed alone, and gone still the moment someone came who carried the Joubert inheritance in her blood like a deed of title.

"It stopped," Wendell said slowly, "because I'm here. Because I'm the keeper. The debt runs through me." She turned to Marisol, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "It never wanted Theo dead. It doesn't want deaths at all. Deaths end remembering. It wants—" she gestured at the six gray figures in their chairs, patient as mountains, "—it wants *rememberers*. People who sit and hold what it takes. That's what a keeper is. Not a guard. A vessel."

Marisol's face had gone the color of the stone. "And the boy?"

"The boy wandered into a bank vault thinking it was a library." Wendell crouched to Theo's level. "Theo. Listen. It's been drawing on you because you were unattended down here—loose change on the table. But the account isn't yours. It's mine. Which means I can do something you can't."

"Like what?"

"Negotiate."

She stood, walked to the edge of the half circle of chairs, and addressed the keepers directly—the first time she had ever spoken to them as anything other than a horror to be fled.

"My grandmother fed you for sixty years," she said. "Her mother before her, and hers before that, back to 1847. You took your payments. You took them *by contract*. This boy is not in the contract. He's not a Bell, not a founder, not a keeper. He's nine." Her voice cracked slightly on the number, and she let it. "So here's the deal. Release what you've drawn from him—all of it, the name included—and take the withdrawal from me instead. I'm the heir. I'm the one you've been carving into the walls. Take from the account that owes you."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and texture.

Then the woman in the fourth chair—his-mother's-face, grandmother's-generation, some keeper's daughter grown gray in service—lifted one gray hand. Slowly. And pointed, not at Wendell.

At the empty wooden chair.

"No," Wendell said.

The hand didn't waver.

"She said no," Theo translated unnecessarily, backing up a step. "Why is she saying no? Wendell, why is the scary chair lady pointing at the scary chair?"

"Because," Wendell said, and her mouth had gone dry, "the well doesn't do trades. It does *successions*. It doesn't want a payment from me. It wants a keeper seated. It's been trying to seat me since the day I inherited." She laughed, short and hollow, without any humor in it at all. "All those photographs. The extra chair. It wasn't haunting me. It was *holding my place*."

Marisol stepped forward, planted herself between Wendell and the chair like a small fierce wall. "Then it can learn to wait," she said, "because she has people now, and people complicate things."

For the first time in two hundred years, perhaps, something in the well chamber changed expression. The keepers' marble eyes shifted—all six of them, in unison—from Wendell to Marisol to Theo, and back. Considering. Recalculating.

And the woman with the familiar face lowered her hand, and mouthed three new words, slow and deliberate, and this time Wendell understood them perfectly, because they were the same words her grandmother's unfinished ledger entry had ended on:

*Three days. Solstice.*

The lights of their flashlights flickered once, all together, like a nod.

Above them, faint through miles of stone, the house settled on its foundations with a long wooden sigh—and somewhere upstairs, Wendell would have sworn, a clock she'd never wound began to tick.
