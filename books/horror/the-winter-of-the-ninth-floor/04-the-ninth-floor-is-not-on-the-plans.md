## 4. The Ninth Floor Is Not on the Plans

The maps lived in the cellar, which was the one room in Wintermere House that smelled of something other than lavender and disinfectant. Down there it was paper and cold stone and, faintly, coal — a smell from before Odile's lifetime, preserved the way the house seemed to preserve everything, whether or not anyone had asked it to.

Bram carried the lantern low so the light pooled on the flagstones and their shadows swung up the walls like slow pendulums. He had not wanted to bring her down here. She had watched him decide not to want to, and then decide to anyway, in the space of one long breath at the cellar door.

"Archive's this end," he said. "Everything from before the fire is boxed. Everything after is in the office upstairs. The plans are in the blue cabinet. Don't mind the damp on the bottom shelves, it doesn't hurt the paper. Seems to know to leave it be."

"Seems to know," Odile repeated.

Bram didn't turn around. "Things in this house get used to what they're for. That's all I mean."

The blue cabinet was a dented steel filing cabinet painted a blue that had once been cheerful and had given that up decades ago. Bram knelt, worked the sticky drawer with two sharp tugs, and lifted out a roll of plans tied with butcher's twine, the way you'd tie a joint of meat. He set them on a trestle table, weighted the corners with four stones that were clearly kept for the purpose, worn smooth and pale in the middle where decades of hands had set them down.

"Original architect's drawings, 1931," he said. "Then the 1958 renovation. Then the 1972 fire-safety refit — that's the one the council has. And the survey done after the fire in '74, when they made the repairs." He untied the twine with fingers that were careful in a way Odile had come to recognize. Careful the way a man is careful with something that has bitten him before. "You wanted to know the building. So. This is the building."

Odile leaned over the oldest plan first. Wintermere House unfolded beneath her hands in ink and faded wash: the long south wing, the chapel, the walled garden drawn with little pencilled trees that some draughtsman had clearly enjoyed himself over. She read the floor designations, running her finger up the stairwell that spiraled at the building's heart.

Ground. First. Second. Third. Fourth. Fifth. Sixth. Seventh. Eighth.

She counted twice, because her finger had gone ahead of her attention, and she wanted to be sure the building and she agreed.

Eight floors. The stairwell terminated at the eighth, in a room labelled COMMON ROOM — UPPER, with a note in a fine antique hand: *principal residents' lounge, south aspect, views.*

"That's the top," Bram said. "Eight floors. Has been since it was built."

Odile looked up at him. In the lantern light his face was all planes and patience, a face like a hillside in winter. He was watching her finger on the paper the way a man watches a weather front.

"And above the eighth?"

"Above the eighth is the roof, and above the roof is the sky, and above the sky is whatever there is above the sky." He said it evenly, without humor, which was somehow worse than if he'd laughed. "That's the order of things."

* * *

She should have let it go. She knew that even then, in the cellar, with the stones holding down the corners of the plans like the corners of a shroud. There was a version of her — the sensible version, the one her mother had hoped for — who would have thanked Bram, made a note about the upper common room, and gone back to her recordings of old women singing songs with holes in them.

But Odile Ashgrove had spent nineteen years chasing the places where the world kept rules, and she had learned the one lesson all such places taught: the rules are the confession. A house that counted its doors at dusk was a house that had once lost count. A building whose caretaker said *that's the order of things* in the voice of a man reciting a sentence handed down by a judge — that was a building with a verdict in its past.

So she unrolled the 1958 plan. Eight floors. The 1972 fire-safety refit: eight floors, though this one had the stairwell drawn with obsessive precision, every tread numbered, every handrail bracket specified, because fire officers are the only people who love buildings honestly.

She counted the treads. Then she counted the treads on the post-fire survey of 1974.

The stairwell had grown.

Not by much. Eleven steps' worth — she counted three times, checked the scale bar, did the arithmetic in her head with the cold clarity that always came to her in these moments, the clarity that had made her good at her work and difficult at everything else. The post-fire survey showed the stairwell running eleven treads longer than it had in 1972, and the extra treads simply stopped, in the middle of what the plan showed as solid hatching — the roof space, the dead attic, the part of a building that is nothing but structure and pigeons.

Eleven steps into the roof, ending nowhere.

"Bram," she said. "Come and count this."

He came. He stood over the plans and he did not count. She could see him not counting, the way you can see someone not looking at a thing, his gaze sliding off the stairwell the way water slides off wax.

"I've counted it," he said. "In 1998, when the lift was put in. I counted it again in 2011. It's eleven steps."

"Eleven steps to where?"

"To the door."

"What door?"

Bram straightened up. For a moment he looked at her — really looked, the first time since she'd arrived — and what was in his face wasn't secrecy. It was the tiredness of a man who has carried something up and down eight floors for fifty years and knows the weight by heart.

"There's a door at the top of the stairwell," he said. "Above the eighth floor. Painted shut. It's been painted shut since the fire. It's not on the plans because the plans say it isn't there, and the plans have been right about everything else, so." He spread his hands, and the gesture was so perfectly, so terribly reasonable that Odile felt the floor of her understanding tilt beneath her, the first slow degree of vertigo. "So we let the plans be right."

* * *

He took her up, because she asked, and because — she suspected — some part of him had been waiting fifty years for someone to ask.

The stairwell of Wintermere House was a spiral of grey stone with an iron rail worn bright at the grip by generations of hands. They climbed past the residents' floors, past the little landings with their night-lights and their framed embroidery, past the seventh floor where Mrs. Callow's door stood open and the smell of toast drifted out like a small domestic ghost. On the eighth-floor landing, Bram stopped.

The landing was like all the others except that it had two doors. One was the upper common room — through the frosted glass, shapes moved, someone was doing something gentle with a radio. The other door was at the top of three more steps.

The three steps were the same stone as all the others. The same wear, the same faint polish. They did not look like an afterthought. They looked like they had always been climbed.

And the door at the top of them was — Odile searched for the word and kept coming back to it — *patient*. A panelled door, oak by the look of it, painted over so many times that the panels had gone soft-edged, the paint built up in thick grey-white layers like frost that had never melted. There was no handle. There was the ghost of a handle, a smooth shallow depression where a handle would be, painted over and over until the paint itself had become the memory of a handle.

No sign on it. No KEEP CLOSED. No hazard tape. Nothing at all, which was its own kind of sign. Every other door in Wintermere House was labelled, numbered, counted. This one was simply not spoken to.

"The painters do it every cycle," Bram said. His voice had gone quiet, not hushed but *lowered*, the way you lower your voice in a church or a sickroom. "Every five years, same as the rest. They don't ask about it. They paint it like the rest and go home."

"Doesn't anyone —"

"Mrs. Enys asked about it once. 1989. She was a cleaner here, new." Bram's hand had found the iron rail and gripped it. "She asked why we paint a door that doesn't open. Next morning she'd handed in her notice. Never said why. Left Wintermere village the same week. I drove her to the station myself. She wouldn't talk in the car. She talked at the station, just before the train came. She said —" He stopped. He looked at the painted door with an expression of profound, disciplined neutrality, a man holding his face very still so it wouldn't say something. "She said the door had thanked her. For asking. She said it had been so long since anyone asked it anything."

The stairwell was silent around them. Below, faintly, the radio in the common room, and beneath that the house's own sounds — pipes ticking, wind working at the south eaves, the building breathing in the way old buildings do.

Odile stepped up onto the landing at the top of the three steps. Up close, the paint on the door wasn't just layered. It was *textured*. She put her fingertips against it — cold, colder than the air, cold like stone in shade — and felt the ridges beneath, and understood that the paint had been applied not in flat coats but in strokes, thousands of strokes, and that the strokes had direction. They flowed. Down from the top of the door and out toward the edges, like paint that had been trying to run, like a thing being poured over and smoothed down and poured over again, for fifty years, to keep it where it was.

Or, she thought, and the thought arrived with the specific chill of things that are true: to keep it *down*.

"Bram. The eleven steps. When were they built?"

"The house was built in 1931 with them. The plans say otherwise. The plans have always said otherwise." He said it flatly, a man reciting a catechism. "I've stood where you're standing and I've asked myself the same question. Here's what I've got, after fifty years. The house is eight floors. The house *insists* it's eight floors. And the stairwell goes up eleven steps past the eighth floor to a painted door, and the house insists that doesn't exist either, and both of those things are true at once, and if you stand here long enough trying to make them agree, you'll feel something in your head start to —" he made a small gesture near his temple, a loosening, a winding-down — "so I don't stand here long. I count my doors at dusk, I keep my rules, and I let the plans be right. It's not cowardice, miss. It's maintenance."

"The fire was in 1974," Odile said. "On this floor?"

"The fire was *said* to be on this floor. The upper common room. Wiring, they decided. Eleven residents were moved out of the wing for six months while it was made good." His jaw worked. "Nobody died. That's the official finding and it's true as far as it goes. But the smoke — the smoke went up. Smoke goes up, that's what smoke does, and there was smoke in the stairwell for three days after the fire was out, coming *down* those three steps, cool as you like, smelling of a fire that was cold. And the door —" His voice caught and steadied. "The door was warm. Not hot. Warm. Like a hand. The fire brigade painted it shut on the fourth day, and it's been painted every cycle since, and I have never once in fifty years let a resident climb these three steps, and I have never once told anyone what I'm telling you now, and I'd take it as a kindness if you'd tell me why you needed to know."

Odile took her fingers off the door. The cold stayed in her fingertips a long time.

"Because the songs stop," she said. "Every resident, mid-verse, at the same lines. Because three of them described the missing verses word for word, identically, like reciting something. Because Maren signs blank papers and calls them for the fire. Because your house counts its doors at dusk like a creature counting its teeth." She looked up at the soft grey panels, the paint that flowed like something poured. "And because I'm a folklorist, Mr. Bram, and everything I've ever learned says this: when a whole community loses the same piece of a story, it wasn't lost. It was *taken*. Somebody collected it. And collections are kept somewhere."

Above them — above the painted door, above the roof, above the sky — the house made a sound.

It was very small. A settling, you would have called it, if you wanted to sleep that night. A single tick of stone under strain, the kind of sound old buildings make. But it came from the door, from behind the paint, from the eleven steps' worth of nowhere, and it had a rhythm to it, and the rhythm was almost — not quite, but almost — the opening bar of the verse that Mrs. Tregowan could not sing past, the one about the winter and the counting and the door.

Bram had gone grey. He put his hand on Odile's arm and drew her back down the three steps, gently, firmly, the way you turn someone from a precipice, and neither of them spoke until they were two floors down and the air smelled of toast and lavender again.

* * *

That night Odile set her recorder on the windowsill of her room on the sixth floor, as she had every night since she arrived, and lay in the dark listening to the house count itself to sleep. Bram's voice, distant, moving along the corridors: *...nine, ten, eleven, twelve. All present.*

She was nearly asleep when she remembered the plans still laid out in the cellar, weighted under their four stones, the 1974 survey open to the stairwell page.

She got up to fetch them. It was that or lie awake doing the arithmetic again, and the arithmetic always came out the same: eleven steps, ending nowhere, in a building that insisted on eight.

The cellar was colder than it had been in the afternoon, or she was colder, or the difference between those two things was not as large as she would have liked. Her torch found the trestle table, the stones, the plans. She rolled the 1974 survey closed, tied the butcher's twine, and was turning to go when the torch beam caught the corner of the topmost plan — the 1931 original, the one she'd looked at first.

It had shifted. Only slightly; the stones were still in place. But the corner nearest the stairwell had lifted, curled back on itself, as if the paper had been warm once and had cooled into a new shape.

And in the margin, beside the stairwell, where that afternoon there had been only blank paper and the draughtsman's faint pencil trees, there was a small neat annotation in ink that had no business being fifty years old and yet looked older than everything else on the page, a hand like copperplate, like a ledger, like a signature practised ten thousand times:

*Eight floors is what was agreed.*

Odile stood in the cold with her torch shaking very slightly, and told herself: ink fades, damp lifts paper, old houses settle, tired women misremember.

Then she carried the plans back up six floors, set her recorder running on the windowsill, and lay in the dark with her eyes open, listening.

At 3:17 by the bedside clock, the recorder's little red light began to pulse. And a voice — courteous, patient, unhurried, with the tone of a man who has all the time that has ever been — said, clearly, into her room, into her sleep, into the architecture of her mind:

"You've seen the plans. Good. Now we can begin properly. Tell me — what do you remember of the year 1974?"
