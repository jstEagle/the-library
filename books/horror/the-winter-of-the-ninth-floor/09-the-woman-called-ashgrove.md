## 9. The Woman Called Ashgrove

Maren knew the morning by the smell of it.

There were mornings when her mind came back to her the way a bird returns to a window it has struck — dazed, bleeding a little at the edges, but present, *here*, beating against the glass of the world until someone noticed. Most days she lived in the fog, in the long grey corridor between 1974 and now, where the years stood in line like patients waiting for a doctor who never came. But some mornings the fog lifted, and she could see the whole ward of her life at once: the fire, the singing, the man on the ninth floor with his patient voice and his ledger, the papers she had signed and signed and signed.

This was such a morning. She woke with her hands already moving, smoothing the blanket flat, the way her mother had taught her to make a bed before she could reach the corners. *Tuck it tight, Maren-love. A loose bed lets the cold in.* Her mother's face came with the memory, whole and warm, and Maren held it the way you hold a match in wind — carefully, knowing it would gutter.

She lay still and listened to the house.

Wintermere talked in the early hours. Pipes ticking like a clock that had forgotten what it counted. Bram's boots on the stairs, slow on the fourth flight, where he always slowed, though he'd never say why. And beneath it all, the faintest sound, like a hymn sung through a wall, which was the sound of the house remembering what it held.

*Eight floors,* she thought. *And one that isn't.*

She had been nineteen the year of the fire. Nineteen and already a singer, the kind of voice that made old men in the village take off their caps, though she'd never thought of it as anything but breathing done prettily. She remembered the cold that November. She remembered the smell of paraffin and wet wool. And she remembered the woman who arrived with a suitcase full of wire and reels, who smiled too easily and asked too many questions, and whose name had been —

Maren sat up.

The name was there. It was there this morning, of all mornings, because the fog had lifted and taken its usual toll of mercy with it.

*Ashgrove.*

* * *

She found her in the day room, because of course she did. The girl — no, not a girl, a woman of forty perhaps, though she carried herself like someone who had never quite learned to put down roots, a woman who moved through rooms the way water moves through a house it doesn't own — sat by the window with her recorder and her notebook, and when Maren came shuffling in, she looked up with that expression Maren had seen once before, fifty years ago, on a different face.

*The look of a person who has found something and doesn't yet know it has found her.*

"Mrs. Voss," Odile said, warm, careful, the way you speak to a bird. "You're up early. Shall I make you tea?"

"You called me Maren before," Maren said. "In the corridor, on Tuesday. You said, *Maren, take my arm.*"

Odile's smile flickered — not faded, flickered, like a candle in a draught. "Did I? I'm sorry, I didn't mean to be familiar."

"You didn't." Maren lowered herself into the chair opposite, the good chair, the one with the view of the moor. "I asked you to. Though I don't remember asking. Isn't that funny." She folded her hands. They were old hands, spotted and knuckled, but this morning they were *hers*, and she was grateful for them. "You have a recorder."

"I'm collecting songs. Before the house is sold. The residents' songs — they shouldn't be lost."

"No," Maren agreed. "They shouldn't. Though it's a funny thing to say, isn't it, when they already have been."

The wind worried at the window. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed, and Maren counted it without meaning to. *One.* The house liked being counted. The house counted back.

"Tell me about 1974," Odile said, and Maren almost laughed, because that was the question, wasn't it, the only question anyone ever really asked her, the question the house itself asked through the wires at night. She had heard it. She heard everything the recorder heard, in her way; the house and she had shared a mind so long that its listening had become her listening.

"I'll tell you about the woman," Maren said. "The one who came before you."

Odile went very still. Stillness like that was trained, Maren saw — the stillness of someone who had learned that sudden movement frightens the thing they're trying to catch. "Before me?"

"You're a folklorist. So was she. Same trade, same tools, same hungry eyes." Maren tapped her own temple. "Same name, if the register was honest, and the register was honest because Bram kept it honest, Bram keeps everything honest except the things that matter. Ashgrove. She signed the visitors' book *E. Ashgrove*, and I signed under her, and I remember thinking what a strange small world it was, that two singers of old songs should come to the same dying house in the same winter."

The recorder's reels turned. Maren could hear them, faintly, the way she could hear the house faintly, and she spoke toward the little machine as much as toward the woman, because she had learned — in the fog, in the fragments — that some things needed to be said into a witness or they never properly happened.

"Her name was Edith. Edith Ashgrove. And she was good, you understand. She was *good* at it, the way you're good. She could sit with an old woman for an hour and come away with a song the old woman's own daughters had never heard. She made people want to give her things. That's the gift, isn't it? Not the collecting. The *making them want to give*."

Odile's hand had moved to the recorder, not to stop it — to steady it. Maren watched her do it and thought: *she doesn't know why she did that. She thinks it's for the machine.*

"She was here three weeks," Maren went on. "November into December. And in those three weeks she noticed what everyone notices and no one says. That we'd stop singing. Mid-verse, always the same place, always the same line, as if the words had been — " Maren searched for it, and found it, because the fog had lifted and the fog was cruel as well as kind — "as if the words had been *taken out of us*. Lifted. Like stones from a wall. You could feel the gap with your tongue, the way you feel a missing tooth.

"And Edith Ashgrove was not a woman to leave a gap alone. She put her ear to it. She recorded at night — oh yes, we had machines then too, great heavy things with reels like dinner plates — and she heard what you've heard. The voice. The questions about the fire. The *courtesy* of it, that's what got her, she told me, that's the thing she couldn't stop talking about. Not a monster's voice. A *clerk's* voice. Patient. Polite. Asking after your mother's maiden name the way a bank asks."

The wind rose. In the corner, the radiator knocked twice, like a knock at a door, and Maren's heart knocked with it, but she kept on, because this morning she could, and mornings like this were loans, and loans were called in.

"She found the ledger," Maren said. "In the archive, behind the water tank. Names and entries. *One summer. Her mother's face. The taste of oranges. Twenty years of Sundays.* All dated 1974, all in the same hand, a hand so neat it looked printed. And she understood what I didn't understand until much later, because I was nineteen and terrified and glad, in the shameful part of me, to be safe. She understood that the fire never happened. Not the way they said. The fire was *offered*. The keeper offered it — a real fire, a true one, the kind that takes a building and everyone in it — and then he offered the alternative. Our memories. Payment for protection. One memory per soul per year of safety, and we signed, all of us, because he made it so *reasonable*, because he was so *sorry to trouble us*, because the smoke was already in the stairwell and he was the only door."

Odile's face had gone the colour of the moor in February. "The fire was real."

"The danger was real. The fire was a *sales technique*." Maren smiled, and knew it was a terrible smile. "He's been keeping us safe from him for fifty years, dear. That's the whole of the contract. That's the whole of the house."

* * *

The tea went cold between them. Odile had not touched hers. Maren drank hers because her hands wanted something to do, and because this morning she could taste things, and tea tasted like being alive, and she wanted to be as alive as possible for what came next.

"Edith worked it out in six days," Maren said. "What's taken you — what, two weeks? You're slower. But then, you're kinder, and kindness is slow." She set down the cup. "She decided to climb."

"The ninth floor."

"There is no ninth floor. That's the point of it. It's not on the plans, it's not in the lift, the stairwell stops at a door painted shut since before the war, and everyone agrees, in that way people agree to a lie that's keeping them breathing. But Edith put her hand on that door and the paint was warm. Like skin. Like a forehead with a fever. And warm paint, she said, is an invitation, and she was not a woman to decline an invitation."

Maren closed her eyes. The memory came whole, which frightened her more than the fog ever had — whole memories were *expensive* here, whole memories were the currency, and she wondered, distantly, what this one would cost her, what she was spending by spending it.

"She climbed on the twenty-first of December. The solstice. She told me she was going, and I told her not to, and she smiled that smile — your smile, dear, the one you do when you've already decided — and she said, *Someone has to return what was taken. That's all a folklorist is, in the end. Someone who gives things back.* And she took her reels with her. All of them. Every song she'd collected, every verse that had been lifted out of us, she'd recorded them from the night voice — he *sang them back to us in our sleep*, you know, that's what the residents dream, that warm room above the clouds, he keeps them there, he keeps them *singing* — and she carried them up like a woman carrying fire the right way round."

The day room was very quiet. Even the house had gone quiet. Even the counting.

"And?" Odile's voice was thin.

"And at four in the morning she came back down." Maren opened her eyes. "That's the part no one believes. She *came back*. She walked into the hall, and Bram — young Bram, Bram with hair — went to her, and she looked at him, and she smiled, and she said, *Good morning, Bram. Lovely weather.* And it was lovely weather. It was snowing, and she'd never liked snow, and Edith Ashgrove had opinions about everything and shared them freely, and she stood there in the hall saying *lovely weather* in a voice with nothing behind it, like a bell with the clapper taken out."

Maren's hands had begun to shake. She let them.

"She lived another forty years. Did you know that? There's a grave in the village churchyard. Edith Ashgrove, 1922 to 2014. She lived here, in this chair, by this window, and every day she signed the papers they brought her — blank papers, *for the fire*, she said, though there was no fire anymore, the fire was used up, spent, a spent argument — and every day she hummed. One verse. Over and over. The same verse, the one she'd carried up, the one the keeper kept, and it was the only thing left in her that moved on its own." Maren looked out at the moor. "I used to sit with her. Toward the end. She didn't know me. But she'd take my hand when I hummed with her, and her fingers knew the tune, and I like to think some part of her was up there still, in the warm room, singing."

* * *

Odile had not spoken for a long time. When she finally did, her voice was careful, the way a voice is careful when it's walking on ice it has just noticed.

"Maren. What was her name? Her full name?"

"You know her full name." 

"Say it anyway."

Maren turned from the window and looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a thing you've been avoiding looking at, the way you finally read the letter you've kept unopened on the mantel for fifty years. And she saw it now, saw it as she should have seen it on the first day, in the hall, in the rain: the same hungry patience, the same trained stillness, the same way of making a room want to give her things. Fifty years, and the house had sent for another one. Or the blood had come back on its own, the way blood does, to the scene of what it lost.

"Edith Ashgrove," Maren said. "And you are Odile Ashgrove, and you did not choose that name, and you did not choose this house, and you have been telling yourself since you arrived that you came here for the songs." She leaned forward, and her old voice dropped to almost nothing, and the house leaned in with her. "Dear girl. Nothing about you is coincidence. You were *called*. The way the fog is called. The way the fire was called. Something up there has been reading your name off the ledger for fifty years, waiting for the ink to walk back in."

Odile stood. Her chair scraped, and the sound went through the quiet room like a crack in glass. "That's not — I found Wintermere in an archive. I applied for a grant. There were *forms*, Maren. There was a *committee*."

"There were forms in 1974 too," Maren said gently. "She showed me the letter that summoned her. It smelled of smoke, and the postmark was a town that burned down in 1911."

The recorder, forgotten on the table between them, turned its reels. And into the silence, soft as ash settling, came a sound neither of them had switched it on to catch — the faint hiss of the overnight track, and beneath the hiss, patient, courteous, unmistakable, a voice asking a question no one had asked it:

*"Will she come up, then? I've kept the room warm. I've kept it warm for fifty years. Ask her — ask her if she sings the second verse."*

Maren watched the colour leave Odile's face, and felt the fog gathering at the edges of her own mind, coming to collect the morning, coming to take the memory back as payment — and she reached across the table and gripped the younger woman's hand, hard, with the last of her lucidity, and said:

"He knows your name, child. He's always known. The only question left is the one Edith never got to answer — whether you climb to *give back* — " and the fog took her, gently, mid-sentence, the way it always did, and Maren Voss smiled at the window and the moor and the snow beginning to fall, and hummed the first line of a verse she could not finish.
