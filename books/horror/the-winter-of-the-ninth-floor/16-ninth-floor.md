## 16. Ninth Floor

The stairs did not end where stairs should end.

Odile had counted them as she climbed — a habit she'd caught from Bram, from the house itself, from the whole counting architecture of Wintermere — and somewhere past the two-hundredth step the numbers stopped meaning anything. The treads kept rising, worn stone giving way to floorboards, floorboards giving way to carpet the color of dried heather, and the light changed with every turn of the stairwell. Not daylight. Not firelight. Something between: the amber glow of an evening that had been burning steadily since 1974.

Behind her, Bram's breathing came in short, determined bursts. She had told him to stay below. He had told her, in the flattest voice she'd ever heard him use, that he had failed to climb these stairs once before in his life and would not make a habit of it.

The warmth reached her before the smell did. Warmth like a hand laid gently on the back of the neck. And then the smell — woodsmoke, old upholstery, tea gone cold in its pot, the particular sweetness of a room that has been lived in hard and loved harder and then sealed away like a letter no one dared open.

The stairwell opened.

Odile stepped out of the dark and into a common room suspended above the clouds.

She knew it was above the clouds because there were windows — tall, narrow windows along the far wall, the kind the plans had never shown — and beyond the glass there was no moor, no sky in any direction she understood. There was only cloud, vast and slow-moving, silvered at its edges, stretching to a horizon that curved the wrong way. The room sat on top of the weather like a boat becalmed on a white sea. When she pressed her palm to the glass, the cold on the other side was absolute, and the warmth inside did not seem to notice it.

*It kept the fire,* she thought. *It kept the night of the fire, and it has been keeping it ever since.*

The room itself was 1974 rendered in perfect, terrible detail. A wireless on the sideboard, its dial glowing. A tea trolley with six cups, five of them used. A jigsaw puzzle half-finished on a card table — a picture of Wintermere House itself, seen from the moor, and someone had been working on the sky. A calendar on the wall had stopped at a date in November she did not need to read to know. And everywhere, everywhere, the amber light of a fire that had never been allowed to finish burning, because the fire had been *paid for*, and what was paid for was kept.

The chairs were arranged in a semicircle, the way they were arranged downstairs in the common room every evening, facing a single armchair by the hearth.

The armchair was not empty. But it was not occupied, either — not in any way Odile could make her eyes agree to.

A woman sat there. Or the shape of a woman. She was knitting without wool, her hands moving in the old patient rhythm, and her cardigan was moth-eaten in places where moths had never been, and where her face should have been there was something Odile's mind kept sliding off of — not horror, not absence, but *incompleteness*, like a photograph left too long in the sun, like a song with its middle verses lifted out. Half of her was vivid: the hands, the set of her shoulders, a green scarf knotted at her throat. The other half was hollow, translucent, a woman you could read a page of a book through.

She was singing.

Odile knew the song. Of course she knew the song. She had heard it broken off mid-verse a dozen times downstairs, in a dozen cracked and careful voices, each singer stopping at the same line as though the words had been lifted out of them — because they had been, because they were *here*, and here they were now, pouring out of this half-a-woman in a voice like wind through a keyhole, complete, unbroken, sung all the way through to the end and then begun again.

Sung to empty chairs.

"Hello," Odile said.

The singing did not stop, but the woman's head turned — the vivid half of it, the half that still had eyes — and found her, and something in the hollow half seemed to lean toward her like a plant toward light.

"You're late," the woman said, between verses. Her voice was pleasant, worn, the voice of someone who had said the same things so many times they had gone smooth as river stones. "I did tell them you'd come. In seventy-four I said, *she'll come, someone will come, someone always comes for the songs.*" A small laugh, dry as paper. "Fifty-one years. You're late, but you're here, and that's the main thing. Are you the one who's come to write them down?"

"My name is Odile Ashgrove."

The knitting hands went still.

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere in the walls of the ninth floor, Odile heard — or felt — the house itself go quiet, the way a room goes quiet when a name is said that everyone has been avoiding.

"Ashgrove," the woman repeated. She looked down at her own hands, the vivid ones, turning them over as if checking them against a list. "That's my name. That was my name." She said it without drama, the way you'd read a fact off a form. "Eleanor Ashgrove. Folklorist. I came to record the residents' songs before the home was sold." A pause, and the hollow half of her face seemed to flicker, like a candle guttering. "That was the letter I wrote. I have written it many times. I am perhaps still writing it."

Odile's legs carried her forward without her permission. She knelt beside the armchair, close enough to see that the woman's eyes were brown, and kind, and so tired that the tiredness had become a kind of geology, layered and ancient.

"Eleanor. I'm — I think I'm your — " She didn't know how to finish the sentence. Descendant? Echo? Replacement? Summons?

"You're what came when the house called," Eleanor said simply. "Houses can't dial. They need a hand to hold the phone. I've been the hand a long while." She resumed knitting, though there was still no wool. "You'll want to know where he is. The keeper. Everyone wants to start with him, and it's the wrong place to start, but I understand. I started there too."

* * *

The keeper was in the kitchen.

That was the first thing that undid Odile — not a throne, not a dark presence coiled at the top of the stairwell, but a kitchen. A 1974 kitchen with a range and a dresser and a row of chipped mugs on hooks, and a tall man standing at the counter with his back to her, washing a single cup with the concentration of someone performing a sacrament.

He was not what she had imagined across all those nights of the recorder's patient voice. She had imagined smoke, or shadow, or a face made of the amber light itself. Instead he was — ordinary. Grey-haired, though grey in the way of something that had been grey for centuries. Tall and slightly stooped, like a man who had spent his life in rooms built for shorter people. He wore a cardigan with leather buttons, mended at both elbows, and when he turned and saw her, his face did the thing she would remember for the rest of whatever her life turned out to be:

It lit up. Briefly. Hopelessly. The way a face lights up when someone comes to the door and you realize, a half-second too late, that they haven't come for you.

"You're the new one," he said. "The Ashgrove that answers." He set the cup on the rack, precisely, handle to the right. "I've been keeping a cup for you. I keep a cup for everyone. It's — " he seemed to search for the word, and Odile understood with a lurch that he genuinely had to search, that the vocabulary of hospitality was all he had left and even that was worn through — "it's what you do. You keep things. That's the whole of it. That's all I am."

"You took their memories," Odile said. "Fifty years of them. You took Maren's — "

"I *kept* them." The correction came quickly, and there was no menace in it. That was the worst part. There was no menace anywhere in him. "There's a difference. A fire was coming — you've read the ledger, I can see it in your face, you have the look of someone who's read the ledger — and the fire was going to take everything. Every one of them. The whole house, the whole night, all at once, and no one would have been kept at all." He said *kept* the way other men said *saved*. "So I made an offer. I'm not proud of the shape of it. But I was young, in my way, and I had never — no one had ever taught me — " His hands opened, closed. "There is a difference between keeping a thing and taking it. I know that now. I have had fifty-one years to know that now. But knowing it and knowing how to give it back are two different rooms, and I have never found the door between them."

Odile thought of the ledger. *One summer. Her mother's face. The first verse of the mill song.* Entries in a neat, careful hand — and she understood suddenly that the hand had been careful the way a lonely person's handwriting is careful, each entry an act of attention, each stolen memory *examined*, *appreciated*, *kept* the way a child keeps beetles in a jar, with love and no understanding whatsoever of the beetle.

"The residents," she said. "Downstairs. They wake up weeping and don't know why. They stop singing at the same line. They sign blank papers because some part of them is still trying to pay a debt they can't remember incurring. That's what your keeping looks like from below."

"I know." And he did look like he knew. He looked like a man who had listened to every weeping night through fifty years of floorboards, unable to come down, unable to stop listening, unable to fix what his own care had broken. "I hear them. I hear everything. That's most of what I do now — listen to the people I emptied, trying to be full." He gestured vaguely at the ceiling, at the clouds beyond the windows. "I was made to be a keeper. Not a thief. There's a difference, and I crossed it, and I have been standing on the wrong side of it for so long that I no longer know the way back across."

"Then I'll show you," Odile said. "I came to return them. All of them. The songs, the summers, the faces. I'll write them back into the ledger — I've done the work, I've recorded everything, I know what was taken and from whom — "

"You can't."

"I already have. It's in my bag, on the landing — "

"You can't," he said again, and now there was something raw under the courtesy, something that had been raw for fifty-one years. "Because giving a thing back is a *keeping* in reverse, and I don't know how to do it, and neither, I think, do you — not alone. Every return has to be *received*. Someone has to open their hands. And the ones downstairs — " his voice caught on it — "the ones downstairs open their hands every night in their sleep, and I have never once been able to put anything in them. I set the memories down beside their beds and by morning they've rolled under the floor and I have to gather them up again before they spoil." He looked at his hands. "I am very good at keeping. I am, I believe, the best there has ever been at keeping. And I cannot give back so much as one verse of one song, and it is going to *unmake me*, if I have not already been unmade, if this — " he touched his own chest, gently, as if checking whether anything was still inside — "if this is not already what unmade looks like."

* * *

She found Eleanor Ashgrove again in the common room, still singing to the empty chairs, and this time Odile sat down in one of them and listened all the way through — the whole song, every verse, the verses that had been lifted out of a dozen throats downstairs, here whole and unbroken and pouring into a room with no one in it.

"Why the chairs?" Odile asked, when the song came around again.

"Because he keeps the residents up here too," Eleanor said. "Not their bodies. Their *seats*. Every memory he ever took, he set a place for. He lays the table every evening. He winds the wireless. He does the puzzle — " she nodded at the card table, at the half-finished sky over Wintermere — "a little more each night, so it will be ready when they come up. He has been setting this room for fifty-one years for people who cannot come, because no one ever taught him that you can keep a thing so well you kill it, and no one ever taught him the other thing either."

"Which other thing?"

Eleanor's vivid half turned to her, and the eyes — Odile's own surname sitting in them, fifty years deep — were wet.

"That you can *give* a thing," she said, "without anyone making you. That someone might climb all these stairs not because the house called them, but because they *chose* to. He's never seen it. Not once. Everyone who ever came up here came because they were taken, or summoned, or paid, or kept." Her knitting hands trembled. "He's going to ask you. He asks everyone, in his way. It's the only question he has left. And Odile — " the name came out of her like something she'd been holding in her mouth for half a century — "whatever you answer him, answer him *true*. Because he doesn't want your memories. He's had fifty years of memories. They're dust to him. He wants the one thing that was never in the ledger."

Behind her, in the doorway to the kitchen, the keeper stood with a cup of tea on a saucer, steam rising off it in the amber light, and waited — patient, courteous, hopeful, hollow — to ask her what he had waited fifty-one years to ask anyone at all.

"He's waiting now," Eleanor whispered. "Be kind. He has never once been answered."
