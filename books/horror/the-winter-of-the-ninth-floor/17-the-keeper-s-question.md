## 17. The Keeper's Question

The common room did not want her to leave.

Odile understood this the way she understood weather, or the mood of a house before a storm — not through evidence but through the pressure of it. The ninth floor held her the way a hand holds a bird: gently, completely, with no intention of harm and no intention of letting go. The wallpaper — roses gone amber with fifty years of sealed air — seemed to lean in when she moved toward the stairwell door. The armchairs arranged themselves by some patient geometry so that every path back to the center of the room passed the first Ashgrove's chair.

The first Ashgrove was singing.

She had been singing since Odile arrived, Odile realized. Not performing. Sustaining. The way a lighthouse keeper tends a lamp: not because anyone is watching, but because the dark is always there, and the song is the only thing between it and everything else. Her voice was thin as paper but it did not waver. It was the missing verses. All of them. Every line that had died in three residents' throats at the same word, every fragment Maren had lost to the ledger of small debts — they were all here, braided together into one long, unhurried melody that filled the warm room like water fills a bowl.

And the keeper stood at the window, watching the clouds.

He was not what she had expected, though she could not have said what she had expected. A monster, perhaps. Something with the architecture of a spider, all ledger-lines and grasping arithmetic. Instead he was a tall, narrow man in a cardigan the color of weak tea, his back to her, his hands folded behind him like a schoolmaster waiting for a classroom to settle. When he spoke, he did not turn around.

"You came up the stairs yourself," he said. "In fifty years, only two have done that. Both of you named Ashgrove." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "I've wondered about that."

"Have you?" Odile kept her voice level. Her recorder was still running in her coat pocket; she could feel its small warmth against her ribs like a second heartbeat. *Let it hear everything,* she thought. *Let Bram hear everything, if he's listening below. Let them all know where I went.*

"Names are invitations," the keeper said. "Yours was written into this house before you were born. I don't know who wrote it. Perhaps you do. Perhaps you don't, which would be its own kind of answer." He turned then, and she saw his face.

It was an ordinary face. That was the horror of it, and also — she admitted, with a lurch she didn't want to name — the tragedy. He might have been any man from any archive photograph: kind eyes going soft at the edges, a mouth made for asking questions rather than answering them. Fifty years of keeping had worn him smooth, like a stone in a stream. Or like a memory that has been handled too often.

"You've been interviewing me for weeks," Odile said. "Through the recorder. Every night. You asked about 1974. You asked about my mother. You asked what I'd trade to belong somewhere." She took a step toward the center of the room, toward the first Ashgrove, whose song did not pause but seemed, somehow, to make room for her voice inside it. "I'm here now. Ask your questions face to face."

The keeper studied her the way she had seen Bram study the door count each dusk — carefully, ritually, as if the act of looking were itself a form of protection.

"I had one question left," he said. "But it isn't the one you think."

* * *

The room was warm in a way that had nothing to do with fire, though the smell of fire was everywhere — woodsmoke and old wool and something sweeter underneath, like toffee burning very slowly at the bottom of a pan. Through the windows there were no moors, no rain, no Wintermere House below. Only cloud, thick and white and endless, moving with the slow grandeur of a glacier. The sun hung at four o'clock and had hung at four o'clock, Odile understood, since the year she was born.

She walked to the first Ashgrove's chair.

Up close, the woman was worse and better than she'd seemed from across the room. Worse, because half her face was hollowed — not wounded, not decayed, simply *absent*, the way a photograph fades where the light has struck it too long. Better, because her eyes, when they found Odile's, were present. Entirely, painfully present.

"You're her," the woman said, between verses. Her voice was roughened silk. "The one after. The one they called down for."

"My name is Odile."

"I know what your name is." A smile touched the intact half of her mouth. "It was my mother's name for a doll. Did you know that? No. Of course not. There's so much you don't know yet, and you're about to trade it all away, and you haven't even asked the right question."

"What's the right question?"

The first Ashgrove looked past her, at the keeper. The song faltered — one bar, two — and the whole room shuddered, the wallpaper roses seeming to wilt and revive in the same instant. She found the melody again, and the room steadied.

"He'll ask you soon," she said softly. "When he does, don't answer quickly. He's waited fifty years for an honest answer, and honesty takes longer than courtesy."

* * *

The keeper drew out a chair for her. It was the courtesy of it that undid her a little — the way he waited until she sat, the way he folded his hands on the table between them as though they were two people meeting over tea in a care home common room, which, in every way that mattered, they were.

On the table lay the ledger. She knew it instantly, though she had only seen its shadow — the archive copy downstairs, the one with names beside entries like *one summer* and *her mother's face*. This was the original. The ink here was not black but brownish-red, the color of dried roses, and the pages breathed faintly when the first Ashgrove sang, rising and falling like the flanks of a sleeping animal.

"You offered yourself," the keeper said. "Through the machine. Three nights ago. Yourself in exchange for their memories — all of them, every resident, returned whole. I accepted. I require you to sign, as everyone signs. That is the arrangement, and arrangements are how this house survives."

"Yes."

"But." He tilted his head, and for a moment the schoolmaster fell away and something older looked out — something tired beyond the reach of language. "Before you sign, I find I must ask. Not the question in the ledger. Not *what will you give*. I have asked that question for fifty years, and everyone answers, and the answers are always the same shape: *anything, anything, take anything but leave me the part that remembers being loved.*"

His hands unfolded. Refolded.

"My question is this." He leaned forward, and the lamplight — there was a lamp on the table; she hadn't noticed it arrive; she suspected the room provided things when the story required them — caught the wet gleam of his eyes. "Has anyone ever stayed here *willingly*? Not traded. Not taken. Not signed away. Chosen. Does such a thing exist — a person who remains in a place because remaining is what they want, freely, without being made to?"

The silence that followed was so complete Odile could hear the clouds moving outside, a sound like the sea heard from very far inland.

She thought of her flat in Norwich, sublet, anonymous, the walls thin enough to hear her neighbors' lives but never their names. She thought of eleven addresses in fifteen years, of boxes labeled in three different handwriting styles, of the folk festivals where she knew everyone's songs and no one's birthdays. She thought of the recorder running night after night in empty rooms, catching voices, because voices were the closest thing she had to company.

She thought of Maren, signing blank papers *for the fire*, lucid in fragments, calling her by another woman's name — and how, even fractured, even halved, Maren had reached for her hand across the blanket and held on like someone who had decided.

"No," Odile said.

The keeper went very still.

"No one has ever chosen to stay here willingly," she continued, and her voice did not shake, because this was the one thing she was sure of, the one thing all her years of listening had taught her. "Because staying here isn't chosen. It's *collected*. You take people's memories and call it payment, and then you keep them, and you call the keeping safety. But a person who cannot remember why they came is not staying. They're *held*. And belonging that's forced—" she met his eyes, and did not look away, and did not soften it— "belonging that's forced is just another theft. You didn't save these people in 1974. You bought them. And buying isn't love, however patiently you wait to be thanked for it."

The words fell into the warm room and did not echo. The room absorbed them, the way it absorbed everything — but she saw the keeper flinch, a small motion, like a man struck in a place he'd forgotten he had.

The first Ashgrove's song stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped. And in the silence, the whole ninth floor seemed to exhale — fifty years of held breath leaving the walls at once, the amber roses fading a shade paler, the clouds outside shifting for the first time, the frozen four o'clock sun trembling on its axis like a coin deciding whether to fall.

"That," the keeper said quietly, "is what I was afraid you'd say."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I needed to hear it from someone who wasn't kept." He rose from the table, and up close, standing, he seemed suddenly less solid — as though the question had been the last thing holding his outline together. "Everyone I have ever spoken to answered the way the ledger trained them to answer. *Anything. Take anything.* No one has ever told me the truth, because the truth requires a person who still owns enough of themselves to tell it." His gaze moved to the first Ashgrove, slumped in her chair, hollow-faced, spent. "You do. For now."

"For now," Odile agreed. "So here's my counter-question, keeper. You asked whether anyone ever chose to stay without being made to. Here's mine: has anyone ever been *let go*? Freely? By you?"

The keeper was silent for a long moment.

"No," he said. "I never learned how. Keeping is the only verb I have left."

"Then learn another one," Odile said, and pulled the ledger toward her across the table, and uncapped the pen that was waiting there — had always been waiting there, she understood now, for exactly this hand, exactly this name. "Because I'm not signing to stay. I'm signing to return. And you're going to help me carry it all back down."

Outside the window, the sun finally moved.

Below, faint through fifty years of sealed stairwell, she heard a door begin to count itself — and this time, the number it reached was thirteen.
