## 22. The Winter of the Ninth Floor

Spring came late to the moor that year, the way a shy guest comes to a party — hovering at the edge, apologizing, arriving only after everyone has stopped watching the door. The snow held on through the first week of April, lying in the hollows like unopened letters, and when it finally went, it went all at once, in a single warm night, and by morning the ground below Wintermere House was running with meltwater and the first black shoots of something determined.

Odile stood at the east window of what had once been the matron's office and watched the water find its way downhill. She had a notebook open on the desk beside her, and a pen, and no memory of what she had intended to write.

This was the shape of her life now. She had grown used to the shape the way you grow used to a limp, or a scar, or a room where a piece of furniture has been removed and the floorboards are paler where it stood. There were gaps in her. She had catalogued them herself, in the first weeks, with a folklorist's precision, before she understood that cataloguing them was like trying to hold water in a net. The gaps moved. They settled. They became, in time, less like wounds and more like weather.

Her mother's face was gone. She knew she had had a mother; she knew the word and its weight, the way you know the weight of a word in a language you no longer speak. But when she reached for the face, she found a warm blur, a smell of bread and coal smoke, a hand that was only a hand. Her childhood had no sound. She had written that down once — *my childhood has no sound* — and then sat looking at the sentence for a long time, because it was true and she could not grieve it properly, and that frightened her more than the loss itself. Grief, she understood now, is a form of memory. Take the memory and the grief goes with it, and you are left standing in a cleared field wondering why the ground is soft.

Her reason for coming was gone too. She knew the story the way you know a story — that she had arrived at Wintermere House in freezing rain to record the residents' songs before the developers came, that she had found the ninth floor, that she had climbed it, that she had signed her name in a ledger in place of fifty stolen lives. Bram had told her all of it, patiently, twice, and then Maren had told her, differently, and then the residents had told her in fragments over supper, each one holding up their piece of her like a shard of a mirror, and slowly the story had become hers in the way that a story becomes yours when enough people who love you keep telling it.

But knowing a story is not the same as remembering it. She had learned the difference the hard way, and the hard way had a name, and the name was Ashgrove, and it was hers, and some mornings she said it out loud just to feel it sit in her mouth like a stone she had chosen to carry.

* * *

The archive was hers now. That had been Maren's idea, or possibly Bram's, or possibly her own in a week she no longer had — the details of the arrangement had dissolved along with everything else, and no one had ever thought to write them down, which Odile found privately funny. The archivist of Wintermere House, hired on terms no one could produce.

The room was the old linen store on the second floor, dry and north-facing, and it held everything the house had decided to keep: the ledger, its final pages now filled with entries in her own handwriting — *returned: one summer; returned: her mother's face; returned: the sound of a child singing in a stairwell* — and the shelf of reel-to-reel tapes from 1974, and the newer recordings, and the boxes of residents' papers that Bram carried down one at a time with the reverence of a man handling relics, which, Odile supposed, was exactly what they were.

Her work was simple and endless. She recorded the residents. She transcribed them. She learned the songs — all the way through now, every verse, including the lines that had been lifted out of fifty years of throats and given back in a single burning night on the winter solstice, when the ninth floor had finally done what it should have done in 1974 and burned, properly, down to its foundations of warm air and held memory, releasing everything it had kept like migrating birds.

She had been there for that. She knew that much. Bram had told her she had been there, standing in the stairwell with soot on her face and an expression he could not describe and had tried, several times, over several suppers, to describe, and failed, and finally settled on: *like someone watching a debt get paid.*

She did not remember it. She had a gap where it should be, a ninth-floor-shaped absence, and sometimes at night she climbed the eight flights to the painted-shut door at the top of the stairwell — no longer painted shut, now simply a door, leading to a scorched and empty floor that the surveyors' plans still refused to acknowledge — and she stood there with her hand on the handle and felt nothing at all, which was either the price she had paid or the peace she had bought, and she had stopped trying to work out which.

* * *

The residents carried her back to herself in pieces, daily, without knowing they were doing it.

It began with small things. Mrs. Enys, who had traded away her mother's face in 1974 and got it back along with everything else, took Odile's hand one morning in the corridor and said, "You have your grandmother's way of standing, did you know? Hip against the wall. Waiting for the world to come to you." Odile had no grandmother she could recall. But she had looked down at herself — hip against the wall, waiting — and something in the description had fit her like a coat, and she had worn it ever since. *I stand the way grandmothers stand.* It was not a memory. It was better than a memory. It was a fact about herself, witnessed, given, kept.

Mr. Tredinnick taught her the Cornish counting song, all twelve verses, and when she stumbled on the seventh he laughed — a dry, papery laugh — and said, "You'll get it. You've got patience. That's the thing I remember most about you from before, girl. You waited out the rain like the rain owed you money." She had no memory of waiting out any rain. But she knew now that she was patient, that she had always been patient, that patience was load-bearing in her character the way a lintel is load-bearing in a wall, and she had Mr. Tredinnick to thank for the knowledge, and she thanked him, and he pretended not to be pleased, and was.

And Maren — Maren carried the largest pieces.

Maren was ninety now, and lucid in a way she had not been in fifty years, because her mind had come back to her whole on the solstice night, every fragment restored, every blank paper's worth of self returned. She remembered 1974. She remembered the fire, and the bargain, and the first Ashgrove — the folklorist who had climbed the stairs to steal the memories back and had been kept instead, half-hollowed, singing to empty chairs for half a century above the clouds. She remembered Odile's arrival, and the recordings, and the negotiation, and the climb. She remembered everything, and she spent it generously, the way rich people spend, handing Odile her own past back in installments.

"You laughed at Bram's door-counting," Maren told her one afternoon, in the garden, wrapped in a blanket against the late spring wind. "First night. You thought it was charming. You said, *a house that keeps rules is a house that keeps promises.* I've never forgotten it. I don't suppose you have either."

"I don't," Odile said. "But I'm glad to know I said it. It sounds like something I'd say."

"It is," Maren said. "It's exactly something you'd say. That's the thing, Odile. Everyone here keeps telling you who you were, and every single one of them describes the same woman. Do you know how rare that is? Most people are different in every mouth. You're the same in all of ours. Whatever the house took, it couldn't take that. The house could take your memories but it couldn't take your *manner*. You're still made of the same material. You just have to be told what you built with it."

Odile looked out at the moor, brown-green and steaming in the sun, and thought about that. *Belonging isn't remembered,* she had told the keeper — she knew this from the story, from Bram, from Maren, from the ledger's last page where her own hand had written it as if to make it true — *belonging isn't remembered, it's practiced.* She had said it in the dark on the ninth floor to a lonely custodian who had never learned how to give anything back, and it had been the hinge on which everything turned.

She was practicing it now. Every day. The kindness the residents carried back to her was not a memory she had lost; it was a habit she still had, and they recognized it the way you recognize a tune from another room. She was relearning herself not by digging but by doing — by standing hip against the wall, by waiting out the rain, by counting doors at dusk with Bram, by singing the songs all the way through, twice, the way the house now insisted, the way the residents now loved.

* * *

Bram counted the doors at dusk still. He would always count the doors. But the ritual had changed its weather. Where it had once been a ward — a fence against something hungry in the architecture — it was now something closer to grace. He walked the corridors at sundown with his ring of keys he no longer needed, touching each door, saying the number aloud, and the residents had taken it up with him, some of them, so that on a good evening the count moved through the house like a wave, voices passing the number from room to room, *nine, ten, eleven, twelve,* and the house settled around the number like a dog around a fire.

Twelve doors. Always twelve now. The thirteenth had not come back, and no one missed it.

The sale had died quietly. The developer's representative had left in February with unsigned papers and a baffled expression, and the follow-up letters had grown rarer and then stopped, and in March the residents had voted — properly, with a show of hands in the common room, Bram counting aloud, because of course he counted aloud — to keep Wintermere House. There was talk of a trust, of heritage funding, of Maren's nephew who knew a solicitor. Odile recorded the whole meeting and labeled the tape *Wintermere decides its own future, 12 April,* and filed it on the shelf beside the 1974 reels, and thought: *there. That's the answer to his question.* The keeper's true question, the one he had asked on the ninth floor, the one she had answered in the dark — *whether anyone ever chose to stay without being made to.* Here it was, answered in a common room with tea and biscuits and a show of hands. People choosing to stay. Not made to. Choosing.

She wondered, sometimes, if he had heard it. If sound traveled down a burned stairwell the way memory had traveled up it.

* * *

She had her answer in April, on a night of hard rain.

She had taken to leaving the recorder running at night — not to catch anything, not anymore, but out of habit, and because the residents liked knowing their songs were being kept even while they slept. She played the tapes back in the morning over tea, and mostly they were what they should be: the house ticking, the wind, Bram's late footsteps, someone talking in their sleep about a dog they had owned in 1961.

But some nights — not every night, and never twice in a row, as if whatever it was had learned the old rules and was being careful with them — there was a voice.

It was faint. It was courteous. It was, she thought, the same voice that had interviewed her through the dark in her first weeks, the patient voice that had asked and asked about 1974, the keeper's voice, the voice of the lonely custodian who had never learned how to give anything back until a woman with the wrong surname climbed his stairs and taught him.

It did not ask anything now. It said one thing, and only one thing, and always the same thing, and always at the very end of the tape, just before dawn, just at the edge of audibility, as if it had to lean a long way down eight floors of burned air to say it.

*Thank you.*

The first time she heard it she had sat very still with her tea going cold, and then she had gotten out the ledger and turned to the last written page and read her own entry — *the debt is paid; the house is free; let it keep what it loves* — and then she had said, out loud, to the empty archive room, to the house entire: "You're welcome."

The house creaked. It might have been the wind. It might have been the heating. It might have been a building that had spent fifty years taking and was now, clumsily, learning the other verb.

She said *you're welcome* every time now. It was part of the practice. Belonging, she had learned, is not remembered and it is not given and it is not even, in the end, chosen — not once, not in a single grand gesture on a burned stairwell. It is practiced, daily, in small counts and small kindnesses, in doors numbered aloud and songs sung through, in *you're welcome* said to the dark on the chance that the dark is listening and trying, in its way, to be polite.

* * *

On the last morning of April, the moor bloomed.

It happened between one day and the next, the way it always did up here — the gorse went from black to gold overnight, whole hillsides of it, blazing under a washed sky, and the residents crowded the windows like children, and Bram opened every door in the house to let the smell in, counting them as he went, twelve, twelve, twelve, and Maren sat in the garden in three blankets with her face turned up to a sun she had almost forgotten owed her nothing.

Odile brought the recorder out and set it on the table beside her, and Maren looked at it, and looked at her, and smiled.

"Shall we?" Maren said.

"Shall we what?"

"The one my mother sang. The one I gave back to you, that night, when the house went silent to listen. You've never recorded it all the way through. I'd like it kept. Properly kept. In your archive, with your label on it, in your handwriting."

Odile set the reels turning. The little spools began their patient work. And Maren sang — all the verses, all the way through, her old voice cracked and true, and the gorse blazed on the hills, and the house stood solid behind them with its twelve doors and its kept promises, and Odile listened and did not remember the ninth floor, and did not remember the fire, and did not remember the price, and found — sitting in the spring sun beside a woman who remembered everything, in a place that had chosen to keep her — that she did not need to.

When Maren finished, there was a long quiet, the good kind, the kind a house makes when it is listening.

And then, from the little machine, faint, courteous, from a long way down or a long way up, the voice said what it always said, and Odile said what she always said back, and the tape turned, and turned, and kept them both.
