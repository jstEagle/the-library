## 6. The Missing-Class Problem

Senna Hallow was the kind of child who arrived at school like weather—you didn't question her presence so much as adjust to it. She sat third from the window in my Year Five class, beside the radiator that clanked like a man trying to get out of a trunk, and she had a habit of finishing her work early and then staring at me with the frank, unblinking appraisal of someone deciding whether I was worth the trouble. Most days she concluded I was not, which I found oddly restful.

On the fourth of December, a Tuesday, her chair was empty.

I noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth—not immediately, but with a slow, tongue-probing certainty that something was wrong with the shape of things. Twenty-two faces looked back at me instead of twenty-three. The room felt lighter by one specific weight.

"Anyone know where Senna is today?" I asked, lightly, the way teachers ask such things—expecting *dentist*, or *her mum's poorly*, or the shrug that means *nobody tells me anything either*.

What I got instead was silence of a particular texture. Not the ordinary silence of children who don't know an answer. This was the silence of children who do know, and have been taught—by whom?—that knowing is not to be shared. A boy named Callum looked down at his exercise book with sudden, tremendous interest in long division. A girl near the front exchanged a glance with her neighbour that lasted half a second and contained an entire conversation.

"All right," I said, and moved on to fractions, because that is what teachers do: we paper over absences with arithmetic and call it resilience.

But I wrote her name on the attendance register and left the little box blank, and all morning the blank box sat there like an open mouth.

* * *

At break I took the register to the office, where Mrs. Vane presided over the front desk with the serene authority of a woman who has answered the same telephone for thirty years. I set it down and tapped Senna's line.

"No contact from home this morning," I said. "No note, no call. Should someone ring round?"

Mrs. Vane's pen stopped moving. It was a small thing—a pen stopping—but I had been at Marrowfen long enough by then to know that small things here were load-bearing. The pen hovered above the page for perhaps two seconds before resuming its journey, and in those two seconds her face did something complicated: a flinch folded inside a smile folded inside nothing at all.

"I'll speak to the family," she said. "It'll be a family matter."

"A family matter," I repeated. "She's nine."

"They're often nine." She said it gently, as if correcting my grammar rather than my concern. "Elodie—Mr. Brightwater—the Hal-lows keep to themselves. If there were anything to worry about, you'd be told what you needed to know."

The sentence was a masterpiece. I've thought about it since, the way you think about a lock pick: *you'd be told what you needed to know*. Not *there's nothing to worry about*. Not *don't worry your head*. Just a precise, calibrated rationing of information, delivered with tea-warm kindness. It was the first time I understood that Marrowfen Primary did not merely contain secrets; it administered them, in doses, by role.

Behind me, the photocopier hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a child laughed, bright and unguarded, and the sound seemed almost indecent.

"Has she been off before?" I asked.

Mrs. Vane filed the register without looking at it. "Children are often off in December," she said. "Colds. Coughs. The dark comes early and families hunker down." Then, brighter, with the brisk pivot of a woman changing records mid-song: "Did you get the new reading scheme boxes? Mr. Okafor was meant to show you the storeroom."

And that was that. I walked back down the corridor past the four desks with names taped over them—taped over, I remind you, not removed; the school had chosen concealment rather than absence—and I thought: *four covered desks, one empty one, and a headteacher who rations worry like wartime sugar.*

I am, by training and temperament, a man who trusts systems. Registers exist so that absences are noticed. Safeguarding policies exist so that noticed absences are acted upon. There is a whole architecture built, brick by bureaucratic brick, precisely so that a nine-year-old girl cannot simply evaporate from a village school while adults exchange glances. What unsettled me that Tuesday was not Senna's absence. It was how smoothly the machinery absorbed it—how every gear engaged exactly as designed, and yet no cog anywhere turned toward the police, the social worker, the phone.

It was as if the system itself had been... rerouted. Long ago. By people who knew what they were doing.

* * *

I tried Okafor at lunch.

Daniel Okafor teaches Year Six in the classroom opposite mine, a broad, deliberate man with reading glasses pushed up into grey-flecked hair and the unhurried manner of someone who has learned that haste in Marrowfen gets you nowhere faster. He was marking books when I leaned in his doorway, and he saw my face and set his pen down before I'd said a word, which told me he already knew why I'd come.

"Senna Hallow," I said.

"Mm." He took his glasses off. That too was telling. You take your glasses off when you want a moment to not see clearly.

"No one's called home. No one's reporting it. Mrs. Vane says it's a family matter. Daniel, our safeguarding policy is laminated. It's *laminated*, which in my experience means someone once failed to follow it catastrophically."

Almost—almost—he smiled. "You've been reading the staff handbook."

"The staff handbook has a section on December absences," I said. "Half a paragraph. It says staff should 'exercise seasonal discretion.' What does that mean?"

"It means what it says."

"It means nothing. It's a hole shaped like words."

He gestured me in and shut the door, and the click of it was very quiet and very final. Through his window I could see the playground, children stamping steam into the cold, alive and loud and gloriously unremarkable. He followed my gaze, then drew the blind halfway, which I chose not to find ominous and failed.

"When I started here," Okafor said, "eleven years ago, a boy in my class didn't come in one December morning. Danny Ferrier. I did exactly what you're doing. Rang home—no answer. Rang the office—Vane said family matter. So I drove to the house after school." He turned his pen over in his fingers. "Lights on. Telly going. Mum answers the door, red-eyed, and says Danny's poorly and he'll be back soon. And behind her, on the stairs, I'd swear—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully. "I'd swear I heard a kid laughing. Upstairs. Danny's laugh. I'd know it; I'd heard it every day for a term."

"And?"

"And I stood on the step like an idiot, and the mum said thank you for coming, and closed the door, and Danny never came back to school. But he wasn't dead, Elodie. That's the thing you need to hold onto, though God knows it doesn't feel like comfort. The mum wasn't grieving. She was—" he searched for it, "—*observant*. Like a woman keeping a vigil she'd been trained for. Like she knew where he was, and that where he was had rules."

"What rules? Who makes rules like that?"

Okafor put his glasses back on, and behind the lenses his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with marking. "You found the key," he said. "In your desk drawer. Don't look surprised—I found mine the same way, my first winter. Brass, older than the building has any right to make its own fittings. UNDERCROFT stamped on the bow."

My hand went to my jacket pocket before I could stop it, and he watched the gesture with something like sorrow.

"Every new teacher gets one," he said. "First December. It's not an accident, and it's not a test, exactly. It's more like..." He exhaled. "Like the school introducing you to something. In its own time. At its own pace."

"Introducing me to what?"

"You asked about Senna."

"Senna."

"Every winter," Okafor said, "since '74."

The words landed in the room and just stayed there. Outside, the bell rang for afternoon lessons, shrill and ordinary, and neither of us moved. Down the corridor, doors opened, feet thundered, life resumed its noise—and inside that small blind-drawn classroom, the sentence hung between us like breath in cold air.

"Since 1974," I said. "Fifty years. Every winter, a child goes missing, and nobody calls the police."

"Oh, the police are called," Okafor said mildly. "Once. By whoever's newest and most stubborn. They come out, they knock on doors, the family says the child's staying with relatives, the relatives confirm it—calmly, rehearsed-ly—and the constable writes it up and goes away, and by spring the file gathers dust and everyone pretends the whole thing was a paperwork hiccup." He shrugged, huge and helpless. "The system works perfectly, Elodie. That's the horror of it. It's just pointed at the wrong job."

"And the children? The ones from previous winters? Do they come back?"

"Some do." He said it so quietly I nearly missed it. "In January, usually. Walking up the lane like they'd been away a weekend. Pale, quiet, hungry. They don't remember much—or they say they don't. Senna's cousin came back in '09. Sat right where Senna sits now, actually. Third from the window." His eyes flicked to the window as if the glass might confirm it. "She used to stare at the radiator sometimes. Like she was listening to it."

"The radiator clanks," I said stupidly.

"Yes," said Okafor. "It does."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I did next, because honesty is the only currency I've ever trusted and I was about to learn how badly it spends.

I rang Senna Hallow's number from my cottage that evening. It rang eleven times and disconnected without voicemail. I rang again. Same. On the third attempt a woman answered—not Senna's mother, an older voice, dry as paper—and when I introduced myself as Senna's teacher she said, without surprise, without warmth, in the tone of a woman reciting lines she'd memorised decades ago:

"She's poorly. She'll be back after Christmas. Thank you for calling."

"After Christmas," I said. "That's six weeks. She's nine years old and missing school for six weeks and nobody's—"

"She's *poorly*," the woman said, and there was something underneath the flatness then, some tremor of feeling held down with both hands, and she added, softer, almost kindly: "Ask Mr. Okafor about the winter of '98, love. Then decide how much noise you want to make."

The line went dead.

I sat in my rented cottage with its smell of old smoke and its single bar of the fire, and I looked at the brass key on the table where I'd left it after the night in the stairwell with my recorder, and I thought about the transcript still folded in my notebook—that child's voice at 3:11 a.m., answering questions nobody had asked aloud, answering one *I* hadn't asked: *What was your name before?*

I had told myself it was interference. Crosstalk from some radio frequency bleeding into cheap equipment. I had told myself a great many efficient, professional things.

Now I laid the transcript beside the attendance register photocopy I'd made—against policy, but I'd stopped pretending policy governed anything real here—and I looked at Senna Hallow's blank box, and the word *poorly*, and *after Christmas*, and *every winter since '74*, and the four taped-over desks, and the key, and the voice in the dark that knew to ask about names.

Individually, each item was deniable. I had denied them all, one by one, with the diligence of a man bailing a boat with a thimble.

Together they were not atmosphere anymore. Together they were a machine. Gears, tolerances, maintenance schedules, trained personnel, fifty years of operation without a single unscheduled stop. Marrowfen did not suffer from this thing happening to it. Marrowfen *ran* this thing, the way a mill runs a wheel—quietly, seasonally, with the resigned competence of people who have learned that the alternative to the machine is being inside it.

And somewhere below the boiler room, down stairs that appeared on no map, behind a door my key fit, a nine-year-old girl was either dead or *waiting*—and I found, to my own disgust, that the waiting frightened me far more than the death would have.

Because the dead, whatever else you say of them, are finished.

The waiting can still be bargained with. And everything in this village, every glance and laminated policy and rehearsed telephone voice, suggested that the bargaining had been going on for fifty years—and that the price was always paid by someone else's child, until it wasn't.

I picked up the key. It was cold in a way the room wasn't, cold in a way that felt less like temperature than like attention.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would get into the records room. Whatever Vane was rationing, whatever Okafor was softening, there was a beginning to all this—a year, a name, a first mistake—and beginnings are the one thing machines can't fully hide. Something happened in 1974. Someone signed something, or stole something, or opened something that should have stayed shut.

The paper trail would be in the school. Paper was the one language I spoke fluently.

I just didn't yet know that somewhere in those records, waiting under fifty years of dust, was a photograph of a caretaker holding a black ledger—and that the moment I saw his face, I would recognise, with a lurch I still haven't recovered from, a man who had done exactly what I was doing now.

A newcomer. Asking questions. Holding a key.

Reaching, like me, for the one thing the vault wanted back.
