## 7. Records Room

There is a particular kind of silence that a school keeps after dark, and I had learned it by my fourth week at Marrowfen Primary: not empty, exactly, but *held*. The building didn't rest at night so much as wait, the way an old dog waits by a door for someone who isn't coming home. I told myself this was the boiler settling. Boilers settle. Buildings breathe. Nothing in a school has any business being awake.

I was lying to myself with great fluency, which is one of my few remaining talents.

The records room sat off the main corridor behind a door that might once have been green and was now the colour of institutional surrender. A laminated sign read STAFF ONLY — PLEASE KNOCK, in the cheerful Comic Sans of a place that wanted very badly to be harmless. During the day it was Mrs. Vane's domain; she kept the key on a ring with about forty others, all of them brass, all of them patient. I had watched her open it twice, both times stepping inside quickly and pulling the door shut behind her with her hip, like someone closing a fridge on something that had moved.

The school map — the one that did not contain the undercroft — listed the records room as a supply cupboard. Another misprint, presumably. Marrowfen Primary was, by its own paperwork, mostly misprint.

Here is what I knew, or thought I knew, going in:

Four desks in my classroom with names taped over them. Mr. Okafor's face when I'd asked, the particular grey he went — not fear, worse than fear, the colour of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. Senna Hallow's empty seat, three days running, and no phone call to anyone. And underneath all of it, humming like a bad wire, the recording on my laptop that I had listened to eleven times and deleted twice and restored from the recycle bin both times, because I am a rational man and rational men do not keep ghosts in their trash folder.

*What was your name before?*

I don't have a name before. That's the whole point of me. But we'll come to that.

The point of tonight was simpler: schools keep records. Records are where institutions confess, usually without meaning to. If children had been going missing from this school every winter since before I was born, then somewhere in that green door there were enrolment books, attendance registers, incident logs — paper trails, and paper doesn't know how to be silent. Paper only knows how to say what happened.

I waited until half nine. The village went dark early and completely; Marrowfen seemed to regard electricity after ten as showing off. I let myself into the corridor with the master key I'd been issued — they gave new staff alarming access here, as if daring them to look — and stood outside the records room listening to the building hold its breath.

The lock was older than me and surrendered on the second try.

Inside, the air was dry and papery and faintly sweet, the smell of glue and dust and decades. Moonlight came through a high window in a slanted grey bar, and in it floated the usual motes, except that in Marrowfen I had begun to distrust even dust. It seemed too deliberate. It hung like an audience.

Shelves ran floor to ceiling along three walls. Boxes labelled in fading marker: *Registers 1961–68*, *Dinner Money*, *Sports Day (DO NOT DISCARD)*. Someone had loved this room once, or at least feared it enough to organise it. I found the enrolment books on the middle shelf, bound in maroon cloth, their spines stamped in gold that had gone the colour of old teeth.

I took down 1970–74 first, because you always start with the wound.

The book opened easily, too easily, and I saw the problem before my brain finished naming it. Pages had been removed. Not torn — razored, cut close to the spine with a steadiness that suggested either great care or great practice. One page gone, cleanly, and the next page beginning again mid-alphabet as though nothing had happened. I turned back. The stubs were there, thin shadows of themselves, and against the light I could just make out the ghost of ink that had bled through from the missing side: fragments of names, the tops of letters, a date.

December. Every fragment said December.

I pulled the next volume. 1975–79. Same surgery. One page excised, December dates bleeding through the stubs. Then 1980–84. Then the eighties proper, the nineties, the two-thousands, right up to the book currently in use, whose most recent December page had been cut out so recently the edges were still pale, unyellowed, raw as a scab picked too soon.

Twenty-three cuts. I counted them twice, sitting on the floor with books stacked around me like a fort built against something, and the number arrived with a click I felt in my teeth. Twenty-three razored pages. Twenty-three faces in my class, counting the four covered ones. I don't believe in coincidence; I believe in pattern, and pattern is just coincidence that's been promoted.

Except — no. Let me be honest, since honesty is the one luxury this story affords anyone. I counted twenty-three because I wanted twenty-three. The actual count was harder to take. Some volumes held two missing pages. Some years, the knife had visited twice.

I sat in the moonlight doing the arithmetic of the dead and got nowhere good.

* * *

It was while I was re-shelving the nineties that I found the photograph.

It had been used as a bookmark, which felt almost profane — a life pressed flat between the pages of a register like a flower someone had lost interest in. A postcard-sized print, curling at the corners, the sepia going brown at the edges the way photographs do when they're dying of age.

A group shot, staff and grounds, taken outside the front entrance on a bright day that looked borrowed from another planet. Teachers in cardigans squinting at the sun. Children cross-legged on the grass in two rows, grinning the ferocious grins of the seventies, gap-toothed and immortal. And standing slightly apart at the edge of the frame, the way caretakers always stand, holding a rake he clearly had no intention of using —

Thomas Grell.

His name was written on the back in pencil, along with *Xmas '74*, in handwriting that belonged to someone who still believed the future would want to know.

He was a big man, Grell, with the heavy forearms of someone who'd spent his life lifting things for other people, and a face that might have been kind once before something had come along and used it up. He wasn't looking at the camera. He was looking down and slightly to his left, at the object in his other hand, the one the rake had been posing for.

A ledger. Black, thick-clasped, roughly the size of a Bible, held against his chest the way you'd hold a thing that was either precious or radioactive and you hadn't decided which.

Everyone else in the photograph was performing for posterity. Grell was the only one who seemed to know the camera was a lie. He looked like a man photographed at his own funeral, aware of it, unwilling to spoil the occasion by saying so.

I turned the picture over. *Thomas Grell, caretaker, Xmas '74.* And beneath that, smaller, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn through:

*he should never have taken it out of the ground*

No signature. Of course no signature. In Marrowfen even the graffiti practised discretion.

I sat with that photograph for a long time. Long enough for the moonbar to crawl off the shelf and leave me in the dark, which I only noticed when I tried to look at Grell's face again and found I was holding him from memory instead. A caretaker. December 1974. A black ledger carried out of a building that officially has no undercroft, in a village that officially loses no children, recorded in books that officially contain no gaps.

Every institution lies the same way: by omission, neatly, with good binding.

And beneath the investigator's thrill — because yes, there was a thrill, I won't pretend otherwise, there is a moment in every hunt when the trail goes hot and your blood forgets to be afraid — beneath that, something colder was setting in. The thrill curdled the way milk curdles: gradually, then all at once, and then it's not milk anymore and no amount of wishing will make it milk.

Because here was the thing I kept circling and refusing to land on. Razored pages are removal. Removal implies shame, cover-up, a district office that wants tidy numbers. That I could understand. Institutions eat their dead all the time; it's practically the job description.

But the cuts weren't recent. They spanned fifty years, made by different hands, in different decades, and every single one of those hands had known to cut in December. Generations of headteachers, each arriving fresh, each finding the tradition already waiting for them like a loaded gun in a drawer. You don't inherit a cover-up that faithfully. Cover-ups decay. Secrets leak. People talk, especially small-town people, especially over sherry.

Unless it isn't a cover-up.

Unless it's maintenance.

Unless every one of those headteachers stood where I was standing, in this same papery dark, holding the same razor, and understood — the way you understand not to whistle at night, the way you understand which stair creaks — that the page had to go, because the name on it had to stop existing anywhere that could be read aloud.

You don't erase a child from a register to hide a crime. You erase a child from a register because the alternative is saying the name, and everyone in Marrowfen knows — in the way water knows downhill — what happens when you say the name.

My hands had gone cold. I noticed this with clinical interest, the way you notice someone else's hands.

* * *

I should have left then. Any sensible man would have left then, taken the photograph, gone home to his cottage that smelled of old smoke, poured the whisky he pretended was medicinal, and booked a train south by morning light.

Instead I did what investigators do, which is mistake compulsion for courage. I went through the boxes.

Mostly it was the sediment of any school: sports results, nativity programmes, a 1988 petition to save the swimming pool (failed). But in a box marked MISC — DO NOT DISCARD, in the same hand that had guarded Sports Day, I found parish material that had no business in a primary school. Burial registers. A tithe schedule. And a folded newspaper clipping from the *Marrowfen Advertiser*, dated the second week of January 1975, four columns wide by column-inch standards but printed small, tucked toward the bottom of page five, between a church fete and an advertisement for tractor parts.

The headline read: **CARETAKER MISSING; SCHOOL QUIET ON CIRCUMSTANCES.**

The article was a masterpiece of the local form. Thomas Grell, 51, last seen the evening of December 21st. Possessions intact at his cottage on Mill Lane. Police inquiries "ongoing." Headteacher Mrs. Edith Marsh (a name I recognised — Vane's predecessor, or her predecessor's predecessor) quoted as saying the school community "wished him well wherever he has gone" and declining further comment. No mention of anything unusual. No mention of a child. No mention of the fact — and I checked this three times against the enrolment stubs — that the December page for 1974 had been cut from the register *before* Grell disappeared, because the bleed-through ink was old, browned, decades settled into it.

They had cut the page first. Then the caretaker went. Or — reorder that sentence however you like; the grammar refuses to make it better.

The clipping ended with a paragraph that had clearly fought its way past an editor: *"Mr. Grell was known locally as a quiet man devoted to the upkeep of the school buildings, particularly the older foundations. Neighbours recall he had of late become troubled, though none would say by what."*

None would say by what. Fifty years later, none were saying still.

I put everything back exactly as I'd found it — the books, the boxes, the photograph I kept, sliding it inside my coat against my chest like contraband, which I suppose it was. I locked the green door. I stood in the corridor in the held-breath dark and listened to the building not-sleep around me, and somewhere below the floor, below the boiler room, below whatever the map refused to print, I heard — or imagined, and God, how I hoped imagined — a sound like a page turning.

Slow. Unhurried. The sound of something reading ahead.

Back in the cottage, I pinned the photograph above my desk and wrote one line in my notebook, the way I used to at the start of every investigation, back when investigations were about grant fraud and missing heirs and things that stayed explained:

*Find Thomas Grell.*

Underneath, without deciding to, my pen added a second line. I stared at it for a long time before I let myself admit I'd written it.

*Find out what he took out of the ground.*

Outside, the fog had come up off the fen the way it does here, swallowing the lane lamp by lamp, and in the silence between the wind's breaths I could hear the school, half a mile away, keeping its winter count.
