## 14. Okafor's Bargain

I should have known something was wrong when Okafor offered to make the tea. In eleven weeks he had never once made the tea. He guarded the staff room kettle with the territorial devotion of a man defending a border post, and he had opinions about how long a bag should steep that he expressed the way other men express political grievances. But that Thursday, the day after I came back from the millpond with a lead box under my coat and a name already inked at the bottom of a ledger I hadn't opened in public, he took one look at me, said nothing, and put the kettle on.

He made it strong. He made it the way I liked it, which meant he'd been paying attention for eleven weeks, which meant everything I'd thought was private had been legible the whole time. That's the thing about being known, I was learning. It doesn't arrive all at once like weather. It seeps.

"Sit down, Mr. Brightwater," he said, and there was something in the way he said the name — a faint, deliberate stress on it, the way you'd test a floorboard you suspected was rotten — that made my skin prickle. He set the mug in front of me and then, instead of sitting opposite, he went to the door, looked both ways down the corridor like a man crossing a road he didn't trust, and closed it.

The staff room at Marrowfen Primary has a window that faces the boiler house and a noticeboard that hasn't been updated since roughly the reign of a different monarch. In the yellow afternoon light, with the radiators ticking, it should have been the safest room in the building. It was not. It had the atmosphere of a confessional with the curtain half open.

"You've got it," Okafor said. Not a question.

I didn't answer. I'd gotten good at not answering. It was, if I'm honest, the primary skill of my adult life.

"Don't," he said, and held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you go polite and vague. I've watched you do it to Vane. I've watched you do it to the vicar. It's very good. It's better than mine, and mine kept me alive for twenty-six years. But you've got a box in your bag with a lead lining, and you've been walking like a man carrying a sleeping child since Tuesday, and I am not going to sit here and drink tea with you and pretend."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed — the ordinary sound of an ordinary school, except that for eleven weeks every ordinary sound at Marrowfen had seemed to me like a held breath finally being released, one at a time, in no particular order.

"How long," I said. It was all I could manage.

Okafor pulled out the chair opposite mine, turned it, and sat on it backwards, arms folded across the backrest, the way he did when he was about to tell a child something difficult and wanted to seem smaller than he was. He is a big man, Emmanuel Okafor. Tall, broad, a voice that could quiet a playground without amplification. He made himself small anyway.

"Nineteen ninety-eight," he said. "February. I was thirty-one. I'd been here four years, same as you'd been here four weeks, and I'd found the same things you found, because the things don't change. The desks. The December pages razored out. The key in the drawer that isn't supposed to exist." He looked at his hands.

I looked at them too. I had looked at them a hundred times without seeing. The right hand was whole. The left hand had a thumb, an index finger, and a little finger, and then a smoothness where the middle two should have been, the skin pulled over in a way I'd always assumed was an old burn. He wore a ring on the index finger that I'd assumed was simply a ring.

"July," he said, watching me see it. "You're doing the arithmetic. Everyone does the arithmetic. Frostbite. In July. In a heat wave — I checked the records later, because I'm a teacher and we check records, it was the hottest week of the decade. I carried that box down those stairs on a Tuesday afternoon in full sun, and by the time I got to the bottom of the old stone, the cold was coming off the walls like the inside of a freezer lorry. I set the ledger on the shelf where Grell's diary said it belonged. I said the words. I did everything right."

He turned the hand over. The scars continued across the palm, pale seams.

"And it gave it back."

"It gave it back," I repeated.

"It handed it back to me like I'd brought the wrong homework. And it took its fee for the inconvenience." He said it without drama, which was the worst way to say it. "Three fingers, gone between one heartbeat and the next. No pain at first — that's the frostbite, they tell you, the cold numbs before it kills. I walked back up those stairs, wrapped my hand in my jumper, and drove myself to the hospital in Norwich, and I told them I'd shut it in a freezer at an abattoir, because I'd worked summers at an abattoir and it was the one story that would hold. And do you know what the doctor said to me?"

I shook my head.

"He said, *you're lucky it wasn't worse.*" Okafor laughed, a short, airless sound. "Lucky. And he was right, in a way. Because I'd brought it back whole. I'd brought the box back with the ledger inside and my name still mine and my fingers — well. It took what it took, and it let me keep the rest, and I have spent twenty-six years being grateful in a way that tastes like ash."

The tea was going cold in front of me. I drank it anyway, because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Why didn't you tell me?" I said. "Eleven weeks. You warned me off the desks. You warned me off the undercroft. You never once said—"

"What was I supposed to say?" For the first time his voice rose, and he pressed it back down like a man holding a lid on a pot. "'Excuse me, new colleague, the school you've just moved your whole life into is built on top of a hole in the world that eats children every winter and once ate three of my fingers, here's a biscuit'? You'd have done exactly what you did. You'd have smiled your polite smile and filed me under *local eccentric, harmless* and gone on digging. Because that's what I did. There was a man before me — a supply teacher, 1986 — who tried to warn me, and I filed him under local eccentric, harmless, and I went on digging, and the only reason I still have a whole hand is that I was too much of a coward to go down there until I'd convinced myself I'd imagined everything, and by then it was February and I'd found the diary and I thought — God help me, I *thought* — that I'd solved it. That returning the ledger was a procedure. That the thing below was a lock and the ledger was a key and I was the clever man with the clever hands."

He unfolded himself from the chair and stood at the window, looking out at the boiler house, at the frost-browned strip of playground beyond it where the shadows of the climbing frame lay long and skeletal on the concrete.

"It isn't a lock," he said. "It's a creditor. And you don't pay a creditor with someone else's money."

* * *

He told me the rest over the next hour, and I am going to set it down here as he told it, as nearly as I can, because I have come to believe that Marrowfen survives on what is written down and rots on what is only remembered.

In 1998 there had been a boy. Okafor wouldn't give me the name — "you don't say the names, that's the first thing, you never say the names out loud, they're currency down there and words are how it counts" — but he described him: small for his age, a gap in his teeth, a coat with a broken zip that his mother kept mending with safety pins because she couldn't afford a new one. December came, and the boy didn't. No report filed. No police called. The same practiced silence, and Okafor, four years in, still new enough to believe that silence was cowardice rather than grief, had decided he would be the one to break it.

He'd found Grell's diary by then — the same pages I found, in the same cottage with the same smell of old smoke, and I felt a strange vertigo hearing him describe the room I'd stood in, the exact water stain on the ceiling, as though we'd both been guests at the same terrible hotel twenty years apart.

"And the diary says it plainly, if you read it right," Okafor said. "Grell thought he was stealing the ledger to save the town. And maybe he was, for a while — twenty years, the town got. Twenty years of no winters taken, because the vault had been robbed and a robbed vault can't collect. But a debt doesn't die because you hide from the bailiff. It grows. And in '94 it started collecting again, and it's been collecting every winter since, because the ledger — the *names*, Brightwater, the names are the account — is sitting in a millpond instead of in the vault, and the vault will not close the account until the ledger comes home *and the debt is paid in full.*"

"Paid in full," I said. "Meaning the names in the ledger."

"Meaning the names in the ledger, freely given, fully remembered." He said it like a man reciting a catechism he hated. "That's the phrase the walls use. I heard it. You'll hear it too, if you haven't already. It doesn't want the book. The book is just paper and lead. It wants what's written in the book, and what's written in the book is *owed*, and you cannot pay an owed thing with something that isn't owed. That's the rule. That's the rule I learned in July with my hand on the shelf."

"So the fingers—"

"The fingers were the handling fee." He almost smiled. "Or the interest. Or a warning shot. I've had a long time to think about the metaphor and none of them are comfortable. The point is: I tried to give it back the ledger and nothing else, and it declined the payment, and it took a toll for my trouble. And then I went back to my classroom on the Monday — I was back on the Monday, Brightwater, bandaged, do you understand, I stood in front of thirty children with a bandaged hand and taught them long division — and in December, another name went on a taped-over desk, and I understood that I had not ended anything. I had just taught the thing below that the town still sends men down, and that the men can be bled."

* * *

We sat in the yellowing light while the radiators ticked and the building breathed its old-building breath around us, and at some point Okafor made a second pot of tea, and I understood that the tea was not tea. The tea was a man who had carried something alone for twenty-six years, setting it down on a table between two mugs, and I was not going to insult him by pretending it was anything else.

"So what do I do," I said.

"That," he said, "is the question I have been waiting eleven weeks for you to ask, and I'm going to answer it, and then you're going to argue with me, and then you're going to do what you were always going to do, because I know the type. I *am* the type." He sat down again, facing me properly this time. "Here is what I know. One: the vault only accepts what it's owed. The names in the ledger. Nothing else counts. Two: your name is in that ledger. Don't look like that — you've seen it, I can tell you've seen it, you've had the face of a man who's read his own obituary since Tuesday. It's been in there a long time. Since before you came. Possibly since before you were born, if the thing below counts the way I think it counts, and I no longer believe it experiences time the way we do. Three: the debt closes when a name is freely surrendered and fully remembered. Grell's diary is explicit. The walls are explicit. It is the only transaction it honors."

"And four?"

"Four." He leaned forward. "Four is the thing I have thought about every single day for twenty-six years, and it is this: the ledger is a list of names *Grell loved.* His mother. His brother. The girl he meant to marry. The children he taught in the Sunday school. Every name he gave to buy the town twenty years. Those are the owed names. Those are the account. And your name is at the bottom of it, and I have spent a very long time asking myself why, and the only answer I can find is that the vault has been *expecting* you. That this is not a trap you fell into. It is an invoice that was issued before you arrived, and the fog that brought you here—" he gestured at the window, at the grey pressing against the glass, "—was the postman."

"That's not an answer to what I do."

"It's the preamble to the answer." He held up the ruined hand, the smooth scars catching the light. "The answer is: give it a false name."

I stared at him.

"Give it a name that isn't owed," he said. "A name you invent. A name you choose. Because here's the thing about you, Brightwater — and I've watched you for eleven weeks, and I've read your file, and I noticed, because I always notice, that your file is very *clean*, suspiciously clean, the file of a man who built himself out of spare parts — here's the thing about you: you have already done it once. You have already taken a false name and made it true. You made it true by living in it, by answering to it, by letting it be the name the register knows and the name your colleagues say and the name on your tea mug. You are the only man in this whole cursed county who has ever *manufactured* a name from nothing. So manufacture another. Go down there on solstice night with a name you built this week, and say it freely, and mean it, and let the vault take it — because a freely given name is a freely given name, and the vault is a creditor, not a connoisseur. It counts. It doesn't verify."

"And the debt?"

"The debt closes." He said it firmly, and then, hearing his own firmness wobble, he looked at his hand. "I believe it closes. I have to believe it closes, because the alternative is—" He stopped. "The alternative is that the only payment it will ever accept is the one written at the bottom of the ledger, and I am not going to say that name out loud in this room, and neither are you."

The radiator ticked. Down the corridor, faintly, a child laughed at something, the ordinary bright sound of it, and both of us flinched, and both of us pretended we hadn't.

"Emmanuel," I said — his first name, which I had never used, and which landed between us like a stone dropped down a well. "You tried to return the ledger and it took three fingers. What do you think it takes from a man who tries to pay it with a forgery?"

He was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had gone very gentle, which was somehow worse than the fear.

"I think," he said, "that it takes everything. I think a false name freely given is a real name freely given, in the only sense that matters down there. I think you would walk out of that undercroft with no name at all — not the false one, not the true one, nothing — and the world would go on knowing you as Brightwater, and the register would say Brightwater, and the tea mug would say Brightwater, and none of it would be *you*, because the you that answers when a name is called would be gone. And I think that might be survivable. I think a man who has already erased himself once might even be *good* at it." He met my eyes. "And I think that is a far better thing than the other thing. The other thing is not survivable, Brightwater. The other thing is what happened to Thomas Grell, who is not dead, do you understand, he is not dead, he is *held*, the way the children are held, because he took what was owed and never paid it back and never paid it forward, and he is down there in the dark with the walls, and some nights, in February, when the wind is right, if you stand by the boiler house, you can hear him counting."

The light was going. Neither of us had turned on the overhead fluorescent, and the staff room had filled up with that blue-grey dusk that makes every room look like a photograph of itself. Okafor stood, and took both mugs to the sink, and washed them, because that was the kind of man he was — the kind who washed up even on the day he confessed to losing his fingers.

"Solstice is in four days," he said, with his back to me. "The roads will be snowed in by tomorrow night. Vane will beg you to leave before then, and she'll be right, and you won't listen, and I'm not going to beg, because begging is for things that might work." He set the mugs on the drainer, precisely, handles aligned. "Take the false name, Brightwater. Build it well. Build it the way you built the first one. And whatever you do—"

He turned around. In the dusk his ruined hand hung at his side, and his face was the face of a man who had rehearsed a speech for twenty-six years and was now, at the end, abandoning the speech.

"—whatever you decide, don't go down there alone. Because I went alone in '98, and I came back with three fewer fingers and the debt still open, and I have thought every day since that if there had been one person at the top of those stairs — one person who knew, one person waiting — I might have turned back. So." He picked up his coat. "I'll be at the top of the stairs. Solstice night. Whenever you go. I'll be there."

He left. The door clicked shut behind him with the small, final sound of a door in a school after hours, and I sat in the darkening room with the ledger in my bag and the cold of the millpond still in my bones, and I took out my notebook — the rational man's notebook, the recorder's notebook, the notebook of a man who believed, right up until this autumn, that the world was legible — and I wrote at the top of a fresh page, in my careful invented hand:

*False names. Build one. Choose well.*

And beneath it, before I could stop myself, before I even understood what my hand was doing, I had written a single line that I do not remember deciding to write, in handwriting that was mine and yet not mine, slanting slightly left where my hand slants right:

*Elias Brandt, 1971–*

The date of my birth. And after the dash, a space.

Waiting to be finished.
