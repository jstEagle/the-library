## 18. Elias Brandt

My mother named me Elias because it was her father's name, and her father was a man who could quiet a room by entering it. I have often wondered whether she understood what she was doing — whether she knew she was handing me an inheritance made entirely of expectation, a coat two sizes too large that I would spend my whole childhood growing into and my whole adulthood shrugging off.

Elias Brandt. Say it slowly and you can hear the problem in it. The *Brandt* is a struck match of a surname; the *Elias* is a psalm. Together they were a boy who was supposed to be both flame and hymn, and I was neither. I was a child who bit his nails to the quick and counted stair treads and believed, with the total conviction available only to the very young and the very frightened, that if I could just be small enough, quiet enough, unremarkable enough, nothing bad would ever find me.

You will have noticed by now how well that worked out.

* * *

We lived in a terrace house at the edge of a town whose name I will not give you, not out of coyness but because I have genuinely lost it — one of the many small amputations I performed on myself, and one I regret more than most. It was a town of brick and canal water, of gulls inland and confused, of a church whose bell rang wrong on Sundays, a half-tone flat, so that the whole congregation unconsciously sang to match it. My father worked at the depot. My mother cleaned offices before dawn and came home smelling of lemon polish and other people's dust.

I had a sister. This is the part where you should lean in, because everything else follows from her.

Maren. Two years younger than me, gap-toothed, ferocious, the kind of child who named every insect she met and held funerals for worms dried out on the pavement. She called our father *the Bear* and our mother *the Wren* and me — this is true, I have never told anyone this — she called me *the Fox*, because I was clever and slippery and slept with one ear open. She was the only person alive who ever gave me a name I wanted to keep.

When I was eleven, Maren drowned.

There. That's the sentence I've been circling for thirty years, the one I've buried under three changes of address and a forged middle initial and a surname invented from a wildflower guide I bought at a motorway services. Maren drowned in the millpond behind the old tannery in October, and I was there, and I did not save her.

I want to tell you it was complicated. It wasn't. Children fall into water; children fail to pull other children out; water does what water does. But here is what I have never said aloud, not to therapists, not to the dark ceiling of whatever bedroom I was failing to sleep in: after it happened, after they pulled her out and laid her on the bank with her hair full of pondweed like some terrible mermaid from a story she would have loved, I felt — beneath the grief, underneath it, in a cellar below the grief —

Relief.

One breath's worth. One heartbeat's worth of *thank God it isn't me*, and then the grief arrived and crushed it flat, and I have spent my entire life since trying to atone for a feeling that lasted less than a second. But guilt doesn't care about duration. Guilt is a miser. It takes the smallest coin and makes it the whole fortune.

* * *

After Maren died, my parents stopped saying my name.

I don't mean they were angry at me, though my mother looked through me for a year as though I were glass and she was tired of cleaning it. I mean something stranger. Grief-stricken people do strange things with language, and ours was a household that had always been slightly over-superstitious in the way of families who have known real loss — we didn't whistle indoors, we didn't put shoes on tables, we knocked wood until our knuckles ached. And somewhere in that fog of folk-belief, my parents arrived at the idea that names were dangerous. That to say *Elias* was to summon the thing that had taken Maren, or to invite it to take the rest of us. My mother took down the framed embroidery of my name above my bed — she'd stitched it herself when I was born, blue thread on cream linen — and burned it in the grate. I watched it curl. I remember thinking, with a clarity that still shames me, *good.*

By twelve I was "him." By thirteen I was "oi." By fourteen, when a teacher at school asked my parents why their son never answered to his own name, my father said, "He hasn't got one," and laughed, and the teacher laughed, and I sat there feeling the truth of it settle into my bones like cold.

Here is what nobody tells you about being unnamed: it is a kind of freedom, and freedom is a kind of wound. Without a name, no one could expect anything of me. No one could say *you're just like your grandfather* or *a Brandt always lands on his feet*. I became wonderfully, catastrophically light. I also became nothing. I walked through my teenage years like a rumor of a person, and I got very good at it, and getting good at it was the second-worst thing that ever happened to me.

The worst was the pond. We've covered the pond.

* * *

I left at seventeen. Not dramatically — I've never done anything dramatically; drama requires a self willing to be seen. I left the way water leaves a cracked pipe: gradually, then all at once. A rucksack, four hundred pounds saved from a summer of warehouse work, a bus ticket south. In my pocket I carried a photograph of Maren that I'd stolen from the album, cut free with scissors so the damage would look accidental. On the back, in her enormous childish handwriting: *ELIAS THE FOX.* Even then, even at seven years old, she'd written my name like it was a title. Like I'd earned it.

I got rid of the photograph eleven years later, in a bedsit in a city I won't name either, during the worst winter of my life, when I decided — soberly, methodically, the way a man decides to amputate his own frostbitten fingers — that I could not carry her anymore. I stood at the window with the photo in my hand and I understood, finally, formally, the transaction I had been making all along. To survive, I would have to stop being Elias Brandt. All of him. The boy who failed his sister, yes, but also the boy who was loved by her, who was *named* by her, who had been somebody's Fox. You cannot bury a body without burying the ground it lies in.

I fed the photograph into the shared incinerator in the yard and watched it go, and I felt the relief again — that same cellar-dwelling relief — and this time I let it stay. That was the moment Elodie Brightwater was born. I chose the name from the wildflower guide, *brightwater*, because it sounded clean, because it sounded like somewhere rain had never fallen, because it sounded like a man with no sister and no pond and no mother who burned his name in the grate. Elodie. Soft, forgettable, a name that slid off the tongue and left no mark. I practiced signing it until the handwriting stopped shaking.

And it worked. God help me, it worked. For twenty years it worked. I became a man with a modest talent for teaching and a modest talent for recording sound and no history whatsoever. Landlords found me dull. Colleagues found me pleasant and instantly forgettable. I dated people who described me later, I'm sure, as *nice, quiet, kept himself to himself*. I had achieved the thing I'd wanted since I was eleven years old: I had made myself impossible to lose, because I had made myself impossible to hold.

Then Marrowfen. Then the undercroft. Then a child's voice on a tape answering questions I hadn't asked, and then — worse, so much worse — an older voice, patient as groundwater, saying *Elias* into the dark of a stairwell where no living soul knew that name existed.

Do you understand now why I couldn't dismiss it? Mrs. Vane thought I was brave, digging through the records. Okafor thought I was reckless. Neither of them understood that it wasn't courage. When a voice says your dead name in a place where your dead name should be impossible, you don't walk away. Walking away is what I've been doing for thirty years, and the voice was the first thing that ever caught up.

* * *

Last night I sat in the cottage with Grell's diary pages spread across the table like a tarot reading, and I read them again, and this time I read them properly — not as evidence but as confession, because that's what they are. A man writing down the terms of his damnation in the hope that paper will hold what he cannot. *It does not take lives,* Grell wrote, in a hand that deteriorated across the months like a slope giving way. *That is the mercy everyone misunderstands. It takes names. The life goes on walking around, but the name comes below, and a person without their name is a coat without a body in it. I gave it the names of everyone I loved so it would leave the town alone. Then I ran. You cannot run from a debt collector who holds your whole heart in a ledger.*

Everyone he loved. I turned the page and found the list fragment, the one I'd skimmed past twice before, and this time I made myself look at it. Names in Grell's careful caretaker's hand. His wife. His sons. His brother. His mother.

And at the bottom, in different ink, older ink, ink that looked like it had been pressed into the page rather than written on it — a line of names that predated Grell's entry entirely. Names from 1974 and before. Names going back. And among them, in the same bruised, surfacing script:

**Brandt, Elias.**

Not my handwriting. Not anyone's handwriting. A name rising through the paper the way a body rises through pondwater, patient, inevitable, arriving exactly on schedule.

I sat very still for a long time. Outside, the snow had started again, fat and silent, sealing the roads, sealing the village, sealing me in with the arithmetic of solstice. Twenty-three voices counting down on the recorder I hadn't dared switch off. Senna Hallow among them, reciting names backward, and I understood at last why the backwardness chilled me more than anything else in those tapes: because a name spoken forward is an introduction, but a name spoken backward is a *retrieval*. Something down there was reeling them in, letter by letter, preparing the accounts for closing.

The vault wants a name of equal weight. Okafor says give it a false one, and I love him for it, and he's wrong, and I know he's wrong because I have been a false name for thirty years and I know precisely how much a false name weighs. Nothing. Less than nothing. You cannot pay a debt in currency you minted yourself.

But Elias Brandt — Elias Brandt is heavy. Elias Brandt carries a drowned girl and a burned embroidery and a mother who couldn't say his name and a single heartbeat of relief in a cellar under the grief. Elias Brandt is the heaviest thing I own, and I have spent my whole life trying to give him away, and now something below the school has offered to take him.

Tomorrow is solstice eve. Tonight I will sit with the ledger and listen to the countdown, and I will try to remember my sister's face without the photograph, which is the last cruelty, the final interest payment on thirty years of forgetting: I gave away the picture to save myself, and now, when I need her most, all I can see is the shape of the hole she left in the album.

The recorder clicked over to a new hour. The counting paused — the first silence in three days — and then, clear as a bell rung flat, the way Marrowfen's church bells must ring, a voice I knew better than my own said, from the speaker, from below, from everywhere:

*Fox. Come down. Bring the book.*

Maren's voice. And she has never once, in all her life or death, called me Elias.
