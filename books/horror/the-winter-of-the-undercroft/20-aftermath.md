## 20. Aftermath

I woke on the undercroft stairs with snowmelt in my shoes and no memory of climbing them.

That's not quite true. I woke with the *sensation* of having climbed them — the ache in my calves, the grit of old stone printed into my palms like a watermark — but the climb itself was gone, along with everything that had come before it. The night was simply absent. Not blank, exactly. Blank implies edges. This was more like a page razored out of a book, and I knew enough by then to recognize the technique.

I sat there for a long while, listening to the boiler tick over above me, waiting for the fear to arrive.

It didn't. That should have been my first warning.

* * *

Morning came to Marrowfen the way it always did in winter: reluctantly, and smelling of coal smoke. I walked back to the cottage, changed my shirt because the collar had frozen stiff overnight, and went to school.

The fog had lifted for the first time since my arrival. That alone should have told me something had shifted in the town's architecture — meteorological or otherwise. You could see the church spire. You could see the hills. You could see, coming up the lane toward the gates, a procession of small figures in coats too big for them, walking with the unhurried certainty of children who have been somewhere long and cold and have decided, provisionally, to come back.

Senna Hallow was at the front of them. She had a scrape on her chin and one mitten missing, and she looked at me as she passed through the gates and said, "You're thinner," in the tone of a child delivering a diagnosis.

"I've been sleeping badly," I said.

"You should eat," she said, and went inside, and that was the whole of our conversation about where she had been for eleven days, and I found I didn't want to ask, which frightened me more than any answer could have.

* * *

The classroom was warm. Twenty-three faces, all present, all accounted for, four desks unshrouded — someone had peeled the taped-over names off during the night, or perhaps they had simply fallen away, the way scabs do when the wound underneath has finally closed. Mr. Okafor stood at the back of the room when I came in, arms folded, and he looked at me the way you look at a man who has come back from a place with no return ticket, checking him for damage.

"Well?" he said.

"The roads are still snowed in," I said, because I couldn't think what else he was asking.

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

He studied me a moment longer. Then he crossed the room and put his hand on my shoulder — Okafor, who had warned me off every question I'd ever asked him, whose remaining seven fingers had once gripped a lead box in July — and squeezed once, hard, and said nothing at all, which from Okafor was a eulogy, a medal, and an apology bound into a single gesture.

He left before the bell. I understood later that he'd gone to be sick in the staff lavatory, quietly, with the door locked. Grief ritualized takes many forms. So does relief.

* * *

Mrs. Vane wept in the corridor outside the hall, openly, standing in front of the noticeboard with its yellowed certificates and its faded photograph of the 1974 football team, and she did not wipe her eyes or turn away when the children filed past her into assembly. She just stood there and let it happen, her face doing something I had never seen a face do in Marrowfen: moving freely, without supervision.

A small boy — Tovey, I think, second row, always chewing his sleeve — stopped and patted her hand as he went by, the way you'd comfort an animal, and she laughed through it, a cracked and terrible sound, and said, "Go on, love, you'll be late."

I watched from the doorway. I felt — I want to be honest here, honesty being the only currency I had left, and even that debased — I felt as though I were watching this through glass. Not coldly. Glass is not cold if you're on the right side of it. But there was a pane between me and the weeping, and the assembly hymn, and the smell of wet wool and porridge, and I stood behind that pane and catalogued it all like a man taking inventory of a house he was fairly sure wasn't his.

* * *

At lunchtime I went to my desk and took out my documents.

This was not a dramatic moment. It should have been; I'm aware of that. A man sits down to discover what he paid, and the violins should come in, and the light should slant. Instead it was grey noon, and the radiator clanked, and twenty-three children were in the hall singing something about holly, and I laid my papers out on the blotter like evidence in a case I'd lost interest in prosecuting.

Contract of employment. *Elodie Brightwater.* My signature beneath it — a confident, slightly theatrical hand, the signature of a man who had chosen his own name and enjoyed the choosing. I remembered practicing it. I remembered the summer, the pen shop, the deliberate decision to give the E a flourish. All of that remained.

What didn't remain was the *why*.

I turned the contract over, as though the reason might be printed on the reverse, like terms and conditions. Nothing. I checked the reference letters — forged, both of them, competently, by me, I assumed, though the forger's motives were also missing. I checked the bank card, the driving licence, the dog-eared photograph I kept in my wallet of a woman I did not recognize, standing in front of a house I did not recognize, holding a child who might have been me and might not.

There was a name on the back of the photograph, written in ink gone brown with age. I looked at it for a long time.

Nothing happened.

That's the only way I can describe it. A name is supposed to be a key that turns in you — opens the door to a whole wing of rooms, other names, other winters, a mother's voice saying it across a kitchen. Mine lay there on the paper and turned nothing. It was a word in a language I had never learned. I read it three times, carefully, the way you'd read a word in Finnish, sounding it out, waiting for meaning to accrue.

It didn't accrue. And underneath the nothing, worse than the nothing, was a small, practical voice — my voice, the wry one, the survivor's — saying: *well, at least the paperwork's in order.*

I put the photograph away. I made tea. I drank the tea. It tasted of tea.

* * *

Here is what I could reconstruct, and I set it down now because setting things down is the only discipline I trust:

I came to Marrowfen in November, in fog, to take a teaching post. I was an insomniac and an amateur recordist. There was a brass key labeled UNDERCROFT. There were recordings — I still have them, stacked in my desk drawer like contraband, and I have not been able to make myself play them, and I have not been able to make myself destroy them, which tells me they matter even if I can't say why. There was a ledger. There was a caretaker named Grell, dead fifty years and somehow still the most honest man in the parish. There was a debt, and a countdown, and a choice, and I know — I know the way you know the shape of a room with your eyes shut — that I chose.

The cost is itemized as follows:

One name. Mine, presumably, the original article, whatever it was. Gone, and with it the entire estate that name owned: the childhood, the flight, the reason I reinvented myself, the person I had been running from and the person I had been running as. Both of them. Evicted together.

In exchange: twenty-three children, minus none. Senna Hallow, home. Mrs. Vane's brother, returned after fifty years — they found him, or rather he was simply *there*, on the Vane doorstep at dawn, forty-nine years old and five, asleep in the snow with his mitten missing, and Mrs. Vane carried him inside and the town closed around the miracle like water closing over a stone, and nobody called anyone, because who would you call?

And in exchange also: me. Alive. Unrecorded. Walking around with a name I invented and no memory of why invention had been necessary, which means the armor survived and the wound it was made from did not, and I am beginning to suspect that is not a bargain at all but a different kind of taking, dressed in mercy's clothing.

But those are thoughts for later. At lunchtime I simply finished my tea and went back to work, because the children were coming in from assembly and someone had to hear them read.

* * *

The afternoon passed in long division and the story of the Firebird. Normalcy, performed adequately. At half past three I dismissed them into the snow and stood at the window watching them go, and Senna looked back once from the gate and raised her mittenless hand, and I raised mine, and neither gesture meant anything we could have explained.

Okafor appeared beside me. He did this now; he had apparently decided I was his responsibility, in the way sailors decide things about lighthouses.

"You don't remember," he said. Not a question.

"I remember everything except the parts that matter."

"That's how it works." He picked up a paperweight from my desk, turned it over, put it down. "Vane remembers her brother leaving. She doesn't remember the winters between. The town remembers '74 and nothing since. It files itself clean, every year." He paused. "You'll get used to the gaps. That's the trap of it. The gaps are comfortable."

"And if I don't want to be comfortable?"

He smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth like a stone in a pocket. "Then you're exactly the wrong man for this town and exactly the right one. Same as Grell." He shrugged into his coat. "Lock up. It gets dark early."

He left. I stood in the empty classroom while the light drained out of the sky, and I listened to the building settle around me — pipes, floorboards, the boiler ticking down in its room above the older stone — and I thought: *the walls are inscribed with thousands of names,* and I thought it the way you recall a fact from a book you read years ago, without the faintest echo of having stood among them.

Then I unlocked my desk drawer, and took out the ledger.

* * *

I hadn't meant to. I want that on the record, addressed to whoever keeps records now that I apparently don't keep my own. I had a list of sensible evening activities — marking, dinner, the paper I would never write — and retrieving the ledger from the drawer where some past version of me had hidden it was not on the list.

But there it was in my hands anyway, lead-grey and cold, heavier than its size, the way guilt is heavier than its subject. I opened it on the desk under the desk lamp.

The pages were mostly blank. Mostly. Names surfaced in them slowly, like bruises rising under skin — I knew that without knowing how I knew — and most had sunk again now, faded to a ghost of ink, their accounts settled. Near the end, one line remained dark and legible. A name I did not recognize. Crossed through with a single stroke, neat and final, like a teacher marking work complete.

My handwriting. I recognized the hand even though I could not recognize the name. That is a sentence I intend to spend the rest of my life failing to get comfortable with.

And below the crossed-out line — below it, waiting, patient as winter — a blank space.

Not an empty space. Empty spaces are passive. This one *waited*. I sat looking at it and felt the same sensation I'd felt on the undercroft stairs that morning, the absence-with-edges, the sense of a room holding its breath — except now the room was a page, and the breath it held was mine.

The radiator clanked. Somewhere below the boiler room, below the modern brick and the older stone, something that had waited since before the school, since before the parish, since before the millpond was drained, adjusted itself — I will not say stirred; stirring implies restlessness, and it has never once been restless — and settled in to wait some more.

I got a fresh pen from the pot on my desk. I don't know why. I tell myself I don't know why.

I turned to the last page, and I began to write.

* * *

Outside, the snow started again, softening the lanes, sealing the roads, keeping Marrowfen Marrowfen. In the morning there would be twenty-three children, and a headteacher who wept freely now and was learning, clumsily, to stop, and a colleague with seven fingers who watched me too closely. In the morning there would be registers to take, and I would take them, and every name on them would be spoken aloud and answered to, which I understand now — dimly, the way you understand weather — is the whole of the work.

But tonight there was only the lamp, and the ledger, and the blank space waiting at the bottom of the last page, and my pen touching down.

The first letters were not my name.

They were theirs.
