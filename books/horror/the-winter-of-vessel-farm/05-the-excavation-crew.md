## 5. The Excavation Crew

Danny Oakes had dug holes in forty-one counties and this was the first time the ground had ever looked back at him.

He told himself that was nonsense, of course. He was a practical man, a foreman of eleven years' standing with the Ministry's survey division, a man who could read soil strata the way other men read newspapers, and practical men did not believe that earth could watch. But there it was all the same, in the way the morning light lay flat across the field behind Vessel Farm — not bright, not dark, just *level*, like the surface of water too deep to have a bottom — and in the way his own breath hung in the air without moving anywhere, as if even the wind had been asked to wait.

"Foreman." Pryce, the junior surveyor, came crunching across the frozen rutted track with his clipboard hugged to his chest like a hymnal. "Local council's sent a man again. He wants to know if we've got the right permits."

"We've got the right permits," Danny said. "We've got every permit they've got. Tell him to go home."

"He says we should know the villagers call this the listening season."

Danny looked up from the pegged-out grid of the first test pit. The snow squeaked under his boots when he shifted his weight. Beyond the site fence — chicken wire and stakes, hardly worth the name — the village of Hollowmere sat in its bowl of white like something set down carefully by hands that hadn't wanted to wake it. Grey stone roofs. One crooked church spire. Chimney smoke rising perfectly straight, as though drawn upward on strings.

"The what season?"

"Listening." Pryce glanced over his shoulder at the council man, who stood at the gate with his collar up and his face the colour of old tallow, making no move to come closer. "That's what he said. He said — I wrote it down — he said, 'You've come in the listening season, and the ground will mind you being here.'"

"Well," Danny said, "the ground can put in a complaint through official channels."

Pryce laughed, but it came out thin, and he walked back to the gate with his shoulders hunched as though the sky were lower over him than over anyone else.

* * *

They were nine. That number mattered later, though Danny couldn't have said why it mattered then; it only lodged itself somewhere under his sternum, the way a splinter lodges, small and wrong and waiting.

Nine men: himself, Pryce, the two drill operators named Cobb and Fenwick, old Hargreaves who'd run augers since before Danny was born, young Tolly straight out of technical college with his hair still cut like a schoolboy's, the geophysicist Marsh with his resistivity meters and his permanent squint, the driver Weller, and the cook-cum-general-hand, a soft-spoken Welshman called Prosser who made tea strong enough to strip paint. Nine men, one Bedford lorry, one Land Rover towing a compressor, and a government remit signed in London that said, in language so dry it crackled: *hydrological anomaly, parish of Hollowmere, test excavation authorised.*

The anomaly was real enough. The Ordnance Survey's gravity readings had shown a void beneath the fields east of Vessel Farm — a cavity, or a filled shaft, or something the instruments couldn't quite agree on — and where the Ministry found a hole it did not understand, it sent men to make the hole smaller by putting parts of itself inside. Danny had done it a hundred times. You pegged your grid, you stripped your turf, you dug your pits in neat one-metre squares, you logged what came out, and six months later some man in an office turned your labour into a paragraph nobody read.

Except here, on the first morning, before a single spadeful had turned, the villagers came out to count them.

Not protest — Danny had faced protests, miners' wives linking arms, students lying down in front of the lorry, and he knew the noise and heat of them. This was nothing like that. This was silence. They came singly and in pairs, down lanes and across the frozen churchyard, and they stood along the fence line at a distance of perhaps twenty yards, and they counted. He saw their lips move. An old woman in a black shawl held up one gloved finger, then another, then another, working her way along the line of them as methodically as a schoolmistress marking sums. When she reached nine she stopped. She looked at the nine shovels standing upright against the wire, handles to the sky, and her lips moved once more, and then she turned and walked back toward the village without hurry, without looking round, the way a person leaves a room where something has been settled.

"They're counting us," Tolly said, delighted. "Like sheep."

"Aye," said Hargreaves, who had not spoken above ten words in two days. "Or like portions."

Nobody laughed. Danny noticed that. On any other site, Hargreaves saying a thing like that would have earned him three jokes and a nickname by lunchtime. Here the men just went quiet and bent to their work, and the only sounds were the bite of spades into frozen topsoil and the far-off complaint of a crow somewhere over the ridge.

The first pit went down through eighteen inches of frost-hardened clay, then through loam so black it shone, then into gravel — river gravel, rounded and clean, which was wrong, because the nearest river ran four miles south and had never come within a mile of this field in the memory of any map. Danny knelt at the edge of the square and took a handful of it and let it run through his fingers. Each pebble was smooth as a sucked sweet.

"Water-laid," Marsh said, crouching beside him with his meter humming. "Sorted, graded, deposited by moving water. Except there's no water." He pushed his spectacles up. "There's never been water here. Not in the borehole logs, not in the drift maps, nothing."

"So how does a river get under a hilltop field?"

Marsh didn't answer. He was looking at the gravel with the expression of a man reading a letter addressed to someone else.

* * *

By the third day they had opened five pits, and the pattern had stopped being interesting and started being wrong.

Every pit, wherever they cut, struck the same stratigraphy at the same depth: clay, black loam, gravel, and beneath the gravel a layer of fine grey ash mixed with fragments of charred bone — animal, probably, though Tolly swore one piece had the look of a knuckle joint about it — and beneath the ash, packed hard as concrete, a floor of puddled clay laid deliberately, levelled deliberately, the way you'd seal a pond or a grave. And pressed into that clay floor, in every single pit, spaced with a regularity no accident could explain, were small white stones.

Nine pits' worth of white stones, though they'd only dug five so far — Danny caught himself doing the arithmetic and didn't like where it pointed. The stones were smooth, egg-sized, chalk-white against the grey clay, and each one had been placed point-down, pressed in like a tooth into a gum.

"It's a cairn," Pryce offered. "Or a ritual deposit. Iron Age, maybe earlier—"

"It's a *floor*," Hargreaves said. "Somebody built a floor over whatever's underneath, and they counted it out stone by stone, and then they burned things on it, and then they buried the lot." He spat expertly into the spoil heap. "And then they went away and hoped."

"Hoped what?" Tolly asked.

Hargreaves looked at the boy for a long moment, and Danny saw the old man decide, visibly, not to answer.

That evening the council man came back. He stood at the gate in the blue dusk while the floodlamps buzzed on and the snow began again, fat slow flakes that fell without sound, and he said, "You'll be wanting to know how deep it goes."

"That's rather the point of us being here," Danny said.

"How deep do you think?"

"I don't think anything yet. The instruments say a void at depth. Could be a mine working, could be a swallow-hole, could be nowt but a fault in the readings."

The man nodded slowly, as if Danny had confirmed something he'd already known. His eyes went past Danny's shoulder to the site, to the five open squares glowing under the lamps like wounds, to the spoil heaps wearing their new caps of snow.

"There's a well," he said. "Old wellhead, back of Vessel Farm. My grandmother said it was deep as the church is tall. Said the founders sank it before they built the first house, before they built the *church*. Said everything else in Hollowmere is younger than the well." He pulled his collar higher. "She also said it was dry her whole life, and her mother's whole life, and her mother's mother's. Dry as a sermon. And my gran was a liar about most things, but not about the well."

Then he touched his cap — actually touched it, an odd formal little gesture, like a man closing a transaction — and walked away into the falling snow, and Danny stood at the gate watching him diminish until the flakes took him, and thought: *he never asked us to stop. Not once. Why has nobody asked us to stop?*

* * *

On the fifth morning the objections finally arrived, and they arrived in the shape of Edith Marrow.

Danny had heard of her already — everyone on the crew had, because she kept the Lamb and Lantern, the only inn for twelve miles, and because Prosser had gone in for supplies on the second day and come back with bread, cheese, lamp oil, and a look on his face like a man who'd seen the sea for the first time. "Proper landlady," was all he'd say. "Proper landlady, and proper *eyes* on her."

She came up the farm track at nine o'clock precisely, alone, in a good wool coat and a headscarf, carrying nothing, and she stood at the gate and waited until Danny came over, and then she looked at him the way an auditor looks at books.

"You're the foreman."

"I am. Daniel Oakes."

"Marrow. Edith Marrow. I keep the Lamb and Lantern." She said it as if the inn were a fact of geography, like a hill. "I've come to tell you what the others won't."

"And what's that?"

"That you're welcome to dig." She said it plainly, without irony, and it landed stranger than any threat would have. "Dig your pits. Log your stones. Write your reports. We'll not stand in your way — the vicar wanted to petition, and I told him no, and the council wanted to refuse your permits, and I told them no too. You're welcome here, Mr Oakes. That's the trouble."

Danny waited. Under the buzzing lamps behind him he could hear Tolly laughing at something, the ordinary sound of it absurd against the woman's stillness.

"You're welcome," she repeated, "and you'll be fed, and bedded, and sold what you need, and when your work's done you'll leave, and none of it will matter, because you were coming whether we welcomed you or not. Do you see? It isn't objection I've come to make. It's *witness*. Somebody ought to be able to say, afterwards, that we knew. That we watched you count yourselves in." Her gaze moved past him, unhurried, along the line of men at the pits, and Danny felt her count them — he *felt* it, the way you feel a hand pass close over the hairs of your arm — one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine. "Nine," she said softly. "Yes."

"Yes what?"

"Nothing." And her eyes came back to his, and they were kind, which was somehow the worst thing about them. "Mind the cold, Mr Oakes. It's going to get into everything before you're done. Mind it especially underground. Cold that comes up from below isn't weather."

She turned and went, and Danny stood at the gate a long while, telling himself she was mad, or theatrical, or both, the way country people sometimes were, full of old words worn smooth of meaning. But that night in the hut he heard the men talking low around the stove, and Tolly asked Prosser what the landlady had meant, calling after him as she left — and Prosser, stirring the pot, said without turning round:

"She said, 'Tell your foreman: nine shovels, nine names.' And then she said—" and here Prosser paused, and the ladle dripped, and the stove ticked, "—she said, 'We counted you the day you crossed the ridge. The ground counted you long before.'"

Nobody spoke after that. Cobb got up and bolted the door, though there was nothing outside but snow, and Danny lay in his bunk listening to the wind try the roof and fail, and thought about the wellhead half a mile off, dry for a hundred years, sitting in its collar of old stones under the drifts like a closed mouth.

* * *

They moved the grid to the wellhead on the eighth day, because Marsh's meters went strange there — not noisy, not dead, but *attentive*, the needle trembling toward the shaft from every angle like iron filings finding north — and because the remit said hydrological anomaly and the anomaly, whatever else it was, lived under that circle of mossy stones.

The wellhead itself was older than anything else they'd touched. The surrounding kerb was dressed stone, each block fitted without mortar, and carved — Danny cleaned the snow off one block with his bare hand and found the carving under the ice — with a border of interlocking figures that might have been wheat sheaves and might have been fingers. In the centre, the shaft mouth was capped with a slab of the same pale stone as the white teeth in the buried floors, and the slab bore a single word, cut deep and worn shallow by centuries of weather:

*LISTEN.*

"Victorian whimsy," Pryce said. "Folk revival nonsense. Tennyson era."

"Victorians didn't sink wells before churches," Danny said, and heard Edith Marrow's voice saying it, and hated that he'd borrowed it.

They drilled the first borehole six feet from the kerb on the ninth day. The auger went down easy through drift and clay, and then at fifty-eight feet it met resistance, and Hargreaves leaned on the rig and frowned and said, "Stone. Big stone. Flat-laid." And they cased the hole and lowered the inspection lamp on its cable, and the light went down and down into the dark, and at sixty-one feet it showed them a surface of dressed stone, level, swept clean — *swept clean*, Danny wrote in the log, and stared at his own words — and set into that surface, directly below the wellhead, a door.

Bolted shut. From the inside.

But that was the next day's horror, and on the evening of the ninth day Danny knew none of it yet. On the ninth evening he stood by the wellhead in the blue hour while the men packed up, and the snow fell straight as plumb-lines, and he became aware — gradually, the way you become aware of being watched in a mirror's edge — that the villagers were out again along the fence, more of them now, a whole silent congregation of them, and that every face was turned not to the men, not to the rig, not to the lorry, but to the capped wellhead, to the pale slab and its worn word.

And as he stood there, the crow that had been complaining over the ridge all week went quiet mid-call, cut off clean, and the silence that followed had weight and texture, like a held breath, like a room where someone has just finished speaking and is waiting to hear whether anyone answers.

Danny Oakes had dug holes in forty-one counties. He knew ground. Ground did not listen.

But at sixty-one feet under his boots, behind a door bolted from within, something in the dark had begun — very faintly, very patiently, in a rhythm no machine on the site would register until tomorrow — to knock.
