## 6. The Door, Sealed from the Inside

The frost came up out of the ground to meet them on the third morning of digging.

Danny Oakes had been driving earth-moving plant for eleven years and running survey crews for six, and he knew every mood soil could have — the sullen clay that clung to buckets, the friendly gravel that gave itself up like coins from a pocket, the chalk that squeaked. He had never known ground that seemed to be pushing back. Not collapsing inward, the way a shaft will when you undercut it wrong. Pushing. As if the whole hillside above Vessel Farm were leaning its weight down the borehole, slow as a glacier, trying to close the throat they'd opened in it.

"Groundwater's odd too," said Prentice, crouched at the lip of the shaft with his clipboard hugged to his chest like a schoolbook. "Should be seeping by now. Forty feet through glacial till and we've not taken a pint."

"Then stop complaining about it," Danny said, and immediately regretted the sharpness. It wasn't Prentice he was cross with. It was the village. It was the way they stood at their garden gates each morning as the crew walked past, not speaking, not hostile either — just *counting*. He'd caught old women doing it with their lips moving. Nine men, nine shovels, nine pairs of boots hung up in the Lamb and Lantern's boot-room, and Edith Marrow dusting around them with her eyes down like she was afraid of waking something.

He spat over the edge of the spoil heap and went down the ladder.

The shaft was timbered now for the first twenty feet, then raw earth below, cut neat as a well — which was what it was, or had been once. The survey maps called it a disused wellhead, nineteenth century at latest, probably medieval. The Ministry wanted test pits before the water board laid a main along the ridge road, and Hollowmere was a dot on nobody's map, which made it cheap and easy and nobody's problem. That was the theory. In practice, the villagers had come out in a body the first day, forty or fifty of them standing in the snow beyond the site fence without signs or banners, just standing, until the light went. They hadn't come back since. Somehow that was worse.

Forty-one feet down, the air changed.

Danny noticed it first as warmth. Not the stale warmth of a deep excavation, which is always a few degrees steadier than the surface — this was a living warmth, breath-warm, and it moved. It rose up the shaft past his face in slow pulses, like something far below was exhaling in its sleep. His headlamp beam wobbled in it. The walls sweated. After three days of dry ground, the cut faces of the earth had begun to glisten, dark as liver.

"Oakes." Prentice's voice came down the shaft, flattened by the timbers into something churchy. "You feeling that?"

"Aye."

"Barometric?"

"In a hole in the ground?" Danny wiped his face with the back of his glove. "Get Weller down here with the mattock. We're close to something."

Close to something. He'd meant the bedrock, the reason for the anomaly, the boring geological answer that would let him write his report and get his crew off this hill and back to somewhere with streetlights. He believed in boring answers the way other men believed in God; it had never once let him down. So he took the mattock himself when Weller hesitated, and swung it two-handed into the base of the shaft, and the sound it made was wrong in a way his body understood a full second before his mind did.

Stone. But not the dull knock of struck bedrock. This rang. A clean, hard, hollow ring, like a bell wrapped in a coat, and it went on ringing after the mattock bounced, a faint singing under the floor of the world.

They worked the rest of the day clearing it. By dusk they had exposed a flat surface four feet across, dressed stone, fitted so precisely that Danny couldn't get a penknife blade into the joints. It wasn't local stone. He knew the local stone — gray gritstone, coarse as bread. This was pale, almost white, fine-grained, and cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. When he laid his bare palm on it, the cold went *up* his arm, deliberate, like a hand closing around his wrist from inside the rock.

"Slab," said Weller behind him. "Coffin lid, maybe. Old burials, they sometimes—"

"It's vertical," Danny said.

Weller stopped talking.

Because that was the thing, the thing Danny would spend the rest of his life failing to explain properly: the stone was not lying flat at the bottom of the shaft like a lid. It stood upright, set into the earth like a door set into a wall, and the shaft had been sunk straight down onto it, forty feet of soil and centuries packed above a doorway that faced *up* — no. He turned it over in his head all evening, sitting in the Lamb and Lantern with a pint going flat in front of him, and couldn't make it sit right any way he tried. A door facing up. A door you would open by falling through it. Unless the door didn't care about directions. Unless down and in were the same thing down there.

Edith Marrow refilled his glass without being asked and didn't meet his eye.

"Your landlady," he said. "This farm. How old is it?"

"Older than the parish," she said. "Older than most things." And then, quietly, wiping the bar that didn't need wiping: "Mr. Oakes. If you find a door down there — and I'm not saying you will — but if you do. Don't knock on it."

Danny laughed. It came out thin. "Who'd be daft enough to knock?"

She looked at him then, finally, and her face was the face of someone watching a cart go over a bridge they know is rotten. "Aye," she said. "That's the question, isn't it. Who'd be daft enough."

* * *

They uncovered the bolts on the fourth day.

It took that long because none of them wanted to be the one down there alone, though none of them said so. They worked in pairs, in shifts of an hour, and the warm breath kept rising past them, and the stone door kept giving up its details slowly, grudgingly, like a prisoner searched by strangers. There was carving around the edge — a border of interlaced lines, worn nearly smooth, and spaced along it at intervals small round depressions like cup-marks, except Danny, who had helped record the cup-marked stones in Northumberland years ago, saw at once that these weren't decorative. They were sockets. Something had sat in them once, long ago, and been taken away.

Except it hadn't. Because at shoulder height, three of them, there were shapes still in place: iron bars, thick as a man's wrist, black with age but not rusted — not rusted at all, after however many hundred years in wet ground — and each bar ran through a staple in the stone and *across*, seating into a socket in the frame beside it.

Prentice got down on his knees in the mud with his lamp an inch from the metal and stayed there a long time.

"Danny," he said at last, and his voice had gone strange and careful, the voice of a man handling his own words like eggs. "Come and look at this. Tell me I'm reading it wrong."

Danny knelt. He looked. He read it right.

The bars were thrown. The bolts were shot home into their sockets from the side where the crew stood — that much was arguable, that much a rational man could argue, because a door bolted from the outside makes sense, that's what doors are for, keeping things in. But the staples told the truth. The staples were mounted on the far face of the door, on the side that faced down into whatever lay beneath, and the throw-levers — the handles, the parts a hand grips — were on the near face, the upper face, the face looking up at them out of the earth.

No. He did it again, slower, tracing the mechanism with his finger like a child learning letters. The lever was on top. The staple was underneath. Which meant the bar slid from the underside outward, which meant the hand that threw it had reached *up through* the door—

"That's not possible," Weller said. He'd come down uninvited and stood dripping at the ladder's foot. "Bolts go on the secure side. Everyone knows that. It's bolted from below, so it's—" He stopped. They all heard it arrive together, the sentence finishing itself in the silence: *so it's locked from the inside.*

"So whatever's down there," Prentice said, "locked itself in."

"Or was locked in," Danny said, "and ate through the bolt-hole to do it." He heard his own voice and didn't recognize it. "Or the door was built around it, and the bolting was done by—"

By whom? By what hands? The founders, Tam Vessel might have said, if Tam Vessel had been there; but Tam Vessel wasn't there, and neither was anyone from the village, and Danny understood suddenly why. They had known. All of them, all along, standing at their fences counting nine shovels. They had known what was under Vessel Farm the way you know what's in your own cellar, and they had spent however many generations not digging, not drilling, not laying water mains, and the government men had come with their maps and their machines and driven a hole straight down through the roof of it.

"We should stop," Weller said. "Phone the office. Get the archaeologists out. This is a scheduled-ancient-monument situation if ever I—"

The knocking started.

Three blows. Measured. Unhurried. Coming from the other side of the stone, from below, from within — and Danny felt them in his knees and his teeth before his ears sorted them out, felt the door transmit them up through the mud and the timbers and the ladder and the frozen ground, all the way, he would have sworn later, all the way up into the sky.

Nobody moved. The lamps made three small trembling circles in the dark of the shaft. The warm breath pulsed once against their faces, soft as a sigh.

Then, again: three blows. Same rhythm. Same unhurried patience. And Danny, whose mind had gone white and empty as the snowfields, found that some older and stupider part of him had already answered the question the knocking asked. It wasn't *let me out.* Nothing so crude. It was the knock of a neighbor at a door in the small hours, the knock that says *I know you can hear me. I know you're awake. Take your time.*

"Up," Danny said. His voice cracked on it. "Everybody up. Now."

They climbed. None of them looked down. All of them listened, which was worse, because as Danny's head broke the surface into the gray afternoon and the snow and the ordinary blessed cold, the knocking came a third time — and this time it was not three blows.

It was four. Then five. Then a little run of them, quick and light, almost playful, like fingers drumming. Like something on the far side of the stone had noticed their footsteps climbing away, and was counting them.

Nine men, nine shovels. Nine sets of boots in the boot-room at the Lamb and Lantern.

Danny stood at the shaft's edge with his heart slamming and looked out across the fields to where Hollowmere's chimneys smoked straight up into the windless sky, every hearth in the village burning, every window shuttered, and he thought: they knew. They've always known. And they sent us down anyway — or they let us come, which amounts to the same thing, which means somebody, somewhere, wanted a hole opened in the roof of that place, and wanted strangers' hands to hold the spades.

Behind him, faint but clear in the dead-still air, rising forty feet up the throat of the old well like a voice up a speaking-tube, came the sound of the bolts sliding.

Not opening. He told himself that all night, lying in the narrow bed under Edith Marrow's sloping ceilings, listening to the house tick and settle. Not opening. Just moving. Testing themselves, the way an old man tests a stiff knee. The door was still shut. The door was still bolted — from the inside, by whatever had waited down there in the warm dark, patient as winter, for exactly this: the sound of digging, the taste of daylight leaking down, and above all the listening — because that was the other thing, the thing Danny hadn't told the others, the thing he'd carry alone up to his cold room while the others drank downstairs and pretended the noise was the wind.

When the knocking stopped, in the last minute before they'd all reached the top, there had been a pause. A long one. And in that pause, plain as speech, plain as a name called up a stairwell, the thing below had made one further sound.

It had knocked out a rhythm Danny knew. Four beats and a rest. Four beats and a rest. The opening figure of a song — he'd heard it drifting up through the floorboards of the Lamb and Lantern every night that week, Edith Marrow singing to herself in the kitchen after hours, low and reluctant, a ballad the village wouldn't sing twice.

Whatever was sealed beneath Vessel Farm knew the tune.

And it was waiting, Danny thought, staring at the ceiling while the frost feathered the inside of the window glass, for someone who knew the words.
