## 10. The Song Begins Again

The cold in the Lamb and Lantern that night had a texture to it, a grain, like the inside of a bell that had been struck once, long ago, and never quite stopped humming. Ivo Whitlock sat in the small back room Edith had given him — not a proper guest room, he understood now, but a room that had been *kept* for something, though he could not have said what — with his tape recorder open on the desk before him like a patient on an operating table.

He had been at the reels for three hours. The lamp was turned low because the low light made the handwriting easier, which made no sense, and he had stopped questioning the things that made no sense. That was the first symptom, though he did not know it yet. In Hollowmere, sense was a currency that lost its value the longer you stayed.

The transcription lay beside the machine. Eleven ballads. Fourteen singers. And in every one of them, without exception, the same wound: a stanza gone. Not skipped — he had learned the difference by now, the way a doctor learns to distinguish a scar from a wound that never closed. A skip has edges. You can hear the singer step over the gap, a little hitch in the breath, a small dishonesty in the rhythm. This was nothing like that. This was a silence with *shape*. The melody bent around it the way a stream bends around a stone that has always been there. The singers did not stumble at the gap. They *avoided* it, the way you avoid the third stair in a house where you have always avoided the third stair, without thought, without memory of when the avoidance began.

He had transcribed it as blank lines. Four of them. Four lines of nothing, held open like a hand.

He was reaching for his pen when the radio in the corner made its first sound.

The set was an old Bush, bakelite and brass, the kind of thing that belonged to the room the way the water stains belonged to the ceiling. Edith had mentioned it once, offhand, in the way of people who mention things they do not want you to look at too closely: *Radio's there if you like, though it's mostly weather, and the weather's mostly wrong.* Ivo had switched it on the first night, found a hiss like sand poured over sand, and switched it off again. He was not a man who needed the wireless. He had come to Hollowmere precisely because it was a place the twentieth century had not finished reaching.

But now the hiss had changed.

It was not that the static had become music. It was subtler than that, and far worse. The static had become *shaped*. It rose and fell. It breathed. Somewhere inside the granular roar of it there was a contour, and the contour was familiar, and the familiarity arrived in his body before it arrived in his mind — a tightening across the shoulders, a slowing of the breath — before the scholarly part of him caught up and put a name to it.

*The Well-Wife's Lament.*

He sat very still. The lamp hummed. Snow ticked against the window like a fingernail, patient, testing.

The static was singing.

Not words. Not yet. But the melody of the Lament was in there, the way a face is in a fogged window — present, undeniable, and one wipe of the hand from being gone. It came in the old modal line, the one that sat wrong on the modern ear, that fell where a major tune would rise, so that every phrase sounded like an apology. Edith had sung it for him on the second night of his stay, one time only, her hands flat on the bar top as though the wood might shift under her. *Down by the water, down by the well, my love went under and no bell knell—* and then the hitch, the avoidance, the shaped silence.

The radio was not hitching. The radio was going *through*.

Ivo's hands were moving before he had decided anything. That was the folklorist in him, the collector, the man who had spent twenty years teaching himself that the first duty was to the record — get it down, get it true, understanding could come later or never. He threaded a fresh reel with fingers that were not quite steady, thumbed the record button, and watched the little red eye open like something waking.

The tape began to turn. The static sang on.

And then — he could not say how long after, thirty seconds, a minute — the melody deepened. The contour sharpened. And inside the hiss, the way a name surfaces in water, came the first suggestion of words.

He leaned forward until his face was nearly touching the bakelite grille. The room was very cold now. He could see his own breath ghosting across the top of the machine, and he noticed, distantly, that the breath was coming out in time with the static, that he was breathing in the Lament's rhythm, that the song had reached into his chest and taken up residence in the oldest part of him, the part that keeps time.

*...and the verse that was never sung...*

He heard it the way you hear someone speaking in the next room — not the words entire, but the fact of words, the architecture of them, half-audible, a voice speaking through a wall of white noise. It was a woman's voice. Or it was no voice at all and his ear was finding a woman in the static the way the eye finds figures in fire. But it *moved* like a woman, it *grieved* like a woman, and it was singing the lines he had transcribed as four blank spaces, and the lines had — he strained, he ached toward them — the lines had *words*, and the words were almost, almost, almost—

The lamp went out.

Not the radio. The lamp. The bulb simply died, a small glass tick in the dark, and Ivo sat in blackness with the red eye of the recorder glowing at him like the last thing alive, and the static went on singing, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the dark had not interrupted the song. The dark was part of it. The dark was the room the song had been sung in, all along, in every kitchen and every church and every sealed room in Hollowmere's long obedient history. You did not hear this verse with the lights on. The lights had never been the point.

He did not turn the lamp back on. He could not have said why. He sat with his hands on his knees and listened, and the half-voice sang, and the snow ticked at the glass, and somewhere below him — below the inn, below the inn's foundations, below the bedrock itself, if bedrock was the bottom of things — something listened back.

* * *

He played the tape at dawn. He had not slept. He had sat in the dark until the window went from black to slate to the color of old pewter, and when the light was finally strong enough to justify a lamp again he switched the machine from record to playback, wound the reel back, and pressed the button with the particular reverence he reserved for first hearings — the moment, always, when a song either became a document or dissolved back into the night that made it.

The tape hissed.

That was all. Sand over sand. The flat, unshaped, democratic hiss of a radio between stations. He let it run. He let it run for four minutes, five, six, the length of the Lament entire, and there was no melody in it, no contour, no grieving woman-shaped presence, nothing but the honest noise of empty air.

He rewound. He played it again. He checked the connections, the heads, the bias, with the methodical calm of a man checking a rope after a fall. Everything was in order. Everything was always in order. The machine was a good machine, a Ferrograph, bought secondhand with his first advance and faithful for nine years. It had recorded Edith's single performance of the Lament and every ballad since, and those tapes were real, those tapes had *content*, he had played them back a dozen times.

But the radio. The radio's singing had gone into the machine and the machine had kept nothing.

He sat back. The chair creaked. Outside, Hollowmere was waking into a morning the color of dishwater, chimneys standing up out of the snow like the pipes of some buried organ, and Ivo became aware that he could hear — faintly, through the floor, through the walls — that other radios were doing something. Not singing. Not now, in the daylight. But making a sound. A low, restless sifting, all over the village, like the sound of a great many people turning over in bed.

He went down.

The public bar was empty at that hour except for Edith, who was standing behind the bar not doing anything — not polishing, not pouring, just standing, with her hands flat on the wood in exactly the position she had used when she sang. The wireless above the optics was on. He had not seen it on before. It was hissing, and the hiss had a shape, and the shape was resting, the way a dog rests its chin on its paws.

"Edith," Ivo said. "Your radio."

"I know." She did not look at it. She looked at him, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she had done before and always gotten the same terrible answer. "It started at midnight. They all did. Every house that's got one. Mrs. Petchy's got one she's never switched on since her husband died — it was on when she came down this morning. Warm, she said. Warm as a loaf."

"Did you hear—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and fast, and her hands pressed harder on the bar. "Don't say what it sang. Not in daylight, not out loud. It hears us say it and it *keeps* it, do you understand? Everything said in this village, it keeps. That's the whole arrangement. That's been the whole arrangement since before there was a village."

"Then it kept the verse," Ivo said quietly. "Last night. It gave the verse back."

Edith closed her eyes. When she opened them she looked older, and tired in a way that sleep would not touch, and — this was the thing that stayed with him, that would stay with him for the rest of his life — *relieved*, in some small and horrible compartment of herself, as though a debt long dreaded had finally been called in and the waiting was over.

"It doesn't give, Mr. Whitlock," she said. "It *offers*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. My mother told me: the year it offers, the year the radios sing the unsung part, that's the year it wants a listener. Not a singer. A *listener*. Someone who can hear the verse whole and hold it." She picked up a glass and began, at last, to polish it, though it was already clean, her hands needing work the way his needed transcription. "And there's only one man in Hollowmere this winter who came here to listen."

The glass squeaked under her cloth. The radio hissed its shaped hiss. Outside, the snow had begun again, fat and silent, filling in the village's tracks behind it, filling in the village's tracks *ahead* of it, and Ivo stood in the empty bar with the memory of a half-voice singing through static and the knowledge — arriving now, settling into him like snow into snow — that the tape in his room was blank. That the machine had heard everything and kept nothing. That whatever had been sung to him last night had been sung to him *alone*, and could not be carried out of this valley in any form but the one it wanted: himself.

He climbed back upstairs. The lamp worked now; the bulb had recovered, or been permitted to. The reel sat on the machine where he had left it. He threaded it, out of discipline, out of the scholar's reflex, and played it one final time from the top.

Sand over sand. Four minutes. Five. Six.

And then — at the place where the missing verse had been, at the exact position in the reel, to the second — the hiss *dipped*. A small, brief, deliberate silence in the noise, four beats long, shaped like a stanza, held open like a hand.

Then the hiss closed over it, and the tape ran out, and the little machine clicked off, and Ivo Whitlock sat alone in the kept room of the Lamb and Lantern, listening to the snow tick at the glass like a fingernail, like a knock, like something below the floor of the world asking — patiently, warmly, in no hurry at all — to be let in to the conversation.

Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, every radio in Hollowmere turned over in its sleep.

And beneath the village, beneath the well, beneath the door with its bolts thrown from the inside, something that had waited two hundred years to be heard began, very softly, to count.
