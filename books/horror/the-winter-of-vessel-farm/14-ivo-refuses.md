## 14. Ivo Refuses

The offer was still hanging in the air of the undercroft when I climbed back into the grey afternoon, and it hung there still as I climbed the stairs of the Lamb and Lantern, and it would hang there, I suspected, for a very long time — perhaps longer than either of us.

*You could be the next keeper,* Tam had said, standing among his ledgers of names with the lamp guttering behind him, his shadow thrown enormous against the wall of tally-marks. Two hundred winters scratched into plaster, one stroke at a time, by hands that had eventually stopped scratching. *You hear the verse. That's rarer than you know. Most folk hear nothing but a hole in the song.*

*I'm a folklorist,* I had told him. *I record things. I don't keep them.*

*No,* he said, and there was no anger in it, only a terrible patience, like water finding the low ground. *That's what you think you do.*

I had left without answering. There are refusals that require words and refusals that don't; mine was the second kind, made with my boots on the stairs and my coat already buttoned. But refusal is not the same as escape, and by the time I reached my room at the inn, with its water-stained ceiling and its window that looked out on the buried lane, I understood that I had only declined an invitation. The invitation itself remained. It sat in the corner of the room like a guest who intended to stay the winter.

So I packed.

I packed the way scholars pack: badly, obsessively, everything twice. My notebooks went into the leather satchel first — eleven of them now, their spines cracked, their pages soft as cloth from handling. Then the tapes. Forty-one reels of Hollowmere's music, each in its cardboard box, each labelled in my own hand: *Marrow, Edith — "The Well-Wife's Lament" — take 1.* Forty-one small coffins of cellulose and song. I wrapped them in my spare jumper because the cold had been making the tape brittle, and because some animal part of me wanted them warm against my body, wanted to carry them the way you'd carry something living.

The Nagra went into its case last. It weighed more than it should have. Everything in that room weighed more than it should have.

Edith found me kneeling over the suitcase like a man praying to luggage. She stood in the doorway with a tray she didn't set down — tea gone cold, I'd have wagered, though the steam said otherwise — and watched me fold my shirts around the dead weight of the recordings.

"You're going, then."

"I'm going."

"To London." She said the word the way you'd say *the moon*. "To your university. To your shelves."

"To people who will listen," I said, and heard how it sounded even as I said it, and hated myself a little for it. "To archives. To other folklorists. This material is extraordinary, Edith. The verse-structure alone — every ballad in this village terminates in the same lacuna, the same deliberate silence, and no collector has ever documented anything like—"

"Ivo." She set the tray down on the washstand with a click that silenced me better than a shout. Her face in the lamplight was old the way the churchyard yew is old, all weather and grain. "Do you know why I agreed to sing for you? Once each?"

"Because I paid you."

"Because I thought you were harmless." She almost smiled. "A soft southern man with his little machine, come to take our songs away and put them somewhere safe where they can't do any harm. That's what I hoped. And then you asked me to sing the Lament twice, and I saw your face when I wouldn't, and I thought: he hears it. God help us, he actually hears it."

"Hears what?"

"The gap." She crossed to the window and looked down into the lane, where the snow had stopped falling but seemed to be waiting anyway, gathered in the eaves and hedgerows like an audience between acts. "Every child in this village learns the songs with the hole already in them. We grow up thinking that's just how the songs go — that the missing bit is like a rest in the music, a place to breathe. You're the first person in sixty years to point at the hole and ask what's inside it." She turned. "And now you want to carry it to London. In a box. On a train."

"It's folklore," I said. "It's meant to travel. That's the whole nature of the thing — songs move, they mutate, they survive by being carried. If I leave these tapes here and this village goes under the snow, the tradition dies with—"

"With us?" She laughed, short and dry. "Mr. Whitlock, the tradition isn't going to die. That's rather the problem. The tradition is the only thing in Hollowmere that has never once been in danger."

I didn't understand her then. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the only inheritance I have left to manage. I did not understand her, and I was too proud to say so, and so I said instead: "The bus comes at seven. I'll be on it."

"There hasn't been a bus at seven since before the war," she said quietly. "There's a bus on Thursdays, if the driver's feeling brave, and he stops at the crossroads and won't come down the farm road. You knew that when you came."

"I came by car."

"You came by car with a man who charged you double and wouldn't turn his engine off while he unloaded your bags." She picked up the tray again, and paused at the door without turning around. "Thursday's tomorrow. If you're still here tomorrow, I'll make you a proper breakfast. If you're not—" She stopped. Started again. "If you're not, mind the ridge road doesn't drift. It drifts fast up there, faster than anywhere else, and it drifts *evenly*, which is worse. Snow that fills a road smooth, like nobody ever passed. Like the road was never cut at all."

She left me with the tea and the suitcase and the sound of her footsteps going down the stairs, each one a little further from whatever she had been trying to tell me.

* * *

I did not sleep. I told myself it was the work — there is always more transcription to do, always another fragment to collate — but the truth is that I sat on the edge of the bed with the lamp burning and listened to the house settle, and beneath the settling I listened for the other thing, the deep slow pulse I had first noticed in the well shaft, the rhythm underneath the village like a heart under floorboards. Some nights it was louder. Tonight it sounded almost contented. Almost patient. As if it had all the time that existed and intended to use it.

Around four I gave up on sleep and gave myself to the tapes instead, because a scholar's fear is best treated with a scholar's medicine. I threaded the reel from my first night at the Lamb and Lantern — Edith singing "The Well-Wife's Lament," her voice thin and true, the fire crackling, my own breathing audible too close to the microphone like a confession.

The song ran its course. Nine verses. And then the silence where the tenth should be — three seconds, maybe four, of room-tone and fire-crackle, and Edith's breath going strange in the middle of it, catching on something, and then the final verse arriving like a traveller stepping back onto a moving train.

I had heard it a dozen times. I had transcribed the gap as blank lines in my notebook, a little rectangle of white space, an honest scholarly notation of absence.

Tonight I did something I hadn't done before. I took the gap and I looped it — spliced the tape, taped the ends together, let those four seconds run in a circle through the machine while I bent close with the headphones on, close enough that the earcups pressed cold against my skull.

Fire-crackle. Breath. Room-tone.

And underneath — so far underneath that I will spend the rest of my life unsure whether I heard it or dreamed it — a voice. Not Edith's. Not mine. A voice like a woman singing through several feet of stone and several feet of water, the melody flattened by distance into something that was less a tune than a shape of attention, rising once, falling once, and stopping exactly where the verse should stop, as if it too had been taught the hole. As if the hole were not an absence in the song at all, but a presence in the room, and the song simply walked around it, the way you walk around a sleeping dog, the way you walk around a grave.

I cut the loop. My hands were steady. I noted that they were steady, in my notebook, in my neat scholarly hand, because noting things is what I do instead of screaming.

Then I finished packing.

* * *

Morning came the colour of dishwater, and with it the wind, which had changed in the night from the west to the north, and a north wind in Hollowmere does not blow so much as arrive. I carried my bags down the stairs — two trips, the Nagra balanced on top of the suitcase like a coffin riding a bier — and Edith was in the hall, as promised, with a proper breakfast laid on the long table: eggs, black pudding, bread still warm, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in.

"Eat," she said. "The crossroads is four miles and the wind's against you."

"I'll eat at the bus stop."

"There's no bus stop. There's a post that used to hold a sign, and the sign rotted off in '61, and nobody replaced it because—" she began, and stopped herself, and pushed the plate an inch toward me. "Eat."

I ate. Partly because the food was good and partly because refusing felt, suddenly, like a dangerous thing to do in this village — as if refusal itself had a price here, as if the whole architecture of Hollowmere were built on the principle that you accept what is offered. Eggs, black pudding, bread. Names given freely at the well. I ate quickly and did not look at Edith while I ate, because looking at her meant asking her what she wasn't saying, and I had spent nine days learning what happened to people in this village who asked.

At the door she put something into my pocket without asking — I felt the shape of it later, a small tin, and inside the tin, when I finally looked, three pieces of hard honey candy gone white with age. Provision for a journey. Kindness, plain and unmagical, from a woman who believed I might not survive the road. I have thought about that tin more often than I have thought about most human faces.

"Thursday," she said. "If the driver comes."

"If the driver comes," I agreed, and stepped out into the wind, and turned south toward the crossroads, and did not look back at the Lamb and Lantern, because looking back is how stories like this catch you.

* * *

The farm road ran south between high hedges furred with frost, and the wind came down it head-on, carrying snow that wasn't falling so much as being flung sideways, stinging, hissing against my coat like static. I walked with my collar up and my bag straps doubled and my chin tucked, and I counted my steps the way I counted verses, because counting is a wall you build between yourself and the dark, and I needed a wall that morning.

Four miles. I had walked them coming in, ten days ago, behind a car whose driver had talked the whole way about football and the price of feed and nothing else, carefully nothing else, and I had thought him merely taciturn. Now I understood he had been filling the silence the way you fill a silence in a house where something listens.

The road rose gently toward the ridge. The hedges thinned. The fields opened on either side, white to the stone walls, white to the sky, the whole world reduced to two colours and one sound — the wind, always the wind, though underneath it, fainter than memory, that pulse. I had stopped pretending I didn't hear it. That was the day's first surrender, though I didn't mark it as such at the time: somewhere on that road I stopped telling myself the sound was blood in my ears, or wind in wires, or any of the respectable explanations. It was the well. It was under the fields, under the snow, under the road I was walking on, keeping its time. And it was keeping time the way a drummer keeps time — for dancers yet to come.

At the top of the rise, where the farm road met the ridge road, I stopped and stamped the snow from my boots and looked for the crossroads.

The crossroads was not there.

Now — let me be precise, because precision is the last tool I have left and I mean to wear it out honestly. There was a crossroads. I had stood in it ten days ago. There was a signpost with one arm pointing to Hollowmere and one arm snapped off years ago; there was a drystone wall on the eastern side with a gap in it where a gate had been; there was a telegraph pole leaning at fifteen degrees with its insulators intact and its wire long since fallen. I remember the pole particularly because I had leaned my bag against it while I waited, and the bag had slid, and I had cursed it, and the cursing had echoed strangely off the wall.

I stood where the pole should have been and there was no pole. No wall. No gap. No signpost. The ridge road ran east–west ahead of me as a shallow trough in the snow — a trough, I noted, with a scholar's dying instinct for detail — but not a driven trough, not a ploughed trough. An unbroken surface, smooth as the skin of milk, running in both directions until the snow-haze took it. No tyre tracks. No footprints. No marks of any wheel or boot or hoof, in either direction, as far as the eye could follow, and the eye could follow a long way in that flat light.

Behind me, the farm road lay exactly as I had left it, printed with my own bootprints, sharp-edged, fresh, a line of evidence running back toward the village. Ahead of me and beside me: nothing. The world unmade. As if the road had been cut through the country and then the country had healed over it, the way flesh heals, leaving no scar.

I am a rational man. I want that on the record too, filed alongside the steadiness of my hands. So I will tell you what I did, which was the rational thing: I checked my map.

I had a quarter-inch Ordnance Survey sheet, 1963 printing, folded to softness along the same creases. I opened it right there in the wind, weighting the corners with my knees, and found the ridge road, and found the crossroads, and found the little circle that marked the signpost, and found — I found that Hollowmere itself was printed in the old style of lettering that the Ordnance Survey reserved for places whose names were traditional rather than current. *Deserted or antiquated*, the legend called it. I had noticed the lettering when I planned the trip and assumed it was a cartographic conservatism, a failure to update.

The map was not out of date. The map had been telling the truth the entire time. Hollowmere was not on any recent map because Hollowmere was not recently anywhere.

I put the map away. The wind took a corner of it and I fought it back into my coat like a man fighting a live bird. And then I did the thing I am least proud of in this whole account, less proud than the refusal, less proud than what came after: I turned east along the ridge road and I walked. Fast. Not running — a folklorist does not run, a folklorist observes — but walking with everything under it that walking can hide. East, because east was toward the main road, toward the valley towns, toward trains, toward the twentieth century, which I had begun to suspect was a place rather than a time, and one you could be exiled from.

I walked twenty minutes. Perhaps half an hour. The snow-haze thickened and thinned and thickened, and the road ran level and white and empty, and my bootprints filled behind me — that was ordinary, that was any winter's day — but ahead of me the snow did something I have never seen anywhere else and hope never to see again.

It drifted evenly.

Understand: snow drifts in sculpture. Wind sculpts. It builds ridges and hollows, waves and whorls, a topography of its own violence. What lay ahead of me on the ridge road was not sculpted. It was *smoothed*. Levelled. Filled to a single flawless plane, as if a hand had drawn a blade across it, and the plane extended ahead of me exactly as far as I could see, and it moved with me. I would crest a slight rise and find the smoothness waiting beyond it, patient, identical, a road that had forgotten it was a road. Twice I stopped and stood still and watched, and twice the haze closed in and the smoothness came closer without appearing to move, the way a tide comes, the way a deadline comes.

The third time I stopped, I turned around.

My bootprints ran back the way I had come — a hundred yards of them, two hundred, crisp and black-mouthed in the white — and then, at the edge of visibility, they stopped. Not faded. Stopped. Beyond that point the road behind me was as smooth as the road ahead, seamless, unmarked, as if no one had ever passed, as if I had never passed, as if the walker who had made those prints had been unwritten from the world at some fixed coordinate and the snow had closed over the sentence of him mid-word.

The wind dropped. All at once, the way a held note drops, and in the silence I heard it clearly for the first time with no wind to blame, no wires, no blood in my ears:

The knocking.

Three strokes, and a pause. Three strokes, and a pause. Patient as arithmetic, rising up through the soles of my boots, through the packed snow, through forty feet of earth and stone, from the direction of Vessel Farm — from below Vessel Farm — and it was not angry and it was not menacing and that was the worst of it. It was the knock of someone who knows you are home. Someone who has knocked before. Someone who has waited out longer silences than this and can wait out yours.

I stood on the ridge road in the dead calm with my recordings under my arm and my refusal in my mouth, and I understood, finally, with the full simple clarity of a man reading his own name on a document, that I had never been deciding anything. The decision had been made for me before I boarded a train, before I answered a letter, before I was born, possibly. Tam had offered me a role. The village had voted on my disposal. The well had measured my steps. And the snow — the snow was simply the outermost wall of the building I already lived in.

Escape is not among the options. I have written that sentence many times since, trying to drain it of its power by repetition, and it has never once worked.

I turned back toward Hollowmere. My bootprints reappeared ahead of me as I walked, one by one, unfolding out of the smooth snow like a story remembering itself, leading me home — and I want you to notice, as I did not notice until much later, that they led me not the way I had come, but by a path I had never taken, a shortcut across the fields, straight toward the farm road, straight past the wellhead, straight to the door of the Lamb and Lantern, where Edith Marrow stood waiting in the doorway with her shawl pulled tight, watching me come across the white fields with an expression I had seen once before on the face of a woman in a ballad: the Well-Wife, at her window, watching her husband walk back out of the water.

"You couldn't leave," she said. It was not a question.

"The road's gone."

"The road's been gone for a hundred years," Edith said, and stepped aside to let me in. "Come and sit down, Mr. Whitlock. There's been news while you were out walking. It's about the vicar."

And behind her voice, faint under the floorboards, under the flagstones, under everything — three knocks, and a pause. Three knocks, and a pause. Keeping time.
