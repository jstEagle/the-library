## 28. The Mob at the Farm

They came at the hour when the light should have been failing but wasn't — the false dawn still hanging in the west like a wound that refused to close — and they came without hurry, which was the worst of it. A mob, I had always believed, was a thing of noise and rush, of torches and broken glass. But Hollowmere's mob moved like a congregation, forty or fifty figures in dark coats trudging through snow that squeaked under their boots, lanterns swinging on short handles, hymnals clutched to their chests as if the books might be weighed against their souls at some checkpoint between here and the farm gate.

I saw them first from Tam's bedroom window, where I had been sitting since before what passed for sunrise, the iron key warm in my fist because I could not make myself put it down. The key was small, older than any lock I knew, its teeth worn nearly smooth by two centuries of palms. It had been pressed into my hand by a man who then walked into black water singing, and I had not yet decided whether holding it made me a keeper or a thief.

"They're coming," I said, though no one was in the room to hear it.

Edith was downstairs. She had been downstairs all night, moving through Tam's kitchen with the terrible competence of a woman who has decided that if the world is ending she will at least meet it with a clean floor and a full kettle. When I came down she was setting out cups — five cups, though there were only two of us — and I understood that the cups were not for guests. They were for the dead. For Tam. For the vicar. For the drowned man from the crew whose name I never learned. For her sister, perhaps, whose name had been given freely at the well so long ago that grief had worn smooth as the key.

"Edith," I said. "Look."

She looked. She did not gasp or wring her hands. She set down the fifth cup with a small ceramic click, precise as punctuation, and said, "Danny Oakes has his father's shotgun."

"Will he use it?"

"He'll use it the way he does everything," she said. "Badly, and meaning well." She untied her apron and folded it over the back of a chair, a gesture so domestic it made my throat ache. "Get your coat, Mr. Whitlock. If we're going to be murdered, we'll not do it shivering."

* * *

We stood in the farmyard because Edith insisted on standing in the farmyard. "Inside is where they'll drag us out of," she said, "and I've seen what this village does to doorways." So we stood on the frozen mud before the house that had kept the door beneath it for two hundred years, and we watched the lanterns come up the lane like a procession of slow orange planets.

The snow around the farmstead was wrong, and had been wrong since the solstice began refusing to end. It fell upward sometimes, in thin silver threads, rising off the fields like breath drawn back into lungs. My footprints from the night before — the ones I'd made carrying Tam's confession up from the flooded undercroft — were still crisp, unweathered, as though time itself had agreed to leave this place untouched while everything in it rotted. And now the villagers' tracks appeared behind them and vanished ahead of them, so that the procession seemed less to arrive than to condense out of the white air, one figure after another, assembling themselves out of winter.

I counted them without wanting to. Forty-one. Then a latecomer brought it to forty-two. Some part of my mind, the folklorist part, the part that had come to Hollowmere to count verses and variants, noted that forty-two was not a tithe number, not a founder number, not any number the ballads cared about — and was almost grateful for the triviality of the thought, the way a drowning man is grateful for a straw.

Danny Oakes walked at the front. He had been a big man when he arrived with his survey crew in the autumn, broad and ruddy and loud, foreman's confidence in every stride. What walked now wore his coat and carried his father's shotgun broken open over one arm like a yoke, but the confidence had been eaten out of him, and what remained was a hollowed thing propped upright by purpose. His eyes found mine across two hundred yards of snow and did not waver, and I thought: *he held the crowd back once already.* Chapter upon chapter of this nightmare, and Danny Oakes had stood between me and these people once before, at the church, when the frost came through the windows. Whatever had broken in him since, something in him was still trying to hold.

Behind him, faces I had recorded songs from. Mrs. Aldery who sang *The Miller's Daughter* with a laugh caught in it. The Hollis brothers, whose harmonies had made me weep into my notebook like a sentimental fool. Young Peter Quill, who couldn't have been more than nineteen, carrying a pitchfork with tines bent from years of honest work. They had fed me, sung for me, argued folklore with me by the inn fire. Now they walked behind a broken foreman with a gun, coming to feed me to a hole in the world.

"The clean solution," Danny called when they reached the gate. His voice cracked on *clean*. "That's what they're calling it. You heard them, Mr. Whitlock. Give the stranger to the well, the water goes down, the sun comes right. No more tithe. No more lists. No more names."

"I'm not a stranger," I said. "You know I'm not. Your own register says—"

"Your name's written, aye." He spat, and the spittle froze before it hit the ground, a small white bead rolling in the snow. "Written by hands that aren't ours anymore. Written by *it*, maybe. That's the point, don't you see? Everything's gone wrong since you came. The vicar's dead. Tam's dead. Three good people drowned in their own church cellar. The sun comes up backwards." He raised the shotgun, not aiming it, just holding it up the way a preacher holds a book. "Before you came, we had a bargain. Bad, but a bargain. We knew the rules. Now there's nowt but you and that key and a door that wants opening."

"The door doesn't want opening," I said. "It wants *hearing*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and if you give me to the well you'll teach it exactly the wrong lesson—"

"Hush now, Mr. Whitlock." It was old Mrs. Aldery who spoke, stepping forward out of the ranks, and her voice was gentle, which frightened me more than Danny's shouting. "We know you mean kind. You've a scholar's heart, everyone says so. But kindness isn't the same as rightness. Our grandmothers knew what to do with a problem like you. The well takes what's given freely, and the rest of us go on. It's not cruelty. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Edith repeated, and stepped forward to stand at my shoulder, and I felt her there like a wall. "Say the word again, Bess Aldery, and I'll tell them all what's in your family's page of the ledger. I'll tell them what your grandmother gave, and how freely."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat.

"You burned the ledger," Danny said to Edith, and there was something raw in it, something almost pleading. "You burned it, Edith Marrow. You freed every name. Do you know what that means? Every soul this village ever gave — it can *see* them now. It knows them now. That's why the water rose. That's why the sun's wrong. You did that to save him." He pointed the broken gun at me, and his hand shook. "And now we do the only sum left. One stranger against every soul in Hollowmere. Even you must see the sums."

"I see them," Edith said. "I also see that Tam Vessel spent his last hour on this earth telling this man to sing true, and walking into the flood so he'd have time enough to learn how. Are you going to spend Tam's death buying the well a second meal? Because that's what you'll do. The well doesn't want a body, Danny. It never wanted bodies. It wants the *listening*. And if you drown the only man alive who can hear the verse whole, you'll have given it the listening forever — on its terms, in the dark, with nobody left above ground who knows the words."

Silence. The false light hung in the west, sourceless, the color of an old bruise. Somewhere beneath our feet — I felt it rather than heard it, a vibration in the soles of my boots, in my back teeth — the water moved. The well was full now; Tam had told me it would fill completely by the true sunrise, whenever the world consented to have one, and the farmhouse sat on top of it like a cork on a bottle.

Peter Quill spoke up, young voice cracking. "My mum says the flood took the Coopers' girl. Took her right through a floor that was dry at supper." His pitchfork trembled. "Is that arithmetic too, Mrs. Aldery?"

"That was the ledger burning," someone else said. "That was Edith's doing."

"And the vicar?" Edith's voice rang out clear and hard. "He burned the lists years ago, before ever I touched a page, and he died with my guest's name wet in his fist. Was that my doing too? Or is it possible — is it *possible*, you stubborn, frightened, singing people — that the bargain was rotten from the start, and everything since the diggers struck that door has been the sound of it coming apart?"

"It's possible," said a new voice, low and level, "that it's neither."

The crowd parted, reluctantly, the way water parts around something it doesn't want to touch. A woman came forward whom I didn't know — gray-haired, in a man's overcoat, with the parish register bound in oilcloth tucked under her arm like a loaf of bread. Behind her came three more elders, and I recognized the shape of what was happening: the village had sent its quiet ones, its keepers-of-records, its Mrs. Alderies of the next generation down. The mob had a second mind, and it had been walking behind Danny all along.

"Miss Halloway keeps the register now," Danny said dully. "Since the vicar."

"I keep it," the woman agreed. She stopped ten feet from us and looked at me with pale, unhurried eyes. "Mr. Whitlock. I've read every word you wrote in the inn's guest book. I've read the letters you posted from our box, or tried to — none of them left the valley, by the way, they're all in the vestry drawer, returned to sender by no postman anyone remembers. You are either the most dangerous man ever to sleep under Edith Marrow's roof, or the most necessary. I've prayed on it. The answer I got was neither." She unwrapped the oilcloth. "So I brought the book. Let's ask it."

* * *

What followed was the strangest trial I have ever witnessed, conducted in a farmyard under a sun that rose in the wrong quarter, with the defendant standing ankle-deep in snow that fell upward, and the evidence being a book that had begun, overnight, to write in itself.

Miss Halloway opened the register on the gatepost's flat stone. The pages turned without help. I watched them turn — watched paper move against paper with no wind and no fingers — until they came to the newest entries, the ones below the vicar's last careful hand. And there, in ink that glistened wet despite the cold, in a hand that was not the vicar's, not Miss Halloway's, not any living hand I knew — a hand that looped and slanted like handwriting trying to remember how handwriting worked — ran a single line:

*The listener sings at the door or the listener is sung.*

Below it, in the same wet ink: *One way or the other. Choose before the water chooses.*

Nobody spoke. The lanterns hissed. Somewhere far off, or not far off at all, I heard knocking — three blows, patient, spaced like a heartbeat — and every head in that yard turned toward the farmhouse, toward the ground beneath it, toward the door sealed from the inside that had been waiting longer than any of their families had kept its vigil.

"There," Miss Halloway said quietly. "There's your clean solution, Danny Oakes. The book gives him to the door — but it gives him *singing*. Not drowned. Not fed. Singing." She closed the register, and I swear the pages resisted her, leaning back against her hands like something reluctant. "Which means the old woman's right and the arithmetic's wrong. We don't get to spend him. We only get to lose him — down those stairs, to that door, with that key. Or keep him here and lose everything else."

Danny stared at the closed book. Then at me. Then at the shotgun in his own hands, as if he'd forgotten he was holding it, as if he'd been holding it for someone else all along and had only just noticed the weight.

"If he goes down," Danny said slowly, "and he sings wrong — like I did, in the church, like the water taught me I did —"

"Then we'll know soon enough," Edith said. "And you can drown us all afterward, if it pleases you."

A laugh went through the crowd at that — a thin, awful, involuntary laugh, the kind grief makes when it slips its leash — and I felt the mob change. Not soften. Change. The pitchforks lowered by degrees, not from mercy but from a dawning, dreadful understanding that the thing they had marched here to do had already been done to them, that the choice had never been theirs, that it had been written in a wet hand in a book that turned its own pages. Fear, I thought, watching their faces. Fear is the one song every village knows without being taught.

Danny broke the shotgun fully open and shook out the shells into the snow, two red cylinders lying bright against the white like drops of blood that hadn't soaked in yet. "I led you here to do a hard thing," he said to them, loud, foreman's voice dredged up from somewhere deep. "I'm leading you home to do a harder one. We stand off. Nobody touches the farm. Nobody touches him." His eyes met mine one last time, and what was in them was not forgiveness — it was something better and worse, a debt he hoped I'd die repaying. "Sing true, Mr. Whitlock. For Tam. For my crewman. For all of them. Sing *true*."

They went back down the lane the way they'd come, unhurried, congregational, their tracks vanishing behind them, their lanterns dimming one by one as if the light were being called home. Until only Edith and I stood in the yard, and the wet line in the register's memory, and the knocking beneath the earth, patient as winter, waiting to hear what we would choose.

Edith picked up the fallen shells and pocketed them — practical to the last, that woman — and then she took my arm, and her grip was iron, and her voice when it came was very quiet.

"The stairs are flooded to the third step," she said. "The water's warm as breath, and it rises while you watch. Tam's key opens the chamber door, and past that is the well-door itself, and past *that*—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Past that is whatever's been knocking since before my grandmother's grandmother gave its first name. I can't go with you. Someone has to stay above and mark the hours, in case the sun remembers which way is east."

I looked at the farmhouse — at the kitchen window where five cups stood on a table for the dead, at the cellar hatch beyond which the stair went down into water the color of tea and the temperature of a held breath. In my coat pocket, the iron key seemed to pulse gently against my palm, like a second heartbeat learning to keep time with mine.

Down there, something vast and lonely and older than the valley had been waiting two hundred years for a listener. It had waited through generations of names given freely, through Tam's long obedience, through the deaths of everyone who might have answered it wrong. And now, at the bottom of a flooded stair, at the seam of a door bolted from within, it was waiting for me.

"Edith," I said. "If I sing wrong—"

"Then the last thing you'll have heard is a friend telling you she believes you won't." She pushed the cellar hatch open. Warm air rose out of the dark, smelling of stone and deep water and, faintly, impossibly, of snowdrops.

I took out the key. I took out my notebook, and tore out the page where I had reconstructed the verse, and read it once more by the light of the false western sun — *we remember you, we name you, you are not forgotten* — and folded it away.

Then I put my foot on the first step, and the water rose to meet it, warm as a welcome, and somewhere below, patient as ever, the knocking began again — three blows, spaced like a heartbeat, like an invitation, like a door about to open from both sides.
