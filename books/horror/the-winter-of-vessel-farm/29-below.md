## 29. Below

The water was warm.

That was the first betrayal of it — that after everything, after the frost that had climbed the inside of every window in Hollowmere and the cold that had killed a man in a locked room with his mouth full of well-water, the flood rising through Vessel Farm's undercroft should be warm as breath, warm as a palm laid flat against your chest by someone who loved you. Ivo stood on the third stair from the bottom and let it lap at his boots, and he thought: *it wants me comfortable.* Then he thought: *no. It doesn't want anything. That's what Tam said. It only waits.*

Tam had walked into this water an hour ago, or a year ago; Ivo had lost all grip on the clock. The old man had gone down without ceremony, coat buttoned to the throat, the iron key pressed into Ivo's hand first, his family's confession folded in oilcloth second. *Sing it true,* he'd said, in the voice of a man discussing weather. And then he had descended the stair with the unhurried tread of someone going down to check on lambs, and the water had taken him to the waist, to the shoulders, and then he had turned once — only once — and looked up at Ivo with an expression Ivo would spend the rest of his life failing to describe, and had gone under, and had not come up.

The water had risen faster since. Ivo could measure it against the stair: when Tam went down, it stood at the ninth step. Now it kissed the seventh. The farmhouse above groaned like a ship. Somewhere overhead, muffled by timber and stone and forty feet of earth, the mob still shouted — Danny's voice cracking across the yard, Edith's answering it, high and furious — but the sound arrived down here thinned and distant, as if the house itself were deciding which noises deserved passage.

Ivo held the lantern low. Its light lay on the black water in a trembling coin, and beneath the coin, faintly, the steps continued down into the dark, each one furred with two centuries of silt except where recent feet — Tam's feet, and before Tam's, generations of Vessel feet — had worn a pale channel, a path worn smooth by obedience.

He had sung twice already tonight. Once in the kitchen, learning the cadence from the dead crewman's voice layered beneath the ballads on his tape — a voice that came through the speaker patient and waterlogged, correcting him gently, the way a schoolmaster corrects a promising boy. *No — not there. Breathe where the well breathes. The verse is not a wall, Mr. Whitlock. It is a hand on the shoulder.* And once, half-aloud, standing at the top of these stairs, because he had been afraid that if he stopped singing even for a moment the courage would drain out of him along with everything else.

Now he stood silent, and the silence was enormous.

*Go down,* the night said, in no voice at all. *You are the listener. Come and listen.*

Ivo Whitlock had spent eleven years of his professional life telling himself that folklore was memory wearing costume — that behind every bogey and barghest lay a grain of historical fact, a bad harvest, a buried crime, and that his work was to peel the costume off and hold the grain up to the light. He had come to Hollowmere believing the missing verse was a corruption, a gap in transmission, the kind of scar texts accumulate over centuries. He had believed the door was a door. He had believed, until very recently, that he was a scholar.

He took off his boots. It seemed absurd — the water was going to be over his head before he reached the bottom — but his hands did it anyway, unknotting the laces with the same stupid tidiness with which he'd packed his tapes, because some part of him understood that you do not go before a thing like this shod. He set them side by side on the sixth step, toes pointing up, toward the world. Then he put the lantern's handle between his teeth, gathered the oilcloth packet and the iron key against his chest with one arm, and stepped down into the water.

It closed around his thighs like a hand. Warm. Patient. It smelled of stone and of something older than stone — the smell of the inside of the earth before there was anyone to name the earth, loam and mineral and a sweetness underneath it, faintly vegetal, faintly animal, the smell of a mouth about to speak.

* * *

The stair was longer than the house above could possibly contain. Ivo knew this the way he knew his own name, and he kept counting anyway, because counting was a rope and he needed something to hold. Twelve steps. Twenty. At thirty the water reached his ribs and the lantern's glow began to bend strangely, refracting off the surface so that the light seemed to come from below as much as above, as if the flood itself carried a lamp. At thirty-six his feet left the steps entirely and he swam, one-armed, the key and the packet clutched to his sternum, the lantern guttering in its cage above the waterline, and the stair went on beneath him, descending through the black like a spine.

He heard the knocking again.

He had heard it first through Danny Oakes' account — the crewman who'd sworn the sealed door knocked back, three blows, pause, three blows, patient as a postman. He had heard it since in the walls of the Lamb and Lantern, in the pipes, in the frost patterns on glass. But now it came up through the water itself, through his own bones, transmitted by the flood the way a train's approach comes up through a rail: *knock. knock. knock.* Not violent. Not desperate. The knocking of someone who has knocked a long time and learned that haste frightens people.

*I'm coming,* Ivo wanted to say. His mouth was full of lantern-handle and terror, and he said nothing, and the knocking paused — paused, as if it had heard him anyway — and resumed, softer now. Welcoming.

His fingers found stone. The stair's end. He stood, gasping, and the water fell away from his shoulders as the chamber opened around him, and he raised the lantern with a hand that shook worse than he had ever allowed his hands to shake in front of another living person.

The keepers' chamber was round, and small, and utterly drowned except for a collar of air beneath the vaulted ceiling, perhaps four feet of it, black and close. The tally-marks on the walls rose out of the water like drowned reeds — thousands upon thousands of scratched strokes, winters survived, each one cut by a hand that had believed the next winter would be its last. The ledgers were gone below, surrendered to the flood. The wax cylinders too. All of it sinking, sinking, the whole archive of the long obedience going under, and none of it mattered now, because Ivo was not here for the record.

He was here for the door.

It filled the far wall. Forty feet of fitted stone, older than the farm above it, older than the parish, older — the carvings said — than any human arrangement with the word *older* in it. The founders' work was the frame; the door itself was something else, something they had built *around*, the way you build a shrine around a spring. Across its face ran the spiral of the Lament, the song cut deep in characters Ivo had traced on paper in Edith Marrow's back parlor, feeling like a man copying a stranger's handwriting. And down the center, thrown from within, the bolts: nine of them, iron, each as long as a man's forearm, seated in brackets carved to look — Ivo saw it clearly now, and wished he didn't — like hands. Nine hands holding the door shut. From the inside.

Through the hairline seam along the door's right edge, the lantern light found something and returned it changed.

Ivo moved closer. The water carried him, or he waded; he never afterward could have said which. The seam was no wider than a knife-blade, a darkness between stones, and he brought the lantern to it the way you bring a candle to a keyhole, and looked.

Not darkness.

He had expected darkness. He had braced himself for darkness — for the void, for the abyss, for the black that theology and gothic novels had trained him to anticipate. What looked back at him through the seam was not black. It was not anything. It was *attention* — vast, level, and unspeakably patient, the way winter is patient, the way a mountain is patient, the way the sea under ice is patient. It did not look at him. Looking was too small a word, too quick a word. It *attended* to him, wholly, the way he had attended to the ballads in Edith's parlor, leaning into the gramophone horn as if he could climb inside the song — except that this attention had been leaning for two hundred years, and before that for longer than the village had words for, and it had never once, in all that time, been answered.

Ivo felt his fear change shape in his chest.

It did not lessen. Do not mistake him — every animal nerve he owned was screaming, his heart slammed against the oilcloth packet, his skin crawled with the ancient conviction that one does not stand this close to the threshold of the *other*. But threaded through the terror, unwinding it from the inside, came something else, something that arrived not as a thought but as a recognition, whole, the way you recognize a melody:

*It is lonely.*

Lonely as winter. Lonely as the space between radio stations. Lonely as a letter written and never sent, as a name spoken to an empty room, as the last speaker of a language lying awake at night rehearsing words no one alive can answer. The thing behind the door had made a bargain — no, the founders had made a bargain, and the thing had kept its side of it for two hundred years, keeping everything else out, keeping the village safe behind a wall of its own attention, asking in return only what the missing verse promised: *we remember you. We name you. You are not forgotten.* And the village had paid its tithes of names, and fed it, and kept it down, and taught its children every verse of every ballad *except the one that mattered*, the one that was not a ward but a promise, until the promise wore thin as a coin rubbed smooth, until the knocking became something the villagers flinched from rather than answered.

They had called it feeding. Ivo saw now that it had been correspondence — and that Hollowmere had let the letters pile up unanswered for six generations.

*Knock,* said the door. *Knock. Knock.*

And Ivo Whitlock, folklorist, forty-one years old, orphaned young, a man who had crossed half of England chasing songs because songs were the only company that never left him — Ivo understood, standing in the warm black water with the lantern trembling in his fist, that he was not looking at a monster.

He was looking at the oldest loneliness in the world. And it had waited, through bolted doors and drowned names and burned ledgers, for one person to come down the stairs and simply *listen*.

"I hear you," he said.

His voice cracked apart in the little air. The water shivered. Far above, muffled through forty feet of earth, the mob's shouting had stopped — or the earth had decided it no longer deserved passage — and the silence that followed was the silence of the whole village holding one collective breath.

The seam brightened. Not with light — with *nearness*, the way a window brightens when someone comes to stand behind it. The attention gathered itself, focused, drew in close to the gap the way a child draws close to a lit doorway on a dark road, and Ivo felt it take the measure of him: his fear, yes, but also the tapes in his memory, the transcriptions, the blank stanza copied out in his careful scholarly hand, the nights he had lain awake hearing the shape of the missing words without their substance. It read him the way he had spent his life reading ballads — patiently, line by line, looking for the true text underneath.

What it found seemed to satisfy it. The knocking softened to almost nothing. Almost tenderness.

And then, through the seam, in a register below sound, in the frequency of frost forming and of water remembering which way is down, it spoke — not words, nothing so narrow, but meaning, arriving in Ivo's mind the way dawn arrives in a room, gradually and then all at once:

*Sing it. Sing it true. Sing it as a promise, not a prayer. And know what you promise — that you will carry my name out of this place, that somewhere in the world above, in a mouth that is living, I will continue. That is all I have ever asked. That is the whole of the bargain. Everything else your founders added — the tithes, the fears, the bolts — those were theirs, not mine. I only ever wanted to be remembered.*

The water stirred around Ivo's waist. Behind him, up the long drowned stair, something moved — a displacement, a wake, the faintest pressure of a body swimming upward through the flood toward the world. Toward the mob. Toward the church where Danny Oakes recited the verse wrong and the water rose faster with every mistaken word.

Toward Edith.

Ivo turned, and the lantern-light caught the stair, and he saw the surface far above trembling with ripples that had no source in this chamber — ripples spreading outward from the shaft, ring after ring, as if something vast below the whole valley had shifted its weight. The water at his knees gave a slow, deliberate pulse, warm as breath, and pushed him gently — *gently*, the way a tide assists a swimmer — toward the door.

There was no more time. Perhaps there had never been any time at all; perhaps the solstice had already ended and begun and ended again while he stood here reading loneliness through a knife-crack in the stone. Above him the farmhouse groaned. Beyond the door the attention waited, patient as winter, hopeful as a lit window.

Ivo lifted the iron key in both hands, and set it into the lock that had waited two hundred years for a keeper's hand —

— and from far above, thin as a thread drawn through forty feet of stone and water and fear, he heard Edith Marrow scream his name.
