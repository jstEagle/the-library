## 3. Zero

Juniper Vane had a system for the counting.

It wasn't a good system. It was the kind of system you build out of denial and laminated charting forms, but it had held for fifteen years, and Juniper was loyal to it the way you're loyal to an old truck that starts on the third try — not with love, exactly, but with a kind of stubborn gratitude.

The system went like this: when a patient began to count backwards from one hundred, you did not correct them. You did not say *Mr. Aldous, there's no need to count anything, you're in hospice, the only appointment left is the one nobody keeps.* You nodded. You adjusted their blanket. You wrote *verbalizations of numbers, non-distressed* in the chart, because *counting down to his own death with eerie precision* was the kind of phrase that got flagged by administrators who preferred their dying patients metaphorical.

Fifteen years. Forty-one patients — and yes, Juniper knew the number, because they counted the counters, which was its own private joke they'd never told anyone, a joke with no punchline except the number itself. Forty-one people had lain in Juniper's ward and counted backwards, and thirty-nine of them had reached zero within a minute or two of their last breath. The other two had stopped at eleven and nineteen respectively, gotten distracted by a hymn or a hallucination of a dog, and died later that week off-rhythm, like dancers who'd lost the music.

Coincidence, Juniper always said. Grief-stricken hallucination. The mind tidying up before it closed the shop. There was even literature about it — terminal lucidity, end-of-life visions, the brain's strange final accounting. Juniper had read all of it twice and underlined nothing, because none of it explained why the counting was always backwards, always from one hundred, always in the patient's own voice but somehow *slower*, as though someone else were feeding them the numbers through a long straw.

Tonight Mr. Aldous was at sixty-one when Juniper came on shift.

"Evening, Mr. Aldous." They hung their coat on the hook by the nurses' station, checked the board. Four patients tonight. Quiet Tuesday. "How are we feeling?"

"Sixty," said Mr. Aldous, to the ceiling. He was ninety-three years old, a retired schoolteacher with hands like folded paper, and he had started counting at seven-forty that morning, which meant he'd been at it for nearly twelve hours. Nobody counted that slowly on their own. It was as if the numbers were being rationed.

"Sixty," Juniper agreed pleasantly. "That's a fine number. Do you know what I had for dinner? Meatloaf. Hospital meatloaf, so really more of a meat-adjacent loaf."

"Fifty-nine."

"You're a tough audience."

Juniper made their rounds. Room 2, Mrs. Okonkwo, sleeping with her mouth open, her breathing doing that thing it did — the long pauses that made Juniper stand in the doorway and wait, professionally, until the next breath arrived like a late bus. Room 4, Danny Pell, forty-six, liver failure, watching the television with the sound off because he said the commercials were lies loud enough already. Room 6 was empty since Friday, bed stripped, the plastic mattress bare and faintly luminous under the night light like something waiting to be sat on.

At eight-fifteen, Mrs. Okonkwo began to count.

Juniper was at the station charting when it happened, and the first thing they felt was not fear but a kind of administrative alarm — *she hasn't started yet, she's not due yet* — followed immediately by the second thing, the real thing, arriving underneath like cold water finding the crack in a basement floor.

Mrs. Okonkwo had never counted. In three weeks on the ward she had spoken perhaps forty words total, most of them in Igbo, all of them addressed to her daughter. She did not know the tune. Nobody had taught her the tune; there wasn't a tune, Juniper reminded themselves firmly, there was no tune, that was the kind of thought you got at hour nine of a double.

"Ninety-eight," said Mrs. Okonkwo, in English, clear as a bell.

Juniper stood up.

"Ninety-seven."

From Room 4, muffled but unmistakable, Danny Pell: "Ninety-seven."

They were synchronized. That was the detail that did it, that slid under fifteen years of armor like a letter through a mail slot. Not just counting — *synchronized*, both of them landing on ninety-seven at the same instant, two voices in different rooms with different diseases and different lives, keeping time together like they could hear each other through the walls.

And then, from Room 1, Mr. Aldous — who had been at fifty-eight, who had been counting all day at his glacial rationed pace — said, "Ninety-seven."

He skipped fifty-eight through ninety-eight. He jumped. Twelve hours of slow descent and he leapt sideways into step with the others, the way you fall into line when the parade comes past your house whether you meant to join it or not.

Juniper walked the hall slowly, the way you walk toward a sound in the dark when you're hoping your own footsteps will talk you out of it. Room 1: Aldous, eyes open, ceiling-bound, lips moving. Room 2: Okonkwo, awake now, her thin hands above the blanket, fingers ticking down with each number like she was playing an invisible piano with only descending keys. Room 4: Danny Pell, sitting up, television forgotten, grinning — actually grinning, with the loose unguarded grin of a child on a sled — counting along.

"Ninety-one," they said together.

"Okay," Juniper said aloud, to no one, in the voice they used for talking themselves down. "Okay. Group behavior. Suggestion. One of them started and the others caught it, like yawning. Like laughing. This is a known phenomenon. Probably. Possibly. I'm going to look it up right after I finish pretending I believe it."

The trouble with the lie was that Juniper had told it so many times it had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket, and smooth things slip through your fingers.

"Ninety."

Juniper did the only useful thing they knew how to do, which was work. They went room to room. They took Aldous's hand — paper, cool, grip still there — and said, "You don't have to count, Mr. Aldous. You can stop any time. It's allowed."

His eyes came down from the ceiling and found them, and for a moment there was the man himself behind them, the schoolteacher, the one who'd spent forty years making children show their work. "I know," he said, gently, the way you'd correct a student who had the right answer by the wrong road. "But it isn't for me, dear."

"What's it for, then?"

"Ninety," said all three patients.

Aldous smiled, small and apologetic, and went back to the ceiling.

* * *

By nine o'clock the whole ward was counting, including — this was the part Juniper would take out later and examine at three in the morning for years afterward — the empty room.

Room 6. Stripped bed. No patient. And yet at 9:04, passing the door, Juniper heard, faintly, from the direction of the bare mattress, a voice too young and too far away to belong to anyone admitted since Friday:

"Eighty-eight."

Juniper stood in the doorway with their hand flat against the frame, the way you check a wall for vibration after a train goes by. The night light hummed. The mattress lay there, innocent, hospital-cornered in its bareness.

"Eighty-seven," said the room.

There is a specific loneliness in hearing a voice where no person is, and a worse loneliness in realizing the voice sounds *lonely itself* — not menacing, not hungry, just calling across a very great distance the way you call hello into an empty house you've just moved into, wondering if anyone will answer.

Juniper did not answer. Fifteen years of discipline. They closed the door softly, as if not to wake it, and hated themselves a little for the courtesy.

At the station, they pulled up the census on the computer and stared at it. Four beds. One occupied by no one. Three counters, synchronized, descending at a rate of roughly one number per forty seconds now — faster than Aldous's day-long crawl, slower than a natural count, metronomic, *scheduled*. Juniper opened a spreadsheet they kept hidden inside a file named STAFFING-METRICS-Q3, because even their secret dread needed camouflage, and logged the times. Eight-fifteen, onset, simultaneous. Rate of descent, consistent. Projected zero—

They did the math twice.

Zero at approximately 11:52 p.m.

Juniper sat back. Down the hall, three voices said "eighty-two" in unison, and the empty room, softer, said it too.

"That's fine," Juniper told the spreadsheet. "People die at night. Night is when we work. This is just... a scheduled evening."

The spreadsheet did not point out that all three patients had been given, by every clinical measure, days. The spreadsheet was better company than most colleagues.

* * *

At ten, the daughter of Mrs. Okonkwo arrived — Adaeze, a broad-faced woman with her mother's hands, who kissed her mother's forehead and then looked at Juniper with the question relatives carry in their shoulders.

"She's comfortable," Juniper said. "She's, ah. Counting."

"I hear." Adaeze listened a moment. "Eighty. What does it mean?"

Here was the fork in the road, the one Juniper had been approaching for fifteen years without ever quite reaching it. Every family, sooner or later, asked some version of *what does it mean*, and every time, Juniper had answered with the smooth pocket-stone: *it happens sometimes, near the end, the mind finds its own way of letting go.* Competence as armor. Kindness as anesthesia, administered first to self.

"It means," Juniper heard themselves say, "that she isn't alone in it. Whatever it is."

Adaeze studied them. "You have seen this before."

"A few times."

"And they reach the end of the numbers?"

Juniper's throat did something complicated. "Yes."

"And then?"

"Then they go," Juniper said. "Gently. It's — honestly, it's the gentlest version of this I've ever seen. Whatever the counting is, it isn't cruel."

This was new information. Juniper hadn't known they believed it until they heard themselves say it out loud: that the counting was not a symptom but a service. That somewhere on the other side of the numbers there was something patient enough to count with the dying, one number at a time, for hours, so that the leaving would feel less like falling and more like descending stairs with a hand on the rail.

Adaeze took her mother's hand and joined in on seventy-four, and her voice cracked on seventy-two, and Juniper stepped out into the hall to give them privacy and stood there under the fluorescent hum feeling the floor of their professional life tilt by degrees, slow and tectonic.

* * *

At 11:30, the sirens started.

Hollowmere was not a siren town. In fifteen years Juniper had heard sirens maybe a dozen times, and always singular, always going somewhere mundane — a kitchen fire, a fender-bender on Route 9. Tonight there were many, layered, moving fast, and they passed the hospice on Mill Street heading east, toward the flats, toward the old industrial lots, toward —

Toward the Bellamy lot. Juniper knew it without knowing how they knew it; everyone in Hollowmere knew the Bellamy lot the way you know a gap-toothed smile in a family photo, the fenced-off acre behind the shuttered church where nothing had been built in a hundred and twenty-five years and where, rumor held, nothing would. There'd been trucks out there all week. Survey flags. A crew from out of town breaking ground on whatever the developer's money wanted broken.

"Seventy-one," said the ward.

No. Wait. Juniper checked the spreadsheet with shaking hands and corrected themselves: the projected rate had accelerated. Somewhere around ten-forty the descent had begun to speed up, one number per twenty seconds, then per ten, as though whatever was rationing the numbers had decided the evening was running short. At 11:30 they were in the sixties and dropping fast, and the sirens were going east, and the arithmetic assembled itself in Juniper's head with terrible, simple clarity:

The counting had started when the digging started.

Not tonight — Juniper couldn't prove that, wouldn't let themselves try — but the *acceleration*, the synchronization, the empty room joining in. Something had changed in Hollowmere this week. Something underground had been uncovered, and the change had traveled through the town like frost through soil, and it had come up through the foundations of St. Ambrose Hospice and found the people closest to the door everyone eventually walks through, and it had said, in effect: *line up. We're starting early.*

"Fifty-eight," said three voices and one empty room.

Juniper stood at the window and watched the lights of the ambulances strobe past, eastbound, and felt the last load-bearing wall of their denial develop a crack you could have posted a letter through.

Because here was the thing, the thing they'd known since the first counter fifteen years ago — a woman named Marguerite Bell, as it happened, Bell like the church bell that never rang true, and Juniper had filed the coincidence in the same drawer as everything else — here was the thing: the counting had never once been wrong. Forty-one patients, thirty-nine zeros, and every zero had landed within a breath of death. Whatever kept the books on Hollowmere's dying, it kept them accurately. It was not a hallucination. Hallucinations don't keep time.

And if the counting was real — if there was truly something on the other end of the numbers, patient and punctual and, God help them, *gentle* — then it was real tonight, for three living patients and one impossible room, and it was accelerating, and the sirens were going toward the place where it lived.

"Fifty-one."

Mr. Aldous turned his head from the ceiling and looked directly at Juniper through the doorway, and his eyes were clear, teacher-clear, the eyes of a man about to ask a question he already knows the answer to.

"Nurse Vane," he said, in his own voice, breaking rhythm for the first time all day. "You'll want to be there when it opens."

"When what opens?"

"Fifty," said the ward.

Aldous smiled, closed his eyes, and rejoined the count.

Juniper looked at the clock. 11:38. Zero in — they ran it again, hating the fluency of their own dread — fourteen minutes. The sirens had faded east. Adaeze was singing to her mother now, low, harmonizing with the numbers instead of against them, having made her own peace with the arithmetic in a way that shamed Juniper's fifteen years of charts.

There were four patients on the ward. Three of them were dying, comfortably, on schedule, and Juniper's job — the job they were good at, the job that had eaten fifteen years and a marriage and most of a life — was to stay. To hold hands. To keep the vigil. That was the law of the ward: you do not leave the dying.

But the fourth patient was an empty bed in a locked room saying "forty-nine" in a child's voice, and the sirens had gone east, and Mr. Aldous — who had taught school for forty years and knew exactly which students needed to be sent out of the classroom — had looked straight at Juniper and told them to go.

Juniper grabbed their coat. Then put it back. Then grabbed it again, and their keys, and stood in the middle of the station like a person forgetting how doors work.

"Forty-one," said the ward.

"If I'm wrong," Juniper announced to the empty station, to the night, to whatever was listening on the far end of the numbers, "I will never forgive myself."

"Forty," said the empty room, close, as if it had drifted up the hall to see them off.

Juniper ran.

The parking lot was cold for July — cold enough that their breath showed, which was wrong, which was another crack in another wall, and Juniper catalogued it for later, for the enormous filing cabinet of *later* they were going to have to open someday soon. The car started on the second try. Old truck. Loyal.

They pulled out onto Mill Street heading east, toward the flats, toward the fence and the floodlights and the flag-marked dirt of the Bellamy lot, telling themselves the whole way — out loud, steadily, like a countdown in reverse — *this is duty, this is duty, this is duty.*

Behind them, faint through the closing car door, the ward reached thirty-eight.

Ahead, over the rooftops, the sirens converged on a glow of floodlights, and the air coming through the vents, even with the heat on, smelled like winter.
