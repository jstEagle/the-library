## 9. Tomás Counts Down

Dale Okafor had worked construction for twenty-two years, and in that time he had learned exactly three things about men: they would complain about the coffee no matter whose turn it was to buy it, they would lie about how much weight they could lift until the hernia taught them honesty, and they would never — not once — admit they were scared until it was too late to be useful.

Tomás Reyes had broken all three rules by nine o'clock that night.

He was lying on the folding cot in the site trailer, under the good blanket from Dale's truck, and he was talking. Not complaining. Talking in voices.

"Is the kettle on, Marta?" Tomás said, in a woman's voice, high and cracked like an old recording. "The kettle. The kettle, Marta."

"That's not Tomás," said Ephraim, who was sitting cross-legged on the floor with a juice box, watching with the terrible calm of a child at an aquarium. "That's Mrs. Halloran. She died in '61."

"How do you know that?" Dale asked, and then immediately wished he hadn't, because the answer was going to be one of those answers.

"She's one of the forty-one," Ephraim said, sucking his juice box dry. "She used to live where the porta-johns are."

* * *

Juniper Vane knelt beside the cot with the unhurried competence of someone who had done this ten thousand times, which, Dale supposed, they had. They turned Tomás gently onto his side, tucked the blanket around his shoulders, wetted a cloth and laid it across his forehead. Their hands moved like they were handling something both precious and breakable.

"His pulse is fine," Juniper said quietly. "Temp's low, but steady. He's not in pain, Dale. I need you to hear that. Whatever this is, it isn't hurting him."

"It's *wearing* him," Dale said. "Like a coat."

"Yes." Juniper didn't flinch from it. "It is. And we can't stop that. So we do what we do for anyone wearing out. We keep them comfortable, we keep them company, and we don't lie to them."

"He keeps asking for people who've been dead fifty years."

"I know." Something crossed Juniper's face — not fear, Dale realized. Recognition. Like hearing a song you'd spent years trying to forget. "I've heard it before. Different words, same music. Fifteen years of it."

"You said you logged it as coincidence."

"Fifteen times." Juniper wrung out the cloth. "I'm a slow learner."

* * *

By eleven, the voices had gone younger.

This was the part Dale could not make himself get used to: the direction of it. Old voices first — cracked, wheezing, full of complaints about kettles and weather and the price of flour — then middle-aged ones, then young men talking about baseball games played before Dale's grandfather was born, and now, at half past eleven, a girl's voice, bright and piping, maybe twelve years old, singing a skipping rhyme about a bell that wouldn't ring right.

"Ring it wrong, ring it long, ring it till the names are gone—"

"Stop that," Ruth Ibarra said sharply, from her corner. She had her ledger open on her knees and her notebook balanced on top of it, writing without looking down. She couldn't hear the voices — she'd told them that hours ago, deaf since a fever at nineteen — but she could feel the cadence through the trailer floor, or read it off their faces, or maybe she just knew things the way water knows downhill. "Don't write it down, either. Don't repeat it. It likes being repeated."

"Then what do we do?" Dale demanded. "Sit here and let him recite the whole town's obituary column?"

"We keep him comfortable," Juniper said again, and there was steel under the softness now. "Dale. Look at me. In my ward, when someone is dying, families always want to fix it. Fix it, fight it, argue with it. And the dying person spends their last days managing everyone else's panic instead of getting to just — be. Rest. Say what they need to say." They smoothed Tomás's hair back from his forehead, gentle as anything. "So we don't panic. We sit with him. That's the whole job tonight. Sitting with him."

"And when he runs out of voices?" Ephraim asked, from the floor. He said it the way kids ask if the car ride is much longer. "When he gets down to the little ones?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody wanted to say what everyone was thinking, which was: *then he gets to zero.*

* * *

At midnight, Tomás spoke in Dale's own grandmother's voice.

Dale was standing at the window, watching frost crawl across the glass in branching white ferns — July, and the trailer windows wore January — when behind him Tomás said, clear as Sunday dinner:

"Dale-baby, you left your boots on my clean floor again."

Dale's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the frost.

"Mama Odette died when I was fourteen," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened. "I haven't thought about that voice in thirty years. I couldn't have told you what she sounded like. But that's *her.* That's exactly her."

"It's not her," Juniper said. Firm, but kind. "You know that, right? It's wearing her the way it's wearing him. A borrowed coat."

"It fits too well."

"Coats fit better the longer they hang in the closet." Juniper got up and stood beside him at the window, shoulder to shoulder. Below, the excavation trench glowed faintly under the work lights, and at its far end the door sat in its frame of ancient iron, shut, patient, two inches of darkness around its edges like a held breath. "My first patient who counted — Mr. Wrenn, 2009 — did this exact thing. Spoke in his mother's voice on night three. His daughter nearly climbed into the bed after him, she was so convinced. And you know what Mr. Wrenn did, once she stopped crying?" Juniper smiled, small and tired. "He rolled his eyes. In his own voice, finally. Said, 'For God's sake, Carol, that woman has been dead forty years, don't take dictation from her.' Made her laugh so hard the nurses came running."

"So what are you saying?"

"I'm saying the dead don't scare me, Dale. I've kept company with them professionally for fifteen years. What scares me is the living forgetting how to sit still." They turned back toward the cot. "Come on. He's asking for water. Well — Mrs. Halloran is asking for the kettle. Close enough."

* * *

They took shifts, though nobody slept.

Ruth worked. That was her shift and her gift: she wrote. Page after page in her neat archivist's hand, timestamped, cross-referenced, every voice catalogued against the parish records she'd photographed on her phone before the sheriff cut the power to the site office. She'd hold up her phone screen for Juniper to see — *Halloran, Margaret, d. 1961, age 74* — and Juniper would nod, and Ruth would write the time the voice arrived, and somewhere in the machinery of her methodical grief-for-the-record there was, Dale realized, a kind of prayer.

Ephraim identified them all. Every single one, without hesitation, including three people Ruth couldn't find in any record at all. "Unbaptized," he said, shrugging, when Ruth showed him the gap in the register. "Some folks just don't get written down." He said it without bitterness, and Dale thought: *he means himself,* and had to go stand by the window again for a while.

And Tomás counted. Underneath the voices, underneath everything, like a river under ice, he counted backwards. Sometimes aloud, sometimes just his lips moving. Ninety-one. Eighty-eight. Eighty-four. Slower than a clock, but not by much, and always downward, and never once — Dale checked, obsessively, keeping his own tally on the whiteboard — never once did the number go up.

Around two in the morning, Tomás surfaced. Really surfaced, his own face coming up through the water of all those borrowed voices, brown eyes finding Dale's with sudden, exhausted clarity.

"Jefe." His own voice. Thin, but his. "Jefe, I'm sorry about the door."

"Hey. Hey, no." Dale grabbed his hand. "You didn't do anything. I broke ground. My call. My name on the permit."

"You should've seen it, though." Tomás smiled, dreamy, drifting. "Behind the hinges. There's a room back there, jefe, and it's not dark, it's just — unwritten. Like a page nobody's wrote on yet. It's been waiting so *long* for somebody to—" His eyes slid away, and the girl's voice came back mid-rhyme, bright and awful: "*ring it wrong, ring it long—*"

"Water," Juniper said briskly, already moving with the cup and straw. "Small sips, Tomás. There you go."

And that was the thing Dale kept coming back to, watching them: Juniper wasn't fighting it. Wasn't praying over him or shouting at the voices or demanding answers about the door. They just — cared for him. Turned him. Sponged his forehead. Held the straw steady while a dead woman's voice thanked them in 1940s Spanish. Treated the horror like a symptom and the man like a man.

"What are you doing?" Dale finally asked, low, when Tomás drifted quiet between voices. "Medically speaking. What's the play?"

"The play is hospice." Juniper said it plainly, no drama in it. "He's leaving. However this works, wherever he goes, he's leaving, and nobody gets to choose that for him — not me, not the thing at the door, not you. All anybody ever gets to choose is whether the leaving happens alone." They adjusted the blanket. "Fifteen years I watched people count down to zero and I told myself it was hallucination because the alternative meant the world doesn't work the way I need it to. You know what I know now? It doesn't matter what I believe. The care matters. The sitting-with matters. If the universe is cruel, the care is rebellion. If the universe is *lonely*—" they glanced at the window, at the frost, at the door beyond, "—then the care might be the only language it understands."

"That's not medicine."

"No," Juniper agreed. "It's better than medicine. Medicine just delays. This—" they nodded at Tomás, sleeping now, lips still moving, *seventy-nine, seventy-eight* "—this is witness. Anybody can delay. Witnessing is the hard part."

* * *

At three-forty, the voices reached a baby.

It lasted four minutes. Four minutes of a sound that emptied the room — not crying, worse, that wordless pre-speech burbling infants make, contented, trusting, the sound of a person who has never once been afraid because they don't know fear exists yet. Ephraim put his hands over his ears, which he hadn't done for any of the others, and Juniper gathered him up onto their lap without being asked, and he let them, and Dale filed that away in the place where he was keeping things he couldn't look at directly.

Then the baby-voice faded, and Tomás sighed — a real sigh, Tomás's sigh, the one he did when the rebar delivery was late — and said, in his own voice, perfectly clear:

"Seventy-seven."

And stopped counting.

The silence rang. Ruth looked up from her notebook. Ephraim lifted his head from Juniper's shoulder.

"He's resting," Juniper said softly. "Really resting. Look at him." And he did look peaceful, genuinely peaceful, the first time all night, breath slow and even, face smooth as a boy's.

But Dale stood at the whiteboard with the marker cap in his teeth, staring at the column of numbers he'd been logging all night, and felt the arithmetic of it settle over him like the frost outside. Seventy-seven. At this rate — he did the math twice, hoping he'd botched it — at this rate, Tomás reached zero a little after dawn.

*Dawn-minus-three-hours,* said some cold clerk in his head, and he hated it.

Outside the window, the frost on the glass had changed. All night it had grown in wild branches, ferns and feathers and lightning-trees. Now, in the space of a minute, it smoothed itself flat and began, deliberately, letter by letter, to write.

Not words. Numbers.

Seventy-six.

Ruth saw it first. She tapped the glass, then held up her phone to the others, camera flash glaring off the ice, and typed fast on the screen and turned it around:

**IT'S COUNTING WITH HIM. IT'S SHOWING US THE TIME.**

"Why?" Dale heard himself ask. "Why show us?"

Ephraim answered from Juniper's lap, around his thumb, in the matter-of-fact tone of a kid explaining why the sky is blue.

"Because it wants us to know it's not stealing him," he said. "It's *keeping* him. There's a difference. Mama Bellamy explained it to me once." He paused, frowning slightly, like a boy trying to remember a dream. "Stealing makes enemies. Keeping makes... arrangements."

Juniper went very still. Dale watched them watch the boy, and saw something move across their face — not fear, not anymore. Recognition again, deep and old, the look of a nurse who has just understood what a family is really saying when they whisper in the hallway.

"Arrangements," Juniper repeated quietly. "Okay." They set Ephraim gently on the cot beside Tomás, tucked the blanket around them both, and straightened up with the air of someone rolling up their sleeves. "Then we've got three hours to learn the terms of this arrangement. Ruth — I want that ledger. Every page. Dale, boil more water."

Dale blinked. "For what?"

"For tea," Juniper said. "If we're going to renegotiate with the dead tonight, we're doing it as hosts, not hostages. Get the good cups."

And outside, on the frozen glass, the frost patiently wrote: *Seventy-five.*
