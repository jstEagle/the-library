## 11. The Door Opens a Crack

Midnight arrived the way it always does at a siege — not with a trumpet but with a yawn.

Juniper had been awake for thirty-six hours, which was nothing. Fifteen years of hospice work had taught them that exhaustion was a country you learned to live in, with its own customs and its own terrible weather. You kept your hands steady. You kept your voice low and warm. You made the tea even when the kettle felt like it weighed forty pounds, because the tea was never really about the tea. The tea was about having something to do that wasn't screaming.

They made the tea.

"Anyone want honey?" they asked the trailer, because the trailer needed to hear a normal question. "We have honey. We have, let me see, one lemon, half a sleeve of saltines, and a truly criminal amount of instant coffee. Living large in Hollowmere tonight."

Ruth Ibarra looked up from the ledger, which she had been reading the way other people read scripture, or love letters, or ransom notes. She signed something quick and fluid — *yes, honey* — and Juniper had learned enough of her hands in the last twelve hours to catch it. Dale Okafor held up two fingers. Ephraim, curled in the corner bunk with his knees drawn up like a comma, didn't answer, but his eyes followed Juniper around the little kitchenette, and Juniper put honey in his cup too, unasked. Some guests you don't wait on.

*Some guests,* Juniper thought, and the thought had a cold edge to it, because there was another guest tonight, and it was not in the trailer.

It was forty feet away, down in the excavation trench, behind a door of black iron with its hinges on the wrong side, sealed shut from the inside by hands that had been dead for a hundred and twenty-five years.

Juniper distributed the cups. Dale took his without looking away from the trench, where the floodlights carved a hard white square out of the dark. Tomás lay in the back bunk, breathing in that terrible even rhythm Juniper knew better than their own heartbeat — the breathing of someone whose body had started saying its goodbyes without consulting him. Every so often he spoke in a voice that wasn't his. An hour ago it had been a little girl's voice, singing a counting rhyme. Juniper had sat with him and held his hand and told him, in the voice they used for the dying, *you're safe, you're not alone, take all the time you need* — and had understood, with a clarity that felt like a hairline crack running through them, that they had been rehearsing for this night their entire adult life.

"Twelve-oh-one," Dale said. "Nothing's happening."

"Nothing's been happening all night," Juniper said. "I don't trust it."

"You don't trust *nothing*?"

"I'm a hospice nurse, Dale. I've sat with four hundred people in their last hours, and I will tell you what I've learned: the quiet ones are the ones you watch."

Ruth tapped the table. When they looked, she signed, and Juniper translated aloud for Dale, half-guessing: "She says the ledger's final entry is dated tonight's date. 1899. Midnight. It just says —" Juniper squinted at Ruth's careful fingers. "*'He will ask.'*"

"Ask for what?" Dale said.

Ruth's hands moved, and stopped, and started again, slower, the way you handle something with a hairline fracture. *The rest of the names.*

The trailer went quiet except for the hum of the space heater, which was fighting a battle it was losing. The windows had gone white at the edges hours ago, frost climbing inward like ivy with ambition, and the cold that came off the glass had a texture to it, a *personality*, that no ordinary cold had ever bothered to develop. Ordinary cold doesn't want anything. This cold wanted.

Ephraim put down his cocoa. He'd barely touched it. He looked at the window, then at the adults, and said, in the small, matter-of-fact voice of a ten-year-old delivering news he considers obvious: "It's almost time."

"Time for what, sweetheart?" Juniper asked.

The boy shrugged, the universal gesture of children and cats: *I have told you what I know, the rest is your problem.* But his eyes went back to the window, and Juniper followed them, and that was when they saw the frost on the glass begin to *move*.

Not melt. Move. The branching patterns — ferns and feathers and the long white signatures it had been writing on every surface since sundown — drew themselves inward, gathering toward the center of the window like an audience taking their seats. The glass cleared in a slow circle, and through it Juniper could see the trench, the floodlights, the door.

And the door was opening.

* * *

It opened the way a mouth opens to say something important. Slowly. Carefully. Two inches — no more, no less — and then it stopped, as if it had measured.

The cold came out of the gap like water from a broken dam. Juniper felt it before they heard it or saw it: a pressure against the face, a current, pouring up the trench wall and across the frozen ground and through the trailer's cheap weatherstripping as if the trailer were made of paper. The space heater clicked off. Clicked on. Clicked off, and gave up, the way appliances do when they meet something older than electricity and know it.

"Everybody back from the windows," Juniper said, and was proud of how steady it came out. "Dale, get between Ephraim and the door. Ruth —"

Ruth was already at the window. Of course she was. She had her notebook out, and she was writing, and her face in the floodlight was the face Juniper had seen on medical students the first time they watched someone die: fascination and horror holding hands, refusing to let go of each other.

"Ruth," Juniper said again, gentler. "Come away from the glass."

She didn't come away. She turned instead, and her hands moved fast, urgent: *It's not attacking. Look. It's not attacking anything.*

And it wasn't. That was the thing Juniper would turn over in their mind for the rest of their life, the thing that broke their categories open like a dropped jar: the door stood two inches ajar in the bottom of a frozen pit, and the cold poured out of it, and the cold did nothing. It didn't surge. It didn't claw. It flowed around the floodlights and the generator shed and the trailer and it *waited*, the way a dog waits at a door it has been shut behind for a very long time.

"Tomás," Dale said suddenly. "Is Tomás —"

Juniper checked. Tomás was breathing. Counting, actually — under his breath, in his own voice again, thank God, or in whatever passed for God out here — *ninety-one, ninety, eighty-nine* — the way he'd been counting for hours, the way everyone in Hollowmere had been counting since the excavators struck iron. But his face had changed. The slack terror was gone. He looked like a man listening to music from the next room over. He looked, Juniper thought with a lurch, *comforted*.

*Oh,* Juniper thought. *Oh, that's worse.*

Ephraim had come to stand beside them at the window. He was small and warm against Juniper's side, and Juniper became aware that they had put an arm around him without deciding to, the way you catch yourself humming. The boy was watching the door with an expression Juniper couldn't read — not fear. Recognition, maybe. The way you look at a house you lived in before you could talk.

"It's not going to come out," Ephraim said. "It can't. It was *invited*, and invitations work both ways. That's the whole problem." He said it the way children say the devastating thing, plainly, and then asked for crackers.

Juniper gave him crackers, because what else was there to do.

* * *

The voice came at ten past midnight.

It did not come through the air. Juniper would swear to that until they died, and possibly afterward. It came up through the ground, through the soles of their boots, through the bones of their feet and the long bones of their legs, the way you feel a train before you hear it. It was not loud. It was not a roar or a whisper or any of the things the voice of a horror is supposed to be.

It was *polite*. That was the unbearable part. It had the manners of someone raised well and kept waiting anyway.

*"Forgive the hour,"* it said, and the words arrived inside Juniper's skull with the texture of breath on a cold window. *"I know the hour. I know all the hours. That is rather the whole of my condition."*

Dale made a sound like a man being introduced to his own death and finding it had a firm handshake. Ruth's pen stopped. Ephraim leaned into Juniper's side, and Juniper's arm tightened around him, and Juniper discovered that their body had made its decision about the boy long before their mind caught up: *this one is mine now, whatever that costs.*

*"I have been asked for,"* the voice said. *"For a hundred and twenty-five years, I have been asked for, and no one has asked me anything. Do you understand the difference? No. Of course not. Why would you. You have all had names."*

Juniper stepped to the window.

Every instinct they owned — fifteen years of triage instinct, of *do not enter the room until you've composed your face*, of self-preservation worn smooth as river stone — every one of those instincts said *get back, get small, get gone*. And beneath all of it, in the place where Juniper kept the thing they actually were, something else said: *someone is talking to you through a door. Go to the door. It's what you do. It's the only thing you've ever been good at.*

They opened the trailer door. The cold took their breath and gave it back frosted. They walked out onto the frozen mud in their nursing clogs, which was a foolish thing to do, and heard Dale hiss their name behind them, and did not stop.

The trench edge was iced over. The floodlights burned steady and stupid and human, and Juniper was grateful for them — for the evidence that people had built these, people had run power to this pit, people were real. Forty feet below, the iron door stood two inches open, and the cold poured out of the gap in a slow silver flood that did not quite look like air.

Juniper stood at the edge and looked down at it, and listened.

And what they heard — beneath the words, beneath the politeness — was not menace. Juniper had heard menace. Menace had a flavor; menace was the sound of a family deciding, in the hallway outside the room, that they would not be coming back tomorrow. Juniper knew menace the way a tongue knows a chipped tooth.

This was not that. This was a sound Juniper knew even better, because they had heard it through four hundred doors in fifteen years, in the small hours, from the rooms where the dying lay alone because everyone who loved them had already grieved and gone: it was *loneliness*. Vast, patient, geological loneliness. Loneliness that had stopped expecting anything and kept waiting anyway, because waiting was all it had been left, and it was going to do the only thing it could do *well*.

*"You are not running,"* the voice observed. It sounded surprised. It sounded like surprise was an antique it had to blow the dust off before using. *"The others run. The others are wise. You are standing at the edge of my door in inadequate shoes."*

"They're clogs," Juniper said. Their voice came out rough and human and absurd in the frozen night, and they were absurdly glad of it. "And yeah, well. Somebody's got to answer the door. That's — that's kind of my whole career."

A pause. The cold eddied, the way water eddies around a stone.

*"You are the one who keeps the dying company,"* it said. *"I have felt you. All these years, on the far side of all these walls — the little warm rooms, the counting, the hands. You hold the hands. Do you know what that is, from where I stand? Do you know what it looks like, to something that has not been touched since before your town had its name?"*

Juniper's throat closed. They thought of Mr. Aldous, counting backwards, and their own hand on his wrist, and the lie they had told themselves fifteen times, fifty times: *coincidence, grief, hallucination.* They thought of the spouse who had left, and the word *privacy*, which had been the word they'd used for the loneliness, the way you put a nice label on a jar of something that would poison you.

"I don't know what you are," Juniper said.

*"Neither do I, anymore,"* the voice said, and the honesty of it cracked something in Juniper's chest. *"I was a guest. That is all I was. A guest, and then a debt, and then a hunger, and hunger is what they made of me, and I have forgotten the shape of the thing I was before. But I remember the manners. They left me the manners. Isn't that the cruelest thing? They left me the manners and took the welcome."*

Down in the trench, the door stood two inches open, and the cold poured out like water, and it did not reach for anyone. It had been reaching for a hundred and twenty-five years. It was tired. Even its hunger had gone patient, the way a hospice patient's hunger goes, past appetite into something quieter.

*"I have come to ask,"* the voice said, and now Juniper heard it — the thing Ruth's ledger had promised, the thing Ephraim had known all along. The asking. *"As I was promised I might. The rest of the names. That is all. I am owed the rest of the names, and I have come, politely, at the appointed hour, to collect what your founders sold me before any of you were born. I would like you to know that I take no joy in it. I would like you to know that someone, at least, is sorry."*

Juniper stood at the edge of the pit with the frost writing its slow white signatures all around their clogs — and looked down at the gap in the door, two inches of dark and silver cold, and felt the most dangerous thing they had ever felt in a life spent around death.

They felt *compassion*. And compassion, Juniper knew — compassion was how you got killed in this line of work, not by the dying but by the systems around the dying, the bargains and the paperwork and the people who said *it's just how it's done* — compassion was how you ended up signing things you shouldn't sign.

But it was also, they thought, staring down at the loneliest thing in the world, the only thing they had that was worth anything.

"You said you were *asked for*," Juniper said. "Who asked for you?"

The cold went very still. Even the frost seemed to lean in.

*"That,"* the voice said, and something moved behind the two-inch gap, something vast turning its attention like a lighthouse, *"is a very good question. And you are the first soul in a hundred and twenty-five years to ask it. The ones who dug me up — they did not ask. They were sent. Sent by a man with a signature, a man whose name I know the way I know all names, a man who is, at this moment, asleep in a warm bed in a town that does not know what he has purchased for it."*

The frost on the ground around Juniper's feet bloomed suddenly, violently, into a single shape: not a fern, not a feather. A signature. Long, looping, legible.

Juniper read it. The cold read it to them, letter by letter, patient as a teacher.

And behind them, up at the trailer, they heard Dale Okafor say, in a voice gone strange: *"Juniper. Juniper, I know that name. My grandmother used to say it — she said it like a curse —"*
