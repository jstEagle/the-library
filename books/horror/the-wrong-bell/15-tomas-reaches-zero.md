## 15. Tomás Reaches Zero

Dale Okafor had spent twenty-two years in construction learning exactly one lesson that mattered: you can't stop a thing from happening by standing in front of it and wishing hard. Concrete cures when it cures. Steel cools when it cools. And men die when they die, no matter how good your hands are at holding them.

He was holding Tomás's hand now, and it was cold — not dead-cold, not yet, but cold the way the trailer's windows were cold, cold like something had gotten under the skin of the world here and was breathing out through it.

"Hey, hermano," Dale said. "You still with me?"

Tomás smiled. That was the worst part. He'd been smiling for two hours, ever since Juniper had stopped trying to keep him awake and started doing whatever it was she did instead — the blanket tucked just so, the damp cloth on his forehead, the low murmured narration of everything happening in the room as if Tomás were a guest at a party rather than a man leaving one. *Ruth found another ledger page. Ephraim ate half a sandwich, which is more than any of us have managed. You're doing fine, Tomás. Everybody here is doing fine.*

"Sixty-one," Tomás said pleasantly. "Sixty. Fifty-nine."

"Yeah," Dale said. "I hear you."

The counting had slowed over the night. Back at midnight Tomás had been tearing through numbers like a man running downhill, thirty seconds to the dozen, and Dale had thought — had *hoped*, God help him — that maybe he'd run out of numbers before he ran out of time. But somewhere around forty he'd eased off, the way a tired swimmer rolls onto his back, and now he counted the way other people breathe. Unhurried. Unbothered. Like he had all the time in the world, which, Dale supposed bitterly, he did. Exactly all of it, and not one second more.

Juniper sat on the other side of the cot, and every so often she did something small and precise — turned his wrist, adjusted the pillow, touched two fingers to his throat — and each time she nodded to herself like a pilot confirming instruments. Dale had watched her do this for hours and finally understood: she wasn't checking whether Tomás was dying. She was checking how comfortably he was dying. As if comfort were a thing you could measure. As if it were a thing you could *give*.

"How long?" he asked her, quietly.

She didn't pretend not to understand. "Dawn's around five-forty. He'll go before that." A pause. "Maybe an hour. Maybe less."

"And there's nothing —"

"There's nothing wrong with him," Juniper said, and her voice had that terrible gentleness in it, the kind Dale had only ever heard from nurses and from people delivering news they've delivered too many times. "That's what I keep coming back to. His heart is slow but steady. His breathing is easy. If I didn't know better, I'd say he was sleeping better than he has in years." She looked down at Tomás, who was smiling at the ceiling and whispering fifty-four, fifty-three, like a man reading a book he already loved. "Whatever's taking him isn't hurting him. It's the gentlest thing I've ever seen, and I hate it."

"Why do you hate it?"

"Because gentle is how it gets you to open the door."

Dale had no answer for that. Outside the trailer window, the frost had written its way across the glass again — those branching fern-patterns, except up close, if you let yourself look, which he tried not to, the branches resolved into letters. Names. Their names, mostly, spelled almost right. *Dale Okfor.* *Ruth Ibarra.* *Ephrim.* Like invitations addressed by someone who'd heard about them secondhand. He'd covered the window with a moving blanket an hour ago. It hadn't helped; the frost came through anyway, blooming on the inside of the glass now, patient as ivy.

"Fifty," Tomás said. "Forty-nine."

* * *

They'd dragged Tomás back from the door at nine o'clock the previous night — four of them, Dale on one arm, young Petey Vasquez on the other, Ruth shouting instructions she could see forming on nobody's lips because everyone was screaming. Tomás had walked toward the excavation trench smiling like a man going out to meet a bus, counting backwards from a hundred, and when they grabbed him he hadn't fought. He'd just said, kindly, "You're going to make me miss it," and something in the kindness had been worse than a fight would have been.

After that they'd zip-tied the site gate, posted Petey at the trench with a flashlight and strict orders, and brought Tomás into the site trailer, where Juniper took one look at him and went into a mode Dale had never seen on a person before. Calm. Not the frozen calm of shock — the practiced calm of someone who has done this exact thing hundreds of times and refuses to be bad at it now.

"He's transitioning," she'd said, which was hospice language, and Dale had wanted to scream *transitioning to WHAT* but hadn't, because Juniper's hands were steady and his weren't, and somebody in this trailer had to be.

Through the small hours the voices had come. That was the part Dale would never tell anyone who hadn't been there. Tomás, drifting between counts, began speaking in other people's voices — and not strangers' voices. Dale knew them. Everyone in Hollowmere knew them.

First it was old Marisol Quintero, dead six years, saying through Tomás's mouth, "Tell my grandson the tomatoes need water," in perfect Marisol cadence, complete with the little laugh she put on the end of complaints. Then it was Hank Pruitt, dead eleven years, younger-sounding than Dale remembered, grumbling about a fence line. Then a woman's voice Dale didn't know at all, and then a child's voice, bright and quick, asking if the ice on the pond would hold, and each voice was younger than the last, like the guest behind the door was playing its collection backward, working its way down through a century of the town's dead toward whatever was smallest and newest.

Ephraim had listened to all of it sitting cross-legged on the floor, chin on his fists, and at the child's voice he'd said, matter-of-fact, "That's Abigail. She's nice. She doesn't remember her last number."

"What happens when you reach your last number?" Dale had asked him, because apparently he was the kind of man now who asked ten-year-olds metaphysical questions at three in the morning.

Ephraim had considered this with the seriousness of a boy discussing weather. "You get to stop being cold," he said. "That's all it wants, really. For everybody to stop being cold. It just forgets that stopping isn't the same as resting." Then he'd yawned enormously, the way children do, as if cosmic horror were mildly boring, and fallen asleep against Ruth's shoulder.

Now, at dawn-minus-three-hours, Tomás reached forty.

* * *

Juniper had explained it once, quietly, while Tomás slept between numbers. "In the ward we call it terminal lucidity when it goes the other direction — when dying people suddenly clear up, say goodbye, mean things. This is like that, but stretched out. He's not confused. He's not suffering. He knows exactly what's happening, and he's not afraid, and I want you to understand that I have watched four hundred people die and maybe six of them went like this. The rest fought. He's not fighting." She'd rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "And I don't know if that's mercy or anesthesia or the cruelest trick there is, and I hate not knowing."

"I want to fight for him," Dale had said.

"I know."

"That's what you do, right? You fight. That's the whole job."

"No," Juniper said. "That's what people think the job is. The job is knowing which fights are yours. Sometimes the whole job is making sure nobody dies alone and scared, and letting go of the idea that you get to decide the terms." She'd looked at him then, really looked, the way she looked at patients. "You broke the seal, Dale. You've been fighting since the minute you did. Nobody's taking that away from you. But tonight, for him, the fight is this: he doesn't leave this world thinking anybody here was ashamed of him. Can you do that part?"

Dale had thought about Tomás's wife, Carmen, three towns over, who thought her husband was on a routine job. About the phone call he'd have to make. About the fact that Tomás had walked toward that door smiling because some part of him, deep down, in a place none of them could reach, had been *invited* — and being invited, after a lifetime of showing up to jobs where nobody knew his name, must have felt like sunlight.

"Yeah," Dale had said. "I can do that part."

So that's what they did. It was the strangest shift Dale had ever worked. They sat with a dying man and made it a good shift. Ruth read aloud from the 1899 ledger in her flat archivist's voice, not the horror parts, the human parts — the Bellamy family's Christmas expenses, a recipe for plum wine penciled in a margin, a complaint about a church bell that "rings false and always has." Tomás laughed at that one, actually laughed, a real laugh from somewhere below the counting. Juniper kept up her soft narration. Dale told work stories — the time Tomás poured a footing in the rain and dared the inspector to find a flaw in it (the inspector couldn't), the time he'd eaten Petey's lunch by accident for a week straight before anyone told him, the time he'd fixed the mayor's furnace on a Sunday and refused payment and demanded pie instead, and gotten it, and eaten the whole pie in the truck.

"You ate the whole pie?" Tomás asked, in his own voice again, faintly scandalized.

"Every slice. Drove home sick as a dog. Worth it."

"Worth it," Tomás agreed, and smiled, and said, "Thirty-eight. Thirty-seven," and closed his eyes.

* * *

It happened at dawn-minus-three-hours exactly, though none of them knew the hour until afterward, when Ruth checked her watch and went very still.

There was no drama. That was the thing Dale would carry, the thing that lodged in him like a splinter. No rattle, no struggle, none of the awful machinery of a bad death that Juniper had described to him once, unwisely, over coffee, back when coffee and normalcy were things. Tomás simply got quieter between numbers. The gaps lengthened. Thirty-one. A pause, long enough that Dale leaned forward. Twenty-nine. Another pause. And then, soft as a man agreeing with someone across a table:

"Twenty-eight."

And nothing after it.

His face didn't change. He was still smiling. Juniper did her quiet instrument-check — wrist, throat, the small nods — and then she straightened up, and pulled the blanket up over his shoulders the way you'd tuck in a sleeping child, and said, "There he goes. Safe travels, Tomás," in a voice that was warm and completely wrecked at the same time, and Dale understood that she meant it, that this was the prayer she had, the only one, and she'd been saying it for fifteen years to people who counted.

And then the frost retreated.

Dale saw it happen. He was facing the window — he'd been facing away from it all night, but grief turns you around sometimes, makes you look at the thing you've been avoiding — and the branching white letters on the glass simply stopped growing. Held. Then, slowly, like breath fading off a mirror, they began to shrink, the fern-patterns pulling back from the center of the pane toward the edges, the names dissolving letter by letter until only a thin lace of frost remained at the corners, and the trailer — God help him, the trailer got *warmer*. Two degrees, maybe three. Enough that Juniper noticed, and looked at the thermometer, and looked at the window, and said, very quietly, "Did anyone else feel that?"

"It let him go gently," Ephraim said. He was awake; Dale didn't know when he'd woken. The boy stood at the window in his too-big coat, watching the frost recede, and his expression wasn't frightened. It was sad, and certain, and older than ten. "When you don't fight it, it takes what it's given and it's satisfied for a while. That's why the town lasted so long. Everybody always gave it somebody eventually, and it always took them nicely, and everybody pretended it was just sickness or luck." He turned around. His eyes were wet, but his voice stayed level. "It liked Tomás. Tomás wasn't scared of it even a little bit. I think it's going to remember that."

Dale sat down on the cot beside his friend's body and put his hand back where it had been, on Tomás's cold wrist, and the arithmetic hit him all at once, the way grief does — not gradually but all at once, like a load shifting on a truck bed.

One frost-retreat per death. One easing of the siege, purchased at exactly the price of a man. And the guest had taken Tomás *gently* — had taken him the way Juniper took a pulse, carefully, professionally, without malice — and Dale could not decide, and suspected he would never decide, whether that made it better or infinitely worse. Because if it could be gentle, then it chose when to be cruel. And if it chose, then the founders' bargain wasn't a haunting at all. It was a feeding schedule.

"We could've had him longer," he said, and heard his own voice crack on it. "If we'd never dug. If I'd never —"

"Stop." Juniper's voice, sharp as a trowel edge. She knelt in front of him so he had to look at her. "You want to do arithmetic? Fine. Here's mine. Fifteen years I've watched people count backwards and die at zero, and every single one of them died alone in a ward full of strangers, because nobody — not their families, not their doctors, not me — nobody would sit with the truth of it. Tomás is the first one in fifteen years who got held while it happened. Who got stories and laughter and somebody telling him about the damn pie. Whatever took him found a man who was *loved out loud*, and it had to be gentle, because there was nothing else in the room to take." Her voice shook and she let it. "That's not nothing, Dale. That's the whole game. That's the only move that's ever worked."

Dale looked past her at the window, where the last lace of frost hung at the corners like lace curtains in a house where somebody was finally resting easy. At Ephraim, watching him with those old eyes. At Ruth, who had opened her notebook and was writing, because that was how she prayed too.

"One frost-retreat per death," he said hoarsely. "It proved it. It showed us the exchange rate."

"Yes," Ruth said quietly, not looking up from her notebook. "Which means it can be negotiated with. Which means—" She stopped. Dale watched her pen hover over the page, watched her lips move silently through some line of the ledger she'd memorized, watched her go pale in the specific way of a woman who has just found the loophole in a hundred-and-twenty-five-year-old contract.

"Ruth?" Juniper said. "What is it?"

Ruth looked up, and there was something in her face Dale had never seen there before — not fear, not scholarship. Hope. The dangerous kind.

"The covenant," she said. "I need to read it aloud. All of it. Right now, while the frost is still thin." She was already gathering pages. "Because if giving it one name freely taken buys us three degrees and an hour of warmth — then I think I've just figured out what buying it a name *freely given* might buy us instead."

Outside, far off toward the highway, headlights swept the fence line. A car, coming fast, at an hour when no honest car comes.

Dale looked at Tomás's peaceful face, then at the lights, and felt the night tilt toward whatever came next.

"Somebody's here," Ephraim said. "And it's not the guest."
