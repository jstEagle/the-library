## 19. Terms Offered Freely

The frost on the trailer window had written JUNIPER VANE in letters two inches tall, and it had spelled it wrong. *Junepir.* Like an envelope addressed by someone who'd only ever heard the name spoken through a wall.

"That's rude," Ephraim said, studying it with his chin on his fists. "If you're going to invite somebody to something, you should at least spell their name right."

"Maybe that's the point," Ruth said. She was sitting with the ledger open on her knees, her notebook beside her, pen moving in quick shorthand she alone could read. "Get the name wrong and you never have to honor what the name means. It's a technicality dressed up as an invitation."

Dale stood at the door of the trailer with his arms crossed, watching the site through the glass. The frost had stopped advancing an hour ago — or rather, it had stopped *retreating*, which after Tomás was its own kind of terrible. It sat on every surface like a held breath. The door down in the trench still stood open those two inches, pouring cold into the July night, and nobody had gone near it since midnight.

Juniper looked at the misspelled frost and felt something settle in their chest. Not calm. Juniper had been a hospice nurse for fifteen years and knew better than to call this calm. It was the thing that came *before* calm, the moment when you stopped checking the chart because you already knew what it said, and the only question left was how gently you were going to do your work.

They thought about Mr. Aldous. They thought about fifteen years of counting backwards and telling themselves it was coincidence, and they thought about how the truth had been sitting there the whole time, patient as a guest, waiting to be offered a chair.

"I'm going to open it," Juniper said.

* * *

The trailer went very quiet. Even the wind outside seemed to lean closer to listen.

"No," Dale said immediately. "Absolutely not. That thing took Tomás."

"It didn't take Tomás," Juniper said. "Tomás walked to it. And when we let him stop fighting it — when I treated him like a dying man instead of a hostage — he went peacefully. At zero. You were there. You saw his face." Their voice caught, just slightly, and they let it. "He wasn't afraid at the end. He was *relieved*. Fifteen years I've watched people die scared, Dale. I know the difference between being taken and being allowed to leave."

Ruth's pen had stopped. She was looking at Juniper with the particular intensity she brought to documents — the look of someone reading a difficult clause. "You're not going down there to fight it," she said slowly. "You're going down there to *renegotiate*."

"The covenant binds the named and their heirs," Juniper said. "That's what you found. But nothing stops a new covenant. Nothing stops someone offering terms freely, without fear." They almost laughed. "I've been afraid my whole life. Of this, of everything, of getting close to anybody in case they counted backwards from one hundred in front of me. I'm done letting fear write the terms. Fear writes terrible contracts. Believe me, I've signed a few."

Ephraim had gone still in a way that made Juniper's chest ache. "It's lonely," the boy said. "I told you. It's been down there so long, and everybody who ever came to the door came to barter with it, or seal it up, or feed it somebody else. Nobody ever just — knocked."

"Then somebody's overdue," Juniper said.

"You don't even know if it will accept," Dale said. "You could walk down there and it could take you like it started to take Tomás."

"It might," Juniper agreed. "That's what makes it an offer instead of a trick. An offer you can't refuse isn't hospitality. It's just a fancier kind of harvest." They pulled on their coat — absurd, in July, but the trench breathed winter — and checked their pockets out of pure habit: pen, notepad, the butterscotch candies they kept for families in the waiting room. Then they stopped, and took out one candy, and held it up where Ephraim could see.

"For after," they said. "If this works, you and I are splitting it on the drive home. That's a promise, and unlike some parties at this excavation, I keep my promises."

Ephraim's face did something complicated — hope and terror wrestling for the same handful of real estate — and then he nodded, once, hard, the way children nod when they've decided to be brave about something no child should have to be brave about.

Ruth stood. "Witnesses," she said. "A covenant needs witnesses. Dale, Ephraim, me. We come as far as the lip of the trench. We see and hear everything. If you're offering terms freely, then by God they'll be *documented* terms." She tucked the ledger under her arm like a shield, which, Juniper reflected, in her hands it genuinely was.

Dale looked at each of them in turn, and Juniper watched a foreman's arithmetic run behind his eyes — three hours to dawn, four souls, one door — and then he grabbed the heavy flashlight off the hook by the door.

"Fine," he said roughly. "But I'm walking you down. And if that thing so much as raises the temperature—" he paused, recalibrated, "—lowers the temperature another degree, I'm carrying you out of there over my shoulder, covenant or no covenant."

"Duly noted," Juniper said, and found, to their surprise, that they were smiling.

* * *

The trench at night was a different country. Frost furred the shoring timbers in white lace; their breath hung around them like small ghosts following politely along. The floodlights Dale's crew had rigged threw long shadows, and at the far end of the cut, the door waited.

Up close, Juniper could finally study it properly, and what struck them — what nearly undid them before they'd said a single word — was that it looked like a *front door*. Not a vault door, not a seal, not a prison gate. A door with a frame painted long ago in a color that might once have been blue. A door someone had meant guests to knock on.

Hinges on the inside. Sealed from within. For a hundred and twenty-five years, something had held this door shut against itself, because it had learned that whatever came through it came to take, and it would rather starve than be a thief again.

"Oh," Juniper said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

The gap breathed cold against their face. And behind the cold, faint as heat-shimmer in reverse, Juniper felt it noticing them — a vast attention turning, slow as a lighthouse beam, wary as a dog that has been kicked at every door it ever scratched.

"Don't be afraid," Juniper said, and heard how strange it sounded, saying it *to the monster*. "That's usually my line."

Behind them, up at the lip of the trench, Ruth's pen scratched. Dale's flashlight beam held steady. Ephraim's small hand gripped the ladder rail.

Juniper put their hand flat against the door. The wood was cold enough to burn, and they kept it there anyway, the way they'd held the hands of a hundred dying strangers: firmly, warmly, without flinching.

"My name is Juniper Vane," they said clearly. "Not Junepir. Not any spelling somebody else decided on. Juniper Vane, and I'm here of my own free will, without fear — well." A breath. "With fear. But I'm not *acting* from it, and I'm told that counts."

The cold pressed back against their palm, listening.

"In 1899, forty-one people made you a promise they had no right to make. They promised you other people's names — their children's names, and their children's children's names — names that were never theirs to give. That wasn't a bargain. That was theft with paperwork. And you've been holding the bag for a hundred and twenty-five years, starving politely at a door nobody opened except to shove somebody else through it."

The frost on the shoring timbers creaked. Juniper kept talking, voice level, the voice they used at 3 a.m. when the morphine clock said it was time and the family couldn't yet say goodbye.

"So here are my terms. New covenant, superseding the old, offered freely and witnessed." They glanced up at the trench lip, at Ruth's raised pen, at Dale's steady light, at Ephraim's wide eyes. "Witnessed by Dale Okafor, Ruth Ibarra, and Ephraim — whose name the old ledger lists among the forty-one, and whom I am telling you right now is *not yours*, was never fairly given, and goes free under these terms, along with every other name in that book."

The cold went utterly still. The whole site seemed to hold its breath — frost, wind, floodlight hum, all of it suspended like the pause after a bell is struck.

"Term one," Juniper said. "A name given, not taken. Mine. Freely. Not harvested, not owed, not crossed out of any ledger — *given*, the way you give a gift, which means you can't lose it and I can't be billed for it later. Juniper Vane. Yours, if you want it, as a welcome."

Their hand was shaking now. They let it shake. Courage, they'd learned over fifteen years of deathbeds, was never the absence of trembling; it was doing the thing with your whole body visibly terrified and doing it anyway, because someone needed it done.

"Term two. Welcome. Not a sealed door, not a starved guest. When you cross — if you choose to cross, and only if you choose — there'll be warmth waiting. Real warmth. Coffee too hot and blankets too many and people who ask how you're doing and wait for the answer. Term three: remembrance. Your story gets told. Ruth's going to publish that ledger with the true account beside it — what was done to you, and what you refused to become all these years, holding that door shut against yourself. No more being the thing in the dark under Hollowmere. You get to be a *person* in the record. That matters. I've sat with enough dying people to know the two things everyone wants at the end are company and to have mattered. You've been denied both for a century. I'm offering both now."

They paused. The cold hadn't moved. Hadn't accepted, hadn't refused. Just — listened, the way the best listeners do, the ones who make you feel like the only person in the world.

"And term four," Juniper said, softer now. "No more counting. Not as a summons, not as a debt-collector's knock. If people count backwards when they die, let it be what I always half-hoped it was — a mercy. A way to leave gently. You take what's freely given and nothing else. Ever. Those are the terms. Take them or leave them. Either way, the door closes tonight on the old bargain, because I am standing here telling you, witness it, Ruth — *the old covenant is refused*. Refused by a free party, without fear, on behalf of everyone it was ever used against."

Silence. Long enough that Dale's flashlight beam trembled slightly, though his hands, Juniper noticed, did not.

Then the cold changed.

It was hard to describe, even for someone who'd spent fifteen years cataloguing the unspeakable in a logbook. The pressure behind the door didn't lessen so much as *turn* — like a great animal shifting its weight, like grief finally setting down a load it had forgotten it could put down. The frost on the timbers bloomed once, wildly, a last burst of branching white — and then, instead of writing a name, it wrote something else.

Two words, in careful, enormous letters, spelled perfectly:

*THANK YOU.*

And beneath them, smaller, almost shy:

*JUNIPER.*

Spelled right.

Juniper laughed — a wet, cracked, entirely undignified laugh — and pressed their forehead briefly against the freezing wood. "Yeah," they said. "That's me. See? Was that so hard?"

The door moved. Not wider — Juniper braced for that, for the flood of cold, for the harvest — but narrower. An inch closed. Then another. As though something on the other side were gathering itself, considering, deciding whether to accept an offer no one had made it in a hundred and twenty-five years.

At the lip of the trench, Ephraim called down, his voice high and clear across the frozen air:

"Tell it it can come to supper!"

Juniper turned, and the boy was leaning over the edge with both hands, lit from above by the floodlights, absolutely certain in the way only children and saints are certain.

"You hear that?" Juniper said to the door. "Standing invitation. Supper. There'll be butterscotch."

The last inch of the gap paused — and held, neither open nor shut, like a breath drawn and not yet released, while somewhere above them the first pale suggestion of dawn touched the frost on the shoring timbers and turned it, for one impossible moment, gold.

Then the cold spoke — not in words, not in voices, but in a single vast exhale that Juniper felt in their sternum, and understood in their bones as the oldest word in any language:

*Yes.*
