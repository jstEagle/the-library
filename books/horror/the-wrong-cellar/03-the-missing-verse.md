## 3. The Missing Verse

The rain came in off the moor that third night like something with an errand, and Quentin Crane sat in his room above the inn's taproom with the candle turned low and the tape recorder humming its small faithful hum, and he did not yet know that he had found the wound.

He had been cataloguing for two evenings by then. It was the work he loved best in all the world—the work he would have done for free if the Leverhulme trustees hadn't insisted on paying him—laying the cassettes out in their numbered rows like specimens pinned to a board, threading each one through the machine, listening with his eyes closed and his pencil hovering over the stave-paper. Hollowmere had been generous beyond his hopes. Four singers of the old repertoire still lived: Edith Garrick up past the churchyard, the Tredwell brothers who farmed the low field, and Maren Dole herself, who sang only when she thought no one was listening, which was how Quentin had gotten most of what he had from her—a harvest song caught through the floorboards at midnight, sung so quietly it was nearly a prayer.

He played Maren's version first that night because it was his favorite. Her voice came up through the tape hiss thin and clear as water over stones:

*The reaper walks the furrow's end,*
*the gleaning-wife walks after,*
*and what they leave between them there*
*is sorrow, salt, and laughter—*

Then the gap.

It was not silence, exactly. Silence has texture; it breathes. This was an absence with edges. Maren's voice simply stopped, held itself suspended for the length of four bars—Quentin timed it, four bars exactly, every time—and resumed on the next line as though nothing were missing, as though the song had stepped around a hole in the floor without looking down.

*And sorrow goes into the ground,*
*and salt goes to the sea,*
*and laughter stays above the earth*
*for those who cannot see.*

He rewound. Played it again. The gap again, four bars, her voice lifting slightly at the end of the last line before it, leaning forward into the space where the verse should have been, and then catching itself, drawing back, going on. He had heard this before, he realized—in other villages, other counties. A singer forgets a verse and papers over the loss. It happened. Songs eroded. That was folklore; that was the whole sad beautiful truth of it, songs wearing away like coastlines.

But then he pulled the Tredwell brothers' recording, made two nights ago in their kitchen with the smell of barley in the room, and found the same song among their repertoire—the harvest song, the one Maren sang, though theirs was rougher, harmonized in the loose unison of men who'd learned it working. And when they reached the place—

The gap. Four bars. Both brothers stopped together, precisely together, as if a hand had been laid flat across both their mouths.

Quentin sat very still. His candle guttered in a draft from under the door and righted itself.

Singers forget independently. That was axiomatic. Two singers in the same village might share a lacuna if they'd learned from the same teacher—but four? He threaded Edith Garrick's tape next, the long waulking song she'd given him in her kitchen with the cat watching from the dresser, and listened to it twice through, following the words on his transcription. There. Not the harvest song this time but the mill-song, a different melody entirely, different century almost—and at the corresponding structural point, where the penultimate stanza should have turned, the old woman's voice dropped away for four bars and returned.

Four bars. Every time. In every song. The same duration, the same placement—not random erosion but surgery. As if someone had gone through the whole repertoire of Hollowmere with a knife and cut out the same organ from every body, and the songs had healed around the wound, scarred over, learned to live without it.

*Like a tooth knocked out,* he wrote in his notebook, and immediately disliked the phrase, because it suggested pain, and extraction, and a mouth.

He worked until the candle was a puddle. He laid the tapes side by side and made a chart, singer against song against timecode, and the chart looked like something from a medical text: a column of identical absences. Eleven recordings. Eleven gaps. And here was the thing that took the scholarly hunger in his chest and began, slowly, patiently, to turn it over into something else: the gaps were not empty. When he amplified them—cranking the gain until the tape hiss became a roar—he could hear, beneath the absence, underneath Maren's suspended voice, a sound like breath drawn in. Held. Released. Every singer, in every gap, drew the same breath. As if the pause were not missing at all but *occupied*, and the singers were stepping around whatever stood in it.

He slept badly, the tapes boxed and stacked against the wall like a little archive of amputations, and dreamed of a mouth full of holes where teeth should have been, and woke with the taste of well water.

* * *

Edith Garrick received him the next morning in a kitchen that smelled of peat and boiled milk, and she knew why he'd come before he'd said six words.

"You've found it," she said. She was small and dry as a wren, hands knotted on the table before her like roots above ground, eyes pale and quick. "I saw you walking up the lane yesterday with your ears on"—she meant the headphones, slung round his neck—"and I said to myself, there's one that's found it."

"Found what, Mrs.—"

"No." The word came out flat and fast, a door closing. "No names. You'll not write my name. You'll not say my name back to me in that voice you've got, the polite voice. If we talk, we talk with the name left out, like the verse." She watched him with an expression he could not read—defiance braided with something that looked terribly like pity. "You want to know about the gap. So do it the way we do. Leave it out and go round."

Quentin set his notebook down carefully. "If that's your condition, I'll honor it. But surely a name harms no one. I'm a folklorist, not a journalist. My interest is the songs."

"Aye," she said. "That's what the last one said."

The kettle ticked on the hob. Outside, rain moved across the low fields in grey sheets, and somewhere a dog gave up barking.

"The last one?" he asked.

"Before your time. Before mine, near enough. A collector man, from London way. Came asking after songs, wanting everything twice—" her mouth bent around the word *twice* like a plum stone "—and he got what he asked for, more or less, and went home on the mail coach, and what he wrote is in some library now, I shouldn't wonder, with names in it." She lifted her chin at his notebook. "Names are debts, young man. You write a thing down, you've taken it. Taken it out of the air, where it can change and be forgiven, and put it in the world solid, where it can be *owed*. So. No name. Say yes or go."

He should have gone. Some part of him, the part that had counted doors last night out of habit he'd told himself was superstition, the part that lit his lamp before dusk without deciding to—that part whispered *go*. But the scholar in him was leaning forward into the gap the way Maren's voice leaned, hungry for the missing bar, and hunger won.

"Yes," he said. "No names."

She nodded once, satisfied, and poured the tea, and for a while they drank in silence while the rain worked at the window.

"It was paid away," she said at last, as if continuing a conversation begun generations ago. "Before I was born. Before my mother was born, likely. The verse—you'll not find it written anywhere, you'll not find it remembered, because it wasn't forgot. Forgetting happens. This was *paid*."

"Paid to whom?"

Her hands tightened on each other. "You're a questions man. I heard that about you too." She looked out at the rain. "There's things too heavy to sing twice, Mr. Crane. Grief, mostly. Guilt. The dead that died wrong. A village carries them the way a mule carries load, year on year, and the load doesn't lighten because you get used to the straps. Well. Long ago—longer than the church, longer than the well-house—Hollowmere found a way to set the load down. To give it away. All the verses that hurt to sing. All the names that hurt to say. Given over, paid out, and the singing's been lighter ever since." Her pale eyes came back to him. "Lighter. Not empty. There's a difference, and you're hearing it, aren't you? Four bars in every song. That's where the weight used to sit. That's the shape of what we gave away."

Quentin's pencil had not moved. He noticed distantly that his hand didn't seem to want to.

"Given to whom?" he asked again, softer.

"To what lives under," Edith said, "and keeps what it's given." Then, quickly, before the words finished landing: "That's all you'll get today. That's more than I meant to give. You'll go home now."

"I don't understand what 'paid' means. Paid how? Paid once, or—"

"*Go home.*" Not angry. Frightened—for him, he realized, with a small cold shock. She was frightened on his behalf, the way you're frightened for a child playing near water. "You keep asking twice, Mr. Crane. You keep asking twice, and one day something will answer twice, and then you'll know what the verse cost, and you'll wish you'd never learned to count bars."

He gathered his tapes. At the door he paused, and against every instinct of courtesy and self-preservation he'd honed in forty years of village work, he asked it once more, twice, the question hanging in the peat-smelling air between them:

"What was the verse, Edith?"

She stood very straight by her cold hearth, small as a wren, and looked at him with an expression he would carry up out of the village with him years later, when most of what else had happened had worn smooth: an expression of terrible, exhausted tenderness.

"That's just it," she said. "Neither do we."

* * *

He walked back down through the rain with his collar up and his mind running hot despite the cold, turning it over, arranging it, telling himself the old woman was addled, that *paid away* was folk-metaphor, that every tradition has its taboo verses and its bogey-explanations. That was the work. That was the discipline: hold the material lightly, let it mean what it means, don't catch fire from it.

But at the bend in the lane where the hedge broke and the flooded quarry showed below, grey-green and still as a pupil, he stopped without meaning to. The water lay flat under the rain, each drop stitching rings that opened and closed, opened and closed, like mouths. Like the gaps in the songs. Four bars wide.

And standing at the quarry's edge, small in the wet distance, was Tam—Maren's boy, seven years old, the one who watched him work and hummed tunes no adult would acknowledge. The child wasn't fishing. Wasn't playing. He stood facing the water with his hands at his sides, and even across the distance Quentin could hear it: the humming. A tune he almost knew. A tune shaped exactly like the harvest song, moving toward the exact place where the verse should be—

—and Tam hummed straight through the gap. All four bars. Note-perfect, unhurried, filling the absence his mother's voice always stepped around, and then stopping, cleanly, at the end, as if a page had been turned.

The rain fell. The quarry breathed its rings. The boy stood at the edge of the black water, humming the verse that had been paid away before anyone living was born, and Quentin Crane, folklorist, holder of a grant and a deadline and eleven tapes full of surgical wounds, understood with a scholar's certainty and a man's rising dread that he was not looking at a child who had learned a forbidden song.

He was looking at a child through whom something was remembering it.
