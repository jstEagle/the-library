## 4. Crews and Contracts

The lorries came up the valley road on a Tuesday morning, three of them, white with government livery, their engines growling against the quiet of Hollowmere like strangers raising their voices in a church. Quentin heard them from his window at the Dole house, where he had been cataloguing the previous night's recordings by candlelight—candlelight because Maren had asked him, gently but without room for argument, not to run the lamp after dark. *It draws attention,* she'd said. He hadn't thought to ask what sort.

He watched from behind the lace curtain as the convoy ground to a halt at the quarry road's mouth. Men in orange vests spilled out, unfolding like a mechanism, setting cones, unspooling cable. At the center of it all stood a woman with a clipboard she barely consulted, dark hair tied back with a violence that suggested impatience with her own hair rather than the world, calling measurements to two men who scrambled to keep pace.

Quentin dressed quickly. A folklorist lived on arrivals; new people in a closed place were a key turned in an old lock. By the time he reached the quarry road, half of Hollowmere had already gathered—not crowding, nothing so honest as crowding, but standing along the walls and gateposts in that loose, watching way he was beginning to recognize as the village's native posture toward anything unfamiliar.

"Excuse me—" He caught the woman's elbow as she strode past. "You'll be the survey team? For the quarry?"

She looked at him the way engineers look at weather: assessing whether he mattered. "Priya Nandra. Hydrological survey, Ministry of Works." She offered a hand briefly, dry and firm. "And you are?"

"Quentin Crane. Folklorist. I'm—staying at the inn."

"A folklorist." Something flickered across her face, too quick to name. "Well. Everyone surveys something, I suppose." She turned back to her crew. "Okafor! I want the bore line staked before noon. We start at the north rim."

Quentin lingered, watching the villagers watch. Old Garrick wasn't among them—he noticed that—but Edith stood at the far edge of the little crowd, wrapped in her shawl despite the mildness of the morning, and her face wore an expression Quentin had last seen on Maren's when he'd asked her to sing the harvest song twice: not anger, not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone watching a familiar story begin again from its first page.

* * *

Priya Nandra had surveyed eleven sites that year and Hollowmere was, on paper, the simplest: a flooded quarry, disused since before the war, water table rising, downstream seepage threatening two farmsteads. Drill cores, sonar mapping, a report, a recommendation. Six weeks, maybe eight.

By the second day she knew the paper was lying to her.

The problem was the village. Not the noise—she'd expected complaints about the compressors, the generator's drone through the narrow lanes, and none came. The villagers didn't object to the noise at all. They objected to the depth.

It began small. An old man appeared at the site fence the first afternoon and stood there while her crew sank the first test bore, and when Priya called out the reading—*four meters*—the old man flinched. Actually flinched, a full-body twitch, as though she'd named a dead relative. He left without a word.

On the third day it was a woman with flour on her apron, standing at the fence through the whole shift, and when Okafor shouted *five meters, ma'am*, the woman crossed herself—the first time Priya had seen any gesture of faith in the entire village—and walked away fast, not looking back.

"They think we're digging a grave," Halloran said, laughing, that evening over the camp stove. Halloran was her best man, steady, a widower with a daughter's photograph taped inside his equipment case. "Or they've got bodies down there. That's my money. Bodies."

"Bodies would be our problem," Priya said. "They're not treating us like a problem. They're treating us like—" She searched for it. "Like a countdown."

She wrote it off. Villages were strange; villages guarded their dead and their superstitions with equal ferocity; she had six weeks of work and a ministry deadline. But that night, reviewing the day's logs by lamplight in the site trailer, she found herself reading the depth figures again and again. Four meters. Five meters. And tomorrow—

Tomorrow they'd go deeper than anyone in this village seemed willing to name aloud.

* * *

Quentin found Maren in the inn kitchen that evening, kneading bread with a force that had nothing to do with bread. Her knuckles were white. Flour dusted her forearms to the elbow like something fallen rather than applied.

"The survey crew," he said, leaning in the doorway. "They're making folk nervous."

Maren didn't look up. "They're making *you* nervous, is what you mean, and you want me to say why so you can write it in your book."

"I'd settle for you saying why so I can sleep."

The dough slapped the board. Once, twice. Then she stopped, hands buried to the wrists in it, and stared at the window where the last light was going copper over the hills.

"My grandmother told me there was a survey once. Before the war. They wanted to drain the quarry, put in a pumping station." Her voice had gone flat, recitative, the voice of a thing learned by rote. "They got to a certain depth and the whole village turned out. Every soul. Stood at the rim and wouldn't let them go further. The engineer called it mob rule. Called the constable. There wasn't a constable in Hollowmere by then—there hasn't been one in fifty years—and the crew packed up and left, and nobody ever said why. My grandmother said the word *six* was never spoken in this house for a generation after."

"That's remarkable," Quentin said, and meant it as scholarship, and saw immediately that it landed as something else entirely.

"It's not remarkable," Maren said quietly. "It's arithmetic. Some numbers you don't count out loud, Mr. Crane. You count doors. You light lamps. You leave certain verses unsung. That's how this village has kept its accounts, and your government friends are about to open the ledger." She went back to the bread, harder now. "Go and eat your supper."

But he didn't go. He stood in the warm yeast-smelling dark of the kitchen and watched her, and thought about the bricked-over cellar mouths under every house in Hollowmere, twelve houses he'd counted, twelve sealed throats, and felt the shape of a question assembling itself that he was not yet brave enough to ask.

* * *

Priya rose early on the fourth day to beat the heat and found a woman waiting at the site fence in the grey half-light. Youngish, tired-eyed, the kind of pretty that exhaustion was slowly erasing. The innkeeper's wife—she'd seen her serving breakfast to the folklorist.

"You shouldn't be here yet," the woman said, without greeting.

"Neither should you."

"I'm Maren Dole." She gripped the chain-link with both hands. "I need to speak to whoever gives the orders. To you, if it's you."

"It's me." Priya came to the fence. Up close the woman smelled of woodsmoke and bread and something underneath both, faintly mineral, like well water. "Mrs. Dole, if you have concerns about contamination or structural risk, I can share the—"

"Stop at six meters."

Priya blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The drilling. The excavation. Whatever you call it." Maren Dole's voice was low and absolutely level, which somehow made it worse than shouting. "Stop at six meters. Don't go below six meters. Please."

Priya glanced back at the trailer, at the bore plan pinned above her desk. The hydrological model required samples at eight, ten, and twelve meters to map the aquifer properly. Anything shallower was a waste of the ministry's money and her season. "Mrs. Dole, I understand there are local sensitivities, but this is a safety survey. The data we gather below six meters is precisely what keeps your farms from flooding."

"You don't understand." For the first time the woman's composure cracked, just at the seam. "There's nothing down there you need. There's nothing down there anyone needs. It's been paid for. It's been *settled*. You dig past six meters and you'll be collecting on a debt that isn't yours, and the interest—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, as though she'd already said too much. As though the words themselves were currency she couldn't afford to spend.

"The interest," Priya repeated.

Maren Dole looked at her then, really looked, and Priya saw that behind the tiredness was terror—not of her, not of the machines, but of something the woman carried around daily like a stone in a pocket. "Please," she said again. "I have a son."

Then she turned and walked back up the lane toward the village, and did not answer when Priya called after her.

* * *

Quentin spent the middle of the day with Edith Garrick, or tried to. The old woman sat in her chair by the window with her hands folded and gave him nothing but courtesies. Yes, she remembered the pre-war survey. No, she wouldn't say more about it. Yes, the tea was fine.

"You watched them arrive," Quentin pressed. "Tuesday. You had a look on your face like—"

"Like I'd seen it before?" Edith smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing in the room. "I have, dear. Not this exact crew. But men come to Hollowmere every generation or so wanting to know what's under things. Under the quarry, under the church records, under the songs." Her eyes slid to his recorder, sitting silent on the table between them. "Wanting to lift the lid and have a look. And Hollowmere lets them get so far, and then it doesn't."

"Why not?"

Edith unfolded her hands and refolded them. "Because some lids are load-bearing."

He waited. She drank her tea. Outside, faintly, he could hear the compressor at the quarry starting up its afternoon drone, and beneath it—or he imagined beneath it—a rhythm, almost musical, that made the old woman's eyes flick toward the window and stay there.

"Your crew chief," Edith said at last. "The Indian woman. Is she a reasonable person?"

"Very. Brisk. Thorough."

"Mm." Edith set down her cup. "Reasonable people are the worst ones, Mr. Crane. Superstitious folk can be warned. Reasonable ones have to be shown, and by then it's generally too late for showing." She looked at him directly. "How deep are they today?"

"Five meters, I believe. They hit dense clay, Priya told me—Nandra, the chief. They expect rock by tomorrow."

Something moved behind Edith's face, deep down, like a fish under ice. "Rock," she repeated softly. "Yes. There's rock."

* * *

That evening Priya stood at the quarry rim and watched the sun go down behind the spoil heaps, and let herself admit, in the privacy of her own skull, that the place unsettled her.

It wasn't the villagers' behavior alone, though that was bad enough. It was the water. Flooded quarries had a character she knew well—green, still, scummed with algae, chemically hostile. This water was different. It was clear. Unnaturally clear, down to a visibility that made no sense for standing quarry water, so that looking into it felt less like looking at a lake and more like looking through a floor. And it was cold. Okafor had lowered a thermometer on a line that afternoon and gotten readings that made him check the instrument twice: four degrees at the surface, in late summer, in direct sun.

"Groundwater feed," she'd said, and Okafor had nodded, and neither of them had believed it, quite.

Now, at dusk, she stood at the rim and listened. The compressor was shut down for the night. The village behind her had gone quiet in that total way villages do, lamps blooming one by one in the windows—more lamps than seemed necessary, she thought, more than a village this size could justify, as if the whole place were lit against something.

From the water below came a sound. Faint. Rhythmic.

She leaned over the edge, telling herself it was wind in the bore casing, water moving against limestone, any of the hundred ordinary sounds a flooded pit makes. It sounded almost like humming. Almost like a tune, very slow, very deep, the kind of melody a child might know and an adult had forgotten.

"Priya!" Halloran's voice from the trailer. "Radio call came in. Ministry wants the full core schedule accelerated. They're asking if we can reach twelve meters by Friday."

She straightened up. The sound from the water had stopped, or she had stopped hearing it, which amounted to the same thing.

"Tell them we'll see," she called back, and did not move from the rim for a long moment, looking down into the clear black water, at the pale suggestion of stone far below—the lintel-shaped shadow her sonar had flagged that afternoon as *anomalous reflector, possible geological formation*, the phrase she had written because the alternative phrases were not available to a reasonable person.

Six meters, the woman had begged her. Stop at six.

Her sonar put the anomalous reflector at five point nine.

* * *

Quentin was awake past midnight, cataloguing tapes, when he heard voices in the lane below his window. Low, urgent, the hushed register of argument conducted where children might hear. He eased the window up an inch.

Maren stood at her garden gate talking to the crew chief—Priya Nandra, jacketless in the cold, clipboard under her arm, clearly having walked up from the site after dark. Their words were mostly lost, but fragments climbed to him on the still air.

"—not asking you to leave. Asking you to stop at—"

"—ministry mandate, Mrs. Dole, I can't simply—"

"—then ask them what happened to the crew before the war. Ask them why the reports stop. Ask them why the pumping station was never built, ask them what the engineer wrote in his final letter, ask them—"

"His final letter?"

A silence. Long enough that Quentin leaned further out, careless of the cold.

"He wrote to his wife," Maren said, very quietly now, and Quentin had to strain. "That he'd found a door. That it was locked from the wrong side. That he was going down in the morning to see who had locked himself in, and whether they needed help."

Another silence, longer.

"There was no morning entry," Maren said. "There was no crew after that. There was a village that stood at the rim until the government went away, and a door that stayed shut, and a number nobody says. Please. Stop at six meters."

Quentin heard Priya Nandra exhale, a long breath, the breath of a scientist standing at the edge of her own discipline. When she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, careful, the voice she probably used for delivering bad news.

"Mrs. Dole. Tomorrow we break through the clay layer. If there's rock at six meters, we'll know by noon." A pause. "If there's something else at six meters—I'll call you myself. Personally. Before anyone else."

"That's not a promise to stop."

"No," Priya said. "It isn't. But it's more than the ministry gave you."

Footsteps moved away down the lane. After a moment, Maren's gate clicked shut, and the latch rattled once, twice, as though her hand wasn't steady enough on the first try.

Quentin sat back from the window in the dark, heart going oddly, and looked at his recorder on the table, its reels still, its microphone capped. He thought about reaching for it. He thought about the fact that he hadn't recorded a single conversation today, and couldn't have said why, except that some part of him—the part that counted doors and lit lamps without knowing he'd learned it here—had understood that tonight was not for keeping.

Below the floorboards, faint as breath, Tam began to hum in his sleep.

Quentin froze. The tune wound up through the joists, patient and sweet and wrong, a harvest song he knew by heart—up to a point. Up to the gap. Up to the missing verse, where every singer in Hollowmere went silent.

Tam hummed straight through it.

And in the morning, Quentin knew, the drills would go down past six meters, and whatever waited at the bottom of that number would finally be asked, after a hundred years of polite silence, to speak.
