## 12. Sunset Arithmetic

Priya Nandra had spent eleven years believing that every problem in the world could be solved with a level, a load calculation, and enough honest arithmetic. She had kept the quarry survey on schedule through two strikes, one flood, and a chief engineer who wept into his coffee when he thought no one was watching. Numbers did not lie to her. Numbers had never once asked her to leave her mother's lullaby out of an official transcript.

She stood now at the long table in the back room of the Dole inn, the site plans spread before her, and she made herself do what she always did: count.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. From the beginning."

Edith Garrick sat at the far end of the table with her hands folded like a woman attending her own funeral. Quentin Crane hovered near the window with his recorder cradled against his chest as if it were an infant he was afraid someone would take from him. Maren Dole had put Tam to bed upstairs an hour ago, and Priya had heard the boy's door lock twice — once from inside, once, apparently, from outside, with a chair wedged under the handle. The innkeeper moved like a woman carrying water in both hands across ice.

"The thing beneath the well," Edith said, in the flat voice of someone reciting a lesson learned so long ago it had worn smooth, "can only come up where a way is opened for it. And only between moonrise and sunrise."

"Opened how?"

"Willingly." The old woman's mouth twisted around the word. "A door must be opened by choice. Not broken. Not forced. It cannot break anything, do you understand? It has never been able to break anything. That is not its nature. It waits to be *let*."

Priya looked down at her plans. At six meters below the flooded quarry floor, her crew's auger had cracked a stone lintel — cracked it, she had seen the hairline fracture running diagonally across the core samples, clean as a fault line in concrete. Her first instinct, the professional instinct, the instinct that had carried her through every collapsed shaft and flooded cutting of her career, had been: *we broke it, therefore we can finish breaking it.* Put charges against the lintel, blow the whole threshold into gravel, and whatever was behind it would be buried under forty tons of rubble by morning.

She had almost said so an hour ago. Maren Dole had looked at her then with an expression Priya had last seen on the face of a woman in Gujarat whose house stood directly in the path of a proposed diversion channel — the look of someone who knew that the engineers' map and the world underneath the map were two different countries.

"You're telling me I can't blast it," Priya said.

"I'm telling you that if you blast it," Edith said, "you will have done exactly what it wants, and you will have done it *for* it. A broken door is still an opening. It doesn't care whether the hand that opens it is gentle or furious. It cares only that the choice was made — and blasting is a choice, Miss Nandra. You'd be choosing. With dynamite, but choosing."

"And if we simply walk away? Seal the site, pour concrete, post a fence?"

"For how long?"

Priya opened her mouth and found nothing there. For how long. Fifty years? A hundred? The seal beneath the quarry had held for centuries before her auger bit into it, but it had not held because of engineering. It had held because of something else — something the village had done, generation after generation, without ever writing it down. Bricked cellars. Songs with holes in them. Children taught to hum everything except one verse. A whole culture of avoidance, maintained with the discipline of monks, and she had drilled through it in four days because it wasn't on any survey.

"The crack," she said instead. "The fracture in the lintel. Is that —"

"A splinter in a fingernail," Edith said. "Painful. Ugly. But the flesh holds. What woke it isn't the crack itself. It's the *air*. For the first time in longer than anyone living can remember, there is a path between here and there that does not require a willing door. A crack. A seam. Enough for a voice to come through." Her eyes went to Quentin's recorder. "Enough for a song to come through. It has been climbing toward the surface through voices, Miss Nandra. Voices are its hands. But a body—" she shook her head slowly— "a body needs a door, and a door needs a chooser."

* * *

Quentin spoke from the window, and Priya noticed that he did not turn around when he did it, as though looking at any of them while saying it might make it more real than he could afford.

"It came to my room last night," he said. "The twelfth doorway. Wearing Edith's shawl. It didn't cross the threshold. It stood there and waited, and it was — polite. That's the part I can't get past. It was *polite*, like a guest who arrives early and won't come in until you ask."

"Because it can't," Priya said, and heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used on crews about to do something stupid. "That's the rule, isn't it? It can't enter anywhere uninvited. So it makes you want to invite it." She pulled the site plan toward her and uncapped her pen, because there was one thing she trusted more than fear, and that was a surface to write on. "All right. Let's treat this like a problem. Moonrise tonight is —" she checked her watch, did the correction for latitude and season in her head — "9:40, give or take. Sunrise tomorrow is 6:12. That's eight and a half hours of danger window. Before moonrise and after sunrise, it's — what? Weaker? Dormant?"

"Asleep," Edith said. "Or as close to asleep as it ever comes. The dark hours are its working hours. Always have been. Why do you think the village counted doors at night, child? Why do you think every lamp was lit before dusk?" She gestured at the room, at the lamps already burning although the afternoon light still slanted gold through the windows. "You don't wait for the wolf to smell you before you build the fence."

Priya wrote: *DANGER WINDOW 21:40–06:12. 8h32m.* Then, underneath: *RULES.*

"One," she said aloud, ticking items off as she said them. "It surfaces only between moonrise and sunrise. Two: it needs a willingly opened door — a real door, a chosen door. Three: it cannot break things, but it can *persuade*. Four—" she hesitated, because item four was the one that had been sitting in her chest since she'd read the transcript that afternoon, since she'd found her mother's lullaby written out phonetically in the mouth of a dead man's cadence coming through a crew member who had never been within six thousand miles of Mumbai— "four: it knows what we've lost. It uses that. Grief is — it's a key. It finds the grief and turns it."

Nobody answered. In the silence Priya could hear the inn itself: the tick of cooling beams, the wind worrying the eaves, and beneath all of it — faint, rhythmic, patient — a humming from upstairs that should not have been audible through a locked door and a floor of solid oak. Tam. Humming the harvest song. All of it. Including the verse that had a hole torn in every adult version in the village.

Maren's knuckles were white on the back of a chair.

"Five," Priya said, harder than she meant to. "The excavation crack is a leak, not a breach. It gives it a voice but not a body. Which means our job tonight is not to fight it. Our job is to make sure that between 9:40 and 6:12, nobody in this village opens anything."

"That's the whole of it," Edith said quietly. "Fourteen hours ago I'd have said it couldn't happen again. Not in my lifetime. But Halloran—"

"Halloran is sedated and watched," Priya said. "Two of my crew are sitting on him, and I've taken the keys to every vehicle." She had found him at the edge of the site at dawn, on his knees in the mud, twenty meters from the excavation shed, weeping his daughter's name into the ground over and over — *Aisling, Aisling, Aisling* — and the cold coming up out of the crack had been visible, actually visible, a shimmer like heat haze inverted. They had dragged him back and he had fought them the way a sleepwalker fights, gently, terribly, apologizing the entire time. "But sedation buys hours, not safety. If it wants him, it'll use his grief whether he's conscious or not."

"Then you understand," Edith said, "that you cannot hold this place with locks alone."

"No," Priya said. "I understand that locks are step one." She turned to a fresh page. "So. Step two."

* * *

They worked through the late afternoon, and it was the strangest engineering session of Priya's life, because the materials were salt and candle flames and songs instead of steel and concrete, and the client was the dark.

Quentin brought out his recordings — the ones he hadn't burned yet; Priya had watched him feed three tapes into Maren's stove that morning with the expression of a man amputating his own fingers — and they built a loop. Hymns, mostly, old ones, harvested from Edith and two other singers before the singers stopped agreeing to sing. Played back-to-back on the recorder's loudest setting, piped through the inn's ground floor, the sound thin and tinny and somehow more substantial than any alarm system Priya had ever installed. Edith called it *the dam*. "It's not protection," she warned. "It's delay. Noise in the water. It buys minutes at the door, nothing more."

Minutes. Priya wrote it down anyway. In her experience, minutes were where sieges were won.

They inventoried the building the way she would inventory a site before a storm: every door, every window, every chimney. There were eleven ways in or out of the inn proper, and Maren knew each one intimately, and by full dark all eleven would be nailed, barred, or salted — coarse grey salt poured in unbroken lines across every threshold, which Priya had privately filed under *folklore* until she remembered that folklore had just spent three days predicting her core samples.

"The cellar," Priya said, and the word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"There is no cellar," Maren said instantly.

"There's a bricked-over entrance behind your kitchen wall. I've seen the mortar. It's newer than the rest of the wall — repointed, maybe thirty years ago. Every house in this village has one. Which means every house in this village has a *mouth*, according to Mrs. Garrick, and yours is twelve feet from where my crew sleeps."

The silence stretched. Maren looked at Edith. Edith looked at the table.

"The brick holds," Edith said finally, "so long as no one opens it. It has held for thirty years because Maren has never once let anyone touch it, never let a child play near it, never so much as hung a picture on the wall above it. Haven't you, Maren."

"No," Maren said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "And no one touches it tonight."

"Then it stays a wall," Priya said. "Fine. But I want it monitored. Someone awake, facing that wall, all night. Rotation of two-hour watches. Same for the quarry road — nobody goes out, nobody wanders, and if anyone *does* start walking toward the site, we stop them physically. Halloran proved it'll pull people. We assume it'll try all of us."

"And if it tries you?" Quentin asked. He had finally turned from the window. Up close, in the lamplight, he looked like a man who had been awake for several nights and expected to be awake for several more. "It knows things, Priya. It sang your mother's lullaby through a stranger's mouth. When it stands in your doorway tonight — and I think it will stand in all of ours — what is it going to offer you?"

Priya set down her pen.

It was a fair question, and she despised fair questions, because fair questions required honest answers, and the honest answer was that she did not know what she would do when the dark put on her mother's voice and asked, politely, to be let in. She was an engineer. Engineers believed in loads and tolerances. Nothing in her training covered the load a human heart could bear before its structural integrity failed.

"I'm going to remember the rules," she said. "That's all any of us can do. Remember the rules when it counts. It can't open the door. Only we can. Whatever it says, however much it sounds like someone we buried — the door only opens if a hand moves it. So the hand doesn't move." She picked the pen back up. "That's the whole design specification. One moving part. We keep the moving part still."

Edith made a sound that might have been a laugh, dry as ash. "Spoken like a woman who has never wanted anything badly enough to damn herself for it."

"I've wanted plenty."

"Not yet," the old woman said, not unkindly. "Not the way it wants you to want."

* * *

By seven the light had gone the colour of old brass, and Priya walked the perimeter alone, checking the nails herself, because she trusted her own thumb more than anyone else's assurance. The village lay quiet around her — too quiet, the particular quiet of a place holding its breath. No smoke from chimneys that should have had supper fires. No dogs. Behind the windows of the houses she passed, she saw lamps lit in every room, every single room, even rooms that could not possibly need light, and she understood suddenly that she was looking at a population performing a ritual so old it had become architecture: *light against the coming dark, counted and doubled.*

At the corner by the churchyard she stopped, because the well was there — the old village well, capped with an iron plate rusted the colour of dried blood — and the air above it was moving. Faintly. Inward. As though the ground were breathing in.

She checked her watch. 7:52. Moonrise in one hour and forty-eight minutes.

Back at the inn, the others had gathered in the back room: Maren standing sentry at the foot of the stairs with a poker in her hand she seemed unaware of holding; Quentin arranging his surviving tapes with the tenderness of a man laying out tools for surgery; Edith in her chair by the fire, hands folded, eyes closed, either praying or listening. On the table Priya's plans lay weighted down with salt cellars and candlesticks, and beside them she had left the page she'd been writing all afternoon, the siege ledger, the arithmetic of the night:

*DANGER WINDOW: 21:40 – 06:12.*
*DOORS: 11. ALL SEALED.*
*WATCHES: ROTATING, 2 HOURS.*
*RULES: IT CANNOT BREAK. IT CAN ONLY BE LET.*

Underneath, in her own handwriting, she had added a line she had not read aloud to the others, because some calculations you keep to yourself until you're sure of the figures:

*SUNRISE IN 14 HOURS. HOLD THE MOVING PART STILL.*

From upstairs, faint through the floorboards, Tam was humming. And as Priya stood listening, the humming stopped — mid-phrase, cleanly, like a needle lifted from a record — and in the silence that followed she heard, very distinctly, from somewhere far below the kitchen, from behind thirty years of brick and older than that, a voice begin the phrase again. Patiently. Correcting the boy's mistakes.

Teaching.

Priya looked at her watch, and at the lamp, and at the wall behind the kitchen where the mortar lines ran straight and true and silent, and she reached for the poker in Maren's hand.

"Change of plan," she said. "Nobody sleeps alone tonight."
