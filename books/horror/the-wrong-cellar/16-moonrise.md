## 16. Moonrise

The moon came up over Hollowmere the way water comes into a lung.

Quentin saw it first through the inn's east window, a white rim rising over the black shoulder of the quarry, and he felt the change before he heard it—a pressure in the ears, like the moment before a storm breaks or a door opens in a house where every door was supposed to be shut. The salt lines at the thresholds held their dull gray glitter. The hymns he'd looped on his recorder played on, Edith Garrick's cracked voice singing *The Lark Ascending* over and over into the dark of the common room, and for a while that was all there was: salt, song, lamplight, and eleven people pretending to be calm in a building that had begun, very softly, to breathe.

Then the walls started talking.

Not the floorboards this time. Not Tam's humming, which had mercifully gone quiet after Maren gave him half a sleeping draught and he'd collapsed against her shoulder at the corner table. The walls themselves—the old lime plaster, the black oak beams, the very bricks that Maren's great-grandfather had laid—began to speak.

Quentin set down his pen. He had been keeping a log, because the log was a ritual and rituals were the only currency he trusted now, and the first thing the walls said was his mother's voice.

*You always were a stubborn boy,* it said, from somewhere behind the wainscoting near the cold fireplace. *You'd sit in your room with your little tape recorder and you wouldn't come down even for supper. I used to stand outside your door and listen to you talking to yourself and I'd think, well, at least he's happy, even if he's strange.*

His mother had been dead eleven years. He had not thought of that particular supper, that particular stubbornness, in—God, he couldn't remember how long. That was the thing, he realized. That was the whole terrible architecture of it. These were not memories he kept. These were memories he had *put down*, the way you put down a heavy load at the end of a long road, and he had forgotten the road, and forgotten the load, and forgotten that he'd ever been carrying anything at all. And now the walls were handing it all back to him, warm and specific and unbearable, in a voice pitched exactly between his memory and his ear.

Around the room, the others had gone still. Priya Nandra stood by the boarded front window with her arms crossed, her engineer's face fixed in an expression of professional neutrality that fooled no one. Halloran was not in the room. Halloran was still locked in the pantry—Maren's idea, after the quarry, after the inch-wide gap in the door that breathed cold air and sang the village's missing verses—and through the pantry door they could hear him weeping steadily, saying *Annie, Annie, Annie* in the rhythm of a man counting beads.

The walls moved on to the others.

To Priya they spoke in a language Quentin didn't know—Tamil, he guessed, or Telugu—and though the words meant nothing to him, the cadence was unmistakably maternal, the rising lilt of a woman calling a child in from the evening heat. Priya's jaw tightened. Her knuckles whitened on her own arms.

To Maren, the walls were gentler, and that was the worst of it.

*The water was cold, love,* they said, in a man's voice, a young man's voice, easy and a little amused, the voice of someone who has never once considered drowning. *But you should have seen the moon on it. You should have seen the way it went all silver, all the way out to the reeds. I wasn't afraid. I want you to know that. I was never once afraid.*

Maren was standing at the hearth with a poker in her hand, and Quentin watched her knuckles change color the way Priya's had, watched her face do something terrible and private—watched it *open*, the way a face opens when it hears a voice it has spent seven years not hearing.

"Tom," she said.

"Don't," Quentin said quietly.

"He's *in the walls*, Quentin. He's—"

"He's not. It is. It's wearing him the way it wore Edith's shawl, the way it wore the face in the twelfth doorway. Maren. Look at me." She didn't. "The thing under the well doesn't remember your husband. It only remembers what you buried. Everything it says to you, you wrote first. Every word of it. You paid it away and now it's reading your payments back to you with interest."

That was the phrase, he realized, even as he said it. *Reading your payments back.* He didn't know yet where it had come from—wouldn't know until tomorrow night, until the cellar archive and the stone scratched with contract-language older than the church—but some part of him, the folklorist's part, the part that had spent twenty years listening for the shape of a story under the noise of one, already understood what Hollowmere had done. It had hired something. It had fed something. And now the thing had come up the well shaft and into the walls of every house in the village, and it was not threatening them.

It was *reminding* them.

That was the horror of the moonrise hours, and Quentin would carry the shape of it, if not the substance, out of Hollowmere forever: the thing did not growl. It did not scratch. It consoled. Every wall in the inn was a confessional running in reverse, pouring back into each listener the exact grief they had once chosen, at whatever cost, to set down. The room filled with it—dead fathers, dead daughters, dead husbands, the child Edith Garrick had lost before the war and had never once named aloud, until the walls named it now, softly, twice, the way you'd wake a sleeping child gently so as not to frighten them.

Edith sat in the corner with her hands over her ears and her eyes closed, and her lips were moving. Quentin got close enough to see she wasn't praying. She was singing. The harvest song, silently, the way you'd hold a rope in a flood—except she stopped, every time, at the gap. At the missing verse. And each time she stopped, the walls seemed to lean in a little closer, the way a creditor leans across a table.

*

By eleven the grief had soaked through the house like damp through plaster, and Maren began to move.

Quentin saw it happen the way you see a flood begin: not as an event but as a change in the water's attention. She set down the poker. She crossed the common room to the front door—the boarded one, the one they'd nailed shut at dusk with Halloran's hammer and Quentin's salt line laid thick across the threshold—and she stood in front of it with her palm flat against the wood, and the voice in the door was Tom's voice, and it was saying her name.

*Maren. Maren, love. It's cold out here. I've been walking so long. Just open it a crack. Just enough to see you.*

"Seven years," she whispered. "Seven years I didn't— I never got to—"

"Maren." Quentin was beside her. He didn't touch her; he'd learned that much, in the last three days, that a hand on the shoulder of a person being spoken to through a wall could be the hand that pushed. "He drowned in the flooded quarry. You told me that the first night, before any of this. Do you remember what else you told me?"

Her palm stayed on the door. The wood was warm. That was the detail Quentin would hate himself for noticing: not cold, not clammy, but warm, like a hand held too long, like a body's warmth soaking through from the other side.

"He was pulled out of the water by the Dole brothers," Maren said, and her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "And they laid him out in the cellar because the ground was frozen. And the cellar—" Her breath caught. "The cellar was already bricked over by then. They had to unbrick it. And when they bricked it up again, after the burial, I was the one who—" She stopped.

"You were the one who what?"

"I was the one who laid the last stone." Her fingers curled against the door, and Quentin saw, with a clarity that would stay with him longer than almost anything else from those nights, that she was not pressing to open it. She was holding it shut. She just didn't know that's what her body was doing, and her body was losing the argument. "I mixed the mortar myself. I said, *stay down, stay down, and keep what I gave you.* And it's kept it. It's kept it all these years, and now it's—" Her voice broke into the young man's cadence, into Tom's easy half-laugh, and Quentin felt the hair rise on his arms, because for one full sentence Maren Dole was not Maren Dole. "—and now it only wants to give a little back, love. Just a little. Just enough to see your face."

Quentin did the only thing he could think of. He began to sing.

It was not a good voice. It had never been a good voice; his mother's ghost in the walls could have testified to that, and briefly, horribly, it did—*you always sang flat, Quentin, even in church*—but he took up the harvest song at the top, the safe verses, the ones every adult in Hollowmere would sing twice without flinching, and he sang them badly and loudly into Maren's ear, and Priya came and took her other arm, and between the two of them they turned her from the door like turning a sleepwalker from a stairwell. She came. She wept into Priya's shoulder. The voice in the door went on calling for a while, patient, courteous, unhurt, the way a fisherman watches a lure rise away from a fish's mouth without anger, and then it too went quiet, folding itself back into the general murmur of grief in the walls, and Quentin understood that nothing had been won. The thing had all night. It had, in some sense that made his skin crawl, all the time ever minted.

But it had taught him something, in trying. It had shown him its own arithmetic.

*

"It counts what we give up," he said to Priya, an hour past midnight, in the kitchen, where the walls were thinner and the grief came through like radio. "Did you notice? The Halloran thing at the quarry—he spoke his grief aloud, and the door opened an inch. Maren nearly opened the door tonight, and the whole house got warmer. It's not feeding on suffering in the abstract. It's feeding on *surrender*. On the moment someone gives a buried thing back to it. And every time we refuse—" He rubbed his eyes. "Every time we refuse, it waits. It's patient. It's a creditor, not a predator."

"You keep using that word." Priya sat with her elbows on the kitchen table, a surveyor's map of the quarry unrolled and forgotten in front of her. "Creditor. You say it like you know something."

"I don't. Not yet. But the shape of it—the missing verse, the sealed cellars, the door that only opens to a willing voice, the way it never breaks a rule, never forces anything—it's not a beast, Priya. Beasts break in. This thing *waits to be let out*, and it keeps books." He looked at the ceiling, where Tam slept, or didn't. "Which means somewhere down there, or somewhere in this village's past, there's a record. A contract. And if there's a contract, there are terms. And if there are terms—"

"Then there's a way to settle."

"Or a way to default," Quentin said. "And I don't think we want to find out what default looks like at six meters."

*

The recordings were his own idea, and he would never know, afterward, whether it had been wisdom or instinct or simply the folklorist's reflex—that a song given freely is worth ten taken by force. He thought of it around two in the morning, standing in the common room with his equipment laid out on the long table: the reel-to-reel, the cassette deck, the boxes of tapes he'd made in four days in Hollowmere. Edith's interrupted harvest song. The innkeeper's wife from two farms over singing a christening rhyme that stopped like a snapped thread. The crew's radio chatter. Tam's humming, recorded through the inn's floorboards on the second night, a tune no adult would acknowledge.

And the recording from the bricked cellar. The one Maren had begged him not to make, and then begged him not to destroy, and then begged him not to play. Tam's small voice singing the full harvest song—verse and gap and verse—in a chorus of overlapping voices, thirty throats in a seven-year-old's mouth.

The walls had been murmuring for hours. But when Quentin picked up the first tape and held it to the lamplight, the murmuring stopped. All of it. The whole house went silent, attentive, the way a room goes silent when a debt is mentioned.

"It wants them," he said aloud, mostly to himself, and felt the silence *agree*.

He fed the first tape into the hearth.

It was Edith's. He watched the label curl—*GARRICK, E. — HARVEST SONG, TAKE 2 — INCOMPLETE*—and he said the words his grandmother had said over burnt offerings in a church he no longer attended, because the walls had reminded him of her that evening, of the smell of her kitchen, of the hymn she'd hummed over the sink, and it seemed only right that the gift should be wrapped in something. The tape buckled. The plastic went green and soft. And the house—the whole house—exhaled.

He couldn't have described the exhale to anyone who hadn't felt it. It was relief and appetite in one breath, the sound of a long thirst getting its first swallow, and it came up through the floor, through the salt, through the nailed doors, and every candle in the room leaned as if in a wind that had no business indoors. And then the walls were quiet. Truly quiet. Not silent the way a trap is silent—quiet the way a room is quiet after a guest has been fed.

Quentin stood over the hearth with his hands shaking and did the math.

Four hours to sunrise, if the pattern held. Eleven tapes left, counting Tam's. The thing had surfaced, or leaned toward the surface, every time something was surrendered to it—Halloran's grief, Maren's almost-opening, Edith's silent singing. And every time *he* gave it a tape, it settled. It went down. It was, he understood with a clarity that felt like cold water, exactly like feeding a fire that is trying to climb your chimney: you cannot put it out, but you can feed it in the wrong place, at the wrong hour, in small enough portions, and buy the night one log at a time.

He fed it the christening rhyme. The exhale came again, softer. The candles leaned.

He fed it the crew's radio chatter, and this time the exhale had a texture of disappointment, and he made a note of that too, in the log he was keeping in a hand that grew less steady with every entry: *prefers grief. Dislikes the trivial. The trivial buys less time.*

He fed it the humming from under the floorboards—Tam's first night, before any of this, when the boy's tune had been only a mystery—and the exhale that time was so deep and so long that the windows rattled in their frames and Priya came to the kitchen door with the poker and stood there watching him burn his life's work one cassette at a time, and her face was the face of a woman watching someone feed their own fingers into a machine.

"How many left?" she asked.

"Eight."

"And when they're gone?"

Quentin looked at the last box. At the tape inside it, the one labeled in his own careful hand: *TAM — FULL SONG — DO NOT PLAY.*

"Then I'll have to find something else it wants," he said. "Or something else to give."

He fed it a seventh tape. The house exhaled. Outside, the moon stood at the center of the sky, white and complete, and the quarry below the village lay black and still, and somewhere down at six meters, behind a door with its hinges on the inside, something patient counted what it was being paid, and found it almost enough, and waited to see what the man with the tapes would offer next.

Quentin picked up the eighth cassette, and as he did, every wall in the inn spoke at once—not in grief now, not in consolation, but in unison, in one voice, in his own voice, saying the words he had said to Maren Dole on his first night in Hollowmere, when he had still been a man who collected songs:

*"Could you sing that once more?"*
