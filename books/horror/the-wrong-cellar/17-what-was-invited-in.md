## 17. What Was Invited In

The cellar beneath the inn was not supposed to exist.

Quentin had counted the doors eleven times now—twelve on the bad night—and he had learned to trust his counting more than his eyes. Maren's inn had one bricked-over entrance like every other house in Hollowmere, a low arch of soot-dark stone behind the kitchen where barrels of nothing had stood since before Tam was born. But at four in the morning, when the walls had stopped speaking and the salt lines held and Priya slept in her boots with a crowbar across her knees, Quentin heard humming from behind that brick. Not Tam's tune. Something older, patient, almost kind.

He should have woken the others. He told himself he would, in a moment. In a moment he was already holding the candle close to the mortar, watching it glisten as though the brick were sweating, and in another moment the brick was not brick at all but a skin of plaster over an older doorway, and the plaster came away under his thumbnail like the rind of fruit.

He did not decide to go down. That was the thing he would later try hardest to remember and fail: there was no decision, only a descending, step by step, as though the stairs had been waiting for his particular weight.

* * *

The archive was small—a room perhaps three meters square at the bottom of a stair that had no business fitting inside the inn's footprint—and it was dry, which no cellar in Hollowmere had any right to be. Dry and warm, faintly, the way a sleeping animal is warm. The candle flame leaned toward the far wall instead of standing upright, as if drawn by breath.

Shelves lined two sides. On them sat objects arranged with terrible neatness: christening gowns folded into squares; a child's shoe; wedding rings threaded onto a bootlace; bundles of letters tied with hair; a ledger whose pages had gone the color of tea. And photographs, dozens of them, each face scratched out—not defaced, Quentin saw as he lifted one closer, but *erased*, the scratches careful and loving, the way you'd stroke a name off a list rather than strike it through.

These were not trophies. They were filings.

His hand shook so badly the photograph chattered against its neighbors. He thought of Edith's hands trembling around her own voice on the tape, and understood, suddenly, that she had been afraid not of what she remembered but of what remembering might cost—the way a man who owes money flinches at the ring of the telephone.

The far wall was stone, older than the inn above it, older than anything else he'd seen in Hollowmere. Candlelight crawled across it and found grooves. Letters. He stepped closer, and the warmth of the room pressed against his face like a palm, gentle, welcoming, and he read.

It was not carved. It was scratched—scratched by hands, by many hands over many years, some letters deep and confident, some shallow as if the writer had been dying as they wrote. The script shifted down the wall the way handwriting shifts across centuries: a formal early hand giving way to a cruder one, then to something barely literate, then back again, generations of fingers all writing the same words because the words were the contract and the contract had to be legible or the whole arrangement failed.

He began at the top left, where the oldest hand had begun, and read aloud in a whisper because some part of him believed the room could not hold what it contained unless someone spoke it:

*WE THE UNDERSIGNED OF HOLLOWMERE DO HEREBY INVITE AND EMPLOY*

Not imprison. Not bind. *Employ.*

*THAT WHICH DWELLS BENEATH THE WELL TO TAKE FROM US THAT WHICH WE CANNOT CARRY: OUR GRIEF OUR GUILT OUR DEAD UNSUNG AND OUR SINS TOO HEAVY FOR SONG. IN EXCHANGE IT SHALL HAVE THE VERSE AND THE REMEMBERING OF THE PAYING AND ONE OF OUR HOUSE EVERY SEVENTH YEAR UNTIL THE ACCOUNT IS CLOSED.*

There was a column of names below, and beside each name a mark—some crosses, some signatures, one a child's drawing of a bird. Forty-one names. He recognized three of them from gravestones in the churchyard, stones worn smooth, dates going back past the parish register's first page.

And then, lower, in a later hand, angrier, pressed so hard into the stone it had nearly gone through:

*THE ACCOUNT IS NOT CLOSED. WE REFUSE FURTHER PAYMENT. WE WILL NOT SEND ANOTHER.*

Beneath that, in yet another hand, smaller, almost apologetic:

*we cannot refuse. we can only forget. let forgetting stand for payment until we find the courage.*

And beneath that, over and over, filling every remaining inch of stone, in hands that deteriorated from hope to habit to desperation:

*the debt stands. the debt stands. the debt stands.*

* * *

Quentin lowered himself onto an upturned crate that had been placed there—placed there, he realized, for exactly this purpose, for whoever finally came down and read—and he made himself think it through the way he'd been trained to think, folklore as document, document as history, history as horror.

They hadn't trapped it. God, they hadn't even *fought* it. They had sat around some fire four hundred years ago, in a village drowning in grief—plague years, probably, or famine, the ledger would say, the ledger would have the numbers—and they had done what villages do with unbearable things. They had found someone to take them away. And when no priest or king would do, when the grief was too large for any human shoulder, they had looked down into the dark of their own well and *hired what lived there.*

A creditor. Not a monster. A creditor.

That was worse. He turned the word over and felt it cut on both edges. Because everything else fell into place with the ugly click of a lock turning. The missing verse wasn't a wound in the songs—it was the *fee*. They had paid it away, deliberately, the way you hand over your watch to a pawnbroker, and the forgetting of the paying was interest compounded annually, each generation owing more memory than the last. The sealed cellars weren't defenses. They were mouths, yes—but mouths are also how you feed something. Every seventh year. One of their house. Sent down.

He looked at the inner face of the door above him—at the scratch marks Priya had described, hundreds of them, old and new—and his stomach turned over slowly, completely, like a body rolling in water.

Those weren't claw marks. Of course they weren't. Things that live beneath wells don't claw at doors. Those marks were letters too—or attempts at them, names mostly, he'd bet his grant on it, names scratched by villagers sent down as interest across four centuries, scratching at the door their own families had sealed behind them, writing themselves into the record because the record was the only place left to exist.

He thought of Halloran weeping his daughter's name toward the quarry gap, and how the door had opened an inch, and he understood that too now. It wasn't hunger that opened doors. It was *accounts receivable.* Grief was the currency. Every confession spoken into the dark was a payment on account, and the thing beneath the well had been running a book on Hollowmere since before England had the name, patient as any lender, because lenders always win in the end. You didn't need to fight a village. You just needed to wait for them to default.

And they had defaulted. Four hundred years of defaulted payments, and the excavation crew had come with their drills and their six meters and their brisk modern confidence, and cracked the seal on a debt that had never once been forgiven.

* * *

The candle guttered. In the guttering, the warmth in the room seemed to lean closer.

Quentin became aware that he was being watched—not menacingly, not the way prey is watched, but the way a bank manager watches a customer who has finally, after decades, walked in to discuss the mortgage. There was attention in the walls. There was attention in the neat shelves, in the folded gowns, in the erased faces. Everything here was *kept*. Nothing was thrown away. That was the difference between a monster's lair and an office, and this room, with its ledgers and its filed sorrows and its upturned crate for visitors, was unmistakably an office.

"I'm not from here," he said aloud, and his voice sounded absurdly young. "I don't owe you anything."

The warmth did not change. But somewhere above him, faintly, through the floorboards, through the inn's old bones, he heard singing—the harvest song, sung complete, verse and all, in a voice like well water rising, and he understood that it was not threatening him. It was reminding him. *You came here asking about the missing verse,* the singing said, in the language of melody rather than words. *You asked twice. Asking twice is how it starts.*

Maren had gone white when he'd asked her to sing twice. He had thought it superstition. It was arithmetic. The second asking was an inquiry into the account, and inquiries woke the debtor's attention, and here he was, sitting in the office, reading the contract, having asked twice and thrice and thirty times with his little machine that caught voices.

Every recording he'd made was a deposition. Every interview was an audit. He hadn't been collecting folklore. He'd been itemizing the estate.

He laughed—one dry syllable with no humor in it—and the sound died in the warm air without echo, absorbed, filed.

* * *

He forced himself back to the wall, to the last section, the most recent writing, because a folklorist reads to the end of the document even when the document is reading him. The final entries were shallow, hurried, recent—within living memory, he judged. One line stopped him cold:

*edith remembers too much. watch her. she may yet pay.*

Edith Garrick, who wouldn't let him write her name down. Who had said the verse was paid away before she was born. Who trembled at her own recorded voice because somewhere in her, deeper than forgetting, the original terms still lived, and she had spent ninety years keeping her head down, making no claims, asking nothing twice, paying whatever small installments a life could quietly surrender.

She wasn't hiding from the thing. She was hiding from the *contract*—from the possibility that she was the last signatory, the last name on the page, the one the account would come due against.

And below that line, fresher than the rest, scratched within days—he touched it and the groove still held grit that crumbled under his fingertip:

*the door is open an inch. the surveyors are loud. send the one who asks twice.*

Him. It had known about him before he'd ever found this room. Perhaps before he'd ever boarded the bus. Perhaps the grant letter itself, the deadline, the archival notice about Hollowmere's "unusual song traditions"—perhaps none of that was chance. Debts attract collectors, and collectors attract auditors, and somewhere in the machinery of his ordinary academic life, a hook had been set and he had swallowed it smiling.

He stood. His legs were unsteady. The candle had burned down to a coin of wax, and in its failing light the contract seemed to move—not the letters, but the shadows between them, settling the way a ledger settles when the figures finally balance.

He should take the ledger. Photograph the wall. Get Priya, get Edith, show them, make them understand that this was not evil to be blasted or fled from but a *bill to be settled*, and bills, unlike monsters, had procedures. Bills could be paid.

But payment meant what the last lines implied, and he was not ready to look directly at that yet. *Send the one who asks twice.* Send him where? Down? Or to the threshold with the verse made whole?

He climbed the stairs. Behind him the warmth followed as far as the doorway and stopped there, politely, at the property line, and he heard—clearly, unmistakably, in a tone of infinite patience that was somehow more frightening than any roar—the sound of a page turning.

Above, in the kitchen, dawn-grey light showed at the window edges. Priya was awake, crowbar in hand, staring at the open arch behind the barrels with an expression he couldn't read. Maren stood with Tam pressed against her side, and Tam was looking at Quentin with those too-old eyes and humming, very softly, the first bars of the harvest song.

"Where were you?" Priya whispered.

Quentin opened his mouth to answer and found that the truth had a shape he couldn't yet speak. So he said the only true thing that mattered:

"It's not a monster." His voice cracked on the second word. "We have to see the contract. All of it. Because I don't think it wants to kill us." He looked back at the dark arch, at the warmth waiting beyond it like held breath. "I think it wants to be *paid*."

Tam stopped humming. In the silence that followed, from beneath the floorboards, from everywhere and nowhere, came a single sound—dry, deliberate, unmistakable.

The scratch of a pen, signing.
