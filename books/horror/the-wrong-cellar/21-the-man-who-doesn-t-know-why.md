## 21. The Man Who Doesn't Know Why

Maren Dole had not slept. She had sat in the kitchen of the inn all night with the lamp turned low, listening to the house settle around her like an old dog circling before lying down, and when the first grey light came through the east window she rose and went to the door because some part of her already knew.

He was at the bus stop.

The bus stop was a bench and a leaning post with a faded timetable nailed to it, set where the lane met the old coach road, and Quentin Crane sat on that bench as though he had been placed there by careful hands. His recording equipment was stacked beside him—the tape machine, the boxes of cassettes, the microphone coiled in its case—all of it neat, all of it orderly, all of it arranged by a man who did not know why he owned any of it. His notebook lay on his knees, closed. Maren could see from thirty paces that it was blank. Not empty—blank. The pages were clean as snow, and there was no dust on his sleeves, no mud on his boots, nothing to say he had walked out of the quarry in the dark with the rest of them, nothing to say he had stood at a stone door and sung until his voice broke and something older than grief let go of this village's throat.

She stopped in her own doorway and did not go to him.

It was not fear. That was the strange part, the part she would turn over for the rest of her life like a stone in the pocket: she felt no fear at all. The air over Hollowmere was clear for the first time since the crews had come, since the machines had bitten down past six meters and woken what slept beneath the well. The well water had gone still at dawn. Edith Garrick had died an hour ago in her bed, peacefully, saying her own name aloud three times as though tasting it after a long fast, and then smiling and closing her eyes. The village felt lighter. It felt swept.

And Quentin Crane sat at the bus stop with a blank notebook and a face like a man waiting for someone who is late.

* * *

She watched him from the doorway while the light came up properly, gold now, spilling down the crooked lanes and touching the wet slate roofs. He tested the latch on one of the cassette boxes, opened it, looked at the tapes inside without recognition, and closed it again. He picked up the notebook, thumbed it, frowned—not the frown of a man who has lost something, but of a man trying to remember what category of thing he might have lost. Then he set it down and looked up the road, toward the place where the morning bus would come from, and Maren saw him check his watch twice, as if time itself were a language he'd half forgotten.

Tam came down the stairs behind her, barefoot, hair uncombed, dragging his blanket.

"Is that the singing man?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Does he know us?"

Maren knelt and took her son by the shoulders. His eyes were clear this morning—clearer than they had been in weeks, since the humming had started under the floorboards, since the chorus of voices had poured out of him at the bricked cellar mouth. Whatever had borrowed him had paid its account and gone back down, or gone wherever things go when their contracts are settled, and her boy was only her boy again. But he remembered. Children always remembered; that was the village's oldest problem and its oldest protection.

"He knew us," she said carefully. "I think he knew us very well."

"Then why's he sitting like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like Grandad used to sit. After." Tam didn't finish. After the funeral. After the well took his father, years ago, in the spring flood—before Maren understood what the well took and what it gave back and what it kept. "Like he's waiting to be told something."

Maren kissed the top of his head and stood. "Go put your shoes on. We're going to see him off."

* * *

The walk down the lane took two minutes and lasted a season. Maren rehearsed sentences the whole way. *You're Quentin Crane. You're a folklorist. You came to record our songs.* Each one felt like a knife she was being asked to hand him handle-first—or blade-first, she couldn't decide, and that was the cruelty of it, that was the thing Edith had tried to warn them about in the last weeks when her voice still worked: *the debt don't care if you mean it kind, girl. A payment's a payment. What you choose not to give him will weigh on you same as what you gave.*

Because here was the arithmetic no one had written down: the contract had been paid. The verse had been sung whole at the threshold by a willing voice, and the voice had fed itself into the account—his grant, his purpose, his reasons, whatever small loves he had carried into Hollowmere in October—and the door had sealed from the outside, hinges groaning shut like a jaw finally closing, and the well had gone quiet. The village's songs were whole again. Maren had sung the harvest song twice yesterday evening, standing in her kitchen, just to feel it, just to weep a little at how easily it came, verse and all, the missing tooth grown back into the gum.

And Quentin Crane had walked out of the quarry with them, blinking at the sunrise, and asked Priya Nandra—in a polite, puzzled, entirely reasonable voice—who she was, and why they were all standing in a hole in the ground at five in the morning.

* * *

"Maren," Tam said, tugging her sleeve. They were close enough now. The man on the bench had noticed them.

He stood up. He was taller than she remembered, or maybe she had only ever seen him hunched over his equipment, bent toward voices. His coat was wrong for the season. There was a smear of quarry dust along one shoulder that he hadn't noticed, and Maren found she couldn't look away from it—that grey fingerprint of the night, sitting on him like a hand.

"Good morning," he said. His voice was pleasant. Educated. Slightly hoarse, as if he'd been talking all night, or singing. "I'm sorry—I wonder if you could help me. I seem to be waiting for the bus to—" He checked the timetable, and Maren watched him read it twice. "Well. Anywhere, I suppose. I appear to have missed my connection somewhere." He smiled, apologetic, charming, hollow. "Do you know this village? I can't think why I'd be in it."

The question hung between them like breath in cold air.

Maren opened her mouth. Everything she had rehearsed was there—*you came for the songs, you came for us, you fixed us*—and everything she had decided against was there too, and the two piles were exactly the same size. That was the mercy-or-cruelty of it, the thing she would never be able to name, not in forty years, not on her deathbed: every true sentence she could offer him was also a wound. To tell him what he'd done was to tell him what he'd lost. To tell him what he'd lost was to make him grieve something he couldn't even see. And the debt was paid. Paid debts were meant to stay paid. Edith had said so with her last lucid breath: *let the man keep his clean pages. It's the only wage he gets.*

"You're passing through," Maren said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "A lot of people do. Hollowmere's not much of anywhere anymore."

"It looks lovely," he said, and meant it, and looked at the crooked lanes and the wet slate and the smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless morning with the mild appreciation of a stranger. Then his gaze snagged—just for a moment—on the bricked-over cellar entrance beside the inn's foundation, the low arch filled with mortared stone, and something crossed his face. Not memory. The shape where memory would have been. Like a man hearing his own name spoken in a crowd and turning to find no one there.

"Do you know," he said slowly, "I have the strangest feeling I've been here before."

"People say that," Maren said. "About Hollowmere."

He looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment—one long moment in which the morning held perfectly still and even the smoke stood straight—she thought he was going to ask her name. And she thought: *if he asks my name, I'll tell him. If he asks anything directly, I'll answer honestly. I owe him that much and no more.* It was a rule she made on the spot, a private contract of her own, witnessed by nobody but the smoke.

But he didn't ask. He glanced down at the equipment at his feet instead, and frowned at it the way a man frowns at luggage that isn't his.

"This isn't mine," he said. "Or—it is mine, I suppose, it was in my name on the train, but I can't imagine why I'd carry all this about. Do you play?" He lifted one of the cassette boxes slightly, offering it to her, and Maren stepped back before she could stop herself.

Inside that box were tapes. Tapes of Edith Garrick's voice with its gaps like missing teeth. Tapes of Tam humming in a voice too deep for a child. Tapes of the harvest song broken at the same bar in eleven different mouths. Tapes, perhaps—she didn't know, she hadn't dared catalogue them—of the thing beneath the well singing the verses whole through a gap one inch wide, cold pouring out like water, comfort offered in a dead husband's cadence. If he played those tapes he would hear his own voice asking questions. He would hear himself being happy here. He would hear the exact contour of everything that had been taken, and it would be like handing a starving man a photograph of bread.

"No," Maren said. "We don't have much use for recordings here."

"No," he agreed, and set the box down gently, and seemed relieved, and that relief frightened her more than anything else that morning. "Somehow I'm not surprised."

* * *

The bus came at seven, as it had come every Tuesday and Friday for sixty years, its engine coughing up the coach road through the mist. Maren heard it before she saw it, and something in her chest clenched like a fist.

"There's your ride," she said.

"So it is." He gathered his things with the competence of habit—hands remembering what the mind had released, buckling cases, coiling cables—and paused with the notebook in his hand. Blank pages. Clean as snow. He looked at it a long moment.

"I write," he said, apparently to no one. "I'm fairly sure I write. Books, or... articles. Something." He turned the notebook over in his hands. "There's nothing in here at all. Isn't that odd? A writer with nothing to say."

"Maybe you said it," Maren said, "somewhere else."

He smiled at that—a real smile, brief and warm, and it went through her like a hook. Because she had seen that smile before, across her kitchen table, over tea she'd made him on his second night, when he'd complimented her stew and asked about the harvest song and she'd refused to sing it twice and he'd laughed and said, *fair enough, some things should only happen once,* not knowing how right he was.

"Thank you," he said. "For the company. It's kind of you, waiting with me. I hope I haven't kept you from anything."

"You haven't," Maren said. "There's nothing keeping me."

The bus rounded the bend, yellow-lit windows, the driver's face familiar and indifferent. Tam shifted at her side. She realized her son had been silent the entire time, watching the man with the intensity children reserve for weather and animals and other things that cannot be lied to.

Quentin Crane picked up his bags. He hesitated—one foot toward the bus, one foot still in Hollowmere, and Maren saw him look back down the lane, toward the inn, toward the well cover in the square, toward the quarry road beyond it, and his brow furrowed in that same bloodless way, reaching for a shape that wasn't there.

"Did I..." he began, and stopped, and shook his head with a small laugh at himself. "Forgive me. I keep having the sense that I've forgotten something important. An appointment, perhaps. Did I leave anything behind? Did we speak before?"

*Yes,* Maren thought. *You spoke. You sang. You stood at a door in the dark and gave away the reason you existed so that my son could sleep without voices in his mouth, and I am going to stand here and let you board that bus without knowing it, because Edith told me to and because I am a coward and because it is the kindest thing anyone has ever done in this village and it feels exactly like the worst.*

"You left nothing behind," she said aloud. "Everything's paid for."

"That's good," he said, satisfied, and the phrase landed on her like a stone dropped down a well—*everything's paid for*—and she waited, heart hammering, to see if the words meant anything to him. They didn't. He nodded pleasantly, thanked her again, and climbed aboard.

* * *

The doors folded shut. The engine revved. Through the smudged glass Maren could see him settling into a seat, arranging his equipment in the space beside him with those competent hands, opening the blank notebook as if hoping the motion of the road might shake something loose onto the pages.

Then Tam stepped forward, off the curb, into the road, and waved.

It was not a polite wave. It was the wave of a child who knows someone—arm high, whole body in it, the kind of wave that expects to be answered. Maren's hand flew toward her son's shoulder and stopped, because there was nothing to stop. The boy was safe. The boy was only waving goodbye.

Through the window, Quentin Crane looked up from his blank notebook.

Maren watched his face. Watched the polite blankness of it, the stranger's face, the face of a man looking at an unfamiliar child in an unfamiliar village through unfamiliar glass. And she watched his hand rise anyway—slowly, uncertainly, pulled upward on strings he didn't know were attached—and his fingers spread, and he waved back.

Not politely. Not the way strangers wave. He waved the way you wave at someone you love from a departing train, the way you wave when you can't speak, the way his hand must have moved once, in another life, ruffling a boy's hair across a kitchen table while a kettle sang and a woman refused, kindly, to sing twice.

The bus pulled away. The hand stayed up until the bend took it.

Tam lowered his arm. He was crying, quietly, without drama, the way children cry when they understand more than they can say.

"He waved," Tam said.

"Yes."

"He doesn't know me."

"No," Maren said. She knelt and pulled her son against her coat, against the smell of woodsmoke and salt, and looked down the empty road where the bus had gone, carrying a man who had been full of their songs and was now empty of their names. "But he does," she said. "Somewhere in him, he does. That's not nothing, Tam. That's the whole payment, and it bought more than they promised."

Above them, in the clear morning, smoke rose straight from every chimney in Hollowmere, and beneath the square, beneath the well, deep under six meters of quarried dark, water lay perfectly still against a door sealed from the outside—hinges quiet, ledger balanced, waiting, as it had always waited, for the next one who asks twice.
