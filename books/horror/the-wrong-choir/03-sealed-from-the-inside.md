## 3. Sealed from the Inside

The call came in at 6:40 on a Tuesday morning, and Freya Fairweather knew before she answered that it was going to be one of those jobs.

"Fairweather Pest Solutions," she said, coffee balanced between her ear and shoulder, boot laces half-tied.

"This is Dale Okafor. I'm running the excavation on the old meetinghouse lot, off Corben Street." His voice had the flat carefulness of a man reading from a script he'd written for himself at three in the morning. "The historical society gave me your number. They said you're the person to call about... animals."

*Animals.* The pause before it was doing a lot of work.

"What kind of animals?"

"That's the thing." A breath. "We pulled a slab of dressed stone out of the ground yesterday. Twelve feet down. It's got a door in it."

Freya stopped tying her boot. "A door in a foundation slab?"

"Not a foundation. There's no building above it. Never was, far as the records show. Just the door, sitting under two hundred years of fill dirt like somebody buried a room and forgot to mention it." He laughed, but it came out wrong, more air than sound. "Look, I'm not saying it's rats. I don't know what I'm saying. But there's a chamber behind that stone and something's been knocking on it since yesterday afternoon, and my crew won't go back down in the pit, and the historical society wants it 'dealt with' before the county hears about it. They said you deal with things."

She should have said no. Seven years in Hollowmere had taught her the shape of calls like this—the ones where *deal with things* meant *don't ask questions*, where the fee was always generous and the silence afterward was always total. She'd built a whole business on not asking. Raccoons in the grange hall walls, she'd told herself last month, standing on a ladder with her flashlight on handprints eight feet up a plaster wall, greasy prints with too many knuckles' worth of spread. Raccoons can't climb plaster, said a small voice she kept locked in a drawer. Sure they can, she'd told it. These are motivated raccoons.

"How much?" she asked.

"Whatever you want. Seriously. Name a number."

That was the second warning sign. People who wanted a job done quietly never haggled, because haggling meant paperwork and paperwork meant witnesses.

"I'll be there by nine," she said, and hung up, and sat for a moment in her kitchen listening to the floorboards beneath her feet, which hummed the way they always hummed—so faintly that on most mornings she could pretend it was the refrigerator, or water in the pipes, or the town itself settling on its old bones. This morning the hum seemed almost pleased.

She told herself that was nonsense. She was getting good at telling herself things.

* * *

The meetinghouse lot sat at the crook of Corben Street where the road bent around a hill nobody had ever built on, as if the town had agreed generations ago to leave that particular rise alone. The excavation site was ringed in orange mesh fencing that someone had already cut and re-tied twice, and beyond it the pit opened in the earth like a mouth—a square shaft twelve feet deep, shored with timber, its floor a churned rectangle of clay and gravel. And set into the far wall of the pit, half-emerged from the earth that had hidden it, stood the door.

Freya stood at the edge for a long moment before climbing down the ladder.

It wasn't a grand door. That was the first surprise. She'd expected something ceremonial—iron bands, carved saints, the kind of thing towns put on their myths. Instead it was plain, almost humble: a single slab of grey stone, maybe four feet wide by seven tall, dressed smooth but left undecorated except along its edges, where someone had cut a border of small dense characters into the rock. The notation ran all the way around the frame like a sentence with no beginning and no end.

And it was sealed. Not locked—sealed. She saw it within ten seconds of dropping into the pit, and once she saw it she couldn't stop seeing it, because it rearranged everything.

The bolt was on the inside.

She ran her gloved fingers over the mechanism twice to be sure. A heavy stone bar, counterweighted, sliding into sockets cut deep in the doorframe—from the inside. From her side of the door there was nothing. No handle, no hinges visible, no keyhole, no ring to pull. If you were standing in the pit, looking at this door, you could knock until the end of the world and nothing would happen. The only person who could ever open it was whoever was already behind it.

Which raised an arithmetic problem so simple it made her stomach turn: a door that bolts from the inside is a door for keeping something *in*. And someone had gone to the trouble of burying the whole arrangement under twelve feet of earth, which meant someone had wanted the keeping-in to be permanent, and quiet, and forgotten.

"Found it fast," said a voice above her. Dale Okafor leaned over the pit's edge, a broad man in his fifties with grey threading his beard and the wary look of someone who hadn't slept. He climbed down the ladder with the stiff care of a bad back. Up close he smelled of diesel and cold sweat. "You see it, then."

"I see it."

"My guys found it Thursday. Backhoe hit it—we thought it was a boulder, then we thought it was an old cistern lid. Took us a day to clear enough to understand what we were looking at." He wiped his hands on his thighs, though they weren't dirty. "You know what's strange? Nobody panicked. You'd think finding a sealed room under a town would be exciting. Everybody just went quiet. Like we'd walked in on something private."

"Has anyone tried to open it?"

Dale looked at her like she'd suggested swimming across a lake in January. "It doesn't open from out here. You noticed."

"I noticed."

"And here's what I keep coming back to." He lowered his voice, though the nearest crew member was thirty feet away and pointedly studying the shoring timbers. "If you wanted to lock something *out*, you'd bar the door from your side. Right? That's how doors work. This one's barred from the other side. Which means either somebody in there is very determined to stay in—" he swallowed, "—or somebody in there is very determined that whatever else is in there stays put. Either way, I don't want to be the guy who finds out which."

Freya crouched by the doorframe and let her flashlight play along the carved notation. The characters were small, angular, dense as stitching. Not Latin. Not runes, exactly—not any alphabet she could name, and yet her eye kept snagging on them the way a tongue snags on a chipped tooth, because some of them felt *familiar*, in the way a face seen once in a crowd is familiar. A hooked symbol like a bird's foot. A vertical stroke crossed by three short bars. A spiral that tightened instead of opening.

She'd seen that tightening spiral before. She couldn't say where. Somewhere in seven years of basements and crawlspaces and the margins of documents she'd skimmed and decided not to think about.

"You okay down there?" Dale asked.

"Fine." She straightened. "I'll need to document it. Photographs, measurements. Then I'll tell you what I recommend."

"And what are you going to recommend?"

Honest answer: *I have no idea, because pest control does not cover whatever this is.* Professional answer: "Depends what the photographs show."

He nodded slowly, and then did the thing she would think about later, when everything had changed and it was too late for small mercies: he reached into his jacket and pressed a folded paper into her hand. A check. The amount written on it was nearly double her usual rate, and the memo line read, in block capitals, ANIMAL REMOVAL.

"Historical society's money," he said. "Miriam Vance signed off on it personally. Her instructions were specific. You're here to confirm the site has vermin activity, so the county will sign off on filling the pit with concrete. Structural remediation. Boring stuff. Paperwork stuff." He met her eyes. "That's the job, if you want it. Confirm it and pour it."

"And if I find something that shouldn't be poured?"

His jaw worked. "Then I guess you decide who you work for."

* * *

She worked through the morning while the crew stayed up top, ostensibly on break, actually keeping a distance that had nothing casual in it. The light in the pit was good—overcast white, shadowless—and she photographed the door from every angle, then the notation, section by section, forty-one frames in all. The camera's screen showed her what her eyes kept sliding past: the carvings weren't worn. Two hundred years underground, minimum, and the edges of every character were crisp as the day they were cut. Stone doesn't stay crisp underground. Soft earth abrades; moisture rounds; roots insinuate. These lines looked freshly made, as if the door had been carved last week and buried yesterday.

In the lower right corner of the frame, at knee height, half-hidden by a shelf of clay still clinging to the slab, there was a second inscription. Smaller. Different hand—shallower cuts, less confident, the letters cramped together like someone writing in a hurry or in fear. She scraped the clay away gently with a trowel and photographed that too.

It was in English. Old-fashioned English, the long-s and doubled vowels of the eighteenth century:

WE GIVE WILLINGLY AND COUNT THE COST
LET NONE OPEN WHO HAS NOT SUNG

Below it, a list of names. Nine of them. Weather had not touched them—they might have been chiseled that afternoon—but someone had come along later, with a harder tool and a heavier hand, and struck through every name so thoroughly that the stone beneath had been gouged and powdered. Erasure done in granite. Whoever they'd been, someone had wanted them not just dead but *unrecorded*, and had cared enough about it to kneel in the dark and do the work twice.

Freya sat back on her heels in the clay and felt the discipline she'd maintained for seven years strain against something stronger.

Here was the protocol, the one she'd written for herself after her first year in Hollowmere, after the grange hall and the handprints and the night she'd heard her own floorboards humming a chord no refrigerator ever made: *Document the ordinary. Discard the extraordinary. Bill for raccoons.* It had worked. It had worked because Hollowmere rewarded not-looking; the whole town was built on it, a civic architecture of averted eyes, and she'd told herself she was just being practical, being professional, being the kind of person who fixed problems instead of investigating them.

But she was crouching in front of a door that bolted from the inside, with nine murdered names at her knees, and the drawer where she kept the small voice was rattling on its rails.

*Take the pictures. Say vermin. Take the money. Pour the concrete.*

Her hands didn't listen. Her hands took the tracing paper from her kit.

She'd bought it years ago for making templates of gnaw-marks, a use so rare she'd forgotten it was in the bag. She taped a sheet over the upper band of notation, smoothed it down, and went over the characters with the side of a carpenter's crayon, working left to right, the wax catching on the crisp-cut edges. It was slow, meditative, damning work. With each character transferred to paper she felt less like a pest controller and more like a burglar robbing a house whose owner was home and pretending to sleep.

The spiral again. The bird's-foot hook. The triple-barred stroke. Her crayon paused over that one, because her hand knew it. Not her mind—her mind offered nothing, a blank wall, professionally maintained. But her hand hesitated over the triple-barred stroke the way a hand hesitates over a staircase rail it gripped as a child, muscle memory arriving ahead of permission.

Where. *Where.*

Her mother's kitchen. A grocery list on the fridge, and beside it, pinned under a magnet, a card of handwritten reminders in Edith Fairweather's cramped slanting hand—and one line that wasn't a reminder at all, that Freya had read a thousand times without reading: three short horizontal bars stacked on a vertical stroke, drawn like a tally, like a signature, like a word in a language her mother had never taught her because her mother had never spoken of anything that mattered.

She'd thrown that card out eleven years ago, when they'd cleared the house. She could still draw it perfectly.

Freya sat in the pit with the rubbing drying in her lap and understood, with the calm clarity that precedes panic, that she had just crossed a line she'd spent seven years pretending wasn't there. The photographs could be deleted. The report could say raccoons. But the rubbing was in her kit now, physical, un-deletable, a page torn out of the town's hidden ledger and tucked into hers, and she had taken it not for evidence, not for money, but because she *had to know*—and wanting to know was the one thing the job, the town, the whole arrangement of Hollowmere required her never to do.

Above her, Dale's silhouette shifted against the grey sky. "Any luck?" he called down.

"Vermin activity," she said. "Consistent with raccoon. I'll write it up."

The lie came out smooth, worn smooth, seven years of practice, and Dale nodded with relief and climbed back out of sight, and Freya remained alone in the pit with a sealed door and a stolen alphabet, telling herself the oldest story of her life: *this changes nothing.*

Under her knees, through twelve feet of packed earth and two hundred years of deliberate forgetting, she felt—or imagined she felt, which amounted to the same thing—the faintest vibration, rhythmic and patient, like breathing. Or like waiting.

Like something on the other side of the door noticing, for the first time in a very long while, that someone outside had finally touched the words.

She climbed the ladder. She did not look back, which took more discipline than anything else she did that day.

At the top, folding the rubbing into an envelope, she caught Miriam Vance watching her from the fence line—a small silver-haired woman with an archivist's posture and eyes that missed nothing, holding a clipboard full of boring paperwork against her chest like a shield. Their gazes met for one second. Miriam's expression didn't change, but she tilted her head, very slightly, toward the envelope in Freya's hand, and then away, and wrote something on her clipboard.

Freya drove home with the radio off and the envelope on the passenger seat, and the hum under the floorboards of her house, when she got there, had changed key.

It sounded, for the first time in seven years, like recognition.
