## 7. Dale's Last Shift

The last night of the excavation smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Dale Okafor stood at the lip of the pit with a thermos of coffee gone bitter in his hand, watching the floodlights turn the exposed stone door into something that looked less like archaeology and more like a wound. Twelve feet down, the dressed slab sat flush in its frame of older, rougher rock, and the shadows pooled around it in a way he didn't care for. Shadows weren't supposed to have texture. These ones did—soft-edged, breathing almost, though he'd never say that out loud where the crew could hear him.

"Foreman says we're done by Friday," said Marcus, climbing out of the pit with mud to his knees. "Structural engineer comes Monday, then they fill it or fence it or whatever."

Dale grunted. He'd been doing this work for twenty-two years, long enough to know that "whatever" was where the trouble lived. He'd dug up septic fields full of bones from old family plots nobody had recorded. He'd cut through a Prohibition-era still that still had mash in it, sweet as rotting apples after eighty years. He'd found a car in a pond once, with somebody's grandfather still belted into it, and he'd been the one who waited with the body until the state police came, because somebody had to.

He knew what ground kept. Ground kept everything. That wasn't the problem.

The problem was that this ground kept something *loud*.

* * *

It had started three nights ago, subtle as a change in barometric pressure. A hum under the generators' drone, low enough that he'd assumed it was the pump running rough. He'd walked the perimeter with a flashlight, checked the fuel lines, kicked the intake housing, found nothing. The hum stayed. It wasn't mechanical. Mechanical sounds had edges. This one had *intention*, if that word meant anything applied to sound, which Dale was pretty sure it didn't, and he was pretty sure anyway that he was too old and too tired and too far from his church years to be having thoughts like this.

Then last night, working alone near the door while the others took their break, he'd heard the melody come through.

Not through the stone. Through the air around the stone, the way heat shimmer comes off asphalt. A lullaby. Four notes rising, two falling, four notes rising again—the one his mother used to sing to his brother Emeka when the thunderstorms rolled off the ridge and shook the trailer on its blocks. The one Emeka had sung back, off-key, before the leukemia got him at nine years old, before Dale stopped believing in a God who'd take a boy mid-verse.

Dale had stood in that pit with his hard hat in his hands and let the song finish. It took maybe forty seconds. Then the hum resumed, patient as groundwater, and Dale climbed out of the pit and sat in his truck until his hands stopped shaking, and told no one.

Tonight was different. Tonight the crew was pulling equipment, loading the mini-excavator onto the trailer, and Dale had volunteered to stay late and secure the site—which meant, though he wouldn't have phrased it this way even to himself, that he wanted to be alone with it. One more time. Just to see if it came back. Just to know whether he'd invented it, whether grief had finally gotten so loud it was leaking out of the ground.

He killed the floodlights at 11:40. The pit went dark except for the moon, half-full and indifferent, and the yellow work lamp he left burning at the ladder.

He climbed down.

* * *

The bottom of the pit held the day's warmth like a cupped hand. Dale stood six feet from the door and listened.

Wind in the silt fencing. Traffic on Route 9, a mile off. His own pulse. And beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, the hum—except tonight it wasn't a hum. Tonight it was tuning. He knew what an orchestra sounded like warming up; he'd sat through enough of Emeka's school concerts, squirming in folding chairs, proud and bored in equal measure. Strings finding their pitch. Voices doing the same, that sliding search for a shared center.

The voices found it.

And then, out of the chord, one voice lifted free and began the lullaby.

Dale's whole body went cold, then hot, then cold again. Because it wasn't just the melody. It was the *voice*. The slight rasp on the high notes where the intubation had scarred his brother's throat. The way Emeka always rushed the third phrase because he was impatient to get to the end, because endings were safe, because Mom would tuck the blanket tighter there. Nobody alive knew those details. Dale barely knew them; he'd buried them so deep he'd needed thirty years and a hole in the ground to dig them back up.

"Emeka?" His voice came out cracked, stupid, echoing against the stone.

The singing paused. The silence behind the door was worse than the song—a listening silence, a held breath the size of a room.

Then, softly, in a voice like a boy's voice remembered rather than heard: *"Dale? You came."*

Dale's knees nearly went. He put a palm flat against the stone slab to steady himself, and the stone was warm. Not sun-warm. Skin-warm. He snatched his hand back.

"You're dead," he whispered. "I carried your box. I was eleven. I carried your box, man."

*"I know,"* the voice said, and there was such gentleness in it, such terrible patience, that Dale felt tears start before he could stop them. *"I've been waiting a long time for someone to open the door. It's dark in here, Dale. It's been dark for so long, and everyone else stopped listening, but you didn't. You never stopped. Come on. Just unbolt it. I'm right here. I'm so tired."*

And there it was—the bolt. Dale hadn't noticed it before, in daylight, with other people around. A heavy iron crossbar set into a channel on the *inside* face of the door, visible now through some gap he couldn't identify, some seam that shouldn't have shown him anything at all. Sealed from the inside. He'd heard the historical society woman say it, heard the pest-control woman photograph it. Sealed from the inside meant whoever was behind it had locked themselves in, or had been locked in by someone who then left, and either way—

Either way, none of that mattered, because Emeka was tired, and Emeka was scared of the dark, had always been scared of the dark, and Dale was eleven years old again standing outside his brother's hospital room unable to go in, unable to do anything, and here, after thirty years, here was one single thing he could actually *do*.

His hands were on the outer mechanism before he registered moving. There was a recessed handle, cleverly countersunk, disguised as drainage stonework. His fingers fit it perfectly. Of course they did. It had been waiting for fingers exactly his size.

"Dale!"

Marcus's voice, from above. Boots on the ladder rungs. "Dale, what the hell, you killed the lights—we saw the lamp move—"

Dale didn't answer. He was pulling. The bolt groaned somewhere inside the stone, a sound like a ship's anchor dragging, and beneath it the lullaby swelled, joyous now, *Emeka's* laugh threading through it, and the warmth coming off the door was the warmth of a crowd, of bodies, of forty people pressed close against the far side, waiting—

Hands seized his shoulders and hauled him backward. Marcus and the kid, Tommy, both of them, digging heels into the mud, and Dale fought them, actually fought them, roaring something he couldn't later remember, until the three of them crashed down at the pit's edge and lay gasping in the moonlight.

The singing stopped. The hum settled back into the ground like water into sand.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Tommy said, in a small voice, "There were voices. Plural. I counted."

"I know," Dale said.

"They sounded—" Tommy stopped. Swallowed. "One of them sounded like my granddad."

Nobody suggested reporting it. That was the thing Dale understood later, lying awake in his truck cab: they all decided, together, without a word, not to look too hard. They'd finish the job, cash the checks, and let the engineers and the historians and the mayor's office figure out what to do with a door that locked from the inside. Ordinary men, choosing blindness, because the alternative was admitting the world had a basement.

* * *

At dawn Dale typed his resignation email in the cab of his truck, thumbs clumsy on the phone screen, and sent it before he could delete it. He showered at the truck stop on Route 9, bought coffee and a road atlas he didn't need—he'd driven these routes for two decades—and pointed the truck south with no particular destination in mind.

He was on the county line when he made himself say it out loud, just once, to the empty cab:

"It wasn't him."

The words sat there. He knew they were true the way you know a load is overweight—you feel it in the axle. Whatever had sung to him had worn his brother's voice like a coat it had found in a closet. It had known the rasp, the rushing third phrase, because it had been *collecting* things for a very long time, and grief was just another key it kept on a hook by the door.

But knowing it didn't help. Because for forty seconds in that pit, Dale Okafor had heard his brother sing again, and some part of him—the eleven-year-old part, the part still carrying the box—would spend the rest of his life grateful. That was the cost of listening. Not fear. Gratitude. Gratitude was how it got you. Fear made you run; gratitude made you come back.

He drove until the hills of Hollowmere sank below the horizon behind him, and only then did he let himself cry, one-handed, at seventy miles an hour, for his brother, for himself, for the town he was abandoning to whatever it had agreed to forget.

Behind him, unremarked by anyone, the hum followed the road for three miles before the ground gave it up.

And in Hollowmere, in a rented house on Cordwainer Street, Freya Fairweather woke at 5:47 a.m. from a dream of her mother's ledgers, certain—with a certainty she would not be able to explain—that someone had just tried to open the door, and that the trying had changed something. She lay in the gray light, listening to the hum beneath her floorboards, and thought: *it knows we're close now.*

She got up and put the coffee on, and began, without quite deciding to, packing a bag.
