## 8. Ink Over Names

The archive room of the Hollowmere Historical Society occupied what had once been the meetinghouse's coal cellar, and it retained the temperament of one: cold in July, cold in January, and lit by four bulbs that buzzed like trapped insects. Miriam Vance had worked down there for thirty-one years, long enough that the cold had stopped registering as discomfort and become simply the temperature of truth. Documents, she believed, kept better in the cold. So did certain kinds of knowing.

She had the parish registers out on two long tables when Freya arrived, arranged in chronological order, each volume weighted open with cloth-covered bricks. The sight of it stopped Freya in the doorway. It looked less like research than like a field hospital—rows of the wounded laid out and tended.

"You got my message, then," Miriam said, without looking up. She was a small woman, mid-sixties, with white hair cut close as a hedge and reading glasses pushed up into it like a second set of eyes. She wore a cardigan the color of wet slate and fingerless gloves, and she handled the old registers the way other people handled infants.

"You said you'd found something about the choir."

"I said I'd found something about the *names*." Miriam finally looked up, and Freya saw that she had been crying—not recently, not dramatically, but in the way of someone who had been crying at intervals all morning and expected to continue. "Sit down, Miss Fairweather. This is going to take some telling."

The tables were crowded with more than registers. There were photocopies of the founding congregation photograph Freya had seen the week before—the one where every choir member's name had been inked over—and Miriam had done something to the copies, photographed them under raking light at different angles, printed enlargements. Under oblique light, the black ink showed its secrets. It was newer than the photograph. You could see the brush of it, the deliberate, careful strokes of someone working slowly, someone who had wanted the obliteration to be thorough but not violent. And beneath the ink, in perhaps a dozen of the forty faces, the ghost of the original lettering rose up like a bruise through makeup: the faint ridges of a pen pressed hard enough to emboss the photographic paper.

"I've been an archivist for three decades," Miriam said. "You develop instincts. You learn what a document is hiding before you know what it's hiding." She tapped one of the enlargements. "This ink was applied within ten years of the photograph being taken. Same batch, same hand, all forty names. One person sat down with a bottle of ink and a fine brush and erased forty people in an afternoon. And they were *careful*, Miss Fairweather. They didn't blot. They didn't scratch. They painted. That's not vandalism. That's record-keeping."

"Whose hand?"

"That's the first thing." Miriam pulled a ledger from the stack at her elbow—the Ash family's early commercial accounts, on deposit with the society since 1938 and, she admitted with a thin smile, never once requested by a living soul. She turned it to a page near the front and laid a transparency of a signature over the photograph of the inked names. The letterforms matched. The particular way the capital A broke at the waist, the small, cramped *h* that looked like it was trying to hide behind its neighbor.

"Ezekiel Ash," Freya said.

"Ezekiel Ash. Founder, first keeper, and the man who signed the town's original charter." Miriam removed the transparency. "He inked them out himself. In his own accounting ledger, on the facing page, there's an entry for 'ink, lamp black, one pint, for the society's use.' He expensed it, Miss Fairweather. He erased forty human beings and charged the materials to the town."

The cellar's cold settled into Freya's shoulders. She had spent seven years telling herself stories about raccoons, about settling foundations, about pipes. She was very good at stories. But she had also spent seven years learning to read a scene—what was disturbed, what was untouched, what had been *arranged*—and everything on these two tables was arranged. "And the death records?"

"Ah." Miriam drew the first parish register toward her, the oldest, its leather scuffed to suede at the corners. "Here's where I need you to look with your own eyes, because I've been staring at this since Tuesday and I want to make sure I'm not mad."

The register was open to the burial records for the years following the town's founding. Hollowmere's parish had kept meticulous accounts—births, marriages, deaths, each in its own hand, the deaths recorded with cause and date and the officiant's initials. Freya ran her finger down the columns. Farmers. Infants. A drowning in the mere itself. An elderly woman, "decline of age." The ordinary sediment of a small town's grief, laid down year over year.

Then, beginning in a particular autumn—the same autumn as the congregation photograph, the same autumn as the prosperity that had built the grange hall and the mill and the fine houses on Chapel Hill—the deaths thinned. Not stopped. Thinned. And interleaved among the remaining entries, in the margins, in a hand Freya now recognized, were small annotations.

Miriam had flagged them with slips of paper. Freya turned to the first.

*No body. No service. Entry made for the record only.*

The next: *Buried by the town's kindness, location unrecorded.*

The next: *Removed to the undercroft. See charter.*

"See charter," Freya read aloud. "What charter?"

"The founding charter has a sealed annex. It's in the county vault in Harrowgate. I wrote for a copy in 1994." Miriam's mouth went thin. "The reply came from a law firm that no longer exists, informing me the annex was 'closed to inquiry in perpetuity.' In perpetuity. That's not legal language. That's liturgical language."

Freya turned pages. The annotations continued, growing sparser as the years went on, until they stopped altogether—and that was the thing that made her hands go still on the paper. Because the deaths didn't stop. The *records* did.

"Here." Miriam opened a second register, decades later, and laid beside it a census transcription. "I've pulled every name from the inked-out choir list—what I could recover from the embossing, thirteen names so far—and run them against everything. Parish books, census rolls, property transfers, the mill's employment ledgers, the school registers, the newspaper." She dealt the documents out like a losing hand. "Take Sarah Merrow. Choir alto, age thirty-one in the photograph. She appears in the census the year of the founding. The year after, she appears nowhere. Not in the parish. Not in the census. Not in a marriage record, a death record, a land sale. She doesn't move away—there's no arrival record anywhere within a hundred miles. She doesn't die. She *stops*."

"People fall out of records all the time," Freya said, and heard her own voice doing what it always did, building the raccoon story, the settling-foundation story. "Women especially. They marry, they—"

"Sarah Merrow doesn't marry. Her family stays in Hollowmere for three more generations—her brother's line runs right through the town to the present day—and in none of those generations does anyone mention her. Not in a will, not in a family Bible, not in a letter. I found her brother's diary in our own collection. He mentions his horse by name eleven times. He never once writes his sister's name." Miriam's voice didn't rise. It got flatter, which was worse. "You know what that is, Miss Fairweather? That's not grief. Grief writes names. That's a hole. Someone cut her out of the world and the world closed over her like water."

Freya looked down at the thirteen recovered names, Miriam's careful penciled reconstructions. *Sarah Merrow. Tobias Fen. Abigail Crane. Josiah Pell.* Ordinary names. The names of people who had stood in a row holding sheet music, forty of them, while the founding congregation beamed behind them.

"Every founder got rich," Miriam said. "You know that. Ezekiel Ash, the Pells—no relation to Josiah, or so they claimed—the Weatheralls, the Crane line—no relation to Abigail either, and isn't *that* a coincidence I'd like to examine someday. Every founding family's fortune dates to within two years of the photograph. And every one of those fortunes has a gap in its accounting, a period where the ledgers go from struggling to flush with no visible cause. No crop breakthrough. No invention. No lucky strike." She looked up, and her eyes behind the pushed-up glasses were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "I've been an archivist for thirty-one years, and I always assumed the founding families were simply lucky, and the rest of us were simply not. That's what luck looks like from underneath. It looks like other people's arithmetic."

"And now?"

"And now I've seen the arithmetic." Miriam gathered the census pages and squared their edges with a small, furious precision. "The prosperity began in the same season the names stopped. Forty choir members. Thirty-nine niches in a door that only opens from the inside. And a town that has been *comfortable* for a hundred and sixty years, comfortable in a way that no honest mill town has any right to be, comfortable through floods and depressions and blights that ruined every town around us." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "I used to give tours, you know. 'Hollowmere: blessed by fortune since its founding.' I said that. With a little flag in my hand. To schoolchildren."

Freya thought of her mother's ledgers upstairs in her own house, the identical handprints, the torn pages, four entries spanning sixty years and every one followed by a page ripped out at the spine. She thought of the notation on the door, the carved phrases she had almost recognized, the way the recognition had felt like a word on the tip of a tongue belonging to someone else. *This was inherited, not discovered.* Her mother had known. Her mother's mother, probably. A line of women walking through Hollowmere with their eyes carefully down, seeing everything, recording everything, tearing out the parts that couldn't be borne.

"Miriam," she said. "The annotations in the parish register. 'Removed to the undercroft.' Who wrote them? The parish clerk wouldn't—"

"The parish clerk in that decade was Ambrose Weatherall." Miriam's smile was terrible. "Founding family. And his annotations get more frequent, and then they stop—right around the time the ink went onto the photograph. As if for a while, someone in the town's machinery was still trying to keep an honest record. Still writing *no body, no service* in the margin like a protest. And then even that was too much, and they stopped writing anything at all, and that's when the names started disappearing from everywhere." She closed the register gently, the way you close a coffin. "The ink came last, Miss Fairweather. First they stopped dying in the record. Then they stopped living in it. The ink on the photograph was just the town catching up to what had already been done."

The bulbs buzzed. Somewhere above them, through the floor, Freya felt it before she heard it—the faintest vibration in the soles of her boots, a hum she had been hearing for seven years and telling stories about for seven years. She watched Miriam, and saw the older woman go still, her head tilting a degree, her hand flattening on the closed register as if to hold it down against a wind that wasn't blowing.

"You hear it too," Freya said.

"I've heard it my whole life." Miriam took off her glasses and polished them on her cardigan with great thoroughness. "My grandmother sang alto, you know. Not in the founding choir—in the church choir, sixty years ago. She had a voice like a warm room. And near the end, when she was losing her mind to age, she used to stand at her window at night and sing harmony with something. I'd ask her what she was singing with, and she'd say—" Miriam's hands stopped moving. "She'd say, *the ones who were counted out, dear. Someone has to keep their part warm.*"

The cold of the coal cellar had gotten into Freya's bones, or something had. She looked at the thirteen names in Miriam's careful pencil. *Sarah Merrow, who stopped. Tobias Fen, who stopped.* Forty people who had stood in a row with sheet music in their hands, willing voices, and been spent like a pint of lamp black, expensed to the town.

"I need the full list," Freya said. "Every name you can recover. And everything the society has on the founding families' finances—the gap years. All of it."

"Why?" Miriam asked, though her face said she already knew, and approved.

"Because my mother kept ledgers on this town for forty years and tore out the pages that mattered, and I am done with torn pages." Freya heard her own voice come out flat and certain, a voice she didn't entirely recognize—her mother's voice, maybe, or the voice her mother had been about to find. "The founders wrote the arithmetic. It's time somebody audited it."

Miriam looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached beneath the table and brought up a box—cardboard, unremarkable, the kind the society used for duplicate newspapers—and set it down between them with both hands, like something that might go off.

"I started pulling those files the day after you photographed that door," she said. "I told myself it was routine re-cataloguing." Her mouth twisted. "Thirty-one years, Miss Fairweather, and I never once went looking. Do you understand what that means? The ink didn't just go on the photograph. It went on all of us. We were taught not to look, and we called it peace."

She lifted the box's flaps. Inside, on top, was a single folder, and on the folder, in Miriam's neat archivist's pencil, was a label. Freya read it and felt the floor of the world tilt a half-degree.

The label said: *Fairweather — see also: torn pages.*

"My grandmother kept it," Miriam said quietly. "Then my mother. Then me. None of us ever opened it. We were waiting, I think, though we'd have shot anyone who suggested it." She slid it across the table. "Your family didn't just know, Miss Fairweather. Somebody gave us instructions for what to do if a Fairweather ever came asking. And the first line says we were to give you this *before* you asked for anything else."

Freya didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"A letter," Miriam said. "Dated 1961. Addressed to 'the daughter who finds the door.'"

* * *

The letter was one page, written in ink gone brown, in a hand Freya knew—knew the way you know a voice through a wall, the way you know footsteps on stairs. Her grandmother's. She had three of her grandmother's recipes copied out in that same hand, tucked in her own cookbook, and she had never once noticed the resemblance until this moment, holding a page that began:

*If you are reading this, you have seen the door, and you have seen the names, and you are angry. Be angry. It is fuel. But hear an old woman first.*

*We did not tear the pages to hide the truth from you. We tore them to hide the truth from the town. A record can be seized. A ledger can be burned. But a thing torn out and carried in the body cannot be confiscated. Your mother carries her pages the way I carry mine. You will carry yours. This is what it means to be a Fairweather: we are the archive that walks.*

*The names on the photograph are not the whole list. There were more, before the photograph, and there have been more since. The town pays in voices and calls the payment fortune. Count what is missing, child. The missing are the ledger.*

*And when you have counted—when you cannot bear the number—remember that we tore our pages because we believed no one could win. Not because we believed no one should try.*

*The difference matters. It is the whole difference.*

*— E.F., 1961*

Freya read it twice. The cellar hummed beneath her feet, and this time she did not tell herself a story about pipes. She looked up at Miriam, who was watching her with the expression of a woman who had waited thirty-one years and a lifetime before that to see this exact face.

"The missing are the ledger," Freya said.

"Yes."

"Then we count." She folded the letter along its old creases and put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where a heartbeat could keep it. "Every name. Every gap year. Every fortune with a hole in it. I want to know the full price of what this town bought—and I want to know who's still paying."

Miriam was already reaching for a fresh pencil, and there was something in her face Freya had never seen there before, something that thirty-one years of cold cellars and careful neutrality had not managed to kill: hope, arriving late and furious, like a choir member who had finally been called.

They worked until the bulbs buzzed and dimmed, and it was only when Freya carried the first box of files up the stairs into the ordinary gray afternoon that she noticed the last line Miriam had penciled on the box's lid, small, almost apologetic, an archivist's note in the margin of a crime:

*Thirteen names recovered. Twenty-seven still unreadable.*

*The ink is still wet.*
