## 9. What the Town Owes

The invitation came on cream cardstock, delivered by hand.

Freya found it wedged in the screen door of her rented house on Corben Street when she got back from the pit at noon—no stamp, no envelope, just the card itself, weighted down against the wind by a smooth gray stone that had been placed there deliberately. She recognized the gesture for what it was before she'd finished reading the first line. Old money didn't mail things. Old money *arrived*, and expected you to feel the arrival in your bones.

*Mr. Cornelius Ash requests the pleasure of your company at Fairholme, Saturday next, four o'clock. A matter of mutual professional interest.*

*Mutual professional interest.* Freya turned the card over twice, looking for a joke, a threat, anything legible beneath the courtesy. There was nothing. Just the Ash family crest embossed at the bottom—a stylized meetinghouse with a bell tower, rendered in ink so dark it looked wet—and beneath it, in small italic script, the family motto: *We Keep What We Are Given.*

She stood on her own porch for a long moment, holding the card up to the October light, and thought about what she knew of Cornelius Ash, which was what everyone in Hollowmere knew and no one said aloud: that he was sixty-one years old and had never held an election he hadn't already won; that his family's name appeared on the library, the clinic, the bandstand, and half the headstones in the old cemetery; that when people spoke of him they lowered their voices without noticing they were doing it, the way you do near a sleeping dog or an open grave.

He was not yet mayor. He was mayor-*select*, which in Hollowmere meant something older than elections—the town council simply confirmed what the name had decided generations ago, and everyone smiled about it, and no one could quite explain why.

Freya set the card on her kitchen table, propped it against the salt shaker, and spent the evening trying very hard not to think about it. She failed. By midnight she had read it eleven times, and by two she had stopped pretending the stone on her doorstep was just a stone. It was the same gray as the dressed slab Dale Okafor's backhoe had bitten into twelve feet under the old meetinghouse lot. The same gray as the door with its bolt that only worked from the inside.

They knew she'd been down there. Of course they knew. They probably knew before she did.

---

Saturday came in cold and clear, the kind of autumn day that made Hollowmere look like the postcard version of itself—the sugar maples along Ashbury Road burning orange and red, the white steeples sharp against a sky scrubbed blue, the whole town arranged like something being offered for sale. Freya drove out to Fairholme in the van, because the van was who she was, and if this meeting was going to be what she suspected it was, she wanted to arrive as herself.

Fairholme sat on forty acres at the town's northern edge, behind wrought-iron gates that stood open—another courtesy, another message: *you were expected*. The house itself was Georgian brick, three stories, built in stages across two centuries by men who kept adding wings the way other families added children. Ivy climbed the east face in disciplined rows. Everything about the place was maintained, manicured, *kept*—and Freya thought, pulling up the gravel drive, that keeping things seemed to be the family business in more ways than one.

A housekeeper met her at the door and led her through rooms that smelled of lemon oil and old paper, past portraits of Ashes going back to a founder with a high starched collar and eyes painted slightly too close together. The founder's plaque beneath him read *Ezekiel Ash, 1797–1861, First Keeper of Hollowmere's Covenant.*

*First Keeper.* Not mayor. Not founder. Keeper.

Freya filed that away where she filed everything now—in the growing drawer of things she couldn't unsee.

Cornelius Ash received her in a study lined floor to ceiling with books whose spines had never been cracked. He rose from behind a desk of dark walnut to shake her hand, and his grip was dry and warm and exactly firm enough, the handshake of a man who had practiced warmth the way other men practiced golf.

"Miss Fairweather." His voice was soft, unhurried, faintly amused, as though the world were a mildly clever joke he'd heard long ago but still appreciated. "Thank you for coming. May I say you're exactly what I pictured? Practical. Unpretentious." He gestured her into a leather chair angled toward a cold fireplace. "Can I offer you tea? Coffee? Something stronger? It's never too early in Hollowmere."

"Coffee's fine."

"Ruth will bring it." He rang a small bell, sat back down, folded his hands. For a moment he simply looked at her, and Freya let herself be looked at, cataloguing him the way she'd catalogue any animal she was called in to deal with: silver hair combed straight back, cardigan the color of oatmeal over a pressed shirt, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead. A grandfather from a catalog. A man built entirely out of harmlessness.

And underneath it, in the way he'd positioned his chair between her and the window, in the way his eyes went once—just once—to the van visible through the glass, then back: a man doing arithmetic.

"You've been busy," he said pleasantly. "The Okafor site. My great-great-grandfather's meetinghouse lot, actually—though I suppose the historical society has told you all their romantic stories about it." He smiled. "Miriam Vance does love her photographs."

"The door," Freya said. "Sealed from the inside. You'll want to tell me it's a cistern access or an ice house. I'd rather you didn't waste both our time."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. The look of a man checking a box he'd already ticked.

"No," he agreed mildly. "I don't suppose I will." He leaned back, laced his fingers over his cardigan, and became, visibly, a different sort of person—not warmer, but more *economical*, the pleasantries shed like a coat left on a hook. "Let me tell you what I know, Miss Fairweather, and you can decide how much of it matters. In 1798, a group of families settled this valley. Within five years they were the wealthiest community per capita in the state. No famine touched them. No fire. No fever. Their livestock bred true, their wells ran sweet, their debts paid themselves. People noticed. People always notice. But nobody ever proved anything, because there was nothing to prove—only luck, and luck is the one crime without fingerprints."

He paused while the housekeeper set down coffee, thanked her by name, waited until the door closed.

"Every generation since, the town has prospered beyond what its geography should allow. And every generation, my family has... attended to the arrangement that makes that possible. It's a responsibility. An inheritance, really, like the house." He spread his hands, apologetic, reasonable. "You've seen the door. You understand there's something under the old lot that requires management. That's all this is. Management."

"Management," Freya repeated.

"Stewardship, if you prefer. The word doesn't matter." He sipped nothing—he hadn't taken a drink himself; his cup sat untouched, a prop. "What matters is this. The excavation has destabilized the site. Any competent engineer would agree the subsurface conditions under the meetinghouse lot are compromised—voids, settling, water intrusion. Structurally unsound. A hazard to the public." He slid a folder across the desk. It was thick, professionally bound, a title page in clean serif type: *GEOTECHNICAL ASSESSMENT — FAIRWEATHER ENVIRONMENTAL SERVICES.*

Her company's name. Her actual letterhead, reproduced perfectly.

"I took the liberty of having my people draft it," Ash said. "You need only review it, amend whatever you like, sign it, and submit it to the county. The finding triggers an emergency abatement order. Concrete pour, fourteen feet, reinforced. The lot is filled, the road is closed, the matter is closed. You'd be paid consulting fees of—" he named a figure that would have covered three years of her usual contracts, "—payable immediately, with a retainer thereafter for ongoing monitoring. Ongoing work, Miss Fairweather. Steady work. The kind a woman in your line rarely gets to count on."

He said it gently. That was the worst part. There was no menace in it at all—only the calm of a man offering a fair price for a fair thing, in a transaction he had clearly overseen many times before, with many hands before hers.

"Your mother," he added, softer still, "was offered the same arrangement. More than once."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, a crow called twice and thought better of it.

"She declined too, I assume," Freya said.

"She *delayed*," Ash corrected, and there was the first hairline crack in the pleasantness, a flicker of something tired and old moving behind his face. "Delaying is its own decision, eventually. But we aren't here to discuss your mother." He tapped the folder once, lightly, almost affectionately. "We're here to discuss what the town owes you. Because that's how I'd frame this offer, and I want you to hear me clearly: I'm not threatening you. I'm *compensating* you. You found something disturbing. You've lost sleep over it—I can see it, you have your mother's face when she was tired. This contract lets you walk away whole. Paid. Respected. The town keeps its prosperity, your neighbors keep their livelihoods, and everyone goes on. That isn't corruption, Miss Fairweather. That's maintenance. Every functioning thing in this valley runs on someone agreeing, quietly, to maintain it."

Freya looked at the folder. Her own name typed neatly on the cover. Seven years of scraped-by contracts, dead mice, wasp nests, raccoons in attics—seven years of being the woman people called last and paid late—and here, at last, was abundance, offered in exchange for one signature and fourteen feet of concrete.

She thought about the greasy handprints eight feet up the grange hall wall. About the rubbing in her glovebox, the notation she almost recognized. About her mother's ledgers with their torn pages, and the word *Keeper* on a portrait plaque upstairs, and thirty-nine names inked over in Miriam's photographs, erased so thoroughly that even the erasure had been tidied.

"Who were they?" she asked. "The choir. Under the founders' names in the congregation photos."

Ash's teacup might as well have shattered, for all the stillness that went through him. It lasted half a second. Then the cardigan settled back over the arithmetic.

"I'm afraid I don't take your meaning."

"Thirty-nine people," Freya said. "Ink over every name. Your family's been paying for this town out of a fund nobody audits. I want to know what the currency was."

For a long moment Cornelius Ash regarded her, and Freya watched him arrive at a decision the way you watch weather change—nothing dramatic, just pressure shifting, light going flat. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone entirely, and what remained was worse: courtesy with nothing inside it, like a furnished room after the tenant's moved out.

"What the town owes them," he said quietly, "is everything. And what they owed the town was their voices. Both accounts were settled a very long time ago, by better men than either of us, and the settlement has kept this valley fed for two hundred years. You may find that monstrous. I've had time to find it *necessary*, and necessity, given enough generations, starts to look like love." He stood, smoothed his cardigan, and slid the folder the final inch toward her. "Take the contract. Think it over if you must—but not for long. Sites like that don't stay stable, and neither do arrangements. Sign it, pour the concrete, and Hollowmere will take care of you for the rest of your life. Decline..." He smiled, and it reached his eyes without warming them. "Decline, and I'd advise you to diversify your client base. Towns are particular about who they trust with their foundations."

Freya stood. She picked up the folder—his eyebrows lifted, hopeful—and carried it to the cold fireplace, and set it on the grate, and left it there.

"That's a signed original," Ash said, mild as milk.

"It's a lie with my name on it," Freya said. "I deal in infestations, Mr. Ash. I know what happens when you seal something in instead of dealing with it. You don't fix the problem. You just make sure it comes back through the walls." She walked to the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, and gave him the only honest thing she'd brought. "Your family didn't keep this town safe. They kept it *fed*. And I think somewhere in those books you never read, you know the difference."

She let herself out. The housekeeper showed her to the door without meeting her eyes, and the wrought-iron gates stood open behind her, and the drive unwound through the burning maples toward a town that looked, in the afternoon light, exactly as prosperous as it had ever been.

It wasn't until she was halfway home that she felt it—the change, subtle and total, like a current shifting under still water. The man mowing his lawn on Delver Street cut his engine as she passed and didn't resume until she'd gone by. The girl at the gas station who'd chatted with her for seven years rang up her coffee without a word, staring at the register. On Mill Lane, two women talking on a porch fell silent mid-sentence, watched the van pass, and resumed in lower voices.

Nobody was rude. Nobody was cruel. That was the terrible craftsmanship of it. Hollowmere didn't turn on you with torches; it turned on you the way a body turns on a splinter—quietly, cellularly, everywhere at once—until you understood that the warmth you'd lived inside for seven years had never been yours. It had been extended to you, conditionally, by people who could withdraw it with a phone call or a nod, and had just done so.

By dusk, three clients had canceled. By nine, a fourth had left a message saying her account had been "handled elsewhere."

Freya sat in her kitchen with the lights off, watching the dark windows of the houses across the street, and understood that she had crossed some line drawn before she was born, by men in starched collars, and that the line had been waiting for her all along.

On the table, the cream cardstock caught the streetlight. *We Keep What We Are Given.*

Under the floorboards, faint and patient, the hum began to rise.

* * *

She was still sitting there at midnight when headlights swept the kitchen wall—slow, deliberate, twice around the block, then gone. Ten minutes later, something struck the front door: not a knock, a tap, polite as a servant.

The porch was empty except for the note, weighted down by the same kind of smooth gray stone.

Block letters, cut from newsprint, glued with care:

**FINISH YOUR MOTHER'S WORK OR LEAVE.**

Freya stood in the cold with the note in her hands, and above the tree line the new moon's absence made the sky feel like a held breath, and behind her, under the floorboards, the hum swelled—forty voices strong now, harmonizing, waiting—as if the thing beneath the town had heard the doors closing against her and was opening its own.
