## 21. Recruiting Ash

Freya had rehearsed the sentence all the way across town, and it still felt like a bone caught in her throat.

*Join the choir.*

She turned it over on the drive up Ash Hill, past the iron gates with their wheat-sheaf finials, past the oaks that had been old when the meetinghouse was new. It was absurd. It was worse than absurd—it was asking a man to stand in a room with the thing his family had spent two centuries feeding, caging, and lying about, and sing to it. She might as well ask him to hand over the deed to Hollowmere.

Which was, she admitted as she parked beneath the porte-cochère, more or less what she was doing.

The house was larger than it needed to be and colder than it should have been in October. A housekeeper took her coat without meeting her eyes—everyone in this town had stopped meeting her eyes since the van burned—and Freya was left standing in a front hall paneled in dark walnut, beneath a portrait of a man whose jawline had been reproduced faithfully down six generations of Ashes. Cornelius had the same jaw. The same way of looking at you slightly above your head, as though you were a horizon he'd already surveyed.

He came down the stairs in a cardigan the color of wet slate. That was the thing about Cornelius Ash that made him dangerous, Freya thought. Not menace. Comfort. He looked like a man who fixed fences and remembered birthdays.

"Miss Fairweather." He didn't offer his hand. "I'll admit I'm surprised. I expected lawyers by now, not a house call."

"I'm not here about the van."

"No," he said. "I didn't think you were." He led her into a study lined with books that had clearly never been read, spines uncracked, dust in the gutters of the shelves. Prosperity's library. He poured himself something amber from a decanter and did not offer her any. "You've come to threaten me. Everyone does eventually. I find it goes better if we skip to the part where you tell me what you want."

"I want you to sing."

The glass stopped halfway to his mouth. For one unguarded second—the first she'd ever seen on him—Cornelius Ash looked genuinely lost. Then the jaw set, the horizon-look returned, and he laughed, a short dry sound with nothing in it.

"Sing."

"Four verses. In the undercroft, before the new moon. With me and Miriam Vance and Dale Okafor and Ruth Ellery and a couple of teenagers who smell like spray paint. Willing voices, Mr. Ash. That's the requirement. That's always been the requirement—that's the part your family has been quietly editing out of the contract for two hundred years."

"Sit down," he said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit *down*." And there it was, just for a moment, under the cardigan: the keeper's voice, the voice that had been giving orders to this town since before it had a name. Freya stayed standing. Something flickered behind his eyes—not anger. Recognition. He knew that tone because he'd heard it fail before, or feared hearing it fail, and the fear moved through his face like weather.

"Cornelius." She used his first name deliberately, watched it land like a slap. "Your binding is failing. You know it. Your men flooded my undercroft and stole my papers because you know it. You're holding the town hostage over a hole in the ground, and every day you wait, the thing behind that door gets closer to walking out *uninvited*. When that happens, no contract saves you. No concrete pour saves you. There is exactly one door out of this for anyone, and it opens onto four verses sung by people who mean them."

"You don't understand what you're asking."

"I understand perfectly. I'm asking you to stand next to the people your family erased and give them back their voices."

"They weren't *my* family's—" He stopped. The glass trembled, very slightly, and he set it down on the mantel with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something when his hands have betrayed him once already and he won't permit a second time.

Freya reached into her bag.

She had debated for three days about whether to bring the ledger. Dale had argued against it—"You show that man anything, he'll burn it like he burned your van"—and Miriam had argued for it with the quiet ferocity of a woman who had spent thirty years in an archive watching powerful families decide what history got to keep. In the end Freya had gone with Miriam, mostly because Miriam had been right about everything so far, and being right was starting to feel less like comfort and more like a job.

It wasn't the founder's ledger itself. That was still missing, stolen with the rubbing. This was Miriam's transcription—photographs, line drawings, every signature and entry copied out in archival pencil on acid-free paper. But the photographs were of the original pages, and the pages were unmistakable.

"The historical society has a complete run of parish registers," Freya said, laying the folder on the desk between them. "Land grants. Meetinghouse minutes. Subscription rolls for the bell. Your family shows up in all of them, Cornelius. Every document this town produced between 1740 and 1760, there's an Ash at the bottom of it. Except one."

"Don't."

"Except the choir roll." She opened the folder. Turned it to face him. "The founding congregation kept a register of its singers. Forty names. Thirty-nine of them inked over sometime in the 1760s—same ink, same hand, same week, judging by the oxidation. One name left blank. And then, on the next page, in different ink, different hand—a new title invented for the occasion. *Keeper.*"

"I said *don't*—"

"And the keeper's name is signed twice. Once as witness. Once as office." She turned the page. "Ezra Ash. First of the name. Look at the date, Cornelius. Look at the date on his signature in the choir roll, and look at the date on his appointment as keeper."

He didn't want to look. She watched him not look—watched his gaze slide off the paper the way water slides off wax, watched him perform the small muscular refusal that his family had apparently been passing down along with the jawline. But the dates were simple. Anyone could do the arithmetic. Three weeks. Ezra Ash had signed the choir roll, and three weeks later he had signed himself into an office that hadn't existed until he needed it to.

"He upgraded himself," Freya said. "That's all a keeper is. A choir member who decided the singing was beneath him and found someone else to do it. Your whole dynasty, Cornelius—your land, your mill rights, your name on the library wing—it starts with a man who was supposed to be standing in that room singing, and walked out, and made the door his job instead so he'd never have to go near it again."

"You don't know that."

"It's written down. In your ancestor's handwriting. Miriam can show you the ink analysis if you want it."

"That is not—" His voice cracked, actually cracked, on the second word, and he turned away from her toward the window, toward the oaks and the valley below where the town lay in its autumn colors like something spilled. "You don't know what it was. What it *is*. You think it's some sad ghost that wants to go home. My family has fed that thing for two hundred years. Do you understand? Fed it. People. Whole people, taken out of the world, and the world let us, because the world *prospered*, and every Ash who sat in this chair told himself the same story: that someone had to hold the pen, that if we didn't, someone worse would. Ezra wrote that. I've read it. I've read every page they left me, Miss Fairweather, I have read the ledger until I could recite it, and I am telling you that whatever you think is behind that door, it is not something you make peace with by *singing to it*."

"Then why are your hands shaking?"

Silence. The clock on the mantel ticked with the fat, self-satisfied tick of expensive machinery.

Freya softened her voice, which cost her something; she hadn't known she had a soft voice left. Seven years of professional flatness, seven years of telling people *it's raccoons*, and now she stood in the house of the man who had burned her livelihood and heard herself speak gently.

"Because you've read the ledger," she said. "And somewhere in all those pages, you already know I'm right. You know what the inked-out names mean. You've done the arithmetic too—you've just never let yourself carry the answer around in daylight." She came around the desk, slowly, and turned the folder so the choir roll faced the window, faced him. "Thirty-nine names, Cornelius. Not criminals. Not offerings. *Singers.* Farmers and midwives and a cooper and two sisters who shared a line because the clerk ran out of room. They agreed to stand in a circle and sing a starving thing calm, and your ancestor stood in that circle with them—for three weeks—and then he wrote himself a new job."

"Ezra was protecting—"

"Ezra was *afraid*." She said it without contempt, and that, she thought, was what finally reached him—not accusation but accuracy. "So were the others, probably. Singing to that thing must have been the worst work ever asked of anyone. But the other thirty-eight stayed. They sang until it took them. He's the only one who left. And everything you are, everything this town's prosperity is built on, comes from that one act of leaving. You're not the heir to a covenant, Cornelius. You're the heir to a desertion."

His shoulders had changed. She saw it happen—the slow collapse of a posture held for decades, the cardigan suddenly looking less like comfort and more like camouflage. He braced both hands on the windowsill and hung his head, and for a long moment the most powerful man in Hollowmere looked exactly like what he was: a middle-aged man alone in a big cold house, holding a debt he'd never audited.

"My daughter," he said, without turning. "Sophie. She's been going to that grange hall. To your rehearsals." A sound that was almost a laugh. "My own daughter. I had men follow her. I told myself it was for her safety." He straightened. Turned. And Freya saw that the horizon-look was gone, and what was underneath it was worse and better: a man looking directly at another person for perhaps the first time in his adult life. "If I do this. If I stand in that hole and sing—if I do the one thing six generations of my family were specifically designed never to do—what happens to the town?"

"The town survives. The town was always going to survive. That was never what the binding was for."

"Then what—"

"The binding was for the *fortunes*." She let it sit. "Yours especially. The ledger itemizes it, Cornelius. Mill rights against a name. Land grants against a name. Your family's prosperity isn't a side effect of the binding. It's the *payment schedule*."

He looked at the folder for a long time. Then he crossed the room, took the decanter, and poured the entire measure of amber liquor into the potted fern beside the desk—an old, automatic gesture, a man declining the crutch because he could feel how badly he was about to need his legs.

"If I say yes," he said quietly, "it won't be because I believe you. It will be because I read Ezra's signature three weeks apart, and I cannot stop reading it, and I understand at last why my father drank, and his father, and why none of them ever slept in the east bedrooms." He picked up the folder. Held it like something hot. "One condition. When this is over—whatever happens down there—the ledger goes public. All of it. Every name, every payment. If my family bought this town with people, then this town gets the receipt."

"Miriam's already building the archive," Freya said. "You can be the first donor."

Something moved through his face that might, in a better life, have been relief. He nodded once, jerkily, a man signing something in his own blood.

"I can't promise I'll be able to sing. I haven't—I don't think I've sung since Sunday school."

"Nobody in that room can sing," Freya said. "Dale hums flat. The teenagers treat pitch as a suggestion. We're not recruiting voices, Cornelius. We're recruiting willingness. It's the only currency the rite accepts."

He almost smiled. It died young, but it lived.

"There's something else," he said. "Something you should have before you go back down there. My family kept one page separate from the rest. Hidden even from the keepers—my father showed me where it was the night before he died, and made me swear on the name that I'd only surrender it if..." He stopped. Swallowed. "He never finished the sentence. *If what*, he never said. But he looked at me like a man handing off a live grenade, and I've carried it for eleven years, and I think—I think your mother was looking for it when she died."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly. Freya heard her own pulse.

"The final verse," she said.

"I don't know what it is. I've never read it. I was too much of a coward, and then I was too much of a keeper, and those turned out to be the same cowardice wearing different coats." He set the folder down and rested his hand flat on it, as if taking an oath on it, which in a way he was. "Come back tomorrow. In daylight. I'll open the safe, and I'll give you six generations of my family's last secret, and after that, Miss Fairweather, God help us both, I'll expect a rehearsal schedule."

Freya walked out through the cold hall, past the portrait of the jawline, and paused at the door to look back. Cornelius Ash stood at his study window with the transcribed choir roll pressed to his chest like a wound dressing, staring down at the town his family had purchased, and even from across the hall she could see his lips moving—soundlessly, clumsily, a man trying to remember how the music went.

Outside, the wind had come up off the hollow, and beneath it—faint, patient, threading through the oaks like light through leaves—she could hear the hum. It sounded, for the first time in seven years, almost hopeful.

Three days to the new moon. Two days to the verse. And somewhere under the hill, forty niches waited in the dark, thirty-nine full and one empty, while the thing behind the stone door began, very softly, to practice.
