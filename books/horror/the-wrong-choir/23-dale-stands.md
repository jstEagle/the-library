## 23. Dale Stands

The shotgun had belonged to his father, and before that to his grandfather, and in all that time it had never once been fired at anything that could hear you coming. Dale Okafor kept it oiled anyway, out of a family habit of preparing for things he hoped would never happen, and on the last night of October he carried it down into the excavation pit behind the old meetinghouse lot and sat himself down on an overturned bucket of concrete anchors like a man settling in for a long wait at a bus stop.

The pit was twelve feet deep, floored with crushed gravel and the churned scars of backhoe treads. At its far end, where the dressed stone rose out of the earth like the shoulder of something buried alive, the door waited. Sealed from the inside. He still couldn't say those words to himself without his stomach doing something slow and wrong.

He had resigned eleven days ago. He had driven until the gas light came on somewhere past Corbett's Ferry, slept four hours in a motel parking lot with the engine running for heat, and woken up with his brother's name in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed in his sleep.

*Emmanuel.*

Emmanuel, who had drowned when they were boys, in the flooded quarry off Route 9, in water so cold it stopped your heart before your lungs finished filling. Emmanuel, who used to sing to himself while he worked because silence made him itch. Emmanuel, whose lullaby their mother had sung in a language she'd half forgotten, a melody that went up where you expected it to go down, and Dale had spent thirty-one years unable to hum it right.

He'd heard it through the door. Clear as a phone call. His brother's voice, unchanged, unaged, singing the lullaby from behind six feet of dressed stone, and Dale had walked toward it with his hands out like a man crossing a dark room toward a lamp, and he had gotten one hand on the crossbar — the bar that could only be thrown from *this* side, which meant someone inside was holding it shut, which meant everything Freya Fairweather had said was true and worse than true — before Marcus and the Ruiz brothers had dragged him back up the ladder, all three of them crying without knowing they were crying, the way men cry, like plumbing failing under pressure.

He'd left town that morning. He'd sworn on his mother's grave he'd never come back.

And now here he was, sitting on a bucket in a hole in the ground with a shotgun across his knees, waiting for Cornelius Ash's men to come and take the door away from him.

* * *

Freya had called him at noon. Not asked — told, in the flat voice she used for facts she didn't enjoy.

"They're coming tonight," she said. "Ash is moving on the site. Flood pumps, chain-link, probably a crew by morning. Once they pour concrete over that door, whatever's behind it stops being a problem we can solve and becomes a problem our grandchildren inherit."

"Then let them pour it," Dale said. "Let them bury the whole thing. That's what I wanted from day one."

"You wanted to not look at it," Freya said. "There's a difference. Burying it doesn't close the door, Dale. It just puts a lid on a pot that's already boiling. The binding fails either way — soon, my mother's notes say within weeks — and if it fails with forty feet of concrete on top, there's no rite, no return, no sending it home. There's just it, loose, in a town full of people who've been paying rent on their prosperity for two hundred years without knowing who held the lease."

Dale had sat in his truck outside a diner in Corbett's Ferry and watched a woman carry a pie across the parking lot and thought: *I don't owe these people anything. I don't even live here anymore.*

"I need one night," Freya said. "Tomorrow night we finish. Tonight I have to get the choir ready, and I can't do both. They'll come for the site after dark because Ash doesn't want witnesses, and the only witness I've got is you."

"Why me?"

"Because you already ran once," she said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible gentleness of a woman who had learned that lying to frightened people got them killed. "And you came back. That means you know exactly what you're afraid of, and you're standing in front of it anyway. That's worth more than courage, Dale. Courage doesn't know what it's walking into."

He should have hung up. He'd rehearsed hanging up during the whole drive back, during the stop at the gun shop in Millbrook where he'd bought shells he prayed he'd never need, during the hour he'd spent in the cemetery telling his mother's headstone what a damn fool her younger son was.

Instead he said, "One night."

"One night," she agreed. "Sunrise tomorrow, we come down. Whatever happens, don't touch the bar. Don't listen to anything that calls itself by a name you love. And Dale—" A pause, the first hesitation he'd ever heard from her. "You don't have to be brave. You just have to not move."

* * *

They came at 11:40, and they came loud, because Ash's men had never in their lives needed to be quiet and didn't see why tonight should change that.

Headlights swept the rim of the pit first, four trucks parked nose-in around the meetinghouse lot, and then boots on gravel, and then voices — six or seven of them, maybe more, carrying flashlights and crowbars and the particular swagger of men who had been told by an important person that what they were doing was legal, or close enough, or at least nobody would ever make them answer for it.

Dale stood up off the bucket. His knees announced themselves. He racked the shotgun, the sound rolling around the pit like a billiard ball in a tin cup, and every flashlight beam swung down and found him at once.

"Hold it right there." The voice belonged to Pete Halloran, who had played second base against Dale in high school and lost, and had apparently never forgiven the county for it. "Site's closed, Okafor. Condemned this afternoon. You're trespassing."

"Condemned by whose signature?" Dale asked. "Ash isn't mayor yet. Election's in three weeks."

"Ash signs what needs signing. You know how it works here."

"Yeah," Dale said. "I know how it works here. That's kind of the whole problem, Pete."

The beams jostled. Somewhere above, a radio crackled. Dale counted silhouettes: seven at the rim, two more drifting toward the ladder at the north end. Nine. Against one man with a pump gun and a bad knee.

"Here's what happens now," Halloran said, and his voice had the practiced patience of a man reading terms aloud. "You put the gun down, you climb out, you go home — wherever home is this week — and nobody writes anything down. Ash is offering double severance to any crew man who shows up tomorrow. Triple if you bring the paperwork from the historical society, whatever Vance gave you. This isn't personal, Dale. It's a job site."

"It's a grave," Dale said.

"It's a *pit*, Dale."

"It's a grave with a door in it, and the door locks from the inside, and you want to pour concrete on top so nobody ever has to think about what that means." Dale heard his own voice come out steady and was distantly surprised, the way you're surprised by a photograph of yourself. "I dug this hole, Pete. My machine opened it. And I'll tell you what I found down here that the historical society report left out."

"Nobody cares—"

"Forty niches cut into the walls. Thirty-nine of them with folded clothes in them. Clothes going back two hundred years. Sunday dresses, work shirts, a christening gown. Somebody's whole congregation went down a ladder into this room one at a time and never came up, and the founding families wrote it off as choir expenses." He let that sit. The flashlight beams had gone very still. "That's what you're concreting over. Not a pit. An unpaid bill."

Silence, except for the wind moving through the dead corn along the lot's edge, and beneath the wind — faint, whether real or remembered Dale could no longer tell — the hum.

Halloran shifted. "You're gonna shoot nine men over a folklore story?"

"No," Dale said. "I'm gonna stand here. That's the whole plan. I'm gonna stand here, and you're gonna have to decide, each of you, whether whatever Ash is paying is worth stepping over me to earn it. Because I'm not moving, and I'm not shooting anybody unless somebody makes me, and I have been praying since noon that nobody makes me."

"This is insane," said one of the others — young, the Voss kid, who'd only been on the crew five months. "It's one guy."

"It's one guy with a shotgun in a twelve-foot hole," Halloran said, "and the county sheriff is Ash's brother-in-law, so if this goes bad, we're the ones who write the report about how it went bad." He shone his light directly at Dale, trying to make him flinch. Dale had spent his whole life being looked through; a flashlight was nothing. "Last chance, Okafor."

And here was the thing Dale had figured out on the drive back, the thing he hadn't told Freya because she would have argued with it: he wasn't standing there for the entity, or the choir, or the erased, or even for his brother, though his brother was in it, wound through all of it like wire through cable. He was standing there because he had run once, and running had not helped. He had driven two hundred miles and the lullaby had come with him, hummed into the motel walls, rising out of the shower drain, threading through the static between country stations. You cannot outrun a sound that lives in your own skull. The only thing you can do is turn around and face the direction it's coming from and plant your feet and say: *not through me.*

His brother had gone into the quarry water to show off. Dale had stood on the bank and done nothing for four minutes — four minutes he had counted and recounted for thirty-one years — before he ran for help. Four minutes. Everything since had been interest on those four minutes.

Not tonight.

* * *

What broke the standoff wasn't the shotgun. It was the singing.

Around midnight, with Halloran's men still strung along the rim like beads on a necklace nobody wanted to wear, the hum beneath the gravel changed key. Dale felt it in his boot soles first, then in his teeth, and then — unmistakably, horribly, tenderly — it resolved into melody. Into *the* melody. Up where it should go down.

Emmanuel's voice, or the shape of it, rose through the pit floor, and this time it wasn't a lure. It wasn't calling him to the door. It was doing what Emmanuel had always done on long shifts, on hot afternoons, on the school bus: singing to keep the silence from itching. Singing the way people sing when they are scared and trying to keep the little ones from noticing.

Dale realized his own mouth was moving. He was humming along — off-key, badly, the way he'd always sung it, the way their mother used to laugh at him for, *you flatten the third note, Daley, every time* — and the voice behind the door bent itself around his wrongness, adjusted, harmonized with his mistake as if the mistake were the point.

The men on the rim heard it too. He watched it move through them, flashlight by flashlight. The Voss kid sat down abruptly on the gravel, like his legs had filed for independence. One of the Ruiz cousins crossed himself with a crowbar still in his hand. Old Tom Beckett, sixty-two years on heavy equipment, took off his hard hat, which Dale had never seen him do on a site, and stood bareheaded in the cold like a man at a funeral.

Because everyone in Hollowmere knew that tune. Nobody knew they knew it. It was in the town the way groundwater was in the town — under everything, taken for granted, never once questioned — grandmothers' songs worn smooth by generations of mouths, and hearing it rise out of the sealed dark, hearing it *harmonize*, was like hearing your own childhood call your name from a locked room.

"Pack it up," Halloran said quietly.

"We're not leaving, we haven't—"

"I said pack it *up*, Ray." Halloran's light found Dale one more time. In it, Dale saw that the foreman's face had gone the color of the dressed stone. "Okafor. You win tonight. Tomorrow there'll be twenty of us and a court order."

"Tomorrow," Dale said, "there'll be ninety singers and a door opening the right way. But sure. Bring the court order."

The trucks pulled out one by one, headlights sweeping the corn, and the last sound they left behind was gravel popping under tires and then nothing, nothing but wind, and the lullaby, fading down through the floor like water finding its level.

Dale sat back down on the bucket. His hands were shaking so hard he had to lay the shotgun flat on the gravel and press his palms against his thighs and breathe, big dumb farmer breaths, until the world stopped swimming.

He had bought them one night. That was all. One night, purchased with a pump gun he'd never have fired and a stubbornness he hadn't known he owned, and tomorrow Ash would come back with lawyers and loaders and twenty men whose fear of Cornelius Ash outweighed their fear of the dark, and the whole thing might end in concrete after all.

But that was tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow had a choir in it.

He fished his phone out of his jacket. One bar, out here at the bottom of the world. He texted Freya: *They came. They're gone. Site holds till sunrise. Tell the choir to hurry.*

Then, because the pit was cold and the night was long and the silence itched, Dale Okafor did the bravest small thing of his entire life: he started to hum. Off-key. Third note flattened, every time. And beneath him, gentle as a hand on the back of a sleeping child, something vast and starving and homesick hummed along.

At dawn, when Freya's truck crunched into the lot, she found him still sitting there — and found, scored fresh into the dressed stone beside the door, in notation he almost recognized, a single new line of music.

Someone had written down his part.
