## 34. Day Two: Ash's Counterstroke

They came at first light, while the frost was still silvering the meetinghouse lawn and the town had not yet decided what day it wanted to be.

Freya saw them from the grange hall window: six men in canvas work jackets, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who have been told the law is on their side. Two carried crowbars. One carried a clipboard, which was somehow worse. Behind them idled a white panel truck with ASH PROPERTY SERVICES stenciled on the side in a font that tried very hard to look municipal.

"They're going for the pit," Dale said. He was already at the door, shotgun broken open over his forearm like a bird he was returning to a neighbor. "Chain's not going to hold six of them."

"It doesn't have to hold," Freya said. "It just has to be seen holding."

She understood the arithmetic of it by now. Ash couldn't stop the rite with violence—not openly, not with half the town already humming fragments of the notation under their breath at the gas station, in the checkout line, in the pews of churches that hadn't sung anything worth singing in decades. What he could do was take the ground. The undercroft entrance was the only door that mattered, and doors were Freya's whole profession. She knew better than anyone that you didn't need to break a door if you could simply stand in front of it and look official.

The clipboard man reached the plywood-and-chain barrier over the excavation pit and began photographing it from several angles, as though documenting a crime scene he intended to commit.

Freya stepped out into the cold.

* * *

The morning went the way she'd known it would go, which did not make it easier to stand through.

The man with the clipboard—his name was Harlan Bexley, and Freya remembered him from her mother's ledgers, a name that appeared every few years beside invoices for "structural remediation"—read aloud from a laminated sheet about public safety hazards and unauthorized occupation of condemned property. His voice had the flat music of a man reciting words he'd been given rather than words he believed, and Freya noticed that he never once looked down into the pit. Not when he photographed the barrier. Not when his men began prying at the chain. Not even when the crowbar slipped and rang against the dressed stone below with a sound like a struck bell, low and wrong, a note that made two of the workmen flinch and step back without deciding to.

"You'll want to move," Harlan said, finally addressing her directly. He said it kindly. That was the trick of Cornelius Ash's operation—it never raised its voice. It offered you coffee while it took your house. "There's a structural assessment pending. Once the site's filled, nobody has to think about any of this again."

"Filled," Freya repeated. "With concrete."

"With concrete," Harlan agreed. "And then we can all get back to normal."

She thought about telling him there was no normal to get back to. She thought about the ledger pages spread across the grange hall floor behind her, itemized columns of names against payments, generations of prosperity balanced like a butcher's books. She thought about Ruth, inside, sitting very still with her hands open on her knees, listening to something the rest of them could only hear as a hum.

Instead she said, "Harlan. Do you know what your grandfather was paid?"

Something moved behind his face—a door opening onto a room he kept locked—and then closed again. "Move along, Ms. Fairweather."

They worked fast after that. The chain came off. The plywood came up. And then the six of them stood at the lip of the pit, looking down at the stone door with its bolt thrown from the inside, and the morning got very quiet.

Freya watched them from twenty feet back and saw the exact moment the job stopped being a job. It happened to each man differently but in the same order: first the stillness, then the swallow, then the slow turn of the head toward the man beside him, checking—*did you hear it too?*

Because the door was singing.

It had been singing since the new moon's dark began, so faintly that only the pit's acoustics gathered it into something audible. A thread of harmony rising out of the stone, patient as groundwater. Freya had stood at that edge for an hour the night before and felt it working on her like water works on a knot—not forcing, just softening, until what was tied comes undone by its own weight.

One of the workmen—the youngest, a broad-shouldered kid whose hard hat sat too big on his head—made a sound low in his throat. Not a word. A note. The same note the door was holding, or nearly, his voice finding the pitch the way a tuning fork finds a table.

"Deke." Harlan's voice cracked across the pit like a whip. "Don't."

But Deke was already humming it properly now, and the second workman had caught it, and Freya felt the thing she had been waiting two days to feel: the moment when a song stops belonging to whoever started it.

* * *

Inside the grange hall, Miriam Vance heard the humming through the walls and put down her pen.

The automatic writing lay in front of her in loose sheets, thirty-nine hands' worth of testimony funneled through her own cramped script. *We are not angry,* one page read. *We are tired of being furniture.* Another: *Tell the girl with the van that her mother's verse is true and we will hold the pitch for her.* Miriam had spent forty years in archives learning to distrust anything that arrived too easily, and here was meaning arriving like weather, everywhere at once, asking nothing but to be passed along.

Her grandmother's alto lived in those pages. She could feel it in the specific way certain sentences curved—an old woman's patience in the phrasing, a habit of leaving space where another voice might come in. Miriam had never met her grandmother, who had stopped appearing anywhere in 1933, who existed now only as inked-over lines in parish registers and a single photograph in which every face looked slightly away from the camera, as though they'd all been told to watch something just off-frame.

She rose and went to the window.

What she saw made her press her hand flat against the glass.

Cornelius Ash's daughter was walking up the meetinghouse road.

* * *

Prudence Ash had not inherited her father's talent for arriving unnoticed. She came up the hill in a red coat, no hat, her hair whipping in the wind off the ridge, carrying a school binder hugged to her chest the way children carry things they've been told to protect. She was seventeen. She walked past the panel truck. She walked past Harlan Bexley, who opened his mouth and then, remarkably, closed it. She walked to the edge of the pit, looked down at the singing door for a long moment, and then turned and faced the six men with the absolute, unarmored directness that only teenagers and the dying can manage.

"I'm here for the choir," she announced. Her voice carried. "My dad says you're all being manipulated. But I've been hearing my great-aunt singing in our walls since I was nine, and last night I finally wrote down the words, and do you know what they say?" She held up the binder. "They say *please*. Forty pages of it. That's what the manipulation is. It's *please*."

Nobody moved. Somewhere in the pit, the harmony swelled a fraction, the way a fire responds to a draft.

"Prudence." Harlan Bexley had found his voice and it came out thin. "Your father—"

"My father signed papers," Prudence said, "that say I'm collateral. I read them. They're in his study, in the green box, under the deeds. Page four. 'Issue of the keeper line, held in trust.'" She laughed, and it was an awful sound, old for her face. "I'm seventeen and I'm *collateral*, Mr. Bexley. So don't tell me what my father wants. I'm what he's spending."

Freya came forward then, slowly, and stood beside the girl at the pit's edge, and did not touch her, because this had to be hers.

"You know the notation?" Freya asked quietly.

"I know it the way you know your own phone number." Prudence wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for crying, crying anyway. "I didn't learn it. I just—have it. Like everyone does. Like it's been sitting in us the whole time waiting for someone to ask."

Behind them, Deke set his crowbar down in the frost. He did it carefully, the way you put down something you might need later, but he set it down. Then the second workman. Then a third, laying his tool across the plywood with a clatter that sounded, in the still air, almost ceremonial.

Harlan Bexley looked at his crew, at the girl in the red coat, at the door in the ground that would not stop singing, and Freya watched the clipboard in his hands become the useless object it had always been.

"We're paid through Friday," he said, to no one, to himself.

"Then you're paid through Friday either way," Freya said. "Sing or don't. But nobody fills this hole."

And Harlan Bexley—who had serviced the Ash accounts for thirty years, who had photographed barriers and filed assessments and never once looked down into the dark—looked down into the dark. Whatever he heard there, it rearranged something in him. His shoulders dropped. He set the clipboard on the plywood, squared it neatly with the edge, an archivist's gesture escaping from a foreman's body, and then, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge, he began to hum.

He was not good at it. It didn't matter. The door took his note and folded it in.

* * *

By noon, the counterstroke had failed so completely that it had inverted.

Cornelius Ash learned of it the way he learned of most disasters—by telephone, from a man whose loyalty had been purchased annually for so long that neither of them had ever considered it might lapse. His site foreman reported that the crew had joined the choir. His security consultant reported that the meetinghouse crowd had grown past sixty. His daughter's school reported, with delicate institutional horror, that Prudence had not attended, and that several other students had also not attended, and that the absent ones were rumored to be rehearsing.

Ash sat in his study among the portraits of his ancestors—all of them painted prosperous, all of them painted looking slightly off-frame—and understood, with the clarity that comes only to men watching the mechanism seize, that power had never been what he thought it was.

He had imagined it as weight. Pressure applied from above, generation after generation, the way his father had applied it, and his grandfather. But standing at the window now, watching smoke rise from chimneys all over Hollowmere in the ordinary shapes of ordinary mornings, he felt the truth of it in his chest like a swallowed stone: his family's power had never been weight at all. It had been silence. An agreement, renewed annually, that no one would listen. And agreements could not be enforced once the parties stopped pretending.

His men had not been defeated. They had defected. There was no word for how quickly a regime dies when its soldiers discover they were only ever audience members.

He picked up the telephone to call the county—to invoke some emergency, some easement, some clause—and stopped with his hand on the receiver, because he could hear, faintly, through the walls of his own ancestral house, the sound of his daughter's voice. High and clear and untrained, coming from the direction of the meetinghouse, a mile downhill, impossible at that distance, unmistakable nonetheless.

She was singing the third phrase. The one that had killed the lights in the grange hall. The one that made something applaud.

Cornelius Ash lowered himself into the chair behind his desk, among his forged and purchased centuries, and put his face in his hands.

* * *

In the grange hall, the afternoon rehearsal sounded like nothing that could be written down.

Ninety-one voices now. Freya counted twice, disbelieving, and Miriam counted a third time from the doorway with her lips moving. Farmers who had shaken their heads on day one. The night nurse, Ruth, already there, already the still center. The excommunicated organist, Mr. Pell, who had brought a pitch pipe and his pride in separate bags and was visibly deciding which to use. Teenagers from the squat, barefoot on the cold boards because shoes, they claimed, flattened the resonance. Harlan Bexley and five of his six workmen, hard hats still on, singing badly and earnestly in the back row. And Prudence Ash in the front, red coat folded over a chair, teaching the alto line to women three times her age with a patience she had clearly inherited from somewhere other than her father.

The notation did what it always did—arrived in them rather than at them. Voices that had never harmonized found each other on the first try. The room's bad acoustics, its flaking plaster and sagging beams, turned out to be exactly right, the way a worn glove is right.

Freya stood among them and did not sing yet. Tomorrow she would give them everything. Today she walked the rows, listening, correcting with a lifted finger, and felt the strangest grief of her life: seven years of believing herself invisible, and here were ninety people making room for her without being asked, shifting on their benches, angling their shoulders, building her a place in the shape of their sound.

Ruth caught her eye during the rest between phrases. The medium's face was pale and wet-eyed and calm.

"She knows," Ruth said softly, meaning the presence beneath the town, the gaunt enormous frightened thing at the center of the stone. "She can hear all of you. She's been learning your voices for two days." Ruth's hands curled closed, then opened. "Freya—she's practicing the fourth verse. The one with your name in it. She keeps trying to sing it and she can't, because it isn't hers to sing. She's waiting for you."

Outside, the light was beginning to fail. Down in the pit, the door's harmony had grown strong enough that passersby on the square stopped mid-stride to listen, heads tilting like birds', and did not leave.

Tomorrow was the new moon's darkest hour. Tomorrow the choir would descend.

Freya looked at her ninety-one voices, ragged and willing and uncounted by anyone who mattered, and felt the last of her fear change into something with edges.

"From the top," she said.
