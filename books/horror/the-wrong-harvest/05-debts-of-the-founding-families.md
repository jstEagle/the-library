## 5. Debts of the Founding Families

Maren Ashdown had poured the folklorist three pints before she decided to tell him.

She'd watched him all evening from behind the bar of the Drowned Shepherd—the way he sat too straight, the way his eyes tracked old Nellie Prewitt out the door after her song died half-sung, the way he'd written something in a small black notebook with hands that weren't quite steady. Tourists didn't watch like that. Tourists drank and laughed and bought postcards of the church. This one watched like a man counting exits.

He was Wendell Coldharbour. She'd known it since he walked in, though he hadn't offered the name. He had his mother's jaw. He had his mother's way of holding still when he was frightened, as if stillness were a door he could hide behind.

Her brother had held still like that too, at the end.

"Last call," she said, though it wasn't, and when the last of the regulars shuffled out into the drizzle she came around the bar, pulled out the stool opposite him, and sat down without asking.

The folklorist blinked. "I can settle up whenever—"

"I know who you are." She kept her voice flat. Bar-flat, trained over ten years of listening to men say things they thought no one heard. "Wendell Coldharbour. Ruth's boy. You left in 1998 and you've come back with a bag full of microphones."

His hand moved toward the notebook. Stopped. "I'm here to record the harvest songs. For an archive. Folk traditions die out when nobody—"

"They don't die out." Maren leaned forward. "They're put down. Like animals."

The rain ticked against the windows. Somewhere upstairs, her father's floorboards creaked once and went quiet—he'd been listening at the stairhead for an hour, and he would pretend tomorrow that he hadn't.

"You were in the pub when Nellie stopped singing," Maren said. "You noticed."

"She stopped mid-verse. Left without paying. I assumed—"

"You assumed nothing. You wrote down the verse she *didn't* sing. I saw you do it." She let that sit. "So let me save you some work, Mr. Coldharbour, because you're going to keep digging either way, and I'd rather you dug with your eyes open."

* * *

She took him through to the back room, past the kegs and the stacked chairs, to where her father kept the ledgers he never showed the tax man—not the money ones. The other ones. She lit the lamp on the shelf because the overhead bulb made the room feel like an interrogation, and this wasn't an interrogation. It was a handing-over.

The ledger was older than the pub. Older than the church roof. Its cover was sheepskin gone hard and dark as wood, and when she opened it the smell came up—tallow and dust and something underneath both, something green and growing, like a field turned wrong-side-up.

"This is the tally," she said. "Every founding family. Every generation. Every name owed."

Coldharbour stood very still. Stillness again, that door. But his eyes went to the page and stayed there, and she watched him read.

It wasn't complicated. That was the thing people never understood until they saw it—it wasn't complicated at all. Columns of names in a dozen different hands across two hundred years. Some entries in ink, some in pencil, one whole decade in what looked disturbingly like blood gone brown. Beside each name, a year. And beside most of the names, a second mark: a small stroke, like a scythe-cut, meaning *paid.*

Paid. As if a name could be paid. As if a person were coin.

"The song is the invitation," Maren said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. She'd rehearsed this in her head for eleven years, in the shower, on the school run, at three in the morning staring at the ceiling. "Sung twice, by the founding bloodlines, every harvest. First time asks. Second time answers. The field gives abundance. The field takes its due. That's the whole arrangement. That's the whole *history* of Harrow's Bend, Mr. Coldharbour, whatever the parish register says about good farming luck."

"And if nobody sings?"

"Then the field collects anyway." She turned pages. Names, names, names. "Slowly. Badly. It doesn't like waiting. 1976, the Hollis farm—blight took everything, and then it took Thomas Hollis, found in the east rows in November, kneeling, no marks on him, smiling. The coroner said exposure. The coroner was a Hollis."

Coldharbour's throat moved. "And this year?"

"This year the singers refuse." She said it plainly because there was no plain way left to say it. "My family sings the answering part. We have since the beginning. Ashdowns, Prewitts, Hollises, Vane—four lines left, four voices needed. Except this year my father won't sing, and Nellie Prewitt won't sing, and old Vane is dead, and his son moved to Leeds and sends Christmas cards like that gets him out of anything."

"Why?" Coldharbour asked, and then, softer, watching her face: "What happened?"

Maren looked at the ledger. At the last entry with a scythe-cut beside it.

"Last harvest," she said, "the song was sung properly. Twice. By everyone who owed it. And the field took its due, same as always." She touched the name. *J. Ashdown.* "Jonah. My brother. Twenty-six years old. He sang the answering verse on the last night of September, and on the first of October they found his coat folded neat at the edge of the north field, and Jonah was nowhere, and Jonah has stayed nowhere for eleven months."

"He ran away," Coldharbour said. Not cruelly. Hoping, she realized. Hoping she'd give him the ordinary explanation, the one he could carry home in his archive.

"People run away," Maren agreed. "People don't fold their coats. People don't leave their boots. People don't get found in dreams by their little sister, standing in rows of wheat that grow in a field we haven't planted wheat in for sixty years, mouthing *come sing me home, Mare, come sing me home.*" She closed the ledger. Her hands were shaking now; she let them. "After that, my father decided God could have the harvest back. Let the fields go thin. Let the village go hungry and angry and afraid. Anything but sing."

* * *

Coldharbour sat down slowly on an upturned crate, like a man whose legs had been quietly removed.

"There's something else," Maren said. "Something I shouldn't show you, probably. My father would skin me. But you're already in it, so."

"In it?"

She opened the ledger to the final page. The newest ink. Fresh enough that if you tilted the page under the lamp, it shone.

There, in handwriting none of them recognized—a hand old-fashioned, formal, patient—was a single line added sometime in the last fortnight:

*COLDHARBOUR, W. — of the blood, unclaimed.*

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's not possible. I only arrived yesterday. Nobody knew I was coming. *I* barely knew I was coming."

"The field knew." Maren watched him touch the page without quite touching it, fingers hovering over his own name like it might bite. "You're Ruth's son. Ruth was a Coldharbour, and the Coldharbours were founding stock, even if your lot left and the name got scratched off somewhere along the way. Blood doesn't stop being blood just because it moves to London." She hesitated. There was one more thing, and she'd carried it alone long enough. "Your mother never sang. Did you know that? Forty years in this village and Ruth Coldharbour never once sang the harvest song. Wouldn't stand for it in her own house. My mother said Ruth used to cover your ears with her hands when it started, when you were small."

"My mother hummed," Coldharbour said faintly. "All the time. Just never... words."

"Yeah. Well." Maren shut the ledger. "Now you know why."

* * *

They stood in the yard afterward, under a sky the color of wet slate, and the rain had stopped the way rain stops here—all at once, like something holding its breath.

"So the singers refuse," Coldharbour said. "And the field collects anyway. And this year it has a fresh name in its book. Mine."

"Yes."

"And if I just drive out of here tonight—"

"It'll wait. It waited thirty years for you, by the look of that ink." She hugged her arms against the chill. "Mr. Coldharbour—Wendell. I'm not telling you this to frighten you. All right, I am, a bit. But mostly I'm telling you because you're the first person in eleven months who came here asking questions instead of humming louder. My father wants to starve the thing out. Nellie wants to pray it out. The vicar wants to file it out. None of them will even say its name, and I don't think it has one, but somebody has to *face* it, and I can't do it alone, and I'm done pretending I'm not terrified."

He was quiet a long moment. In the dark beyond the gate, the north field lay black and flat and patient, and Maren did not look at it, because looking at it felt like knocking.

"Your brother," Coldharbour said finally. "In your dream. He asked you to sing him home."

"Every night for a month. Then it stopped." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it and let it happen anyway. "I used to think the stopping meant he'd given up. Now I think it means he's *waiting.*"

Coldharbour turned toward the field. And Maren saw it—saw the exact moment his face changed, the rationalist draining out of him like water through chalk, because something out there in the black rows had begun, very faintly, to hum.

A tune with no words. The tune of a woman who'd spent forty years keeping her mouth shut.

"You hear that," Maren whispered. It wasn't a question.

"Yes," said Wendell Coldharbour. "And I think—I think I've heard it before. A long time ago. On a porch, singing to me."

The humming stopped. The silence it left behind was worse—shaped, expectant, like an open mouth.

"Tomorrow," Maren said, "we find out what your name is doing in that book. And then we decide what we're going to do about it. Both of us. Agreed?"

He nodded once, not taking his eyes off the field.

Above the north rows, the clouds tore open for a moment, and the moonlight showed the wheat—or what should have been bare stubble, this late in the season—standing tall, green, and bending, all together, all in one direction.

Toward the cottage where Tam was waiting.
