## 7. Open House

The flyer appeared in her mailbox on a Tuesday, printed on paper so thick it felt like an apology for something.

*Grayhollow Consolidated School cordially invites you to our Annual Open House & Founders' Night. Refreshments served. All faculty required.*

Lena read the last two words twice. *Required.* Not encouraged, not requested — required, in the same font as the fire drill schedule, which she had come to understand was the only register in which Grayhollow told the truth about anything.

"Founders' Night," she said to the empty staff room, testing the phrase.

Dolores Marsh, the attendance secretary, looked up from her crossword. "You'll want to wear something nice," she said. "The families notice."

"The families?"

Dolores filled in four letters — *ASH*, down — and said nothing else, which by now Lena recognized as the town's second language.

* * *

She spent Thursday evening deciding what one wore to be noticed by founding families. Her professional wardrobe consisted of three blazers and a funeral dress, and the funeral dress was still hanging in the closet with its dry-cleaning tag attached, because taking the tag off would have meant admitting the event was over, and Lena was not ready to have that argument with herself again.

She settled on the navy blazer and the good blouse, the one Daniel had bought her two Christmases ago with the confidence of a brother who did not know what his sister's size was but guessed bravely. It fit. It always fit. She suspected he'd asked their mother.

"You'd have hated this," she told the mirror while she pinned her hair. "Open houses. You'd have made some joke about the cheese cubes."

The mirror said nothing, being a mirror, but lately its silences had begun to feel less like absence and more like discretion, and Lena had taken to covering the hallway mirror at Mrs. Okafor's before bed without being reminded. The rules were easier to follow than to question. That, she was beginning to suspect, was the entire design of rules.

* * *

The auditorium had been transformed. Bunting — actual bunting, in a color that could only be described as institutional maroon — draped the stage, and folding tables bore punch bowls and platters of the promised cheese cubes, which were, in fact, present, arranged in a spiral so precise it suggested either great love or great fear.

Above the stage, newly polished frames caught the light: portraits.

Lena had walked under these paintings every day for six weeks without truly seeing them, the way you stop seeing a stain on the ceiling until someone points it out. Tonight they had been cleaned. Tonight they *looked back*.

Three portraits. On the left, a man with a beard like a storm front and eyes the color of pond water — the plaque beneath him read *EDMUND ASHCOMBE, 1871*. In the center, a woman whose expression managed to be both serene and hungry, the way cats look at birds through glass: *ABIGAIL VERE, 1874*. And on the right, a younger man, soft-jawed, smiling as though he'd just heard a joke he intended to repeat later, at your expense: *SILAS ASHCOMBE, 1889*.

"They had them restored over the summer," said Principal Voss at her elbow, materializing the way he always did, without footstep or shadow. He was holding two cups of punch and offered her one with the ceremony of a man presenting a medal. "A labor of love. The families are very devoted to their history."

"The families," Lena said, accepting the punch mostly to have something to do with her hands. "You keep saying that like it's a single organism."

Voss smiled. Voss's smiles were excellent — calibrated, warm, deployed with the timing of a metronome. "Small towns, Ms. Nakamura. Everything here is related to everything else." He sipped his own punch. "You'll want to meet them tonight. They've been asking after you."

"Asking after me," she repeated. "Why?"

"I imagine because you're new." He said it lightly, but something underneath the lightness moved, the way water moves under ice. "New things interest them."

He drifted off toward the punch bowl, and Lena stood alone under three dead people's gazes and decided, professionally, that she disliked this evening already.

* * *

They found her within twenty minutes, which was impressive given that she'd been doing her best impression of furniture near the science fair display.

"Ahh. The new teacher." The voice belonged to a tall woman in her sixties with silver hair arranged like weather and a cardigan that probably cost more than Lena's monthly rent. She extended a hand manicured to the exact pink of healthy gums. "Margaret Ashcombe. This is my brother, Charles, and our cousin Evelyn Vere."

Charles Ashcombe was built like a bank — broad, gray, reassuringly immovable. Evelyn Vere was small and quick-eyed, with the particular alertness of a person who has spent a lifetime listening at doors.

"Lena Nakamura," Lena said, shaking hands. Margaret's grip was cool and dry and lasted exactly one beat longer than comfortable. "I teach eleventh-grade English."

"We know," said Evelyn, pleasantly.

A pause opened up, the kind of pause Lena usually filled with grading. She reached for the cheese cubes instead.

"You must tell us how you're settling in," Margaret said. "Grayhollow can be — an adjustment. The fog, especially, in October. Some people find it oppressive."

"It's honest fog," Lena said. "At least it doesn't pretend to be anything else."

Charles laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, and for a moment all three faces lost their polish and became simply human, tired, amused. Lena felt something in her chest loosen and immediately distrusted it. That was the trick of charm, she thought. It wasn't a mask. It was a door left open on purpose, so you'd walk through believing you'd chosen to.

"And how is the house?" Evelyn asked.

Lena's hand stopped halfway to the cheese spiral. "Which house?"

"Mrs. Okafor's," Evelyn said. "On Halloway Street. We heard you took the room at the end of the hall." She smiled, small and quick. "It's a lovely old place. So much history in those walls."

"I'm sure," Lena said carefully. "Though I confess I mostly notice the radiator."

"The radiator," Charles agreed solemnly, "is a war crime."

There it was again — the flash of the human, the joke, the warmth. Lena laughed despite herself and then catalogued the laugh the way she'd catalogue a student's suspiciously improved essay: noting the improvement, questioning the authorship.

* * *

They were very good, she had to admit that. Over the next hour they never once crowded her. Margaret appeared beside her at the history display and murmured fascinating things about the school's first hundred years; Charles rescued her from the vice-principal's monologue about budget cycles; Evelyn complimented her classroom's bulletin board with such specificity — *"the way you arranged the essays by theme rather than grade, that shows real respect for the work"* — that Lena went home pleased with herself before realizing she'd never mentioned her bulletin board to anyone.

That was the detail that kept her awake. Not the portraits, though those were bad enough — she'd caught Silas Ashcombe's painted smile seeming to widen whenever she looked away and back, which was impossible, which was paint, which was *probably* paint. Not even the moment when Margaret, pressing Lena's hand in farewell, had said, with terrible gentleness:

"If anything is troubling you, dear — anything at all — you need only ask. The families help their teachers. We've always helped our teachers."

No. What kept her awake was the bulletin board. Because it meant they'd been inside her room. After hours. Looking closely. And instead of feeling violated, some treacherous part of her had felt *seen*.

Grief did that, she knew. Grief made you grateful for any attention, the way a starving animal was grateful for a hand, even one holding a leash. Daniel had been gone eleven weeks and she missed him in the specific places: the passenger seat, the other side of the diner booth, the phone at 9 p.m. on Sundays. A town full of strangers offering to find "whatever's troubling you" was not a substitute. But God, it was a warm coat on a cold night, and cold nights were coming.

* * *

Mrs. Okafor was awake when Lena got home, sitting in the parlor with tea and a book she wasn't reading.

"How was Founders' Night?" she asked, in the tone of a doctor asking about pain levels.

"Loud," Lena said. "Cheesy. Literally." She hung her coat. "I met the Ashcombes and the Veres."

Something crossed Okafor's face — not surprise; resignation with long tenure. "And they were charming."

"Frighteningly. One of them knew my bulletin board."

Okafor closed her book. For a while she just looked at Lena, and Lena had learned by now that Okafor's silences were load-bearing; you didn't rush them.

"When I was a girl," Okafor said finally, "there was a spider in my grandmother's kitchen. Big thing, garden spider, striped legs. My grandmother wouldn't let anyone kill it. She fed it, actually. Little scraps." She turned her teacup slowly on its saucer. "I asked her why, and she said, *'Because it catches what comes in through the window.'* And I said, but it might bite *us*. And my grandmother looked at me for a long time and said—" Okafor's eyes came up. "*'It will. If we ever stop being useful.'*"

Lena stood very still in the doorway. Down the hall, behind its cloth, the mirror waited with whatever patience mirrors had.

"Go to bed, child," Okafor said gently. "And Lena — when they offer to help you? However kindly they offer?" She lifted her teacup, and her hand, for the first time since Lena had known her, was not entirely steady. "Remember the spider eats what it catches, too. It just prefers you not think about that part."

* * *

Upstairs, Lena sat on her bed with the lamp on and wrote in her notebook, because writing was how she graded reality, and reality was currently failing to show its work.

*Founding families: attentive, informed, generous. Know things they shouldn't. Want to "help."*

*Rule of teaching: when a struggling student suddenly becomes sweet, check what they want.*

*Rule of towns, apparently: same rule.*

She paused, pen hovering, then added:

*Daniel would say I'm paranoid. Daniel would also say, "Lena, if three rich old families are being nice to you, one of you is about to get robbed."*

She almost smiled. Then, because the notebook was private and the night was long, she wrote the truest sentence of the evening:

*I miss him so much I would listen to anyone who said his name kindly.*

Downstairs, faint as breath, the house creaked — the sound a floor makes, or the sound a throat makes before speaking. Lena clicked off the lamp and lay in the dark counting doors, twice, the way Ruth Calloway's lesson plans had taught her, and somewhere above the auditorium across town, she would have sworn — if she believed in swearing such things, which she was rapidly learning to — three painted smiles held steady in the dark, patient as spiders, waiting for her to ask for help.
