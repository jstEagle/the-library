## 8. Daniel at the Window

Lena had a system for grief. She'd built it herself, laminated it mentally, and followed it the way she followed lesson plans: grief in the morning before coffee was not permitted; grief during grading was inefficient; grief after ten p.m. was allowed but only with tea, because wine made her maudlin and maudlin Lena said things on the phone she had to apologize for later.

The system had held for eleven months. It had even held through the funeral, through the awkward casserole phase, through the moment when someone at the reception — a cousin of Daniel's whose name she'd never retained — had squeezed her arm and said *he's in a better place* with the serene confidence of a person who had never once considered where places actually were.

It did not hold on the fourth of October, when her reflection waved back wrong.

She noticed it first as a delay. She'd been brushing her teeth in the little washroom off her room, watching herself in the mirror above the sink — the hallway mirror stayed covered now, per Mrs. Okafor's rules, a dish towel clothespinned over it like a birdcage at night — and her reflection raised its toothbrush a half-second after she did. Or maybe she raised hers late. That was the thing about a lag: you couldn't tell which side of the glass was behind.

*Tired,* she told herself, spitting and rinsing. *You've been sleeping badly. You're seeing what you expect to see.*

But Lena Nakamura did not believe in tiredness as an explanation. Tiredness explained typos, not physics. And so she stood there in the washroom doing the experiment she would have assigned any student caught claiming the impossible: she moved her hand in a sharp, sudden arc — no wind-up, nothing predictable — and watched.

Her reflection followed. A beat late. Like an echo that had gotten lost on the way.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the mirror, to the room, to nobody. "Okay."

She went downstairs to make tea, because the system required tea, and Mrs. Okafor was in the kitchen re-caulking a window that didn't need it, which seemed to be how the woman spent most evenings — performing small maintenance on a house that never quite accepted it.

"You look like you've seen something," Okafor said without turning around.

"I think my mirror is buffering."

That got a pause. The caulking gun stopped. "Which mirror?"

"The bathroom one. It's — I don't know. Slow."

Mrs. Okafor set down the gun and looked at her then, and there was something in her face that wasn't surprise. It was more like the expression of a doctor hearing a patient describe, accurately, the second symptom of a disease they'd hoped wouldn't progress. "Did you cover it?"

"It's the bathroom mirror. You said the hallway one."

"I said cover them." Okafor's voice was gentle, which from her was worse than stern. "After dark, all of them. Every glass in this house, if you can manage it. Windows too, when the fog sits low."

"That's — Mrs. Okafor, that's not a thing mirrors do."

"No," Okafor agreed. She picked the caulking gun back up. "They don't. Not usually. Not anywhere else." She squeezed a bead of sealant along the frame with terrible precision. "Tea's in the blue tin. The good one. You'll want it tonight, I think."

Lena wanted to ask what that meant. She wanted to ask a great many things, and she could feel the questions lining up in her chest like students waiting to be called on, hands straining, and she knew — the way she always knew — that if she called on one of them, the others would get restless.

She took the tea upstairs instead.

* * *

The dream, if it was a dream, started with chalk dust.

She was in her classroom at Grayhollow High, third period, taking attendance, and every name she read out was Daniel's. *Daniel Nakamura.* Here. *Daniel Nakamura.* Here. Twenty-eight desks, twenty-eight Daniels, all of them raising their hands with his particular lazy half-raise, the one their mother used to scold him for — *the substitute will think you don't know the answer, Danny* — and Lena woke with her heart going like a fire alarm and her cheeks wet and the clock on the nightstand reading 3:12 a.m., which was, she would learn, a meaningful time in the Halloway house, though nobody had warned her yet.

The moonlight came through her window in a long silver stripe across the floorboards, and at the end of the stripe, where the yard began, someone was standing.

Not standing. Waving.

Daniel.

He looked exactly as he had at twenty-six, the last real age he'd been — the cowlick at the crown that no barber had ever defeated, the flannel shirt with the frayed cuff he refused to replace, the grin that started crooked on the left. He was waving the way he used to wave from the platform when her train pulled out, big and unembarrassed, both arms if he could manage it, as though waving were a competitive sport and he intended to medal.

The window was right there. Three steps. Maybe four. The latch was old; it would open easy; the night air smelled like it might smell like him, like cedar and cheap deodorant and the burnt sugar of gas-station candy.

*Lena.*

His voice came through the glass, muffled and warm, the voice of a hundred childhood arguments and a thousand inside jokes. *Lena, come on. It's cold out here.*

And some animal part of her brain — the part that had carried her through the funeral, through the scandal, through every professional catastrophe of the past year by simply refusing to stop moving forward — understood, with total clarity, that this was wrong. Dead brothers did not stand in yards. Dead brothers did not wave. Grief did not work this way; grief was a weight you carried, not a person who visited.

But another part of her — larger, older, hungrier — had already taken a step.

Her bare foot found the floorboard. Cold. The second step brought her close enough to see the details, and the details were perfect, and that was the problem, wasn't it? Memory blurred. Memory softened edges and lost freckles. This Daniel had every freckle. This Daniel was sharper than memory, which meant he wasn't memory, which meant —

*Lena. Please. I've been waiting so long.*

Her hand rose toward the latch. She watched it rise the way you watch someone else's hand, from very far away, from the bottom of a well, and somewhere above her, faintly, a rule surfaced:

*Never answer knocks before dawn.*

It wasn't a knock. But it was a door. And it was not dawn.

Her fingers stopped an inch from the cold brass.

In the yard, Daniel's smile changed. It didn't fall — it *adjusted*, the way a mask adjusts, settling into a configuration that was almost right but arranged by someone who had studied smiles rather than worn them. The wave slowed. Kept going. Slower. Slower. A metronome winding down.

*You'll come out eventually,* the thing in the yard said, still in his voice, still warm, and that warmth was the worst part, the unforgivable part. *Everyone does. Ask your landlady.*

Then the fog rolled up the lawn like a sheet being drawn over furniture, and he was gone, and Lena stood with her hand hanging in the cold air, shaking, until the sky went gray at the edges and the birds started, and only then did she let herself cry — properly, hugely, in violation of every item in her system — sitting on the floor with her back against the bed, because the floor was real, and the bed was real, and the latch was real, and none of those things were her brother.

* * *

She didn't tell Okafor about Daniel. She told her about the mirror.

"The lag is getting worse," she said over breakfast, pushing eggs around her plate. "Last night it — I don't know. It felt less like a delay and more like a disagreement."

Okafor nodded slowly, buttering toast with the focus of a surgeon. "First it copies you late. Then it copies you early. Then—" she paused, and Lena watched her decide how much truth the morning could hold, "—then it stops copying you at all, and starts making suggestions."

"Suggestions."

"Mirrors are doors, Miss Nakamura. Bad ones. Drafty ones. Things lean against them from the other side and push." She slid the toast onto Lena's plate whether she wanted it or not. "Eat. You didn't eat yesterday either. Whatever's leaning, it wants you thin and tired and alone. Don't cooperate."

There was something almost funny in it — being coached on supernatural self-care like exam-week nutrition — and Lena laughed, a small cracked sound that surprised them both. Okafor's mouth twitched.

"There," the older woman said. "That. Keep doing that. It's armor, laughing. Cheap, ugly armor, but it holds."

"What happened to you?" Lena asked quietly. "In this house. What happened that made all these rules?"

Okafor looked at her for a long moment, and Lena saw the answer assemble itself and then get filed away, deliberately, like a letter marked *not yet.*

"When you're ready to hear it," Okafor said, "and I'm ready to say it, we'll both know. Until then — count your doors, child. Twice. And whatever knocks tonight—"

"It won't knock," Lena said. "It waves."

Something crossed Okafor's face then, quick and dark, gone before Lena could read it. "Waving's worse," she said simply, and left for market.

* * *

School was school, which was to say a controlled burn disguised as a Tuesday. Her juniors were workshopping personal essays, and Lena moved between desks correcting comma splices and trying not to notice that seat twelve was empty again — Priya Chandrasekaran, absent nine days, unremarked by anyone, her name on the seating chart now crossed through in red ink that hadn't been there Monday.

Only Marcus Ito met her eyes. He sat two rows from Priya's empty desk, and when the class bent over their drafts, he slid a folded paper to the aisle edge of his desk without looking up. Lena collected it with a stack of essays, smooth as passing change.

Under her desk, hidden by her own body, she opened it. A list of names — seven of them, dates beside each, the last date three weeks ago. And beneath the names, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, one word:

**INVITED**

And below that, smaller: *Please don't stay past October. Please please please.*

She looked up. Marcus was staring at his own blank page, jaw tight, pen motionless. He'd written *please* three times. Teachers noticed things like that. Teachers noticed everything; it was the occupational hazard and the whole point.

When the bell rang, she kept him a moment with a pretext about his thesis statement, and murmured, "I'm working on it. I promise. Go home before dark."

He nodded once, and there was such relief in his face — such naked, exhausted gratitude at simply being believed — that Lena felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing wall relocating. She had spent a year believing nothing she couldn't grade. This boy believed everything, and survived on nothing, and he had handed her the only thing he had.

She would not waste it.

* * *

The house started talking that evening.

At first she thought it was the pipes. Old houses groaned; she'd grown up telling herself that about every dormitory and rental she'd ever lived in, and the rationalization was right there, familiar and comfortable, like an old sweater. Pipes. Settling. Wind.

But pipes didn't laugh.

It came up through the floorboards around nine — a low, breathy chuckle, two notes, rising. Then a voice, indistinct, like a radio in another room, words dissolving before they reached her. She put on headphones. The voice came through the headphones. She turned on the shower. The voice harmonized with the water.

*Lena.* Daniel's cadence. Daniel's habit of stretching her name into three syllables when he wanted something — *Leee-na* — deployed now with surgical accuracy. *Lena, I found your keys. You're always losing your keys. Come find me and I'll give them back.*

She sat on her bed with the lights on and every lamp she owned burning, and she made lists. It was the only weapon she had: lists. *What I know:* the house lags. The house speaks. The house wears my dead brother. *What I don't know:* why me, why now, what it wants. Under *what it wants* she wrote, after a long pause: *me. Probably. Everything here wants me to open something.*

Because that was the pattern, wasn't it? The window. The knocking it promised. Even the mirror, lagging, suggesting — everything in this house was a door angled toward her, and every door was patient, and patience, she was beginning to understand, was the local predator's entire strategy. No claws. No chase. Just an endless, courteous offering of exactly what you couldn't refuse, priced at exactly what you couldn't pay.

Around midnight the voice stopped pretending to be distant.

It came from the wall beside her headboard, close as a phone pressed to her ear, and it was gentle, and it was kind, and it said: *You blame yourself. You shouldn't. You were driving, yes, but the deer came out of nowhere, and Danny grabbed the wheel, Danny chose, and you've served eleven months of a sentence nobody gave you. I can commute it. I can take it back. One word, Lena. One signature. That's all signatures are — one word, given freely.*

Tears ran down her face, steady and silent, and she let them, because crying was honest and the voice was not, and she needed one honest thing in the room.

"You're not him," she said to the wall. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Danny never bargained in his life. He gave things away. He'd have hated you on principle."

A silence. Long. Almost wounded.

Then, softly, from the other wall now, in a voice that was still Daniel's but colder, stripped of its warmth like paint stripped from wood: *The landlady counted her doors twice for forty years, and it saved nothing she loved. Count yours, teacher. See what the house has added.*

And it laughed — that two-note rising chuckle — and faded down through the floors, into the basement, into the dark under the house, where Lena now understood something was kept.

She didn't sleep. At four a.m., gray light finally leaking into the world, she took Ruth Calloway's lesson plans from the drawer where she'd hidden them — Ruth's neat handwriting, Ruth's increasingly frantic margins — and reread the last page for the hundredth time.

*Don't look at your face after midnight. Count the doors twice. If the house offers you someone you've lost, it has already decided what you'll pay. Refuse the shape of the offer, not just the price — refuse the OFFER. It can't want what you won't name.*

*It can't want what you won't name.*

Lena looked at the ceiling, at the water stain shaped vaguely like a reaching hand, and felt the truth of it settle over her like snowfall: the house had found the one word that undid her, and it would keep saying that word, in that voice, forever, unless she learned to stop answering to it.

"Danny," she whispered — testing, grieving, defiant. "I miss you. I'll miss you tomorrow too. And the day after. That's the deal I already signed."

The walls said nothing.

But downstairs, distinctly, a door she had never heard open before drew itself slowly shut — and when she crept to the landing and counted, twice, heart in her throat, the hallway had thirteen doors.

That morning there had been twelve.
