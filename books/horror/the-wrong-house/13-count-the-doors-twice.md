## 13. Count the Doors Twice

Ruth Calloway's handwriting was beautiful, which Lena found unsettling in itself. Teachers who wrote that neatly usually had their lives together, and dead women were not supposed to have neat handwriting. It felt like receiving a postcard from someone who had died mid-vacation.

*Count the doors twice,* the note said. *Once at noon and once after dark. If the numbers disagree, believe the second count.*

Lena sat on her bed with the lesson plans spread around her like tarot cards, a pencil tucked behind her ear out of pure habit — she hadn't actually written anything by hand since graduate school, but the pencil made her feel like a professional investigator instead of a woman hiding from mirrors. Outside, the fog had come down early, swallowing the streetlamps one by one, and Mrs. Okafor's radio played something tinny and cheerful two floors below, as if the whole house were whistling past its own graveyard.

"Okay, Ruth," Lena said aloud. "We're doing this."

She told herself it was an exercise. A teacher's brain needed puzzles the way lungs needed air, and this was a good puzzle: inventory the house, establish a baseline, compare against future data. There was nothing supernatural about counting doors. People counted things all the time. She counted attendance every morning. She counted the minutes until lunch with more devotion than she'd ever given to prayer.

The fact that she was doing it because Ruth's notes had predicted, with eerie precision, everything that had happened to her so far — well. That was just good pedagogy. Trust your sources.

* * *

At noon the next day — Saturday, mercifully, no school, no empty desks wearing red ink — Lena walked the Halloway house with a legal pad and the methodical cheer of a substitute taking attendance.

Mrs. Okafor watched her from the kitchen doorway with an expression that might have been amusement or might have been grief wearing amusement's coat.

"You're counting," Okafor said.

"I'm counting."

"You'll want to do it again after dark."

"That's the plan." Lena clicked her pen. "Ruth's notes are very insistent about it."

Something moved behind Okafor's eyes — not surprise; recognition. "Ruth counted too. First week of October, her last year." She turned back toward the kitchen. "There's coffee if you want it. Real coffee, not the school stuff. You look like you need something real."

The ground floor gave her six doors: front, back, kitchen pantry, the parlor closet, the door under the stairs that opened onto nothing but coats and the smell of cedar, and the cellar door with its new padlock — Okafor had locked it after Lena's basement expedition, and neither of them had discussed why, which was its own kind of conversation.

Second floor: four doors. Her room, the bathroom, Okafor's room at the front, and the linen closet whose hinges sang a single flat note when opened, like a choir member who'd given up on harmony.

Third floor, under the sloped roof: two doors. One to the attic crawl space, one to a storage room full of furniture draped in sheets, where the afternoon light came through a round window and made the sheets look, briefly, like people deciding whether to stand up.

Twelve doors total. Lena wrote the number at the top of the page and boxed it twice, the way she did with final grades. Twelve. A tidy, sane, defensible number. She almost liked it.

"There," she said to the empty hallway. "Twelve doors. See? The world still has rules."

The house said nothing, which she chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

She spent the afternoon grading — Marcus Ito's essay sat at the top of the pile, and she'd read it three times without absorbing a word, because his last line kept snagging her: *You're the only one who looked.* She'd written *See me Monday* in the margin and then crossed it out, because what could she possibly say? *Come to my haunted boarding house and we'll compare notes on the apocalypse?*

By five o'clock the fog had thickened into something you could have sliced and served. By six, the light through the windows had gone the color of old bruises. By seven, Mrs. Okafor knocked gently on Lena's door and handed her a mug of tea she hadn't asked for.

"It's time," Okafor said simply.

"For what?"

"You know for what." The landlady's face was unreadable, but her hands, wrapped around her own mug, were not steady. "I'll be downstairs. If you knock on my door before dawn, I won't answer — you remember the rule. But I'll hear you. And I'll be listening for anything that isn't you."

"That's not comforting at all," Lena said, and was surprised to find her voice almost steady. Almost warm. Fear, she was discovering, had a way of sanding away everything except what mattered, and what mattered right now was that this strange, damaged woman was offering to listen for her all night.

Okafor almost smiled. "It's not meant to comfort you. It's meant to keep you." Then she went downstairs, and her footsteps faded, and the house settled around Lena like a held breath.

* * *

The second count began at 7:40 p.m., legal pad in hand, pen clicking like a metronome.

Ground floor. Front door — present, solid, unremarkable. Back door — present. Pantry — present, smelling of flour and something older underneath, something mineral. Parlor closet — present. Coat door under the stairs — present. Cellar door — present, padlocked, and Lena noticed her own reflection in the padlock's brass curve was a half-second late raising the pen, and she made herself look away before the lag could become a conversation.

Six. Fine.

Second floor. Her room — present, though standing in its doorway she had the odd sensation of being expected, like the room had set out a place for her at a table. Bathroom — present. Okafor's room — present, door shut, a strip of warm light beneath it, and Lena felt a pang of tenderness for the woman behind it, sitting up listening. Linen closet — present, hinge singing its flat note.

Ten.

Third floor. Attic crawl space — present. Storage room — present, sheets undisturbed, round window black with fog.

Twelve.

Lena stood on the third-floor landing and let out a breath she'd been rationing since sundown. "Twelve," she announced to the house, writing it down. "Same as noon. Ruth, your notes are—"

She stopped.

Because there was a door across the landing.

There had been no door across the landing at noon. At noon there had been wall — smooth plaster, water-stained in the shape of a running horse, which Lena had privately named Gerald. Gerald the wall-horse. She had looked at Gerald not four hours ago. Gerald had been reassuringly, boringly present.

Now there was a door.

It was narrow, taller than the others, painted a gray that didn't quite match any other surface in the house, as if it had been color-matched to a photograph of the house rather than the house itself. There was no knob. There was no frame trim. It simply stood in the wall where Gerald used to be, flush and patient, the way a word stands in a sentence that hasn't been spoken yet.

Lena's pen hovered over the paper. Her hand was shaking, and she hated it, and she let it shake, because pretending otherwise was how people in Ruth's lesson plans had died.

"Thirteen," she whispered, and wrote it, and boxed it once, then twice, then a third time until the box became a small cage of graphite.

* * *

Here is what Lena Nakamura knew about herself, and what she clung to now like a rail in a rocking train: she did not spook easily, and when she did spook, she got organized. Grief had taught her that. When Daniel died, everyone around her dissolved into casseroles and clichés, and Lena had responded by building spreadsheets — funeral costs, thank-you cards, the estate paperwork nobody else would touch. Structure was how she loved things. Structure was how she survived them.

So she did not run downstairs. She sat down on the third step of the attic stair, put the legal pad on her knees, and studied the door the way she'd study a student's suspiciously improved essay: looking for the seams.

No knob. No hinges visible. No draft around its edges, which was wrong, because every door in the house leaked fog-colored cold except this one, which leaked nothing at all, as if the wall behind it weren't a wall but a held tongue.

And it breathed.

Not moved — breathed. The gray surface swelled by a fraction, the way a sleeping animal's flank rises, and fell, and rose. Slow. Even. Patient. Counting its breaths the way she'd counted her doors, except it only needed one.

"Okay," Lena said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Okay. You're not real. You're a draft. You're condensation. You're—" She ran out of rational explanations, which was becoming a familiar and humiliating experience, like reaching the bottom of a bag of chips far sooner than the serving size promised.

Ruth's notes. She flipped through them with numb fingers, past the seating charts and the lesson sequences, until she found the page she'd read a dozen times and never fully believed:

*If you find a door that wasn't there before, do not open it, do not knock on it, do not speak to it. It opens for whoever answers it. Answering can be as small as saying hello. I answered. God forgive me, I was lonely, and I answered.*

Below that, in handwriting gone jagged, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*It will sound like whoever you miss most. Do not correct it. Do not tell it it's wrong. Every correction teaches it your voice better.*

Lena closed the notebook very carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

Then, because she was still, despite everything, a teacher — because the habit of checking on things was load-bearing in her soul — she looked at the thirteenth door one more time.

It had moved closer.

Not much. Perhaps a foot. Perhaps less. But the landing was small and the geometry was exact in her memory, and the door now stood at the top of the attic stairs instead of across from them, and its gray surface rose and fell, rose and fell, and beneath the fog-muffled silence of the house she thought she heard — faintly, from behind it — a sound like someone humming.

Daniel used to hum while he cooked. Badly. Always the same three bars of whatever song was stuck in his head, always losing the tune and finding it again like a man feeling along a wall in the dark.

"Nope," Lena said aloud, and stood up, and backed down the stairs without turning around, because Ruth's notes had also said — she checked, later, hands trembling over the pages in her lamplit room — *never turn your back on it either, but never turn to face it going away; walk backward, count your steps, and stop at nine.*

She took nine steps backward. On the ninth, her heel found the second-floor landing, and she turned and descended the rest of the way at a pace she maintained was dignified and knew was flight.

* * *

Downstairs, the kitchen was yellow and warm and smelled of cinnamon, and Mrs. Okafor sat at the table with two mugs poured, as if she'd known the exact minute Lena would come through the door. Maybe she had. Maybe that was just what surviving this house for forty years taught you — the house's schedule, its tides, the hours when it sharpened itself.

"Thirteen," Lena said.

Okafor nodded, unsurprised, and slid a mug toward her. "What did it sound like?"

"Nothing. Breathing. And—" Lena wrapped her hands around the mug and stared into the tea. "Humming. Something Daniel used to hum."

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was gentle in a way that cost her something; Lena could see the gentleness being paid for, coin by coin, out of some old private account. "Mine hummed too. My mother's hymns. I was eight." She turned her mug a slow quarter-turn on the table. "That was 1961. The Ashcombes signed for me. My mother thought I'd drowned in the creek. They let her think it for eleven years, and then they gave me back, and by then I was—" She stopped. Started again. "Returned wrong. That's what my grandmother called it. Returned wrong. I knew things I shouldn't have known. I flinched at doors. I still do."

Lena looked at this woman — this careful, rule-making, tea-brewing woman who had built a fortress of small instructions around a wound the size of a childhood — and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't fear.

"The rules," Lena said. "Cover the mirror. Don't answer knocks. Count the doors."

"They're not superstitions." Okafor's eyes came up, and they were fierce. "They're a fence. Every rule is a fence post, and I've spent fifty years planting them, and the house walks the fence line every night looking for a gap." She reached across the table and, with terrible deliberateness, turned Lena's legal pad toward herself. Read the two boxed numbers. Twelve. Thirteen.

"It's never shown itself this early before," Okafor said quietly. "Ruth had three weeks in October. You've had eleven days." She looked up. "It wants you, Miss Nakamura. Not the way it wanted the children. The way it wanted Ruth. It wants a keeper. Someone to hold the door shut from inside."

"From inside," Lena repeated. The phrase landed somewhere cold. Her predecessor had died holding a door shut — she'd read it in the town archive, between the lines of a politely vague obituary. She had assumed it was a metaphor.

Okafor stood, carried both mugs to the sink, and stood with her back to Lena, watching the dark window where the fog pressed like a face learning the glass.

"Tomorrow night is the new moon," she said. "The darkest night it gets. If the house is going to make you an offer, it will be tomorrow night. And Lena—" she said the name deliberately, the first time she'd ever used it, and it sounded like a hand extended across a wide gap, "—whatever it offers you, however much sense it makes, however tired you are — you say no. Can you do that? Can you promise an old woman who was returned wrong?"

Lena thought about Daniel. About the humming. About how easy it would be, tomorrow night, to say yes to three bars of a badly remembered song.

"I can try," she said.

Above them, faint through the ceiling, from the direction of the third floor, came a sound like a door settling on hinges that had never been installed.

Neither of them looked up. Both of them listened anyway.

* * *

That night Lena slept — or lay in the dark performing a convincing imitation of it — with Ruth's notebook open on her chest and the lamp burning, because the notes' final instruction on the matter of doors was this, underlined three times:

*When the thirteenth door comes for you, it means the house has decided. It doesn't want your fear anymore. It wants your signature. Sleep with the lights on. Sleep with the truth on. And whatever knocks at midnight — remember that the real ones never knock. They wait for morning.*

At 11:58 p.m., someone knocked on her door.

Three times. Softly. Politely.

And then, in a voice she had buried in June and would have known through concrete, through years, through death itself, Daniel said:

"Lena? It's cold out here. Let me in."
