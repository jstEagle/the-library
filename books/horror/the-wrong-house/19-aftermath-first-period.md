## 19. Aftermath, First Period

The first thing Lena noticed about morning was that it was ordinary.

She had braced for something else — ash on the windowsills, the wallpaper peeled back like a wound, the hallway carpet stained with the memory of everything that had walked it. She came down the stairs at six-forty with her shoes in one hand and the ledger clutched to her chest like a nursing infant, and she found Mrs. Okafor in the kitchen frying eggs, and that was all. That was everything. Bacon grease popping in a pan. A radio murmuring weather. Sunlight coming through the window in the honest, unremarkable way sunlight does.

"Sit," Okafor said, not turning around. "You look like a woman who's been holding a door all night."

"I have been holding a door all night."

"Then the eggs are earned." She slid a plate across the table. "Your arms are shaking."

Lena looked down at her hands. They were. She set them flat on the wood and watched the tremor slowly drain out of them, the way a struck bell goes quiet. "The mirrors," she said. "I haven't looked yet."

"They're only mirrors now." Okafor said it simply, the way she'd once said *never answer knocks before dawn*, but the weight had gone out of the words. "I checked. Twice. I've been checking things twice for fifty years; old habits don't die just because they should." She sat down across from Lena with her own plate and studied her over her glasses. "The hallway one shows the hallway. The bathroom one shows you with your hair doing that thing it does. That's all they've ever been supposed to do."

Lena ate. The eggs were perfect and she tasted almost none of them, but she ate, because Ruth Calloway's notes had been very clear on the subject of keeping your body fed and your wits about you, and Ruth had earned the right to be obeyed posthumously.

"I keep waiting," Lena said finally, "for it to be a trick. I keep thinking the sun is part of it. That any second the light will go gray and the doors will start moving and—"

"Then you'll be late for first period."

Lena looked up.

Okafor was buttering toast with the serenity of a woman who had personally survived worse and intended to be difficult about breakfast regardless. "You heard me. It's Monday. You have twenty-three students — twenty-three, count them twice if it makes you feel better, and I suspect it will — and a homeroom attendance sheet, and a town that is going to wake up this morning with a hole in it where a great deal of ugly used to be, and they are going to need one person in that building who knows exactly what happened and refuses to pretend otherwise." She pushed the toast toward her. "The house is dead, Lena. The *school* isn't. That's the part that takes longer."

* * *

The walk to Grayhollow Elementary took eleven minutes in daylight and felt like walking through a town that had been recently washed. The fog was there — the fog was always there, it was practically the municipal bird — but it sat politely at the edges of things instead of seeping into them. Mrs. Pruitt was sweeping her porch. Mr. Delgado's dog was committing some minor crime against a garden gnome. Two children ran past Lena with a stick they had clearly decided was a sword, screaming in the pure, unburdened way children scream when nothing in their town has ever traded them for anything.

She stopped on the sidewalk and let them pass and felt something in her chest crack — not painfully. More like ice going out of a river.

The school was the same brick building it had always been, except that the portraits were gone from above the auditorium doors. All four of them. The Ashcombes, the Veres, the Halloways, old man Gray himself — unhooked overnight, and in their place the wall showed nothing but clean plaster and four paler rectangles, like the building itself had exhaled.

Inside, the front office was in mild, ordinary chaos. A secretary Lena had never seen before — young, harried, a laminated *TEMPORARY* badge pinned crooked to her cardigan — was fielding phone calls.

"Superintendent's office? Yes, he's — no, ma'am, I can't — ma'am, I'm just the temp, I don't know where Principal Voss went, nobody seems to—" She caught sight of Lena and mouthed *help* with the desperation of a drowning sailor.

"Who's calling?" Lena asked.

"The *school board*. And the *state*. And a man who keeps asking about 'records' and then hangs up." The temp lowered her voice. "Is it true the principal just — left? In the night?"

Lena thought about fresh ink in a ledger, a signature drying in the dark. She thought about a man who had traded children for a comfortable career and had woken yesterday to find his signature burning off the page.

"I think," Lena said carefully, "that Mr. Voss found the terms of his employment had changed."

The temp blinked. "Okay. Sure. Do you know where the attendance binders are? Because I've looked everywhere and—"

"Supply room. Second shelf. The green one's current." It was strange, how quickly the ordinary reassembled itself around her, like water finding its level. She was giving directions to a temp about binders. Twenty hours ago she had been dragging a door off its hinges while the dead of six generations poured past her like cold water going home.

She went to her classroom.

* * *

The desks were all facing forward. That was the first thing. For weeks the room had felt subtly wrong — desks angled toward the windows, toward the door, toward nothing, a geometry she'd blamed on janitorial carelessness. This morning they were rows, neat and dumb and perfect, and Lena stood in the doorway and laughed, one short cracked sound, at the sight of twenty-three desks arranged with the humble ambition of going absolutely nowhere.

The seating chart on her desk was clean. The red-inked names were gone — not crossed out, not corrected, *gone*, the paper smooth as if no pen had ever touched it. She turned it over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting she didn't recognize but somehow did, was a single line:

*You counted the doors twice. Good girl. — R.C.*

Lena pressed the paper flat against her chest and stood there in the empty classroom for a long moment, doing the thing she had promised herself she wouldn't do, and then did it anyway, and then blew her nose on a Kleenex from the box Ruth had probably bought, and got out her attendance sheet.

* * *

The students came in the way students do — in a tide, in clusters, in that particular adolescent weather system of elbows and gossip and one boy (Devon Okafor's grand-nephew, she'd learned; the family got around) who was eating chips at 8:04 in the morning with the defiance of a constitutional right.

And Marcus Ito came in last, slouching, backpack over one shoulder, alive.

Lena's hand went to the desk edge and gripped it.

He looked — fine. That was the unbearable part. He looked fine. He dropped into his seat, bumped fists with Devon, complained audibly about the math homework, and when Lena called the roll and reached his name, he said "Present" in the flat, bored tone of a boy who had never in his life been anything else.

"Marcus," she said, before she could stop herself. "Are you — do you feel all right? You were out Friday."

"Was I?" He frowned, genuinely puzzled, and Lena watched him search for the missing days the way you'd pat your pockets for keys. "Huh. I guess. I don't really — I had this weird dream, maybe? About a door?" He shrugged, and the shrug was seventeen years old and completely ordinary and it nearly undid her. "Anyway, did we do anything Friday? Am I behind?"

"No," Lena said, and her voice only wobbled a little. "You're not behind. You're exactly where you should be."

"Cool." He opened his notebook. Inside the cover, she could see, he still had the list — the note he'd slipped her weeks ago, the names, the word *invited* underlined twice. But when he caught her looking, he glanced down at it with the vague embarrassment of a kid discovering an old doodle, and he peeled it off, crumpled it, and tossed it toward the wastebasket. It missed. He didn't bother retrieving it.

Lena retrieved it after class. She smoothed it out and put it in her bag next to the ledger. Some things you keep, even when the boy who wrote them no longer knows why. *Especially* then.

* * *

Attendance.

She stood at the front with the sheet and the class settled into that pre-roll silence that is the closest thing schools have to a sacred pause. Outside the window, the fog was burning off. Someone's lawnmower started up three yards away, a small mechanical heartbeat.

Lena looked down at the names and found her hands were shaking again, just slightly, and she understood why. Because for weeks this list had been a battlefield report. Every name had been a question. Every empty desk had been an answer she wasn't allowed to say out loud. She had stood at this podium and read the roll like a woman calling names into a well and listening for the splash.

"Adler, Priya."

"Here."

"Boone, Caleb."

"Here." A cough. "Present. Sorry. Here."

"Calloway—" She stopped. There was no Calloway on the list. There never had been. But she said the name anyway, quietly, under her breath, the way you'd pour one out, and then she cleared her throat and moved on, and if Devon noticed her eyes were wet he had the grace to be very interested in his chips.

"Delgado, Rosa."

"Here."

"Ito, Marcus."

"Present," he said, and grinned at her, and she made it through the next six names on autopilot.

"Okafor, Devon."

"Here, and I did the reading, you can check."

"I will check, Devon."

"See, this is why nobody likes you." Laughter around the room, easy and ordinary, the sound of children who had never been afraid of their own classroom. Lena let it wash over her.

She reached the bottom of the list. Every name had answered. She read it again from the top just to be sure — *count it twice, good girl* — and every name answered again, and then she set the sheet down on the podium and stood there for a moment with her hand flat on it, and the class, sensing something they couldn't name, went quiet.

"Ms. Nakamura?" Priya Adler, hand half-raised. "Are you okay?"

Lena considered the question. She thought about the honest answer — *I spent last night holding a door against the dark while the ghosts of your town's victims walked through me on their way to wherever they're going, and this morning the mirrors only show me my bad haircut, and I don't entirely trust the sun* — and then she thought about what a teacher actually says.

"I'm fine," she said. "I'm better than fine. I'm — present." She smiled, and it came out crooked but real. "Everybody take out your books. We have a lot to get through, and I intend to be here for all of it."

* * *

At lunch she sat in the empty staff room with the ledger open in front of her, because Okafor was right — the house was dead, but the school wasn't, and a ledger full of burned signatures was still a ledger full of names, and names were evidence, and evidence wanted somewhere to go. The state investigator's card was in her pocket. She'd called from the pay phone by the gym — the pay phone, because she trusted exactly one piece of technology in this town and it was the one that couldn't show her anything.

The signatures were gone. Every page. Where the founders' names had been there were only pale scorch-marks, the ghosts of ink, but the *children's* names remained — written in a different hand, a clerk's hand, patient and terrible — and those, Lena understood, were hers now. Not to keep. To carry.

"Ms. Nakamura?"

She looked up. The temp from the office stood in the doorway, twisting her lanyard.

"Sorry to bother you. There's — someone's here to see you? A woman. She says she's from the district. She says—" the temp consulted a sticky note "—she says she's here about 'the vacancy.'"

"The vacancy."

"Your principal's office. It's, um. It's open. District policy, apparently, they need someone in it by end of week, even interim." The temp lowered her voice. "And the weird thing is, she asked for you *specifically*. She said — I wrote it down, because it was weird — she said, 'Tell Ms. Nakamura the district has always valued people who keep good records.'"

Lena looked down at the ledger. At the pale scorch-marks where the signatures used to be. At the children's names, patient as seeds.

Outside the staff room window, the fog had come back — just a little, just at the treeline, the way fog does in October, the way fog is allowed to.

"Tell her," Lena said, closing the ledger, "I'll be right there. And tell her I'm bringing my records with me."

The temp left. Lena sat for one more moment in the warm, ordinary, sunlit room, listening to the distant sound of children at recess — alive, all of them, loud and graceless and gloriously uninvited — and then she picked up the ledger, and the crumpled note, and Ruth's lesson plans, and went to see about a vacancy.
