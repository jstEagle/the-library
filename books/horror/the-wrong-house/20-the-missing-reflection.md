## 20. The Missing Reflection

The morning after the longest night of her life, Lena Nakamura made coffee in Mrs. Okafor's kitchen and discovered that she had become very good at not looking at reflective surfaces.

It wasn't a conscious skill. It arrived the way grief had arrived after Daniel's funeral — fully formed, already practiced, as though some part of her had been rehearsing for years. She poured the coffee with her eyes on the pot. She caught her own face in the dark window over the sink and looked through it, past it, at the fog lifting off the yard. When she passed the hallway mirror — uncovered now, because Okafor had taken down the cloth herself with something like ceremony — Lena kept her gaze fixed on the doorframe beside it.

"You're doing the thing," Okafor said from behind her. She was frying eggs, which was absurd and wonderful. The world had nearly ended twelve hours ago, and here was a woman in a floral apron making breakfast like it was any Tuesday.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you pretend glass isn't there." Okafor slid the eggs onto two plates. "I did it for eleven years. You'll throw your neck out, looking sideways like that. Sit down."

Lena sat. The kitchen was small and yellow and smelled of butter and black tea, and outside the window the Halloway house stood in the early light looking exactly like what it now appeared to be: an old house with bad wiring and good bones and a basement that smelled only of mildew. She had gone down there at dawn, before the bus came, before school, and found the ledger exactly where she'd left it on the workbench, its pages ordinary paper, its ink merely ink. Voss's signature had dried to brown. The founders' names were just names. Whatever had lived between those lines had moved out.

Or been evicted.

"Eat," Okafor said. "Then I'll show you."

"Show me what?"

Okafor set down her fork. Her hands were steady, but she took a breath first, the way people do when they've said a hard thing many times to an empty room and are saying it to a person for the first time. "What it cost you."

"I know what it cost me." Lena touched her own face without quite meaning to. In the bathroom mirror that morning — one glance, involuntary, the way you check a wound — she had looked *fine*. Tired, wrung out, hair doing something unforgivable, but fine. Herself. She'd decided not to examine how much relief that single glance had given her, or how suspicious relief should have made her.

"You know what you *paid*," Okafor said. "You don't know what they *kept*."

* * *

They started in the hallway.

"Stand there," Okafor said, positioning Lena three feet from the big oval mirror with the tarnished frame. "And look."

Lena braced herself and looked.

The mirror showed the hallway perfectly. The peeling wallpaper with its faded pattern of birds. The runner rug worn pale down the center. The door to Lena's room at the end, standing open. Morning light falling across everything at the same slant, catching the same dust motes. It was, Lena thought, the most accurate mirror she'd ever seen — every detail rendered with an almost loving fidelity, the kind of reflection that didn't flatter or distort but simply reported.

The hallway was empty.

She looked up. Okafor stood beside her, silver-haired, arms folded, reflected faithfully at her shoulder. The lamp on the hall table was there. The coat hook was there. The crack in the ceiling plaster was there.

Lena was not there.

"Oh," she said. It came out very small.

"Look again," Okafor said gently. "Really look. Take your time. I'll be right here."

Lena looked. Her mind — the mind that graded papers, that believed in rubrics and red pens and the fundamental fairness of things that could be measured — went through its whole repertoire of explanations in about four seconds. Angle. Lighting. Some trick of the old glass, mercury backing gone patchy, blind spots developing the way old mirrors develop. She stepped left. The empty hallway shifted accordingly; the light source stayed consistent; the physics held. She raised her hand.

Nothing raised anything back.

There was no lag anymore. That was the thing she noticed third, after the absence itself and then the absence of the absence. For weeks the mirror had run a beat behind her, then a lifetime behind her, then not behind her at all but *beside* her, independent, wearing her face like a borrowed coat. Now there was no second self at all. No delayed twin, no hollow-eyed older version still teaching in an empty classroom, no Daniel waving from the yard. Just the hallway, complete, accurate, and missing exactly one person.

"It's not broken," Lena heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange — flat, careful, the voice she used when a student handed in plagiarized work and she wanted to give them every chance to confess. "A broken mirror would show me wrong. This shows everything else *right*."

"Yes," Okafor said.

"So it's choosing. It's choosing to leave me out."

"No, child." Okafor turned from the glass and faced her, and her eyes were wet and furious and kind all at once, which was a combination Lena was learning was simply what Okafor's eyes did. "It's not leaving you out. It's *holding* you. What you're seeing is the house keeping what it kept. Your image didn't vanish, Lena. Your image is *in there*. It just hasn't figured out how to come back yet. Or it can't. Or—" she stopped, jaw tightening, "—or it's what the rent cost, and nobody gets a refund."

Lena stood very still.

Somewhere below them, in the walls, the house made a sound. Not the breathing sound, not the rearranging sound, nothing with intent in it. Just an old house settling on a cool morning, pipes ticking, wood contracting. Ordinary sounds. She was going to have to relearn which sounds were ordinary, and she suspected the relearning would take years, and she suspected she would never entirely trust it.

"The bathroom," Lena said. "This morning. I saw myself."

"You saw a woman who looks like you," Okafor said. "Look closer next time. Look at the eyes. Is she tired the way you're tired? Does she flinch where you flinch?" She paused. "Does she blink when you blink?"

"How do you know all this?" And then, before Okafor could answer, because the answer was already assembling itself in Lena's chest with a horrible cold clarity: "Because it happened to you."

* * *

Okafor made more tea. They took it into the front parlor, which Lena had avoided until now — the room with the tall windows and the second mirror, the one over the mantel, draped in a sheet since her first night in the house. Okafor pulled the sheet off with both hands, briskly, like a woman stripping a bed.

"I was taken in 1963," she said. "I was nine years old."

The mirror over the mantel showed the parlor: the sagging horsehair sofa, the dead fireplace, the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a closed hand. Morning sun. Dust. Empty room.

"My mother signed," Okafor went on. She spoke evenly, the way you recite something you've had carved into you so long the edges have worn smooth. "Not the founding families — my mother was nobody, a widow, cleaning houses for the Ashcombes. But prosperity has a trickle-down, in towns like this. The Ashcombes told her the house needed a child now and then to stay... satisfied. Told her it was like tithing. Told her God would understand. And my mother, who had four other children and no money and a sickness coming that winter, sat down at their kitchen table and wrote my name in a book." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Lena had ever seen. "People think the horror needs monsters. Mostly it needs arithmetic."

"What happened?" Lena asked quietly.

"I was in the house nine days. My mother repented — or got scared, or got greedy about having me back, depends which story you want — and she came and hammered on the door for a night and a day, and the town let her, because by then the arrangement was old enough to be embarrassing and the families wanted no fuss. And the house..." Okafor's hands tightened around her teacup. "The house gave me back. Mostly. All of me except—"

She nodded at the mirror.

Lena looked. The parlor. The sofa. The window light. One elderly woman sitting in a wingback chair, holding tea, wearing a floral apron.

One younger woman on the sofa, holding tea, wearing yesterday's clothes.

Both of them present. Both of them accounted for.

Wait.

Lena counted again, the way Ruth Calloway's notes had taught her to count doors — twice, always twice. Woman in the chair. Woman on the sofa. Two women, two reflections, correct, matching, *right* —

Except.

"She's not moving with you," Lena whispered.

Okafor's reflection sat in the chair with its cup halfway raised, and it was accurate down to the steam curling off the tea, down to the exact drape of the apron — but it was a half-second late. A half-second behind, the way Lena's own reflection had been that first week, back when it was only a trick of tiredness, back when it could still be explained away.

"Eleven years late," Okafor said. "That's what's left of mine. It catches up a little more each decade, they tell me. By the time I'm ninety it might keep proper time." She set down her cup. "The house doesn't take the whole person, Lena. It takes the part that answers to your name in the glass. And it keeps it. Feeds it, maybe. Lets it live in there, going about your business, being you, a beat behind. That's the mercy everyone talks about, in the families. *The children aren't killed.* As if that settles it."

Lena's tea had gone cold in her hands. She hadn't noticed drinking any of it.

"Marcus," she said. "The ones who came back. The students who 'transferred away' and came back —"

"Some came back. Some were returned wrong, like me. Some weren't returned at all, and their reflections walked around in them instead, and nobody noticed for years, because a child a half-beat slow is just a child who's shy, who's dreamy, who needs to apply herself." Okafor's voice cracked, just once, and she pressed it flat again. "I've taught Sunday school in this town for forty years, Miss Nakamura. I watch the children in windows. It's a habit you don't lose."

* * *

Lena spent the afternoon conducting what she could only later describe as an audit.

She couldn't help it. It was who she was — the woman who color-coded her gradebook, who once spent an entire weekend building a spreadsheet to track which students owed her pencils. If something had been taken from her, she was going to establish precisely what, itemized, with documentation.

The bathroom mirror: showed the small tiled room, the dripping faucet, the towel rack. Did not show her. She stood in front of it for ten full minutes and felt less afraid than she expected and more bereaved than she knew how to describe. It was like attending her own memorial service and finding the photo display featured someone else.

Her hand mirror, in her purse, from home, from *before*: showed her. Fully, promptly, accurately. She checked it six times, tilting it, walking with it, holding it under different lights. Present and accounted for.

"That one came with you," Okafor said, watching from the doorway. "The invitation covers the house and what serves the house. Glass that belongs to Grayhollow, to the families, to the school — that's theirs. Your little mirror is yours. It never signed anything."

"So I'm only missing in *their* mirrors."

"You're only missing where *they* can see you." Okafor said it lightly, but Lena heard the weight underneath. "Which, in this town, is most of them."

At four o'clock, Lena walked to the school — she hadn't been able to face it that morning, had called in with a hoarse-voiced lie about illness, and Voss's office had answered with a recorded message saying the principal was "unavailable indefinitely," which was either ominous or the best news she'd gotten all year — and she stood in the empty hallway outside her classroom and looked at the long window there, the one that caught her reflection every afternoon at this hour.

Empty hallway. Afternoon light. Rows of desks visible through her classroom door.

No teacher.

She laughed. It startled her, the laugh — a short, cracked, genuine thing — and it echoed strangely in the corridor, and she realized she was laughing because the alternative was understanding it, and she wasn't ready to understand it. Not yet. Maybe not today.

Because here was the thing she kept circling and refusing to land on: she remembered the night. All of it. The thirteenth door, and Daniel behind it — Daniel, *Daniel*, alive or near enough, smiling at her with the crooked smile he'd had before the accident straightened nothing because death doesn't need to fix what was never broken — and she remembered stepping toward him, and she remembered dragging the door off its hinges instead, and she remembered holding it against the dark while the children passed through her like cold water, going home, going free, going wherever the dead go when somebody finally holds the door.

She remembered choosing. That was hers. Nobody could reflect it away.

But the woman in the glass — the woman who'd arrived on the bus in September with a one-way ticket and a box of Daniel's books she couldn't bring herself to unpack — that woman had stayed behind. Was staying behind. Was, at this moment, somewhere in the silvered skin of every mirror in Grayhollow, walking the halls of the house that kept her, teaching an empty classroom, growing older at a half-second's distance, waiting for a sunrise that had already happened without her.

"I'm sorry," Lena said aloud, to the window, to the hallway, to whoever was listening from the other side of the light. "I couldn't do it any other way. I hope you know that. I hope you'd have done the same."

The window showed her nothing, which was, she supposed, a kind of answer.

* * *

She came back to the house at dusk, and Okafor was waiting in the kitchen with two plates of soup and an expression Lena had learned to read as *we need to talk about something I've been avoiding.*

"Sit," Okafor said. "Eat first. Bad news keeps better on a full stomach."

"That's not true."

"No," Okafor agreed. "But soup helps anyway."

Lena ate. The soup was chicken and rice and aggressively salted, and it was the best thing she'd tasted in months, and she ate all of it, and Okafor watched her with the particular satisfaction of a woman whose primary love language is portion size.

"Now," Okafor said. She reached beneath her chair and brought up a canvas bag, worn soft at the handles, and set it on the table between them. From it she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it, and laid it flat.

Photographs. Dozens of them, decades deep, corners soft with handling. School pictures. Class photos. Snapshots from picnics and church socials and county fairs. Children, mostly. Some adults. And along the bottom edge of each photograph, in Okafor's small precise handwriting, dates and names and — on perhaps a third of them — a single red checkmark.

"What am I looking at?"

"Everyone the house ever took, so far as I could ever learn. Fifty-one years of records, because the official records get burned or lost or 'misfiled,' and somebody has to remember." Okafor tapped the red checks. "These ones came back. Like me. Like Marcus, if Marcus comes back." Her finger moved to the unchecked majority and rested there, and her voice dropped. "These ones didn't."

Lena looked at the faces. Boys with cowlicks. Girls with missing teeth grinning around the gaps. A whole Brownie troop in one shot, 1971, five girls squinting into the sun — and Okafor's red pen had marked two of them. Lena thought of the voices that had passed through her in the dark, dozens of them, generations of them, and felt the photographs in her hands become suddenly, unbearably heavy.

"Why are you showing me this now?"

"Because tomorrow you're going to do something with that ledger," Okafor said. "Don't look surprised. You carried it out of that basement like a woman carrying a baby out of a fire. You're not going to bury it in the yard and you're not going to burn it, because you're a teacher, and teachers believe evidence does something." She almost smiled. "So. Tomorrow, someone from the state is coming to Grayhollow. An investigator. Because last night, while you were holding a door against the dark, I was on the telephone to a cousin of mine in the capital, and I told her things I've been swallowing for fifty-one years, and she made some calls, and there is a woman driving up from the city as we speak whose job is exactly the kind of arithmetic you're good at."

Lena stared at her. "You called the state. Last night. While I was—"

"While you were busy, yes. Somebody had to make the boring phone calls. Heroics are for the young." Okafor gathered the photographs back into their oilcloth, unhurried. "But listen to me now, girl, because this is the important part. That investigator is going to want the ledger, and she's going to want your testimony, and she's going to want names — the founders, the families, everyone who ever signed. And you're going to give them to her. And then this town is going to know it was you. The woman with no reflection. The outsider. The one who broke it open." She met Lena's eyes, and behind the warmth there was steel, decades of it. "They will smile at you in the grocery store and slash your tires in the same week. Are you ready for that?"

Lena thought about it honestly, the way she tried to do everything — grading herself fairly, even now, even in this.

"No," she said. "But I don't think ready is a requirement. I think it's just a thing you do anyway. Like attendance."

Okafor barked a laugh — a real one, sudden and unguarded — and then pressed her hand briefly over her mouth as though embarrassed by it, and Lena found herself laughing too, and for a moment the two of them sat in the yellow kitchen laughing at absolutely nothing funny at all, which was, Lena would think later, the most human moment the Halloway house had hosted in a hundred years.

"One more thing," Okafor said, when they'd settled. She slid a final photograph across the table. It was old, creased down the middle, a class picture from 1963: rows of children on courthouse steps, squinting into a sun that had probably seemed eternal. "Third row, second from the left. That's me. Look at the girl next to me."

Lena looked. A small girl with braids and a gap-toothed grin, one hand thrown casually over young Adaeze's shoulder like they were best friends, like sisters.

On the back, in faded pencil, a name: *Ruthie C.*

Lena's breath stopped.

"She was taken the same month I was," Okafor said quietly. "Returned the same week. We were the only two, that year, that came back. We swore we'd watch out for each other. Then she grew up and took the teaching post, and I begged her not to, and she said—" Okafor's voice caught and steadied. "She said somebody had to hold the line from the inside. She wrote to me every week for thirty years. Her last letter came the day before she died. I never opened it."

She pushed a sealed envelope across the table. Lena's name — no. Not Lena's name. *For the one who comes after,* in Ruth Calloway's careful teacher's handwriting.

Lena picked it up. The paper was thin and cold, and through the window behind Okafor, the last light was going out of the sky, and somewhere down the road, faint but unmistakable, she heard a car engine climbing the hill toward Grayhollow — the investigator, arriving early, arriving tonight.

"Well," Lena said, and broke the seal.
