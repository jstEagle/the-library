## 15. Juno's Room

The wall behind the linen press was wrong.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the only thing I had left by then. The house had been eating my records for two days — pages going soft and blank in my hands like wet paper, photographs developing into gray smears, Marta calling me *sir* in a voice that had stopped trying to place me. Every document I'd hidden inside Winterbourne House had begun to die. But the wall — the wall was wrong in the opposite direction. It was too *right*.

The rest of the east corridor had settled over a century and a half: plaster gone hairline-cracked, wallpaper faded to the color of weak tea, skirting boards scuffed by six generations of feet. This section of wall was seamless. No cracks. No fading. The wallpaper matched the pattern used elsewhere in the house but not the pattern's *age* — it was as if a piece of 1849 had been cut out of time and pasted over whatever stood behind it.

And there was a draft. Cold air moving against my knuckles when I pressed them flat to the paper, faint but steady, like breath through teeth.

I had come to the east wing looking for anything Odile had touched. Her diary — the ledger of small mercies, as I'd come to think of it — mentioned a room she shared with Tessaly "above the chapel stair," and I'd spent an afternoon finding nothing above the chapel stair but dust and pigeons. But the diary also said something else, something I'd read four times before its meaning landed:

*Juno keeps her room as she kept it. We agreed it should stay.*

Kept it as she kept it. Not *kept her room*. Kept it *as she kept it* — preserved, maintained, held in amber. Someone had been tending this room for a hundred and seventy years, and no one alive had told me it existed.

I went back downstairs for the crowbar Marta kept in the boot room, and on the way I passed the tall mirror at the landing turn. My reflection raised the crowbar a full three seconds after I did. It looked, I thought, less like lag now than like rehearsal. Like something practicing being me, getting the timing down, preparing for the moment it would no longer need to wait.

I didn't look at it again. I walked faster.

* * *

The bricks came out easier than they should have. That frightened me more than resistance would have. A wall built to hide something fights you; this one seemed to have been waiting, mortar crumbling at the first honest pull, dust sighing out of the gap like a held breath finally released. Behind it: darkness, cold, and a smell I couldn't immediately name — lavender, yes, but under it something older and cleaner, the smell of a room that has been closed so long the air itself has gone archival.

My phone flashlight showed me a short passage, maybe two meters, ending at a door. Oak, iron latch, no lock. Carved into the door's lower panel, small enough that you'd miss it unless you were kneeling, were four sets of initials:

**O.B.T.J.**

Odile. Bram. Tessaly. Juno.

I knelt there longer than I needed to. There is a particular kind of fear that looks, from the inside, exactly like reverence, and I have never learned to tell them apart.

The door opened without sound. Hinges recently oiled. Someone had been here. Someone had been coming here, quietly, for years — Serafina, I would learn later, every month on the anniversary, though she would deny the tenderness of it even while admitting the fact.

The room beyond was small and perfect and terrible.

Terrible because it was *lived in*. Not lived in the way ruins are lived in by mice and mold — lived in the way a room is lived in when its owner has only just stepped out. A narrow bed with a quilted coverlet, the fabric unfaded where a body's weight had pressed it. A washstand, pitcher and bowl, a hairbrush with long silver strands still caught in the bristles. A wooden chair drawn up to a writing desk, and on the desk, a candle stub worn down to a coin of wax, and beside it —

A journal. Leather-bound, water-stained, tied shut with a ribbon gone gray. And beneath it, squared neatly, a folded dress of good black wool, laid out the way clothes are laid out for a funeral or a wedding, though which one I couldn't have said.

I stood in the doorway and did not go in. Some instinct older than documentation told me that crossing this threshold was a decision, and that once made, it could not be unmade. Everything else in this house had been lies wearing the shape of inheritance. Whatever was in this room was true, and truth, I was learning, is not gentle. Truth is the thing you break yourself against.

Then I thought of Aurel in his coffin with his scar on the wrong cheek, and of my own reflection rehearsing my gestures in the dark glass, and of my notes dissolving page by page in a house determined to unwrite me, and I understood that I had already crossed every threshold that mattered. I went in.

* * *

The journal opened to the middle, because that's how journals fall when their owners read them often, and the first words I saw were these:

*They asked me again last night if I was certain, and I confess I laughed at them. As if certainty were a coat one might mislay. Bram wept. Odile did not weep, because Odile does not weep where we can see it, but she held my hands so hard I will wear the marks to church Sunday and be glad of them. Only Tessaly is angry, and her anger is the truest kindness in this house, because it means she cannot imagine losing me, and I can. That is the whole difference between us, and it is why it must be me.*

I sat down on the floor with my back against the bed — I could not bring myself to sit in the chair; the chair felt like hers in a way the floor did not — and I turned back to the beginning.

Her hand was beautiful. Round-bellied letters, confident, the writing of someone taught late and grateful for it. The early entries were domestic, almost funny: complaints about the cook's temper, about Bram's singing ("he flattens every note he loves, which is why we let him sing them"), about the master's new mirrors arriving in crates from Venice, each one wrapped in oilcloth "like a body prepared for burial, which Mrs. Hearn says is unlucky to joke of, so of course we all joked of nothing else for a week."

I read the house's sickness arrive in real time. The master — Elias Winterbourne, whose portrait hung in the great hall with the inscription FOUNDER beneath it, a title I now knew to be a lie with a frame around it — had bought the mirrors in the spring of 1848. By autumn, entries like this began:

*Mr. E. stands before the long glass in the gallery for hours. Cook says he is admiring himself. Cook is a fool. He is not admiring. He is listening. And whatever is answering him has begun to answer in his own voice, which is how a thing teaches itself to be you.*

*The maids will not go up the west stair after dark. Mary saw her mother's face in the washstand glass, and Mary's mother is buried in Dorset, and Mary says the face smiled at her "the way she smiled the day before she died," which is a sentence I wish I had never heard and cannot stop hearing.*

*It wears Mr. E. now. I do not mean a mask. I mean it sits behind his eyes the way a lodger sits behind a curtain, and sometimes the curtain moves. Last night at supper he told a story about his boyhood in Lisbon that Mr. E. told us himself, word for word, except that in this telling his brother drowned instead of living, and he watched our faces as he said it, hungry, the way a man watches to see whether his lie has taken root.*

And then, in a hand suddenly smaller, pressed hard into the page:

*Bram found the old rite. In the priest's books, of all places, misfiled among the psalms — a binding hymn, four voices, sung at the hour when the glass is thinnest. It will hold the thing in its own country, the world behind the world. But the hymn demands a price, because everything true demands a price, and the price is this: one of the four singers must be given up to forgetting. Not death. Worse. Everyone who ever loved her will lose the memory of having loved her. Her name will slide out of every mouth that ever spoke it. She will walk out of the world's mind like a guest leaving a party early, and no one will notice the empty space where she stood, because the space itself will close.*

*Four of us. One price. We drew lots like children and I cheated.*

*I cheated. I marked the lot. I have been asking God's forgiveness for the method and none for the act, because I am not sorry. If it must be one of us, it will be the one who chose it freely, because a debt paid unwillingly is not paid, only postponed, and I will not have my people living postponed lives.*

I stopped reading. My hands were shaking, and I set the journal on my knees and looked around the little room — at the brush with her silver hair, at the dress folded for a future that never came, at the candle burned down by a woman who sat in this exact spot writing herself out of existence on purpose — and I understood that I was reading the diary of a person who had already decided, months before the night of the hymn, what she was willing to lose.

I thought of Serafina burning the ledger page. Of Tessaly's descendant spending sixty years refusing to finish a song. Of mercy breaking a seal. Of my own meticulous boxes of records, my photographs, my ledgers — my terror, always, of being forgotten, of evidence rotting, of the past slipping loose.

Juno had been afraid of nothing I had ever been afraid of. She had been afraid only of the postponement. Of other people paying for her safety.

* * *

The later entries were shorter. The hymn approached. She wrote the way people write when they are packing a life into fewer and fewer words.

*Odile has written out our parts fair, on music paper she stole from the schoolroom. We practice in the chapel loft with the door barred, quiet as thieves, and Bram still flattens every note he loves. Tessaly will not look at me. I have caught her twice trying to trade lots with Odile behind my back, as if I wouldn't notice, as if I haven't known her hands since we were girls scalding sheets together. I have told Odile to watch her. Love makes people stupid in exactly the ways that kill you.*

*Last night I dreamed the hymn was finished and I woke in this room and could not remember my own name, and it was the sweetest terror I have ever known, because it meant it had worked, and then I remembered everything, and I lay in the dark and grieved in advance for the grief no one would get to feel for me. Do you understand? That is the strange mercy hiding inside the monstrous price. They will not mourn me. Their mourning is what I am buying away. Somewhere there is a version of this house where Tessaly grows old without an ache in her chest she cannot name, and I find I love that woman, that unwounded Tessaly, more than I love my own memory.*

*If any soul ever reads this — and I write it knowing the odds, knowing what forgetting does to paper as well as flesh — know this one thing, and let it be the whole of my testament:*

*Being forgotten by everyone is bearable. I have counted the cost as honestly as I know how, and it is bearable. What is not bearable is being forgotten by the ones who mattered — and so I have made provision. I have told them. Odile knows, and Bram, and Tessaly, though she pretends otherwise. On the last night, after the hymn, when the forgetting comes down like snow, they will carry the memory of me deliberately, the way you carry a flame through wind — cupped, protected, chosen. Forgetting takes what is left loose. It cannot take what is held on to. Let the world drop me. Let my three keep their fists closed around me until they die, and let them teach no one, and let the keeping end with them, because a memory passed hand to hand like contraband is worth ten carved in marble.*

*The ones who matter will remember. That is the entire arithmetic. Everything else is bookkeeping.*

I read that passage three times. Then I did something I had not done since Aurel died: I put my head in my hands and I wept, alone, in a dead woman's room, behind a wall built to keep her truth safe from a family built to keep it silent.

Because I saw it then — saw it whole, the way you see a landscape when lightning holds the sky open for one white second. Six generations of Winters had inherited silence and called it loyalty. Serafina had kept the glass quiet and called it mercy. The founders' portraits hung in the hall, four servants' faces painted over with masters' names, and the one who paid the true price wasn't even granted the dignity of erasure-with-a-story. She was just *gone*, and the hymn stayed broken, and the thing in the glass grew fat on the difference.

And the ritual — the ritual I was supposed to complete, the fourth verse, the price I was supposedly fated to pay — it demanded what Juno gave. Forgetting. Erasure. The world's mind closing over the space where I stood.

Everyone had assumed the terms were fixed. Serafina assumed it. Bram's confession assumed it. Even the presence in the mirrors assumed it — it was pre-paying my price, editing me out of photographs and memories, greedy for a forfeiture it believed it was owed.

But Juno hadn't been erased by the ritual. She had *volunteered* — and volunteering changed the transaction. A debt paid unwillingly is not paid, only postponed. She wrote those words herself. Which meant the inverse must also be true: a price taken by force, extracted in advance by a hungry thing that wanted me unwritten before I ever sang — that was not payment either. That was theft dressed as destiny.

And if the terms could be bent by willingness — if Juno's free choice was the only reason the seal had ever held at all — then perhaps the final verse was not a fixed text to be recited. Perhaps it was a choice to be made, and choices, unlike texts, could be made differently.

I didn't know yet how. I only knew that the answer was in this room, in her hand, in the arithmetic she'd trusted me — some future stranger — to find.

* * *

Near the back of the journal, wedged between the final pages, I found a loose sheet folded in quarters. Music paper, schoolroom-ruled, in Odile's fair copying hand: the founding hymn, all four voice parts, through the third verse. And beneath the third verse's final barline, in a different hand — Juno's, hurried, the ink blotted where the pen had paused too long — a fragment. Four lines of verse, half-written, crossed out, rewritten:

*What was taken, let be taken —*
*what was given, let it stand.*
*Let the ones who held the flame*
*say the name with their own—*

It broke off there. Mid-word. Mid-line. The ink blot spreading under the final stroke like a wound.

She had been writing the fourth verse herself. Before the hymn, before the forgetting, she had tried to author her own ending — and the fragment suggested she hadn't been trying to escape the price. She'd been trying to *amend* it. *What was taken, let be taken.* Accept the cost. But — *what was given, let it stand.* Let the giving count. Let the ones who held the flame say the name.

Remembering instead of forgetting. Witness instead of silence. She had seen it, a hundred and seventy years ago, in this room, by this burned-down candle — and she had run out of time, or courage, or ink, and the verse had died unfinished, and the hymn had closed on Bram's mercy and Tessaly's refusal, and the breach had been breathing ever since.

I copied the fragment into my notebook — the notebook from the car, the one copy the house hadn't reached — pressing hard enough to emboss the page beneath. Then I sat for a long while in the lavender-and-dust dark, holding a dead woman's unfinished sentence, feeling it weigh more than the coffin upstairs.

When I finally stood, my legs were stiff and my eyes were raw, and I noticed for the first time what was pinned to the wall above the desk, yellowed and curling: a scrap of embroidery, crude, the work of someone teaching herself stitches. Thread on linen, in colors gone soft, four small figures holding hands beneath four stitched letters.

O. B. T. J.

And below them, in tiny cross-stitch, a single line I hadn't been able to read from the doorway:

*THE ONES WHO MATTER WILL REMEMBER.*

I took the music sheet. I left everything else exactly as it was — the dress, the brush, the candle, the journal open to her testament — because rooms like this are not tombs. They are instructions.

In the corridor, my reflection was waiting at the far end of the east passage, standing perfectly still in the dark glass, watching me with my own eyes. It didn't lag anymore. It simply waited, patient as mortar, and as I passed it raised one hand — not mimicking me this time, but greeting me, or beckoning — and its mouth moved around words I couldn't hear.

I didn't stop to read its lips. I had a hymn to finish, and for the first time since I'd come home to bury my brother, I knew not only that it could be finished, but *how* — and I knew exactly who I needed standing beside me when midnight came: the woman who had spent her whole life refusing to sing, and the two others brave enough, or foolish enough, to stand in a house full of mirrors and raise their voices anyway.

There were four parts in the hymn. There were four of us, if the priest could be persuaded.

And somewhere in the walls, something that had waited six generations for the fourth verse heard me humming Juno's fragment as I walked — and began, very softly, to hum along.
