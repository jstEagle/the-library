## 21. The Unreflected Man

The morning after the mirrors shattered, Winterbourne House was the quietest place Rafael had ever stood in.

Not silent — he had learned the difference in the last three weeks. Silence was the held breath of the halls, the lag, the waiting. Quiet was different. Quiet was a house that had nothing left to hide. Sunlight came through the great hall's tall windows and lay on the floor in plain rectangles, and the floor was only a floor, and the air smelled of dust and cut grass and, faintly, of the vinegar Marta used on the windows, because there were no windows left to clean and she cleaned them anyway, out of habit, out of grief for the ritual of it.

Rafael stood where the great mirror had been. There was a scar on the wall where it had hung for a hundred and seventy years — a paler rectangle, a ghost of a ghost — and he raised his hand toward it, the way he had raised it toward a hundred mirrors in the last month, the scientist's test, the metronome of the self.

Nothing looked back.

He had known it would be this way. He had sung the third verse with his whole chest, had felt his name being unpicked from the world stitch by stitch, and he had chosen the fourth verse anyway. But knowing a thing and standing in the evidence of it were different countries, and he found he could not stop testing the border.

He raised his hand. No hand rose to meet it. He tilted his head; nothing tilted with him. He took out his phone and turned the camera on himself and the screen showed the wall behind him, the scar of the mirror, the morning light — and where a man should have stood, a soft smudge, a lens flare, an error in the glass.

"Right," he said aloud, because saying things aloud had become a way of proving he was still in the room. "Documented."

His voice worked. That was something. He had spent the night after the shattering — he refused to call it the ritual, or the sacrifice, or any of the words Serafina kept reaching for — lying in his childhood bed listening to the house settle, testing himself like a patient cataloguing symptoms. Voice: present. Weight on the floorboards: present, creaking in the third stair as always. Hunger: present, and enormous, the way hunger is after you have sung yourself nearly out of existence. Reflection: gone. Utterly, comprehensively gone. He had checked the black water of the horse trough, the polished brass of the door knocker, the dark screen of the television, the eyes of the cat that had come back to the kitchen door that morning as if nothing had happened. The cat had looked straight through him and yowled to be let in, and he had let it in, and it had wound around his ankles and then looked up, puzzled, at the space where the hand that opened the door seemed to come from, and had decided, in the way of cats, not to care.

The cat could smell him. The world could not see him.

He went down to the kitchen, where Marta was making tea, and this was the first mercy of the day: she looked up when he came in. Not through him. *At* him.

"There's bread," she said. "And don't stand in the doorway like a ghost, Rafael, you're not one yet."

"Not yet," he agreed, and sat, and she set a cup before him, and the cup steamed, and his hands were steady around it, and for four or five minutes it was almost possible to believe the cost was a thing that could be paid in installments.

* * *

The priest — Father Anselm, who had arrived at the chapel at midnight with his cassock buttoned wrong and his skepticism worn like armor and left at three in the morning with neither — came by at ten. Rafael watched him come up the drive through the window and braced himself for the sliding gaze, the way strangers' eyes had begun to treat him: a glance that touched him and kept going, snagging on nothing, the way a hand slides off wet stone.

But Anselm came up the steps, knocked, and said, when Rafael opened the door, "You look terrible. Have you slept?"

"You can see me."

Anselm gave him the look he imagined the man gave parishioners who asked obvious questions about transubstantiation. "I sang with you until my voice gave out. I held Marta's hand when the glass showed her Daniel. I watched a dead man's face come apart like smoke. Yes, Rafael. I can see you." He stepped inside, out of habit stamping his boots, and Rafael understood that he was standing inside the smallest parish in the world: the congregation of four, of which he was the only one who could not be seen anywhere else.

They sat in the morning room, which was the room with the fewest scars, and Rafael did what he always did with unbearable things: he made an inventory.

"Test one," he said, and held up his notebook. "You can see this."

"I can see the notebook. I can see you holding it."

"Test two." He passed the notebook across the table. "Read the last line."

Anselm read it. "'Fourth verse, altered per Juno Vasse's hand: *what was chosen, let it be kept; what was loved, let it be named.*'" He looked up. "Your handwriting is a disgrace for a man who photographs ashes."

"The house was eating my records. I was writing fast." He took the notebook back and felt something in his chest unclench — a knot he had been carrying since the third verse, when Serafina had looked through him and his own name had slipped away like a coin through a hole in a pocket. The documents survived. Whatever had been done to him inside the house's borders, the paper outside his own skin still knew him. His handwriting still said *Rafael* to anyone who read it.

"Test three," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he intended. "Outside. If I walk to the village. Will they see me?"

Anselm was quiet for a moment. He was a big man, weathered, with a face built for doubt, and Rafael had come to trust the way doubt moved across it like weather across a field.

"I don't know," the priest said. "I walked back to the rectory at four this morning and I remembered every word of the hymn, which I did not know before midnight, and I remembered your face, which I'd barely looked at before. Whatever you did, it wrote you into the people who were there. That's what the old ritual did too — that's what Odile paid. But the old ritual wrote a *forgetting*. You wrote—" He stopped, turned his cup, turned it again. "You wrote the opposite. I don't know what the opposite does when it meets a stranger. Nobody's ever done the opposite before."

*The opposite,* Rafael thought. *Six generations of Winters keeping the glass quiet with silence, and the counter-spell is a hymn of witness.* It was so simple it was almost insulting. Almost. He had felt the third verse taking him apart; there was nothing insulting about the price. Only the terms had changed, and the terms were everything.

* * *

He walked to the village that afternoon, because Rafael Winterbourne had never in his life met a question he could leave untested.

The road down from the house ran through beech woods, and the light came down green and moving, and for the first mile he felt almost like a man on an ordinary walk. Then he passed the first house on the lane, and Mrs. Aldous — who had known him since he was nine, who had sold him lemonade from a card table every summer, who had wept at Aurel's funeral two days before the world ended — was pruning her roses, and she looked up, and her eyes went across him like water over a stone.

"Good afternoon," Rafael said.

Mrs. Aldous looked at the empty lane, frowned faintly at the sound, decided on wind, and went back to her roses.

He stood in the lane for a long moment. A car came up behind him and he stepped aside and it passed — the driver's eyes flicked to the mirror, to the road, to the empty verge where a man was standing, and flicked away. Not hostility. Not fear. Just the smooth, total, frictionless absence of him, the way you do not see a particular tree in a forest. He tried the shop. The bell over the door rang — the bell knew him, at least — and the shopkeeper said "Be with you in a moment" to the room in general, and Rafael stood at the counter and said "It's Rafael Winterbourne, from the house," and the shopkeeper looked directly at him, through him, and said to the room, "Hello? Anyone there?" and Rafael walked out with the bell ringing behind him like a small, stupid, faithful alarm.

He sat on the churchyard wall across from the shop and let it happen to him. Grief, he had learned, was best documented in real time, before the mind could edit it into something bearable.

*Entry,* he wrote. *The world has a blind spot exactly my size.*

It was not that they forgot him. Forgetting would have been a wound with edges. This was worse and cleaner: he had simply never been in their field of view at all. Mrs. Aldous had not failed to recognize him. Mrs. Aldous had never had anything to recognize. He was a gap in the lane's population, a bell that rang itself. He thought of Juno Vasse, one hundred and seventy years in her bricked-up room, writing by candlelight that being forgotten by the living didn't matter if the ones who mattered remembered — and he understood, finally, that she had not been consoling herself. She had been describing the actual physics of it. The forgetting did not reach the people who had sung with you. It did not need to. The hymn's price was paid in strangers.

He thought of Aurel, and had to stop writing for a while.

* * *

Serafina found him at the churchyard wall at dusk. She had walked down from the house — he saw her coming along the lane, small and straight-backed in her black coat, seventy-nine years old and moving like the walk was a debt she intended to pay in full — and when she reached him she did not say *there you are* or *I've been looking for you*. She said, "Mrs. Aldous's roses have black spot. Someone ought to tell her."

"You can see me."

"I sang the alto line in 1963, Rafael, and the descant at midnight two nights ago. I am apparently stuck with you." She sat beside him on the wall with the care of a woman whose knees had opinions, and they looked together across the lane at the lit windows of the village, at the small warm rooms where people were eating dinner and not seeing the two figures on the wall, because they could not see her either.

That was the thing he had not understood until he watched the lane fail to register her. Serafina had spent her whole life in this village, and the village had spent her whole life not-quite-seeing her — the Tessaly woman, the odd one from the house, the one who never finished sentences about family history. She had been half-erased for decades by refusal alone, by the discipline of not singing, of not finishing, of keeping the glass quiet with silence. The hymn had only finished the job.

"You knew," Rafael said. "When you offered me the fourth verse. You knew what it would cost, and you also knew you'd pay a share of it just for standing in the choir."

"I knew what the old verse cost," Serafina said. "I did not know what yours would do. Nobody did." She was quiet a moment. "It was a good verse. The alteration." She said it the way she said everything that mattered — flatly, looking straight ahead. "Juno would have liked it. *What was loved, let it be named.* Tessaly broke the seal over a line like that. Bram wrote that she refused to let Juno pay, and the whole house has been bleeding for a hundred and seventy years because mercy interrupted a spell. And you — you put the mercy *inside* the spell. You made the remembering the seal itself." Her voice did something very small at the end. "I have been refusing to finish that hymn for fifty years because I thought the choice was between a lie and a sacrifice. You found a third thing. I don't know how."

"I had a better archive," Rafael said. "Four servants who wrote everything down. That's the whole trick. Somebody loved them enough to keep records, and the records kept the truth alive until somebody could use it."

Serafina turned and looked at him then, fully, the way no one else in the world could, and Rafael understood that this — this exact gaze, this being *held* in someone's attention — was now a currency he had in exactly three accounts on earth, plus a cat.

"Your brother," she said. "You haven't said his name since the hall. Not once. I've been listening."

Rafael looked down at his notebook. The last entry was the blind-spot line. Below it, the page was blank, and the blankness had a shape.

"The thing in the glass wore him," he said. "For three weeks it wore him at me. *Aurel, breathing. Aurel at the end of the corridor. Aurel begging me to stop.* And the whole time the actual Aurel was in the ground under the wrong name, with a stranger's corpse wearing his face above him." He turned the pen in his fingers. "I keep waiting to feel that the real one is gone. But it turns out I buried him the day I arrived. The singing didn't take anything. It only — closed the account." He looked up. "Say it with me. I want it on the record."

Serafina did not ask what he meant. She was Tessaly's blood; she understood records.

"Aurel Winterbourne," she said. "Born four minutes after you. Died at thirty-four. Liked the wrong music. Laughed like a door coming off its hinges."

"Laughed like a door coming off its hinges," Rafael repeated, and wrote it, and the writing was steady, and the dusk came down over the village, and the lit windows burned on, warm and blind.

* * *

They walked back up in the dark, and at the gates of Winterbourne House Rafael stopped, because the house had one more thing to show him, and he had learned to let it show things before it told him.

The great hall was dark, but the scar on the wall where the mirror had hung was not dark. It glowed faintly, the way a bruise glows through skin — a rectangle of pale light with no source, and in it, very faint, like a photograph developing in cold water, there were reflections.

Not his. He stepped into the light and the light did not take him; he had no reflection to give. But the rectangle filled, slowly, with the hall as it had been — the great mirror, whole, and in it a procession of faces: Odile with her chin up and her eyes furious and alive; Bram, big and careful, his hands folded like a man at a funeral he intends to survive; Tessaly, who had Serafina's exact stubborn mouth; Juno, last, smallest, smiling like someone who has already decided and is at peace with it. Four servants in a master's mirror, six generations deep, waiting in the glass the way a letter waits in a drawer.

And beneath them, cut into the wall where no one had ever thought to look because the mirror had covered it for two centuries, there was writing. Old writing. Chisel-deep. Rafael put his hand on it and read it by the light of the dead mirror, and his heart went very loud and very quiet at the same time.

*ODILE. BRAM. TESSALY. JUNO.*

Four names. Not in a diary, not in a hymnal's margin, not in a ledger someone could burn — cut into the load-bearing stone of the house itself, by hands that had decided the founding of the place should be signed by its actual founders. The house had been wearing the lie like a coat for a hundred and seventy years, and underneath, the truth had been in the walls the entire time.

"They signed it," Rafael said. "They signed the house."

Behind him, Serafina drew a breath that shook. Marta, in the kitchen doorway with a candle, said nothing at all, but her hand went to her mouth.

And the pale rectangle brightened — just slightly, just enough — and Rafael understood that the house was not finished with him. That the hymn had closed the breach, but a breach is not the same as a debt, and six generations of silence had left other things unwritten: a grave under the wrong name that needed a right one; a hymnal on a lectern with a fourth verse that existed now only in three living memories and one notebook; a village full of people who had sung in their sleep without knowing it, whose reflections had been a choir, and who deserved to know what their mouths had done. And a ledger, his ledger, nearly finished, missing only its last entries.

He opened the notebook to a clean page. His pen hovered. Somewhere in the walls, the four names held their breath.

"Entry," Rafael said, and began to write.
