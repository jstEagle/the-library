## 7. What the Mirror Keeps

Felix had decided, by the third morning in the cottage on Rue du Sel, that the house was simply old, and old houses did things.

They settled. They ticked and groaned as the fog came off the marsh and pressed its damp fingers into every seam of the walls. They kept strange hours. His grandmother's house in Lyon — the one real home he'd ever known, before she died and the aunts divided her furniture like carrion birds with good manners — had groaned too, and he had learned to read those sounds the way other children learned to read weather. *That creak means cold coming. That rattle means wind from the sea.*

The cottage spoke a dialect he didn't know yet, but he was learning it. He wrote down what he learned, because that was what he did. The ledger was nearly full now: the exact pitch of the floorboard outside his bedroom (a low B-flat, sharp in damp weather), the way the kitchen tap coughed twice before producing water, the fact that the front door required you to lift the handle while turning the key or it sulked and refused to open at all. He found this last one charming, in a stubborn-grandmother sort of way. The house had opinions. He could respect that.

He was less able to respect the mirror.

* * *

It started small, the way all terrible things do — politely, almost apologetically.

He was brushing his teeth on Thursday morning when he noticed that the man in the bathroom mirror was holding his toothbrush slightly wrong. Not wrong, exactly. Just... not yet right. Felix moved the brush to the left side of his mouth; the reflection followed, but with the lag of a bad telephone connection, a half-breath behind. He stopped. The reflection went on brushing for one more stroke.

Felix spat, rinsed, and stood very still, gripping the sink with both hands, watching himself watch himself.

"Sleep deprivation," he told his reflection, and his reflection agreed approximately one second later.

He was a teacher. Teachers know all about sleep deprivation — they carry it like a professional credential — and he knew precisely what it did to a mind. It made the world feel slightly dubbed, out of sync, like watching yourself through frosted glass. So he did what any sensible man does with an inexplicable phenomenon: he tested it scientifically, then wrote it down.

That evening he sat on the edge of the bathtub with his ledger open on his knees and a box of matches beside him. He struck one. In the mirror, the flame appeared a beat after the sulfur smell reached his nose. He dropped a coin from waist height and caught it; the mirror-Felix dropped his coin late, caught it late, looked up late. He held up the ledger itself, open to Tuesday's entry, and felt something cold walk down his spine, because the mirror showed the page blank for a full second before the ink bloomed across it like frost forming in reverse.

One second. Always one second. As if the glass were somewhere else entirely — a room just like this one, a few meters removed, receiving news of him slowly, by post.

He wrote: *Bathroom mirror lags approx. 1 sec. All objects consistent. Cause unknown. Possibly plumbing-related.* He stared at "plumbing-related" for a long moment, because even his handwriting seemed to be begging him to come up with something better, and then he closed the book and went to bed and lay awake listening to the house breathe.

* * *

Friday brought fog so thick that the school bell seemed to come from inside his own chest, and Friday night the mirrors stopped merely lagging and began to lie.

Or not to lie. That was the thing he couldn't decide, standing in his bedroom at two in the morning in his socks, heart hammering. Because the mirror over the dresser wasn't showing him his bedroom. It was showing a bedroom — *this* bedroom — but different. The wallpaper was a faded rose pattern instead of the current gray. The bed was iron-framed, hung with lace. And there were people in it.

A woman sat on the stool before the vanity — the same stool, he realized, the same worn maple stool that was currently sitting under the window three feet to his left. She was braiding a child's hair. Her hands moved with the absent fluency of someone who has done this ten thousand times, dividing, crossing, tying off with a scrap of ribbon. The child sat patiently between her knees, a boy of six or seven, thin, dark-haired, swinging his feet because they didn't reach the floor.

Felix's first thought — and he would be ashamed of this later, though not very — was *burglars*. His second thought was *actors*. His third thought arrived without permission and stayed without invitation:

*I know that face.*

Not the woman's. The boy's.

Felix crossed the room in three steps and put his hand flat against the cool glass, and the woman looked up — not at him, through him, past him, at some point in her own century — and smiled, and said something he couldn't hear, and went back to the braid. The boy swung his feet. The lamp on the vanity burned with a soft yellow light that made no dent whatsoever in Felix's actual, electrically lit bedroom.

He stood there until the scene faded — not cut off, but faded, the way a photograph yellows, the rose wallpaper deepening to gray, the figures going translucent and then gone, until the mirror showed only Felix again, pale and barefoot and one second behind himself.

He did not sleep. He wrote, in a hand much less steady than usual: *Bedroom mirror, 2:10 a.m., showed room as it was. Woman braiding child's hair. Boy approx. 6 yrs. Duration approx. 4 min. The boy—*

He stopped writing. He looked at the sentence. He picked the pen back up and finished it, because he was a documenter, and documenters finish their sentences even when the sentences are screaming:

*The boy has my face. Not similar. Mine.*

* * *

Saturday he did what any reasonable person would do: he avoided the mirrors, and then, being Felix Joubert, spent the entire day failing to avoid them and taking notes instead.

There were more mirrors in the cottage than he'd noticed when he'd moved in. This is the trick of houses — they show you what you need on the first day and keep the rest in reserve. A tall oval in the hallway he'd assumed was a painting under a sheet (it hadn't been under a sheet; it had been turned to face the wall, which should have been his first clue). A small round one above the kitchen shelf, spotted with age. A long narrow one in the stairwell, positioned so that anyone climbing the stairs would meet their own eyes halfway up, which struck Felix now as either poor design or extremely deliberate design.

By afternoon he had catalogued seven mirrors, and every one of them was showing him something that wasn't Saturday.

The hallway oval gave him a winter scene: snow against the window, a man in shirtsleeves chopping wood in the yard beyond, breath steaming. The kitchen mirror showed hands kneading bread — just hands, forearms, a flour-dusted apron, nothing else, as if the rest of the cook had been cropped out by some careful editor. The stairwell mirror was the worst. It showed the stairs themselves, and on the stairs a boy sat doing sums in a copybook, tongue between his teeth, pencil gripped wrong, getting the answers right anyway.

Felix sat down on the fourth step, next to where the boy wasn't, and watched him work.

"That's the seven-times table," Felix said aloud, stupidly, uselessly. "You've got your sevens wrong. Seven times eight is fifty-six."

The boy didn't look up. But his pencil paused, hovered, and then corrected the figure — fifty-six — and continued.

Felix walked out of the house and stood in the lane breathing fog until his lungs ached, and then he walked back in, because it was his house, or his rented house, or whatever it was, and running away from your own staircase seemed undignified.

* * *

Here is what he knew, and here is what he refused to know, and the distance between those two things was exactly the width of the family Bible on the parlor shelf.

He knew the surname on the flyleaf was Joubert. He knew the child's drawings in the bureau drawer were signed with a wobbly, triumphant *Félix* — and he had told himself, firmly, that Félix was a common name, that half the boys in France named Félix had drawn lopsided houses with smoking chimneys, that coincidence was the mortar of the universe. He knew Madame Oury had gone very still when he'd mentioned his surname at the interview — a stillness he had filed, at the time, under *bureaucratic caution*, the look of a woman checking whether a new hire would prove troublesome.

He took the Bible down now and set it on the kitchen table under the lamp, and opened it to the family pages at the front, where births and deaths and marriages are recorded in fading inks, each hand different, each generation's entries smaller and more cramped than the last, as if the family were running out of room or courage.

Joubert, Étienne, m. Rousseau, Camille, 1889.
Joubert, Henri, b. 1891.
Joubert, Marguerite, b. 1894.

And below that, in a later hand, entries that thinned out like a stream losing itself in sand. No deaths recorded after 1943. No marriages. The page simply stopped, mid-family, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets what he was saying.

Marguerite. He turned the name over. It snagged on something. When he was small — four, five — there had been a word the aunts used when they wanted him quiet, a name invoked like a bogeyman or a warning: *Sit still, or Marguerite will get you.* He'd assumed she was a story. Children assume everything frightening is a story; it's how they survive the true ones.

In the mirror over the dresser — he could see it from the kitchen doorway if he stood at exactly the right angle, and he hated that he knew that — the rose-papered room flickered back to life. The woman was at the vanity again, but this time she wasn't braiding hair. She was writing. She sat with a pen in her hand and a book open before her, and she wrote and wrote, and occasionally she looked up toward the door of her own room as if listening for footsteps, and Felix understood, with a certainty that bypassed his brain entirely and lodged somewhere under his ribs, that she was keeping a ledger. That she had been the keeper of records before him. That the meticulousness in his own handwriting — the dates, the times, the compulsive precision of it — had been inherited the way eye color is inherited, passed down a line of people whose whole survival strategy was *write it down, write it down, or it never happened.*

And then the woman in the mirror did something new. She stopped writing. She turned her head — fully, deliberately, the way no reflection turns — and looked directly out of the glass at Felix standing in the kitchen doorway, and her mouth formed words, slow and clear, shaped for lip-reading, and he read them twice to be sure:

*You're late. I've been waiting since 1943.*

The mirror went dark. Not black — just ordinary, showing the empty bedroom, the gray walls, the bed he hadn't slept in properly for three nights.

Felix sat down hard on a kitchen chair. The house ticked around him, settling, breathing, patient. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something reading over his shoulder.

"It's remembering me," he said to the empty kitchen, and heard how mad it sounded, and said it again anyway, because saying things aloud was how he checked whether they were real. "This house isn't haunted. It's *remembering* me."

But that wasn't quite right either, and he was a precise man, and precision was the only weapon he had ever owned. He got the ledger. He wrote, slowly:

*Correction. The house isn't remembering me. It can't remember what it never met. The house is remembering something that hasn't happened yet. Or — worse — it's rehearsing. These aren't memories. They're preparations. A stage dressed before the play. A table laid before dinner.*

He looked at the word *dinner* and did not like it, and could not have said why, and wrote underneath, in letters that pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*Tomorrow I go back to the attic. There's an almanac up there. Two hundred years of harvest moons, and numbers written beside them like a tally. I counted nineteen students in my classroom on Monday. The register says twenty-six.*

*Seven missing. Seven marks on the last page of that almanac, in fresh ink.*

*I am going to find out what this family has been counting.*

Upstairs, faintly, from behind the darkened glass of the dresser mirror, came the sound of a pen scratching across paper — steady, patient, keeping time with his own.
