## 8. Madame Oury's Ledger

The morning after the almanac, Felix did not sleep. He sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open before him and the almanac beside it, and he compared numbers the way a man might compare two photographs of the same face—one alive, one not.

Two hundred and eleven. That was the number beside this year's harvest moon, and it was also, he now understood with a certainty that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone, the number of names the house had taken since the first moon was marked in that careful antique hand. Two hundred and eleven people, fed to a cottage on Rue du Sel one moon at a time, and the village had prospered every single year. The fishing had been good. The apples were famous as far as Rennes. Nobody in Sainte-Brume, he was quite sure, had ever gone hungry.

He had taught school for nine years before coming here. He had worked in cities where children went hungry in plain sight, and he had told himself that was the world's ordinary cruelty, the kind you could only sand down, never stop. But this—this was cruelty with a ledger. Cruelty with an almanac. Cruelty that kept accounts.

And he was a man who kept accounts too. That was the joke, if it was a joke. He had spent his whole life writing things down because no one else would remember them, and now he was in a village that had industrialized the practice of forgetting, and he could not decide whether he had been summoned or simply harvested, like a field crop that had wandered in from a neighboring farm.

At eight o'clock he washed his face, noting—always noting—that the mirror in the bathroom was now a full second and a half behind him, and that this morning it had shown him a man with slightly grayer hair, as though the reflection were aging toward some appointment of its own.

Then he walked to the school.

The fog was thick that morning, the kind that turned the village into a rumor of itself. He passed the bakery and smelled bread, real bread, and hated how comforting it was. He passed Madame Guillou, who swept her step and wished him good morning with the warm smile of a woman who had absolutely no idea what he was about to do to her town, and he wished her good morning back, because he was a coward in small ways if not in large ones.

Madame Oury's office was at the end of the hall, behind a door of dark oak with a frosted glass panel. Through the frost, her silhouette was already visible, seated, motionless, as if she had been waiting for him since before he decided to come. Perhaps she had. Perhaps in Sainte-Brume the decisions were made first and the people arrived afterward to carry them out.

He knocked.

"Come in, Monsieur Joubert." Her voice carried through the oak as though the door were a formality. "You're early. Good. I've made coffee."

* * *

The office smelled of coffee and floor wax and something underneath both, something mineral and old, like the inside of a well. Oury sat behind a desk that was too large for the room, and she had, Felix noticed, cleared its surface of everything except a single ledger—bound in green leather, fat with years, its corners worn to a soft brown roundness.

It was the twin of his own ledger. Not similar. Twin. The same leather, the same stitching, the same brass clasp, and when she laid her hand flat upon it, he saw that the clasp was tarnished in exactly the pattern his was, as though both had been bought on the same day in the same shop by the same hand.

"Sit," she said, and poured the coffee herself, two cups, no ceremony. She was a small woman, Madame Oury, silver-haired and precise, with the kind of face that made you think of a headmistress from a children's book—the kind who turned out, in the third act, to have been feeding the children to something all along. "You went into the attic."

It was not a question, and Felix saw no profit in pretending otherwise. "I found the almanac."

"Mm." She sipped her coffee. "And you counted the numbers."

"Two hundred and eleven."

"Two hundred and eleven," she agreed, with the satisfaction of a teacher hearing a correct answer. "You always were quick. Your aunt was the same. Marguerite could do sums in her head faster than any child I ever taught, and I have taught children for—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes like a fish under ice, "—for a very long time."

"You knew her."

"I taught her. I taught her father, and his father, and I will teach—" she stopped herself, delicately, the way one stops oneself from saying a word that would spoil a surprise. "I teach the children of Sainte-Brume, Monsieur Joubert. That has always been my work. The school is the school. But a school is not only a place where children learn. It is a place where children are *counted*."

Felix set down his cup. His hand was steady, which surprised him. "The register," he said. "Twenty-six names. Nineteen students."

"Ah." She smiled, and it was a warm smile, a genuine one, and that was the worst part. It was the smile of a woman who loved her work. "You've noticed. Of course you've noticed. You're a documenter, Monsieur Joubert. You notice everything. That's why you were chosen."

"Chosen by whom?"

"By the house." She said it the way one says *by the weather*. "The house on Rue du Sel has stood since before the village had a name. It is older than the church. Older than the families. And like everything old, it gets hungry. We feed it, and it keeps the fog gentle, the fish fat, the apples sweet, and the graves—" she tilted her head, considering, "—the graves stay empty a very long time here. People live long in Sainte-Brume. Ask anyone."

"I'm asking you."

"And I'm telling you." She opened the green ledger.

Felix had expected, he realized later, to feel horror at what was inside it. What he felt instead was recognition, and the recognition was worse. The pages were ruled like his own. The handwriting was like his own—not identical, but the same family of hand, the same downward slashes on the sevens. And the entries were not spells or prayers or anything occult at all. They were attendance records. Dates. Names. Small annotations in a careful teacher's script.

*Went home,* one entry read. *Settled in well.* Another: *Doesn't like the cold. Happy there.* Another, simply: *No one will miss this one.*

"Every founding family tithes," Oury said, turning pages with the tenderness of a woman showing family photographs. "One name per generation, per family. The least remembered. That's the rule, and it's a merciful rule, if you think about it. The house can't take someone the village holds close—the grief would spoil the harvest, the way grief spoils milk. So we give it the ones already slipping. The quiet boy. The girl whose mother died and whose father drinks. The cousin nobody visits. The child who moved here last year and made no friends." She looked up. "The child who teaches here and has no family, no wife, no one who writes to him. Shall I go on?"

Felix's throat was dry. "The students who 'went home.'"

"They did go home." She closed the ledger gently. "The house is a home, Monsieur Joubert. It has been waiting for you your entire life. Did you really believe the cottage was a coincidence? The rent so low? The beds already made? You are a Joubert. The house has been keeping your room since 1943."

"Since Marguerite."

"Since Marguerite." For a moment—for just a moment—the warmth left her face, and what was underneath was very old and very tired and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food. "She refused, you know. Your great-aunt. She found the almanac too, clever thing, and she refused to be counted, and she ran, and the house caught her halfway across the marsh, and we wrote her out of every record in this village so thoroughly that even her own nephews forgot she'd existed. Your grandfather had a sister. He went to his grave without knowing it. That is what refusal costs." She folded her hands. "But you—you're sensible. I can see it. You document things. You understand that a record is a kindness. That a name in a ledger is a person *kept*."

"Kept," Felix repeated. "Like preserves."

"Like anything worth keeping."

The coffee had gone cold in his cup. He looked at the green ledger, at the twin clasp, at the sevens slashed downward like little graves, and he understood, finally, the shape of the trap, and it was so elegant he almost admired it. They hadn't needed to threaten him. They had only needed to give him a cottage with his family's name in the Bible, a classroom full of empty desks, and time. He had been documenting himself into the count since the day he arrived. Every entry in his ledger was a brick in the wall of his own keeping.

"Here is my offer," Oury said, and her voice softened into something almost maternal, and he understood that for her it was not performance; she had been offering this choice to Jouberts for two hundred years and she had gotten good at it. "The moon rises in nineteen days. The count needs one more. It needs a Joubert—the house prefers to finish a family line it started. If you join willingly, you choose it. You walk into the keeping with your name in your own hand, and it is—gentle. I am told it is gentle. You will be home, Monsieur Joubert. Truly home, for the first time in your life. The boy in the mirror, the woman braiding hair—they are waiting for you. You will never again be the man no one remembers."

"And if I refuse?"

The warmth returned, and it was terrible. "Then you are counted. The house takes what it takes, in its own way, at its own hour, and it is not gentle, and no one will write *went home* in your hand, because your hand will be the one writing." She rose, came around the desk, and stood beside him, small and silver and smelling of floor wax, and she rested one light hand on his shoulder like a grandmother at a confirmation. "But Monsieur Joubert—Felix, may I?—I don't think you'll refuse. I've watched you this whole month. You write everything down. A man who writes everything down is a man who wants, more than anything, to matter. And I am offering to make you matter *forever*."

He looked up at her. "Madame Oury. Who tithed you?"

The hand on his shoulder went still.

"Two hundred years," Felix said quietly. "You said you'd taught children for a very long time. Someone wrote *no one will miss this one* about a little girl once, and the house kept her, and she learned the ledger, and she became the ledger. Didn't she? You're not the keeper of the count, Madame. You're the oldest entry in it."

For three full seconds, the only sound in the room was the fog pressing against the frosted glass.

Then Madame Oury smiled, and took her hand from his shoulder, and picked up the green ledger, and said, with perfect bureaucratic pleasantness:

"Nineteen days, Monsieur Joubert. Do give my regards to your aunt."

He was three steps down the corridor when he heard her voice behind him, calling after him with the brightness of a woman announcing a school assembly:

"Oh—and Monsieur Joubert? Solène Faure will be staying late for remedial lessons this evening. Do tell her not to wait up. The fog gets thick after dark, and little girls who wander are so easily *misplaced*."

Felix stopped. The corridor, with its nineteen empty hooks and its nineteen full ones, stretched away in both directions, and somewhere behind the frosted glass of the office door, he heard—very softly, very patiently—the sound of a green ledger being opened, and a pen being dipped, and a name beginning to be written.
