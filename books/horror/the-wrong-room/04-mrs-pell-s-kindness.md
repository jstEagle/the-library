## 4. Mrs. Pell's Kindness

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was the only reason Ansel opened the door.

He had been awake since six, cataloguing. That was how he thought of it — not investigating, not worrying, merely cataloguing — standing in the hall with his notebook and counting doors the way a man might count sheep, except that sheep were supposed to change and doors were not. Seven last night when he went up. Eight this morning when he came down. The eighth stood between the kitchen and the pantry where no door had ever been, painted the same green as the others, fitted into the wall as though the wall had always wanted it there.

He had written: *Day 3. Door count discrepancy. Possible structural anomaly / possible error in prior count.* And then, underneath, in smaller letters he tried to make look like an afterthought: *The house is paying attention to me.*

So when the knock came — two soft raps, knuckles rather than fist — he stood with his hand on the latch for a long moment, listening. No breathing on the other side. No weight shifting from foot to foot. Just the ordinary sounds of a village morning: a dog somewhere, a cart, wind moving through the yew hedge along the front path.

"Mr. Winterbourne?" A woman's voice, warm as a stove. "It's Edith Pell, from next door. I've brought you bread, love."

*Love.* She said it the way other people said *good morning*, without ceremony, as if it were simply what he was called here — as if she'd been calling him that since he was small enough to be carried through this gate.

Ansel opened the door.

Mrs. Pell was perhaps seventy, small and round as a thrush, wrapped in a coat the colour of wet slate with a scarf wound up to her chin. Her hair was white and pinned in a crown of careful silver curls, and her face was one of those faces that seemed assembled entirely for kindness — soft cheeks, eyes crinkled permanently at the corners, a mouth that rested halfway to a smile. She held a cloth-covered basket against her chest with both hands, the way you'd hold something alive.

"You'll be wanting breakfast," she said, before hello, before anything. "Nobody eats properly their first week in one of these old houses. They get so busy *looking* at everything they forget to put anything inside themselves." She was already past him, or nearly — she paused at the threshold, one sensible shoe on the flagstone, and looked up at him with an expression that was suddenly, briefly, very serious. "May I come in?"

It occurred to him later — much later, when understanding had curdled everything — that she asked. That she waited. That she did not cross until he stepped back and said, "Of course, please," because whatever else Hollowmere was, its manners were older than its houses.

She bustled into the kitchen, set the basket on the table — *the table*, he noticed, the one from the photographs, the one with the chair that wasn't — and began unpacking it like a woman who had done this a thousand times in a thousand kitchens. A loaf still warm enough to fog the cloth. Butter wrapped in waxed paper. A jar of honey with a handwritten label. Blackcurrant jam. A small pot of dripping.

"There's no shame in taking charity your first month," she said firmly, catching his expression. "There's shame in letting a neighbour go hungry when you could help it. Sit down, love. You're making the room nervous, hovering."

"The room's nervous?"

"Rooms are never anything but nervous." She found his kettle without being told where it lived, filled it at the sink, set it on the hob. She moved through his grandmother's kitchen the way water moves through a channel it has cut itself — knowing every turn, every shelf, every stubborn drawer. "That's why we keep them warm and busy. A busy room hasn't time to think."

Ansel sat. It seemed the safest available action. He opened his notebook out of habit, then closed it again; something told him Mrs. Pell would not be flattered by transcription.

"You knew my grandmother," he said.

"I knew *of* her." The kettle ticked as it heated. "Before my time, mostly. Agnes Winterbourne. Everyone knew *of* the Winterbournes." Something passed over her face like the shadow of a bird — quick, gone before he could name it. "You have her hands. Long fingers. Good for writing things down."

"That's more or less my whole profession."

"I know what your profession is, love. Folk songs. Recording them before they go." She set out two cups — his cups, from his cupboard, chosen without looking. "That's why I came, partly. To warn you off."

"Off the songs?"

"Off the *asking*." She turned and looked at him directly, and for a moment the warmth in her face was like a lamp behind frosted glass — present, illuminating, but not to be touched. "Tam Yarrow tells me you were at the Green Man Tuesday night. Asking after verses."

"He sang beautifully. I only wanted—"

"I know what you wanted. It's a good want. It's an *honourable* want." She poured water over the leaves, and the smell rose up between them, dark and familiar. "But you've been away from here too long to know how things are sung in Hollowmere, and nobody's told you yet, because telling is a kindness and this village has been rationing its kindnesses for a long while now." She sat down across from him, folded her hands on the table, beside the honey, beside the loaf, and took a breath like a woman preparing to carry something heavy up many stairs.

"So I'm going to tell you. And you're going to think I'm a silly old woman, and that's all right. Silly old women keep villages alive."

* * *

The rules, as Mrs. Pell gave them, were three.

"One," she said, holding up a thumb. "Keep your lights burning after dark. Every room you mean to use, and the hall besides. Not candles — candles flicker, and flickering is a kind of asking. Oil lamps or electric, steady light, right down to the sockets. A dark room in this house is an empty chair, Mr. Winterbourne, and empty chairs get *sat in*."

"By whom?"

"By what's been waiting to." She said it without drama, the way you'd say *by the postman*. "Two." A second thumb. "Count your doors each morning and each night. Same number going up as coming down, same number tonight as tomorrow. If the count changes—" she paused, and her hands tightened around her teacup, "—you don't remark on it. You don't touch the new one. You don't open it, however warm the room behind it seems, however well you think you know what's through it. You write the number in a book and you wait for it to settle back."

"And if it doesn't settle back?"

Her eyes went to the window, to the grey light and the yew hedge, and stayed there a moment too long. "Then you come to me, love, and we'll see."

"Three," she said, before he could press. "Never answer a knock after the second bell. We ring the church bell at nine of an evening — you'll have heard it, it carries. Anyone knocking after that bell has rung twice already, do you understand? Once for the living, once for the dead, and nothing at all should be ringing a third time. So if it comes after nine — and it will come, love, it always comes to new faces — you let it knock. However polite it sounds. However much it sounds like someone crying. However much it sounds like—" and here her voice did something he had never heard a voice do, it *stepped aside from itself*, leaving a gap where a word should have been, "—however much it sounds like family."

The kettle had long gone cold. Neither of them moved to heat it again.

"Mrs. Pell," Ansel said carefully, "I'm a folklorist. I've collected in forty villages across three counties. Superstition is my trade — I love it, I document it, I respect it. But rules like these usually have a story underneath. A reason they got made. If you tell me the story, I can—"

"No."

It was not sharp. That was the terrible thing about it — it was gentle, absolutely gentle, the *no* of a mother stopping a child from touching a hot stove, and it closed the subject the way a door closes, completely, with a small sound.

"Not yet. Not from me. When you're ready, you'll find it yourself, because you're a Winterbourne and finding things is in the blood." She rose, briskly, and began repacking the empty basket, and just like that the warmth came flooding back into her as if a valve had reopened. "Now. You'll take supper with me Thursday. I do a rabbit pie Thursdays, and there's no sense in a grown man living on toast and archival enthusiasm. Bring your appetite and leave your notebook."

"Mrs. Pell—"

"Edith." She fixed him with a look. "You'll call me Edith, because I've known you since before you were born, whether you know it or not, and 'Mrs. Pell' is for vicars and bailiffs."

"You couldn't have known me before I was born. My grandmother left Hollowmere before my father was even—"

"Yes, love." She patted his cheek on her way to the door, and her hand was cool and dry and trembled, just slightly, and he understood suddenly that the trembling was not age. "That's exactly what I said."

* * *

She left the bread, the butter, the honey, the jam, and the dripping, waving off his protests with a flap of her hand. At the gate she stopped, holding the wicker against her chest again, and looked back at the cottage — not at him, at the cottage — with an expression he would spend the rest of the week trying to describe in his notes and failing. It was the way you look at a house you have loved and are afraid of. It was the way you look at a sleeping animal that is much larger than you remember.

"Mind the lights," she called. "All of them, every night. Even the ones you think are silly."

Then she was gone down the lane, small and slate-coloured against the grey morning, and Ansel stood in the doorway with the smell of warm bread behind him and the cold at his front, feeling something he had not felt in years and could not immediately name.

It took him until noon, sitting over the loaf with butter melting into it, to name it. It was the feeling of being *expected*. Not watched — he knew watched, the cottage had been watching him since the first night, attentively, hungrily, the way the extra chair attended every photograph. This was different. This was the feeling of a place setting laid at a table. Of a name known in advance. Of a door held open before you reached it.

His whole life he had been the man outside the lit window. Orphan young, raised by a grandmother who loved him fiercely but flinched at lullabies, who never finished a song if she could help it, who went quiet whenever he asked about Hollowmere and once, memorably, burned a box of photographs in the garden while he watched and said only, *"Some pictures keep better empty."* He had built a career out of listening to other people belong — their harvests, their hearths, their handed-down verses — and gone home to rented rooms with tapes full of other families' voices.

And now a small round woman had called him *love* like she'd known him always, and brought him bread, and told him the rules of survival as casually as recipes, and asked him to Thursday supper, and said *I've known you since before you were born, whether you know it or not.*

He should have been afraid. He was afraid — the fear sat in him like a swallowed stone, cold and specific. But beneath the fear, shamefully, warmly, unmistakably, was the other thing. The thread. The first thin golden thread of belonging, offered freely, and he had taken hold of it with both hands before he could stop himself.

He wrote in his notebook that afternoon:

*Subject: E. Pell, neighbour. Age ~70. Knows the house intimately — navigated unfamiliar cupboards blind. Insists on three rules (lights/doors/second bell). Refuses origin of rules. Claims acquaintance predating my birth. Contradiction unresolved.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then, below, he wrote:

*She brought bread to a stranger. In a village this frightened, that is either innocence or defiance. I don't believe she's innocent.*

*Thursday. Rabbit pie. Leave the notebook.*

*God help me, I'm looking forward to it.*

* * *

Dusk came early, as it does in November, sliding down the hills like a lid. Ansel went through the cottage lighting lamps — oil in the parlour, electric in the kitchen, the hall bulb he'd thought silly and now, remembering her voice, screwed in with particular care. Seven rooms lit. He counted the doors going up: eight. He counted them again: eight. The green door between kitchen and pantry stood where it had stood that morning, patient as furniture.

At nine o'clock, far off across the fields, the church bell rang. Once. A single deep bronze note that seemed to travel through the ground and up through the soles of his feet and settle somewhere behind his sternum.

He stood in the lit hallway, listening to the silence after it, and heard — or thought he heard, or told himself he only thought he heard — the faintest sound from beyond the front door. Not a knock. Not yet. Just a settling, the way a house settles, the way a guest settles into a chair.

He checked the lights twice more. He wrote the door count in his book. He went to bed with every lamp burning and lay awake listening, and the cottage listened back, and somewhere down the lane a small window glowed steadily in the dark where a woman named Edith kept her own vigil, and the night leaned close around them both, and waited, politely, for the third bell that must never ring.

It knocked for the first time two nights later.
