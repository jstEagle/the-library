## 15. They Take the Kind Ones First

The bread was still warm.

That was the first thing Ansel understood, standing on Mrs. Pell's doorstep with his fist raised to knock a third time — that the loaf she'd left him yesterday sat in his kitchen under a cloth, and it had been warm when he'd cut into it at dawn, and warm bread meant her oven had been lit, and her oven had been lit no more than hours ago, and yet her cottage stood dark and silent as a held breath.

He knocked anyway. Twice. The rule was never answer after the second bell, but there was no bell here, only his own knuckles against wood gone grey with weather, and the sound of them fell into the house and did not come back.

"Mrs. Pell?" His voice sounded wrong in the lane — too loud, too alive, the voice of a man who had not yet learned what Hollowmere did with its kindnesses. "It's Ansel. It's Ansel Winterbourne."

He said the surname deliberately. He had been saying it deliberately for two days now, ever since the journal came up from beneath the hearthstone wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of cold iron and older cold than that. *Let them hear the name,* he'd told himself. *Let the thing know whose blood is walking around loose.* He had thought of it as a kind of armor. Now, with no answer coming through the door, he wondered if it had been an invitation instead.

He tried the latch. Unlocked — of course unlocked; this was Hollowmere, where doors were kept for the things outside, not the people within — and the cottage opened onto a dimness that smelled of lavender and yeast and, beneath both, faintly, of a fire burned down to nothing.

* * *

He had been inside her home before, twice: once to return a borrowed umbrella, once because she'd insisted he stay for tea while rain hammered the lanes like something impatient to be let in. Both times the cottage had been so full of her that it had seemed less a house than a body she wore — every surface soft with use, every shelf crowded with jars labeled in a hand like bird tracks, every chair shaped by decades of one particular sitting.

Now it was a shed. That was the only word his mind would give him. A shed where someone had once lived.

The table was bare. Not cleared — bare, as though nothing had ever stood on it, the wood pale where a teapot's shadow should have worn it dark. The dresser drawers slid open at his touch and gave up nothing: no spoons, no doilies, none of the small hoarded treasures he'd glimpsed that rainy afternoon. The photographs on the mantel — he remembered photographs, a stiff-collared man she'd called *our Walter*, children on a beach — were blank paper in cracked frames. Not empty frames. Frames holding paper the color of old milk, as if whatever had been printed there had been drunk out of the emulsion.

His own hands were shaking. He made himself go room to room anyway, because a folklorist documents, and documentation was the last discipline he had left, and because some stubborn animal part of him believed that if he could just record enough — describe enough, name enough — the world would hold its shape.

In the bedroom, the bed was made with corners sharp enough to draw blood. On the pillow lay a single sprig of rosemary, browning at the edges, laid straight as a bone.

He knew what rosemary meant. Every archive he'd ever worked in knew what rosemary meant. *For remembrance.* Laid on a pillow the way you'd lay flowers on a grave.

Except there was no body. There was no anything. There was a made bed and a cooling hearth and warm bread in his own kitchen, and somewhere between those facts a woman named Edith Pell had been taken out of the world so gently that even her house seemed embarrassed to remember her.

* * *

The village noticed by not noticing.

That was the horror of it, and he walked the lanes all morning learning it. He stopped the postman — a round, red-faced man named Cobb who had nodded to him every day since his arrival without once asking why a stranger was recording songs in a dying village — and asked, casually, carefully, whether Mrs. Pell was about.

"Who?"

"Edith Pell. Rose Cottage. She brings me bread."

Cobb's face did the thing that faces in Hollowmere did now, the slow smoothing-over, the pleasant fog rolling across features that had briefly shown landscape beneath. "Don't know a Pell," he said, and then, kindly, because everyone here was kind right up until they weren't anyone at all: "You feeling all right, sir? These long nights get into a body."

"She brought you your post yesterday," Ansel said. "She takes it in for half the lane when the box sticks. You thanked her. I saw you."

"I don't recall." Cobb smiled. The smile had nothing behind it, not malice — worse, vacancy, a window with the curtain drawn. "Lovely morning, though. Bit of frost coming, they say."

Ansel stood in the lane and watched the man walk on, whistling, and understood that he was watching a theft in progress. Not of a person. Of a place in the world. Mrs. Pell was being unpicked from Hollowmere stitch by stitch, and the thread was going somewhere, and the somewhere was the extra chair at his grandmother's table, which in the newest photographs was no longer empty.

At the shop, the girl behind the counter — Maren, seventeen, freckled, the only person under sixty he'd spoken to at length — frowned when he mentioned the name, and for one heartbeat something flickered behind her eyes like a fish turning under ice.

"Pell," she repeated. "Pell. Wasn't there—" Her hand rose halfway to her mouth. "There was someone who used to say my name like she'd known it since I was born. She used to give us barley sugar off the high shelf when Mam wasn't looking." The frown deepened, then healed. "Funny. Can't picture her face."

"Maren." He gripped the counter. "Her name was Edith. Edith Pell. She lives — lived — at Rose Cottage, end of the lane, blue door, roses over the—"

"Rose Cottage is empty," the girl said, gently, the way you correct a child or a madman. "Been empty since before I was born, they say." And then, because she was seventeen and kind and Hollowmere's kindness was the only currency the thing hadn't figured out how to counterfeit completely, she leaned across the counter and lowered her voice. "Mr. Winterbourne. My gran says when a name goes quiet like that, it's been *spent*. She says it like it's money." Her eyes were frightened and trying not to be. "What's spending our names, Mr. Winterbourne?"

He had no answer he could give her. He bought candles he didn't need and walked back up the lane past Rose Cottage, and its windows looked at him with the flat patience of eyes closed in sleep.

* * *

It was his fault.

He made himself say it, sitting that afternoon at his grandmother's kitchen table with the journal open and the iron key beside it and the photographs spread out in their terrible sequence, because grief that isn't named curdles into something useless, and he needed everything he had to be useful tonight.

Not his fault the way a child means it — not *I broke it* — but his fault the way a debt collector means it. His presence had been a payment. He saw it now, laid out in the logic of the journal's cramped entries and the arithmetic of the missing. For two hundred years the Winterbournes' invitation had hung open like a wound, and the village had kept the thing fed the way you keep any hungry animal fed: regularly, quietly, with what you could spare. Borrowed memories. Missing neighbors. A verse sung twice by someone who couldn't resist, once a generation, maybe twice. A slow bleed, calibrated. Sustainable. The village had learned to live the way a body lives with a tapeworm — diminished, but living.

And then he had come. The blood itself, walking back through the door the family had fled. The thing hadn't needed to ration anymore. Here was the main course sitting down at the table, and everything else — Tam Yarrow's smile at his hearth, Mrs. Pell's rosemary on her pillow — was not feeding anymore. It was *clearing the plate*. Making room. Removing every hand that might have reached for Ansel, every voice that might have called him back from the chair, every memory of warmth that might have taught him what he was losing.

They take the kind ones first. Not because the thing hated kindness. Because kindness was the rope, and it was cutting the ropes one by one, and it had started with the kindest rope of all.

He thought of Mrs. Pell's warnings, given wrapped in pleasantries like pills pushed into a dog's throat. *Keep the lights on, love. Count the doors. Never answer after the second bell.* She had known. She had known exactly what was coming for her, and she had spent her last days teaching a stranger the rules of survival, and she had done it smiling, calling him *love* like she'd known him always — and God, God, maybe she *had* known him always, maybe she'd known his grandmother, maybe the whole shape of her life had bent toward this winter, toward keeping one Winterbourne alive long enough to sing one song wrong.

"You knew," he said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the photograph where the silhouette in the extra chair leaned slightly toward the camera as though listening. "You knew and you stayed anyway. Why didn't you run? Why didn't you make me leave?"

But he knew why. He'd read it in the journal, in his grandmother's hand growing shakier page by page: *It cannot be driven out by strangers. It cannot be starved by flight. Only the blood that bid it welcome can bid it farewell, and farewell must be sung, not said, and sung alone, and sung false.* And beneath that, in ink pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*I could not do it. I was a coward. I ran and let Hollowmere pay my debts. If you are reading this, you are braver than I was, or the chair has finally caught you. Either way — sing.*

The lights guttered. All of them at once, a single indrawn breath of flame, and Ansel went very still, and counted the doors aloud — seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve — and heard, beneath his counting, beneath the wind worrying the eaves, a breathing that matched his own so precisely it might have been his reflection learning the words.

Twelve doors. There had been nine yesterday.

* * *

Evening came down like a lid.

He did what had to be done first, because the journal had been clear about order and order was all he had. He ate — Mrs. Pell's bread, the last of it, and he ate it standing at the sink with tears running freely into the crust because refusing her bread felt like a second death and eating it felt like communion, and he chose communion. What shares your bread may share your name. Let his name be tangled with hers, then. Let the thing have to chew through that.

Then he took down every photograph from every wall and laid them face-up on the kitchen floor in chronological order, a century of Winterbournes arranged like evidence, and he knelt among them and spoke to each face in turn. Names. He gave them their names, all the ones the journal supplied, all the ones the parish records had surrendered before the tearing started. *Margaret. Old Josiah. The twins, Peter and Paul. Grandma — Edie, your name was Edie too, isn't that strange, Edie and Edith, the two of you bracketing me like bookends.* The dead deserved their names said in their own house. And names, he was beginning to understand, were not decoration. They were architecture. The Unmaking Song required him to name the thing invited, and to name himself its blood, and he suspected — he *hoped*, with the desperation of a man hoping a rope holds — that every name spoken truly in this room tonight was another nail in the frame of the door he meant to close forever.

Outside, the light failed. Inside, the lamps burned steady, and the new doors stood along the walls like guests waiting to be introduced — twelve of them now, each one warm-lit at the edges, each one humming faintly with the sound of a childhood he'd had and hadn't had, rooms furnished with a mother's laugh he couldn't verify and a father's coat on a hook he couldn't swear to. He did not look at them directly. Looking, the journal warned, was halfway to entering, and entering was halfway to sitting, and sitting —

The chair was warm. He checked it once, couldn't help himself, laid his palm flat on the seat of the extra chair at the table, and snatched his hand back as though from a stove. Warm as a lap just vacuated. Warm as welcome.

"Not yet," he told it. His voice cracked in the middle and he hated it and kept talking anyway. "Not tonight. Tonight you get a song, and it's the worst song you've ever heard, and then you get nothing. You get *no one*. Do you hear me? No one sits. No one stays. Not because I'm alone — because I'm *not*. That's the trick, isn't it? That's the whole trick. You need me lonely more than you need me willing."

The breathing behind the walls paused. Then resumed. Patient. Amused, almost.

He turned to the photographs on the floor, to the oldest one, Margaret Winterbourne 1804, stern and sun-faded, and found his rage again, because rage was fuel and the night was long. Two centuries of this. Two centuries of a village paying rent in its own children's faces, and his family running, generation after generation, leaving the bill on the table — and the ones who stayed, the Pells and the Yarrows and the Cobbs and the Marens, paying it in coin that couldn't be replaced. Tam's face, already unremembered by his own family. Mrs. Pell's name, dissolving in the mouths of the people she'd fed.

"They take the kind ones first," he said to Margaret's faded stare. "Did you know that? Did you sit in this kitchen and know that, and invite it in anyway?"

The photograph, of course, said nothing. But the wind changed key against the glass, and for a moment — one clear, cold moment — he heard harmonies underneath it, many voices, patient voices, voices he had recorded himself and played back into the world like a fool opening gates, and among them, thin and sweet and unmistakable, a melody he knew.

Mrs. Pell's humming. The tune she'd hummed over her kneading, wordless, rising.

It was singing his name.

* * *

He worked until his voice was raw. Memorizing. The Unmaking Song was short — cruelly short, three verses, the length of a lullaby, because the worst things always wear the smallest shapes — but every note had to live in him, not on the page, because the page would be ash by midnight if the thing had its way, and because a song sung from paper is a song sung twice, and twice was invitation, and this one must be sung once, wrongly, on purpose, alone.

Once. Wrongly. Alone. By Winterbourne blood. Before sunrise on the longest night.

Sunset had been at twenty past four. He glanced at the clock — five minutes past six — and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all day. Ten hours. Ten hours of lamplight and counting and the polite footsteps in the hall that circled and circled and never quite entered, because entering required a yes, and the yes was being courted out of him hour by hour, door by warm door, photograph by photograph, memory by offered memory.

Ten hours. And somewhere out in the freezing lanes, a village full of hollowing people, and one cottage with a blue door and no roses left on the wall, where a woman had laid rosemary on her own pillow because she'd known no one else would be left to mourn her properly.

Ansel stood, took the iron key from the table, and held it until its cold bit into his palm — a good pain, a true pain, the pain of metal that had never been invited anywhere.

"I'll say your name every day," he promised the darkness, and meant Mrs. Pell, and meant Tam, and meant all of them. "Every day until it sticks. Until this whole rotten village remembers you whether it wants to or not. That's my price. That's what I want in exchange."

The nearest of the twelve doors swung softly inward, revealing lamplight and the smell of baking and, distantly, a woman's voice calling a child in from the cold — a child's name that was almost his.

Ansel Winterbourne picked up the journal, faced the longest night, and began to count the doors again, out loud, all the way to sunrise.
