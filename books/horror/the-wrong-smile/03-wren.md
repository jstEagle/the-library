## 3. Wren

The last measurement of the day was the porch, because Ivo always saved the porch. It was a habit from her first job, a bungalow in Rensselaer that had taught her the value of ending on something simple—something with four corners and a roof pitch she could confirm with her eyes closed. Porches were honest. Porches were the handshake a house offered before it got complicated inside.

Hollowmere's porch was not honest.

She'd known that since the moment she'd stepped onto it that morning, when her boots had made a sound she couldn't file: not the knock of boards, but something softer, the way a page sounds when it's turned. All day the thought had sat in the back of her skull like a splinter she'd decided not to dig out, and now, at 6:47 in the evening, with the light going the color of weak tea and her laser measure warm in her hand, she stood on the dishonest porch and ran the final numbers.

Exterior wall to exterior wall, the house was sixty-one feet, four inches. She had walked that with a tape that morning, dragging the steel through the weeds, calling the numbers into her recorder in her flattest professional voice. Sixty-one four. She had the recording. She had the tape. She had the photographs, timestamped, geotagged, the whole liturgy of proof that Ivo Pemberton performed daily the way other people performed prayer.

The porch measured nine feet deep.

Which meant the front door should sit nine feet from the porch's edge.

She measured it again, because the first reading had to be wrong. The laser ticked, hummed, and returned: fourteen feet, six inches.

She measured it a third time. Fourteen feet, six.

She stood in the middle of the porch with the red dot trembling on the doorframe and did the arithmetic she'd been doing all day, the same subtraction, the same impossible remainder. The house was bigger inside. Not by much. By five feet, six inches, every time, in every room, with the patience of a signature written over and over in the same hand.

*Equipment error,* she had written in her notes at noon. *Unit may be affected by humidity. Re-calibrate overnight.*

The unit was not affected by humidity. She had calibrated it in the parking lot of a Denny's in Cortland that morning, against a steel reference target she kept in the trunk for exactly this kind of doubt, and it had agreed with the target to within a millimeter. Ivo Pemberton did not own instruments that lied. It was, in a life that had been carefully reduced to the measurable, the one thing she could still believe without effort: that the tape did not flatter, the laser did not dream, the plumb line did not hope.

So the house was lying. And the house was lying *consistently*, which was worse, because consistency implied intention, and intention implied a liar, and liars were people, and there were no people in the empty rooms she had spent nine hours measuring.

From inside the house came the sound of the Delacortes' dinner. Marcus's voice, pitched low and careful, the way men spoke when they were afraid of being overheard by their own children. Sela's voice answering, too controlled for fifteen. Theo not answering at all. Ivo had noted that at lunch, professionally: *younger twin, male, 13, largely nonverbal during site visit. Possible selective mutism, grief-related.* She had written it down the way she wrote everything down, and the writing had not helped, because some part of her had recognized the silence. She had been that silence once. She knew its weight, how it sat in a child's chest like a stone in a shoe.

She should go in. She should knock on the door, present her preliminary findings—*structure sound, discrepancies under investigation, full report within ten business days*—and take her leave like a professional. Marcus had offered her dinner twice. She had declined twice, with the smile she used for clients who wanted to become friends.

Instead she stood on the dishonest porch, and the light died by degrees, and the pond behind the house went from silver to pewter to a darkness that seemed to hold its breath, and Ivo Pemberton, who did not believe in haunted houses, noticed that she was waiting for something.

She noticed it the way you notice you've been holding a breath.

*Waiting for what?*

The porch light came on behind her—motion sensor, she told herself, though nothing had moved—and threw her shadow long and doubled across the boards, one shadow from the bulb behind her and one from the last light in the west, so that she seemed to have two selves, slightly out of agreement about where she was standing.

And at the far end of the porch, in the corner where the rail met the house, where the shadow of the chimney fell thickest, there was a third.

It was small. That was the first thing. Smaller than a child, or the size of one seen from a long way off, the way distance shrinks things without moving them. It sat on the rail with its feet hanging, and it was made of the dark—not standing in the shadow but *composed* of it, the way a word is composed of letters—and over the whole of it lay a faint dusting of pale, as if it had been rolled in chalk dust, or flour, or the powdery residue of a blackboard erased a thousand times and never quite clean.

Ivo did not scream. Screaming was a loss of data. She stood very still and let her professional mind do what it was built to do: categorize. Visual artifact. Shadow play. A coat on a rail, a garden ornament, an owl. She ran through the list the way she ran through a checklist before signing a report, and the list came up empty, because the figure turned its head.

It turned its head the way a child turns to look at you when you've said its name.

And Ivo Pemberton, forty-one years old, licensed property surveyor, a woman who had not wept at her father's funeral because she had spent the service calculating the volume of the cemetery plot, felt something open in her chest—a hairline crack, fine as a fault line in a foundation, running through thirty-four years of load-bearing disbelief.

She knew him.

She knew the tilt of the head. She knew the way he sat, feet swinging, heels knocking the rail in a rhythm she had not heard since she was seven years old and had heard every day for two years before that: *knock, knock, knock-knock*, the little syncopation, the secret knock they'd had, so she'd know it was him and not the wind in the maple outside her window.

She knew the chalk dust, because Wren had been drawn before he was born. She had drawn him—on the driveway, on the cellar floor, on the back of the garage where her mother couldn't see—with the fat white chalk that came in buckets of sidewalk chalk, and he had stood up off the pavement, and that had been fine, that had been the most natural thing in the world, because she was six and the world was still soft at the edges. He was made of shadow and chalk because that's how she'd made him: the outline first, in white, and then the filling-in, which was the dark.

He had gone away when she was seven. Or she had gone away from him. She had never decided which, and then she had stopped deciding anything about him at all, the way you stop deciding about a dream by the time you're brushing your teeth.

The figure on the rail swung its feet. *Knock, knock, knock-knock.*

"You took your time," it said.

The voice was small and dry and familiar as her own handwriting. It was a child's voice with an old patience in it, the way a riverbed is a child's shape with a mountain's history.

Ivo's mouth was dry. "You're not real," she said, and heard how young her own voice came out, how it climbed toward the register of a girl standing in a driveway at dusk with chalk on her fingers.

"No," Wren agreed, agreeably. He swung his feet. "You never liked real. Real took things."

The pond behind the house made a sound. Not a splash—something more deliberate, like a single drop falling from a great height into still water, though nothing moved on its surface.

"You took your time," he said again, and there was no accusation in it. There was something worse. There was the tone of someone who had known exactly how long, who had counted.

"Twenty-five years," Ivo said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The number arrived in her mouth the way measurements arrived on the laser display: precise, unbidden, true.

"Mm." Wren tilted his head, and the chalk dust on him caught the porch light and glimmered, faintly, like frost. "I didn't count the years. I counted something else. You'll want to be careful with the counting here. Everyone counts wrong, the first time."

"What does that mean?"

He smiled. She remembered the smile—she remembered it with her whole body, a cold hand flat against her sternum—because Wren's smile had never been a comfort. It was the smile of a child who knows something the adults don't and has decided, kindly, to wait.

"You'll work it out," he said. "You always did like the working out. You'd measure the driveway with your feet, over and over. Forty-one feet. You were very sure about the driveway." He looked past her, at the dark windows of Hollowmere, and his voice went quieter, and the quiet in it was the quiet of the pantry at 3 a.m., the quiet of Theo. "The house likes people who measure. It keeps them longer."

"Keep them—" Ivo stopped. Recalibrated. Drew the professional voice up around her like a coat. "I'm here to appraise the property. I'll be gone by Friday."

"Friday," Wren said, tasting the word. He swung his feet once more and did not knock the rhythm, and the absence of the knock was somehow louder than the knock. "That's what the last one thought. She had lovely instruments. Brass and glass. She measured everything." He slid down off the rail, and he did not so much stand as *arrive*, the way a drawing arrives on a page, all at once and already finished. He was not quite as tall as her hip. He smelled, faintly, of pencil shavings and pond water. "She's still here, in a way. Ask the house about 1971. Houses remember what they're given."

"I'm leaving," Ivo said.

"Yes," Wren said. "You always were good at that."

And when she turned back, the corner of the porch was empty. Not vacant—*empty*, the way a page is empty after an erasure, with the ghost of the drawing still pressed into it. There was chalk dust on the rail where his feet had hung. She touched it with one finger and it was cold, and it did not smudge, and it was not dust at all but something finer, something that clung to the whorls of her fingerprint like it was glad to be held.

Ivo walked to her car. She did not run. Running was data she refused to generate.

* * *

She got two miles before she pulled over.

The road out of Hollowmere's valley was narrow and black-walled with trees, and her headlights made a tunnel of it, and at the two-mile mark there was a gravel turnout where a historical marker stood—the kind the state put up for battlefields and birthplaces—and she pulled into it without having decided to, her hands doing something her mind had not authorized.

She sat with the engine running and the heater pushing air that smelled of the car's own plastics, and she looked at her hands on the wheel, and one finger still had the faint pale residue on it.

*Verify a measurement,* she told herself. *You left your plumb line reference on the porch rail. Go back, collect it, log the calibration, go to the motel, sleep, run the survey again tomorrow with fresh equipment. That's all this is. That is all this is.*

She knew where the plumb line was. It was in its case, in her bag, in the passenger seat, three inches from her elbow. She had packed it herself at 6:15, and she had watched herself do it, because she always watched herself pack, because losing a tool was the one sin her profession did not forgive.

The engine idled. The historical marker stood in the headlights. She leaned forward and read it, because reading was better than thinking.

**HOLLOWMERE.** *Settled 1804. Note: original survey of 1971 by E. Marsh, County Assessor, remains the plat of record. Marsh disappeared October of that year; survey never amended.*

Ivo read it twice. The third time, she didn't read it so much as feel it, the way you feel a draft from a door you thought was closed.

*E. Marsh.* Wren had said *she*. Wren had said *ask the house about 1971*. And here it was, on a roadside sign, in the state's own flat institutional font, the first corroboration—small, bureaucratic, real—the way corroboration always is: a footnote, a filing error, a name on a plaque.

A surveyor had come here in 1971 and measured this house, and had not come back out.

Ivo sat in the turnout with her blinker ticking—she'd left it on, the little rhythm of it, *tick, tick, tick-tick*, and she did not turn it off, because the rhythm was almost, almost a knock she knew—and she made a list, because lists were how she kept the world from coming apart. One: the house measures wrong, consistently, deliberately. Two: a figure on the porch that she recognized, that her body recognized before her mind permitted it, that spoke in the voice of an imaginary friend she had not thought about in thirty-four years and had apparently never stopped measuring time against. Three: a vanished surveyor. Four: a family in that house with a silent boy who counted things in pantries, and a girl who watched staircases the way her mother must have, and a father who said yes to things in the dark.

Five: none of this was in the appraisal scope of work.

She could drive to the motel. She could email Marcus Delacorte in the morning and cite a scheduling conflict, refer the file to a colleague in Binghamton, and be at Priya's by Sunday for the rehearsal dinner, and stand in the corner of the reception hall with a glass of wine and her professional smile, invisible in the way she had made herself, the way she had asked—she had asked *someone*, hadn't she, she had asked at seven years old, she had asked with chalk on her fingers—to be made un-takeable, un-takeable meaning unseen, unseen meaning safe.

*You never liked real,* Wren had said. *Real took things.*

Her father had been real. Her father had taken the car and a suitcase and had not come back, and she had been seven, and that was the year Wren had gone away, or the year she had stopped drawing him, and she had never once in thirty-four years put those two facts in the same sentence, and now they were in the same sentence, sitting there in her mind like two measurements that should not agree and did.

The house was bigger inside. The house was lying consistently. And somewhere behind her, two miles back down the black road, a family was eating dinner in rooms that did not fit inside their own walls, and a boy was being quiet in a way she recognized from the inside, and a small dry voice had said: *the house likes people who measure. It keeps them longer.*

Ivo Pemberton put the car in gear.

She told herself it was to verify a measurement. She said it out loud, in fact, in the flat professional voice she used for recordings, so that the saying of it would make it true the way recorded things were true. "Returning to site to verify a measurement discrepancy," she said, and turned the car around in the turnout, and the headlights swept across the historical marker, and E. Marsh's name went by like a face at a window.

Two miles. The trees leaned. The pond, when she reached it, was a sheet of black with one set of ripples moving across it against the wind, a single line, traveling, unhurried, the way a finger travels down a ledger.

The porch light was on. The house stood back from its own yard, deeper than the deed allowed—she had measured that too, that morning, and logged it as a survey error in the county plat, and now she thought of Edith Marsh's brass and glass instruments and did not log anything at all.

At the far end of the porch, in the corner where the shadow of the chimney fell, something small sat on the rail, feet hanging, heels knocking.

*Knock, knock, knock-knock.*

Ivo turned off the engine and sat in the ticking silence of the cooling car, and the crack in her disbelief widened by exactly one hair's breadth, and through it, faint as chalk dust, came the smell of pencil shavings and pond water, and the knowledge—unmeasured, unlogged, true—that she had not come back for the plumb line.

She had come back because the house was counting, and Wren had all but told her the number was wrong, and Ivo Pemberton had never in her life been able to leave an equation unbalanced overnight.

She got out of the car. The porch boards made the sound of a page turning.

"Friday," she said to the dark corner, her voice steadier than she had any right to expect.

Wren's heels stopped knocking. In the shadow where his face would be, something glimmered, the way chalk glimmers on a blackboard just before the lesson begins.

"That's what I like about you," he said. "You always come back for the numbers."
