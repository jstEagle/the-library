## 6. What Wren Counts

The room above the Hollowmere Inn smelled of lavender and old radiator dust, and Ivo had spent forty minutes convincing herself that was all it smelled of.

She sat on the bed with her laptop open to the day's survey data, a glass of wine she wasn't drinking sweating on the nightstand. The numbers were still wrong. She'd checked them eleven times, recalibrated twice, run the laser measure across the master bedroom floor three separate times from three separate corners, and every time the interior came back larger than the exterior footprint allowed—by four feet, seven inches, along the north wall. The same margin in every room. The same margin as the hallway. The same margin, if she was honest with herself, as the discrepancy she'd measured at age nine with a tape measure and a flashlight in the house on Corliss Street, before she'd decided she'd imagined that too.

Four feet, seven inches. It didn't drift. Errors drifted. Errors were noise, scattered, sloppy, indifferent. This was consistent. This was *deliberate*. A discrepancy repeated with this precision wasn't a malfunction; it was a signature.

Ivo closed the laptop.

Outside, dusk had gone the color of a bruise healing. The inn's window looked east, away from Hollowmere House, which she had chosen deliberately when she booked it, and which had turned out to be pointless, because she could feel the house behind her anyway, the way you feel someone standing at your back in an elevator. She told herself that was nonsense. She told herself a lot of things lately, and the telling was starting to feel like labor, like hauling buckets up from a well that kept refilling.

She was reaching for the wine when the lamp flickered.

Not browned out—flickered, once, like a blink. And when the light steadied, Wren was sitting on the dresser.

He hadn't come through the door. She was almost certain of that, though certainty about Wren was a currency she'd stopped carrying twenty-five years ago. He sat where the TV remote should have been, small and neat, his legs dangling off the edge of the walnut dresser like a child waiting out a sermon in church. He was made of the same things he'd always been made of: shadow with a grain to it, like charcoal rubbed on paper, and chalk dust, faintly luminous, settling on him and never quite landing. His face was the face she had drawn for him at seven—a smudge of a face, features suggested rather than committed—and his eyes were two places where the shadow went all the way through.

He did not say hello. He said, "You took your time," again, because apparently that was going to be his whole personality now, and then he folded his chalk-white hands in his lap and waited.

Ivo did not scream. She noted, distantly, that she didn't scream, and filed that away as evidence of something—shock, or exhaustion, or some deep structural flaw in her that had been there since childhood and had simply been waiting for the right load-bearing moment to show itself.

"You're not real," she said. Her voice came out level. Professional. She was almost proud of it.

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly.

"You're a hypnagogic hallucination triggered by stress and sleep deprivation."

"Probably."

"You're a memory with legs."

"I've been called worse." He swung his feet. They made no sound. Nothing about him made any sound, and that was somehow worse than everything else—the total silence of him, the way the room's ordinary noises seemed to bend around him like water around a stone. "You measured the hallway today."

It wasn't a question. Ivo's hand, moving toward the wine, stopped.

"The north wall runs long," Wren said. "They all run long. Four feet, seven inches. You noticed."

"Equipment error," Ivo said. "Consistent calibration drift across—"

"Ivo." He said her name the way he'd said it when she was seven, patiently, the way you'd correct a child who insisted the stove wasn't hot. "You wrote it in your log. *Discrepancy uniform across all sampled rooms. Margin constant.* You underlined it. You don't underline errors. You underline findings."

She had underlined it. She had underlined it and then hated herself for underlining it, and then written *equipment error* underneath in smaller letters, like a man scrawling *not guilty* on a verdict.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"To talk about the count."

"There's no count."

"There's always a count." Wren slid off the dresser without appearing to move through the intervening space—he was simply standing on the carpet now, chalk dust sifting from him in a slow, silent fall that never reached the floor. "Houses keep accounts, Ivo. Old ones especially. Houses that have stood a long time learn to notice who's inside them. Not visitors. Visitors pass through like weather. *Residents.* The ones who sleep under the roof more nights than not. The ones whose names get spoken at the dinner table. The house counts them. It has always counted them. That's not cruelty. That's just what it is. A scale weighs. A clock ticks. Hollowmere counts."

Ivo sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, because her knees had opinions. "This is a metaphor," she said. "Grief manifests as—"

"It's arithmetic," Wren said, with something almost like apology. "That's the part nobody wants. Everyone expects ghosts to be poetry. Rattling chains, moaning in attics, unfinished business. But it's not poetry. It's bookkeeping. The house keeps a ledger of who lives in it, and the ledger has to balance, and when it doesn't balance—" He spread his small hands. Shadow hands, chalk knuckles. "Things get corrected."

"Corrected how?"

Wren looked at her. Those eyes, those holes in him, held nothing and reflected everything, and in them Ivo saw the dim lamp, the closed laptop, her own face, pale and drawn and lit from one side like a woman in a painting about bad news.

"You already know," he said gently. "You found Edith Marsh's file today. Or you will tomorrow. County records, 1971. You'll read her notes. You'll see where the handwriting goes."

"I haven't requested anything from county records."

"Not yet."

*Not yet.* The words landed in Ivo's chest like a coin dropped down a well—she heard herself not hearing them, felt the future arrive ahead of schedule. She thought of the surveyor's notes she hadn't read yet, deteriorating page by page into a child's round letters, and realized she was describing something she had no way of knowing, describing it in detail, the way you describe a dream you haven't had yet but are about to.

"Stop," she said.

Wren stopped. He was good like that. He'd always been good like that—obedient in the specific, terrible way of things that exist only because you need them to.

"The Delacortes," he said instead, softer. "Three living residents. Marcus. Sela. Theo. Three names that should be on the ledger. But the house counts four."

The room got very quiet. Quiet beyond its own quiet, the radiator ticking somewhere far away, the wine glass sweating, the lamp burning steady now as if ashamed of having blinked.

"The mother," Ivo said. Her voice sounded strange to her. Thin. "Marisol Delacorte. She died fourteen months ago. Car accident on Route 9. I read the obituary. I read the death certificate. People don't stay on ledgers after they—"

"People leave ledgers when they're let go," Wren said. "Has anyone let her go?"

And Ivo thought—unhelpfully, horribly—of Marcus Delacorte's face that afternoon, the widowed father with his desperate politeness and his eyes that kept drifting to the staircase whenever the house creaked, drifting the way a man's eyes drift toward a door he's expecting someone to come through. She thought of the fourth place set at the dinner table. She'd noticed it during the walkthrough. Four chairs, four place settings, and only three plates with food, the fourth setting laid with perfect care and nothing on it, and when she'd asked, Marcus had smiled and said his daughter liked setting the table the old way, and Sela, thirteen years old and watchful, had looked at her father with an expression Ivo had needed two full days to identify because she'd never seen it outside a mirror:

The expression of a child watching an adult lie to protect something the adult can't afford to name.

"She asked to stay," Ivo said. She didn't know why she said it. The words came up from somewhere below her professional training, below her skepticism, below even her memory—some sediment layer of her, some aquifer. "Didn't she. She asked to stay, and somebody said yes."

Wren tilted his head. The chalk dust sifted. For a moment he looked less like a gargoyle and more like what he had actually been, twenty-five years ago: a friend, sitting cross-legged on a bedroom floor, about to explain something difficult to a seven-year-old who had just learned the world could take things away.

"Ledgers can be corrected," he said. "That's what I came to tell you. That's the whole message. The house is counting four in a household of three, and the extra name has weight, and weight accrues interest, and interest, in a house like this, gets paid in—" He paused, choosing, and Ivo understood with cold clarity that he was choosing words *for her benefit*, translating something vast into the only language she trusted. "In square footage," he finished. "In walls. In rooms that run four feet, seven inches too long. The house is holding the difference somewhere, Ivo. It's storing the imbalance. And storage fails."

"Or the family leaves," Ivo said. "People move. People sell houses. That's literally my job—I appraise houses so people can sell them. We list it, we market it, the Delacortes walk away, problem solved. Ledgers don't follow you to a condo in Hartford."

"No," Wren agreed. "But they don't close, either. An open account doesn't vanish because you moved out of the building it's attached to. It just waits. And the Delacortes have been feeding it." He saw her expression and raised a hand. "Not knowingly. Marcus leaves things. Bread on the counter overnight. A cup of tea going cold by the fourth chair. Little surrenders, little yeses. Every yes is an entry. He thinks he's being kind to a ghost. He's making payments on a debt he doesn't know he owes, and the house accepts payment, and acceptance extends the term."

Ivo stood up. She needed to stand. She crossed to the window—not the east-facing one, the other one, the one she'd pretended didn't matter—and pulled back the curtain, and there it was, two miles off across the dark fields: Hollowmere House, black against a sky that hadn't quite committed to stars, every window dark except one.

The fourth-floor window. The one over the master suite. The one that, according to her own measurements, shouldn't have a view of anything but attic joists.

A light moved behind it. Slow. Patient. The light of someone walking a familiar path in a familiar room, checking, tidying, staying.

"That's not possible," Ivo said. "There's no power running to that wing. I checked the panel myself. There's no *floor* at that elevation. My own instruments—"

"Your instruments are starting to tell you the truth," Wren said behind her. "That's what happens next. First the numbers go strange. Then they go honest. Then they start answering questions you didn't ask." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something older and sadder, the voice of the thing she'd invented at seven to sit with her in the dark after her father's chair went empty. "You should know what you're looking at, Ivo. Before it looks back properly. The house isn't haunting the Delacortes. Haunting is what dead people do. The house is *counting* them. And when the count comes due—if it comes due—it won't take their breath or their blood. It will take the space they occupy. It will fold them in. Neatly. Along the seams. The way paper folds. And the rooms will run four feet, seven inches longer, forever, and no one will ever measure the difference, because everyone who could have will be part of the measurement."

Ivo turned around. Wren stood in the middle of the rented room, small and chalk-pale and utterly calm, and she hated him for the calm, and loved him for it, and hated herself for loving him, because love was the one instrument she'd never figured out how to calibrate.

"How do I fix it?" she asked. "The ledger. How do you correct a ledger?"

For the first time, Wren hesitated.

It was a small hesitation—a flicker, a shutter, the chalk-dust brightness of him dimming by a degree—but Ivo had spent her whole life reading fine print, and she read it now. He knew the answer. He had known it since the porch. And he was deciding, right there between one heartbeat and the next, how much of it to tell her, and the fact that he was deciding meant the answer cost something, meant the answer had a price tag she couldn't read yet.

"Records," he said finally. "Ledgers answer to records. If you want to understand what correction means—in this house, under these rules—go find the last person who tried. Edith Marsh. 1971. County archive opens at eight." He was fading as he spoke, his edges losing definition, chalk dust rising off him like smoke in reverse. "Read her notes to the end, Ivo. All the way to the last line. And when you get there, remember that she wrote it in a steady hand, right up until she didn't."

"Wren—"

"You took your time," he said, from everywhere and nowhere, from the grain of the shadow by the dresser, from the year 1998, from the well of her. "Don't take more of it than you have."

The lamp blinked once.

The dresser was empty except for the TV remote, sitting exactly where it should have been, and Ivo Pemberton stood alone in the lavender-and-radiator-dust room with her heart going like a plumb line in wind, looking at the far dark shape of Hollowmere House and its impossible lit window, doing the one thing she had sworn never to do again.

She believed it.

Not fully. Not cleanly. Belief, for her, would never be clean again—it came in like water through a cracked foundation, finding every fault line, and she could feel it spreading now through all the load-bearing walls of her life. But it was in. It was measurable. And the first number it gave her was this: county records opened at eight, and it was barely nine o'clock, and she had a laptop, a phone, and a professional license that could get her into archives most people didn't know existed.

She picked up the wine, poured it down the bathroom sink, and opened a new document.

At the top, in the steady hand that would hold—for now—she typed: *Delacorte appraisal, addendum, unofficial. Subject property: occupancy discrepancy.*

Then she began searching for a surveyor named Edith Marsh, and found her in eleven seconds, and found the word *missing* attached to her name in thirty-one, and did not stop typing until long after the light in the fourth-floor window went out.
