## 7. The Previous Appraisal

The county records office occupied the basement of the Hollowmere Municipal Building, which had been a courthouse once and still carried the posture of one—tall windows fogged with wire mesh, a marble threshold worn into a shallow valley by a century of official feet. The clerk, a woman named Doris with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the unhurried authority of someone who had outlasted every supervisor she'd ever had, led Ivo past the deed vaults to a room of gray steel shelving where the appraisal files lived.

"Property records are public," Doris said, unlocking nothing, because there was nothing to lock. "Structural surveys, tax assessments, improvement permits. If it was filed, it's here. If it wasn't filed—" she shrugged, a small geological event, "—it never happened."

"That's the job," Ivo said.

Doris looked at her over the glasses, and something in the look was almost kind. "You're the one out at Hollowmere. The Delacorte place."

Ivo hadn't mentioned the address yet. She was sure she hadn't. "Word travels."

"Forty years in this office, honey. Word doesn't travel. It settles." Doris pulled a box from the shelf—cardboard, acid-stained at the corners, the year range stenciled on the side in fading block letters: 1968–1974. She set it on the reading table under the fluorescent light that buzzed like something trapped. "Appraisals are alphabetical by surveyor within the year. You'll want the Marsh file."

Ivo went still. "I didn't say a name."

"You didn't have to." Doris tapped the box once with a fingernail lacquered the color of old bandages. "Everybody who comes down here about Hollowmere wants the Marsh file. There've been four of you in my time. Five, counting the man who came twice." She adjusted her glasses. "He came twice because he didn't believe himself the first time. That's not a criticism. That's just what I observed."

"What happened to him?"

"He stopped coming," Doris said, and left her alone with the box.

* * *

The file was thin. That was the first thing Ivo noticed—the physical wrongness of it, a 1971 appraisal for a property of Hollowmere's size should have been an inch of paper at minimum, plats and photographs and soil reports and the carbon copies of correspondence. This was eleven pages, clipped with a brass fastener gone green at the edges, inside a folder whose tab read MARSH, E. — HOLLOWMERE HOUSE — FINAL.

*Final.* Files didn't say *final*. Files said *amended*, *superseded*, *withdrawn*. *Final* was a word for verdicts.

Ivo put on her own reading glasses—a professional affectation, her prescription was fine—and began at the beginning, because beginnings were where she lived.

Page one was immaculate. Edith Marsh had been, by every evidence, an excellent surveyor. The letterhead was crisp: *Marsh & Associates, Licensed Property Surveyors, Est. 1958*. The date was April 12, 1971. The opening paragraph was a model of the form:

> *Commissioned by First Savings & Loan of Calloway County to establish boundary confirmation and interior square footage verification for the property known as Hollowmere House, parcel 44-C, prior to refinancing. Exterior dimensions taken this date under clear skies, temperature 61°F, wind negligible.*

Then the measurements. And Ivo felt the small cold hand close around the back of her neck before she understood why, because the numbers were familiar—not approximately familiar, not *in the neighborhood* familiar, but exact. The east wall: 41 feet, 7 and three-quarter inches. Her own laser measure had returned 41'7¾". The north elevation, including porch: 68'2¼". Hers: 68'2¼". Fifty-four years apart, two different instruments, two different women, and the house giving up the same numbers to the sixteenth of an inch.

Houses settled. Foundations shifted. A building measured in 1971 and again today should disagree with itself by fractions, by drift, by the slow argument between frost and concrete. This house agreed with itself perfectly, the way a liar agrees with himself when he's rehearsed.

She turned the page.

Pages two through five were the exterior survey, complete and confident, Edith Marsh's handwriting a tidy architectural print, each numeral formed with a draftsman's discipline. Ivo found herself liking this woman across the decades—the marginal note on page three (*re-measured N corner; first reading suspect, instrument or operator TBD*) showed a mind that distrusted its own instruments before it distrusted the world. That was the correct order of operations. That was the creed.

Page six was where the interior survey began, and page six was where the handwriting began to change.

It was subtle at first. Ivo might have missed it if she hadn't spent ten years reading other surveyors' field notes for a living, learning the biometrics of penmanship the way other people learned faces. The letters were still Edith Marsh's—but they were *slower*. The confident forward slant had eased toward vertical. The numerals had grown rounder. On page seven, rounder still, and the margins—which had held dry professional annotations (*verify w/ county plat*)—now held things like:

> *The hallway is longer today than yesterday. Confirmed twice. Same tape, same endpoints. Do not write this part down. (Written down anyway.)*

Ivo sat back. The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere above her, through the floor, the municipal building conducted its daytime business—footsteps, a telephone, a door—and all of it sounded very far away, sounds arriving from another country.

*Do not write this part down. Written down anyway.*

She knew that sentence. Not its words—its *shape*. It was the shape of a person arguing with themselves in real time on the record, a professional watching their own disbelief fail and documenting the failure because documentation was the last tool left. Ivo had written sentences like that. She had written them in her own field book three days ago, in the rented room at the Hollowmere Motor Court, after the thermal camera had shown her a heat signature standing motionless on the porch stairs at 2 a.m., human-shaped and four feet tall, and she had typed into her log: *anomaly consistent with sensor artifact; recommend recalibration*—and then, in the paper notebook she kept for herself, the one no client ever saw: *There is nothing there. There is nothing there. Check again tomorrow.*

She turned the pages faster now. She told herself it was efficiency. It was not efficiency.

Page eight. The handwriting had changed hands. There was no other way to describe it. The letters were still legible, still English, but they belonged to someone younger—the loops too large, the baseline wavering, the kind of writing produced by a wrist without fully developed fine motor control. A child's handwriting, executed in Edith Marsh's ink, in Edith Marsh's file, in the middle of a licensed structural survey.

And the content had changed with it. The dry survey language was still there, wearing the new handwriting like a borrowed coat:

> *Interior room 4 measures 14 ft 3 in by 11 ft 9 in. Exterior does not contain room 4. Exterior has never contained room 4. Asked Mr. Aldous (owner) about room 4. He said which room. He has lived here nine years. He said which room.*

Below that, in the same childish script but pressed harder into the paper, the pen nearly through:

> *the count is wrong*

Ivo became aware that she was holding her breath, and made herself breathe, because breathing was measurable and measurable things behaved.

Page nine. The child-hand had degraded further, letters collapsing into a scrawl that leaned off the ruled lines as though sliding downhill. Whole passages were crossed out—not edited, *buried*, the pen dragged back and forth until the paper shone silver with graphite and tore in places. But the crossings-out had been done carelessly, or desperately, and Ivo could still read underneath them. She read them the way you read anything forbidden: completely, immediately, against your own interest.

> *~~Mrs. Aldous says there are five of us at dinner but I only see four and she sets the fifth plate herself and smiles at the chair~~*

> *~~the cellar door was bricked over in the photos from '58 and it is open now and it was not open when I got here~~*

> *~~Mr. Aldous asked me today how long I have lived here. I said I am the surveyor. He looked at me the way you look at someone who has said something sad.~~*

Ivo's mouth had gone dry. She looked up at the shelf labels—1968–1974, ordinary cardboard, ordinary dust—and made herself perform the ritual: check the source, verify the provenance, consider alternative explanations. Forgery? To what end, in 1971, in a refinance file nobody would read? Mental breakdown? Possible. Documented, gradual, internally consistent mental breakdown that tracked precisely with the same measurement anomalies Ivo herself had logged four days ago?

Her laser measure disagreed with the exterior footprint by 6.2 percent. Every room. Every time. As if the discrepancy were deliberate. A signature.

Edith Marsh had found the same signature. In 1971. And then—

Page ten was almost blank. One line, centered, in handwriting so young it could have come off a first-grade worksheet, the letters enormous and careful, each one drawn rather than written:

> *he counts us at night. i hear him. he is very patient.*

And beneath it, smaller, in what might have been Edith Marsh surfacing one last time—or drowning:

> *my name is edith marsh. i am forty-three years old. i live at 14 corliss street. if anyone reads this, my sister's number is under M in my desk at the office. tell her i'm sorry about the car.*

Ivo stared at that line for a long time. *Sorry about the car.* The specific griefs of the doomed—they were always so specific, so mundane, the mind reaching for the smallest thing it could hold. She thought of her father, and stopped thinking of her father, in the practiced way of thirty years.

Page eleven was the last page. It was not a survey page. It was a sheet of the same county bond paper, but it had been folded in half and half again, and when Ivo unfolded it, a pressed flower fell out—a violet, browned to tissue, intact enough to show it had been picked whole, stem and all, by someone who wanted to keep something alive.

The final entry. One line. The handwriting on it was neither Edith Marsh's nor the child's. It was rounder than the child's, more certain, the letters of someone very young who had been writing for a long time beyond their years, and it was written in a brownish ink that Ivo refused, professionally, to identify:

**count of five, corrected**

No date. No signature. No closing punctuation. Just the period at the end of the sentence, sitting on the page like a full stop placed by something that knew exactly what a full stop meant.

* * *

Ivo photocopied all eleven pages, then photographed them as well, then stood holding the originals and understood that she did not want to put them back in the folder. She wanted to take them. She wanted to take them the way you want to pull a splinter—to remove the thing from the body even if the wound stays.

"Photocopies are fifteen cents," Doris said from the doorway, making Ivo flinch hard enough to rattle the table. Doris watched her with those forty-year eyes. "You can't take the original. Nothing leaves this room except copies. That's the rule."

"Right." Ivo slid the folder closed. Her hands were steady. She noted that they were steady, and noted that noting it was itself becoming a habit, a tally, a count. "Doris. The Marsh file. What happened to her?"

Doris came into the room and did not touch the folder, but stood near it the way people stand near graves they haven't visited in a while. "Officially? Left town. Office closed up, sister came and cleared out the desk, car turned up abandoned eventually—out past the old Hollis farm, back acre of some property, engine rusted solid. Unofficially?" She took off her glasses, which somehow made her older. "Unofficially, there's a woman in this county who will tell you her mother waited by the phone for a call that never came, and there's a plot in the Corliss Street cemetery with a stone that says EDITH MARSH, 1927–1971, and no body under it, because you can't bury what nobody found. The family needed the stone more than the truth. Families usually do."

"The police report?"

"There isn't one." Doris said it flatly, and let the flatness do its work. "Grown woman, no dependents, car left behind, office closed voluntarily—on paper that's a person who walked away. People walk away." She put her glasses back on. "Except her sister swore Edith would never leave without saying goodbye, and her sister was right, and being right didn't help her any."

Ivo gathered her photocopies. Eleven pages, warm from the machine, smelling faintly of toner and dust. "The others," she said. "You said four of us. Five, counting the man who came twice. What did the others find?"

"They found what you found." Doris walked her to the stairs. "And they each decided something different. The first one decided it was a hoax and wrote a clean report and got paid. The second one decided it was dangerous and wrote a clean report and got paid, and moved to Arizona, far as I know. The third one—" Doris paused at the bottom of the stairs, one hand on the rail. "The third one was a young man, very thorough, very serious. He came down here twice, like I said. Second time he asked me not for the Marsh file but for everything *adjacent* to it. Permits, census fragments, obituaries. He was building a picture. And then he stopped coming, and I heard later he'd recommended the bank decline the loan, no explanation given, and he moved away too. Everyone moves away. That's the thing about this county, honey. It keeps the houses and exports the people."

"And me?" Ivo heard herself ask. "What do I decide?"

Doris smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, and not entirely not one. "That's not mine to observe," she said. "But I'll tell you what I tell all of you, free of charge, since you paid your fifteen cents." She leaned in slightly, and the fluorescents caught the wire in her bifocals. "Edith Marsh was the best surveyor this county ever had. Better than you, probably, no offense. She measured everything twice. She trusted her instruments and she checked them and she documented like a court reporter. And the last thing she left behind wasn't a measurement at all. It was an apology about a car." Doris straightened. "Whatever's out at that house, it doesn't beat you with numbers. Numbers are how it *invites* you. You follow the discrepancy, and the discrepancy follows you back, and one day you're writing in a hand that isn't yours and calling it field notes."

"I don't write in anyone's hand but mine," Ivo said.

"No," Doris agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

* * *

The drive back took nineteen minutes. Ivo knew because she timed it, because timing was a prayer she still believed in.

She drove with the photocopies in a document sleeve on the passenger seat, and at the long curve where Route 9 bent around the reservoir, she pulled over—not because she needed to, but because her hands wanted the wheel less than they wanted the papers, and she had learned to obey the stronger instrument.

She spread page eleven on her knee. **count of five, corrected.**

Five. The Delacortes were three: Marcus, Sela, Theo. Three living residents. Wren had said the house counted wrong—counted four when there should be three, because the dead mother was still on the ledger. Four. But Edith's final entry said five.

Unless the ledger had changed since 1971. Unless counts could go up as well as down. Unless *corrected* didn't mean finished—it meant *balanced*, the way a debt balances, and debts could be reopened.

Or unless—her mind offered it reluctantly, the way the body offers pain—unless the count already included someone who didn't know she was on it.

She thought of Wren on the porch. *You took your time.* Twenty-five years, unchanged, chalk-dust and shadow, waiting with the patience of something that had been assigned a task and had never once doubted it would be completed. She thought of the thermal camera's four-foot figure on the stairs. She thought of her own field book, and the sentence she'd written three nights ago in the dark: *There is nothing there,* and how many times she'd written it, and why a person writes a thing repeatedly unless some part of her is keeping a tally of denials, counting them, the way you count stitches, the way you count—

"Stop," she said aloud, to the car, to the reservoir, to herself.

The house counted its residents. Fine. Hypothesis, unverified. The house had counted Edith Marsh. Also hypothesis. But hypotheses had a way of becoming conclusions at Hollowmere, and the paper trail in her lap ran in only one direction: eleven pages from competence to a child's hand to a brown-ink full stop, a surveyor following her own measurements inward like a thread into a shell, and at the center of the shell, not a pearl.

A mouth.

That was the image, and she let herself have it because naming a fear was a form of measuring it: she was following a paper trail into a mouth, and the trail was laid out neatly, page by page, the way food is arranged.

She started the car. In the rearview mirror, Route 9 lay empty behind her, gray and straight, and she watched it for three seconds longer than driving required, confirming—professionally, rigorously—that nothing was following her.

Nothing was.

Something was ahead. The trees thickened along the road the last two miles, and the light through them went green and underwater, and when Hollowmere's chimneys rose above the tree line, Ivo saw that there was a car already in the drive that hadn't been there that morning—Marcus's truck, yes, but beside it a bicycle lying on its side, wheels still turning.

Theo's bicycle. Wheels still turning, though no one was near it.

Ivo parked behind the truck, took the document sleeve, and got out. From somewhere inside the house—through walls that measured larger than they should, through air that smelled of pencil shavings and pond water—came a sound she catalogued before she could stop herself, because cataloguing was the last tool left.

Counting. A boy's voice, high and steady and terribly calm, counting upward without hurry, as if he had all the time in the world, or as if the world had exactly as much time as his count allowed.

"—forty-one, forty-two, forty-three—"

Sela Delacorte was standing at the edge of the porch, and when she saw the document sleeve under Ivo's arm, the girl's eyes went to it—not to Ivo's face, to the *papers*—with an expression Ivo had seen only once before, fifty-four years ago, in a photograph of a woman named Edith Marsh: the expression of someone recognizing a fellow reader of a book that should never have been opened.

"You found her file," Sela said. It wasn't a question. "I need to show you something. I've been drawing it. You'll want to check it against your numbers."

Inside, Theo's voice rose, serene and terrible, past fifty.
