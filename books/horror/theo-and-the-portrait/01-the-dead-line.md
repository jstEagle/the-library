## 1. The Dead Line

The ON AIR light was the only red thing in the world at midnight, and Emrys Thackeray loved it the way some people loved a fireplace — for the warmth of it, and for the way it made the dark outside feel intentional.

"Good morning, insomniacs," she said, leaning into the microphone the way she'd learned to in her first year at WQRT, back when the station still had a midnight slot worth filling and she still had a voice that didn't crack on certain words. "You're listening to *The Dead Line* with Emrys Thackeray, broadcasting live from a basement studio in a town that swears it's asleep. It isn't. I can hear you."

She let the board breathe — the hiss of open frequencies, the low hum of the transmitter two floors up, the sound she'd once described on air as *the station dreaming*. Outside the studio's single window, October had gone to work on the maple trees, and the parking lot was a shallow lake of copper and rust-colored leaves that the wind kept trying to organize and failing.

"Tonight's topic," she said, "is last words. Not the famous kind — not Goethe asking for more light, not whatever Oscar Wilde said about the wallpaper. I mean the small ones. The last thing you said to somebody that you didn't know was the last thing. Call in and tell me. Or don't, and spend the next eight hours regretting it, which is also a valid way to spend a Tuesday."

Tuesday. She'd said it lightly, the way she said everything, and only after the word was out did she feel it catch on something in her chest, a fishhook of a date. Last Tuesday. She let the silence run two seconds too long and covered it with a breath, smooth, professional, the kind of sleight of mouth you could do after eleven years of midnight shifts.

The board lit up. Line one.

"WQRT, you're on *The Dead Line*."

The voice that came through was a woman's, mid-fifties maybe, with the flat calm of someone reading from notes. "Is this live? Really live?"

"Really live. Who am I talking to?"

"Delia Marsh." A pause, long enough that Emrys glanced at the board to see if the call had dropped. "I wanted to tell you about my last words. You asked."

"Go ahead, Delia."

"I said, *hand me that wrench.*" The woman laughed — a small, dry sound, like paper turning. "To my husband. He was under the truck. I said, hand me that wrench, and he said, which one, and I said, the half-inch, and he said, you're standing on it. And I laughed and bent down to pick it up, and that was it. That was the last thing. *You're standing on it.*"

Emrys had heard ten thousand stories from ten thousand midnights. She knew the rhythm of grief on a phone line the way a nurse knew a pulse. This had the rhythm. But something underneath it was wrong — a hollowness in the signal, as though the woman were calling from inside a large empty room, or from the bottom of a well, or from somewhere the sound had to travel a very long way to reach the microphone.

"That's a hard one," Emrys said gently. "Where are you calling from tonight, Delia?"

"St. Ambrose," the woman said. "The new section. Past the fence, where they put the ones from last week."

Emrys laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, a little wicked, the laugh that had kept her employed through three format changes and two station managers. "St. Ambrose Cemetery? Delia, I admire the commitment to the bit, I do. You're my kind of caller."

"It's not a bit." No anger in it. That was the strange part. No defensiveness, no hurt. Just the flat calm of someone stating a fact about the weather. "I died last Tuesday. The fourteenth. The jack slipped. I wanted you to know the last words because you asked, and because nobody else asks anymore. Everybody just brings flowers and stands around being sad at each other, and nobody asks the dead what they said."

Emrys looked at the call screen. The number field was blank. Not withheld — *blank*, an empty white rectangle where ten digits should have been, as though the call had come from a phone that had never been assigned to anyone.

"Delia," she said, and kept her voice level and amused because that was the job, that was always the job, "if you're calling from St. Ambrose, then who's keeping your husband's coffee warm?"

The pause that followed was the longest two seconds of her professional life.

"He doesn't make coffee anymore," Delia Marsh said. "He just sits in the truck."

Emrys's mouth was still smiling. She could feel it, the smile hanging there on her face like a mask nailed up in an abandoned house. "Okay. We're going to take a break — Delia, thank you for calling, and I mean that. You've given us all something to think about. Let's go to the phones. Line two, you're on *The Dead Line*."

Line two was a man who wanted to talk about his dog, and Emrys rode that call for eleven grateful minutes, laughing in the right places, steering with the easy hand of long habit, while some cold small thing in the back of her skull kept repeating *he just sits in the truck* in Delia Marsh's flat paper voice.

Because here was the thing she wasn't saying on air. Here was the thing she was going to go home and not say to her empty apartment and not say to the ceiling at four a.m.

She knew Delia Marsh.

Not well. Delia Marsh had been the clerk at the hardware store on Corliss Street for as long as Emrys had lived in this town, a broad, capable woman with sawdust in her hairline who had once spent forty minutes helping Emrys pick the right anchor bolts for a shelving unit and had refused, flatly, to let her buy the wrong ones. And last Wednesday, standing in the checkout line at the grocery store, Emrys had overheard two women by the magazines talking in the hushed, relishing way people talk about a death. *Did you hear about Delia Marsh? The jack. Right there in the driveway. Her husband found her.*

Last Tuesday. The fourteenth.

Emrys had not known Delia Marsh well. But she knew the hardware store's number by heart, because she'd called it twice that summer, and she was looking at it now on the sticky note on her monitor, and the number on the note was not the number that had lit up line one. Line one had no number at all.

*Everybody just brings flowers and stands around being sad at each other, and nobody asks the dead what they said.*

"Let's take another call," Emrys heard herself say, and her voice was steady, her voice was always steady, that was the whole trick of her, that was the brand, the woman on the midnight frequency who was never rattled. "Line three."

Line three clicked open, and for a moment there was nothing on it but that hollow room-silence, that well-bottom silence, and then a voice said:

"Emmy."

Not *Emrys*. Not *Ms. Thackeray*. Not *you're on the air*.

*Emmy.*

The name went through her like a nail through wet wood. There were exactly two people alive — had been exactly two people, she corrected, and the correction made the studio tilt — who had ever called her that, and one of them was her mother, and her mother had been dead for nine years, and the other one —

"Emmy, it's me."

The voice was Wren's voice. Of course it was Wren's voice. It was the voice that had sung harmony three inches from her ear in the back seat of their mother's Buick; the voice that had called her at two in the morning from a dorm in Bellingham to say *I met someone*; the voice that had gotten harder and stranger over the years, clipped and distant, the voice that had said *I can't do this anymore, Emmy* two years ago and then not called again. Emrys knew that voice the way she knew her own hands.

And Wren had drowned on Saturday.

That was the other thing the grocery store magazines had been for, the other thing the two women had been whispering about when Emrys came around the endcap with her basket. *The Thackeray girl. The one who used to be around, the radio one's sister. Out at Miller Pond, they think she slipped. Nobody found her for two days.*

Nobody found her for two days. Emrys had driven out to Miller Pond on Sunday and stood on the bank in the rain while men in a boat dragged the gray water, and she had thought, with a detachment that frightened her even then: *she's been under there since Saturday. She's been under there this whole time, under the surface, in the dark, alone.* She had gone home and not slept and come to work and put the red light on, because that was the only thing she knew how to do with a feeling that size, which was to put it in a box and broadcast around it.

"Line three," Emrys said, and her voice did not waver, and she would think about that later, the sheer inhuman steadiness of it, the way grief and terror had apparently agreed between them to save it all for later. "You're on *The Dead Line*. Go ahead."

"Emmy." A breath, wet-sounding, close to the mouthpiece. "Don't hang up."

"Who is this?"

"You know who it is." The voice was patient. That was the worst of it — the patience, the gentleness, the tone Wren had used when Emrys was nine and had broken the snow globe and thought the world was ending. "It's counting us, Emmy."

"Counting." Emrys's hand found the edge of the console and held it. The board's lights were steady, green and red, ordinary, sane. "Counting what? Who is this? Give me a name."

"You know my name. You said it at the pond. You stood on the bank and you said it and I heard you, I heard it through the water, it was the only thing that got through." The wet breath again. "Listen to me. Listen. It's counting us. Don't let it finish."

"Finish what?" Emrys heard her own voice climb and dragged it back down. "Wren — if this is Wren — finish *what*?"

"Finish *me*."

The word came out of the speaker and into the studio and it seemed to Emrys, in that moment, that the room got colder, that the red ON AIR light dimmed as though something large had passed in front of it, that every sound in the building — the transmitter's hum, the ventilation, the tick of the clock over the door — stopped at once, the way a forest goes silent when a predator moves through it.

"It started with the others," Wren's voice said, faster now, urgent, fraying. "It goes down a list. It paints them and then it's done with them and then it moves on. Emmy, I can feel it. I can feel it *working*. I'm — I'm not all here anymore. Parts of me are going somewhere and I can't — " The voice caught. "Don't let it finish me. Please. You were always the one who finished things. Finish it first. Find where it — "

The line went dead.

Not faded. Not dropped with a click and a dial tone. It went dead the way a candle goes out — one instant present, the next instant a small black absence where a presence had been, so total that Emrys's ear, pressed to the headset, rang with it.

And then the transmitter died too.

She knew it before the meters told her. She knew it by the silence, the deep architectural silence of a building that has stopped broadcasting, a silence with a shape, a pressure, like the whole station holding its breath. The ON AIR light flickered, guttered, went out. The console's lights died in a wave, board to board, left to right, as though something were walking through the room switching everything off behind itself. The clock over the door stopped at 12:47.

Emrys sat in the dark with the headset still on her ears, listening to nothing, and her heart was going like a trapped bird and her hands — she raised them into the faint green glow of the emergency strip over the fire exit and looked at them — her hands were shaking. Not trembling. Shaking, a fine violent shudder, the hands of a woman on her third day without sleep or the hands of a woman who has just heard her drowned sister's voice ask her for help.

*It's counting us. Don't let it finish.*

She took the headset off. She put it down. She stood up, and her knees held, which surprised her, and she walked the length of the dark studio to the transmitter room door, and the stairwell beyond it was pitch black and cold and smelled like dust and hot metal, and she climbed it anyway, one hand on the rail, because she was Emrys Thackeray and she did not believe in ghosts, she had built an entire career and an entire personality on not believing in them, and there had to be a reason the transmitter was down, there was always a reason, a breaker, a storm, a squirrel in a substation, the mundane machinery of a rational world.

The transmitter room was dark. The transmitter was dark. Every light on it was out, and it was not humming, and in eleven years she had never once heard that room be silent.

She found the breaker panel by feel. Every breaker was thrown. She reset them one by one, and the panel hummed, and somewhere above her the tower took its first breath of power, and she stood in the cold metal room with her shaking hands pressed flat against the panel and said, out loud, to no one, in a voice that came out smaller than she'd ever heard it:

"She's dead. She's been dead since Saturday. Dead people don't call radio stations."

The transmitter, warming up behind its cage, made no argument.

* * *

She finished the shift. Of course she finished the shift. The board came back up at 1:15 and she put on music, long wordless stretches of it, and she let the phones ring and ring and did not answer them, and at 1:40 she called the number for the hardware store on Corliss Street from memory, knowing no one would answer, and no one did, and at 2:30 she called directory assistance and asked, in a voice she kept very casual, for the listing of a Delia Marsh on Fenwick Road, and the automated voice read it out to her, cheerful and indifferent, and Emrys thanked it and hung up and sat looking at the ten digits she had written down without deciding to, as though her hand had its own plans.

At 5:52 a.m., the sky over the parking lot went the color of a bruise healing, and Emrys signed off.

She leaned into the microphone. The red light was on. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were still shaking, a tremor running through them like current through a wire, and she folded them in her lap where the microphone couldn't see.

"That's the show," she said. Her voice was steady. It was always steady. It was, she was beginning to understand, the only steady thing about her. "To everyone who called tonight — thank you. Especially the ones I didn't get to." A breath. "You're listening to WQRT. I'm Emrys Thackeray. Get some sleep if you can. And if someone you've lost ever calls you — "

She stopped. On air. Dead air, for three full seconds, which was a sin in broadcasting, which was a firing offense, which she had never once committed in eleven years.

" — answer," she said. "Good night."

She killed the light. She sat in the gray dawn silence of the studio, and she looked at her shaking hands, and she thought about the blank space where a phone number should be, and the stopped clock, and the thrown breakers, and a woman's voice saying *he just sits in the truck* in the flat tone of someone reciting facts from the far side of a fence.

And then she thought about the thing Wren had said last, the thing that had been drowned under all the rest, the thing her mind had been carefully not touching all night the way you don't touch a burn.

*Find where it —*

The line had died there. But Emrys had spent eleven years taking calls from strangers in the dark, and she had gotten very good at hearing the ends of sentences that callers couldn't bring themselves to say.

*Find where it lives. Find where it works. Find where it's counting from.*

She pulled a legal pad across the console, and with a hand that would not stop shaking, she wrote down the only thread she had: a list of the dead, and the dates they'd died, and the dates — she was almost sure, she needed to check the logs, but she was almost sure — that they had called her show.

Delia Marsh. Died Tuesday the fourteenth. Called tonight, Wednesday the fifteenth.

And Wren. Died Saturday the eleventh. Called tonight, Wednesday the fifteenth.

Five days. Four days. The calls were coming days after the deaths, but they were coming *in order*, oldest first, like a list being read from the top.

Emrys stared at the pad until the numbers blurred, and outside the window the sun came up over the copper leaves, ordinary and golden and obscene, and she thought: *something is counting. Something is working its way down a list, and it started before Delia Marsh, and it will not stop at my sister.*

She picked up her keys, and she went home to not sleep, and in the morning she was going to start pulling phone records.
