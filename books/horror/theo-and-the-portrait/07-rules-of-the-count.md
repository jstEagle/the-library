## 7. Rules of the Count

Cordelia Aldridge had learned to sleep in intervals of ninety minutes, because that was how long a person could safely be still.

She had timed it herself, over decades of notebooks filled with dates and rotations and small, careful observations. Ninety minutes was the outer edge. Past that, if you stayed too long in one room — past that, if you let yourself *settle* — the house began to notice. Not the house, exactly. She had stopped blaming the house years ago. Houses were only wood and plaster and bad wiring. What noticed was older than the house, patient as varnish, and it lived in the gallery at the end of the east hall, behind a door she kept locked with three separate keys and one superstition.

Tonight she woke at 1:47 a.m., four minutes before her alarm, which meant the house had woken her, which meant something had shifted.

She lay in the dark of her room on the ground floor — Cordelia never slept above the ground floor; stairs were time, and time was what they could not afford — and listened. The boiler ticked. The wind worried the loose shingle above the porch like a tongue working a sore tooth. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a brush being loaded against the lip of a jar.

*The new woman,* Cordelia thought, and sat up so fast her neck cracked.

Emrys Thackeray had been in the house six days. Six days of cheerful skepticism, of tapping mirrors and taking notes, of asking questions in that radio voice of hers — warm, level, designed to make people confess things. Silas liked her. That was the dangerous part. Mara had started leaving her door open when Emrys walked past, another dangerous part. And Theo — Theo had taken her hand on the second day, unprompted, and Cordelia had watched it happen from the kitchen doorway and felt thirty years of vigilance tilt on its axis like furniture sliding across a tilted floor.

Cordelia put on her robe, took the ring of keys from the hook by her bed, and went down the hall to check the count.

* * *

The rule was this: the portrait always finishes someone.

Not might. Not could. Her grandmother, Adela, had written it out in 1912 in a hand gone shaky, on the back of a funeral notice for a man who did not have a funeral because there was nothing left to bury:

*It finishes someone. It does not care who. It only cares when.*

Adela had been nineteen when Edmund Hollowell died at his easel in the autumn of 1911, mid-stroke, his heart giving out with the brush still wet against the canvas of the family portrait he'd been painting of the household — five figures, himself among them, though he'd left his own face blank, saying he'd save the best for last. The family had buried him. The family had kept the portrait, because it was valuable and because grief makes people stupid about objects. And then, over the following winter, the portrait had begun to finish itself.

First the background deepened. Then the light shifted, moving through painted afternoons no one remembered sitting for. Then the figures began to sharpen — not change, sharpen, the way a photograph comes up in solution — and with each sharpening, somewhere in the county, someone died. A farmhand. A schoolteacher. Adela's own father, found at the bottom of the cistern with his face turned up toward a sky that had already moved on without him.

Adela understood before anyone else did. She stood in front of the portrait one night in February and watched the painter's blank space where his face should be begin, very slowly, to fill — and she understood that the dead man had not stopped working. He had simply changed materials. He needed a face to finish himself, and he would take whichever one the paint completed first, and the paint completed whoever stood still longest, whoever loved the house most, whoever consented — knowingly or not — to be part of the picture.

So Adela packed a bag and left before dawn, and the filling stopped. The face half-formed, hovering, hungry. She came back in the spring, married into the name Aldridge, and spent the rest of her life learning the rules the way other women learned recipes.

*Keep them moving. Keep them unfinished. Never let anyone love the house more than they fear it.*

Cordelia had inherited the notebook along with the keys, and she had added to it, in her own tidy hand, for thirty years.

* * *

The gallery door was unlocked.

Her stomach dropped through the floorboards. Three keys, and the third one — the brass one, the one she'd had blessed by a priest in 1987 who told her afterward that he'd rather not be paid — hung on the inside hook, untouched. Which meant either she had forgotten to lock it last night, an error she had not made since the Reagan administration, or the door had decided it wanted to be open.

Inside, moonlight fell through the tall windows in pale columns, and the portrait waited at the end of the room the way it always waited: enormous, gilt-framed, five figures arranged on the lawn of the house in its younger century. Edmund Hollowell stood at the edge of his own composition, brush raised, his face a smooth oval of primed canvas. He had been mid-gesture for a hundred and thirteen years.

Cordelia crossed the floor slowly, slippers silent, and looked.

Nothing had changed. That was the relief and the terror of it — nothing had changed, and yet the air in the gallery tasted different, the way a room tastes after someone has just left it. She stood before the canvas and counted the figures twice, an old habit, lips moving. Father. Mother. Girl. Boy. Painter. Five. The sixth space — the space that opened whenever the count ran hot, the space that had worn Wren's drowned face for two nights last week until Cordelia burned the canvas and it healed itself by dusk — the sixth space was empty now. Just lawn. Just painted grass, waiting.

But the brushes. The jar of turpentine on the side table, which Cordelia dusted around and never touched, which had been dry as a bone since 1994 — the jar held liquid. It caught the moonlight and threw it back, and the smell reached her a moment later, sharp and sweet, and under the sweetness something organic and wrong, like water that has held something too long.

Someone had been here tonight. Or something had been preparing for someone.

"Damn you," Cordelia said softly, to the room, to the painter, to her own failing memory. "Damn you, we had a system."

The system was movement. That was all it had ever been, and it had worked, God help her, it had worked for three decades. Every member of the household rotated rooms on a schedule Cordelia kept in her head and in the notebook both, because paper burned and heads, whatever else they did, stayed attached. No one slept in the same bed two nights running. No one lingered in the gallery. Birthdays were celebrated early, off-site, in restaurants where the mirrors were cheap and honest. Photographs were taken sparingly and never, ever posed — a posed photograph was a sitting, and a sitting was consent, and Cordelia had burned more family snapshots than she cared to remember.

Silas thought she was eccentric. Mara thought she was controlling. Theo — Theo, poor lamb, Theo knew, because Theo had seen his mother in the mirror, standing a heartbeat behind the living, and children who see that kind of thing don't need the rules explained. They just need them kept.

And now there was Emrys Thackeray, sleeping upstairs in the east wing, in the room directly above the gallery, in a bed that hadn't been slept in since Cordelia's brother was alive — a stranger with a skeptic's smile and a dead sister's voice on her answering machine, and Cordelia had hired her, had *agreed* to it, because Silas had looked at the woman with an expression Cordelia hadn't seen on his face since Elena died, and what was she supposed to do? Stand in the doorway with the notebook and say *no, love, we can't have joy in this house, joy is how it finds the edges of you?*

Well. Yes. That was precisely what she was supposed to do. She just hadn't managed it.

* * *

Back in her room, Cordelia took out the notebook — the current one, the eleventh — and read by lamplight, though she knew every line.

*1979. Mother finished. I was twenty-six. I had gone to town for aspirin and come home to find the sixth figure wearing her face, complete, hands folded, smiling. The funeral was closed casket. There wasn't enough of her left to be open. Rule confirmed: it takes the one who is most at rest. Mother had finally stopped moving. She said she was tired. Tiredness is a door.*

*1994. Offered myself. Sat in the gallery at midnight and told it, aloud, take me instead, take my face, spare Daniel and his babies. It refused. Refused. I sat there until sunrise feeling like a beggar turned away from a feast. Wrote it down so I'd never forget: desperation is not consent. It wants willingness. It wants you to choose it the way you choose sleep. God forgive me, I have never been able to want anything that much since.*

That was the sentence she had never spoken aloud to anyone, not even to the dark: that after 1994, some part of her had been relieved. Relieved, and then ashamed of the relief, and then hollowed out by the shame, until vigilance was all that remained — vigilance and the rotation schedule and the three keys and the ninety-minute sleeps. Love, in this house, had been reduced to logistics. She fed them. She moved them. She counted them at breakfast like a shepherd counting sheep with wolves at the treeline, and if her voice was sharp sometimes, if Mara rolled her eyes and called her General Cordelia behind her back — well. Sheep didn't thank the dog either.

She turned the page.

*2003. Elena begins lingering. Warned Silas, gently. He thinks I disapprove of her garden. Let him think it.*

Elena. Cordelia closed her eyes. Elena had loved this house the way the portrait required — wholly, rootedly, as if the place were a body and she its blood. Cordelia had warned her in the only language she had, obliquely, over tea: *don't get comfortable, dear, houses notice.* And Elena had laughed and said Aunt Cordelia, you act like the walls are listening, and Cordelia had said nothing, because what was there to say that wouldn't end with a padded cell in her future?

When Elena died — properly died, in a hospital, of the thing in her lungs that no portrait had any hand in — Cordelia had driven straight home from the ward and gone to the gallery at 3 a.m., and the fifth figure had already begun to change. The 1911 girl's face softening, reshaping, becoming a face Cordelia knew. Becoming Elena, tired, waiting, patient as varnish.

The mirror-lag had started that same week. Cordelia was almost sure. Almost sure it hadn't been there before.

* * *

At 4 a.m., unable to sleep, she climbed the stairs.

Stairs were time. But the notebook also said, in Adela's shaking hand, *when the jars fill, count faces*, and Cordelia had smelled turpentine, and there was one more face to count.

She went first to Mara's room. The girl slept tangled in blankets, phone glowing on the pillow beside her head, breathing the deep aggrieved breaths of seventeen. Alive. Unfinished. Cordelia stood in the doorway long enough to confirm the rise and fall of her chest and moved on.

Theo's door was ajar, as it always was; the boy had never once closed it fully, not since his mother died, as if a shut door might trap him on the wrong side of the heartbeat. He was asleep with the lamp still on, a book of constellation charts open on his chest. Alive. Cordelia eased the door wider, checked the mirror on his dresser — running true, showing only the sleeping boy, only now — and felt the tight thing in her chest loosen by one degree.

Then the east wing. The guest room. The room above the gallery.

Emrys Thackeray's door was closed. Light seeped underneath it, gold and unsteady — candlelight, or lamplight, or the blue flicker of a screen. Cordelia raised her hand to knock and heard, through the wood, a voice.

Emrys was talking to someone. Low, rhythmic, the cadence of a broadcast — and beneath it, faintly, a second voice. A caller. Cordelia pressed her ear to the door and caught fragments: *—if you're just joining us—* and *—tell me where you are—* and then a silence, and then Emrys again, softer, saying a name Cordelia couldn't catch, saying *I'm sorry, I'm so sorry—*

The radio, Cordelia thought. The woman talks to a radio in the middle of the night. Grief does that. Grief keeps the lines open.

But her hand stayed flat against the door, and her heart was doing arithmetic it didn't want to finish. Because the guest room radio had been unplugged. Cordelia had unplugged it herself, the day Emrys arrived — old habit, dead appliances, no voices in the house but the ones with pulses — and she had wound the cord around the set in a neat coil that she could see, even now, even through a closed door, in her mind's eye.

A voice coming out of an unplugged radio was not on any page of the notebook. But it fit. Oh, it fit. The house had always used voices. It had used Elena's voice on Silas, that terrible spring, luring him down to the lake with promises Cordelia had never let him repeat aloud. It had used their mother's voice on Cordelia herself, once, in 1979, the week after the funeral — *come sit with me, darling, I'm so tired of waiting* — and Cordelia had gotten as far as the gallery door before Adela's training had caught her by the collar like a mother cat.

It was talking to the new woman. Already. On her sixth night.

Cordelia stepped back from the door. Downstairs, faint as breath, she heard it again: the wet kiss of a brush loading against glass.

She stood in the dark hallway between the sleeping children and the listening woman and understood, with the weary clarity of thirty years' practice, that the count had begun again — that it had perhaps never stopped, only paused, the way a painter pauses to mix a color — and that this time the rotation schedule and the three keys and the ninety-minute sleeps might not be enough, because the portrait had found something new to work with.

It had found someone willing to listen.

Cordelia went downstairs to fetch the notebook, the old one, Adela's — because tomorrow she was going to have to tell Emrys Thackeray everything, and for that she would need every rule, including the one she had never written down, the one that lived only in the scar tissue of 1994:

*It cannot take what will not sit. But it can wait a lifetime for you to tire.*

Behind her, above the gallery, the light under Emrys's door flickered — once, twice — and went out.
