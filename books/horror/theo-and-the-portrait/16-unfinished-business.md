## 16. Unfinished Business

The county archive occupied the basement of the old Carnegie library in Ashford Falls, a room that smelled of cold stone and older paper, lit by humming fluorescent tubes that buzzed like trapped insects. Emrys had been there since nine in the morning. It was now past two, and she had filled eleven pages of her notebook with dates, deeds, obituaries, and one photograph that she kept turning face-down whenever it looked at her too long.

The photograph was of Edmund Hollowell.

It came from a society page of the *Ashford Falls Courier*, dated March 1909 — a grainy halftone reproduction of a man standing beside an easel in a garden gone wild with ivy. He was tall, narrow through the shoulders, with a beard trimmed close to the jaw and eyes that the cheap printing process had rendered as two smudged holes. Even degraded by ink and time, the gaze came off the page with a weight she recognized. She'd felt it before, she realized. In the gallery at Hollowell House, from the unfinished fifth figure. From the canvas itself, if a canvas could be said to look.

*He's still looking,* she wrote in the margin, then crossed it out. Skeptics didn't write things like that. But she was no longer sure what she was. The word *believer* sat wrong too — belief implied faith, and faith implied comfort, and there was nothing comfortable about what she'd seen in that house. The mirrors running a second behind. The portrait growing hands overnight. Wren's drowned face surfacing in wet paint like a body rising in a lake.

She turned to the stack of clippings she'd requested from the archivist, a stooped woman named Mrs. Pell who had worked the desk since, by the look of her, sometime during the Nixon administration. Mrs. Pell had pulled everything on the Hollowell family without being asked twice. "Nobody's asked about them in years," she'd said, sliding the folder across. "People around here don't. There's a reason the house went to the state and not to any family." She hadn't elaborated. She'd simply looked at Emrys over her glasses for a long moment and added, "You're the radio woman. The one who takes calls from the dead."

Emrys hadn't corrected her. That was how far she'd fallen.

The clippings told a story in fragments, the way old newspapers always did — a story interrupted by its own ending.

Edmund Hollowell, born 1861, second son of Josiah Hollowell, shipping magnate. Educated abroad. Returned in 1888 with a reputation and a wife, Margaret, and settled into Hollowell House, which his father had built as a monument to money and which Edmund set about converting into a monument to himself. He painted. He painted obsessively, compulsively, according to every account. Portraits mostly — his own above all. The Courier's art column, such as it was, noted in 1897 that Hollowell had produced "no fewer than forty self-portraits in nine years, each more exacting than the last, as though Mr. Hollowell were engaged in some protracted negotiation with his own reflection."

Emrys read that sentence three times. *A negotiation with his own reflection.* Her pen hovered over the page. Everything in her wanted to dismiss it as Victorian purple prose. Everything else in her — the part that had heard her sister's voice through a dead transmitter — leaned closer.

* * *

The obituary was where the story stopped making sense, or started making the other kind.

EDMUND HOLLOWELL, PAINTER, DEAD AT 50
*Noted Artist Stricken in His Studio; Final Work Unfinished*

The article ran on November 14th, 1911. He had been found by a housemaid on the morning of the twelfth, collapsed at his easel in the third-floor studio, dead of what the coroner called "cardiac failure" and what the article's single line of local color called "a death so sudden the brush was yet wet in his hand." He had been working, the piece reported, on a self-portrait commissioned — oddly, the reporter seemed to think — by himself, for himself, a work he had reportedly told friends would be "the truest likeness ever made, because it would be finished by someone who knew the subject completely."

Emrys set the clipping down flat on the table and pressed both palms against her eyes until she saw constellations.

*A self-portrait he never finished.*

She thought of the gallery at Hollowell House. The long room on the east side, paneled in dark wood, hung with the family's history in gilt frames — and at the far end, under a sheet Cordelia Aldridge kept tucked around it like a bandage over a wound, the great unfinished canvas. Five figures. A father, a mother, three children, posed on the house's front steps in the fashion of 1911. Painted in Hollowell's unmistakable hand — she'd compared it to the signed works in the hall, the same nervous precision, the same light that seemed to come from nowhere in particular.

Except the five figures were wrong now. They moved between viewings. And the fifth figure was becoming Mara and Theo's mother, wearing her face with the patience of something that had all the time in the world, because it did.

And there had been a sixth figure. Wren. For one terrible morning, Wren's drowned face had floated on the canvas beside the family, and when Cordelia burned the painting, it had come back whole by dusk, and the sixth figure had grown hands.

*It always finishes someone.* Cordelia had said that in the kitchen, in her flat, tired voice, the voice of a woman who had been keeping vigil for thirty years. *The portrait claims whoever it completes first. My grandmother left before it could finish her, and it took her brother instead. So we keep moving. We keep the faces changing. We don't sit still long enough to be completed.*

Emrys had thought, at the time, that it was madness dressed as strategy. Now, in the archive's cold light, with the obituary bleeding through the back of the clipping onto her fingers, she understood that Cordelia had simply known things Emrys was only beginning to learn.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't stop adding itself up:

Edmund Hollowell died mid-self-portrait. His final work — the thing he'd called the truest likeness ever made — was left incomplete. And the last painting in his studio, the one the maid found him slumped before, was not the self-portrait at all.

Emrys had found the reference an hour ago, buried in a follow-up piece from December 1911 about the disposition of the estate. A passing sentence, easy to miss: *"The studio contained, besides the unfinished self-likeness, a large group portrait of the Hollowell family upon the front steps, also incomplete, which will be offered among the household effects."*

Two canvases. An unfinished self-portrait, and an unfinished family portrait, both abandoned on the same November morning.

One of them was still hungry.

* * *

She needed the estate records. Mrs. Pell produced them without comment — a ledger bound in cracked leather, the handwriting of three different probate clerks marching across its pages. Emrys traced the inventory of Hollowell House's contents, lot by lot, sold at auction in the spring of 1912. Furniture. Books. Silver. And under *Paintings and Artworks*, a list that made her stomach drop slowly, like an elevator whose cable had begun to sing.

Forty-one self-portraits, sold in lots. Landscapes. Studies. And at the bottom, in the clerk's careful hand:

*Lot 117 — Group portrait (unfinished), oil on canvas. Withdrawn.*

Withdrawn. Not sold. She flipped forward, looking for it in later inventories, tax assessments, anything. Nothing. The painting had simply vanished from the record, the way things vanish when nobody wants to write their name next to them.

But the house had kept it. Of course the house had kept it. It hung in the gallery now, five figures deep and counting, and the family that lived beneath it had inherited it the way you inherit a curse — silently, through walls and blood, without signature or consent.

Emrys closed the ledger and sat very still in the fluorescent hum.

Here was what she knew now, assembled piece by piece like a jigsaw whose picture only revealed itself at the edges: Edmund Hollowell had spent fifty years trying to paint the perfect image of himself and dying with it unfinished. Whatever he was — whatever he'd become in that last hour, brush wet, heart stopping, the truest likeness just out of reach — he had not accepted the incompleteness. He had built a workaround. Or the workaround had built itself out of his obsession; she genuinely couldn't tell anymore, and the distinction felt less important than it once would have.

The family portrait was the mechanism. Five figures, posed and waiting. It didn't take the living outright — it *finished* them. It painted a person stroke by stroke, night after night, gathering their likeness the way a spider gathers silk, and when the likeness was complete, the person belonged to it. Claimed. Folded into the composition. Another figure in Edmund Hollowell's endless group sitting, another face he could study, another step toward the completion he'd been denied.

That was why the figures changed. That was why the fifth figure was becoming Theo and Mara's mother — the portrait recycled its material, wore its claimed faces like costumes, or perhaps the claimed lingered inside it, layered like pentimento, ghost-images under the paint. That was why it had reached for Wren first, plucking her drowned face out of the static of the dead to use as bait, as scaffolding, as a sixth figure it could build toward the family and make itself inevitable.

And that was why the mirrors ran a second behind. The whole house was lagging, she thought. The whole house was a half-finished painting, waiting for its final stroke.

* * *

There was one more file. Mrs. Pell brought it unasked, setting it down beside Emrys's elbow with the delicacy of someone handling something that might bite. "Coroner's inquest," she said. "1911. Sealed for sixty years, open record since. You'll want the testimony of the housemaid. And the doctor's notes." A pause. "And there's a letter. Found in the studio. It's copied in full."

The inquest was brief and banal — a man of fifty, a weak heart, a cold studio, done. But the doctor's notes, appended in a sloping copperplate, contained a detail the newspaper had declined to print: when they moved the body, they found that Edmund Hollowell's hands could not be pried apart. Both fists were clenched around the brush, and the brush — this was the part the doctor had underlined twice, as though hoping emphasis would make it explicable — the brush was dry. Bone-dry, though the maid swore the stroke on the canvas had been wet when she found him that morning, glistening, as if freshly laid.

As if the painting had taken the paint back. As if it had drunk the last of him out of the brush and left the tool empty, spent, finished with him exactly as he had failed to finish himself.

Emrys read the letter four times.

It was addressed to no one. Undated, though the paper's edge showed it had been torn from the same pad as his working sketches, and the ink was the same iron-gall brown as the marginalia in his surviving notebooks. It read, in a hand that began controlled and deteriorated line by line into something frantic:

*I have understood my error. Forty self-portraits and I never once saw myself, because the eye cannot see the eye. The mirror lies by omission — it gives me everything except the seeing. But others see me. Others have always seen me truly, and I have wasted fifty years painting myself when I should have been painting them, gathering their sight of me, letting their faces do the work mine cannot.*

*A portrait of a family is a portrait of the painter reflected in every eye that sits for him. When it is complete, I will be complete. I have only to finish, and I am finished, and it is the same word, and I have only just now heard it.*

*They will sit for me. Everyone sits, in the end. Consent is a formality the living are happy to sign.*

Emrys put the letter down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected shaking. Instead there was a terrible clarity, the kind that comes after a fever breaks, when the room is finally still and you can see how sick you've been.

*Consent is a formality the living are happy to sign.*

But it wasn't. Not entirely. Cordelia had proven that in 1994, when she'd offered her own face to the canvas and been refused — *too willing*, she'd said, with thirty years of guilt packed into the words. Desperation didn't count. Bargains struck from fear didn't count. The portrait — Hollowell — wanted something specific. A face given freely. A sitting, not a sacrifice. Someone who walked to the easel with open eyes and said *yes* and meant it.

Mara's mirror had confirmed it weeks ago, trading secrets with her dead mother's reflection: *The painter needs a face that consents.*

And tonight — Emrys's blood went cold as she remembered — tonight the radio had broadcast tomorrow's show early, and the dead caller had read the date and a single name. *Theo.* The boy. Ten years old, paint under his fingernails, the one who talked to mirrors and hoped the new caretaker would see what he saw. The portrait's sixth figure had a complete face now, and it wasn't Wren's anymore, and the countdown had a name attached.

A child couldn't consent. Not truly. Not in any way that would satisfy a thing that had waited a hundred years for the real thing. Which meant either the rule didn't apply to children — a possibility Emrys refused to finish thinking — or the portrait would find a way to manufacture consent, to wear down a ten-year-old the way water wears stone, night after night, mirror after mirror, until the boy walked to the easel on his own feet and said yes in his own voice.

Unless someone got there first.

Emrys stared at the letter, at the last legible line, and felt the plan assemble itself in her chest with the slow, inexorable click of a lock finding its key.

*When it is complete, I will be complete.*

Hollowell wanted completion. That was the whole engine of it — fifty years of self-portraits, a death mid-stroke, a century of borrowed faces. He wasn't a monster feeding on the living for appetite's sake. He was an artist with one unfinished masterpiece and an eternity to correct the error, and he would take any face, any likeness, any completion he could get.

So give him one he can't refuse.

Not a child's face. Not a frightened bargain. A consenting adult, walking to the canvas freely, offering the one thing he'd been starving for — and offering it in a way that would bind him, close the trap around *him*, finish him inside his own unfinished self-portrait the way he'd finished dozens of others inside the family painting. Emrys didn't know the mechanics yet. She suspected no one did; Cordelia's grandmother's survival had been luck, not method. But the principle was there in the letter, plain as paint: the portrait took what was given. If what was given was a face freely offered, fully consented, held out like a key —

— then maybe the door swung both ways.

She knew what it would cost. She was not naive; she had spent three weeks in that house and she had read the letter four times and she had watched Wren's face surface in wet canvas. Sitting for that portrait might mean becoming part of it. Might mean the mirrors showing her image finishing somewhere she wasn't. Might mean everything. Cordelia had offered in 1994 and been spared by refusal; there was no guarantee of refusal this time, and no guarantee of survival if it accepted.

And there was Silas.

She thought of him in the greenhouse, soil on his hands, telling her about the voice on the radio the night he almost walked into the lake — his wife's voice, calling him gently toward the water, and how he'd stood at the shore with his shoes already off. She thought of the wordless agreement they'd made among the tomato plants: *keep each other here.* She thought of the way he said her name now, like it was a thing worth saying.

If she did this, she might break that agreement in the worst possible way. If she did nothing, the portrait would take Theo, and she would break it anyway, along with everything else.

The archive clock ticked past four. Somewhere upstairs, a librarian pushed a cart across creaking floorboards, and the sound came to Emrys muffled, distant, like a broadcast from another station, another life.

She packed her notebook, returned the folders to Mrs. Pell, and paused at the desk. The old woman looked at her over her glasses with those eyes that had seen decades of researchers come and go.

"You found what you were looking for," Mrs. Pell said. It wasn't a question.

"I found what he was looking for," Emrys said. "I'm just deciding whether to give it to him."

Mrs. Pell studied her for a moment. Then she reached beneath the desk and produced a small card, yellowed with age, and slid it across. On it, in faded pencil, was an address Emrys already knew, and beneath the address, a single line in a shaky hand: *My grandmother's copy of the inquest letter has a postscript the county never filed. Ask Cordelia Aldridge why she never let anyone read it.*

Emrys turned the card over. Blank. She looked up, but Mrs. Pell was already stamping returns, already elsewhere, as if the conversation had been a courtesy paid and concluded.

Outside, October had gone gold and low, the sun dropping fast behind the hills, and Hollowell House lay six miles north with its mirrors ticking a second behind and its portrait waiting with a brush that was never dry. Emrys got in her car and gripped the wheel and did not start the engine for a long minute.

Then she drove north, rehearsing in her head, over and over, the sentence she would have to say to Silas Aldridge before midnight — the sentence that would either save a ten-year-old boy or cost her everything she'd found in that house:

*I know how to end it. And I'm the one who has to sit for the portrait.*
