## 22. Sign-Off

The studio smelled of fresh paint and old wood, which Emrys decided was the only honest smell a room could have. Three weeks ago the space had been Cordelia's sewing room, all dust sheets and mothballs and a window that looked out over the east lawn where the greenhouse stood whole again, its glass panes replaced one by one on Saturday afternoons by a man who whistled while he worked. Now it held a mixing board Silas had bought secondhand from a station going dark in Ashford, two microphones with foam windscreens still smelling faintly of the factory, and a hand-lettered sign Theo had made in shop class: *THE DEAD LINE — LIVE FROM HOLLOWELL HOUSE.*

Emrys had argued against the name. She'd lost the argument in under a minute.

"Test, test." She leaned to the mic, her voice coming back through the monitors warm and slightly too loud. "Levels look good?"

"Good from here," Mara said from the doorway, arms crossed, wearing the expression she'd worn for three weeks straight — the one that said she was supervising, not helping, and would deny enjoying either.

Beyond her, in the hall, Cordelia sat in the good chair they'd carried up from the parlor, knitting something enormous and gray that refused to declare itself as scarf or blanket. Theo lay on the floor with his headphones half off, sketching. He'd filled three notebooks since the night in the gallery. Mostly he drew the house. Sometimes he drew the portrait — always ruined, always blank, always with a small figure standing in front of it holding another figure's hand. He never explained the drawings. Nobody asked him to.

The transmitter hummed. The signal light glowed amber, then green.

"We're live," Mara said quietly, as if announcing something sacred, and maybe she was.

Emrys settled into the chair, pressed the fader, and let the breath move through her before she spoke.

"Good evening. It's eleven-oh-six on a Thursday night in late November, and this is the last broadcast of *The Dead Line*."

She'd rehearsed the opening seventeen times. It came out nothing like any rehearsal — softer, rougher at the edges, true.

"For nineteen years I've sat behind a microphone and taken calls from strangers in the dark. Insomniacs. Night-shift workers. Grieving people who couldn't say the names out loud anywhere else. People who wanted to believe someone was listening, and people who already knew no one was, and called anyway." She glanced at the window, where her own reflection looked back at her exactly when she moved — neither ahead nor behind, just present, just now. "I built a career on being the skeptic in the room. The voice that said: it's grief talking, it's coincidence, it's the mind reaching for comfort in the dark. And then, one October week, the dark reached back."

In the hallway, Cordelia's needles went still.

"So before I take the phones tonight — and I will take them, every call, until the hour runs out — I owe you all an accounting. Not the exposé I promised myself I'd deliver. There won't be one. Some things you don't put on tape; you put them in a house, and you keep the house."

She thought of the gallery, how it looked now. They'd debated for days what to do with the wall where the portrait had hung. In the end, Theo solved it: he painted over the space himself, an entire mural of the lake at dawn, done in colors that didn't exist in 1911 or any other year anyone had drowned. In the corner, tiny, almost hidden: six figures on a dock, none of them finished, all of them together.

"The short version," Emrys said, "is that something in this house was counting people. It counted for a long time. It doesn't count anymore. And the reason it doesn't is a twelve-year-old boy who broke every rule we thought were rules, because the only rule that mattered turned out to be the one nobody wrote down: you don't get claimed if somebody claims you first."

Theo, without looking up from his sketchbook, gave her a thumbs-up.

"There are families listening tonight who lost people in October. I know some of your names. I heard some of your voices — or voices that wore your faces like borrowed coats. I can't give you back what was taken. But I can tell you what I learned on my knees on a gallery floor with paint burning up my arms: the dead aren't waiting to be finished. That was the lie. That was always the lie. They're waiting to be *let go.*"

Her hands were steady on the board. Three weeks ago they hadn't been. Three weeks ago she'd woken every night at 3 a.m. with the ghost-sensation of cold water climbing her forearms, and Silas had learned — without ever being told — to leave a cup of tea on the nightstand outside her door, and to knock once, softly, so she'd know the house was full of the living.

The phone line blinked.

Emrys looked at it for a long moment. Then she took the call.

"Dead Line, go ahead."

Static first — but ordinary static, the honest kind, the kind that was just distance and atmosphere and nothing else. Then a voice. Old. A woman's voice, papery and unhurried, like someone speaking from the far end of a long hallway with the windows open.

"Is this her? Is this the radio lady?"

"It's me. Who's calling tonight?"

"My name was Edith Marsh. I lived on Corben Street forty-one years. I died on the ninth of October." A pause, gentle as a hand laid on a shoulder. "You read my name off the list. You said it right. Most people got it wrong for seventy years — Marsh, not Marshall. You said it right."

Emrys closed her eyes. Behind her eyelids she could see the playback session in the gallery, the seventh voice beneath the family's testimony, patient as tide, reciting names. She had listened to that recording until she knew the list by heart. She had never expected the list to call *her.*

"I remember you, Edith," she said. "Ninth of October. I'm sorry it took me so long to say it properly."

"You said it right," the voice repeated, satisfied, and there was something underneath the words now, something loosening, like ice going out of a river in March. "That's why I called. Not to haunt anybody. Just — when you said it right, something set down. Something heavy I'd been carrying since the ninth. I've been walking around with it and I couldn't put it down until somebody said my name like I was still a person."

"You are," Emrys said. "You were. You are."

Mara had come fully into the room now. Theo sat up, headphones around his neck. Even Cordelia had abandoned the knitting and stood in the doorway, one hand pressed flat against the frame, watching the phone line's little red light pulse like a slow heartbeat.

"I hear the boy fixed it," Edith said. "The painter man. My mother used to tell stories about Hollowell House. Said you could hear it counting if you walked past on a fog night. One, two, three, like stones dropped down a well." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Well. Let him count his own face forever, I say. Fair's fair."

"Fair's fair," Emrys agreed, and meant it with her whole chest.

"I don't have long. I can feel the — oh, what do you call it. The signal going soft. Like a radio drifting off station." The voice was quieter now, further away, but not frightened. That was the thing Emrys would carry out of this night and into every night after: it wasn't frightened. "I wanted to say thank you. To you, and to the family that took you in. We heard you, all of us, that night in the gallery. When you gave your face. We felt the pull stop pulling. Like a door closing gently instead of slamming. First quiet in years, some of us. First quiet in decades."

Emrys found she couldn't speak for a moment. She let the silence sit on the air, because silence, she had finally learned, was not the enemy. Silence was where the living and the dead could stand in the same room without hurting each other.

Then she leaned in close to the microphone, and said:

"To everyone who called — thank you. You can rest now."

The static changed. It didn't fade so much as *exhale* — a long, slow release of everything it had been carrying, dissolving into the plain white noise of an empty frequency. And in the last second before the line went clean, Edith Marsh breathed one word, soft as snowfall:

"*Thanks.*"

Click. Dial tone. Then nothing at all — the good nothing, the nothing that was just a phone line doing its ordinary job in an ordinary world.

The ON AIR light burned green. Emrys sat with her hand resting on the fader and let herself feel it: the weight leaving. Not all of it — she suspected some weights were permanent tenants — but enough. Enough to breathe all the way down.

"That was Edith Marsh," she said to the microphone, to the county, to whoever was awake and listening in kitchens and trucks and hospital corridors. "Corben Street. Forty-one years. Say her name tonight, if you're the kind of person who says names. It matters more than I ever believed it did."

She looked at the family. Silas had appeared behind Cordelia at some point, drawn down the hall by the sound of his own house breathing differently, and stood now with his arm around his sister-in-law's shoulders. Mara had wiped her eyes with the aggressive efficiency of a girl who would deny crying under oath. Theo had turned to a fresh page in his notebook and was drawing — she could see it from here — a telephone, with a small bright star where the receiver met the ear.

"One more piece of business," Emrys said, "and then the phones are open till midnight, and after that this frequency goes back to the jazz programming, God help us all." A ripple of laughter from the hallway — actual laughter, in this house, in this season. "As of tonight, *The Dead Line* is signing off. Permanently. Not because the story's over. Because the story was never mine to keep telling. It belonged to the callers. All of them. The living ones and—" she smiled, and her voice caught only a little, "—the ones who taught me the difference between a haunting and a goodbye.

"But the studio stays. Right here, in this room, in this house. Starting next month, it belongs to the kids. Mara's going to run a show about local history — the real kind, the kind with names said properly. Theo's doing a midnight music hour, and I'm told the playlist is 'mostly normal.' Cordelia has threatened to do a gardening segment and I, for one, am terrified."

"I'm not threatening," Cordelia called from the doorway. "I'm promising. Someone has to teach this generation about soil."

"And me?" Emrys turned her chair toward the window, toward the reflection that kept perfect time with her now, and found she was smiling at it. "I'll be around. Fixing the greenhouse gutters. Learning to bake things that don't qualify as crimes. Sitting for a portrait, eventually — a real one, oil on canvas, commissioned by unanimous family vote, and yes, Theo gets final approval on whether I'm allowed to smile in it."

"He says no smiling," Mara reported. "He says it's 'artistically dishonest.'"

"He's not wrong," Emrys admitted. "But I'm negotiating."

She let the moment run a few seconds longer — the hum of the board, the rain starting soft against the glass, the four people in the doorway who had become, without anyone signing anything, her people — and then she opened the phone lines.

"Lines are open, Hollowell County. Tell me your names. Say them right. I'm listening."

The calls came the way calls should come: truckers on the interstate reporting black ice near the mill road; a nurse on nights who wanted to dedicate a song to her whole ward; a farmer asking, genuinely, whether Cordelia's gardening segment would cover garlic, because his had failed three years running and his wife was beginning to talk. Ordinary voices. Living voices. The frequency full to its edges with the warm, mundane, miraculous noise of people who were all, at least for tonight, exactly where they were supposed to be.

At 11:58, the last caller of the night came through.

No introduction. No request. Just breathing — slow, even, peaceful — and beneath it, so faint Emrys had to turn the monitor gain up to be sure, the ghost of a melody. A lullaby. Wren's lullaby, the one their mother had sung, the one Wren had hummed into a payphone-static October night: *it's counting us, Emmy, don't let it finish.*

It was finished. It was so finished.

"Wren?" Emrys whispered, though she didn't key the mic. Some conversations weren't for broadcast.

The breathing continued a moment longer. And then — not words, never words, Wren had never needed words between them, not since they were girls sharing a room and a flashlight and every secret — a single note of the lullaby, clear as a struck bell, rising through the static like a bubble through dark water.

*Goodbye, Emmy. Goodbye, goodbye.*

The line clicked. The dial tone sang once, sweetly, and faded.

Emrys sat very still. Rain traced silver paths down the window. In the glass, her reflection watched her with her mother's patience and her sister's crooked half-smile — no, she corrected herself, blinking — with her own crooked half-smile, hers alone, earned.

She keyed the mic one final time.

"That's the hour. From all of us at Hollowell House — sleep well, county. Lock your doors against weather, not against anything else. The mirrors in this house run on time now."

She reached for the fader. Paused. Looked at the family gathered in the doorway, at Theo already holding up his sketchbook to show her the drawing: the six figures on the dock, and now, added since she'd last looked, a seventh — small, waving, halfway to becoming a bird.

"And if anyone calls," Emrys said softly, "tell them the line's open. It'll always be open."

She slid the fader down. The ON AIR light dimmed to amber, then to nothing.

Silence — the good kind, the kind with rain in it, the kind with a family in it.

Emrys took off her headphones, hung them on the stand, and went home.
