## 15. Midnight in the Churchyard

The fire died at half past eleven, and it did not die like fires die.

Hazel had fed it everything — the old chair from the back bedroom, the stack of *Countryman* magazines in the loft, a kitchen chair broken down with the poker. The flames took each offering greedily and then, between one breath and the next, simply stopped. Not guttered. Stopped. The logs sat blackened but unconsumed, radiating a cold that was somehow worse than the cold of the room itself, as if the fire had been asked a question it refused to answer.

The oil lamp lasted another twenty minutes. Then its flame shrank to a blue bead no bigger than a currant, hung there trembling, and went out without smoke.

In the dark, Theo's voice came up through the floorboards, patient as water finding a crack.

"You'll be cold soon, Hazel-bird."

They had not lit a candle. They were not going to light a candle. They sat wrapped in their grandmother's quilt on the kitchen floor with their back against the Aga, which was stone dead too, its iron giving off nothing, and they thought about Mrs. Aldous's face when she'd finally spoken that afternoon — the fury in her, ninety years of fury banked down to embers — and they thought about nine names that did not exist anywhere except, perhaps, in the mouth of the thing walking through their house.

The churchyard was four hundred yards away. Down the lane, past the frozen duck pond, through the lych-gate that hadn't closed properly since 1987. Hazel knew this because they had walked it that morning in daylight, taking photographs of weathered headstones for a project they had already begun to understand would never be published.

Four hundred yards. In this dark. Past him.

But the alternative was sitting in a house where fire had forgotten how to burn, listening to a voice that knew their childhood nickname, waiting for dawn that might not come, because the sun had stalled at the horizon six hours ago like a coin balanced on a table's edge.

Hazel stood up.

* * *

The lane was luminous. That was the first thing — not dark, exactly, but lit from below, snowlight and frostlight and something else, a pale diffusion with no source, so that every rut and gatepost cast two shadows, one to the left and one to the right, and neither shadow was Hazel's own shape. They kept their eyes on the ground and walked. Their breath came out in flags. Behind them, the cottage windows watched, and ahead the church tower rose black against a sky that had no stars in it, only a smooth grey pressure like the inside of a shell.

At the duck pond the ice sang — a long, high note, rising, the sound ice makes when it is expanding, when it is growing, and Hazel did not look at the pond but they heard, beneath the singing, small footsteps keeping pace along the far bank. Light ones. A child's weight on frozen ground.

"Hazel-bird," the footsteps said, in Theo's voice, though Theo was — Hazel was almost sure — still behind them, still in the house, still standing in the kitchen doorway where he had stood all evening, hands at his sides, waiting the way he always waited. "You're going the right way. I'm glad you're going the right way."

Hazel walked faster.

The lych-gate leaned open. Inside the churchyard the snow lay unbroken — no footprints, no animal tracks, nothing, a field of white so even it looked poured rather than fallen — and the headstones rose out of it at their drunken angles, fifty of them, eighty, a century and a half of Wrenlow's dead leaning together like conspirators at a funeral. The church itself was shuttered. Its east window held a faint stain of moonless light, some residual glow in the glass, and it made the whole building look like a lantern with the wick pinched out.

Hazel stopped just inside the gate and counted.

They didn't know why they counted. Later they would understand: some part of them had already done the arithmetic, had read the parish registers until three in the morning with numb fingers, had found the pattern Mrs. Aldous's confession had pointed to like an arrow. Nine children across two centuries. Nine entries in the burial register left blank. Nine lines in the grange book marked only with a date and the words *gone to relatives*, in winter, always in winter, always within a year of a harvest so good the vicar recorded his thanks in the margin.

Nine children who had gone to relatives and never arrived.

And here, in the oldest corner of the churchyard, where the stones had worn down to featureless fingers of rock, there was a patch of ground — Hazel saw it now, saw it clearly, the way you see a thing you have been refusing to see — where no grass grew. Where the snow lay thinner, melted through from beneath, exposing a rectangle of dark earth roughly the size of a grave. Roughly the size of nine graves, packed close, shoulder to shoulder, the way children sleep when they are frightened of the dark and crowd into one bed.

"Oh," Hazel said aloud. Their voice fell flat in the dead air. "Oh, you're buried here. You've been here the whole time."

"They're not buried," said Theo.

He was standing among the worn stones. Hazel had not seen him arrive; there had been no footprints on the path, and now there were none around him either, just the boy-shaped absence of snow, as though the ground declined to record him. He looked exactly as he had looked on the porch, exactly as he had looked twenty years ago, exactly as he had looked at the kitchen table describing the smell of Hazel's grandmother's cardigan: a child of perhaps eight, in clothes out of no decade at all, pale as paper, with eyes the colour of deep ice and an expression of such unguarded tenderness that Hazel's chest hurt looking at him.

He was crouched by the bare patch of earth, one hand resting on it lightly, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping dog.

"They're not buried," he said again. "Buried is what happens to people who are finished. They're kept."

"Kept." Hazel's breath came out ragged. "Theo—"

"I knew you'd come." He looked up, and his face opened like a door. "I hoped you'd come. Nobody comes here, Hazel-bird. Nobody has come here in a hundred years. They walk past the gate and they look at the nice stones with the names on them, the ones with the verses about peace, and they never once look over here." He patted the dark earth gently. "Because there's nothing to see. That's the trick of it. That's what forgetting looks like when it's done properly. It looks like nothing at all."

Hazel should have run. Every animal instinct said run; the churchyard was a trap shaped like a question, and they had walked straight into its centre. But their feet wouldn't move, because Theo's hand was on that soil and his voice was doing something to the air, filling it the way a tuning fork fills a room, and underneath the terror there was the other thing, the thing that had been there since the porch, since the frost footprints, since the voice saying their childhood nickname: the longing. God help them, the longing.

"Tell me their names," Hazel said.

It was the wrong thing to say or the right thing; they couldn't tell which. Theo's whole body changed. He rose off his heels like something surfacing, and his face — his terrible, beautiful, unchanged face — went bright.

"You want to hear?"

"Yes."

"No one has ever wanted to hear." He turned back to the graves and knelt again, both knees in the thin snow, and laid his palm flat on the frozen earth, and began, in a voice pitched low and clear, the way you speak to someone sleeping:

"Edith Marlowe. Called Ede. She liked to make daisy chains in summer and dared me to eat a snail and laughed until she cried when I did. She was seven. She was afraid of thunder and she held my hand through every storm, and I held hers." His thumb moved on the soil, a slow stroke. "She went first. 1811. The harvest before her was the finest anyone could remember. Barley to the eaves."

Hazel's throat closed.

"Tobias Fenwick. Toby. He collected things — buttons, birds' eggs, a wasp's nest he swore was empty and wasn't. He was nine and he wanted to be a sailor and he asked me once if the sea was real, because he'd never seen it." Theo's voice caught — actually caught, actually broke, and Hazel felt the break go through them like a fishhook. "I told him it was real. I told him it was bigger than anything. He was so happy. 1849. Wheat heavy enough to bend the carts."

He named them all. He went down the row of them the way you'd go down a row of candles, touching each one alight: Alice Pym, who was five and couldn't say her r's and called him Feo. Samuel Aldous — and here Hazel's blood went to ice water — *Aldous*, 1903, Mrs. Aldous's uncle or cousin or kin of some kind, a boy who whittled boats from pine offcuts. The Verrall twins, Constance and Mary, eight years old, inseparable, taken together in 1921 because, Theo said softly, "they wouldn't let me take just one, they hid together under the bed, and I waited, and I waited, and in the end they came out holding hands and asked if they could stay together, and I said yes, I promised, and I keep my promises, Hazel-bird, whatever they say about me, I keep my promises."

Ronald Quill. 1940. He'd been evacuated-adjacent, sent to Wrenlow from London, and he'd been homesick for a city he barely remembered, and Theo had been the only one who never told him to stop crying.

And last, at the near edge of the bare earth, alone, as if the others had left room: a smaller patch, a shallower one.

"Margaret Aldous," Theo said. "Peggy. She was six." He was quiet a moment. When he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere Hazel had never heard it go — down past sadness into something older than sadness, something geological. "Her sister sang the song short ever after. Did you know that? One verse instead of two. Starving me. And everyone called it kindness. Everyone called it protection." His hand flattened on the smallest grave. "Nobody asked what it was like for Peggy, being remembered by no one. Being paid for and erased. Being the price and then not even being allowed to be the memory."

The wind moved through the churchyard then — the first wind in hours — and it carried the smell of woodsmoke and, impossibly, of barley, ripe barley, harvest-smell in the teeth of midwinter, and Hazel understood suddenly that this was the weapon. This was how it worked. Not claws, not cold, not any of the apparatus of nightmare. Grief. Grief deployed with perfect precision, grief administered by a child's voice naming children lovingly, grief that made you kneel down in the snow beside him and put your own hand on the earth and mean it.

Hazel's knees were bending. Hazel could feel them bending.

"He keeps them," whispered a voice in their memory — Mrs. Aldous, that afternoon, gripping their wrist hard enough to bruise. "That's what nobody understands. He doesn't eat them. He *keeps* them. And being kept by that thing for two hundred years is worse than any eating, girl, worse than any eating there ever was—"

"Hazel-bird." Theo was looking up at them now, kneeling in the snow, and his ice-coloured eyes were wet. Actually wet. Two hundred years old and wet-eyed, and the tears didn't fall, they just sat there shining, refusals. "You did the sums, didn't you? I can always tell. You've got the look. The look people get when they finally count." He smiled, and the smile was devastating, and it was also, Hazel saw now, at the very bottom of it, underneath the tenderness, hungry. "Nine. Two centuries. And every single one of them mine, every single one loved, every single one *kept* — and the village got its harvests, and the village got its warm winters, and the village slept soundly, and then they sang me short and called it virtue." He rose. He was small; he had always been small; he had been small on the porch twenty years ago and he was small now and somehow he filled the entire churchyard, filled the sky, filled the space inside Hazel's ribs. "So here is what I want you to carry back across that lane, folklorist. Here is your missing verse."

He opened his mouth.

And from the direction of the cottage, faint but unmistakable, cutting across the frozen fields like a blade — a human voice began to sing.

Two verses. Rising and falling. Old words in an old tune, sung slightly sharp, sung by someone old, sung *twice*.

Mrs. Aldous was singing the Inviting.
