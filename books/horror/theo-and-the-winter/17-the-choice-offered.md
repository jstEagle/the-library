## 17. The Choice Offered

The fire had gone down to a red argument with itself, and Theo sat across from it the way he always had — cross-legged on the hearthstone, hands folded in his lap like a child at assembly. The flames did not cast him any shadow. I had noticed that hours ago and filed it away with everything else I couldn't afford to think about yet.

Outside, the dark pressed against every window like something listening.

"You've been counting," Theo said.

"I've been working." My voice came out rougher than I intended. I'd been awake for — I didn't actually know how long. The clock on the mantel had stopped at 11:47 sometime around what should have been midnight and hadn't moved since, and neither had the frost on the glass, and neither, I suspected, had I.

"You count when you're frightened," he said. "You counted the stairs at Mrs. Aldous's house when you were six. Fourteen, and the seventh one creaked. You counted the seconds between lightning and thunder during the storm that knocked the beech down. You counted the days until your mother came home from the hospital, and you stopped at nine because nine was when I arrived and I made the waiting bearable."

"Stop."

"That's the first thing you have to decide," he said gently. "Whether you want me to stop. Because after tonight, no one will ever be able to make me stop again. One way or the other."

I looked at him. Twenty years, and he hadn't changed — the same cowlick, the same gap in his teeth, the same wool coat with the buttons done up wrong, top to bottom, the way children do them. That was the detail that had undone me as a kid, I realized now. Not his face. The buttons. No adult would ever dress him; he dressed himself, badly, forever, and some part of my nine-year-old heart had understood that this meant he belonged to no one and needed someone desperately.

He needed someone still. That was the whole horror of it. He needed someone every generation, and the village had solved the problem the way villages solve everything — by burying it and singing over the grave.

"The terms," I said. "Mrs. Aldous said there were terms. She wouldn't say what they were. She said *you* would say."

Theo unfolded himself and stood. He crossed to the window where the frost had grown inward overnight, ferns of ice reaching from the corners toward the center of the glass, and stood looking out at nothing — or at everything, the whole held breath of Wrenlow behind its shutters.

"The old bargain was simple," he said. "A song sung twice opens the door. A child remembers me until they're ten. Then the village sings me away, and the child forgets, and the forgetting is the payment. Their memory of me goes into the ground with the winter, and I go with it, and the fields come up kind in spring." He turned. "Do you understand what the payment actually is, Hazel? It isn't the child. It's never been the child. It's the remembering. Memory is the only food I have. Every year they fed me one childhood and then took it back."

"And the nine," I said. My throat was dry. "The nine graves in the churchyard."

The temperature in the room dropped by a degree I could feel in my teeth.

"The nine were not the bargain," Theo said. "The nine were theft. Years when the village got greedy — sang the Inviting twice but refused the forgetting, kept their children's memories, kept me hungry through the whole turning of the year. Hunger makes anything cruel, Hazel. Even me." He said it without self-pity, which was worse. He said it the way you'd read a weather report for a storm that had already drowned the town. "I took what I was owed. They wrote *went to relatives* in the parish book and put up no stones, because stones have names on them, and names are a kind of remembering too."

I thought of the grange book. *The song sung twice, the door held open, the child given.* Great-grandmother Edith's handwriting, steady as a ledger. I thought of Mrs. Aldous's twin, 1954, and the old woman's fifty years of singing the carol short on purpose, starving him, dooming her neighbors' schedules to keep faith with a dead girl. Everyone in this village had blood on their hands and most of them had never even learned which ancestor had spilled it.

"So what are the new terms?" I asked. "You said inverted."

Theo came back to the fire. He sat down across from me, and for the first time all night — all twenty years — he looked tired. Not monstrous. Tired, the way only something very old can look tired, weariness in the bones of the world.

"Inverted," he agreed. "Two doors, Hazel. Same doorframe. You choose which side of it you stand on."

He raised one hand, and the firelight bent toward it, and I swear the room leaned in.

"The first door: remember. Take me fully — not the ten years, not the fragments, all of it. Every game, every winter, every word we ever spoke, restored and permanent. And not just yours." His eyes — I had never been able to decide what color they were, and I understood now that they were whatever color the rememberer needed — held mine. "Take the nine as well. Their names, their voices, their games, their last nights. Everything the village buried. Carry it. All of it, in your head, for the rest of your life, and live. Live long, live whole in body, walk out of here at sunrise with every memory that was ever stolen from this parish sitting behind your eyes like a second skull."

"And the cost?"

"You carry it," he said simply. "That's the whole cost. Nine lifetimes of remembering, pressed into one. Most minds are houses, Hazel. This would make yours a churchyard. You would know the weight of every stone."

I wrapped my hands around the cold teacup I'd been holding for hours without drinking. My fingers didn't want to work properly. Cold does that, past a certain point — it stops being a sensation and becomes an opinion your body has about the world.

"And the other door?"

"Forget." The word came out of him flat and careful, like setting down something breakable. "Not just me. Everything. Your childhood entire — the cottage, your grandmother's kitchen, the smell of the stove, the beech tree, the storm, the fourteen stairs. All of it goes. You'll wake at sunrise a stranger to yourself before the age of — well. Before me. You'll have documents, photographs, a family who loves you and whom you'll have to learn like coursework. But you'll walk away whole. Light. Free. The village keeps its silence, the graves stay nameless, and you get to be a person with a future instead of an archive with legs."

"That's not whole," I said. "That's amputation."

"It's mercy," Theo said. "It's the only mercy I have left to give, and I'm giving it to you because you're the first one in two hundred years who asked what I wanted instead of what the village owed." He smiled, and it was his old smile, the one from the porch, the one that had made nine-year-old me feel chosen. "Don't mistake it for kindness. I don't want you to take it. But I want you to know it exists, because a choice isn't real unless both doors open."

I set the cup down. It clicked against the table too loudly in the stopped time.

"Why?" I asked. "Why those two? Why not just — take me? You took nine children. What's one folklorist?"

Something moved behind his face then, quick and wounded, and I understood I had said the cruelest thing possible in this room.

"Because I never wanted to take anyone," he said quietly. "I wanted to be kept. There's a difference, and the difference is two hundred years of graves." He looked into the fire. "The nine weren't a choice I offered. They were a bill I collected. You're the first person I've ever asked. Do you understand what that means to me? Two hundred years, and tonight is the first night I've been able to say *choose*, instead of *pay*."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled — or stepped. I chose not to check which.

I tried to do the arithmetic. That's what I do; it's the only tool I've ever had. I sat there with my useless scholar's mind and tried to weigh a self against a graveyard.

Door one: I keep myself, all of myself, including the parts that have been lying to me since I was nine — including the truth about that last winter, which I could feel waiting behind a locked door inside my own head, patient as Theo on a porch. In exchange I become the vessel for nine erased children. I would know their favorite games. I would know how each of them spent their final night, and whether they were afraid, and whether they called for mothers who never learned they needed calling for. I would know it all, every day, forever, and I would be the only person alive who did. That's not a life. That's a job with no wages and no retirement.

Door two: I lose everything before roughly the age of ten. My grandmother's hands kneading dough. The exact pitch of the wind off the fells. Whatever Theo had been to me — and God help me, some traitor corner of my heart still insisted he had been good, that the loneliness of that year would have eaten me alive without him. I'd trade all of it for a clean slate and a sunrise. People rebuild. Amputees learn to walk again. That's practically the human specialty.

But.

But the village would win. That was the thing I kept circling back to, the splinter under the arithmetic. Door two wasn't escape; it was the bargain performed one final time, with me as the child given. Forget the friend, sing the song, let the graves stay nameless, let Mrs. Aldous's sister stay *went to relatives in the winter*. Two hundred years of Wrenlow nodding along, and I'd be the last verse of it. They wouldn't even have to lift a finger. I'd erase myself, politely, and call it survival.

And door one — door one was martyrdom wearing my own face. Carry the dead so the living can keep pretending. Become the archive so the village can stay a lie. Also a version of their bargain, really. Also me doing the village's remembering for it, forever, unpaid.

Both doors were theirs. Both doors had been built by them.

"There's a third option," I said.

Theo went very still. Stillness, I was learning, was his tell. When the world rearranged itself for him, things went quiet first.

"No," he said. "There isn't. I've had two hundred years to look, Hazel. There are two doors."

"Then you haven't been looking hard enough." I stood up. My knees cracked; I'd been sitting so long the cold had gotten into the joints. "You said the bargain runs on a song sung twice. Sung by the village, renewed by the village, broken by the village. Every term of this thing has *Wrenlow's* fingerprints on it. So why does the choice have to be mine alone?"

"Hazel—"

"And why," I went on, my voice climbing, "does the choice have to happen *now*, in this kitchen, in the dark, with a stopped clock and a dying fire, which — forgive me — is exactly how every bad bargain in every ballad I've ever collected gets struck? Midnight, a fire, a fork in the road. I'm a folklorist, Theo. I know how these stories go. The offer always comes at the lowest ebb, when you're too tired to do the math."

For a long moment he said nothing. The frost on the windows creaked, growing.

"You're stalling," he said at last, and there was something almost admiring in it.

"I'm thinking," I said. "Which is more than this village has ever done."

"You're afraid of what you'll find if you go back to that last winter." He said it softly, and it landed like a knife between ribs, because it was true. "You want a third door because the first two require you to already know what happened when you were nine. What *you* did. What you sang, and when, and why I left mid-game." He rose too, and up close he smelled — this undid me — he smelled exactly the same, woodsmoke and snow and something sweet underneath, like the inside of an old piano. "You can't choose a door, Hazel, until you remember what's behind you."

The clock ticked.

One tick. The first in hours. 11:48.

Time, pooling. Coming back like blood into a numb limb, painful, inevitable.

"What's happening?" I whispered.

"The longest hour," Theo said, and his face was full of something I hadn't seen on it before — pity, maybe, or dread, or the terrible tenderness of someone about to show you a wound you gave them. "You want your third door? Fine. Here's where third doors come from. Behind you, Hazel. Come and see what you did."

The fire guttered. The room tilted like a boat.

And the last thing I saw before the kitchen dissolved was the wool coat, buttons done up wrong, and Theo's hand extended — not grabbing, never grabbing, he had never once grabbed —

waiting, as he had always waited, to be taken.
