## 19. Sung Twice

The longest hour ended the way a fever breaks — all at once, and leaving you colder than before.

Hazel knelt in the churchyard snow with Theo's voice still hanging in the air around them, nine names spoken like rosary beads. The east was still black. Somewhere behind them, the cottage chimney had gone dark, the fire drowned by whatever pooled time did to flame. Mrs. Aldous stood at the lychgate in her nightclothes and her dead sister's coat, and she was looking at Hazel with an expression Hazel had never seen on a living face: hope so old it had fossilized into something like terror.

"You can't," she said. "Whatever you're thinking, child, you can't."

"I'm not a child," Hazel said. "That's rather the point."

The words came out steadier than they felt. Inside, everything was arithmetic — the impossible kind Theo had offered them, remember-everything or forget-everything, nine lives weighed against one childhood. Hazel had spent the whole long hour turning it over and finding the flaw in both terms, the same flaw: either way, the village walked free. Either way, Wrenlow got to keep its bargain without ever having to look at what the bargain was.

There was a third line in the ledger. There always had been. It just required someone willing to sign it.

"The grange book," Hazel said, standing. Their knees cracked; the cold had gotten into them somewhere during the long hour, or maybe they'd been cold since they were nine. "1898. *The song sung twice, the door held open, the child given.* That's how it works, isn't it? Not a village contract. A household one. Whoever sings it owns what it calls."

Mrs. Aldous's mouth worked. "It's not ownership," she whispered. "It's inheritance. You don't want to inherit that."

"Great-grandmother Edith signed for this family." Hazel's breath came out in a white flag of steam. "The debt runs through Yarrow blood. I checked the parish rolls while you were pretending to be deaf. Every singer who ever truncated the verse — Aldous, Pym, Cattermole, all of them — they were singing *around* the debt, not through it. But the Inviting has never once been sung whole in this village. Not in a hundred and twenty-six years. They've been starving you," Hazel said, turning to Theo, "on purpose. And letting children pay the arrears."

Theo stood among the nine unmarked graves with his hands at his sides, and for the first time since the porch, he looked uncertain. It was such a small thing — a tilt of the head, a fractional widening of those winter-colored eyes — but it undid Hazel more than any of the horror had. He had been patient for two centuries. He had never once been unsure.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"What no one here has had the nerve to do."

* * *

They walked back through the village together, and the village watched.

Doors Hazel had seen barred now stood open a hand's width, faces in the gaps — old Pym with his shotgun held wrong, the Cattermole sisters pressed together like one person wearing two coats, young Tam Yarrow from the farm, who couldn't have been more than thirty and who had the look of a man watching his own funeral procession go by. No one spoke. The snow squeaked underfoot. Above them the sky hung wrong, starless and low, the stalled solstice pressing down like a lid.

At the well, Mrs. Aldous caught Hazel's sleeve. Her grip was astonishingly strong for ninety-one.

"He'll take you apart," she said. "Not cruelly. That's the trap of him. He'll wear your memories like coats until you can't tell which one is yours. I watched him do it to my sister over one winter and she smiled the whole time—"

"I know what he does." Hazel gently freed their arm. "I've been listening to him for three days. But you've all been doing something worse for a hundred years. You've been making children carry it and then erasing the carrying." They looked down the street toward the cottage, where frost climbed the windows in patterns like handwriting. "He asked me to choose between remembering and forgetting. Both choices leave the village clean. I'm done keeping this place clean."

"Hazel—"

"It's sung twice," Hazel said. "Both verses. Twice through. By an heir who knows what they're signing." They managed something that was almost a smile. "You taught me the first verse when I was six, Mrs. Aldous. Before you stopped yourself. You've been an accessory this whole time too."

The old woman went grey. She didn't deny it. She stepped back from the road as if from deep water, and she made, of all things, the old gesture — the one villagers made when passing the churchyard at dusk, thumb pressed to the sternum, warding something older than the church behind her.

* * *

The cottage door was open when Hazel reached it.

Of course it was. It had been open since 1979, since whatever ancestor had held it wide and sung, and invitations didn't expire. Hazel stepped through into a house that smelled of woodsmoke and cold iron and, faintly, unbearably, of the gingerbread their grandmother used to make — a smell that had no source, that was simply the house remembering warmth and offering it up like evidence.

Theo stood in the kitchen doorway.

He had followed, or he had simply been there; with him there had never been a difference. In the lamplight he looked almost exactly as he had on the porch the first night — small, neat, patient, his coat buttoned to the throat, his boots dry despite the snow. Twenty years unchanged. Two hundred years unchanged. A child-shaped hole the village kept refilling with new children and calling the filling a payment.

"You walked away from my offer," he said. There was no accusation in it. Only wonder, which was worse.

"I'm counter-offering."

"There are only two terms. There have only ever been two terms."

"Terms are written by whoever holds the pen." Hazel set the tape recorder on the kitchen table — the machine that had translated him all along, that had carried his missing verse beneath their own questions like a stowaway. They pressed record. If this worked, the machine would keep the truth even if nothing else did. If it didn't work, well. Evidence seemed beside the point.

Then Hazel did the thing they had spent three days being afraid of, and one long hour learning to want.

They began to sing.

* * *

The first verse came up out of them like water from a deep well — Mrs. Aldous's cracked alto underneath it, and beneath that, their mother humming it as a fragment, a lullaby amputated mid-line, and beneath *that*, something older, something that belonged to the house itself. Hazel sang the lines every villager had choked on, the ones that stopped mid-breath in every recording Hazel owned:

*Come in from the weather, the door stands wide,*
*the fire is lit and the bread is tied,*
*no name we'll ask and no price we'll tell,*
*but what the winter gives, we give as well.*

The lamp guttered. The frost on the windows bloomed outward like ferns unfurling in fast-forward. And Theo —

Theo made a sound Hazel had never heard from him. Small. Involuntary. The sound of a thing that had waited two hundred years hearing its own name in a language everyone else had agreed to forget.

"Don't," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle, child and something vast sharing one throat. "Hazel. You don't know what you're — the second verse isn't yours to—"

"It's mine tonight." Hazel's voice shook and they let it shake and sang anyway. "Everything in this house is mine tonight. That's what heir means."

And they sang the verse no archive held, the one the tape recorder had whispered back at them from beneath their own questions, the one nine children had died inside of:

*What's given in gladness is taken in kind,*
*a memory traded, a face left behind,*
*sing me twice and hold me near,*
*I'll keep your winters warm and clear —*
*and what I keep, I keep for years.*

The house moved.

Not shook — *moved*, the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight, floorboards flexing like ribs, the walls taking a slow breath. The lamp died. In the dark, every window went white with frost, and then the frost began to recede, pulling back from the glass in long retreating tides, because the house understood what was happening before anyone in it did: the invitation was changing hands.

Hazel sang the first verse again. Their voice was wrecked now, raw as a scraped knee, and they sang through it.

*Come in from the weather, the door stands wide—*

The door slammed. All of them — front door, back door, the cupboard doors, the little gate on the garden path that hadn't latched since Hazel was nine — every door in the cottage shut at once with a sound like a full stop. Then, slowly, deliberately, the front latch turned itself. Locked. For the first time in forty-six years, the door of the Yarrow cottage was locked against the winter, and the winter knocked politely, and was not answered, because the winter was already inside.

Twice through. Both verses. Done.

Hazel stood in the dark kitchen, chest heaving, and felt it settle onto them — not pain, exactly. Weight. Like a coat sewn from every winter since 1898, laid across the shoulders by hands that had been waiting generations for someone to hold still long enough.

"Oh," Hazel said quietly. "Oh, it's *heavy*."

"That's why they gave children," Theo said softly. Behind him, impossibly, dawn-grey light was already bleeding through the receded-frost windows, though the sky outside was still black — the house making its own morning, or borrowing his. "Children bend. Children forget. Adults only break." He crossed the kitchen and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Hazel could see that his eyes weren't winter-colored after all. They were the exact brown of the grange book's ink. Of Edith Yarrow's handwriting. Of course they were. Everything about him had been written by them. "You could still stop. The terms aren't sealed until sunrise. Walk out the door and forget, and it all goes back to waiting."

"No," Hazel said. "It doesn't. Because I know all nine names now, and I'm going to say them out loud tomorrow where the whole village can hear, and forgetting doesn't work on people who insist." They sat down heavily at the kitchen table, the weight settling deeper, and looked up at the unchanged boy their great-grandmother's generation had invited in and their grandmother's generation had sung away mid-game. "Sit down, Theo. We've got until sunrise, and I intend to use it. You're bound to me now — which means you answer to me. And the first thing I want is the truth about each of them. All nine. Their real names, their real last days. Every detail you've been keeping warm all this time."

Something moved through Theo's face that on anyone else Hazel would have called relief.

"They called me imaginary," he said, sitting, folding his small hands on the table like a schoolboy. "For two hundred years. Do you know what it is, to be remembered only as a lie?"

"Yes," Hazel said. "I wrote a book once. Nobody believed that either."

Outside, far off across the fields, the true sun finally began to drag itself toward the horizon — hours early, hours late, the solstice breaking like a promise kept by the wrong person. The frost on the windows stayed gone. In the tape recorder's small red eye, the reel turned and turned, keeping everything.

And beneath the table, where the lamplight didn't reach, Hazel's shadow had begun — very slightly, very patiently — to change shape.
