## 21. The Archive of Small Voices

The grant office wanted a report by the first of February. Hazel wrote three drafts of it in January, all lies of omission, and mailed none of them.

The first draft was academic: *Fieldwork conducted in Wrenlow, Norfolk, between the 14th and 22nd of December yielded significant material regarding truncated carol traditions and regional variation in winter song.* It read like an obituary for a stranger. The second draft was honest and unusable: *I recorded a thing that should not have a voice, and my machine gave it one.* The third draft was just the word *no* typed forty times down the page, which at least had the virtue of being true.

In the end they sent a single paragraph withdrawing from the project, citing personal circumstances, and returned the advance in full. The program officer called twice. Hazel let both calls go to voicemail and listened to neither message. Some doors you don't open twice.

The tapes were harder.

There were eleven of them, ninety minutes each, labeled in Hazel's own careful hand — *Aldous, E., interview 1*; *carol fragments, mixed voices*; *solstice night, cottage interior*. They sat on the desk in the London flat like nine small coffins, or maybe ten, depending how you counted. For two weeks Hazel walked past them every morning on the way to the kettle. For two weeks the tapes said nothing, which was itself a kind of speech. Theo had never needed to raise his voice either. He had simply been there, patient as frost, until you turned toward him.

On a grey Tuesday in the middle of January, Hazel put the recorder on the kitchen table, threaded the first tape, and pressed play — not to listen. To make sure it still worked. To prove, one last time, that the voice was only magnetism and plastic, that it could be reduced, erased, unmade.

Hazel's own voice came out of the speaker, thin and professional: *"Mrs. Aldous, could you sing me the verse after 'the door stands wide'?"*

And beneath it — not layered over it, not behind it, but *inside* it, the way a stone sits inside a plum — something answered. Not words, exactly. A hum that resolved, if you let your ear go soft, into the missing verse. *The child is given, the child is glad, the fire burns long where the fire once had—*

Hazel stopped the tape.

Sat very still.

Then took all eleven cassettes to the bathroom, because it was the only room with a lock, and fed them one at a time into the basin, snapped each of them across the spine — the sound was worse than snapping bone, a wet plastic complaint — and ran the tap hot over the brown ribbon until it curled and bled its rust into the water. It took forty minutes. The flat smelled like burnt sugar and old pennies. When the last ribbon went down, Hazel sat on the edge of the tub and shook, not crying exactly, more like a house settling after a storm has passed through it and taken part of the roof.

The recordings had been the whole point of the grant. The archive was supposed to be the good that came out of Wrenlow — songs preserved, voices saved for scholars not yet born. And Hazel understood now, with the terrible clarity that arrives too late to be useful, that preservation was precisely what the village had spent two hundred years refusing to do. Every singer who stopped mid-verse. Every household that would not sing a line twice. They weren't being stubborn or superstitious or quaint. They were starving something on purpose, generation after generation, and calling the hunger mercy.

They had buried the children twice. Once in the ground, and once in the forgetting.

Hazel could not bring back the ground. But the second burial — that, maybe, could be undone.

* * *

The notebook cost four pounds from a stationer's near the British Library. Hazel chose it deliberately: plain, sewn binding, heavy cream paper, the kind of book built to outlast its owner. Not a field notebook with its grid lines and its pre-printed headers. A book with no structure at all, because structure was a kind of authority, and Hazel had no authority here. Only witness.

The first page took three days.

Not because the information was hard to find. The parish records had given up their dead easily enough once Hazel knew to look — nine names, nine winters, nine entries reading *gone to relatives* or *sent to her aunt in Norwich* or, in one case, simply a dash where a destination should have been. The difficulty was that writing the names down meant saying them aloud, and Hazel had learned in December what happened when you gave things in Wrenlow their whole name. You invited them in.

But this was London. This was January. The radiator clanked and a bus sighed at the stop outside and someone's television laughed next door at nothing. If the rules followed Hazel here, then so be it. Some invitations you make knowing the cost.

Hazel wrote:

**Edith Marcham, aged 7, winter 1811.**
**Thomas Marcham, aged 9, winter 1811.**
**Sarah Pettifer, aged 8, winter 1846.**
**Walter Dunn, aged 6, winter 1863.**
**Alice Yarrow, aged 10, winter 1898.**
**Frederick Cobb, aged 7, winter 1921.**
**Margaret Aldous, aged 9, winter 1954.**
**Peter Loxley, aged 8, winter 1972.**
**Samuel Reeve, aged 6, winter 1989.**

Nine names. Two hundred years. An average of one child every twenty-two years — roughly a generation, Hazel noted, and hated themselves for noting it, because the scholar in them never slept, never stopped counting, even now. Especially now.

Alice Yarrow's entry in the grange book had read: *the song sung twice, the door held open, the child given.* Great-grandmother's handwriting, upright and unhurried, the same hand that had recorded lambing counts and butter prices on the facing page. That was the detail that had kept Hazel awake through most of early January — not the horror of it but the *domesticity*. The giving of a child recorded between the price of wool and the date the well froze. As if it were a harvest. As if it were, in some accounting the village had agreed upon without ever saying aloud, exactly that.

Under each name, Hazel wrote everything the records and the interviews had yielded. Edith and Thomas Marcham, siblings, taken the same winter — the parish register showed their parents' burials eleven months later, side by side, and Hazel chose to believe the village had starved those two parents of answers rather than that grief had done the work alone. Sarah Pettifer's aunt in Norwich had no record of ever receiving a niece; Hazel had written to the city archives and received a photocopy of a household ledger showing no addition in the winter of 1846, and had sat with that photocopy for a long time, thinking: *she never arrived anywhere. She left everywhere.* Walter Dunn's father had carved a wooden whistle the Christmas before his son vanished; it was listed in a 1930s inventory of church leftovers, described as "child's toy, provenance unknown," and Hazel wrote down the inventory number because provenance unknown was a sentence that deserved to be corrected somewhere, even in pencil.

For Margaret Aldous, Hazel wrote the least and thought the most. Mrs. Aldous had died on the twenty-seventh of December, six days after the solstice, in her sleep, according to the neighbor who found her. The village held no funeral for her that anyone would describe to Hazel. There was a plot in the churchyard, though — Hazel had seen it before leaving, a fresh mound beside an older, weathered stone whose inscription had worn to almost nothing. Almost. Enough letters remained for Hazel to kneel in the frozen grass and trace them with a gloved finger until they assembled themselves: *MARGARET*, and below it a second name mostly gone, and dates from 1945 to 1954.

Two Margarets. One grave, or nearly one. The twin who stayed had spent seventy years sleeping — when she slept at all — beside the sister she'd sung short.

Hazel wrote in the memorial book: *Margaret Aldous, aged 9, winter 1954. Her sister tried to keep her. The trying cost the village its schedule and cost my family its quiet. I do not know if it was enough. I think she did not know either.*

It was the closest Hazel could come to forgiveness, and it wasn't forgiveness. It was arithmetic. But the book was not for absolution. It was for the other thing — the thing Theo had wanted, the thing he'd been starved of, the thing Hazel had finally understood on the porch at dawn while he faded like breath off glass: to be remembered accurately, by someone who knew the whole shape of you, cruelty and loneliness and all, and stayed anyway.

* * *

The problem was the ninth name. Or rather, the space where a tenth should have been.

Because there had been a tenth. Hazel was sure of it now, in the way you're sure of a scar's exact shape without looking. Winter of 1994. A child of nine who played hide-and-seek past dark with a friend no one else could see, a friend who waited on porches and knew where you'd hidden before you'd hidden, a friend who was — this was the part Hazel had spent thirty years sanding smooth — *kind*. Patient. Funny, in his quiet way. A friend who left mid-game one evening and never came back, and whose absence had been explained so smoothly, by so many adult voices, that the child had eventually accepted the explanation the way you accept weather.

*He was imaginary, love. You're too old for that now. Off to bed.*

Except he hadn't been imaginary, and Hazel hadn't been too old, and the game hadn't ended — it had been *ended*, interrupted, the village gathering at the boundaries of the Yarrow garden to sing him away mid-round, Hazel crouched in the cold frame behind the greenhouse with their hands over their ears, hearing the Inviting sung backward and feeling something enormous and gentle get pulled out of the world like a splinter.

The recovered memory had come back whole during the longest hour, and it had not gotten easier with repetition. Every time Hazel reconstructed it — and they reconstructed it often, compulsively, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — the same detail surfaced last and cut deepest: Theo had known. Standing in the snow at the edge of the lamplight, being sung away, he had looked directly at the cold frame, directly at the gap in the glass where Hazel's eyes were, and he had raised one hand. Not waving goodbye.

*Counting.* He'd been counting. *One* — the lift of his fingers — the way you signal to a hider that the seeking has begun, that the game goes on, that you haven't been forgotten even if you've been found.

He had spent his last minute in the world telling a nine-year-old that the game continued. That was the kindness Hazel had sanded away for thirty years, because remembering it meant remembering that everyone else — the singers, the parents, the whole warm lit village — had chosen the lie over the truth, and had made a child complicit in her own friend's erasure before she was old enough to know there was anything to be complicit in.

Hazel turned to the last written page of the memorial book and, after a week of not doing it, wrote:

**[No name given], aged 9, winter 1994. Taken nowhere. Left everywhere. Returned to no one, because the one person who might have come looking was taught, gently and thoroughly, not to.**

Then, underneath, smaller:

**I am coming back for you. Both verses. Once.**

* * *

The book grew through late January the way a coral reef grows — slowly, additively, from small dead things made into shelter. Hazel drew floor plans of the churchyard from memory and marked the nine unmarked graves by pacing distance from the lych-gate, the measurements absurdly precise and useless to anyone else and essential to Hazel. Hazel transcribed the Inviting in full — both verses, the missing one rendered in pencil so light it was nearly apology — and then, after a night of staring at it, did not cross it out. Erasing it felt like lying. Instead Hazel wrote above it, in red ink: *Do not sing. Do not sing. This is a map of a wound, not a door.* Three times, so the warning itself could not be mistaken for a refrain.

Hazel wrote descriptions of Theo that contradicted each other and let them stand: *small, about eight, coat too big, buttons mismatched* beside *tall as the doorframe, older than the churchyard, eyes like the gap under a door at night.* Both true. Neither complete. The villagers had each remembered a different friend, sized to their own childhoods, and Hazel began to suspect that Theo had never had a fixed shape at all — that he was whatever the lonely child of a given decade needed a companion to be, which meant the village hadn't invented him so much as tailored him, season after season, into the perfect instrument of their bargain. You could not love a thing properly if you kept refitting it. Hazel wrote that down too, and underlined it, and wished it applied only to monsters.

By the second week of February the book was sixty pages and Hazel had begun to think of it not as a document but as a *house* — a small, crowded, badly-lit house where nine children and one impossible friend could finally sit down together at a table that had room for all of them. Belonging, Hazel decided, writing this on the inside cover, means keeping what others buried. Not avenging it. Not even understanding it, fully, because understanding curdles into explanation and explanation curdles into excuse. Just keeping. Being the place where the names stay legible.

The villagers, when Hazel had gone door to door in the days after the solstice with the nine names spoken aloud, had responded in ways that split cleanly along a fault line Hazel suspected had run through Wrenlow for two centuries. Some wept — Mrs. Loxley, Peter's mother, ancient now herself, had gripped Hazel's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, "Say it again," and Hazel had, and she had wept again, and thanked them. Others had slammed windows. Mr. Cobb, Frederick's great-nephew, had told Hazel to get off his land and had stood in his doorway watching until Hazel reached the lane, and his face had not been angry. It had been terrified, and under the terror, unmistakably, relieved — the relief of a man watching someone else carry a weight he'd spent his life pretending wasn't there.

Hazel had published nothing. Would publish nothing. The scholarly apparatus — the citations, the comparative verse tables, the neat theory of folkloric truncation that would have made Hazel's career — all of it belonged to a person who had stood in Mrs. Aldous's kitchen asking cheerful questions about carols, and that person had been sung away too, mid-game, along with everything else from the winter of 1994.

What remained was the book. Cream paper, sewn binding, sixty pages and growing. Nine names. One friend. One heir who had claimed the bargain rather than break it, and carried it now the way you carry a coal from a dying fire — carefully, at arm's length, but carrying it.

* * *

On the last night of February, Hazel finished the final entry, closed the book, and sat with both hands flat on its cover, feeling the faint warmth of the lamp-soaked paper, and admitted the thing they had been circling since London.

The book was not finished. Could not be finished. Because a memorial that ends is a memorial that closes, and closing was the village's verb, the village's whole grammar — *closed the ledger, closed the grave, closed the subject, closed the door.* What Hazel had promised on the porch, singing Theo's name into the ragged dawn while windows slammed shut one by one up the lane, was not a monument. It was a practice. Something renewed. Something that required showing up, annually, in the cold, whether or not anyone watched, whether or not anything answered.

And there was another matter, smaller and sharper, that Hazel had been avoiding with the discipline of a scholar avoiding a footnote that undermines the thesis.

The bargain had been bound to Hazel — sung twice through, claimed as heir, the house shaking, the frost receding. Bound, but not dissolved. Hazel had chosen a third path instead of the offered two, and third paths have terms nobody writes down. All winter, in the London flat, small things had been true that shouldn't be: the kettle whistling a phrase of melody Hazel didn't recognize until they did. Frost on the inside of the bedroom window, on the warm side, arranged in small footprints leading nowhere. And once, only once, waking at 3 a.m. to the certain, calm knowledge that someone was sitting patiently at the kitchen table downstairs, waiting to be noticed — a knowledge that arrived without fear, which was somehow the frightening part.

Nothing had crossed. Nothing had knocked. Whatever remained of Theo lived now where Hazel had put him: in the memory, in the name, in the book. But a bound thing tests its bindings, the way water tests ice, without malice, without intent, simply because testing is what pressure does.

December would come. The solstice would come. And Hazel already knew — had known since the tapes went into the basin, since the first name went onto the first page — that they would be on the porch of the Wrenlow cottage when it did. Not to renew. Not to call anything in. To do the one thing the village had never once done in two hundred years of singing short and burying deep and calling it mercy.

To sing it once, all the way through, for no one — a funeral, not an invitation — and then, for the first time in the history of that song, let it go.

Hazel blew out the lamp. In the dark, the memorial book sat on the desk like a small lit window in an unlit house, and outside, far north, under whatever sky Wrenlow was wearing tonight, the frost lay thick on a porch where small footprints had once led to a door — and would, Hazel was quite sure, again.
