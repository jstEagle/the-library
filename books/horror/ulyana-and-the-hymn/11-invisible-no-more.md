## 11. Invisible No More

The hymnbook was warm.

That was the first thing Gideon noticed, before the weight of it, before the smell of mildew and old glue and something underneath that might have been honey or might have been rot. Maribel Voss had set it on the counter of the funeral home's back office between them like a card player laying down a losing hand, and when Gideon reached for it—when his fingers actually closed on the cracked leather cover—it was warm. Body-warm. Coffee-mug-left-too-long warm.

"Books aren't supposed to do that," he said.

"No," Maribel agreed. She had her arms crossed and her reading glasses pushed up into her gray curls, and she looked like a woman who had been waiting forty years to be relieved of a package she couldn't return. "They're not."

The torn edge of the final page caught the light from the desk lamp, ragged as a wound. He'd seen it last night in the station archive—the founders' hymnal, *First Congregational Society of Harrow Creek, 1931*, nine names inked inside the front cover, one of them a face he knew from a photograph he'd carried around like a splinter for thirty years. His mother's face. His mother's name: *Eleanor Underhill*, written in a careful schoolgirl hand, the letters leaning slightly right, as if even at nineteen she'd been in a hurry to get somewhere.

He touched the torn stub where the last verse used to be.

And the world turned its head.

* * *

It didn't happen all at once. That was the terrible part. If it had been thunder, if it had been a bell, he could have braced against it. Instead it happened the way dawn happens—gradually, then completely, and by the time you notice you can't remember what the dark felt like.

Maribel blinked at him. Blinked again. Her mouth opened, closed, opened.

"Gideon," she said. Not *Mr. Underhill*. Not *you there*. Not the vague, sliding-around-him way people at the gas station said *sir* without ever quite looking at him. She said *Gideon* the way you say the name of someone you've known your whole life, someone whose birthday you'd feel bad about forgetting. "Gideon Underhill. Lord. I've known you since you were in the church nursery and I never—" She pressed her hand flat to her sternum. "I never once thought about you. Isn't that awful? You'd come into the funeral home to pay for an obituary run and I'd look straight through you like you were the coat rack."

"You're not supposed to see me," Gideon said. His voice came out thin. "Nobody's supposed to see me. That's the whole—that's how I've stayed—"

"Stayed what?"

Alive, he didn't say. Safe, he didn't say. *Mine*, he didn't say, because he wasn't sure yet whose voice that word belonged to.

He left the funeral home at four in the afternoon with the hymnbook zipped inside his canvas bag, and the walk home—which should have taken eleven minutes—took fifty, because everyone wanted to talk to him.

* * *

The first was Mrs. Okafor from the corner house with the wind chimes, who had lived on Delano Street for as long as Gideon had been alive and had never once acknowledged his existence beyond a nod so minimal it could have been a neck spasm. She was out watering her mums. She saw him coming from half a block away, dropped the hose—actually dropped it, water fanning across the sidewalk—and waved both arms over her head like she was guiding in a small plane.

"Gideon! Gideon Underhill!" She hurried down her walk in her slippers. "You got tall. Well, I suppose you did, I wouldn't know, would I, but you've got your mama's chin, don't you? Eleanor's chin exactly." She took his arm. Took his actual arm, her fingers curling around his elbow with the proprietary warmth of a great-aunt. "You come in for lemonade. I've got lemonade. I've had lemonade since June waiting on somebody to drink it."

"I'm—I'm expected somewhere."

"Expected where?" Mrs. Okafor demanded, genuinely scandalized, as though the concept of Gideon being expected anywhere was a theological impossibility. And the worst part, the part that made his skin crawl in a way no dead caller ever had, was that she was *right*. Nobody expected him anywhere. Nobody ever had. That was the arrangement. That was the covenant. Be forgettable, be unremarkable, be a coat rack, and the thing beneath the town would slide its attention past you like water past a stone.

He made his escape with a promise of lemonade tomorrow that he already knew he'd keep, because refusing Mrs. Okafor now felt like refusing gravity.

Two blocks later, a dog followed him.

Then three dogs.

By the time he crossed Main Street he had a procession—a retriever mix, a beagle, an enormous shaggy thing that might have been part sheepdog and part bear—all trotting at his heels in loose formation, tails going, ears up, wearing expressions of profound relief, as though they'd spent their whole lives searching for him and had only just now been given his scent. The retriever kept pressing its nose against the back of his knee. The beagle sang a low, contented hound-song. A woman watering her window boxes stared at the parade, then at Gideon, and said, "Huh. They never do that," and then, squinting, "Wait. Do I know you? You look like somebody. You look like *somebody*."

"I have one of those faces," Gideon said.

"You do not," she said, with unsettling certainty. "That's the thing. You have the opposite of one of those faces."

* * *

He got home at dusk. The dogs arranged themselves on his porch steps like a jury and refused to leave. He counted his doors—front, back, cellar, the little door under the stairs that led nowhere and which he checked anyway, always, because rules were rules—and all four were where they belonged, and for the first time in his adult life the counting gave him no comfort whatsoever, because counting doors was a ritual for a man nobody could find, and Gideon Underhill had just walked through town as the most findable man in Harrow Creek.

He sat at his kitchen table with the hymnbook open in front of him and a cup of tea going cold beside it, and he thought, very carefully, about what had changed.

Not the book itself—he'd carried it through town zipped in the bag, and the bag hadn't mattered. It was the touch. Skin to cover. Maribel had handled it for years—had kept it in a drawer, had dusted around it, had probably moved it during the great reorganization of 2011—and she'd stayed herself. But the moment Gideon's fingers found the leather, the moment the heir touched the inheritance, something had gone out through the ground like a ripple through a pond, and every living thing in Harrow Creek had turned toward the center of it.

Including, he suspected, the thing at the bottom of the pond.

He tested it, because he was the kind of man who tested things. He lifted his hands off the book, waited a full minute, and then laid them back down.

Under the floorboards—under the foundation, under the clay and the limestone and whatever older, wetter layers lay beneath the limestone—something shifted. Not sound, exactly. Pressure. The particular pressure of being watched, except the watcher was beneath him and vast and patient, and its attention swung onto him the way a searchlight swings onto a boat that has wandered out of the shipping lane. He felt it take his measure. He felt it *recognize* him—not Gideon Underhill, invisible man, but Eleanor's boy, the missing heir, the unfinished verse walking around in a cardigan with cold tea.

The dogs outside began to sing. All three of them, heads back, a low rising harmony that Gideon recognized with a lurch in his stomach, because he'd heard that melody before—hummed under static, threaded through Dale Pruitt's last call, playing underneath everything like a second heartbeat. The hymn. The dogs were singing the hymn.

He took his hands off the book. The singing stopped mid-phrase. The pressure receded, grudgingly, like a tide going out but keeping one finger on the shore.

"Okay," Gideon said aloud, to the kitchen, to the dogs, to himself. "Okay. So we don't touch it. We put it in a box. We put it in a box inside another box, and we think."

The problem with thinking, he discovered over the next hour, was that visibility didn't turn off when he stopped touching the book. Whatever had woken in him had woken all the way. At seven o'clock his neighbor Ted Rasmussen knocked on the door to invite him to a barbecue, an event so unprecedented that Gideon stood there with his mouth open until Ted asked if he was having a stroke. At eight, the phone rang—his landline, the number he'd never given anyone—and a cheerful woman from the First Congregational welcome committee welcomed him to town, warmly, personally, noting that he'd lived on Delano Street for nineteen years and they really should have done this sooner. At nine, he went out to bring the trash bin up from the curb and found a casserole on his porch step with a note in unfamiliar handwriting: *Saw the lights on. Welcome home. —The Hendersons.*

Welcome home. As if he'd been away. As if he'd finally arrived somewhere he'd always been meant to be.

It should have been nice. That was the trap of it, and he could see the trap clearly and still felt its teeth. Thirty years of loneliness, and now the whole town was warm to him, and the warmth was real—Mrs. Okafor's lemonade would be real lemonade, Ted's barbecue would be real burnt chicken—and every ounce of it was aimed at him by something that wanted him found. He was a lighthouse now. Everything in the dark could see him, and everything in the dark was swimming closer, smiling.

His phone buzzed. The station line. He almost didn't answer.

"K-H-A-Rrow Creek, this is Gideon."

Silence. Then, faintly, underneath the silence, the hymn—just the first four notes, over and over, patient as dripping water. And beneath that, deeper, a voice that wasn't a voice, more like the memory of pressure on eardrums:

*Found you.*

He hung up. He unplugged the phone. He stood in his kitchen in the dark—didn't turn on the lights, some old instinct insisting the rituals still mattered—and listened to his own heart, and to the dogs on the porch, who had started singing again, softly, sweetly, like a lullaby.

Through the window, down the hill, he could see the water tower against the stars. It looked the same as it always had. It looked exactly the same, and somehow that was worse.

Gideon thought about his mother, nineteen years old, writing her name in a hymnbook in a hurry to get somewhere. He thought about the torn page, wherever it was, in whatever drawer or grave or pocket of the past. He thought about Ulyana on his porch at dawn, unchanged, saying *you stopped singing* like it was the simplest thing in the world.

If he was visible now—if hiding was over whether he liked it or not—then maybe the question wasn't how to go back to being a coat rack. Maybe the question was what a visible man was *for*.

He was still standing there, thinking that dangerous thought, when the knock came at the front door. Three slow knocks. Polite. Wet-sounding.

Through the frosted glass of the door panel, a shape stood on his porch among the singing dogs, and it was shaped like Dale Pruitt, and it was dripping.
