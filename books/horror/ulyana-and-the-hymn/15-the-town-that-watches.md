## 15. The Town That Watches

The first night there were three of them.

Gideon noticed them on his way out for coffee at midnight—his one concession to the living world, a thermos run to the all-night diner two blocks down, timed carefully between songs so he'd miss nothing on the board. Three people standing across the street from the station, under the dead elm that had been dying since before he was born. An older man in a bathrobe. A woman in a raincoat despite the clear sky. A teenager in pajama pants and a letterman jacket, which Gideon found almost endearing until he remembered the context.

They weren't doing anything. That was the thing. They were just standing there, faces tilted up toward the station's lit window like sunflowers toward a sun that hadn't risen yet. And they were smiling. Not grinning, not leering—smiling the way people smile at babies, or at old photographs, or at the moment before a birthday cake comes out of the kitchen.

He watched them for four minutes from behind the glass of the front door, thermos forgotten in his hand. They didn't move. The teenager's jacket flapped in the wind; the man's robe shifted around his ankles. But their heads never turned, their smiles never flickered, and none of them checked a phone or looked at a watch or did any of the hundred small fidgets that living people do when they're waiting for something.

When "Moon River" ended and the automation cued up, Gideon went back inside and locked the door twice and told himself it was nothing. Small towns are strange at night. People wander. Maybe there was a meteor shower. Maybe the diner was closed.

Maybe.

* * *

By the third night there were eleven.

He counted from the booth window, marking them off against the fogged glass with a dry-erase pen because counting was what he did, counting was the whole architecture of his survival—doors, lights, callers, and now neighbors. Eleven, arranged loosely along the sidewalk in no particular order, some facing the building, some facing each other as though mid-conversation, though their mouths never moved. The woman in the raincoat had brought a folding chair. She sat in it with her hands folded in her lap like she was waiting for a church service to begin, and her smile had not changed since Tuesday.

At 2:14 a.m., during the weather spot, they all began humming.

It came through faintly, through the double glass and the hum of the transmitter, a sound like a refrigerator singing to itself—but Gideon knew the melody now. He'd been learning it every night in this very room, Ulyana's voice threading through his like a second bloodstream. The hymn. The incomplete version, the one that looped and circled and never arrived anywhere, the musical equivalent of a sentence that keeps forgetting its own ending.

Eleven people, humming in the cold, smiling at a radio station.

Gideon did the weather. His voice only shook a little. He'd gotten good at that—the way you get good at carrying something heavy by never once setting it down.

* * *

Maribel Voss arrived at four in the morning on the fourth night, which meant she'd either been awake or had learned to dress quickly in the dark, and given everything else about Maribel, Gideon suspected both.

She stood beside him at the booth window, coffee in hand—he'd started keeping a second mug after chapter whatever-this-was of their shared nightmare—and looked out at the crowd without surprise.

"Twenty-two tonight," she said.

"You counted too."

"I've been counting things since 1974, Gideon. It's the family business." She sipped. "There's the Kowalski boy. He plays trumpet. Badly, I might add, I sat through three middle school concerts when his grandmother died and we handled the arrangements." She pointed with her chin at a heavyset man near the curb. "That's Tom Ellery. Sold me my car. Talked for forty minutes about rust-proofing."

"They're not possessed," Gideon said. It wasn't quite a question. He'd been turning it over all night, watching them, and possession had a look he was starting to recognize—the dead callers had it, a certain wrongness in the eyes, a puppet-string quality to the stillness. These people didn't have that. These people looked *comfortable*. They looked like they'd finally found a good parking spot.

"No," Maribel said. "Worse."

"Worse how? You keep saying that word like it has a definition."

She set her coffee down on the console next to the fader, which made him wince—there were rules about liquids near the board, rules he'd kept for nine years, and apparently those rules were among the many casualties of recent weeks. "Possession takes you over," she said. "Hope doesn't. Hope leaves you exactly where you are and just... points you somewhere. Look at them. Nobody dragged them here. Nobody's making them stand in the cold in their bathrobes."

"Then why are they here?"

"Because they can feel it." Her voice went quiet, the way it always did when she talked about the tower, like lowering the volume would lower the subject. "It's been getting louder since your mother stopped singing. Fifty years of static, and now there's a hole in the song again, and holes want filling. People feel that the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth. They don't know what it is. They just know that if they get close to the source, the feeling gets... sweeter."

"The source being me."

"The source being the broadcast. Which is you, lately." She turned to look at him, and in the booth's dim light her face had the specific exhaustion of someone who has spent four generations apologizing for something. "You've been playing music all night, every night, holding the line. And underneath your show, whether you mean it or not, you're humming. I heard it through my kitchen radio last week while I was embalming the Pruitt service. You hum when you think nobody's listening."

"I do not."

"Gideon. You're humming right now."

He stopped. Listened inward. There it was, faint as a pilot light—the melody, running under his thoughts like groundwater. He honestly couldn't remember when it had started. Somewhere between the archive and the rehearsal, between Dale's dripping politeness and the night the phones sang, the hymn had moved into him without paying rent.

"Okay," he said. "Point taken."

* * *

The gentle horror of it, he decided somewhere around dawn, was the ordinariness.

On the fifth night, the crowd brought snacks. Someone had set up a folding table with a thermos of cider and a plate of cookies under the elm, and people took turns going over for refreshments the way you would at an open house, except nobody spoke, and everybody smiled, and the hymn-humming continued uninterrupted, muffled slightly by cookie.

He recognized more faces now. Mrs. Adeyemi from the library, who had once fined him eleven cents in 1998 and apologized for it for a decade. The Vasquez twins, who ran the hardware store and had never in his life been seen apart, standing shoulder to shoulder like bookends. Pastor Lund, who had to be seventy-eight years old, wrapped in a quilt, beaming up at the window with the serene joy of a man who believed he was early for the rapture.

And they waved. That was new. When he crossed the booth to refill his coffee, several of them lifted their hands in small, pleasant waves—not frantic, not hungry, just neighborly, the wave you give someone taking out their trash.

It should have been funny. Part of him, the part that had survived thirty years of overnight radio by finding the absurdity in everything, wanted to laugh: the whole town showing up to his show like it was a drive-in movie, bringing cider, wearing slippers. If he'd read this in a script he'd have flagged it as overwriting.

But he'd looked at Pastor Lund's smile for too long. And the smile wasn't blank. That was the trap of it. A blank smile you could dismiss—a mask, a malfunction, something done *to* a person. This smile was full. It was warm. It had decades of Sunday potlucks and borrowed ladders and casseroles after funerals in it. It was entirely, authentically *them*, and what it was full of was hope, and the hope was aimed at him like a floodlight, and every hour he sat in this chair and held the frequency, the light got brighter.

They weren't victims. They were volunteers. And you couldn't save people from a thing they were lining up for with cookies.

* * *

Ulyana appeared during the six o'clock news, sitting cross-legged on top of the console, which was a new level of disregard for broadcast equipment that Gideon chose not to address.

She was watching the crowd through the glass with her head tilted, the way she watched everything, like a bird considering a window. Eight years old forever, in the yellow dress that had no business surviving thirty years of his imagination, if imagination was ever what she'd been.

"They're happy," she said.

"They're creepy."

"They're happy *and* creepy. Both things are true." She swung her feet. "I used to watch people like that. Before you. From underneath. Everyone looks like candles from underneath. Little flames walking around, and you want—" She stopped herself, which she almost never did, and Gideon felt the temperature of the room change by a degree. "The tower wants them close because close is easier. Easier to hear. Easier to reach. Easier to harvest when the time comes."

"So I'm bait."

"You're a porch light." She said it gently, the way she said most terrible things. "Moths come to porch lights. It's not the light's fault. But the light could turn off."

"And then?"

"Then the moths go looking for another light." She looked at him with those eyes that were the exact color of lake water over something deep. "Or the dark comes to the porch instead."

* * *

Maribel came back on the seventh night with a folder, which in Maribel's language was the equivalent of arriving with a fire extinguisher.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'm kind of on the air."

"It's the automation block. I checked the log before I came in, I'm not an amateur." She spread the folder on the guest desk: photographs, property records, a legal pad dense with her small, furious handwriting. "I did what you asked. I went through who's coming, night by night. Cross-referenced with the founding families, then with everyone else."

"And?"

"And it's not random, but it's worse than a pattern." She tapped a photograph of the crowd from two nights ago—someone had been photographing them, and it took Gideon a second to realize it must have been Maribel, standing in the dark across the street, documenting her own neighbors like evidence. "Founding blood first. Voss, Halloran, Ellery, Adeyemi—yes, even the Adeyemis, they married in in '61, the hymn doesn't care about paperwork. Then in-laws. Then friends of the families. Then..." She flipped the page. "Everyone else. In order of proximity. To the families, to the tower, to *you*. It's spreading outward like a stone dropped in a pond, and the pond is the whole town, and the stone is—"

"Me."

"The broadcast. You. Same thing now, I'm afraid." She took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and then some. "My grandfather used to say the bargain had rules. The thing stays fed, the town stays safe, and the *town* —the actual people—stay asleep to it. Comfortable. Unaware. That was the mercy in it, if you can call it that. Four generations of us carrying the weight so nobody else had to feel it." She laughed, short and bitter. "Your mother breaks the ritual, the binding starts slipping, and now the whole town can feel the hymn under their feet. And do you know what they're doing with that feeling?"

Gideon looked out the window. Mrs. Adeyemi was waving at him again. The pastor raised his cider cup in a small toast.

"They're *enjoying* it," he said quietly.

"They're hopeful." Maribel put her glasses back on. "That's the word I keep coming back to. Not hypnotized, not enslaved. Hopeful. Whatever's under that tower has been leaking comfort into this town for fifty years—good harvests, mild winters, the mill staying open when every other mill in the county closed. People soaked in it their whole lives without knowing. Now the source is loud, and all that old comfort is rising up in them, and they're walking toward it in their slippers with a plate of snickerdoodles, smiling, because for the first time in their lives they can feel where the good comes from." She met his eyes. "You can't arrest hope, Gideon. You can't lock it out. I've tried locking doors my whole life—it's the family specialty—and hope walks through them like weather."

* * *

At 5:40 that morning, the teenager in the letterman jacket knocked on the station door.

Gideon watched him through the glass, heart doing something arrhythmic. The kid—Kowalski, the trumpet player—stood politely on the mat, hands at his sides, smiling up at the camera above the door. After a moment he reached into his jacket pocket and held up a piece of paper, pressing it flat against the glass.

Gideon should not have opened the door. Every rule he had ever lived by, every counted door and kept light, said do not open the door. But the kid was maybe sixteen, and the note was written in careful pencil, and some stubborn human part of him needed to know.

He unlocked one lock. Kept the chain on. Cracked the door two inches.

The paper read, in round schoolboy letters:

**THANK YOU FOR THE MUSIC. WE CAN HEAR IT GETTING CLOSER TO THE ENDING. PLEASE DON'T STOP.**

Below it, smaller: **MY MOM SAYS TELL YOU SHE'S PROUD OF YOU.**

Gideon stared at it. "Your mom," he said. His voice came out strange. "What's your mom's name?"

The kid just smiled. The same smile as everyone else, warm and full and patient, and behind it, way back in the dark of his pupils, something waited with the courtesy of a caller holding on the line.

"She says you'll remember soon," the kid said pleasantly. "Everybody says so. We all say so."

Gideon closed the door. Counted the locks—one, two, three. Went back to the booth. Sat down. Cued the next record with hands that mostly worked.

Through the glass, twenty-six people smiled at him in the gray pre-dawn, and beneath his ribs, unbidden, the hymn hummed on—and for the first time, Gideon noticed that he knew how the next verse went, and that some quiet, hopeful part of him, a part that wore his mother's face, was already singing it.
