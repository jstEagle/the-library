## 20. The Final Verse

The wind at the top of the water tower smelled like pennies and pond water, and Gideon Underhill—forty-one years old, invisible for most of them, currently the most visible man in Harrow Creek—stood on a catwalk slick with condensation and thought, absurdly, of his microphone technique.

Chin up. Shoulders loose. Speak past the microphone, not into it. His mother had never taught him that; the radio had. Thirty years of overnight shifts had taught him everything except the one thing he needed now, which was how to sing.

"You're stalling," Ulyana said.

She stood beside him at the rail, small and eight years old and utterly unbothered by the wind that was trying to peel him off the tower like a decal. Her braids didn't move. Her cardigan didn't ripple. The storm bent around her the way water bends around a stone, and Gideon found that, of everything happening tonight, this was the detail that steadied him. Some things stayed put. She was one of them.

"I'm not stalling," he said. "I'm doing a soundcheck."

"Into the sky."

"The sky is a very large microphone."

She considered this with the grave seriousness she'd given all his jokes since 1987, when he'd invented her on the walk home from school and she had apparently, in some sense that still made his teeth ache, invented herself right back. "The transmitter's carrying," she said. "Maribel says the whole town's listening. The dead too."

"I know. I can hear them breathing."

"They don't breathe, Gideon."

"Then I can hear them *not* breathing, and it's worse."

Below them, Harrow Creek glowed under the brownout—streetlights the color of weak tea, porch lights flickering like the town itself was having second thoughts. Every house had someone standing in the yard or at the window, faces tipped up toward the tower. Not possessed. Hopeful. Maribel had said that, and Maribel had been right, and it was so much worse than possession that Gideon had stopped letting himself think about it directly, the way you don't look straight at the sun or at your own funeral.

The thing beneath them filled the tank. He could feel it through the soles of his shoes—a slow pressure, a patience the size of a reservoir. It had risen through the water while he climbed, wearing faces as it came: Mrs. Halloran in her good blue dress. Dale Pruitt with his kind, ruined voice. A dozen others who had called his show in the last week, and dozens more from decades before, all of them stacked in the dark water like photographs at the bottom of a drawer, all of them singing counterpoint in a key that made his fillings hum.

*Sing the verses you like,* they'd offered, an hour ago, through every phone line and every dead mouth at once. *Sing the pretty ones. We'll give her back to you. Your mother. Whole. Warm. She'll remember your birthday.*

He had almost said yes. He could still feel the shape of the almost in his mouth, sweet and rotten, like fruit gone over.

Then Ulyana had cut the transmitter, and the silence afterward had been the loudest thing he'd ever heard, and in that silence he had understood the last verse the way you understand a locked door: by standing in front of it long enough.

*The final verse demands a name freely given.*

Not a name sung. Not a name written in a hymnal. A name *given*—handed over like a house key, like a child, like the only thing you own.

"Gideon." Ulyana touched his hand. Her fingers were cool and dry and exactly as he remembered them from thirty years of remembering. "When it starts taking, don't fight it. Fighting makes it take more. It's like—"

"Quicksand," he said. "Like the radio. You never fight the board. You ride it."

"You were always better at the metaphors."

"I had a very good imaginary friend."

Something moved in her face—not a smile, exactly, but the place where a smile would live in a person who had a face the way a hymn has a face. "I'm not imaginary," she said. "I'm the harmony. I was made to hold the part your founders couldn't. Nine singers, and the ninth part was always empty, and they filled it with a fragment of the song itself and sent it out to wait." She looked at him. "It waited a long time. It got lonely. It got fond of a lonely boy who talked to the dark on his walk home from school, and it forgot, a little, what it was for."

"Ulyana—"

"You should know," she said, and her voice did something he'd never heard it do, something small and mortal, "that I don't want to do this. The binding. I know what it costs and I know who pays and I—" She stopped. Started again, in her flat, patient, wrong-and-wonderful voice. "But the tower is thirsty, Gideon, and the town is in the glass, and you are the last verse's heir, and I am the harmony, and neither of us was made to be happy. We were made to be *true*. So."

"So," he said.

"So. On my count. And Gideon—" she squeezed his hand, hard, for someone with no bones that mattered, "—whatever you lose tonight, I will keep it. That's not the ritual. That's just me."

He nodded, because his throat had closed, and because somewhere below, in the dark water, the thing had begun to sing the hymn's first verse—*the founders' verse, the welcoming verse*—and there was no more time for anything but the truth.

Ulyana counted.

"One," she said, and the wind stopped.

"Two," she said, and the dead below went silent, a whole reservoir holding its breath.

"Three," she said, and Gideon Underhill opened his mouth and sang.

* * *

The first three verses came out of him like water from a tapped well—his voice cracked and untrained and completely sufficient, because the hymn had never needed beauty. It needed accuracy. It was a lock, and locks don't care if the key is pretty.

He sang the founding verse, the one the nine had sung in 1931 with their hats off and their doubt politely hidden, and he felt the thing below shift its weight, annoyed, a dog hearing the leash.

He sang the feeding verse, the one Maribel's family had twisted into a bargain—*grief for safety, names for quiet*—and he sang it *straight*, unwarped, the way it was meant to go, and the brownout lights below flickered and steadied, flickered and steadied, like a town remembering its own pulse.

He sang the verse of the interruption, the 1974 verse, the one his mother had died in the middle of, and here his voice broke clean in half, and Ulyana's harmony came in under it like a hand under an elbow—cool, dry, exactly on pitch—and carried the phrase to its end so the verse would not die twice.

And the thing in the tank rose.

It came up through the access hatch and over the rim of the catwalk like a tide with opinions, and it wore the faces of his callers, all of them at once, Mrs. Halloran and Dale Pruitt and the others arranged across its surface like a congregation seen through dark glass. Dale's face was nearest, and Dale's mouth moved, and the voice that came out was the warm radio voice Gideon had trusted across a hundred sleepless nights.

*You could stop,* Dale said. *Verse four of five. You could stop and keep what you have. Your rules. Your doors. Your little invisible life. Nobody would even know you tried.*

"That's the point," Gideon sang, and kept singing, because the verse didn't pause for conversation. "Nobody would know. That was always the point. I'm done with it."

*Your mother stopped,* the faces said, gentle, terrible. *She stopped and she is still here. She is right here. She is the water. She is so tired, Gideon. She just wants to go home and you are singing the door shut.*

The verse wavered. One note wavered, half a beat, and the thing *poured* toward that note like darkness toward a crack, and Ulyana's voice came in like a blade:

"He knows where she is," Ulyana sang, in harmony, to the thing, to Gideon, to the town, to God if God was on frequency. "He knows, and he's singing anyway. That's what *freely* means. That's the whole verse. Sing, Gideon."

He sang.

* * *

And then the last verse, and there was no more stalling, no more soundcheck, nothing between him and the sentence the hymnal had torn out in 1931 because even nine brave founders couldn't stomach writing it down:

*The door is shut by what is given,*
*not by blood and not by breath;*
*the lock turns on a name forgiven—*
*a name surrendered unto death.*

"It wants a name," Ulyana said, and her voice was shaking, and it was the most human sound Gideon had ever heard. "It has to be given. Not sung. *Given.* You have to mean the giving, Gideon, all the way down, or it won't turn the lock, and if it doesn't turn—"

"I know what it means."

He thought about his name. Forty-one years of it. Gideon Underhill, the man nobody remembered meeting, the voice on the AM dial at 2 a.m., the boy who counted doors because his mother vanished and the world had taught him early that being unnoticed was the only armor that never rusted. His name was the last thing he owned. It was the shape of every door he'd ever locked behind himself.

The faces in the water were all looking at him now. Even the storm had gone quiet. The whole town below was silent, a thousand hopeful faces in weak tea light, and somewhere in the station Maribel Voss was holding the phone lines open with her tired human hands, and Ulyana stood beside him with the harmony held in her throat like a bird in a closed fist.

"Gideon," she whispered. "Last chance. I can carry it alone. I was made—"

"You were made to be *true*," he said. "So am I. Count me in."

She closed her eyes. "One," she said.

He breathed.

"Two."

He let go of the rail.

"Three."

"Gideon Underhill," he said. And then, because the verse required it, because the lock required it, because *free* required it, he said it the way you say the one thing you can never unsay: "I give it. I give it gladly. Take the name. Take the man. Let the door be shut."

Ulyana sang.

* * *

The harmony she'd been created to hold was not a sound. He understood that now, too late and exactly on time. It was a *place*—a slot in the structure of the hymn that had been empty since 1931, a negative space shaped like a ninth voice, and when she filled it, the hymn closed around the name he'd given like a fist around a coin, and the thing beneath the tower screamed with every dead face it owned, and the water went down.

Not all at once. Like a tide going out. The faces sank in order—Mrs. Halloran last, giving him a small, formal nod, the nod of a caller whose call has finally been taken—and the pressure through his shoe soles eased, and the brownout lights below came up gold and warm and ordinary, and somewhere a dog started barking the way dogs bark at nothing at 3 a.m., which is to say, at nothing, thank God, at nothing.

The door was shut. The hymn was whole. The town was saved.

And the song, having been given a name, began to collect.

* * *

It didn't hurt. That was the horrifying part. It felt like a warm hand smoothing a page.

His mother's face—no. Not her face. Her *name*, the sound of it, the way "Ruth" had tasted in his mouth when he was small and brave enough to say it out loud. The word went soft in his memory like a wet stamp, and then it was a blank, and then it wasn't even a blank, it was just a place where a word should be, and he reached for it and found smooth paper.

"Ulyana," he said. "My mother's—"

"Ruth," Ulyana said instantly, gripping his hand. "Ruth Underhill. She sang alto. She wore her hair pinned up with a pencil because she was always losing the pencil and finding it again. She hummed while she cooked and she burned the toast every single morning and she called you 'Gid' when she was happy and 'Gideon' when you were in trouble and you were in trouble a lot because you were always taking radios apart."

The name came back—not his, but near him, held by her, a word he could see through a window he couldn't open. He nodded. He kept standing. The song took the next thing.

The name of his street. Ulyana gave it back. The name of his school. Given back. The year he got his first radio, the name of the man who'd sold it to him, the smell of the solder his mother used when she fixed the antenna—each one smoothed away, each one caught by Ulyana's voice and held, held, *held*, her harmony no longer just a part in a hymn but a net under a falling man, and she was reciting now, fast, fierce, his whole life read back to him like a caller's dedication:

"Your first show was a Tuesday. You played a request for a woman who'd lost her dog. You counted the doors of the station every night for thirty years—four doors, then three, then the fire exit that stuck. You were kind to the dead when they called. You were kind, Gideon, you were *kind*, that's the thing the song can't take because it isn't a memory, it's *you*—"

But it was taking, and he could feel how much there was left to take, and how much of him was already smooth paper, and his knees were going, and the catwalk was very far above a very ordinary town, and the last thing he thought, with the warm hand smoothing the page of him, was that he couldn't remember what he'd been so afraid of all those years, being seen—being seen seemed fine, being seen had turned out to be the best thing that ever—

"Ulyana," he said. His own name was going now. He could feel it going, the word *Gideon* going soft at the edges like a stamp in the rain. "Ulyana, what's my—"

She caught him as he folded, both arms, an eight-year-old girl holding up a grown man with the strength of the thing she was made from, and she lowered him gently to the catwalk under the wide gold sky of a saved town, and she took his hand, and she leaned close, and she began, softly, in the voice she'd been made to hold:

"Gideon," she said. "Your name is Gideon. Gideon Underhill. Let me tell you who you are."

Below them, in the station, a transmitter hummed on, playing dead air into a town that was already starting to forget.
