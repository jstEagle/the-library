## 22. Ulyana and the Hymn

Ulyana had been made from a hymn, and a hymn, she had learned, is just a promise that learned to breathe. She was the harmony part—the second voice, the one that exists only to make the first voice sound like itself. She had been created to hold a note beneath the water tower in 1931, and she had been holding it, in one way or another, ever since.

She had not expected the note to be a man.

Gideon slept on the studio floor now, most nights, in the nest of blankets Maribel Voss had smuggled in from the funeral home's break room, because Maribel was a practical woman and practical women know that grief and comfort both run on coffee and clean linens. The transmitter hummed its low overnight hum. The ON AIR light glowed red and steady, the way a hearth glows, and Ulyana sat beside Gideon with his hand in both of hers, and she did the only thing left to do.

She remembered for him.

* * *

The song had taken his name first.

That was how it worked—she had understood it by the second dawn, watching him blink at her with the polite blankness of a stranger on a bus. The binding wanted a name freely given, and Gideon had given his, and so the song collected it the way a river collects a coin: without malice, without even noticing. Then it took his mother's face. Then the smell of the station's coffee, which had been terrible and which he had loved. Then the memory of counting doors, which had been the whole architecture of his courage, and Ulyana felt it go out of him like heat leaving a house with the windows open.

By the third morning he had asked her, kindly, who she was.

"I'm Ulyana," she said. "You made me up when you were eight. Except you didn't. I was made up long before that, by nine people who should have known better, and you found me anyway, because you were the kind of boy who went looking in the wrong places and found the true ones."

He considered this. He had a way of considering things—head tipped, eyes narrowed, as though the world were a caller he wasn't sure he should put on air. "That sounds made up," he said.

"It is made up," Ulyana said. "Everything good is. Sit still. I have a story."

* * *

The first story she told him was about a woman named Ruth Underhill, who sang the fourth verse of a hymn on a night in 1974 and did not finish it, and whose son was six years old and waiting at home with a glass of milk he never drank.

"Was she mine?" Gideon asked.

"She was yours," Ulyana said. "She had a laugh that came out sideways, like a hiccup that had ambitions. She hummed when she washed dishes. She was frightened her whole life of being noticed, and she loved you so loudly that she couldn't help being noticed anyway, and everyone on her street knew her as the woman who laughed at her own jokes before she got to them."

Gideon's mouth did something small and involuntary. Not a smile. The place where a smile kept its coat checked.

"I don't remember her," he said.

"No," Ulyana said. "But your mouth does. Look at it. It's trying."

* * *

She told him a story every night, because the song took something every night, and she had learned that the taking was slower, much slower, when the memories were being worn instead of stored. A memory that is spoken aloud, that lives in two bodies instead of one, is harder to carry off. The hymn could strip a man of what he kept. It had never learned to strip a man of what was given away.

So she gave everything away.

She told him about the dead callers—Mrs. Halloran, who had apologized for calling during her own funeral; Dale Pruitt, who had begged him not to finish the song and had meant it and had also, in the end, been grateful. She told him about the night the phones all lit up at once and he had held the board steady with shaking hands because someone had to, and she watched his face while she told it, and his face did not know the story, but his hands, resting on the mixing board in the dark, knew it perfectly. They settled on the faders like birds coming home to a ledge.

"You did that," she said.

"Did I?" he said.

"You did," she said. "You were terrified the whole time. You did it anyway. That's the whole man, Gideon. That's the entire recipe. Terrified, and does it anyway, and counts the doors after."

"How many doors are there?" he asked, because he asked every night, because some questions outlive the memories that planted them.

"Eleven," Ulyana said. "Count them with me tomorrow. You always get to nine and then you have to start over, and I have never once had the heart to tell you that you're wrong, because the counting isn't the point. The counting is the point."

He looked at her for a long moment. "You're strange," he said, with something like wonder.

"I was made from a hymn by nine people in 1931 and I have the face you invented for me when you were eight," Ulyana said. "Strange is the least of it."

* * *

Maribel Voss came by on the fifth evening with a casserole and a locked drawer's worth of notes she could no longer explain to herself.

The town had woken clean. That was the bargain's last mercy: the song, having eaten its name and its payment, had smoothed the week flat behind it. Nobody remembered the storm. Nobody remembered the neighbors standing in the street at night with their faces tilted up toward the tower like sunflowers that had given up on the sun. Nobody remembered Gideon Underhill at all, which was the payment's cruelest line item, and the one Ulyana had argued with the hymn about for three days, uselessly, the way you argue with weather.

Maribel remembered because she had written it all down and locked it in a drawer, and a locked drawer is a kind of promise too. She stood in the studio doorway with the casserole and looked at Gideon—polite, blank, handsome in the worn way of a photograph left in a window—and her face did the thing faces do when they meet a person they have grieved and must pretend they haven't.

"Do I know you?" Gideon asked.

"No," Maribel said, steady as a dial tone. "But I brought chicken. That's how strangers meet in this town. It's practically the law."

She set the casserole down and stayed for two hours, and told stories of her own—about a funeral home and four generations of tired women, about a bargain that had seemed reasonable one comfortable year at a time—and Gideon listened the way he apparently always listened, which was completely, leaning forward, as though the story might get away from him if he blinked. And Ulyana watched Maribel leave, and watched her pause at the door with her hand on the frame, and heard her say, very quietly, to no one who could remember it:

"You were the best of us. I'm sorry it took all of us to find that out."

The door closed. Gideon said, "She seems sad."

"She's carrying something heavy," Ulyana said.

"Should we help?"

"We already did," Ulyana said. "That's the strange part. That's the part I'll tell you tomorrow."

* * *

The song kept taking. But it took more slowly now, and Ulyana began to notice the shape of what it left behind.

It left kindness. That was the bedrock, apparently—the layer beneath all the memories, the thing he was before he was anything in particular. A stranger's kindness, unattached to any name or history, like furniture left in a house by a previous owner. He held doors for people who didn't see him. He straightened the chairs in the studio. He found a stray dog on the sixth night and fed it half a casserole, and the dog, who like all animals still saw him perfectly, refused to leave, and so they had a dog now, and the dog's name was—after considerable debate—Eleven.

"Because there are eleven doors," Gideon explained, and then frowned. "Why are there eleven doors?"

"Because you counted them," Ulyana said, "for thirty years, to stay invisible, and it worked, and then one day you stopped counting so you could be seen, and it was the bravest thing this town has ever seen, and nobody saw it, except me. I see it every day."

He looked at her the way he looked at callers who said something true by accident. "You make me sound like somebody," he said.

"You are somebody," Ulyana said. "You're the somebody I've been waiting thirty years to sit on a porch with. But we'll get to that story. Stories go in order. That's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Mine," she said. "I'm the harmony. I keep the order."

* * *

On the ninth night she told him the story of the porch.

"You were forty-one years old," she said, "and you came home at dawn from a shift, and I was standing on your porch. I looked exactly like I looked when you were eight, because I don't know how to be anything else. And you looked at me, and I have never seen a face do two things so completely at once. You were terrified. And you were—" she paused, savoring it, the way she savored the held note beneath the tower, the note that had finally resolved after ninety-three years— "you were *glad*. Glad in a shameful, buried, eight-years-old way. Because you had spent your whole life making sure no one saw you, and here was the one person who had always seen you, standing on your porch, patient as a porch light."

"What did I do?" Gideon asked. He was sitting up now, cross-legged on the blankets, and Eleven was asleep against his knee, and the red ON AIR light made a small warm world of the studio.

"You slammed the door," Ulyana said. "And then you counted it twice."

Gideon laughed. It came out of him sideways, like a hiccup with ambitions, and Ulyana felt the whole night tilt toward that laugh, felt it the way the founders must have felt the hymn take hold under their feet—something enormous and old, moved by something very small.

"That sounds like me," he said, astonished.

"It is you," she said. "See? You're still in there. The song took the name off the door, but it's still your door. It's still your laugh. I'd know it anywhere. I've been listening for it since 1931."

* * *

He believed her a little more each night. That was the mechanism, she discovered—belief was also a memory, and it could be given away too, and given belief was even harder to steal. Every story she told, he carried a piece of it out loud, retelling it back to her in his own words, clumsy and wrong in the details and perfectly right in the shape, until the story lived in both of them and the song could not pry it loose.

"You're going to run out of stories," he said one night, worried.

"Never," Ulyana said. "You're going to make new ones. You make them constantly. Yesterday you named a dog Eleven. Last week you tried to fix the transmitter with a rubber band and an apology. You are a story factory, Gideon Underhill. You've been manufacturing them your whole life. You just keep forgetting to keep the receipts."

"My name," he said slowly. "You keep saying it. Gideon Underhill."

"Does it fit?"

He turned it over. She watched him turn it over, the way she had watched him, night after night, turn over every gift she brought him. "It fits," he said finally, "like a coat I've been told is mine. I can't prove it. But it hangs right."

"That's how names work," Ulyana said. "You don't prove them. You wear them until they're true. Yours has been true for forty-one years. It'll keep."

* * *

On the twelfth night, she told him the last story she had. It was the one about the tower.

She told him about the final verse, and the name freely given, and how he had sung it with her—his voice cracked and human and afraid, her voice underneath it, the harmony she had been made to hold—and how the thing beneath the water had worn the faces of his callers and sung counterpoint, and how he had sung anyway. She told him about the moment the binding closed, and the silence after, and the dawn, and the way he had sat down on the tower's rusted catwalk and said, "Well. That's the sign-off," in the exact tone he used for ending a broadcast.

"Did it hurt?" Gideon asked.

"Everything that matters does," Ulyana said. "But here's the part I need you to hear, and I need you to wear it, and give it back to me tomorrow, and the day after. Are you listening?"

"I'm listening," he said. "I always listen. Apparently."

"You gave your name so that a town full of people could wake up safe and never know your name," Ulyana said. "That is the most invisible act I have ever witnessed. You spent your whole life hiding so nothing could find you, and in the end you hid the greatest thing you ever did inside the one place nothing could take it from you: inside other people, who don't even know they're carrying it." She squeezed his hand. "You didn't lose your name, Gideon. You *planted* it. And I am the ground it's planted in. And I do not forget. It's the one thing I was built to do."

The studio hummed. Eleven dreamed. Somewhere in the town, eleven hundred people slept safely without knowing why, and one of them, in a house with a locked drawer, had written on a card she couldn't explain: *Tell the man at the station thank you,* and had not done it, and would not know why she kept the card.

"Ulyana," Gideon said.

"Yes?"

"I don't know who I am. But I know who *you* are. Isn't that strange? You're the clearest thing I know."

"That's not strange," Ulyana said, and her voice did not waver, because harmonies don't waver; that's what they're for. "That's the design. I was made to be the part you can hold onto. Hold on."

* * *

The nights found their rhythm, the way nights do.

Each evening Maribel brought food and left a little more of herself in the chair by the board, and Gideon learned her the way he was learning everything: from scratch, and completely, and she found, to her astonishment, that being known from scratch was its own strange grace—she got to be the woman with the casserole instead of the woman with the drawer, at least for a few hours a night.

Each evening Ulyana told the stories back. The milk he never drank. The laugh with ambitions. The eleven doors, counted twice. The rubber band and the apology. The dog. The porch. And Gideon gave each one back to her, wearing them in, and the song reached for them nightly and found, more often, that they had already been given away, and went away hungry.

And at the end of each evening, they went out to the porch.

Because Gideon had begun, without knowing why, to end every night the same way: he would stand at the studio door and count—*one, two, three*—all the way to eleven, and then he would look at Ulyana and say, "Okay. All accounted for. Let's go sit where I can see the street." And they would sit on the porch of the little station house with the light on, because the light stayed on now, always, a matter of policy, and Eleven would arrange himself across their feet like a draft excluder with opinions.

The town went by, not seeing him. And that was all right. That was the old magic and the new one both: he had spent thirty years hiding so nothing would find him, and he had spent one terrible week being found by everything, and what he had arrived at, at the end, was the smallest possible audience. One dog. One woman made of a hymn. One porch light.

"Tell me the one about the laugh," Gideon said, on a night in late autumn, breath fogging, hands wrapped around a mug of coffee that was terrible and that he loved.

"Again?" Ulyana said. "You asked for that one last night."

"I know," he said. "It's my favorite. I don't know why. It feels like mine."

"It is mine," she said. "It's the one I keep closest. All right. Once more."

And she told it—Ruth Underhill, the sideways laugh, the dishes and the humming—and Gideon laughed his sideways laugh at the right place, because he always got to the right place now, and Ulyana joined him underneath, low and steady, the harmony part, the second voice, the one that exists only to make the first voice sound like itself.

And because it was late, and because the town was asleep, and because some promises are just songs that learned to breathe, they did what they always did at the end. They sang. Softly. So softly. The hymn, all the verses, even the last one, even the one with the name in it—*Gideon Underhill, given freely, given gladly*—sung at a volume calibrated to reach exactly one listener, and that listener was the dark, and the dark could listen all it wanted.

Nothing heard.

Two figures on a lit porch, doors counted, singing softly.

It was, Ulyana thought, holding the note, holding the man, holding on—the smallest audience in the world, and the only one that had ever mattered, and it was enough. It was, at last, after ninety-three years, exactly enough.
