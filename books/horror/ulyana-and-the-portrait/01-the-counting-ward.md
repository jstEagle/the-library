## 1. The Counting Ward

The dying count backwards. I want to be very clear about that from the start, because everything else I might tell you — about Hollowmere, about the house, about the girl on the porch who hadn't aged a day in thirty years — will sound like the ramblings of a woman who has been awake too long and alone too often. But the counting comes first. The counting is the foundation, and you cannot build a haunted life on anything less.

I'm a hospice nurse. Twelve years now. People assume it's grim work, all weeping families and morphine drips, but mostly it's quiet. You learn to read a room the way sailors read weather. You learn which silences mean *soon* and which ones mean *not yet*, and you learn that the dying are rarely afraid of what everyone assumes they're afraid of. They're afraid of being a burden. They're afraid of unfinished business. Mostly they're afraid of the dark, which is such an old fear that it's almost respectable again.

And sometimes — not always, not even often, maybe once every few months — they count.

It starts the same way every time. The breathing changes. Not the wet rattle or the Cheyne-Stokes stuttering that textbooks prepare you for; this is something else, a rhythm like someone settling into a chair they've been standing beside for hours. And then, so softly you have to lean in, lips barely moving:

"One hundred."

I heard it first from Mr. Abernathy, three years ago, room 4, pancreatic cancer, a retired high school math teacher who had spent his last week correcting my dosage arithmetic under his breath. He counted down from one hundred over the course of an afternoon, unhurried, serene, like a man descending a staircase he knew by heart. At zero he said nothing at all. He just stopped, and the monitor stopped with him, and I stood there with my hand on the call button feeling as though I'd witnessed something private that I was never meant to see.

I rationalized it, of course. I'm good at rationalizing; it's practically a clinical skill. Terminal patients experience end-of-life phenomena — visions, visitations, terminal lucidity. There's literature on it. There are whole conferences. A man counting down could be reliving a countdown, a launch, a New Year's Eve, the final seconds of some race he'd run in 1962. The brain does strange things when it's shutting down. It plays its greatest hits.

Then Mrs. Okafor — no relation, though I checked, because by then I was checking things — counted down from one hundred in room 2, and flatlined at zero. Then Mr. Delgado. Then a woman named Ruth whose family swore she'd never been good with numbers, couldn't balance a checkbook to save her life, and yet there she was at two in the morning, ticking down through the nineties with the fluency of a metronome.

Here's the part I never told anyone, the part that turned rationalization into a full-time job: I always knew. Hours before. Sometimes a full day before, I'd walk past a room and feel it — a pressure behind my ears, like the moment before a storm breaks, or before your ears pop on a descent. And without fail, within a day, that patient would begin counting.

Three years of this. Three years of telling myself it was coincidence, pattern-seeking, the apophenia that haunts anyone who works around death long enough. We find faces in wallpaper and prophecies in tea leaves; finding countdowns in the dying is hardly a stretch. I wrote it off. I wrote it off so thoroughly and so professionally that I almost believed it.

Almost. Because there was one more thing, and it's the thing that kept me up at night with the lights on like a child: they never said what came after zero. No one ever counted below it. Whatever staircase they were descending, zero was the bottom, and the bottom was where they left us.

*

Mr. Whitlock was in room 6, ninety-one years old, a former ferry captain with hands like braided rope and a family scattered across three states who visited in shifts of guilt. I'd felt the pressure behind my ears that morning on my way in — that low atmospheric dread, the sense of a held breath — and I'd adjusted his care plan quietly, told the on-call doctor to expect a change, made sure his daughter's number was current. Routine. Precaution. Nothing a rational woman would call prediction.

He started counting at 4:17 in the afternoon.

"One hundred."

His daughter was in the hallway on her phone, laughing at something — a mercy, I've always thought, that the families so rarely hear it. I sat down in the vinyl chair beside the bed and took his hand. His eyes were closed. His face was calm in a way that no dying face has any right to be, calm the way deep water is calm.

"Ninety-nine."

"Hi, Captain," I said softly. "I'm here."

He didn't respond. They never do. That's another detail I filed away and refused to examine: the counters never acknowledge you. They aren't talking to you. They're talking to something, or listening to something, or — and this was the formulation I hated most, the one that arrived uninvited at 3 a.m. — *following* something. Keeping pace with it. Matching their steps to steps already taken.

"Ninety-eight."

I did the math I always did, the comforting little arithmetic of it: at roughly one number per minute, I had an hour and forty minutes. Time enough to chart, to call the daughter back in, to make the room ready. The counting was slow, ceremonial. Whatever was happening, it wasn't panic. It was liturgy.

Except tonight it wasn't slow.

"Ninety-seven. Ninety-six. Ninety-five."

My head came up. Mr. Whitlock's lips were still barely moving, but the pace had changed — quickened — like a clock running fast, like a descent turning into a fall.

"Ninety-four. Ninety-three."

I pressed the call button. In the hallway, his daughter laughed again at whatever was on her phone, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from the far side of thick glass.

"Ninety-two. Ninety-one. Ninety—"

"Captain," I said, and my voice cracked on it, which embarrassed me even then, "slow down. Take your time. There's no rush."

But of course there was a rush. There had always been a rush; I just hadn't understood what it was rushing toward. The numbers tumbled out of him now, faster and faster, ninety and eighty-nine and eighty-eight, his chest rising in shallow quick pulls, and I stood up because sitting suddenly felt obscene, because some animal part of me wanted to be upright for whatever was coming, and the monitor began to chirp in a way I didn't like, and still he counted —

"—seventy-three, seventy-two, seventy-one —"

— and I thought, absurdly, *he'll never reach zero at this rate, he'll run out of numbers before he runs out of heart,* and then I thought, colder, in a voice that didn't feel like mine: *no. He won't. Zero is where he's going. Zero is where they all go.*

"— sixty, fifty-nine, fifty-eight —"

The door swung open behind me and Priya, the evening nurse, took one look at the monitor and moved for the crash cart, and I waved her off — not yet, not yet, let him finish, God forgive me, let him finish — because some superstitious marrow-deep part of me believed that interrupting the count would be worse. Would be *rude*. And I have never in my life been so frightened of being rude to something.

"— thirty, twenty-nine —"

Faster now, the numbers blurring together, his mouth barely articulating them anymore, and the air in the room had gone cold in a way the thermostat couldn't explain, cold the way a well shaft is cold, and I remember thinking *well shaft, why did I think well shaft, why that image* —

"— ten, nine, eight —"

Priya was saying my name. Rosalind. Rosalind, his sats are dropping, do we intervene, ROSALIND —

"— four, three, two —"

I looked down at his face. His eyes were open. When they'd opened, I couldn't say. They were fixed on the ceiling above his bed, on a point slightly left of the light fixture, and there was nothing afraid in them. There was nothing in them at all except attention, total and terrible, the attention of a man arriving at a door he'd been walking toward for ninety-one years.

"— one."

A pause. One second. Two. Long enough for me to think, with a lurch of relief that lasted exactly as long as it deserved to: *maybe this time. Maybe this time he stops at one.*

"Zero," said Mr. Whitlock.

And the line went flat.

*

They gave me the letter an hour later, while I was still washing my hands. I don't know how many times I washed them. Priya had gently suggested I take the rest of the shift, and I'd agreed in a voice I didn't recognize, and then I stood at the sink in the staff bathroom scrubbing at nothing until my knuckles burned, because Mr. Whitlock's hand had gone cold in mine at the exact moment the monitor went flat, cold all at once, cold like stone, cold like water at the bottom of somewhere deep.

The envelope was waiting in my cubby. Heavy paper, cream-colored, my name typed in a font that belonged to law offices and obituaries. *Rosalind Blackwood.* Nobody calls me Blackwood. Nobody's called me Blackwood since I left Hollowmere at nineteen and swore I'd buried the name along with everything else in that town.

Inside: a notice of sale. The Blackwood house — the house I'd inherited at twenty-five and never once returned to, the house I paid taxes on out of some account I tried not to look at too closely, the house that appeared in my dreams roughly weekly wearing different wallpaper each time — had been sold. Sold at auction, apparently, for unpaid back taxes I had absolutely paid, with documentation attached proving that my payments had somehow never arrived. The new owner was listed only as a trust. The letter was very sorry. The letter required my signature on enclosed papers releasing all claim, or, failing that, legal proceedings would commence.

At the bottom of the page, beneath the signature line, someone had handwritten a single line in pencil, in a small, old-fashioned cursive that made the hair on my arms stand up:

*You should come see it before it's gone. Some things need a Blackwood.*

No name. No return address beyond a postmark I knew by heart, the way you know the shape of a scar.

I stood in the bathroom holding the letter and listened to the hum of the fluorescent lights, and behind my own pulse, faint but unmistakable, steady as a metronome descending a staircase it knew by heart, I heard — for the first time not in a dying stranger's mouth but in my own head —

"One hundred."
