## 17. The Signing

I have spent my whole adult life believing that paper is the most honest thing in the world. Paper doesn't flatter. Paper doesn't forget. Paper sits there with its little ruled lines and waits for the truth to be written on it, and if you write a lie, the lie stays visible forever, sitting on the page like a stain you can point at.

I was wrong about paper. I want that on the record. Somewhere, in whatever notebook survives me, let it be written: *Yara Eriksen was wrong about paper.*

Because at 9:47 on a Thursday morning, while my voice was still echoing through the PA system with the twenty-ninth name of thirty — *Peter Lomax* — Headmaster Gerald Halloway walked into the main hall carrying a leather folder under his arm like a man delivering a pizza.

He didn't run. That's what I'll remember longest about him, I think. Everyone else in Blackfen Secondary was coming apart at the seams — Dennis Okafor standing at the east wing doors with a fire axe he'd clearly taken from its glass case without ceremony, parents pressing against the front gates like water against a dam, three hundred children in the hall watching me at the microphone with the particular stillness of kids who know they're inside something historic and are deciding whether to be frightened or thrilled — and Halloway just *walked*, unhurried, his tie perfectly straight, his face wearing the expression of a man about to decline a second helping at dinner.

"Miss Eriksen." He set the folder on the table beside the microphone stand. "That will be quite enough of that."

"One more name," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Then we're done."

"You misunderstand the situation." He opened the folder. Inside were the papers — I knew them instantly, the way you know your own handwriting: heavy cream stock, the school crest embossed at the top, the deed of conveyance from 1974 with its annexes and amendments, and at the bottom, a signature line that had been waiting fifty years for a name. "The situation is that this institution has an asset which has been, admittedly, imperfectly managed. And I am going to close it out. Properly. With documentation."

Even now, even then, with the air in the hall gone thick as pond water, some part of me wanted to laugh. He'd brought paperwork. The man had come to end a haunting with a *signature page*.

"That's not how it works," I said. "The names have to go back first. You can't sign over what's already been returned."

"Everything," said Halloway, uncapping his fountain pen — an actual fountain pen, because of course — "can be signed over. That is rather the entire history of this country."

And he signed.

I watched it happen. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. His pen touched the line. The ink went down — a clean black stroke, the confident opening curve of a capital G. And then the ink *stopped agreeing with him*.

It crawled.

There is no other word for it. The stroke lifted off the page like a living thing, a thin black thread raising itself off the cream paper, and it began to move — back along the signature line, coiling, gathering, pulling itself into a knot that sat on the page and *pulsed*. Halloway pressed harder. More ink came out of the pen and joined the knot. The letters of his name peeled away one by one and slithered up the margin, and where they had been, the paper showed nothing at all — not blankness, but absence, a place where writing had been considered and refused.

"The names are already loose," I told him. I hadn't planned to say it aloud; it simply arrived, the way truth does when it's tired of waiting. "You can't file them now. They've been spoken. They're in three hundred pairs of ears."

Halloway looked up at me, and for one moment — one long, terrible moment — I saw past the headmaster entirely, down to whatever had been running the place for four generations, and it looked *hungry*. Not evil. Evil would have been almost restful. Hungry. The look of an institution that has been fed on schedule for fifty years and has just been handed an empty bowl.

"Then we do it the other way," he said quietly.

The lights went out.

Not flickered. Went out, all of them, hall and corridor and east wing together, and in the sudden dark every window in the building turned mirror-grey at once — I felt it rather than saw it, a pressure change, the way your ears pop before a storm — and somewhere above us, in the ceiling space, something began to move with the sound of thirty desks being dragged across a wooden floor.

The children screamed. Of course they did. I'm telling it calmly because calm is the only voice I own, but the hall became a single animal noise, and the grey light from the windows started to *breathe*, brightening and dimming in a rhythm I recognized with my whole body, because I'd seen it in a kitchen mirror for two weeks: one second behind, one second early, time itself being chewed.

"Dennis!" I shouted into the dark. "The doors!"

"I've got 'em!" His voice, from far away, from the east wing, and then the crash of the fire axe through wood, once, twice — he was breaking open the classrooms, letting the daylight in, doing the thing he should have done in 1974 when he was nine years old and someone told him to run.

But the room wasn't interested in the classrooms anymore. The room was interested in the hall. In three hundred children. In *volume*. It had lived for half a century on one stolen class, rationed, hoarded, and now that its meal was escaping it intended to eat the entire school in one sitting, and I understood — standing at that microphone in the breathing dark — that names alone weren't going to stop it. The names could starve it. But starving things get desperate, and desperate things grab.

Unless you give them something else to hold.

I have thought about this next part a great deal since, and I still can't tell you whether it was courage or arithmetic. Probably both. Probably they were always the same thing.

I picked up the leather folder. The papers inside were warm — body-warm, though no body had touched them in decades — and the signature line was empty again, patient, the knot of rejected ink dissolved back into nothing. It didn't need Halloway. It had never needed Halloway specifically. It needed *a resident*. Someone living, willing, documented. Someone who would trade their outside for the room's inside, permanently, so the room could finally die with its last occupant accounted for.

Aldridge had done it in 1998 to keep it from spreading. She'd traded herself for containment.

I could do it for release.

My sister's name was the thirtieth name. I hadn't read it yet. I'd saved it — deliberately or cowardly, I genuinely don't know — because saying it aloud meant admitting her into this story, and for eleven years I had kept her in a separate archive, a private grief, laminated against exactly this kind of contamination. Marian Eriksen. Taken in 1984, aged nine, from a street she'd walked a thousand times, while I was at home doing homework ten feet away. The room had taken one child per decade, and 1984 had been its decade, and the intake list in the 1974 file had her name on it in a clerk's careful hand, filed between a boy from the estate and a girl nobody ever reported missing.

I lifted the microphone. The feedback squealed and died.

"Marian Eriksen," I said.

Saying it did something to the room. I felt the dark flinch — actually flinch, like a hand pulled back from a stove — because that name had never been spoken in this building, had been carried in by accident inside a grieving girl's schoolbag fifty years late, and the room recognized it the way you recognize a debt collector's knock.

Then I picked up the folder, and I walked toward the east wing.

* * *

The corridor to Room 4 was full of grey light that moved wrong. Every window along it had become a mirror, and every mirror showed the same thing: the classroom, 1974, thirty children at their desks, and the door standing open — which it had never done before, not in any reflection, not in any dream. It was open the way a mouth opens. It was open the way a trap isn't a trap anymore but an invitation.

Behind me, in the hall, I could hear Dennis shouting my name, and Halloway shouting something else — threats, probably, or terms; men like him only have the one vocabulary — and I kept walking, because if I stopped walking I would start thinking, and thinking was a luxury for people who weren't holding the last name in the ledger.

The room was cold in the way that photographs are cold. I crossed the threshold and the door closed behind me with the soft finality of a filing cabinet drawer, and there they all were: the class of 1974, unchanged, unaged, thirty children sitting at desks arranged for thirty, and Miss Aldridge standing at the board with chalk dust on her fingers and fifty years of held breath in her shoulders.

"You shouldn't have come in," Aldridge said. Her voice was rusty, a hinge unused. "The papers—"

"Are in my hand." I set the folder on the teacher's desk. Outside the windows — real windows, here, showing a June day in 1974 that had never ended — the fire alarm was ringing, ringing, ringing, the drill that never stopped. "One signature. Me. And everyone walks out."

"No." It wasn't Aldridge who said it. It came from everywhere, from the walls, from the wood of the desks, from the chalkboard where half-erased arithmetic dated itself forever. The room's voice was the sound a building makes settling, stretched into speech. *Resident. Sign. Then they go.*

"And if I don't?"

Silence. The particular silence of a thing recalculating.

"If I don't sign," I said, "you take the school. Three hundred children. You'll choke on them, but you'll try, because you're starving and starving things don't do sums. So here's my counteroffer, and I want it noted that I learned negotiating from fifty years of institutional paperwork." I took out my pen — my own pen, the one I'd documented everything with since I was nineteen years old and lost my sister and decided that writing things down was the only prayer that worked. "I sign as resident. The class of 1974 walks out. All thirty. Including the one you took in 1984. That's the deal. Take it or spend eternity digesting a secondary school."

The room considered. I felt it considering — a pressure behind my eyes, a taste of chalk and smoke. And then, slowly, the desks began to move. Sliding aside. Making an aisle. From the front row to the door.

An aisle toward freedom, or an aisle toward the signing desk. Even now I couldn't swear which. I walked it anyway.

I reached the teacher's desk. I opened the folder. The signature line waited, and beneath it, in small print I had somehow never noticed in any of the copies I'd studied, a line of text had appeared, fresh as wet paint:

*In consideration of the foregoing, the undersigned agrees to assume residency in perpetuity, in exchange for the conveyance and return of all listed parties.*

All listed parties. Thirty names. Thirty-one, counting the new line the room had added at the bottom of the ledger — my line, pre-filled, *Yara A. Eriksen*, waiting only for the ink.

I put the pen to the paper.

And a small hand closed around mine.

* * *

Ida Voss was the smallest child in the class and the oldest-seeming, and her hand around my wrist felt like a bird's foot and a vice at the same time. She was looking up at me with eyes that had watched fifty Junes pass through a window that never changed, and she said, in a voice like dry paper:

"Don't."

"It's the only way," I said. "Someone has to stay. That's the rule. Your rule."

"Our rule," Ida said, "was made by the room. We've been following the room's rules for fifty years and look where it got us." She turned to the class. "Everyone heard the names? All of them?"

Thirty voices, in ragged unison: *"Yes, Ida."*

"Then we're already out. Names spoken are names returned. She read us to the whole school. There's nothing left to sign over except her, and we are not—" and here her small voice cracked, fifty years old and nine years old at once, "—we are not *trading*."

The room shuddered. The windows went dark. The aisle of desks began, very slowly, to slide shut.

And Ida Voss, smallest and oldest, did the bravest thing I have ever witnessed, and I include everything that came after. She climbed onto a chair, and then onto the teacher's desk, and she put her hand flat on the deed of conveyance, on the signature line, on my unfinished name, and she said to the room, in the tone of a nine-year-old informing a substitute teacher that the real teacher always lets them leave five minutes early:

"We refuse the trade. We *refuse* it. You can't close the file. Nobody's signing. And you can't keep us, because somebody came back for us — she read our *names* — and the rule underneath your rule, the one you hoped nobody would ever find out, is that you only ever held us because *no one came back*."

The room went absolutely still.

"Someone came back," Ida repeated. She looked at me. "Didn't she."

Outside the windows, the endless June of 1974 flickered — and for the first time in fifty years, the fire alarm stopped ringing.

The silence it left behind was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

Ida took her hand off the papers, took my hand instead, and nodded toward the door, which was standing open onto a corridor that no longer looked like 1974 at all — it looked like Thursday morning, grey English daylight, the sound of three hundred children and their parents beyond it.

"Walk," she said. "We all walk. That's the whole trick. That was always the whole trick."

But behind us, at the board, Miss Aldridge had not moved. She stood with the chalk in her hand, looking at the door, at the daylight, at the fifty years she'd paid to hold it shut — and I saw her make a decision, and I opened my mouth to argue, and that is when the room, cheated of its signature, enraged past reason or paperwork, began to bring the whole building down around us, and the last thing I saw before the mirrors shattered outward, one second early, was Marion Aldridge turning back to the blackboard and picking up the chalk.
