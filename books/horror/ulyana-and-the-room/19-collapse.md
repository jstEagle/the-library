## 19. Collapse

The last name came out of me like a tooth.

I'd read twenty-nine of them over the PA, and the room had convulsed with each one — I felt it through the soles of my shoes, through the floor, through the fillings in my teeth, a shudder running through the east wing like a dog shaking off water. But the thirtieth name was the one I'd been carrying since the assembly began. The one I'd written on my palm in biro that morning, because I didn't trust paper anymore, and didn't entirely trust my own hands either.

"Margaret Eriksen," I said.

My sister's name. My sister's name, spoken aloud in a school corridor for the first time in — I did the arithmetic as the word left my mouth, the way I do, the way documentation people do — thirty-one years. Not since my mother had said it into a police tape recorder in 1994, in a voice I can still hear, though I have a horrible suspicion I won't be able to for much longer.

The room folded.

I don't have a better verb. I've spent three weeks trying to find one, and *folded* is what my notebook says, in my handwriting, underlined twice, so folded it is. The corridor shortened. That's the only way I can describe it. The east wing, which I had walked every morning for three weeks — forty-two paces from the staff room door to Room 4, I had counted, because of course I had — contracted around me the way a fist contracts, and the walls came in not as walls but as *paper*, as though the whole wing had been a document all along and someone was closing it.

The lights went amber. Then brown. Then the particular colour of old varnish.

And through the closing gap I saw Room 4, and Room 4 saw me.

* * *

You should understand what it looked like from inside, because I was inside, and because nobody else who was inside is available for comment.

The desks were still in their rows, thirty of them, and the children were still at the desks, but they were *looser* now. That's the word. For fifty years they'd been held the way specimens are held — pinned, pressed, arranged — and now, with each name read out in the daylight, something had unclenched. Ida Voss was standing. She was the smallest of them and the oldest-seeming, a nine-year-old with the posture of a woman who has waited out a siege, and when the room began to fold she didn't flinch. She looked at the closing walls the way you look at a bill you've been expecting.

"It's working," she said. "Don't stop talking."

"I'm not—" The corridor had folded me nearly to the doorway. "I've said them all. That was the last one."

"Not all." Ida's eyes went to the board, where the woman — Miss Aldridge, though she was younger than me now, or older, the room had never settled on which — stood with her hand still resting on the chalk ledge. "Say hers again. The room keeps what it's given last."

I said it. Margaret Eriksen. And the second time it came out steadier, and the room folded harder, and somewhere behind me — outside, in the real world, in the corridor that still technically contained my body — I heard glass begin to think about breaking.

* * *

Aldridge came toward me through the folding room the way people walk in dreams, each stride covering more ground than it should.

"You understand what happens now," she said. Not a question. She'd had twenty-seven years to rehearse the sentence.

"The papers are void. The names are returned. The room starves." I had it all by heart. I have everything by heart; that's the whole tragedy of me.

"That's the mechanism." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone doing arithmetic they hate. "The mechanism isn't the same as the exit. A room like this doesn't die clean, Miss Dunmore. It dies the way a held breath dies. And something has to be holding the door when it does, or it takes the corridor with it. The school. The children outside. Everyone who came running when you started reading."

Behind her, the thirty desks were emptying. The children were standing, one by one, the way a congregation stands, and they were looking at the doorway, and the doorway was looking at me, and I want to be honest in this account because honesty is the only thing I have left that the room hasn't eaten: for one full second I wanted to stay.

Not out of fear. Out of *belonging*. That's the thing nobody warns you about. The room doesn't hold you with terror. It holds you with the terrible comfort of a place where every name is known, where the register is never wrong, where thirty children turn and look at you when you arrive as though you were expected, as though you were *on the list*. I had spent my whole adult life documenting things because once, when it mattered, no one had documented my sister. And here was a room that documented perfectly. Forever. I understood, in that second, why Aldridge had signed.

"Yara." Ida's hand found mine. Small, cold, absolutely certain. "You're not on the list. That's the whole point of you."

* * *

Aldridge straightened her cardigan. I will never forget that. The room was collapsing around us, the walls were varnish and the light was 1974, and the woman straightened her cardigan, because she had been a teacher for thirty years and some reflexes outlive everything.

"I signed in 1998 to keep it contained," she said. "It was the only lever I had. One resident, sealed in, and the room stops spreading. That was the deal. The room has always needed a keeper more than it needs children — the children are just what it eats. The keeper is what it *is*." She looked around at her classroom with an expression I can only call professional. "And now the deal's terms are void. The names are returned. My signature bought nothing. Which means—" and here her voice did something I wasn't expecting, something warm, something almost relieved, "—which means I'm free to do the job properly. Not as payment. As choice."

"You don't have to," I said. And I meant it, and I also knew, even as I said it, that it wasn't true, that it was the kind of thing you say at doors.

"Miss Dunmore." She almost laughed. "I have been the last resident of this room for twenty-seven years. Do you know what happens to a keeper when the room dies and no one holds the door? Nothing happens to her. That's the problem. She doesn't get out. She gets *archived*. Filed. Kept, in the dark, in the thing she kept." She stepped backward, toward the board, toward the centre of the folding, and the room gathered around her like a coat. "But if someone holds the door from the inside while it closes — if someone stands in the doorway and keeps it shut while it starves — then it ends. All the way. And the one holding it goes with it. Quickly. Cleanly. As a person, not a filing cabinet."

"There has to be another—"

"There's always another form," she said. "That's what this place ran on. Forms. Signatures. Someone always willing to put their name at the bottom." She looked at me over the heads of thirty children who were, even now, moving toward the door I was standing in, moving toward the daylight, moving toward fifty years of being missed. "You refused to sign. Do you know how long it's been since anyone refused? Since anyone chose the harder thing over the efficient thing? Let me have this. Let me be the one efficient thing this room ever got, and let it be the thing that kills it."

I said her name. Her first name — Enid, which I had learned from the 1998 staff photograph, which I had documented, which was mine to say.

"Enid Aldridge."

"Out," she said. "Take them out. And Miss Dunmore — the mirrors. When the room goes, the mirrors go. They've been a second behind all this time. At the end, they'll be a second *early*. Don't flinch. It isn't aimed at you."

* * *

I walked thirty children out of Room 4.

I want that sentence to sit on the page exactly as plain as it is, because no sentence I have ever written — and I have written a great many, in binders, in notebooks, in the margins of other people's official records — will ever be as plain or as true. I walked them out, single file, because that is how children leave a classroom, and Ida Voss came last with her hand in mine, and the corridor unfolded ahead of us like a hand opening, and the daylight in it was the ordinary grey of a Blackfen Tuesday, and it was the most beautiful light I have ever seen.

Behind us, Aldridge stood in the doorway of Room 4, facing inward.

She didn't say goodbye. She said, "Register's complete," which is a teacher's goodbye, and the only kind she had left.

Then the door — the actual door, the one stencilled 4 over 3B, the one that had been two doors and one door and no door for fifty years — closed, and I heard her weight come against it from the other side, and I understood that I was hearing the last sound Enid Aldridge ever intended to make: a woman holding a door shut against a dying dark, with her shoulder, with her whole professional life, with her name.

* * *

The mirrors went one second early.

I was in the corridor, thirty children around me, teachers running toward us from the hall, Dennis Okafor standing at the east wing's end with the fire doors open and daylight behind him and tears on a face that had spent forty years learning not to cry on school property. The windows of the east wing — and there were mirrors there, I'd never noticed them properly, mirrors in the display cases, mirrors in the old toilet corridor, a whole nervous system of glass running through the wing — the mirrors all turned at once.

Not toward us. Toward the room.

And then, one full second before it happened, they showed it happening: the shatter, the collapse, the wing exhaling fifty years of held breath. I saw the glass break before it broke. I saw Aldridge's silhouette in the last of it, upright, shoulder to the door, and I saw her nod — at me, at Ida, at the daylight, at the record — and then the mirrors went, all together, outward, away from the room, away from us, a thousand fragments flying toward the windows like iron filings toward a magnet, and the sound was not a crash.

It was a bell. Fifty years late for a fire drill that had never ended, the east wing rang like a bell, and then it was only glass on linoleum, and silence, and thirty children standing in ordinary light.

The wing didn't fall down. That's the anticlimax nobody prepares you for. The walls stayed up. The lights came back to their proper white. Somewhere a radiator, freed from whatever had been using it as a throat, clanked and hissed and began, very quietly, to do its job. Room 4, when I looked at it — and I looked, I documented, I will always look — was a classroom. Just a classroom. Chalk dust. A board with half-erased arithmetic dated June 1974, and beneath it, in a hand I recognised from a 1998 staff photograph, four words:

*All present. All accounted.*

The desks were empty, and for the first time in fifty years, empty was all they were.

* * *

Ida Voss stood in the corridor, in the daylight, and did not know what to do with her hands.

"They'll be waiting for us," she said. Not the children behind her — the other ones, the ones the names had been returned to, the ones who had been missing since 1974 and were, as of that morning, missing in a completely different way. "Our parents. However long it took them. They'll have waited."

"Yes," I said.

"You came back for us." She said it like she was checking the arithmetic. Like she still didn't quite believe the sum. "Nobody came back. That was the whole thing. That's why the room could hold us. And then you—" She stopped. Nine years old, fifty years old, both at once. "You read us out loud. In front of everyone. Do you know how loud that was? From in there? It was like the sun."

I knelt down to her level, because there are things you shouldn't say standing over a child, even a child who has outwaited half a century.

"You're going to go out those fire doors," I said, "and there are people out there — police, parents, a man named Dennis who has been sorry since 1974 — and they are going to know your name. All of them. It's in the newspaper already. I made sure."

"Ida Voss," she said, trying it out in the open air.

"Ida Voss," I agreed. "Year Six, June 1974. Good at fractions. Kept a diary."

Her face did something at that — *kept a diary* — a small, astonished crack of light. "You read it?"

"I'm going to catalogue it," I said. "Every page. In the archive. So that the record of you is the one *you* wrote, and no one else's version ever gets filed over it again."

She considered this with the gravity of a headmistress, and then she took my hand, and she led her class out into the Tuesday.

* * *

I stayed in the corridor while they went. Someone had to witness it. That is my whole vocation, in the end — someone has to witness it, or it didn't happen — and so I stood with my notebook and I documented thirty children walking out of a wall into daylight, and my hand shook so badly that half the entry is illegible, and I have decided not to correct it. Some records should shake.

The last thing, the thing that propels me to write this even now, with my hands still unsteady, is small.

The glass. The shattered mirrors. Dennis and I swept it that evening, because the caretaker in him couldn't leave it overnight and the witness in me couldn't leave him to sweep it alone. And among the fragments — in the last dustpan, from the display case nearest Room 4 — there was one piece of mirror that hadn't shattered outward like the rest.

It had shattered inward. Toward the room. And on its silvered face, one second early, one second late, I don't know anymore — there was a reflection that had no one standing in front of it.

A woman. Cardigan. Shoulder to a door. And beside her, smaller, holding her hand, a girl of about nine with my sister's face, looking out at me from inside a mirror that was already broken, and mouthing — clearly, patiently, the way you mouth words to someone across a noisy room — two words I will spend the rest of my life documenting, and the rest of this account explaining:

*Thank you.*
