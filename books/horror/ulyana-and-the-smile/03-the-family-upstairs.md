## 3. The Family Upstairs

Cyrus had planned to be gone before anyone came home.

That had been the whole architecture of the morning: arrive at nine, walk the exterior, shoot the elevations, log the foundation cracks, and be back in his truck with the engine warm by eleven. A condemned house did not require an interior inspection to condemn. The county's paperwork only needed his signature and his photographs, and neither of those required him to climb the porch steps of 14 Marrow Lane and put his hand on a door he had last touched at nine years old, in bare feet, in October, running from something he had never been able to name in daylight.

But the county had sent the notice of inspection to the property address, not his office, and the notice had been taped to the front door with a strip of yellow plastic that had yellowed further in the weather, and someone — someone with small, careful handwriting — had added in ballpoint pen beneath the county stamp: *We still live here. Please knock.*

He should have driven away. He told himself, standing in the yard with his clipboard against his chest like a shield, that a surveyor's job was the structure, not the occupants. The structure was condemned. The occupants would be notified through proper channels. The ballpoint pen was not his problem.

He knocked anyway.

The door opened on the second knock, and the smell hit him first — not rot, not mildew, though the house had both in abundance, but frying plantain and something spiced and warm, the smell of a home being made inside a place that had never once, in all the years he had lived in it, smelled like one. A woman stood in the doorway, maybe forty, with a headwrap the color of persimmons and the tired, appraising eyes of someone who had learned not to open doors without looking through the peephole first.

"Yes?"

"Ma'am. My name is Cyrus Ishikawa. I'm with the county — I'm the surveyor assigned to this property." He held up the clipboard, the laminated badge, the whole laminated apparatus of his legitimacy. "I'm afraid there's been some confusion. This house has been scheduled for condemnation."

The woman's face did something complicated. It went still first, the way water goes still before it freezes, and then it moved through disbelief and landed somewhere that looked, horribly, like recognition. Not recognition of him. Recognition of the sentence itself, as though she had been waiting for someone to say it.

"I knew it," she said quietly. "Grace Okonkwo." She put out her hand, and he shook it, and her grip was strong and dry. "I knew it the day we moved in. Tunde said, Grace, you got this house for half what it should cost, and I said, Tunde, that is exactly why I don't trust it."

"May I come in? Just to — I can explain the timeline. What happens next."

He heard himself say it in his professional voice, the one he used for tearful homeowners and angry ones, the voice that was calm because it was empty. And he heard, underneath it, the other voice, the boy's voice, saying *don't go in, don't go in, you swore you'd never go in* — and he stepped across the threshold anyway, because thirty years of running had taught him that the only thing worse than going in was standing on the porch with the door open and the dark of the foyer looking back at him like a held breath.

* * *

The house had been changed. That was the first thing. Someone had loved it back into a shape it had never held when he was a boy: the walls of the front room painted a deep warm green, photographs on the mantel, a couch with a crocheted throw, a child's drawing taped to the refrigerator visible through the kitchen doorway. His mother's house had been all brown and shadow and the smell of dust and something faintly sweet underneath, like fruit going bad in a drawer no one opened. This was the same floor plan. He could feel it under the paint — the same load-bearing walls, the same sightline from the front room to the kitchen, the same stairs going up with the fourth step that had always creaked. But it was like seeing a scarred face made up for a wedding. The damage was still there. It had just been asked, politely, to wait in the other room.

Grace brought him tea he didn't want and he held it anyway, because refusing felt dangerous in a way he couldn't justify. Her husband, Tunde, came down the stairs — a big man, soft-spoken, with reading glasses pushed up into his hair and the careful movements of someone who worked with his hands, an engineer maybe, or a machinist. He shook Cyrus's hand with both of his.

"So the county," Tunde said. "The county wants the house."

"The county has declared it structurally unsound. There's foundation settlement, and — " Cyrus heard himself reciting the report he hadn't finished writing, the cracks he'd photographed that morning, the east wall leaning two degrees off plumb. "Honestly, sir, the house should not be occupied. I'm sorry. I know that's not what you want to hear. There's a relocation process. It's slow, and it's not fair, and it's the only thing I can offer you."

Grace was watching him with those appraising eyes. "You said *assigned.* You're assigned to this house. Have you been here before?"

The question went through him like a splinter finding the old channel.

"No," he said.

It was not even a lie he had to construct. It was the oldest lie he owned, worn smooth as river glass from thirty years of handling. *Have you lived around here? No. Do you know this street? No. Did you used to — no.* He had said it to teachers, to girlfriends, to a therapist in his twenties who had finally stopped asking and started just looking at him with a particular patient sorrow.

"You grew up here," Grace said.

The tea trembled in its saucer. He set it down on the side table with exaggerated care, a man performing calmness for an audience of one.

"What makes you say that?"

"You looked at the stairs when you came in. Not at us. At the stairs. The way you look at something you used to trip on." She folded her hands. "My mother was like that with her mother's house. Some houses get into the body. You can't fake that look."

Tunde laughed, a warm rumble, and clapped Cyrus on the shoulder. "She does this. She reads people like blueprints. Ignore her, she's always right, that's the problem." But he was looking at Cyrus too now, and there was something in the look that wasn't quite laughter. "You want to tell us what's wrong with the house? The real thing? Grace says it hums at night. I told her it's the old wiring. But you've been in the walls today. What did you find?"

Wiring, Cyrus wanted to say. Settlement. Moisture intrusion. He wanted to hand them the safe vocabulary, the words with no teeth in them.

What he said was: "How long have you lived here?"

"Fourteen months," Grace said.

"And have you — " He stopped. Started again. "Do the rooms stay where you put them?"

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Tunde's smile stayed on his face but stopped being connected to his eyes. Grace set down her own cup.

"Once," she said slowly, "I painted the hallway. Took me a weekend. Came back Monday after work and the paint was — it wasn't gone. It was just on the wrong walls. Like the hallway had turned around while I was gone and I'd painted it facing backward. I thought I was losing my mind. Tunde said I was tired." She looked at her husband. "I wasn't tired, Tunde."

"I know," Tunde said quietly. And Cyrus understood, with a lurch, that this was not a new conversation between them. That they had been having it in low voices for fourteen months, in bed, in the car, in the kitchen after the child was asleep, and that the county man sitting in their front room was the first outside witness either of them had dared to show the evidence to.

"The house is old," Cyrus said. "Old houses shift."

"Old houses don't shift their hallways," Grace said.

And then, from the stairs, a voice — small, bright, entirely unafraid:

"Is that the house man?"

A girl came down the steps the way children come down stairs when they have decided stairs are beneath them, which is to say sideways and too fast, one hand on the rail, seven or eight years old, in a yellow dress with a crooked hem and a bandage on one knee decorated with cartoon sharks. She stopped two steps from the bottom and looked at Cyrus with the frank, total attention that only children and cats can manage.

"This is Amara," Grace said. "Amara, this is Mr. Ishikawa. He works for the county."

"You're the house man," Amara said again, with the satisfaction of a correct identification. Then, without any transition at all: "Did you count the doors?"

Cyrus felt the floor tilt. Not literally — the floor was level, he had shot it that morning, he had the numbers — but something behind his eyes listed sideways, the way a picture goes crooked on a wall.

"Why do you ask that?" he said. His voice came out strange. Flat.

"Because there's fourteen," Amara said. "I counted. Mommy says there's eight rooms so there can't be fourteen doors but I counted them twice and it's fourteen both times." She held up her hands, all ten fingers, and then folded two down and held up the remainder, eight, as if demonstrating the mathematics of the adult objection. "Mommy says I counted wrong. But I don't count wrong. I'm very good at counting."

"She is," Grace said, and there was a thread of something in her voice — not fear, not yet, but the raw edge of it, the place where a mother's rationality starts to fray. "She is very good at counting. It's just a game she plays. Kids play games."

"Who taught you the game?" Cyrus asked.

He had to know. He had to hear her say it, or hear her say anything else, because the alternative was the memory rising in him unbidden: himself at six, at seven, at eight, standing in this same foyer with his eyes closed, counting aloud in a whisper, *one door, two door, three door,* because a voice had told him — a kind voice, a patient voice, a voice that smiled so wide he could hear the smile in it — *that's it, Cyrus, you're so clever, count them every day and the house will know you know, and it only takes the ones who don't know.*

Nobody had taught him. That was the thing he had never been able to explain to anyone. Nobody had taught him and yet he had known the rules perfectly, completely, as though they had been written somewhere inside him before he was born.

"A friend," Amara said.

"Which friend?"

"My porch friend."

Grace stood up. It was not an abrupt motion; it was the motion of a woman who had rehearsed standing up in exactly this moment. "Amara, go wash your hands for lunch, sweetheart."

"I already washed."

"Wash them again. Go on."

Amara went, thudding up the stairs two at a time, and paused halfway, and turned back, and looked at Cyrus over the banister with an expression far too old for the yellow dress and the shark bandage.

"She's still there," Amara said. "Your porch friend. She told me you'd come back."

* * *

The kitchen was suddenly very loud with the absence of sound. Grace had gone white in a way that makeup and daylight couldn't explain. Tunde had gone very still, the stillness of a man listening to a sound in the night and deciding whether to wake his wife.

Cyrus found that he was standing. He had no memory of standing.

"What," he said, and had to start again. "What did she say. Exactly. Her words."

"Amara — " Grace began.

"Her *words,*" Cyrus said, and heard his own voice crack like a boy's, and did not care. "Please. I need — please."

Grace sat back down slowly. She looked at her husband, and some whole silent marriage passed between them, and then she looked at Cyrus and said, in the voice of a woman reciting something she had tried very hard to forget:

"She said — Amara came in about two months ago, at bedtime, and she said, 'Mommy, the porch friend says the man who used to live here is coming back, and when he comes, everything will be decided.' And I said, what porch friend, and she said, 'the lady on the steps who smiles, she's been there since before we moved in, she says she's been waiting a very long time for the counting to finish.'" Grace's hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as though the table might otherwise float away. "I told her she dreamed it. I told her — she's a child, she has an imagination, every child has a — "

"Every child has an imaginary friend," Cyrus said.

"Yes."

"Mine did too."

The sentence fell into the room like a stone into a well. He heard it go all the way down.

Upstairs, faintly, through the ceiling, he could hear Amara in the bathroom, water running, and — he strained, he would swear for the rest of his life he heard it — a small clear voice counting, unhurried, contented, safe as a lullaby:

*"...eleven doors, twelve doors, thirteen doors..."*

A pause.

*"...fourteen doors. All done."*

Cyrus was at the bottom of the stairs before he knew he'd moved. Grace was beside him. Tunde was behind them both, and none of them spoke, because what was there to say — *don't count, sweetheart, stop counting* — when counting was the only thing, the one thing, the single fragile ritual that had kept a nine-year-old boy alive in this house thirty years ago, and none of them knew yet which way the arithmetic ran.

Amara came out of the bathroom drying her hands on her dress. She looked up at the three adults crowded at the foot of the stairs, and she smiled — a bright, easy, childish smile — and Cyrus felt his stomach drop through the floorboards, through the foundation, down into the settling dark, because he knew that smile. He had worn that smile. It was the smile you learned in this house when you figured out that the safest thing to be was the child who seemed fine.

"Can the house man stay for lunch?" Amara asked her mother.

And Cyrus, who had a truck full of equipment and a report to file and thirty years of good reasons never to be in this house after the light started going, heard himself say:

"I'd like that very much."

He stayed through lunch. He stayed through the afternoon, asking questions dressed as small talk, learning that the Okonkwos had moved from Lagos by way of Houston, that Tunde worked nights at the turbine plant, that Grace taught third grade, that Amara liked sharks and counting and drawing. Especially drawing. She drew him a picture while he watched: a house with too many doors, and on the porch, in crayon, a lady in a dress.

The lady had no eyes. Just a smile, drawn in a single wide stroke, curving almost to the edges of the page.

"She's pretty," Amara said. "She's the nicest friend I ever had. She says she knew you."

Outside, the light was beginning to fail. The shadows came off the treeline at the back of the lot and crawled up the lawn the way they always had, the way he had watched them from this same window as a boy, and Cyrus understood with a calm, terrible clarity that he was not going to make it back to his truck before dark — that he had known it since the moment he knocked, that he had perhaps known it since the ballpoint note on the door, since the day he left, since the day he was born.

He was going to have to walk out onto that porch at dusk.

And she was going to be there.
