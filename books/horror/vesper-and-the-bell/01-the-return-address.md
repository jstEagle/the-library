## 1. The Return Address

I am writing this on the 14th of March, in the front room of the house at 11 Ossuary Lane — the name is not a joke, though it should be; the lane was named for a churchyard that hasn't stood since before the war — and I am writing it because I have always believed that what is written down cannot be argued with later. Facts. Measurements. Times. The temperature of grief. If I record everything now, while it is still happening, then no one — including me — will be able to tell me afterward that it went some other way.

So. The facts.

I arrived at 4:47 in the afternoon. The taxi from the station cost £38.40 and the driver talked the entire way about his daughter's wedding. I remember this because I remember everything today with an awful, granular clarity, as if my mind has decided that if it cannot hold one thing together it will hold all things together instead. He asked me if I was going to a wedding or a funeral and I said funeral and he said, "Ah," and then kept talking anyway, which is what people do. Grief makes them nervous. They fill the silence around you like sandbags.

The house looked smaller than I remembered. That's the standard observation, I know — everyone says it, everyone writes it in their notebooks — but it deserves recording because it wasn't only smaller. It was *tilted*. Not visibly. Nothing you could photograph. But standing at the gate, looking up at it, I had the distinct sensation that the house was leaning toward me by some fraction of a degree, the way a listener leans in when they've been waiting for someone.

It is a narrow Victorian terrace, three storeys plus attic, brick gone the color of wet ash. My grandmother built nothing and owned everything; the house came down through her side, through her mother, through whoever came before, back past the point where anyone kept records. There are seven windows across the front if you count the attic dormer, and I counted them twice because the first time I got six and could not account for the error. Seven. The middle window on the second floor — Vesper's room — was dark, but the curtain moved when I watched it. A draught, obviously. Old houses breathe. I wrote *draught* in the notebook and underlined it twice, which is what I do when I don't believe myself.

The garden gate sticks. It has stuck since I was nine years old; you have to lift it as you push. It did not stick today. It swung open at a touch, smooth and oiled, as if someone had been through it recently and often.

I noted the time: 5:02 p.m.

* * *

My mother opened the door before I knocked. She was standing in the hall with her hand already on the latch, and either she heard the taxi or she felt me coming up the path, or — I record this without interpretation — she simply knew. Ada Oyelaran is sixty-one years old and she has aged ten years in the eleven days since the phone call. Her hair, which she has kept dyed a stubborn black for as long as I can remember, was white at the roots and unwashed. She wore a cardigan over a nightdress. It was five in the afternoon.

She looked at me for a long moment. Too long. Three seconds, four. I know because I counted, because counting is what I do instead of feeling things at their full size.

Then she said, "Vesper."

Not *Casper*. Not *you're here*. Not *my son*. She said my sister's name, in the flat tone of someone reading it off an envelope, and she stepped aside to let me in.

"Ma," I said. "It's Casper."

She didn't correct herself. That's the fact I keep circling back to, the one I've written out three times in different inks trying to make it behave. She didn't say *oh, God, of course, forgive me, you look so like her.* She didn't do any of the things a grieving mother does when she misnames her surviving child. She just closed the door behind me and said, "You'll want to see the arrangements," and walked into the kitchen, and I stood in the hallway smelling camphor and cut flowers and something underneath both — something mineral, cold, like the air off a well — and I wrote in my notebook, standing up, in handwriting I barely recognize as mine:

*5:06 p.m. Mother called me Vesper. Did not correct.*

Underneath it, after a pause long enough that the ink dried between strokes:

*Or did not notice.*

* * *

The house smells the way it has always smelled, which is to say it smells like every house that has been lived in by the same family for too long: dust and old cooking and furniture polish and, beneath it, the particular sweetness of paper rotting slowly inside closed rooms. But there is a new note in it now. Lilies, yes — the funeral flowers arrived ahead of me, white lilies banked on every surface, so many of them the air is thick enough to chew. And under the lilies, that cold mineral smell. I followed it once, down the hall, and it was strongest near the door to the cellar, which was locked, which is normal, which has been locked my whole life.

The kitchen is where my mother holds court, and it was where the funeral director was waiting.

His name was Mr. Adeyemi — I got it from the card he left propped against the teapot, because he never actually introduced himself aloud. A small man, gray-suited, with the careful stillness of someone who has learned to move slowly around the bereaved. He stood when I came in, offered his hand, and I took it, and his palm was damp and very cold.

"Casper," my mother said, "this is the gentleman from Restful Acres."

"Mr. Ishikawa," he said. "We spoke on the phone."

"We didn't," I said. "I spoke with a woman. Ms. Ferrers?"

"Yes." He sat back down. "Ms. Ferrers handles the office. I handle the—" and here he paused, and I watched him choose a word and reject two others, "—the preparation."

I sat down opposite him. My mother stayed standing at the counter with her back to us both, doing something to a kettle that required no attention whatsoever.

"I'd like to see her," I said.

The kettle clicked. Mr. Adeyemi looked at the table.

"The viewing hours are tomorrow," he said. "Ten until noon. Family first, naturally."

"That's not what I asked. I'd like to see her now. Tonight. Before anyone else."

"It's not customary—"

"She's my twin."

He looked up then, briefly, and I want to be precise about what I saw in his face, because I have spent the evening turning it over and I want it fixed on the page before memory sands it smooth. What I saw was not sympathy. Sympathy I would have understood. What I saw was the expression of a man who has opened a door he expected to be one thing and found it another — a flicker, quickly shut, of something like recognition. Or dread. Or both wearing each other's coats.

"The coffin is sealed," he said.

"Sealed." I let the word sit there. "Why is it sealed? She died eleven days ago. What's the delay, and why is the coffin sealed?"

"There were... complications regarding the certification," he said. "The coroner released her late. We've had less time than we'd like." His eyes went to my mother's back, then away. "Sealing is standard practice in these cases. For dignity."

"For dignity," I repeated.

"For everyone," he said, and I heard the correction land somewhere underneath the sentence, aimed at me, and I did not understand it yet, and I have written it down exactly as I heard it because I suspect I will need it later.

My mother turned from the counter. "Casper. Let the man do his work."

"He hasn't done any work," I said. "There's no body laid out, there's no register, there's nothing in the front room but lilies. Where is she, Ma? Where is she actually?"

"In the cool room at Restful Acres," Mr. Adeyemi said, too quickly. "She'll be here tomorrow morning at nine. The service is at eleven."

I looked at my mother. She looked at the kettle. The kettle, having finished boiling some minutes ago, sat there steaming gently, ignored, and nobody made tea, and the silence stretched until Mr. Adeyemi gathered his card and his hat and said he would see us tomorrow, and let himself out, and I noticed — I noted, 5:31 p.m. — that he did not say goodbye to my mother, and she did not say goodbye to him, and the door closed behind him with the softness of a door being closed by someone who does not want to wake anything.

* * *

I took the front room. It's the room I always take, the one with the good bed and the window onto the street, and Vesper used to complain that I got it because I was born eleven minutes earlier and claimed seniority. Eleven minutes. Our whole lives measured in that gap. She was born second and she died first, and I keep thinking there is an arithmetic in that that someone, somewhere, has already solved.

I unpacked nothing. I sat on the bed with my notebook and recorded the room:

Ceiling rose cracked in the shape of a river delta. Wallpaper — the same paper, God help us, the same yellowed roses, unfaded in a rectangle behind where the wardrobe stands, so the wardrobe has not moved in thirty years. Carpet worn pale in a path from door to window. On the windowsill, a ring-mark from a cup, and beside it, faint, in pencil on the wood, a row of tally marks at child height: four verticals and a diagonal, four verticals and a diagonal, over and over, maybe forty sets, carved by some child counting days or counting something else. I don't remember making them. I don't remember Vesper making them. But they are at exactly my height at age eight, and the pencil pressure is mine — I know my own hand the way you know your own walk.

I sat with that for a while.

Then I went downstairs to ask my mother about dinner, because grief or no grief, bodies run on schedule, and routine is a rope you cling to in fast water. She was in the parlor, sitting in grandmother's chair — she never sits in grandmother's chair — with her hands folded in her lap, looking at the wall above the fireplace where the family photographs hang.

"Ma. Do you want me to make something?"

She didn't turn around. "Vesper always liked the fish," she said. "On Fridays. You remember how she'd stand at the counter and pick the bones out herself, so patient, so patient, and she'd leave the head on her plate and you'd laugh—"

"Ma."

"—and she'd look at you across the table and you'd both be laughing before either of you had said a word, because that's what it was like with you two, nobody outside it ever understood—"

"*Ma.*"

She stopped. Her hands tightened in her lap, knuckles rising like small stones under the skin. In the mirror above the fireplace — the big oval one, grandmother's mirror, black-framed, brought from Lagos in a crate marked FRAGILE in a hand dead fifty years — I could see her face, and I could see mine over her shoulder, and I want to write down what I saw even though I don't trust it, because I don't trust anything tonight.

Her face was wet. She was crying without any sound at all, tears running straight off her jaw, and her mouth was moving around words that made no noise, and I realized she was praying, or reciting, or counting — her lips were keeping time with something.

And in the glass, my reflection was smiling.

I was not smiling. I want that on the record, dated, timed: 6:12 p.m., I was not smiling. My face was doing what it has done all day, which is nothing, which is held tight and neutral, jaw set, the face I wear in hospitals and police stations. But in the mirror the corners of my mouth were lifted, gently, warmly, the smile of a man greeting family at a door, and it lasted perhaps two seconds, and then it settled into my own expression like water finding its level, and I couldn't swear afterward that it hadn't been mine all along.

I put it down to exhaustion. I put it down to the light. I put it down in the notebook anyway:

*6:12 p.m. Mirror, parlor. Saw [illegible]. Probably fatigue. Probably.*

I tore that page out. Then I smoothed it out and taped it back in, because written things must not be argued with later, including by me.

* * *

Dinner was fish, because I cooked it, and my mother ate none of hers, and we sat in the kitchen with the radio off and the fridge humming and neither of us speaking, and it was almost bearable, almost ordinary, and I was nearly grateful.

At half seven she stood, collected both plates, and began to wash up. I dried. This is the oldest choreography in the world; our hands knew it without us. And somewhere in the middle of the third plate she said, quietly, without turning her head:

"You'll stay for the whole wake, won't you. All of it. Through Sunday."

"Of course."

"And you won't go into the attic."

I dried the plate I was holding. I set it down. "Why would I go into the attic?"

"Your grandmother's room is up there." Water ran over her hands. She was washing a plate that was already clean. "It's been locked since the funeral. Since her funeral. Nobody goes in."

"Ma, that was six years ago. Why would I—"

"Promise me." She turned then, and her face was scrubbed and dry and terrible, the face of a woman who has been awake for eleven days deciding exactly which lies to tell her remaining child. "Before the wake ends. Promise me you'll be gone before the wake ends."

"Gone? Gone where? Ma, I came home to bury my sister. I'm not leaving until she's in the ground."

Something crossed her face when I said *in the ground* — something quick and involuntary, like a flinch, like a woman hearing a word in a language she'd hoped was forgotten. She picked up the clean plate again and washed it again.

"Vesper," she said.

"Ma. *Casper.*"

But she was already elsewhere, hands in the water, lips moving around that silent counting again, and I stood there holding a dish towel in an empty kitchen, drying nothing, and outside the window the light was going the color of a bruise, and from somewhere — the garden, the lane, the cellar, I couldn't place it, and I have listened for it since and cannot find it again — I heard a sound I can only describe as a bell heard underwater. One note. Soft. Round. Patient.

It rang once.

My mother did not react. The dog next door did not bark. The sound was either for me alone or for no one at all, and I have written down the time — 7:43 p.m., dusk, one note — and tomorrow at nine they bring the coffin to this house, and tomorrow at ten the viewing hours begin, and I have decided, whatever Mr. Adeyemi says about seals and dignity, that I am going to look at my sister's face before I bury her.

A twin knows a twin. Whatever is in that coffin, I will know whether it is her.

I will record what I find.
