## 9. The Bell in the Well

I want to record the facts in order, because order is the last instrument I trust. The house has been dismantling my instruments one by one — first my eyes, then my mother's memory, now my stopwatch — but a notebook and a stubborn man can still make a kind of church of the truth. So: facts.

Fact one. At 6:41 p.m. on the third day after I arrived home to bury my sister, I heard singing coming from the ground.

Not from the house. From under it. Under the yard, under the flagstones, under the roots of the dead fig tree at the property's eastern edge where the grass grows in a circle that no mower has ever been able to explain. A woman's voice, or something wearing a woman's voice the way my reflection wears my face — accurately, patiently, with an interest in being believed.

I had been sitting on the back step with the notebook open, transcribing the previous night's mirror tests, when the sound arrived. It did not begin so much as surface, the way a drowned thing surfaces: gradually, then all at once, and always with the sense that it had been beneath you the whole time.

The song had four verses. I know this because it sang them twice through while I sat there not breathing, and both times it stopped at the same place — stopped mid-phrase, actually, on a held note that bent downward like a knee giving way, like a voice deciding against its own conclusion. Then silence, thick as wet cloth. Then it began again.

I wrote down what I could catch of the words. They were in English, mostly, but arranged wrong — the grammar of a language that has been underwater for fifty years. *Bind the quick to keep the kind. Bind the kind to keep the quick.* Over and over, with small variations, the way a hymn varies only to keep the mouth moving while the mind goes elsewhere.

At 7:03 p.m., the sun touched the roofline of the Okonkwo place next door, and the song cut off between syllables. Not faded. Cut. As if someone had lifted a needle from a record mid-word.

I sat on that step until full dark, and the notebook page in front of me had gone soft with sweat from my hand.

* * *

Here is what I know about the old well, assembled from childhood memory and from questions I put to Ada that evening over a dinner neither of us ate:

There was a well once, at the eastern edge of the property, near the fig tree. My grandmother had it capped sometime before I was born — a concrete slab poured over the mouth, flush with the ground, unremarkable unless you knew to look. Vesper and I found it when we were eight. We used to stand on it and jump, two feet together, to feel the answer come up through our bones: a hollow note, a *whum*, like striking the lid of a piano with your fist. We called it the drum. We thought the whole earth was a drum and we had found its skin.

Ada says there is no well. She said it twice, calmly, while looking at the middle distance over my shoulder, which is where she looks when she is protecting me from something. She said her mother had a cistern filled in decades ago, nothing more, and then she stood up from the table and began washing dishes that were already clean, and I understood that the conversation was over in every way except the one that mattered.

But Nkechi, when I called her that night, went quiet for four full seconds — I counted; the stopwatch habit is a disease now — and then said, very carefully, "Uncle Sunday fell down it when he was a boy. Before they capped it. He said he could hear it ringing all the way down." And then, quieter: "Casper, why are you asking about the well."

Because, cousin. Because the singing comes from the east side of the yard. Because the concrete slab I jumped on as a child is cracked now, a hairline fracture running corner to corner like a seam splitting, and out of that crack, at twilight, rises a sound that knows four verses of a song my grandmother sang to this house every dusk until the day she died.

Because I have started to suspect that everything in this family that we were told was filled in, closed, finished, buried — is none of those things. That we are a household of capped mouths, and something underneath us has been talking into the plug for fifty years.

* * *

I waited for the next evening the way you wait outside a surgery. I told myself I was gathering data. I told myself a great many things, and I will record them here so that some future reader — some future me, God willing there is one — can see exactly how a rational man talks himself to the edge of a hole in the ground.

At 6:30 p.m. I positioned myself at the kitchen window with the stopwatch, the notebook, and Ada's field glasses, which I took from the hall closet without permission because the house and I are past the point of permissions. At 6:38 the crack in the slab began, faintly, to sing.

I say *began to sing* and I stand by it. This was not resonance. This was not wind over a cavity, though I recorded the possibility in the notebook and underlined it twice, clinging to it like a man clinging to a rail in floodwater. Wind does not hold pitch. Wind does not repeat a phrase with the same ornamentation twice. And wind certainly does not pause, at the end of the fourth verse, in a way that sounds — I write this with my hand steady, I am making it stay steady — in a way that sounds like a breath drawn before a fifth line that never comes.

The held note lasted eleven seconds. Then the sun dropped below the Okonkwo roofline, and the well went silent as a shut mouth.

I was across the yard before I decided to cross the yard. That is a fact too, and I record it without excuse: my body made the decision and my mind followed it the way my reflection follows me, a half-step late, holding the notebook like a Bible.

* * *

The slab is maybe three feet across, poured rough, with the year stamped in it by someone's thumb before the concrete set: 19—, and then the last digit smeared by rain into a gray ghost. The crack runs through the stamp. Up close, the singing had stopped, but the air above the crack moved. Not wind — there was no wind anywhere else in the yard, the fig tree's dead branches standing still as wire — but a slow, tidal exhalation, cool and mineral-smelling, like breath from a cellar. Like breath from a throat.

I knelt. I put my palm flat on the concrete.

Warm. The slab was warm, on an October evening when every other stone in that yard was cold enough to bite. Warm the way a forehead is warm. And beneath my palm, so far below the frequency of hearing that I felt it rather than heard it, something was keeping time.

I am going to write the next part plainly, because plainness is all I have left, and because if I dress it up even slightly I will be able to convince myself later that I invented it.

I got the crowbar from the garage. I worked it into the crack. The concrete came up easier than concrete has any right to come up — forty years of cap lifting off in three pieces, as if it had never truly set, as if it had only ever been resting there, waiting to be asked. Beneath it: a round throat of old brick, descending into a dark that my flashlight gave up on at about twenty feet. The air that came out smelled of iron and river water and, underneath that, faintly, of incense. Church incense. Someone had burned frankincense down there, once, or something that wanted to smell like it.

And at the bottom, far down, at the exact limit of the light's reach, something caught the beam and threw it back.

Not water. Water doesn't flash like that, doesn't return light in a single deliberate pulse, like an eye adjusting.

A bell. Drowned in the well's black water, hanging from a rotted rope or a chain or nothing at all, and it turned — slowly, with the gravity of something enormous — and the light slid along its flank, and I saw that it was covered in writing. Rows of it. Ringing it, top to bottom, the way text rings a bell tower wall. I could not read a word of it at that distance, but I recognized the *shape* of it, because I had spent two nights with my grandmother's hymnals, looking at verses blacked out so thoroughly the ink had soaked through a hundred pages beneath.

The bell was written in the same hand. Or the blackout was copied from the bell. I genuinely do not know which direction the borrowing ran, and I think about that constantly now, the way you worry a loose tooth.

I leaned over the mouth of the well to see better.

* * *

Reader — future reader — whoever is holding this notebook in whatever year: I leaned over the mouth of the well, and the world changed hands.

Understand what the lag had been, up to that moment. For three days the mirrors had run behind me. One second, then 1.2, then — last night, in the bathroom, at 2 a.m. — a full two seconds of my own face acting out a script I hadn't received yet. I had built my whole understanding of the thing on that single axiom: *it follows.* Whatever wore my shape in the glass, it came after. Echo, not author. I could live inside that axiom. It had walls and a roof.

I leaned over the well, and in the curved darkness of the water far below, my reflection looked back up at me.

And it moved first.

I had not yet tilted my head. I know this because I remember the instant — the held instant, the photograph of it that grief and terror take without permission: my hands braced on the brick lip, my shadow falling into the shaft, my face still level, still mine. And down in the water, small with distance, my own face was already tilting. Already leaning further over the dark than I had leaned. Its mouth opened around words I had not said, and the water carried them up the shaft to me, thin and clear as a struck glass:

*"Look how deep it goes."*

My voice. Coming up to meet me, going down to meet it. We passed each other somewhere in the middle of the dark, my silence and its speech, and for one vertiginous second I did not know which of us was the echo — whether I was the man at the lip of the well or the thing at the bottom of it, looking up, waiting for myself to arrive.

I fell backward. Actually fell — flat on my back in the dead grass, the flashlight rolling away, the sky wheeling overhead, my heart going like a fist on a door. And here is the detail I have circled in the notebook until the paper tore: as I lay there gasping, the singing resumed. Softly. From directly beneath my spine. Four verses, unhurried, patient as tide. And this time, at the end of the fourth verse, it did not stop on the broken note.

It stopped on the *first word* of the fifth.

One word. Held. Offered. The way you hold out a hand.

Then the sun finished setting, and the well was only a well, and the cold came up out of the ground like something exhaling after a long performance, satisfied, or nearly satisfied, or hungry in the specific way that things are hungry when they have almost finished persuading you.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat at the kitchen table with every lamp in the house burning and wrote this account, and at some point — I don't know when, the clock said 3 and then it said 5 and the hours between are missing like torn pages — I became aware that I was humming.

Four verses. Perfect pitch. Every ornamentation exact, including two notes I am certain I never consciously registered, notes that exist nowhere in my memory and yet lived somewhere in my mouth, ready, rehearsed, *placed there.*

I clamped my jaw shut so hard my teeth ached till morning.

On the table in front of me, my grandmother's oldest hymnal lay open — I don't remember taking it down, don't remember opening it — to the page where the fifth verse should be. The blackout there is different from all the others. Thicker. Applied wet, pressed hard, worked into the grain of the paper with a fingernail or a knife. And beneath the ink, if you tilt the page against the lamplight at the correct angle, the pressure of the pen that wrote the original words has left its ghost in the paper's body — grooves, ridges, the skeleton of the sentence surviving the murder of its ink.

I have not tried to read it. I want you to know that. Whoever finds this: as of this entry, I have not tried.

But I know the shape of the first word. I've known since the well offered it to me, held out like a hand.

It is my grandmother's name.

And the well is waiting for tomorrow's dusk, and so — God help me, and this is the truest sentence in this notebook — so am I.
