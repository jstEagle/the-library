## 3. One Second Late

The mirror in the hallway was lying to her.

Paloma noticed it on her fourth morning at Mrs. Abernathy's house, and she noticed it the way you notice anything wrong with a room you're still learning: not as a fact but as an itch. She'd been brushing her teeth, watching herself in the tall oval glass that hung between the coat hooks and the door to the kitchen, and something about her own face had seemed—off. Not distorted. Not aged or hollowed out or any of the things a horror-movie brain wants a mirror to do. Just *late*. She raised her toothbrush and the woman in the glass raised hers a fraction of a heartbeat afterward, like a dancer who had learned the choreography from a recording.

She stood there with foam in her mouth and made herself test it again. Raised her left hand. Watched. The reflection followed, smooth and correct and unmistakably behind. Not enough that anyone else would catch it. Enough that she did.

"Old silver," she said aloud, to hear a voice in the house that wasn't hers. "Old mirrors delaminate. The backing oxidizes. It's nothing."

Her voice came back to her instantly, which was somehow worse. The mirror could lag behind her hand but not behind her mouth, as if it were only pretending to be broken.

She spat, rinsed, and went to work, and by the time she was unlocking her classroom door she had filed the whole thing under *quirks of old houses*, right next to the floorboard outside her room that groaned like a settling animal and the way the portrait of Vesper Halloway always seemed to have turned its head a few degrees since yesterday. Teachers learned to triage their unease. You couldn't stand in front of twenty-five children if every small strangeness got to vote.

The roster still bothered her more than the mirror, anyway.

Twenty-six names. Twenty-four desks. She'd counted twice, then a third time with a red pen, tapping each name against each chair like she was taking attendance for a class of furniture. Two names had no chairs, no faces, no file folders in the office cabinet—Ruthie Okonkwo and Samuel Bright, whose entries in her gradebook sat there in her own neat handwriting, though she had no memory of writing them. When she'd asked Principal Voss about it, he'd given her a smile with too much patience in it and said transfers, paperwork lag, don't worry your head. When she'd asked Mr. Ferro, the janitor, he'd stopped mopping and looked at her with an expression she was still trying to decode, and said: "You count 'em at dusk, you'll get a different number."

Then he'd told her not to stay past sundown, and gone back to his mop as if he hadn't.

Paloma Vasquez had survived four years at a charter school in a district that treated teachers as disposable hardware. She knew what it looked like when adults closed ranks around a lie. It looked exactly like this, except usually they weren't kind about it.

At lunch she opened the notebook.

It was nothing dramatic—a composition book, college-ruled, bought at the gas station on Route 9 because the town's single shop closed whenever it felt like closing. On the first page, under the date, she wrote:

*Marlow Creek School — documentation log.*
*Day 4. Roster discrepancy: 26 names / 24 seats / 24 students present. Names without bodies: R. Okonkwo, S. Bright. Principal's explanation: transfers (unverified). Janitor's explanation: none offered.*

*Hallway mirror reflects ~0.5 sec late. Possibly degraded silvering. Monitoring.*

She hesitated over the last line, feeling ridiculous, then wrote it anyway. That was the point of the notebook. If she was going to be afraid—and some animal part of her, the part that had kept her alive through worse neighborhoods than this, had already voted yes—she was going to be afraid *in writing*, where fear had to hold still and be examined.

* * *

The yearbooks were in the school library, in a locked case, because of course they were.

Mrs. Tull, the librarian, was one of the founding-family women—the ones Dee's classmates called *the choir ladies* without being able to say why—and getting the key required a small performance. Paloma wore her most boring cardigan and explained that she was building a bulletin board, *Decades of Marrow Creek*, wouldn't that be sweet, and Mrs. Tull studied her for three full seconds before unlocking the case and saying, "You can look. In here. They don't leave the room."

"In here" meant the reading alcove with no windows and one lamp, which felt less like a privilege than like a stall.

Paloma started with the oldest volume they had, 1958, and worked forward, and for the first forty minutes she found nothing but ordinary evidence of an ordinary dying mill town: bad haircuts, marching bands, ads for feed stores long dead. Then she reached 1971 and hit the first gap.

It wasn't a missing page. It was a missing *face*.

A sixth-grade class photo, thirty-one children arranged on bleachers, and one rectangle of bare gray board where a child should have been. Cut out—not scribbled over, not torn, but excised with a blade so fine the surrounding faces were untouched, their edges crisp, the cut lines straighter than any scissors could manage. Someone had removed this one child the way a surgeon removes a tumor, carefully, completely, leaving healthy tissue all around.

Paloma turned the page. Another class photo, another clean absence. Different grade. Same year.

She went back through the volumes slowly now, and once she knew what to look for, the gaps were everywhere. 1963: two absences. 1969: one. 1977: one. 1984: two, side by side, best friends presumably, erased together. Never more than two in a year. Never zero years in a decade. And in the back of each book, where the office stamped the enrollment counts—31, 30, 29—the arithmetic never acknowledged what had been taken. The numbers simply didn't include them, as if the children had been subtracted before the ink dried rather than after.

Her hands were steady. She noted that about herself with detached interest, the way you note weather. Her hands were steady and her mouth had gone dry and somewhere below her sternum a cold thing had begun to unfold, patient as a fern.

*Eleven,* she wrote later, in the notebook, sitting in her parked car with the doors locked though it was barely three o'clock. *Counted eleven cut faces across nine volumes. Some volumes missing entirely. 1958–1993, then the books stop. Ask why they stop.*

Underneath, after a moment:

*Ruthie Okonkwo. Samuel Bright. Check dates against cut-out years?*

She didn't know yet how the names connected. But the shape of the thing was no longer deniable, and she found that some part of her had already moved past denial into logistics, into lists, into the grim comfort of method. Fear could ride along. Fear just didn't get to drive.

* * *

That night the mirror was worse.

She had hung a towel over the hallway oval—petty, childish, effective—and so it was the bathroom mirror that caught her, the small one above the sink, while she washed her face. Cold water. Eyes shut. She toweled off and opened her eyes and her reflection opened its eyes half a second later, and this time the lag was not subtle. This time it was a gulf. The woman in the glass looked at her with the flat patience of a photograph, and then, catching up, blinked, and Paloma saw—she would swear this later, in the notebook, in ink—in the space *between*, in that stolen interval when the mirror was showing her something that had not quite happened yet, the surface of the glass was not empty.

There were hands against it.

Small hands. Child-sized, pressed flat from the inside, fingers spread, the way children press against windows—at buses, at doors, at the glass of aquarium tanks, at everything they've been told they cannot reach. Five fingers each, two palms, and behind them a dimness that was not her bathroom, that was not any room she knew, a dark that had depth and coldness and, faintly, at its bottom, the suggestion of rows. Like desks.

Then her own face arrived, late and ordinary, and the hands were gone, and Paloma stood gripping the sink with water running off her chin onto the porcelain in slow ticks.

"No," she said, and her voice cracked the silence of the house. "No. That's not—that's a trick of low light, that's—" But the sentence ran out of road. There was no physiological explanation for hands. There was no optical explanation for hands. There were hands, or there was madness, and madness at least had a treatment plan.

She walked to the hallway, took down the towel from the oval mirror, and made herself look. Nothing. Just her own exhausted face, arriving precisely on time, and behind her shoulder the dim corridor, the coat hooks, the narrow stairs.

And Vesper Halloway.

Not in the flesh—of course not in the flesh. On the landing behind her, visible in the mirror's depth, the portrait hung at the end of the upstairs hall, and even reflected and shrunken and forty feet away, the painted woman's eyes had found the glass. Found *her*. Paloma turned to look up the actual staircase, at the actual portrait, and in the painting Vesper faced forward as she always did, serene, hands folded, 1911 in faded script beneath her name.

But in the mirror, the painted mouth was open. Slightly. As if mid-word. As if someone had paused a film of the portrait at the exact frame where speech begins.

Paloma climbed the stairs with her heart going like a fist on a door, stood in front of the portrait until the trembling stopped, and searched the painted face for the open mouth. It was closed. Composed. A lady in a high collar keeping her secrets the way ladies in 1911 were taught to.

But her eyes—

Every morning since Paloma had arrived, she'd thought the eyes had shifted. She'd blamed light, fatigue, the angle of the stairwell. Now, close, she understood it wasn't the eyes that moved. It was what they were aimed at. Today they were not looking out at the hall, at the viewer, at the safe middle distance of formal portraiture. Today they were looking *down*. Toward the base of the frame. Toward the floor.

Paloma knelt.

Behind the heavy frame, in the shadow where wall met wainscoting, scratched into the plaster in a hand that shook—or hurried—were letters. Small, deep, old. She leaned close and read them twice, three times, tracing them with a fingertip without touching, and the cold fern under her sternum bloomed all the way open, because the words were not a warning and not a prayer.

They were lyrics.

Four lines of a song she almost recognized—the rhythm of it snagged somewhere in her memory, in the half-heard humming of the choir ladies on Main Street, in the tune the older kids sang at the fence line so the school would let them go home—but these four lines were not those verses. These were different. These came after.

And beneath the last line, in smaller strokes, pressed hard enough to chip the plaster:

*SHE WILL ASK FOR THE THIRD. DO NOT GIVE IT TO HER ALONE.*
