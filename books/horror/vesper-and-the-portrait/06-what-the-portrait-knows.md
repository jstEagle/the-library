## 6. What the Portrait Knows

The mirror in the hallway had learned a new trick.

Paloma noticed it on Thursday morning, standing with her toothbrush halfway to her mouth, watching the reflection of the portrait at the end of the hall. In the glass, Vesper Halloway's painted eyes were open. On the wall behind Paloma's shoulder—she turned to check twice—the eyes were closed, as they always were, half-lidded and serene beneath the varnish of 1911.

In the mirror, they were wide. And they were looking at her.

She finished brushing her teeth because the alternative was to stand there all day, and she had twenty-two students waiting for her, minus however many were left after this week's attrition. Twenty-one now. She'd stopped keeping count out loud.

But that night, alone in the rented room with the lamp on and her documentation notebook open, she went back to the hallway mirror and made herself look properly. Not glance. Look.

The lag was still there—that stolen second between the room and its reflection, the beat where the glass seemed to be deciding what to show her. She'd learned to work with it, the way you learn to work around a limp. Wait for the image to catch up. Don't trust the interval in between.

Except lately the interval wasn't empty anymore.

Tonight, in the lag, Vesper's mouth moved.

It wasn't the painted smile from the canvas. It was a real mouth, flesh-colored against the dark oil tones of the background, opening and closing with the deliberate patience of someone who has been trying to communicate for a very long time and has learned not to waste the attempt. The words came too fast for Paloma to read—or maybe they came slow, and it was the lag itself that scrambled them, syllables arriving out of order like a letter cut into strips and reassembled wrong.

"Slower," Paloma whispered. "Do it slower."

The mouth kept its pace. Patient. Almost kind.

She grabbed the notebook and wrote down what she could catch: *third* — or maybe *heard* — then something shaped like *verse*, then a word that might have been *paid* or *prayed* or *afraid*. Her hand shook so badly the letters leaned sideways.

"You hid it." She said it aloud, testing the theory against the woman's face. "The third verse. You're the one who hid it."

Vesper's mirrored mouth pressed into a line. Not a no. A *yes, and*.

*And what?*

The lag swallowed the answer. The mirror caught up to itself, and there was only the portrait again: closed eyes, folded hands, the small brass plaque reading VESPER HALLOWAY, 1911. Dated the year before the first child vanished, if the yearbooks were honest—and they weren't, but their lies had seams, and Paloma had spent three nights mapping every one.

She sat back on the bed and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

1911. Vesper Halloway, painted by some local artist whose name had been scraped off the stretcher bars. 1912, Elias Wren, age nine, gone from the church steps. Then one every few years, sometimes two, sometimes a gap long enough to make the town believe it was over. Eleven names she'd recovered so far, eleven faces cut from eleven yearbooks with a steadiness that suggested ritual rather than malice. And a portrait dated to the beginning, hanging in the house of a landlady who never spoke of it, in a town where the founding families still hummed a lullaby at dusk.

Vesper hadn't been taken. Vesper had been *kept*. Painted into oil and varnish and hung in a hallway for a hundred years, and the price of that imprisonment was written all over her face if you knew how to read the lag: she had known the third verse, and instead of singing it, she had hidden it, and hiding it had cost her everything except the ability to warn whoever came next.

"Me," Paloma said quietly. "I'm whoever came next."

The lamp flickered. Outside, dusk was coming down over Marrow Creek the way it always did—not falling so much as settling, like silt in still water. Somewhere down the street, faint as breath through a wall, someone was humming.

She wrote one more line in the notebook, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*The third verse exists. She paid for hiding it. Find out what she wants me to do with it.*

* * *

Friday morning, Dee Okafor's desk was empty.

Paloma told herself it was nothing. Kids got sick. Kids skipped school. Kids in towns like this, she reminded herself, had complicated lives, and an absence on a Friday meant nothing more than a weekend starting early.

She took attendance anyway, and when she reached the O in the alphabet her voice did something strange—a small hitch, a half-second stall, like a record skipping—and the classroom waited for her in silence until she said "Absent" and moved on.

At recess she stood at the fence line where Dee had first mentioned the song. The older kids weren't singing today. They stood in loose clusters near the chain-link, not playing, not talking, just facing west toward the tree line with the particular stillness of children who have been taught to wait for something. When the bell rang they filed inside without being told twice.

Paloma found Dee's best friend, a small solemn girl named Priya, crouched by the drinking fountain.

"Do you know where Dee is?"

Priya looked up with an expression far too old for her face. "Her mama said she's got a fever."

"Have you seen her?"

"No." Priya's voice dropped. "Nobody's seen her since Wednesday. Her mama's telling people fever, but—" She glanced toward the office, where Principal Aldous stood in his doorway watching the hallway with his arms crossed, and whatever she was going to say next went back down inside her. "Fever," she repeated, flat as a recitation, and walked away.

Paloma spent her lunch period driving past Dee's house. The windows were dark. A man she didn't recognize sat on the porch in a folding chair, large and patient, the way men sit when they've been hired to sit somewhere. He watched her car pass once, twice, and on the third pass he raised one hand—not waving. Counting.

By last period her hands wouldn't stay steady, so she gave the class silent reading and stood at the window, doing sums in her head. Eleven missing across a century. One missing since Wednesday. The founding families' covenant, the archive night, the librarian feeding her photocopies into the burn barrel while two of the Hollis brothers stood by with their hands in their pockets. She had thought she had time. She had thought the song needed dusk, needed a gathering, needed the whole machinery of the town to swing into motion.

But maybe it didn't need any of that. Maybe it only ever needed a name said at the right hour by the right bloodline, and Dee's name had been said, and now there was a folding chair on a porch and a fever story that fooled nobody, least of all the children.

She drove home fast. The sun was low and orange and she hated how beautiful it made the creek look.

* * *

The hallway mirror was already lagging when she came through the door.

She didn't stop to take off her coat. She stood in front of the glass and waited for the stolen second, and when it came, Vesper was there—closer than before, her mirrored face filling more of the frame, as though she had spent the whole day walking forward through whatever country existed behind the paint.

Her mouth moved. Paloma read what she could:

*Listen.*

Then: *Not with your ears.*

Then a word she couldn't parse at all—two syllables, the shape of them familiar, like a word she'd known her whole life and forgotten where from—and then Vesper's expression changed. It wasn't urgency anymore. It was grief, plain and enormous, the grief of a woman who had been dead for a century and had just watched another child's name get added to a list she couldn't reach.

"I know," Paloma said. Her voice cracked on it. "I know she's gone. Tell me how to get her back."

Vesper shook her head slowly. Once. Twice. And then her mouth formed words with exaggerated care, shaping each one like a mother teaching a child to speak:

*You. Cannot. Save. Them.*

The lag ended. The mirror settled. Paloma stood gripping the frame hard enough to hurt her fingers, staring at the closed painted eyes, feeling the sentence land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*You cannot save them.*

Fine. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Fine. If the ones already taken were beyond reaching—if eleven children and a hundred years of silence were a debt that couldn't be repaid—then it wasn't about saving anyone. It had never been about saving anyone. It was about the song itself, the third verse, the thing Vesper had hidden at the cost of her own face and hands and voice. The founders had built a covenant on a melody, and melodies could be broken, but only by someone who knew all of it.

"Teach me," she said to the mirror. "However I have to learn it. Teach me."

For a long moment there was nothing but the hallway breathing around her, the house ticking as it cooled, dusk thickening outside the window like ink dropped in water.

Then, in the glass, Vesper smiled.

It was not the serene painted smile. It was small and fierce and terribly sad, and it was the first honest thing Paloma had seen in Marrow Creek.

The portrait's lips began to move—and this time, in the lag, the words came slow enough to read.
