## 6. The First Verse Returns

Tomás Herrera had not slept a whole night through since his wife died, and so he was awake when the radio began to sing.

It stood on the shelf above the woodstove, an old battery set he kept for the farm reports, and it had been off for three days — he remembered switching it off, remembered the small click of the dial, remembered the silence that followed. But at some hour past midnight, in the blue-black hush of the house where he had lived for fifty-one years, the radio filled with static. Not the flat white hiss of dead air. This static moved. It rose and fell. It shaped itself into long phrases, held notes, pauses where breath would go.

And beneath the crackle — or inside it, or wearing it like a coat — something was singing.

Tomás sat up in bed with the blankets pooled around his waist and listened the way a rabbit listens: absolutely still, ears straining, every part of him turned toward the sound while pretending to be nothing at all.

He knew the song.

That was the worst of it. That was the thing he would spend the rest of what remained of his life trying to un-know. The melody winding through the static was the one the folklorist woman had played back to him in his own kitchen two weeks ago, the fragment old Marta used to hum before she stopped humming anything at all, the verse every singer in Vesper broke off at the same line as if they'd walked into glass. *The door is shut and the door is warm* — no. He wouldn't finish it. Even now, alone, even with the dark pressing against the windows like a face, he would not finish it.

But the radio finished it. The radio sang the whole verse, and then it sang it again, and each time the voice beneath the static came a little clearer, a little less like weather and a little more like a person standing very close behind him in an empty room.

Tomás got up. His knees cracked. He crossed the cold floorboards and put his hand on the radio's dial and clicked it off, and the singing stopped, and the silence afterward was worse, because silence had a shape now, and the shape was waiting.

He stood there a long time, an old man in his undershirt with his hand resting on a machine that should have been dead.

"Go away," he said to the kitchen. His voice sounded thin and foolish. "We put you away two hundred years ago. We put you away."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the wind moved through the pines above the quarry road, and it did not sound like wind. It sounded like a congregation taking a breath.

* * *

He did not sleep again. He made coffee he didn't drink and sat by the window watching the road, and when the sky went from black to iron to the color of old pearl, he told himself he would go out and check the fences, because checking fences was what Tomás Herrera did, had done every morning of his working life, and a man could hold himself together with such small repetitions the way you hold a wall together with mortar.

He remembered the fences. He remembered them fine.

What he did not remember — what he discovered, standing in his yard with the frost smoking off the grass, looking down at his own hands — was why he had come outside at all. There had been a reason. It had been enormous. It had been sitting on his chest all night like a stone. And now it was gone, and in its place was only the yard, and the fences, and a strange new quietness in his head, like a room recently vacated.

He counted backward from the morning to steady himself. Tuesday. He had gone to the co-op on Monday. Sunday he'd eaten at his daughter's table — no. No, that wasn't right either. His daughter lived in Zaragoza. He ate at Rosa's table Sundays, Rosa who led the choir, Rosa whose mother had been his cousin. He could see the table. He could see the stew. He could not, when he reached for last Sunday, find it. He found instead a smooth blank place, the way snow fills a footprint, level and white and utterly without history.

"You're tired," he said aloud. "You're seventy-eight years old and you didn't sleep. That's all this is."

The quarry road started at the end of his lane. He knew that. Everyone knew that; the blasting for the reservoir had been shaking dishes in Vesper for a month. What he did not know — what arrived in him suddenly, completely, with the certainty of a memory rather than a thought — was that he needed to walk up that road. Now. Before the light got strong. Before anyone else was awake.

His legs began to carry him before his mind agreed to anything.

* * *

The morning fog lay in the quarry like milk in a bowl. The blasting had opened a great raw wound in the hillside, terraces of cut rock, yellow machines sleeping under tarps, and at the center of it all, half-emerged from the bedrock like a tooth from a gum, the door.

Tomás had seen it once, from a distance, the day the foreman woman stood at its edge with her arms crossed and her jaw set. A slab of grey stone fitted into the living rock so perfectly the seam was thinner than a knife blade. Hinges, the men said. Hinges on the *inside*. They'd poured concrete at the base by way of doing something, anything, and the concrete had cured wrong, crazed with hairline cracks, as if the stone beneath it had refused the gift.

Now he stood ten feet from it, and the fog beaded on his eyebrows, and he understood that his feet had brought him here because something had asked them to.

The door was warm. He could feel it on his face, a warmth like standing near bread ovens, like standing near a body.

"It's you," Tomás said. His voice came out steady, which frightened him more than screaming would have. "You're the one on the radio."

The seam of the door breathed. Not opened — breathed. A finger's width of darkness appeared along its edge and vanished, appeared and vanished, slow as sleep.

And the singing started again, and this time it was not in the radio, and it was not in the wind. It was in him. It rose up through his chest from somewhere below his heart, in a register he had never owned, and it knew the words, all the words, the whole first verse, the one that ended at the line where every singer in Vesper had walked into glass:

*The door is shut and the door is warm,*
*and warm things want to open—*

"No," Tomás said, and his mouth kept singing.

He felt it happen then, and this is the part he would never be able to tell anyone properly, because language is built for describing things that stay outside the body. The verse moved through him like water through a channel, and where it passed, it took things. Not painlessly, not violently — gently, the way a river takes the bank, grain by grain, so patiently the bank doesn't notice until it's gone. He felt his wedding day go. Fifty years married to Pilar, and he felt the church, the smell of the orange blossom, her hands trembling on his sleeve — felt them loosen, drift, dissolve into the current of the song and flow out of him toward the door. He grabbed after them. He grabbed with everything he had, the way you grab at a hat in the wind, and caught nothing but the shape of having lost something.

"Pilar," he said. "Pilar Herrera. Born March second. We had a farm. We had—" 

A farm. Yes. But where? Which valley? Whose land had bordered theirs? He had farmed that land for forty years and the name of the neighbor to the north slid away from him even as he reached for it, and in its place the verse poured in, filling the vacancy it had made, occupying him room by room like a tenant with a key.

*and warm things want to open,*
*and open things want out.*

He tried to run. His body stayed. He stood in the fog ten feet from the door with his arms hanging loose and his mouth moving, and somewhere very deep inside, in the last lit cellar of himself, Tomás Herrera screamed and screamed and could not make his face show it.

The sun came up over the eastern ridge and burned the fog off the quarry floor in long rags. The machines woke. Somewhere below, a man shouted good morning to another man, and the ordinary sounds of the world went on, indifferent, and Tomás understood with perfect clarity that the world would go on being ordinary right up until it wasn't, and that no one — not the foreman, not the priest, not the folklorist woman with her little silver microphone — knew yet what had happened here at dawn, standing in the milk-white cold.

The verse ended. The door's seam closed. The warmth withdrew like a hand pulled back through a window.

And Tomás Herrera, hollowed, stood blinking in the strengthening light and wondered, mildly, the way a man wonders about weather, why he was standing in a quarry.

* * *

They found him at seven.

Delia Cruz came up the track in her pickup because the night crew reported lights moving near the site after hours, and she found an old man she half-recognized standing at the barrier fence with his hands hooked in the wire, staring at the door with an expression she couldn't read — not fear, not awe. Recognition, maybe. Or homesickness.

"Señor. You can't be here." She took his arm. "This is a work site. How did you get past the gate?"

Tomás looked at her. He looked at her for a long moment, and Delia would say later, telling it in the bar with her voice low, that the look was the worst part — that it was the look of a man reading a sign in a language he'd studied as a boy and forgotten, knowing the words meant something, knowing only that he couldn't remember what.

"I'm looking for my father," he said. "He's late coming in from the high pasture. Have you seen him? He'll be wanting his supper."

Delia's blood went cold in the morning warmth. "Señor Herrera," she said carefully — she knew him, everyone knew him, he'd sold eggs and lamb to the crew all month — "your father has been dead a long time."

Something flickered across the old man's face. Something surfaced and struggled, a drowning man's hand breaking water. "Pilar," he said. "My wife is called Pilar. Isn't she? Tell me she's called Pilar."

"She was," Delia said, because she didn't know what else to say. "She died, señor. Two years ago. You came to my house after the funeral. You brought flowers."

"Two years." He said it the way a man says a number in a foreign currency, converting, failing to convert. "I don't remember two years ago. I don't remember—" His hands left the fence and pressed against his own temples, hard, as though he could squeeze the missing years back in like juice from a fruit. "I was a young man. I was walking up this road and I was a young man and now I'm — what happened to me? What did you do to me?"

"We didn't do anything." Delia heard her own voice shake. Behind her, two workers had stopped to stare, and one of them, the younger one, Esteban, crossed himself without seeming to notice he'd done it. "There was blasting yesterday. Maybe — maybe the noise, maybe a fall—"

"The singing," Tomás said suddenly, and gripped her wrist with a strength that startled her. "Did you hear it? All night, on the radio. Every station. It was singing the old verse, the one nobody finishes, and I knew it, I knew all of it, and now I don't know my wife's face." His eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, and his grip bruised her wrist. "It took the years out of me like drawing thread from cloth. Forty years, señora. It has my forty years. It's wearing them."

"Okay," Delia said. "Okay. Sit down. Let's sit down." She guided him to a rock, and he sat, and he let her, and when she looked back at the door — just once, quickly, the way you look at a dog that has gone quiet — the seam of the stone was flush and seamless and cold-looking, and there was frost on the ground everywhere except within a pace of it, a clean bare ring, as though the rock were breathing heat.

She drove him down to the village herself. He sat in the passenger seat with his hands folded in his lap like a schoolboy, and halfway down the hill he asked her, politely, who she was, and then asked it again four minutes later, and then a third time, and each time his voice was pleasant and puzzled and entirely empty of her, and Delia Cruz drove faster than she had ever driven on that road.

* * *

By noon the whole village knew. By noon Isandro Vasquez was sitting across from Tomás in Rosa's front room with her recorder running, and Father Anselm stood by the window with his hands hidden in his sleeves, and Tomás answered their questions with the terrible patience of the very old and the very lost.

He remembered his childhood perfectly. He could name his schoolmaster, describe the flood of his ninth year, recite the prices of things in the market of 1954. Everything before the age of thirty-eight was intact, bright, close enough to touch. Everything after was a wall of fresh snow.

"It went in order," he said. He was holding a cup of tea he never drank. "That's what I keep thinking. It didn't take them all at once. It took them the way you'd eat something. From the outside in. First the newest years, and it was working inward, and if someone hadn't found me—" He stopped. Looked at his hands. "How long was I up there?"

"Hours, maybe," Isandro said gently. "Since before dawn."

"Then it stopped on its own." He looked up at her, and his eyes were clear and appalled. "It wasn't full. Understand? It stopped because it wasn't finished eating, not because it was done. It will come back for the rest. It knows where I live."

No one spoke. On the shelf beside the window, Rosa's little kitchen radio sat silent, unplugged, its cord coiled around itself like something bound.

Isandro reached out and touched the old man's knee. "Tomás. The verse it was singing. The one on the radio. Can you still hear it? In your head?"

And Tomás Herrera's face changed — emptied, smoothed, went serene in a way that made Rosa cross herself openly — and when he answered, his voice was not quite his own. It was pitched lower, rounder, a voice like a well.

"*The door is shut and the door is warm,*" he said, "*and warm things want to open.*"

Then he blinked, and shuddered, and was an old man again, weeping quietly into his tea, asking what he had said, asking why everyone was looking at him like that.

Isandro stopped the recorder with fingers that were not quite steady. Father Anselm pushed himself off the wall and crossed the room and knelt by Tomás's chair, and the priest's face was grey as quarry dust, and when he spoke it was barely above a whisper.

"That's the founders' first verse," Anselm said. "Word for word. The sealing verse. It hasn't been sung in this village for two hundred years." He looked up at Isandro, and then at the silent radio on the shelf, and then at the window, beyond which the quarry hill stood black against the noon sky.

"And something taught it to him in one night," he said. "Which means the door isn't remembering the song anymore." He swallowed. "It's rehearsing."
