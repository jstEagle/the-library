## 7. Documentation Is a Kind of Prayer

Tomás Herrera had not spoken a full sentence in three days.

Isandro found him on the fourth morning sitting in his daughter's kitchen, hands flat on the table like a man waiting for a verdict that had already been read. He was seventy-eight years old and he did not know the name of the woman who kept refilling his coffee cup. He did not know the name of the town. When she'd first arrived in Vesper, he had been the one who told her — with a craftsman's pride — about the almond harvests of his boyhood, about the bell that used to ring for lost sheep, about a song his grandmother sang that he would not sing twice.

Now he looked at her with the polite blankness of a stranger at a bus stop.

His daughter, Lucía, met Isandro at the door with red-rimmed eyes. "The doctor from Salamanca says it's dementia. Early-onset, he thinks, though at his age—" She stopped, wringing a dish towel. "But it isn't, is it. It's not like that. He didn't forget gradually. He forgot *backward*. Like water running out of a basin."

Isandro had no answer for her. What she had was a recorder, and a stubbornness she had mistaken all her life for a virtue.

"May I sit with him?" she asked. "I want to try something."

Lucía hesitated, then stepped aside.

* * *

She had not planned the experiment. It assembled itself out of desperation and insomnia, the way most of her good ideas did. She set the recorder on the kitchen table between the coffee cups and the sugar bowl, wound back to the third interview she'd conducted in Vesper — Tomás himself, eleven days ago, his voice cracked and low, singing the fragment he'd allow: four lines of a ballad about a door and a stone and a mouth that must be fed. He had stopped mid-fifth-line, gone white, refused to continue, refused even to hear the melody hummed back to him. But he had sung those four lines once, and she had caught them, clean, on tape.

She pressed play.

Tomás's own voice filled the kitchen, thin and wavering through the little speaker, singing words he could no longer find. And Isandro watched something happen to the old man's face — a slow tide coming in. His brow furrowed. His lips moved, following along a half-second behind his recorded self, like a man walking in his own footprints. By the second repetition his hands had lifted off the table. By the fifth, tears were running down into the gray stubble of his beard, and he said, in a voice like a hinge unused for decades:

"That's mine. That's — I know that. Who took it? Who took it out of me?"

Lucía made a sound against the doorframe, small and involuntary, like a bird striking glass.

Isandro's own hands were shaking as she stopped the recording. Not from fear — or not only. From the vertigo of a hypothesis confirming itself too fast, too completely, in a kitchen that smelled of coffee and floor wax. The songs were not folklore. She had known that since the photographs of the quarry door, since Father Anselm's face going gray as wet ash. But this — this was different. This was evidence with a pulse.

The thing behind the door was unmaking people verse by verse, memory by memory, working backward through their lives the way a fire works backward through a fuse. And the recordings — her cheap, grant-funded, battery-hungry recordings — were holding the line. A dam made of magnetic tape.

"Play it again," Tomás said. "Please. While I still know to ask."

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She told herself she would transcribe for an hour.

The pensione room she rented from Señora Ibáñez had a desk built for a schoolchild, its varnish worn to bare wood in the shape of two forearms. She spread her notebooks across it — six of them now, the spines softening, pages buckled where rain had gotten at them in the church archive — and began the work of turning sound into ink.

It was slow going. The fragments were in an old dialect, half of it pre-dating the standard orthography, and the singers' pronunciations disagreed with each other in ways that were either corruption or — the thought arrived cold and stayed — *variants*. Different verses. Different movements of a single long composition, scattered deliberately across the village like pieces of one letter mailed to different addresses so no single interception could read it whole.

She worked by lamplight while the town slept around her, and the town's sleeping felt different now. She thought of it as held breath. Two hundred years of held breath, and somewhere down at the old quarry, something exhaling against stone.

By three in the morning she had the first fragment fully transcribed, and she discovered something in the writing of it that listening had never shown her. The meter was not decorative. The stresses fell in a pattern — long-short-long-long, rest, long-short-long-long — and when she tapped it out with her pen against the desk, her heartbeat tried to follow it. The rhythm was a harness. A bridle. Whoever composed these verses had built them to be ridden by a human voice, to give the voice somewhere firm to stand while it did whatever terrible necessary thing the words required.

Documentation is a kind of prayer, she wrote in the margin, and then sat looking at what she'd written, because she hadn't meant to write it and it was true.

She slept three hours. She dreamed of a door with hinges on the inside, and woke with her pen still in her hand, having written in her sleep — or near enough to sleep — a single line she did not remember composing:

*count the rests; they are also notes.*

* * *

The days took on a liturgy.

Up before dawn. Coffee that had gone past bitter into something medicinal. Transcription until the light was good enough to work by, then interviews — the few singers left who would speak to her, which was fewer every day, because word had moved through Vesper the way word moves through small places, underground and fast: the outsider's machine keeps things. Keeps them safe. Or keeps them *taken*, depending on whom you asked.

Then, each afternoon, the walk up to the Herrera house with the recorder, and the playing of the fragments for Tomás, whose erosion slowed but did not stop — she could measure it now, in lines remembered and lines lost, a ledger she kept in the back of the sixth notebook. Four lines held. Then three and a fraction. Then three, steady, when she doubled the sessions. The thing below the quarry pulled; the tape pushed back; Tomás hung between them like laundry in opposing winds.

He began to wait for her. She would see him at the window as she came up the hill, and there was something unbearable in it — the dignity of a man reduced to four lines of borrowed song, guarding them like coals carried in cupped hands.

"You're the archivist," he said to her one afternoon, using a word she'd never taught him. "My grandmother said there would be an archivist. She said, *when they come to take the song down wrong, someone will come to take it down true.*" He looked at his own hands. "I don't remember her saying it. Lucía tells me I said it yesterday. Isn't that a strange thing — to have your words come back to you as news?"

"It's a strange time," Isandro said.

"Sing with me," he said. "Tape can hold a thing. But a voice holds it warmer."

So she sang with him, badly, her scholar's ear and unschooled throat doing their best, and if the act was a prayer then it was the first prayer she'd ever made without irony, and she was surprised to find that irony, under pressure, was one of the first things to go.

* * *

Mateo Reyes appeared at her door on the ninth night, standing in the rain with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at.

"I heard you've been going to the Herrera house," he said.

"Word travels."

"In Vesper, word *is* travel. There's nothing else here." He didn't come in. Rain ran off the brim of the pensione's little awning between them, a beaded curtain. "I told you I wanted nothing to do with this. I want you to know I haven't changed my mind."

"All right," Isandro said.

"But my grandfather's copy of the second fragment — the one my family kept, the one I wouldn't sing for you —" His jaw worked. "I played your recording of Tomás. The four lines. I told myself I was checking it for accuracy." A short, unhappy laugh. "I've listened to it forty times. And last night I caught myself singing the next part. The part after. The part I swore I'd never let out of my mouth." He looked up at her, and the rain-light made his eyes look bruised. "It wants out, señora. It's been locked in me my whole life and now that the door down there has cracked, it wants *out*, and I don't know how much longer I can be a wall instead of a man."

"Then don't be a wall," Isandro said. "Be a scribe. If it's going to come out of you anyway, put it somewhere it can do some good. Give it to the page. Let me hold it with the rest."

He stood in the rain a long moment, wrestling with two hundred years of inherited silence, and she watched him lose — watched the loss look almost like relief.

"I'll need coffee," he said finally. "And I don't sing pretty."

"Neither do I. We'll be accurate together."

* * *

He came the next evening, and the evening after that, and by the end of the week he had annexed the corner of her room nearest the window as if he'd always owned it, hanging his wet hat on the hook Señora Ibáñez had provided for exactly no hats in living memory. He would not touch the recorder — "machines lie," he said, though she suspected it was closer to the truth that machines *don't* lie, and he feared what they'd say — but he sang for the page, slowly, line by line, stopping whenever the words turned toward the fifth line of any fragment, flinching away from it like a hand from a stove.

"The founders cut the songs at the same place," Isandro said one night, watching him rub his sternum as though the music sat there like swallowed stone. "Every version. Every singer. They all stop at the same point in the melody."

"Because the rest was never meant to be sung alone," Mateo said. "My grandfather told me that much before he died, and then made me swear to forget it, which —" he gestured at himself, at the room, at the whole unraveling town — "you can see how well that went. The verses are load-bearing, he said. Sing one without the ones before it and it doesn't seal anything. It *opens*. Like calling a name without knowing why you're calling."

Isandro's pen had gone still. Outside, the wind moved through the empty streets of Vesper, through houses whose windows were dark earlier every year, and she thought of Tomás's ledger in the back of her notebook, the lines won and lost, and of the crack in the quarry door a finger's width wide, breathing.

"Then we need all of them," she said. "In order. Before it finishes opening on its own."

"We need more than that." Mateo was looking at the window, at the black hills beyond, where the quarry lay. "But yes. Start with the verses."

They worked until two, until three. She learned the way he took his coffee — black, with a spoon of honey, a contradiction he defended fiercely — and he learned that she hummed when she transcribed, tunelessly, unconsciously, and that being teased about it made her ears go red. Small knowledge. The kind that accrues between people sharing a lamp in a cold room, and neither of them named it, because naming things in Vesper had consequences.

One night, near the end of the second week, she looked up from the page and found him watching her with an expression she couldn't file under any heading she owned — tenderness crossed with grief crossed with the particular guilt of a man who knows the bill for all this will eventually come due, and suspects he is the currency.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. You had ink on your face." He reached over — hesitated — and touched her cheekbone with his thumb, wiping away a smudge, his hand warm from the coffee cup, and the touch lasted perhaps a second longer than hygiene required.

Isandro returned to her transcription and did not get another clean sentence written for ten minutes.

* * *

On the fifteenth day, Tomás lost the third line.

She knew it before Lucía called, because she had begun to feel the losses herself — not the memories, not yet, but a pressure behind the eyes on certain nights, a sensation like a page being turned somewhere just outside her field of vision. She climbed the hill in weather that had turned cold early, and found the old man calm in a way that frightened her worse than weeping would have.

"Don't grieve," he told her, patting the table beside his elbow. "Sit. Play it. We'll hold what we can hold, and what slips, slips. My grandmother buried three children and planted almonds over them. She said grief is just love with nowhere to put itself." He smiled at her, gap-toothed, serene. "You put it in the notebook, girl. That's as good a place as any."

She played the fragment. He recovered the line for nearly an hour, singing with her, gripping her hand with a strength that belied everything. And in the last minutes, lucid as glass, he leaned close and said:

"There's a fifth verse. There always was. My grandmother knew where it was written and she burned the place it was written, or so she claimed, and she died with smoke in her lungs and a smile on her face, and I never understood the smile until now." His eyes were wet and bright. "You'll find it anyway. People like you always find it. That's not a warning, archivist. It's a request. Find it. Finish it. And when you learn what it costs —"

He stopped. The line slipped from him even as he reached for the thought, and his face went smooth and pleasant and vacant, and Lucía came in from the kitchen to find Isandro sitting very still with the recorder running, capturing the sound of the room after a man leaves it.

* * *

That night she added a seventh notebook to the row on the desk, and ruled its first page into columns, and at the top of the left column she wrote *what I remember*, and at the top of the right column she wrote *what I have verified*, and then she sat for a long time looking at the space between them, which was where she lived now.

Because there was a new entry forming, and she did not trust it.

Her sister. She had a sister — had *had* a sister; the tense wobbled when she reached for it. Elena. A fire, when they were girls. A promise made in a stairwell or a doorway, smoke in the air, someone's hand in someone's hand. She could feel the shape of it the way you feel the shape of a word on the tip of your tongue — and when she reached for the details, they came back wrong. Too neat. Phrased like prose. *We promised we would never leave each other in a burning building.* No child phrased a promise that way. Something was handing her her own past pre-written, polished, paraphrased — and she could not prove it, because the proof would have been the messy original, and the messy original was what was missing.

She pressed record on the machine and spoke into it for two hours, everything she could dredge, refusing to polish, leaving the gaps open and ugly, timestamping her own soul. When she finished, her voice on the playback sounded like a stranger reporting a theft.

Mateo knocked at midnight and found her at the desk with seven notebooks and red eyes, and he didn't ask what was wrong, because in Vesper everyone was learning what was wrong; he only took the cold coffee away and brought hot, and sat across from her, and said, "Read me tomorrow's list."

So she read him the list — fragments to collect, singers to visit, the ledger of Tomás's lines, the notation problem in the second movement, the rests that were also notes — and he listened the way men listen when the list is the only raft and they both intend to drown politely rather than let go of it.

At the end he said, quietly, "There's a verse my family kept whole. All five lines. Sealed in a book my grandfather's grandfather bound himself. I told you I'd never open it." He turned his hat over in his hands. "I'm telling you I'll open it tomorrow."

"Why?" Isandro asked. "What changed?"

Mateo looked at her — at the ink on her fingers, the notebooks, the recorder still warm from holding a woman's stolen childhood — and something moved through his face that he chose not to translate into words.

"Come at dawn," was all he said. "And bring every blank page you own."
