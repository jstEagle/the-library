## 12. The Third Verse, Sung Wrong

Rosa Ibarra had led the choir of San Sebastián for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once had to teach a congregation to be afraid of a song.

She stood in the nave on the morning of the attempt, watching them file in — not the usual scattered faithful but nearly the whole village, two hundred souls in their winter coats, breath steaming in the cold stone air. They came because the well water ran black. They came because Tomás had stood at the quarry at dawn with forty years gone from his face and his eyes. They came because Father Anselm had stood at the pulpit the Sunday before and said, in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant, that the time had come to sing.

They came because there was nowhere else to go.

"Again," Rosa said to the small cluster of choir members gathered near the front, and lifted the photocopied page — Isandro's transcription, the third verse rendered in careful pencil, the founders' old notation decoded at last. "From the top. And this time, *together*. If we scatter, we're lost."

Marisol, who had sung alto beside Rosa since they were girls, frowned at the page. "It sits wrong in the throat, Rosa. Like it wants to go somewhere else."

"It does want to go somewhere else. That's the point." Rosa tapped the page. "The founders built it to go somewhere else. Every note is a stone in a wall. We are not singing *to* God this morning. We are singing *at* a door, and we had better mean it."

The words came out harder than she intended. She softened nothing. There was no time for softness. The crack in the quarry door had been a finger's width a week ago; Mateo Reyes had come back from checking it yesterday and would not say how wide it was now, only that his face had gone the color of the limestone, and that was answer enough.

* * *

They rehearsed through the morning, and the verse learned itself into their bodies the way old hymns do, syllable by syllable, and by noon Rosa almost believed.

The third verse was strange music. It moved in circles — the melody rising and returning, rising and returning, never quite resolving, like a question asked over and over by someone who will not accept the answer. Isandro had explained it once, in her quick foreigner's way, pencil flying across the page: *It's a lock. The repetition is the tumblers. You have to hit each phrase the same number of times, in the same order, or the mechanism jams.* She had said it like a scholar. She had looked, when she said it, like a woman reading her own handwriting on a letter she didn't remember writing.

That was the other thing. Rosa had noticed it at rehearsal on Tuesday, and again on Wednesday: Isandro Vasquez forgetting. Small things first — where she'd set her coffee, the name of the man who ran the hardware store. Then larger. She had called Rosa by her mother's name, dead twenty years, and laughed it off, and Rosa had laughed with her, and then gone home and sat in her kitchen in the dark for a long time.

The thing behind the door was eating the village's memory, and it had begun with the woman who knew the songs best.

Which meant, Rosa understood, that they were building a wall out of sand while the tide came in. But the founders had known that too, hadn't they? The founders had sung it anyway. She turned that thought over all through the rehearsal, and it neither comforted nor discouraged her. It simply was. Some things you do because there is nothing else, and the doing is its own reason.

"Places," she called at one o'clock, and the village arranged itself in the pews like a congregation, which was what it was, and like an army, which was what it needed to be.

* * *

Father Anselm stood at the altar with the founders' score open before him, the old parchment gone soft as cloth at the folds. He had not wanted this. Rosa had watched him argue with Isandro and Mateo for two nights in the rectory, his voice low and desperate — *the Church sealed those pages away for a reason, incomplete knowledge is how people die* — and she had watched Isandro answer him with terrible gentleness: *Father, the incomplete knowledge is already in the air. It's humming through a crack in a door. The only question is whether we answer it with the whole verse or let it finish the song alone.*

Anselm had yielded the way old men yield to the young — not because they are convinced, but because they are tired, and because they love.

He raised his hand now, and the nave went quiet, so quiet Rosa could hear the wind worrying the cypress outside, could hear old Emilio's knees creak as he settled into the bass section, could hear her own heart, which was behaving disgracefully for a woman who had sung in front of crowds her whole life.

"Third verse," Anselm said. "For Vesper. For everyone we've lost to it already." His eyes went, briefly, to the empty pew where old Tomás usually sat — Tomás was at home now, stabilized, Isandro's recordings playing beside his bed on a loop like a lullaby, but he would not be singing today, and everyone knew why. "For everyone we have not yet lost."

Rosa lifted her hands.

They began.

* * *

It worked.

She would remember that, afterward — that it *worked*, that for eleven full cycles the wall went up stone by stone and held. The sound of two hundred voices moving in that circling, unresolved melody filled the church the way water fills a vessel, and it was not beautiful, exactly — it was too strange for beauty, too insistent — but it was *strong*. Rosa felt it in her sternum, in her back teeth, in the floor under her feet, a low thrumming like the church itself had become an instrument. The candle flames stood perfectly still. The wind outside stopped. Even the light through the windows seemed to lean in and listen.

And somewhere, far off, beneath the music, Rosa heard — or felt, or imagined, she could never afterward say which — a grinding. Deep. Mineral. The sound of a door pressing back into a frame that had been cut for it two hundred years ago.

The second cycle. The fourth. The seventh. The melody circled, rose, returned. The tumblers turned. Somewhere in the earth under the quarry, something that had been leaning its weight against two centuries of song felt that weight return, and Rosa would have sworn on her mother's grave that she heard it *recoil*.

At the ninth cycle, the woman next to her — Pilar, the baker's widow, a warm strong alto — went silent.

Rosa kept singing. There was no choice; the verse did not permit hesitation, the tumblers would not wait. But she turned her head, and she saw it happening, and her blood went cold in a way she had never known blood could go.

Pilar's mouth was still moving. Her lips shaped the words. But no sound came out, and her eyes had gone wide and white-rimmed, and her hands, which had been folded in her lap, were now gripping the pew in front of her hard enough that her knuckles had gone the color of bone.

She had forgotten the words. Mid-line. Mid-*syllable*. The verse was still circling in her body — Rosa could see it, the way you can see a sleeper running in a dream — but the words themselves had been taken, scooped out of her like the flesh of a fruit, and what remained was the shape of singing with nothing inside it.

And it spread.

The tenth cycle: three more voices gone, then five. The melody that had been a wall became a wall with holes in it, and through the holes Rosa heard — faint, thin, patient — a sound from far beneath the floor. Not the grinding now. A hum. Almost a harmony. The thing behind the door had found the tune, and it was *singing along*, filling every gap the forgotten words left, threading its own voice through the village's like a weft through a broken warp.

"No," Rosa said, into the music, to no one, to everyone. "Don't listen to it. Don't — the words, think of the words, *hold the words* —"

The eleventh cycle. Half the congregation was mouthing silence now. The other half sang on, and their singing had begun to sound ragged, frightened, each voice alone in a crowd, and the hum beneath the floor rose sweetly to meet every gap, and Rosa understood with a clarity that felt like falling: the verse required *all* of them. The founders' village had been smaller. The founders' village had been whole. Memory itself was the weapon, and the enemy had spent a week sharpening it, taking a name here, a face there, a line of a song, so that when the village finally stood to sing together, it would stand up incomplete.

It had known. It had *planned* for this. It had been hollowing them out for exactly this moment.

The twelfth cycle began, and Emilio — old Emilio, eighty-six, bass as deep as the church foundations, who had sung at Rosa's wedding and her mother's funeral — opened his mouth for the descending phrase, the one that anchored the whole verse, the keystone note, and nothing came out.

He tried again. His face worked. His hands rose to his throat.

And the hum beneath the floor poured into the gap where his note should have been, and the whole structure of the verse — the circling, rising, returning mechanism that Isandro had called a lock — *jammed*.

* * *

Rosa felt it before she heard it: a shudder through the stone, a wrongness in the air like the moment before lightning. The candles all bent sideways at once, though there was no wind. The light through the windows went the color of old iron.

The backlash came back along the song.

She could not describe it then and could not describe it now, except to say that the verse had been a road, and the road had collapsed, and everything that had been traveling it — two hundred voices' worth of intention, of courage, of desperate collective will — fell all at once back into the church like water finding its level. It passed through her and she heard herself scream, though she did not remember deciding to. It passed through Marisol, who clutched the pew and wept without knowing why. It passed through the children in the back rows, who had been brought because the founders' notation said all voices, and who now cried out in their sleepless, wordless terror.

And it passed through Emilio last of all, because Emilio had been singing the keystone.

He stood up. That was the terrible thing — he *stood*, straightened out of his seat like a man called by name, his face turned up toward the rafters, his mouth open, his eyes fixed on something above the altar that was not the crucifix. For one moment he looked not old but ancient, a man worn down to his last essential outline, and then the outline itself began to go.

"Emilio," Rosa said. She was moving toward him, she realized, though she had no memory of leaving her place. "Emilio, sit down, Emilio—"

He turned his head and looked at her. And there was nothing in his face. Not confusion, not fear — nothing. She had known this man for sixty years. She had known his laugh and his grief and the way he sang the low notes with his eyes closed. She looked into his face and it was a house with all the furniture carried out, every room empty, the windows reflecting only her own horrified face back at her.

He said one word. It might have been a name. It might have been the last syllable of the verse, still trying to complete itself. It might have been nothing at all.

Then he folded, gently, like a coat slipping off a hook, and Anselm caught him before his head struck the stone, and the church was full of screaming, and Rosa was on her knees beside him doing the work her hands knew — tilting, listening, pressing — while her mind stood somewhere outside the whole scene and observed, with a coldness she would hate herself for later, that his heart had stopped the moment the verse jammed, that the keystone had been pulled out of him, that the thing behind the door had not merely survived their song.

It had *billed* them for the attempt.

* * *

They laid him in the vestry because no one could bear to carry him out past the congregation, and the congregation could not stop singing — that was the worst of it, that half of them were still singing, still circling the melody with the words gone, holding the shape of the wall because letting go felt like opening a door. Anselm knelt over Emilio and prayed the last rites with a voice that shook on every word, and when he came to the part about the resurrection of the body, his voice failed entirely, because they had all seen what had been taken, and it was not the body. The body was fine. The body had been the least of him.

Rosa stood at the vestry door with Marisol's arm around her shoulders, and she watched Isandro Vasquez push through the crowd.

The folklorist knelt by the body, and she did not pray. She took Emilio's hand and held it, and her lips moved — Rosa caught fragments, and understood she was reciting, giving him back whatever pieces of him she had recorded, his voice, his songs, the names of his grandchildren that he'd told her about in their interviews, feeding them to him like a fire being fed after the flame has gone. It was too late. Rosa knew it was too late. But she watched the woman do it anyway, and something in her chest cracked open, and what came out was not grief.

It was fury.

"Get up," Rosa said. Her voice came out low and terrible and she did not recognize it. "All of you. Stop singing. Stop *kneeling*." She turned on the congregation, on their wet faces, their trembling hands. "It took Emilio because we were already half-empty. It has been emptying us for weeks — a name here, a face there, a line of a song — so that when we finally stood together, we'd stand up *incomplete*. It planned this. It has been planning this since the day the road crew's drills touched that stone."

Silence, except for the ragged dying-away of the last voices.

"And we will not win by singing harder." She looked at Isandro, still kneeling, still holding the dead man's hand. "We will not win by courage. Courage is nothing against a thing that eats the memory of courage out of you while you're standing on it. The founders had something we don't. They were *whole*."

Isandro looked up at her then, and Rosa saw the folklorist's eyes, and what she saw in them frightened her more than the hum beneath the floor. Because Isandro was listening to the word *whole* the way a starving woman listens to the word *bread*.

"There's a fourth verse," Isandro said quietly. "In the founders' score. Anselm showed me the notation last night." She rose, still holding Emilio's hand a moment before letting it go, letting it rest. "And Rosa — the fourth verse can't be sung by a village. It doesn't ask for a congregation."

Marisol's grip tightened on Rosa's arm. "What does it ask for?"

Isandro's gaze went, without her seeming to will it, to the back of the church — to where Mateo Reyes stood alone by the doors, the last of the founders' blood, his face gray as quarry stone.

"It asks for a name," she said. "And there's only one name left."

Outside, far off, beneath the wind and the weeping and the bells that Anselm had not yet found the strength to silence, something behind a cracked door began — softly, sweetly, in a voice that was almost kind — to sing *Mateo's name*.
