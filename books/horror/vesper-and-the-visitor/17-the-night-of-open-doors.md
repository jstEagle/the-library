## 17. The Night of Open Doors

Delia Cruz woke to the sound of her own front door swinging on its hinges.

Not opening — she had heard doors open all her life, and this was not that. This was slower, a long wooden groan, as if the door had been standing open for hours and was only now remembering to complain about it. She lay in the dark of her bedroom listening to it move in a wind she could not feel through the closed window, and she thought, with the flat clarity of a woman who has stopped believing in coincidences: *I locked that door. I checked it twice.*

She always checked twice. Twenty-three years running crews had taught her that a lock unchecked is an accident waiting to be scheduled.

The hallway floor was cold under her bare feet. The door at the end of the hall stood wide to the gray pre-dawn, and beyond it her yard stood silvered with frost, and beyond the yard the road ran down toward Vesper, and along that road, as far as the light reached, other doors stood open too. She could see them from her porch like a row of pulled teeth: the Herrera place, the old post office, the chapel side-door that Father Anselm kept chained since spring. All open. All breathing out the warm smell of slept-in houses into air that should have been empty.

And people standing in their doorways. Not coming out. Just standing, holding their robes closed against the cold, looking back into their own homes the way you look into a room where something has died.

"Marisol," Delia called across the road, because the Herrera girl was out there in a nightgown, eleven years old, barefoot on the frost. "Marisol, get inside, honey."

The girl turned. Her face was fine. Her face was completely fine, and that was the worst part, because when she spoke her voice came out level and puzzled and wrong.

"Señora Cruz, who is Marisol?"

* * *

Rosa Ibarra found the choir list useless by seven o'clock.

She had kept it for forty years — names, ranges, which soprano could be counted on for funerals and which tenor went flat when he cried — and she sat at her kitchen table with it spread out before her, making marks. A check meant present. A line meant gone. By the time the sun cleared the quarry ridge she had drawn more lines than checks, and the lines were not for absence. They were for forgetting.

Elena Vásquez had come to her door at first light asking why there were strangers' clothes in her closet. The Bautista brothers, who shared a house and half a lifetime of arguments, had met on the street and shaken hands like men being introduced. Old Pilar Sotomayor stood in the churchyard calling her dead husband's name, not grieving — searching, turning in circles, a woman who had lost something in a field and could not remember what it looked like.

Every one of them had slept near an open door.

Rosa understood the pattern before she understood anything else, because patterns were what a lifetime of music taught you. Seven days. Everyone who had lost time had lost exactly seven days — the week since the third verse had faltered in her church, since Doña Mercedes had fallen dead between one note and the next, since the crack in the quarry door had sealed halfway and then stopped sealing, hungry, waiting. The entity had taken its payment in installments. It had reached through every unlocked threshold in Vesper and helped itself to a week of each household's life, the way you'd take eggs from a coop — politely, thoroughly, leaving the shells.

It had not taken hers. She had sat up all night after the funeral, singing the third verse under her breath in the dark, over and over, and her door had been locked, and her door was still locked, and she understood that too. You did not leave a song unfinished in a house where the thing outside knew how to listen.

She gathered the survivors in the church because there was nowhere else, and because if the village was going to break, it would break better under a roof built for grief.

* * *

Tomás did not go to the church.

Tomás went to his wife's grave, because he had woken at dawn with forty years missing and then, an hour later, with something worse: the memory of her, returned whole. Her name — Lucía, Lucía, he said it aloud to make sure it stayed — her laugh, the way she hummed while kneading bread, the exact weight of her head against his shoulder in the last winter. All of it back, all at once, like water returning to a dry well, and he knelt in the frozen grass of the churchyard sobbing with gratitude and terror in equal measure, because he knew now what it felt like to have a person erased from him, and he knew the thing in the quarry could do it again whenever it liked.

He had been its first. He remembered standing at the quarry fence at dawn weeks ago with nothing behind his eyes but morning, and he remembered Isandro Vasquez finding him there, and he remembered her playing those recordings beside his cot night after night, feeding him back to himself verse by verse until the hollow places filled.

She was at the churchyard gate when he finally looked up. He hadn't heard her come. She looked like a woman who had been awake for days — he knew the look; he'd worn it himself — and she was carrying her notebooks hugged to her chest like a child carries a schoolbook in a storm.

"You felt it come back," she said. Not a question.

"All of it." He wiped his face with a hand that shook. "Niña, all of it. Why would it give back what it took?"

Isandro looked down the hill toward the quarry, where the concrete trucks idled behind the company's new fence, and her face did something Tomás had seen once before, on his grandfather, the day the bank took the farm: the look of a person doing arithmetic they already know ends in loss.

"Because it's finished taking," she said. "It has what it needs. Now it wants us to run."

* * *

By noon, half of Vesper had decided to run.

They packed in the ugly way people pack when fear does the packing — armfuls instead of suitcases, children carried without coats, photographs left face-down on tables because no one could remember whose faces they were. The Herrera family loaded their truck in silence, and Señor Herrera stood a long moment at his threshold before pulling the door shut behind him, and the sound of it closing was so ordinary that his wife began to cry.

Delia worked the road. It was the only thing she knew how to do — stand in front of moving vehicles and organize them. She waved the convoy past the quarry turnoff, past the company fence, past the concrete cap that was supposed to have ended everything and had only made the thing beneath it patient. The authorities had come and gone with their gas-leak report; the company had signed off on entombment; and now the village itself was evacuating, and Delia stood in the middle of the road directing traffic away from a hole in the world, and no report, no contract clause, no chain of command covered any of it.

A car slowed beside her. Mateo Reyes leaned out, and his face was gray.

"They're going," he said. "Most of them."

"I know."

"If enough of them leave—" He stopped. He didn't finish it, but Delia had spent three months learning the shape of this thing, and she finished it herself: if the village emptied, the verses would have no voices. The fourth verse had been sung in Rosa's bargain with the whole town behind it, diluting the claim, and it had cost Rosa her voice and bought half a seal. A village of two hundred could share a debt. A village of thirty could not. An empty valley was just a bigger door.

"Then stop them," Delia said.

"With what?" And there it was, the question underneath everything, the question the Church had dodged for two centuries and the company had buried under concrete and the founders had answered only once, at a price no one had ever written down. "Delia, I can't ask these people to stay. Their children are waking up not knowing their own names. What do I say? *Stand ground*?"

Delia watched the last truck crest the ridge and dwindle. The road was empty now except for frost and tire tracks and the two of them.

"You tell them the truth," she said. "That running doesn't work. That it knows how doors work better than we do." She nodded back toward the village, at the houses with their windows dark, at the doors — she made herself look — every one of them shut now, every one of them, she would bet her life, unlocked from the inside by hands no one had seen. "I've poured concrete over that thing and it hummed through the pour. There's no wall it respects. There's only the song."

* * *

In the church, Rosa counted what remained.

Thirty-one souls sat in the pews, and the number hurt worse than any casualty list she had ever read, because these were not the brave ones or the faithful ones particularly — they were the ones who had nowhere else to go, the old, the stubborn, the rooted, and a handful who had simply woken up too hollowed to know which direction flight pointed. Father Anselm stood at the altar looking like a man whose institution had failed him so thoroughly that only the building itself still held meaning. Isandro sat in the front pew with her notebooks open across her knees, writing, always writing, in that fast desperate hand, and Mateo stood beside her not touching her but near enough that the nearness said everything.

"We are thirty-one," Rosa said, and her voice — the voice that remained, thin and airless where the entity had taken the richness — carried anyway, because some things carry regardless. "Last Sunday we were two hundred and we sang the third verse together and it worked, God forgive us, it worked, until we forgot. We forgot because it *made* us forget, and we thought that meant we were too weak." She laid her flattened palm on the hymnal board. "We were never too weak. We were singing a song built to be sung by fewer voices than ours. Stronger ones. Willing ones."

Isandro's pen stopped moving.

Rosa saw it stop. She had spent a lifetime watching musicians hear something in a score before they could explain it, and she watched it happen now on the folklorist's face — the moment the notation resolves, the moment the impossible instruction becomes legible. Isandro looked down at the founders' score open in her lap, at the final movement with its strange dense clusters of notes, and her lips moved, reading, translating, and then went very still.

"What is it?" Mateo asked quietly.

"Nothing," Isandro said. "Not yet. Let Rosa finish."

But her hand had moved, casual as breathing, to cover the page.

Rosa continued. She told them what they already knew and needed to hear spoken: that the fourth verse had been sung in Mateo's name and paid for with her voice, and that the seal had held halfway, and that halfway was a debt outstanding. She told them the entity had taken a week from every open door and would take more, that it was patient now because patience was working. And then she said the thing that Delia, arriving late and standing in the back beside Tomás, would carry out of the church and repeat to herself for the rest of her life:

"It offered Mateo mercy because it wanted us divided — one man's sacrifice against everyone's survival, so we would spend our love arguing. It opened our doors because it wanted us scattered. Every move it makes is a move to make us smaller. So we will not be smaller." Her ruined voice cracked and she let it. "We are thirty-one. Tomorrow we learn the fourth verse properly, all of us, every voice we have left. And the day after, whatever remains of us sings it again."

No one cheered. That wasn't the kind of village this was anymore. But no one left, either, and in a church full of people who had spent the morning fleeing, staying was its own kind of anthem.

* * *

Isandro walked home alone in the blue hour, and the doors watched her go.

That was how it felt. Every house she passed stood shuttered and silent, but she could feel the thresholds the way you feel a missing tooth — places where the boundary between inside and outside had been tasted, tested, found permeable. At her own rented room she stood a long moment with her key in the lock before going in, and then, inside, she did what she had done every night for a week: she dragged the dresser against the door, lit the lamp, and opened the founders' score.

The final movement waited for her the way it had waited for two hundred years.

She had told Rosa the truth — *not yet* — but not the whole truth, and lying to Rosa had cost her something she suspected she would not get back. Because she had deciphered it that afternoon, in the church, while Rosa spoke and Mateo stood close and warm and alive beside her, and the notation had resolved into instruction, and the instruction was simple the way all terrible things are simple.

The fifth verse could not be sung by the village. Could not be diluted, channeled, shared. The fourth verse had proven that — half a seal for a whole town's courage and one woman's voice. The fifth demanded a single singer, named in the score's own margin in handwriting gone brown with age, and the requirement was written not in words but in the music itself: a line that began on the singer's first memory and ended on her last, every note a thing surrendered, a melody that could only be performed once because it consumed its own material as it went.

*A willing sacrifice of everything the singer loves.* Everything the singer *remembers*. Sung from the far side of the door.

There was a space in the margin where the founder's name had been written, and the ink had been scraped away — razored, like the hymnal pages, like the parish registers — because even the founders had not wanted the invitation legible. But the musical requirements remained. Someone who stays behind willingly. Someone with a whole remembered life to give.

Someone like a folklorist who had spent months recording her own childhood against the erosion, who had a sister and a fire and a promise archived in notebooks and in flesh, a life documented so thoroughly it could be handed over entire.

Isandro sat with the lamp burning and her hand flat on the page, and outside, faint through the walls, she could hear the quarry humming — patient, satisfied, certain that tomorrow the village would scatter and the debt would come due. It did not know yet. It thought it had won by making them afraid.

Let it think so. Fear was a door too, and she had learned from the best how doors worked.

She turned to a fresh notebook page and wrote, in her fast desperate hand, the first line of a plan she would tell no one:

*Tomorrow, copy everything. Leave nothing of me unrecorded.*

Then she blew out the lamp and, in the dark, began to practice.
