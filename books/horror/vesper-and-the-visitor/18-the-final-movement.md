## 18. The Final Movement

The score had been waiting for her the way a trap waits — patiently, without malice, with a kind of architectural indifference.

Isandro found it on the third night of reading Anselm's papers by lamplight in the archive's back room, where the smell of vellum and candle smoke had soaked into the stone over two centuries and now rose like a confession whenever the room warmed. The founders' original score was bound in calfskin gone the color of old teeth, its margins crowded with annotations in at least four hands, some of them shaking, one of them — she was almost certain — written in a hurry, by candlelight, by someone who knew they would not finish.

She had been translating the fifth movement for two hours before she understood what she was looking at.

The notation was wrong. Not corrupted — wrong. Deliberately, precisely wrong, in the way that only a trained musician or a very careful liar could manage. The time signature shifted at the fifth verse's opening bar, and where every other movement of the ritual-song resolved toward the tonic — toward home, toward the village, toward the door — this one resolved outward, past the final cadence, into a rest that had no business existing. A rest marked not with the usual squiggle but with a small, careful drawing: a hand, palm open, fingers relaxed.

She had seen that hand before. In the margins of the razored hymnals. In the carvings above the door at the quarry, worn nearly smooth by generations of touching. She had photographed it a dozen times and catalogued it as *decorative*.

It was not decorative. It was a signature. It was an instruction.

*Whoever sings this verse must be the one who opens the hand.*

Isandro set down her pencil and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, in the rectory, Father Anselm was sleeping the sleep of a man who had given away the last of his secrets and was not sure yet whether it had been a kindness or a cruelty.

She looked again. She made herself look again, the way she had made herself look at the photographs of Tomás's face after the first verse took his forty years, the way she had made herself look at the black water in the well bucket, at the crack in the door a finger's width wide, at the notebook page where her sister's name had been that morning and was, by evening, a smudge of graphite and a hole shaped like a person.

The fifth movement's vocal line climbed where the others descended. Every verse before it had been a lowering — the founders singing the thing down, down, down into the bedrock, pressing it flat with melody the way you'd press a lid onto a boiling pot. But the fifth verse ascended. It rose past the comfortable range of the alto who had first notated it, past the tenor annotations squeezed between the staves, up into a register that no untrained village voice would reach and hold and *keep* — and at its summit, where the melody finally, finally came to rest, the notation changed one last time.

The note was not written on the staff. It was written *above* it, floating free, tethered by nothing, and beside it, in that hurried fourth hand, a single word in old Spanish:

*Quedándose.*

Staying. The one who stays.

She worked it through the way she worked everything through — methodically, obsessively, the way she'd been working since the night she realized her own childhood was being rewritten behind her eyes. The verses were a lock with five tumblers. The first four could be sung by the village, by the descendants, by the desperate and the brave — Rosa had proven that, had paid her voice to prove it. But the fifth tumbler was not a note. It was a position. It required a body on the inside of the door, a voice sounding from within the cage, a singer who could not walk away when the last phrase ended because there would be no *away* to walk to.

And the payment — she traced the little marginal figures, the tally marks beside the ascending line, one for each phrase — the payment was not blood, not breath, not years. The marginalia was explicit once you knew to read it. Beside the fourth phrase, in the shaking hand: *toda la vida recordada.* All the remembered life. Not the life itself. The *remembered* life. Everything the singer carried. Every face, every fire, every promise, every song they'd ever learned and every song they'd ever refused to sing twice.

The entity fed on memory. She had known that since Tomás. She had known it in her own skull since the night she'd reached for her mother's voice and found a page instead.

The fifth verse was not a wall. It was a meal — a final, willing, total meal, offered from inside the door, and in exchange the door closed forever, because a lock that has been fed everything has no reason to open again. The founders had known. The founders had drawn a hand opening, palm up, fingers loose: *here, take it, I give it.*

One of them had stayed. Two centuries ago, someone had stood on the inside of that seamless stone and sung themselves into nothing so that a village could keep its wells and its children and its ballads — and then the survivors had done the most human thing imaginable. They had razored the pages. They had inked out the names. They had taught their children to stop mid-verse, to refuse the song twice, to forget on purpose — because the last line of the ritual was not a melody you could carry safely. It was a door that only opened inward.

Isandro sat very still for a long time.

The lamp hissed. The vellum breathed its candle-smoke breath. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse through stone, she could almost hear it — the thing at the quarry, humming through the crack, patient, hungry, *finishing the work in reverse*, verse by verse, name by name, memory by memory, and now it had begun on her, on the sister and the fire and the promise, editing her the way she edited field recordings, cutting the noise, keeping the signal, and the signal was *her*.

She thought about the notation. She thought about the rest that had no business existing.

Then she did the thing she had become very good at, the thing that had kept her whole this long: she documented. She copied the fifth movement onto a fresh page in her smallest notebook, in her tightest hand, and beside it she wrote nothing of what it meant. She wrote a false annotation — *requires further study; notation ambiguous; consult Anselm re: alternate reading* — and she buried the true reading in a place no one would think to look: in the field notebook, in her private shorthand, the one she'd invented in graduate school, the one no one else alive could read.

*The last verse must be sung from inside. The price is everything remembered. It cannot be Mateo. It cannot be anyone who is loved, because the door does not ask for love — it asks for the whole of a life, and a life freely given must be one the giver can bear to lose, and no one in this village can bear to lose what they have left. But I have already lost most of mine. I am, God forgive me for the arithmetic, the cheapest voice available.*

She closed the notebook. Her hand was steady. She noticed that it was steady and found that strange, and then found that she had stopped being surprised by the strange things her body did now, the calm, the clarity, the terrible tidiness of a mind that had decided something and was already arranging the furniture of its remaining days.

She thought of Mateo.

That was the trap inside the trap, she understood now. The entity whispered his name through the crack because he was the last founder's blood and the door had a taste for symmetry — the line that built the cage, the line that feeds it. But the score did not require his blood. She read the notation again to be sure, a fourth time, a fifth, hating herself a little for hoping she was wrong. It required a willing voice and a whole remembered life. The blood requirement was the fourth verse's, and Rosa had already broken that one open, had diluted it through the whole village, had paid for the dilution with her voice — and in doing so had shown Isandro the way. The rules bent for courage. The rules bent for love, if the love was shaped correctly.

The entity wanted Mateo because Mateo was what she loved, and the thing behind the door had learned, over two centuries of listening, that the fastest way into a human heart was through the thing it could least afford to lose.

*Then I will be the thing it cannot afford to lose,* she thought, and almost laughed at the arrogance of it, the folklorist's arrogance, the scholar's belief that understanding a thing means mastering it. But it was not arrogance. It was the plainest arithmetic she had ever done. The door needed a singer who stayed. She could stay. She had been staying her whole life — in archives, in dead villages, in the pauses where other people's songs broke off. She had made a career of standing inside other people's silences.

This one would simply be the last silence, and she would be inside it, and it would hold.

She blew out the lamp.

* * *

She told no one.

That was the discipline of it, and the mercy. She had watched Rosa come back from the quarry with her voice gone, watched the village learn to read the choir leader's hands the way you learn to read weather, and she knew what telling them would do. Anselm would try to find a theological escape hatch and lose a week they didn't have. The villagers would either forbid her or — worse — begin rehearsing her eulogy in their eyes, and she could not sing a closing verse through a room full of people already grieving her. Grief was a memory too. Grief was exactly the kind of thing the door would taste in her voice and refuse.

No. If the fifth verse required everything remembered, then she would go to it *light*. She would spend her remaining days the way she had spent her whole career: distributing the archive. Every memory that mattered would be copied out — into notebooks, into recordings, into Mateo's keeping — so that when she stood inside the door and opened her hand, she would not be emptying a life. She would be surrendering a *duplicate*. The originals would live in ink and magnetic tape and the stubborn memory of a man who grew tomatoes in soil his family had shamed.

It was not a loophole. She was honest enough with herself to know that. The door would take what she carried, and what she carried was herself, and there was no version of the notation that let her survive the singing. The rest with no business existing had no business existing precisely because the person who reached it did not.

But the village would keep her sister. The village would keep the fire, and the promise, and the recordings of songs no one would sing twice — they would sing them now, she would make sure of it, twice and three times and until the syllables wore smooth as the carvings above the door. She had come to Vesper to record songs that were dying. She would leave behind songs that could not die, because she had made them everyone's, and a song in thirty mouths is a song no hunger can reach.

That was the shape of it. Love against duty, and she had spent three sleepless nights looking for the seam between them, and there wasn't one, and the absence of the seam was, in the end, the answer. She loved Mateo. She loved this stubborn, erasing, half-hollowed village that had tried so hard to forget and was now, song by song, learning to remember. Duty was love with its sleeves rolled up. She would do both. She would do both by doing one.

* * *

On her last evening at the archive — though no one but her knew it was the last — she sat with Anselm's predecessor's letters one final time and found, in the earliest of them, a line she had skimmed past a dozen times:

*The door is not cruel. It is only thorough. It takes what is offered, and it offers in return the one thing we cannot make: an ending.*

She copied that out too, in her shorthand, on the page where the true reading lived, and beneath it she wrote the last thing she would ever write for herself:

*If you are reading this, Mateo — and someday you will be, because you are the man who reads everything I leave behind — then it worked. Don't look for the seam. There isn't one. I chose both.*

She closed the notebook, and the lamplight caught the cover, and somewhere far off, down past the church, past the shuttered houses, past the road Delia had sealed with chain and concrete barriers, the quarry hummed its patient, hungry note — and Isandro Vasquez, folklorist, archivist of the almost-forgotten, hummed it back.

Perfect pitch. Perfect memory. For one more night, both were hers.

Tomorrow she would begin the copying. Tomorrow she would start giving everything away — the notebooks, the tapes, the sister, the fire, the promise — arranging her life into the hands of the only person she trusted to carry it. Tomorrow she would kiss him at the quarry fence and lie to him with her whole loving heart.

But tonight, she sat alone in the candle-smoke dark with the founders' score open on her knees, and she sang the fifth verse's first phrase — silently, breath only, no voice at all — to learn its shape.

It fit her mouth like a key she'd been carrying all her life without knowing what it opened.

Outside, the first snow of the season began to fall on Vesper, soft and silent, covering the road to the quarry — and covering, too, the fresh footprints of something that had climbed up out of the cracked door's shadow and stood for a long time beneath her window, listening, before it turned and walked back down into the dark.
