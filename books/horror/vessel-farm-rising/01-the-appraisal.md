## 1. The Appraisal

The satnav gave up on me a mile out, which I took as a bad omen and then, being who I am, as a data point. Country lanes don't confuse machines; they confuse the assumptions machines are built on. Hollowmere Farm sat at the end of one of those assumptions — a lane that bent twice where the map said once, then opened onto a yard of frozen mud and a house that looked like it had been waiting for someone to arrive so it could stop pretending to be empty.

I'm Kestrel Okafor. For eleven years I've measured other people's buildings for a living: walls, floor areas, damp courses, the honest arithmetic of square footage. Houses don't lie to surveyors. They can't afford to. A house that lies gets found out by the next buyer, and then everyone calls the last person who signed the report.

So when I got out of the car with my laser measure, my clipboard, and my thermos of coffee gone lukewarm from the drive, I was in an excellent mood. The estate agent had said the vendor wanted a quick turnaround. Quick turnarounds mean good fees. The frost made everything look scrubbed and new, like the world had been reset overnight to factory settings.

Hollowmere Farm was a two-storey stone farmhouse, Georgian bones with Victorian additions, slate roof sagging gently at the hips the way old roofs do, like a tired horse shifting its weight. Flint walls. Six windows across the front, one of them boarded. A barn converted sometime in the nineties by someone with more enthusiasm than skill. All of it ordinary. All of it, I noted with professional satisfaction, *appraisable*.

The front door wasn't locked. It also wasn't latched — it swung at my touch with a slow, deliberate ease, the way doors move in houses where someone has just left through them. I stood in the doorway for a moment, listening. Nothing. The particular nothing of an unoccupied building, which is different from the nothing of an empty field. Fields have wind in them. Empty houses have held breath.

"Hello?" I said, because protocol, and because saying it aloud makes you feel less like a burglar.

The hallway answered with its own acoustics, which is normal. What wasn't normal — though it took me most of the morning to admit this to myself — was that the hallway seemed to answer *slightly late*, as if it were considering my voice before echoing it back.

I got to work. That's the trick with any job that makes your skin prickle: get to work. Numbers are a kind of prayer against nonsense. I started in the parlour, measuring wall to wall, noting the inglenook fireplace, the beam over the window that some ancestor had hacked flat with an adze. Twelve feet four by fourteen feet two. Ceiling height seven foot six at the lowest point. I wrote it all down in my tidy hand, and the numbers behaved themselves, and I felt better.

The kitchen was next. Flagstone floor, Aga long since cold, a dresser built into an alcove. I paced it twice because the first measurement came out odd — not impossible, just odd, the room reading three inches narrower along the north wall than the south, which happens in old houses when walls lean. Except these walls didn't lean. I checked with the level on my phone. Plumb as a churchwarden.

Three inches. I wrote down both figures and moved on. Surveyors learn early that a house full of perfect right angles is a house that's been rebuilt, and rebuilt houses tell you less than crooked ones. Crookedness is history. I told myself the discrepancy was history too.

* * *

The trouble started on the stairs.

Not immediately. The stairs were fine going up — thirteen treads, worn dished in the middle, banister polished by a century of hands. I measured the stairwell, photographed the plasterwork, noted a hairline crack above the landing that I'd flag for the structural report. Then I went up to the bedrooms.

There were five. Four of them were exactly what you'd expect: small, cold, papered in patterns that had gone out of fashion twice each. In the largest, the wallpaper was a pattern of tiny blue birds, faded almost to grey, and I remember thinking that whoever had chosen it had probably been choosing hope.

The fifth bedroom was at the end of the corridor, behind a door that stuck. I shouldered it open and stopped.

It was a child's room. Not in the way the others were rooms that children might once have slept in — there were no toys, no small furniture, no evidence of occupancy at all. It was a child's room the way a stage set is a kitchen. The proportions were wrong for the house: the ceiling lower than it should have been given the floor below, the window smaller, positioned oddly high in the wall, the way windows are in rooms designed to be looked at rather than lived in. And the wallpaper —

The wallpaper was stars. Yellow stars on a deep blue ground, printed slightly crooked, the repeat misaligned every fourth row. I knew that wallpaper. I knew the specific flaw in the repeat, the star that had been printed upside down just above where the light switch would be if the room had a light switch, which it didn't.

I had that wallpaper when I was seven. My mother put it up herself, in the flat on Delamere Road, and she got the rows crooked in exactly that way, and I used to lie in bed finding the upside-down star and feeling privately that it was mine, that the universe had misprinted one thing just for me.

That flat was two hundred miles away. That wallpaper hadn't been manufactured since 1994. I checked the date on my phone twice, standing in the cold of a stranger's bedroom, breathing air that smelled faintly of something I couldn't name — not dust, not damp. Something warmer. Something like the inside of a coat pocket.

I did what professionals do. I measured the room. Nine feet by eight feet two. Wrote it down. Photographed the wallpaper, though the photo came out blurred, the stars smeared into streaks like rain on glass. I told myself: coincidence. Mass-produced pattern, millions of rolls, someone somewhere had the same crooked print. The human brain is a pattern-matching machine that fires on false positives; this is well documented; this is why we see faces in electrical sockets.

Then I went back downstairs, and the hallway was longer.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I have. Going up, the corridor from the stairhead to the fifth bedroom had taken me perhaps fifteen steps. Coming back, counting without meaning to, the way you count stairs, I reached twenty-two before the landing arrived under my feet. I stopped. I turned around. The corridor stretched away behind me, door-lined, dim, entirely plausible, and entirely twenty-two steps long.

I walked it again. Fifteen steps.

Third time: nineteen.

I stood at the top of the stairs with my laser measure in my hand like a weapon and aimed it down the corridor. 4.6 metres. I aimed it again. 6.1. Again: 5.2. The numbers weren't holding still. They weren't wild, either — they hovered, the way a compass needle hovers near iron, as if the corridor itself were undecided, or curious, or trying several answers to see which one I'd accept.

I laughed. Out loud, alone, in an empty house. It came out higher than I intended.

"Very funny," I said, to the house, or the corridor, or whatever agent of the vendor had rigged — what? Mirrors? Forced perspective? There was no mechanism I could imagine that would do this, and I am very good at imagining mechanisms. It's half my job.

I finished the upstairs quickly after that, working fast, the way you work in a room where someone is watching television with the sound off. Back down. The hallway on the ground floor was fine. Perfectly fine. Eleven steps both ways, twice. I noted the discrepancy upstairs in my book — *corridor, inconsistent internal dims, possible subsidence, recommend further investigation* — and the act of writing it down calmed me enormously. Words in a grid. Problems with recommended actions. This is how I keep the world in its box.

* * *

The window was worse.

It was in the back bedroom, the one over the kitchen, a casement looking east over the garden toward a line of elms and, beyond them, the flat grey suggestion of the marshes. I leaned on the sill to gauge the boundary line for the site plan, and the garden was wrong.

Not dramatically wrong. Wrong the way a photograph is wrong when it's been colour-graded. The elms were bare — fair enough, it was February. But the frost on the grass lay in long shadows that belonged to a low sun, and the sun outside the window was high and white and wintry in a way that cast no shadows at all. Two lights in one scene. And the grass — I looked properly, and the grass was long, unmown, browned at the tips, summer-killed, while beyond the fence the field lay in the same hard green-and-white frost I'd driven through an hour ago.

Last year. Or some year. The window was showing me a different season, patiently, without comment, the way a screen shows a screensaver.

I pulled back from the glass. The reflection in the pane showed me the room behind me: bed-shaped shadow, wardrobe-shaped shadow, and me, clipboard and all, looking exactly as rattled as I felt. When I turned around, the room was just a room. Bare boards, water stain on the ceiling, a wasps' nest husk in the corner like a paper lantern. Ordinary. Almost apologetic.

I went downstairs and drank my terrible coffee standing in the yard, facing the car, letting the real February get into my lungs. Frost. Mud. The smell of silage from a farm I couldn't see. Present tense. I ran the morning back through my head itemising it, filing it: fatigue, low blood sugar, an old house with drafts and echoes and a head full of suggestibility. I've appraised two hundred properties. Not one of them has ever done anything but sit there.

Except.

Except that when I'd photographed the parlour ceiling, checking the shots later on the little screen, there was a patch of plaster above the inglenook that hadn't been visible to the naked eye. A faint discolouration, shaped — and I know how this sounds, I've had eleven years of colleagues telling me how things sound — shaped like a room. Walls, a doorway, a small high window. Rendered in the plaster the way damp renders itself, in tide-marks and blooms, except the bloom had corners. Corners and proportions.

Nine feet by eight feet two.

My childhood bedroom, rendered in a stranger's ceiling, at the exact dimensions I had measured it an hour before. As if the house hadn't shown me the wallpaper out of malice or memory, but had *taken a copy*. As if I'd walked through with my laser measure clicking and the house had clicked back.

I deleted the photo. Then I undeleted it, because deleting evidence is what guilty people do and I refuse to be guilty of anything before lunch. I finished the exterior elevations, photographed the boarded window, the sagging roof hips, the barn. I worked until the light began to go, and the whole time the house sat behind me with that held-breath quiet, and I had the distinct, unprofessional, unforgivable sensation of being *measured*. Wall to wall. Beam to beam. Noted down in a tidy hand.

At the gate I paused, because the light was doing something strange over the marshes — a grey thickening, like smoke without fire — and because the silence had changed texture. Underneath the wind, underneath the distant traffic of the A-road, there was a sound I couldn't place. A single tone, felt more than heard, coming from everywhere and nowhere. From the direction of the village, maybe. From the ground.

From the church, I thought, and then corrected myself, because I'd driven past the church and its tower was visibly empty. No bell. Just an open belfry like a pulled tooth.

The tone swelled once, softly, and stopped.

Behind me, in the empty house, something settled — a beam easing, a door finding its frame. The sound of a ledger being closed. Or opened.

I got in the car and drove back toward the village, and it wasn't until I was halfway there that I realised I was humming. A tune I didn't know, in a minor key, patient as dripping water. Somewhere in the back of my mind, a voice that sounded unsettlingly like mine was counting steps.

Twenty-two. Twenty-two. Twenty-two.
