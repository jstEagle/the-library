## 4. Sunrise, Freely Given

The house had stopped pretending to be a house.

Sable had dragged the dresser from the spare bedroom across the hall and wedged it against her bedroom door, then the desk, then the bookshelf with its paperbacks she'd never read. It wasn't a barricade meant to keep something out. She understood that now. The Visitor did not use doors. It used reflections — the dark window over the cornfield, the bathroom mirror, the black screen of her dead television — and it arrived a half-second before its own image caught up to it, so that it was always already in the room before the room agreed to show it.

She sat on the bed with the founding ledger open across her knees and a cigarette lighter in her hand, though she hadn't smoked in six years. The ledger's leather cover was warm, faintly, like an animal that had been sleeping against her all night.

On the first page, in ink gone brown as dried blood, was the name.

She had read it forty times since midnight. *Ossuary Vane.* No — not Vane. Her eyes kept sliding off it, the way eyes slide off a face in a mirror that isn't quite yours. She made herself look again, letter by letter, tracing each one with her fingertip until the word held still: **OSSUARY HOLLOW-CREEK**. The valley itself. The town had named its hunger after the place, or the place after its hunger; the ledger did not care which came first. Beneath the name, in a different hand, newer, neater — Aldous's hand, she knew it now, the same careful loops he used on faculty memos — was written:

*Sable Vasquez, invited, willing.*

A lie in beautiful penmanship.

Outside, the corn moved without wind. It had been moving for hours, a long slow parting of stalks radiating outward from the field's center, as if something enormous were turning over in its sleep down there among the rows. Every few minutes the window glass flickered — not broke, flickered — and in the flicker she saw faces. Small faces. Wren's mother's mouth on Wren's face. A boy's face wearing a man's patient smile. The seven children, worn like masks by something that had been collecting them the way other things collect coins.

"You could run," said the reflection in the dark window. It spoke with Wren's voice, and Sable's chest clenched, because Wren was downstairs in her kitchen ten minutes ago, holding the ledger's torn corner, saying *I'll stand with you, Miss Vasquez,* before Sable sent her home with strict instructions to lock every door and not look at anything silver.

"No, I couldn't," Sable said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the room — too present, too real, the only thing in the house that happened exactly when it happened. "And I'm not going to."

The glass flickered. The Visitor leaned closer, and its borrowed face did something no child's face should do: it waited.

Sable looked down at the ledger. At her own forged name. At the columns beneath it stretching back two hundred years — names of children, dates mid-semester, and beside each one, in the margin, small notations in a clerk's hand: *barn raised. well sweetened. frost held off till May.* Prosperity, itemized. Love, traded at market price.

"I know what you are," she said. "You're not a monster. Monsters take. You were *given* to. That's worse, isn't it? Two hundred years of parents deciding their luck was worth more than their children, and calling it duty, and calling you the thing to blame." She turned the page. "They wrote your name down so they'd never have to say it out loud. So it would stay a transaction. Well. I'm going to say it."

The lighter trembled in her hand. She let it.

"Ossuary Hollow-Creek," she said.

The corn stopped moving.

Every reflective surface in the room went still at once — the window, the mirror behind her, the brass lamp base, even her own eyes in the dark, which she felt rather than saw. The name filled the room the way cold fills a room when a door opens in winter. And then, for the first time all night, the reflection in the window moved *before* she did — the Visitor flinching ahead of its own image, arriving somewhere new inside itself.

"You have a name," Sable said, softer now. "They took your name and made it a contract clause. They took children and called it invitation. They forged my consent because they've forgotten there's any other way to open a door." She set the ledger flat on her knees. "So here is mine. Not signed. Not coerced. Not traded for a sweetened well."

She lifted her chin and looked directly into the window, into the thing wearing seven stolen faces.

"I give you my name freely. Sable Vasquez. Not as sacrifice. As witness. Come and see what was done in your name. Remember it. Carry it. Because a covenant built on coercion only holds while everyone agrees to lie — and I am done lying for this town."

The silence afterward had texture. It pressed against the windows. In the mirror, the room behind her reflected perfectly, instantly, for the first time — and in that instant of perfect reflection, the Visitor stood in the room itself, between the bed and the door, tall and wrong and made of every yearbook photograph that had ever gone unexplained.

It reached for her.

She didn't flinch. That was the whole of the ritual, she understood suddenly — not the words, not the ledger, but the not-flinching. The covenant required fear; fear was the currency; fear was how two hundred years of parents had paid. She offered the one thing the ledger had no column for: a free gift, given without terror, without bargain, without the small print of survival.

Her name left her like breath leaving lungs. For one vertiginous moment she was nothing — no roster, no records, no documentation, no self — and the Visitor held her name in whatever served it for hands, turning it over, finding no hook in it, no debt, no chain.

A covenant built on coercion cannot hold what is freely given. The thought arrived with the certainty of arithmetic. The gift had nowhere to bind. It simply passed through, and where it passed, it burned.

The ledger ignited in her lap.

Not from the lighter — she hadn't clicked it. The pages browned and curled from the center outward, two hundred years of itemized prosperity going up in a bloom of heat that did not singe her nightgown. The Visitor made a sound then, and it was not Wren's voice or any child's voice; it was the sound of a very old weight being set down after a very long carry. Its borrowed faces slid away one by one, like masks dropping, and beneath them there was nothing at all — nothing, and then, briefly, something like relief.

The mirrors synced. All of them. The spoon fell and fell in the reflection at the same moment it fell in her hand. The window showed only corn, still corn, ordinary corn going gray at the edges with the first suggestion of dawn.

Then the light came up over the valley, and Sable heard the screaming start — joyful, ragged, human — from the direction of the Harrow farm.

* * *

She found them in the cornfield, seven children standing in the flattened circle where the stalks had parted, blinking in the sunrise like children waking from a nap taken in a strange bed. They were older than their rosters said. Time had passed for them somewhere, in whatever room the Visitor wore. But they remembered. That was the miracle Sable would spend years failing to explain to anyone who hadn't seen the ledger burn: they remembered everything, including their mothers' signatures, including the exact weight of the pen.

Wren got there first, having disobeyed every instruction, and did not go pale this time. She walked into the circle and took the smallest girl's hand, and the girl — a Delacroix, Sable realized, from the 1987 enrollment records — looked at her with recognition instead of fear.

By noon, the town knew. There was no hiding it; prosperity collapsed the way a fever breaks, all at once and messily. The bank called in loans it suddenly couldn't honor. The wells, sweetened for generations by arithmetic no one wanted to examine, went bitter in a single afternoon. Aldous resigned by letter, his careful penmanship shaking for the first time in thirty years, and no one in Hollow Creek ever spoke his name comfortably again.

Some parents wept at their doors. Some could not come out at all. Justice, Sable learned, does not require a courtroom; sometimes it is simply the truth arriving, outside any law, and standing in your yard until you look at it.

* * *

In September, Hollow Creek Elementary opened with a class of twenty-three, seven of them new-old students whose paperwork Sable reconstructed herself, line by line, in her meticulous hand. On the first day, Wren raised her hand and asked if they could talk about what happened, really talk about it, and Sable said yes, and pulled the blinds against the morning glare.

That evening, in the rented house, Sable paused in the hallway out of habit and checked the mirror.

It showed only the present: a tired woman, a quiet room, a wall where a barricade had been. Nothing arrived a second early. Nothing waited behind the glass.

But as she turned away, she thought she heard — very far off, at the edge of the valley, under the wind in the corn — a sound like a great name being spoken once, gently, by someone finally setting down a burden. And she found she was not afraid.

She was a witness now. That was her name, freely given, and she intended to keep it.
