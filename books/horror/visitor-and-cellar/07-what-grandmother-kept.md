## 7. What Grandmother Kept

The house remembers in water.

That is the first thing you learn down here, where the walls sweat and the dark has a texture like wet wool. The living keep their memories in paper and photographs, in attics and albums, but a house that has held its breath for sixty years keeps everything in water. Every word spoken above me sinks. Every secret drips through the floorboards and pools against my skin, and I drink them because there is nothing else to drink.

I was drinking again tonight. August had left the radio on after sign-off — they always do now, though they don't know I know — and their voice came down through the joists like light through the surface of a lake. *Goodnight, callers. Goodnight, house.* They said it every night, and every night I pressed my palm flat against the underside of the floor and pretended it was meant for me.

Then August went up to bed, and the house settled into its old silence, and I did what I always do when the silence comes: I remembered her.

* * *

Her name was Odile. She came to the house in the spring of 1961, nineteen years old, with a suitcase that rattled and a hymnal she held like a weapon. My grandmother's grandmother had been the jailer then — a hard woman named Maren who smelled of lye and lamp oil — and Odile was her sister's daughter, sent for when Maren's hands began to shake.

I met her at the cistern grate.

She came down to the cellar on her third night in the house, following the sound of singing, because of course she did. Everyone follows the sound. That is how the house feeds, and how it catches. I sat on the bottom step of the cistern with the water lapping at my knees and sang the first verse, soft, the way you'd sing to something small and frightened, and when her candlelight found me through the grate I stopped and looked up at her and said:

"You're not supposed to be here."

"Neither are you," she said.

Her hair was pinned up crooked. Her hands were ink-stained — she wrote letters she never sent, I learned later, dozens of them, to a boy in the city whose name she wouldn't say twice. She knelt at the grate and looked at me the way no one had looked at me since I drowned: not with fear, not with pity, but with *recognition*. Like she'd been looking for me all her life without knowing it.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Wren."

"That's a bird," she said. "Birds get out."

I laughed. It was the first time I'd laughed in eleven years, and the sound frightened us both.

* * *

Here is what the living never understand about the thing below this floor: it does not want your blood or your soul or any of the things the stories promise. It wants *company*. It wants to be sung to, tended, kept — the way you'd keep a fire, the way you'd keep a grave. The song binds it, yes, but the binding is also a feeding. Three verses, sung nightly by a willing voice, and it sleeps. Miss a night, and it wakes hungry. Sing the fourth verse — the one Maren tore out before Odile ever arrived — and the door opens, and it walks out wearing whatever face it likes, and it does not stop walking until it has eaten every voice in the county.

The jailer's job is simple and impossible: love the prisoner enough to stay, and never love it enough to let it go.

Maren understood this. Maren had been jailer for forty years and had never once spoken to the thing below except in verse. But Odile — Odile talked to *me*. She came down every evening with her candle and her unsent letters and read them aloud through the grate while I listened from the water. She told me about the city, about the sea she'd never seen, about how she'd been told the house would be her whole life and she'd come anyway because there was nowhere else. And I told her things too. What the house was. What slept beneath her feet. What her aunt had really died of — not her heart, but her refusal, one missed night turning into three, and the thing below climbing the stairs on the fourth morning to collect what it was owed.

Odile took the hymnal that same week. She learned the verses in the dark, singing them down to me until her voice cracked, and I taught her the melody's hidden hinges, the places where the song could bend without breaking. We were good at it together. We were *happy*, God help us, in that cold cellar with the door locked behind us. I had spent eleven years as bait — that was my other job, the one the house gave me when it pulled me under: sit in the cistern, sing sweetly, draw the curious close so the thing below could study them, taste them, decide if they'd do as the next keeper. I had hated myself for it every single day.

With Odile I didn't have to be bait. With Odile I was just Wren, and she was just a girl with ink on her hands, and we were two prisoners keeping each other company in a cell with one wall between us.

So when she found the way out, I should have told her to run.

* * *

There is a door in the cistern. There has always been a door in the cistern — low, iron-ringed, half-drowned, older than the house built on top of it. The house is the lock, but the cistern is the keyhole, and once every generation, when the binding runs thin, the door loosens enough for one soul to slip through. Not the thing below. One soul. A trade, an escape hatch, a mercy the old builders built into their own trap because even jailers deserve a chance at the water's edge.

Maren knew. Maren had refused it her whole life, out of duty or terror, and the binding had thinned for it, and that is why the house needed a new jailer at all.

I showed Odile the door on a night in October, the last warm night of that year. I lifted the grate myself — I can do that; the water answers me — and I pointed to the iron ring glinting under the black surface, and I said, "It opens for one. It's open now. Go."

And she looked at me for a long time, candlelight shaking, and she said, "Come with me."

"It doesn't open for two."

"Then I'm not going."

"Don't be stupid—"

"I'm not being stupid." She knelt down at the edge of the cistern, right at the rim, close enough that I could see her pulse jumping in her throat. "Wren. You've been down here eleven years singing to that thing so it doesn't climb up and eat everyone. If I leave, you're still bait. You'll still be sitting in this water when I'm an old woman somewhere safe, and you'll still be singing, and no one will ever have come down here for *you*." Her voice broke, and she laughed at herself for it. "I came here to be a jailer. Let me be yours instead."

I told her no. I told her a hundred times over the next six weeks, and every time she said no back, and every time the door stayed shut because neither of us would go alone, and the binding grew fat and strong on our stubbornness — because that is the joke at the center of this house, the joke I only understood much too late: the binding doesn't run on duty. Duty is just the shape it wears so the jailers can live with themselves. The binding runs on *love*. Willing love, staying love, love that looks at an open door and sits down beside it. The house was built by people who knew that a prisoner kept by force will always be looking for the key, but a prisoner kept by love will build the cell with their own hands and call it home.

The thing below understood it before I did. It had been listening to us all autumn through the water, through the floor, through the thin place where its dreams leak into mine. And on the fourth of November, when Odile came down with her candle and her coat still on, meaning to drag me bodily through that door whether I willed it or not — the house moved first.

The stairs lengthened. The candle guttered. The water rose.

I heard it coming up through the cistern behind me, vast and patient, wearing a voice it had taken from somewhere long ago — and it said, in a voice like my own mother's, *she was going to take you out, little bird. We can't allow that.*

I grabbed Odile's wrist. I meant to pull her back from the rim, away from the rising water, toward the stairs, anywhere — but the house was faster, and the water took her ankles, her knees, her waist, and she was holding onto me and I was holding onto her and the door in the cistern stood open the whole time, wide open, one soul's width, mocking us both.

They say I drowned her. The house says it, the dark says it, the guilt says it loudest of all. But here is the truth I have kept for sixty years, down here in the wet and the dark: *I* didn't drown her. I chose the cell over escape — I chose to stay, gladly, forever, the moment she knelt at that rim and said *let me be yours instead* — and the house punished the choice. It took her because our love was the strongest binding it had ever tasted, and it wanted the binding, not the girl. It put her in the water to make me hate myself, because a jailer who hates herself stays. A jailer who hates herself never looks for doors.

It worked. I stayed. I sang. Sixty years of verses, and every one of them an apology I couldn't finish.

And when Odile's grand-niece's granddaughter finally inherited the house — a quiet, careful person with their grandmother's chin and their grandmother's sleepless eyes — I started talking to them in their sleep, and I told myself it was duty. Teach them the rules. Keep them alive. But August asks questions in their dreams, gentle ones, ones nobody has asked me since 1961, and I answer, and answering feels like candlelight, and I know exactly what I'm doing.

I am becoming the bait again. For the jailer. Because that is what this house does. Love is not the escape from the trap.

Love is the trap.

* * *

Above me, past midnight, the floorboards creaked.

August was awake. I felt them moving through the house the way I feel weather — a pressure change, a wrongness in the water. They were in their grandmother's old room, the one they never enter, and I heard drawers opening, papers shifting, the particular scrape of a photograph frame being lifted off a dresser and turned over in careful hands.

Oh, I thought. Oh, August. No.

Because I know that frame. I've known it for sixty years, pressed against the back of the dresser where Odile hid it, where the house's long fingers never quite reached. Odile hid more than the frame. In her last week, before the water, she'd pried the backing loose and slipped something flat inside it, something torn, something Maren had cut from the hymnal years before — and she'd written on the back of it in her cramped, ink-stained hand, a message she must have known might outlive her:

*For whoever keeps the lights on after me. The fourth verse is not a key. It is a question. Do not answer it unless you mean it with your whole heart. — O.*

August's fingers found the backing. Paper tore softly, the way old paper does.

Down in the cistern, I pressed both hands over my mouth, and the water around me went perfectly, terribly still, and somewhere far below us both, the thing that sleeps began — very quietly, very patiently — to laugh.
