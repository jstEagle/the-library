## 3. Fifteen Years of Explanations

The past, Ulyana would learn much later, does not stay buried. It just waits for you to come home and give it a room.

* * *

Fifteen years earlier, the kitchen of the Dravenmoor house smelled of cinnamon and burnt toast, which was Grandma Odile's signature combination and, she insisted, her secret to longevity. "The toast is the important part," she'd say, scraping it with a butter knife like she was performing minor surgery. "A body needs a little ruin in it to know it's alive."

Teenage Ulyana sat at the table with a geometry textbook open to a page they had no intention of reading, and Vera sat across from them doing Vera things — which that autumn meant drawing. She filled sketchbook after sketchbook with the same subject: the woods behind the house, but wrong. The trees too tall. The spaces between them too deliberate. Like the forest had been arranged by something with an eye for composition and no interest in mercy.

"You're going to crease the page doing that," Ulyana said, because it was easier to criticize the textbook than to say what they actually wanted to say, which was: *Don't go. Don't do the thing I know you're going to do.*

Vera didn't look up. Her pencil moved in small, furious strokes. "You sound like Grandma."

"Grandma's right."

"Grandma's right about toast. Grandma thinks the frost on the windows is 'just weather.'" Vera finally lifted her head, and there it was — that look she'd been wearing for months, the one that made her eyes seem lit from somewhere behind, like a window in an empty house. "You don't hear it at night? Honestly? You sleep through it?"

"Hear what?"

"*That.*" Vera jabbed her pencil toward the ceiling, toward the second floor, toward the room that had been empty since Grandpa died and that Grandma, for reasons she declined to explain, still vacuumed once a week.

And Ulyana — this was the part they would replay for fifteen years, in motel rooms and waiting rooms and the long gray corridor of every sleepless night since — Ulyana shrugged. "I hear the house settling. Old houses settle. That's all it is, Vera. It's all it's ever been."

Because that was the arrangement between the twins, unwritten but iron-clad: Ulyana explained things, and Vera was permitted to be afraid. Ulyana was the one who read the encyclopedia entries on infrasound and thermal expansion and read them *aloud*, in a soothing voice, like a doctor delivering a reassuring diagnosis. Vera was the one who heard footsteps upstairs when everyone else was asleep, who woke with frost on the *inside* of her window, who once came down at three a.m. white as milk and said, "Something counted us, Uly. It got to nine and then it started over."

And Ulyana's job — their sacred, lazy, cowardly job — was to say: *It's the pipes. It's the wind. It's nothing.*

"You always have an explanation," Vera said quietly. "That's the scariest thing about you."

"That's the *safest* thing about me."

"No," Vera said. "Safe would be admitting you don't know. Explanations are just fear wearing a little suit."

Ulyana laughed. They actually laughed, and they would remember the sound of their own laughter for the rest of their life, how bright and stupid it was, how it filled the warm kitchen and bounced off the cinnamon and the burnt toast and the geometry they weren't reading.

"You're being dramatic," they said. "You're always dramatic. It's your best quality and your worst one."

Something went out of Vera's face then. Not anger — worse. *Relief.* Like she'd been holding the argument open as a door, hoping Ulyana would walk through it, and now she was free to close it herself.

"Okay," she said. She closed the sketchbook. "Okay."

* * *

Present. The kitchen was not warm. The kitchen was a museum of cold, the stove a dead gray animal, the refrigerator humming a note slightly flat, as though even the appliances had gone into mourning. Ulyana stood in it at two in the morning holding a glass of water they had no intention of drinking, because sleep had become a country they could no longer get a visa for.

The funeral had been four days ago. Four days since the lid came up and their own face looked back at them. They had not told anyone. Who would they even tell? Mrs. Aldous next door, who had met them at the property line with a casserole and the words "Don't unpack, dear," delivered with the pleasant authority of a woman announcing a change in bus schedules?

The house ticked and settled around them. Old houses settle. That's all it is.

Ulyana almost smiled at themselves. *Explanations are just fear wearing a little suit.* Vera had been a better writer at seventeen than Ulyana would ever be.

Above them — and they felt it before they heard it, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — the ceiling shifted. A footstep. Then another. The particular rhythm of it snagged on something in Ulyana's chest, a fishhook of recognition: *heel, ball, pause. Heel, ball, pause.* Vera had walked like that her whole life, a little hesitation at the end of every step, as if the floor might be a trick.

Grandma used to say Vera walked like she was "leaving room for an apology." Ulyana used to say she walked like the house was listening.

The house was listening. Ulyana stood in the cold kitchen and listened to their dead twin pace the empty room above them, heel-ball-pause, heel-ball-pause, patient as a metronome, and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding for four days: the footsteps had started the night they arrived. The house had kept the appointment.

"Say something," Ulyana whispered to the ceiling. Their breath fogged, which it shouldn't have, not indoors, not yet. "Say anything. Yell at me. You were always better at yelling."

The footsteps stopped.

In the silence that followed, Ulyana heard — or imagined, and God, they hoped imagined, and God, they hoped *not* — a sound like a pencil being set down on a desk.

* * *

The fight, when it finally came, three days after the kitchen, happened in the barn, which was fitting, because the barn was where the twins kept everything they didn't want Grandma to find: a cache of comic books, a bottle of peach schnapps with two inches gone, Vera's sketchbooks from the *really* bad year, and a dartboard with the faces of certain Vesper Hollow townspeople inked onto the cork.

"You're not coming," Vera said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation either. It was worse. It was a weather report. "You're staying. You're going to stay here and explain things until it's your turn."

"My turn for *what?*"

Vera looked at them for a long moment, and Ulyana watched her decide — actually watched the decision happen behind her eyes, a door closing, a light going off room by room — that Ulyana was never going to understand, and that loving someone who wouldn't understand was its own kind of slow drowning.

"The ledger," Vera said finally. "In the church basement. Have you ever even looked at it?"

"It's a list of founding families. It's a boring list. I *have* looked at it, fourth grade, field trip, Mrs. Pell made us—"

"There's a column, Uly. It just says *Given.* And our name's in it."

Ulyana laughed again. God. The laughter again. "It's a donation column. *Given to the church fund.* Vera, I swear—"

"Read it," Vera said. "Actually read it. Before you explain it to me. Just once in your life, read the thing first and explain it after." She was crying now, furious about it, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like the tears were a mechanical fault. "I'm leaving tomorrow. The bus comes at six. I'm telling you because you're my twin and you deserve to hear it from me and not from the town, and I'm asking you — one time, I'm asking you — to come with me."

And here was the moment. Here was the hinge the whole rest of Ulyana's life had swung on, though they wouldn't know it for years. Vera stood in the barn doorway with her bag already packed behind her, her sketchbooks under her arm, eighteen years old and terrified and *braver than anyone Ulyana has ever known since*, and she asked.

And Ulyana said: "You're being ridiculous. You're chasing a feeling. There's nothing *here*, Vera. There's nothing in the ledger and nothing in the walls and nothing in the woods. It's an old town that's bad at keeping young people. That's all. That's the explanation. You don't need one because there's nothing to explain."

The barn was very quiet. Somewhere in the loft, a pigeon shifted its weight.

"Okay," Vera said again. The same *okay*. The door-closing *okay*. She shouldered her bag. "When you finally run out of explanations, Uly — and you will, everyone does, the house makes sure of it — don't explain what I did. Just remember I tried to take you with me."

She left at six. Ulyana knew because they got up to watch the bus go, from behind the curtain of their bedroom, telling themselves they hadn't, telling themselves the shape at the window was a reflection.

The last words they ever exchanged in person were Ulyana's. *That's the explanation.* Fifteen years of carrying that. Fifteen years of composing better ones — in the shower, on trains, at 3 a.m. in a dozen rented beds: *Come with me, I'm sorry, you were right, I was scared, I didn't want it to be true because if it was true then I'd spent my whole childhood failing you.* None of the drafts ever got sent. There was no address. There was just, eventually, a phone call from a sheriff's office, and a casket, and a face.

* * *

Present. Morning came late and gray to Vesper Hollow, the way it did in November, the sun rising like an afterthought. Ulyana had slept — finally, horribly — three hours on the living room couch with every light in the house on, and dreamed of the barn, and woken with the word *ledger* in their mouth like a stone.

They made coffee. They burned the toast, on purpose, because a body needs a little ruin in it to know it's alive, and because Grandma Odile had been dead two years and someone had to keep her terrible traditions. The smoke alarm didn't go off. Ulyana noticed that. The smoke alarm had a fresh battery; they'd checked it the first night, the way you check things when you're pretending to be a functional adult in an inherited house. It simply hadn't gone off. As if the smoke had been *permitted*.

"Stop," Ulyana said out loud, to the kitchen, to themselves. "You're doing the thing. You're explaining the smoke alarm. She's been dead two years, the alarm is old, it's *nothing*—"

The word *nothing* came out and immediately curdled in the air, because from upstairs came the sound of a window sash sliding up — the empty room's window, the one Vera used to open at night no matter how cold it got, the one Grandma called "that girl's little door to the wolves."

Ulyana set down the coffee. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; they'd expected shaking, and instead there was this terrible calm, the calm of a person who has finally stopped arguing with the evidence.

They went up the stairs. Heel, ball, pause — they caught themselves doing it. Vera's gait. Grief does that, maybe. Or the house does. Hard to say which was worse.

The empty room was exactly as it had been when they'd dumped their suitcase in it four days ago against Mrs. Aldous's advice: bare mattress, bare dresser, curtains the color of weak tea. The window stood open six inches, and cold poured through it like water through a crack in a dam, and the frost on the glass — on the *inside* of the glass — had formed in ferns and feathers, and in the center of the pane, where the frost grew thickest, there was a shape that was not a fern and not a feather.

It was a chair. Small, crude, drawn in ice as if by a child's finger: a chair at a table, pulled slightly out, as if someone had just stood up from it. Or was about to sit down.

Ulyana stood in the cold a long time. Then they closed the window, and their breath made a small ghost, and they said, to the room, to Vera, to whatever was wearing her footsteps: "I'm not explaining this one."

The room said nothing. But the cold eased — a degree, maybe two — the way a held breath eases. And downstairs, in the hall closet where Grandma Odile had kept the photo albums, the ones Ulyana had been avoiding since the funeral, stacked in their hundreds of weighted pages — something settled, softly, like a deck of cards being cut.

Ulyana went down and stood in front of the closet with their coffee going cold in their hand.

"Fine," they said. "The albums. But I'm reading them before I explain them. Just this once."
