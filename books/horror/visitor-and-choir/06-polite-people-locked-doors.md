## 6. Polite People, Locked Doors

The invitation arrived the way everything arrived in Vesper Hollow: without warning, without explanation, and with an assumption of consent that bordered on theological certainty.

It was a cream-colored card tucked into the storm door frame, written in a looping hand that belonged, Ulyana was fairly sure, to Mrs. Petra Voss, who ran the general store and the church committee and, if rumor held, half the town's opinions. *The congregation of St. Ambrose requests the pleasure of your company at a supper of condolence, Saturday evening, six o'clock.* Below that, in smaller script, as though it were a footnote rather than the entire point: *In memory.*

No name. *In memory* — full stop. As if the deceased had been a season, or a fiscal quarter.

Ulyana stood on grandma's porch holding the card and laughed once, a short ugly sound that startled a crow off the fence.

"In memory of what?" they asked the crow. The crow declined to speculate.

They went anyway. Not out of politeness — grief had burned through their supply of that somewhere around the third casserole — but because Mrs. Aldous had taken their arm outside the churchyard two days prior and said, in her papery voice, "Go to the supper, dear. Watch who watches you." And then she'd patted their hand twice, which from Mrs. Aldous constituted either deep affection or a coded warning, and Ulyana genuinely could not tell which.

So on Saturday evening they put on the black sweater Vera had bought them three Christmases ago — *you look like a very sad poet,* she'd said, delighted — and walked down Crooked Elm Road toward the church hall, breath steaming, boots crunching, feeling like a guest at their own funeral who hadn't gotten the dress code right.

* * *

The hall smelled of roast pork, clove candles, and something underneath both that Ulyana couldn't name — a cold mineral smell, like snow on stone, like the inside of the house when the furnace failed. Long tables draped in white cloth. Lanterns, because the hall's electricity had apparently been "temperamental since the last frost," which raised the question of what the last frost had done to deserve blame for the wiring.

"Ulyana Dravenmoor!" boomed Deacon Harlan Pike, a broad man with a beard like a hearth brush and a handshake engineered to communicate sincerity through sheer pressure. "We're so glad you came. Sit, sit. You must eat. Grief starves people, we can't have that."

He said the word *grief* comfortably, the way one says *weather*. It was the first thing anyone had said all evening that acknowledged Ulyana had lost anything at all.

And yet — Ulyana noticed this within the first four minutes, seated between the Pikes and a couple named Fenwick whose smiles seemed bolted on — nobody said her name.

Not once. Not in the grace, not in the toasts, not in the murmured condolences delivered over the green beans. They said *your loss*. They said *this difficult time*. They said *she* exactly twice, both times by Mrs. Voss, and both times followed immediately by a change of subject so abrupt it left skid marks: *"She always did love this hall — more potatoes, Reverend?"*

It was like watching a town perform surgery on its own vocabulary. Every sentence approached the wound and swerved.

"You know," said Mr. Fenwick, leaning in with the confidence of a man about to say something helpful, "my grandfather always said the Dravenmoor twins were the finest thing this valley ever produced."

"Did he," said Ulyana.

"Oh yes. Finest thing." He beamed. He did not elaborate. He did not say *were*, past tense, except that he had, hadn't he — *were* — and now he was smiling at his plate as though the verb had not just done something terrible in front of everyone.

Ulyana ate a potato. It was excellent. That was somehow worse.

The questions began around the second course, and they all wore the same clothes.

"So you'll be staying through the frost, then?" asked Mrs. Voss, ladling gravy with the serenity of a woman pouring fate.

"I'm staying until the estate is settled," Ulyana said.

"Mm." A nod, gracious, noncommittal, entirely unconvinced. "And after?"

"After what?"

"After the settling." She smiled. Her eyes did not participate. "Frost comes early here. Roads close. Best to know your plans before the knowing gets taken out of your hands."

"That's — a very ominous way to ask about my travel schedule."

Mrs. Voss laughed, a bright genuine-sounding laugh, and moved the gravy onward down the table like a relay runner passing something heavy.

But the question kept coming back, wearing different coats. Deacon Pike asked whether Ulyana was "settled in for the winter." Young Tom Fenwick asked if they'd "be around come hard weather." Even the reverend himself, a small gray man named Osgood with hands folded permanently as if in pre-emptive apology, managed to work into a blessing the phrase *"through the cold months ahead, however many they may be"* — which got a murmur of amen from the room that sounded, to Ulyana's ears, distinctly satisfied.

By dessert — apple dumplings, dense as bricks, sweet enough to hurt — Ulyana had counted eleven variations of the same question. Eleven. They'd started keeping tally on a napkin partly for sanity and partly because counting things was the only ritual they trusted anymore.

Eleven townspeople wanted to know how long they were staying.

Zero townspeople would say their sister's name.

"Do you know what I find interesting?" Ulyana said, to the table generally, in a tone they recognized as their mother's — the dangerous one, the pleasant-blade one. "Nobody here has said her name. Not once. All evening. You've thrown a dinner *for her*, and she's the only person in this room nobody will mention."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like packed snow.

Then Deacon Pike laughed heartily and clapped Ulyana on the shoulder hard enough to relocate something. "Well! Grief makes us strange, doesn't it! More dumpling?"

"No," said Ulyana. "Thank you. No."

"More dumpling," Pike confirmed, and put one on their plate.

* * *

They escaped during the clearing of plates, claiming exhaustion, and nobody stopped them — but everybody watched them go, thirty-odd faces turning in unison with the smooth synchrony of a field of sunflowers following the sun, and Ulyana felt those gazes settle on their back like a coat they hadn't asked to wear.

Outside, the cold hit like a fact. The stars were ferocious, the kind of sky you only get where there's nothing between you and the rest of the universe, and the frost on the church hall windows had grown, since supper began, into long feathering patterns that looked less like ice than like handwriting practiced by someone with too much time and no paper.

"Sheriff Brannic will see you home," said Mrs. Voss, appearing at the door with Ulyana's coat already in her hands. How she'd gotten it, Ulyana didn't know. How she'd known Ulyana was leaving before Ulyana knew it themselves — also unknown. "Roads are slick. We look after our own here."

"Do you," said Ulyana, taking the coat. "That's the general impression, yes."

Brannic was waiting by his truck, engine running, headlights cutting twin cones through the dark. He was a tall, weathered man with a face built for patience and eyes that used it. He opened the passenger door without a word.

"I'm capable of walking four blocks," Ulyana said.

"I don't doubt it." His breath fogged. "Walk anyway. I'll drive slow behind you. Consider it professional courtesy."

"Consider me considering it."

They walked. The truck crawled alongside at funeral pace, and the whole arrangement was so absurd that Ulyana almost laughed — escorted through a town of four hundred people as though the danger were out here, in the open, instead of sitting politely at dinner tables asking about travel plans.

Halfway up Crooked Elm, Brannic rolled down his window.

"They mean well," he said.

"Do they."

"Most of them. Some of them mean well the way a flood means well." He drummed the wheel once. "You asked why nobody says her name."

Ulyana stopped walking. The truck stopped walking, which is to say idling, which is to say the night went very quiet around the engine's mutter.

"It's not disrespect," Brannic said, looking straight ahead through the windshield at nothing. "Around here, saying a name is a kind of invitation. Old habit. Older than the church, probably. You don't call across the ice, you don't whistle at night, and you don't speak the names of the dead in company." A pause. "Especially not *that* name. Especially not lately."

"Lately meaning since I got here."

"Lately meaning since the frost started coming down early." He finally turned his head, and in the dashboard glow his face was unreadable in a way that struck Ulyana as professionally maintained. "You're a smart person, Dravenmoor. Smart people ask good questions. Just — do me one favor. When you find your answers, find them quietly. This town has a long memory and short patience, and I'd hate to write a report about you."

"A report about me," Ulyana repeated. "What report? I haven't done anything."

"Not yet," said Sheriff Brannic, and rolled up his window, and drove away, leaving Ulyana alone on the frozen road with the words *not yet* hanging in the air like breath.

* * *

Grandma's house was dark, of course. Grandma's house was always dark now; the electricity worked fine, but the house had developed a preference for darkness the way old dogs develop preferences about food, and arguing with it exhausted everyone involved.

Ulyana let themselves in, locked the door, threw the bolt, dragged the hall chair under the knob — then stood there feeling ridiculous, because the chair-under-the-knob maneuver was something Vera used to do, back when they were teenagers sharing this house every summer, back when Vera was convinced the attic breathed. Ulyana had teased her mercilessly about it. Now here they were, performing her rituals like inherited choreography.

"Fine," they told the empty hallway. "You win. Again."

They made tea they didn't drink. They checked the photographs on the mantel — eight chairs in the dining room picture, still eight, thank God, though they caught themselves counting twice, then a third time, the way you check a lock.

Upstairs, the footsteps started at 11:40.

They'd been coming for three nights now, always the same: the soft uneven tread along the upstairs hall, pause at the linen closet, pause at the window seat, then down two steps and back. Vera's gait. Vera's exact route — the patrol she'd walked every summer night of their childhood, checking doors, checking windows, checking for whatever it was she was sure was coming. Ulyana had memorized that rhythm without ever meaning to, the way you memorize a heartbeat.

First night, they'd gone up with a flashlight and found nothing but cold floorboards and their own galloping pulse.

Second night, they'd stayed in bed and told themselves it was the house settling. Houses settled. Houses in hard frosts settled *vigorously*.

Tonight, the third night, Ulyana sat on the bottom stair in the dark with a mug of untouched tea and listened, and did neither of those things, and felt something in their chest crack slightly along a fault line that had been forming since the funeral home.

Because tonight, the footsteps paused at the top of the stairs. Tonight, they lingered there, longer than usual, longer than any settling house had any business lingering, and Ulyana — exhausted, paranoid, grieving, furious at a dinner party's worth of polite evasions — heard in that pause something unmistakably patient. Something waiting to see if they'd come up. Something that knew they wouldn't.

"Vera," Ulyana whispered, and hated themselves for it, and whispered it again anyway. "Vera, if that's you—"

The footsteps resumed. Down the hall. Away. The linen closet door opened with its familiar dry click, and closed.

Ulyana sat on the stairs until the tea went fully cold, doing arithmetic they didn't want to do. Eleven people asking how long they were staying. A sheriff warning them to find answers *quietly*. A town that wouldn't speak a dead girl's name, as if names were invitations, as if something might answer.

And a house that answered to her walk.

Their eyes drifted, without permission, down the hall — to the closet under the stairs. Not the linen closet upstairs. The other one. The little door beneath the staircase that had been painted shut for as long as Ulyana could remember, that grandma had always called "the coat cupboard, don't fuss with it," that Vera had once, age thirteen, pressed her ear against and announced, with terrible fourteen-year-old gravity: *it's hollow in there. There's a room behind it.*

Everyone had laughed. Grandma hadn't.

Ulyana set down the cold tea, crossed the hall, and laid their palm flat against the sealed door. The wood was colder than the rest of the house. Colder than the walls. Cold in a way that felt less like temperature and more like attention.

Somewhere upstairs, faint and even, the footsteps started again — and this time, they were coming down.
