## 7. What Vera Left in the Walls

The closet in the upstairs hallway had been sealed for as long as Ulyana could remember, which was a strange thing to say about a closet, because closets were not the kind of thing that accumulated history. They accumulated coats. They accumulated boxes of Christmas lights that worked for exactly one season before surrendering to entropy. They did not, as a rule, get *sealed* — nailed shut, painted over, and then, apparently, forgotten by an entire family for two generations.

And yet. Ulyana stood in the upstairs hallway of their grandmother's house on the fourth morning after the funeral, holding a cup of coffee that had gone cold out of sheer neglect, and looked at the door that was not a door.

It was painted the same yellowed cream as the hallway walls, so thoroughly that the paint had filled the gap between door and frame like frost filling a window crack. There was no handle. There had never been a handle, as far as Ulyana could recall. As a child they had asked Grandma about it exactly once, and Grandma — a woman who would talk your ear off about canning tomatoes or the moral failures of the county zoning board — had gone quiet in a way Ulyana had never seen her go quiet before or since, and said, "That's just how the house is," and then had made pancakes, aggressively, as if pancakes could settle the matter.

The house, Ulyana was learning, was full of things that were "just how the house is."

They had spent the first three days doing the sensible things: meeting with the funeral director about paperwork, fielding phone calls from an aunt in Duluth who wanted to know if the house would be sold, and not sleeping, or sleeping badly, in the narrow bed in the back bedroom where the footsteps had been. The footsteps had not repeated. Ulyana had almost convinced themselves of that. Almost, in the way that a person almost convinces themselves the sound they heard at 3 a.m. was the furnace, even though the furnace was downstairs, and the sound had been on the stairs, and had paused — *paused* — at the fourth step, the loose one, the one Vera used to skip with a little hop because hopping over it made her feel like she was getting away with something.

Ulyana set down the cold coffee, went to the kitchen, and came back with the flat pry bar from the toolbox under the sink, which was the only toolbox in the house and had been Grandma's, containing three screwdrivers, a hammer with a cracked handle, and a jar of nails sorted by nothing in particular.

"You were going to do this eventually," Ulyana told the sealed door, in the tone one uses with a cat that is about to be taken to the vet. "Better on my terms."

The pry bar bit into the seam. The paint cracked in a long white line, and beneath it the wood was pale and clean, as though the door had been shut only yesterday. That was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was the smell that came out when the door gave — not the stale, mousy smell of a closed room, but something colder, cleaner, like the air inside a snowbank. Like the air on the porch the morning Vera left.

The door swung inward with a groan, and Ulyana stood in the doorway of a closet that was not a closet.

It was deeper than the hallway wall had any right to permit. The house was not large. Ulyana had measured nothing, but they knew, the way you know the geography of a childhood home, that this wall backed onto the bathroom, and that the bathroom was not nine feet deep. And yet the space in front of them stretched back into shadow, a narrow room lined with shelves, and on the shelves — stacked with the meticulous care of someone who had organized them many times — were notebooks. Dozens of them. Composition books, spiral-bound, hardcover journals with cracked spines, a few loose sheaves of paper held together with binder clips gone brittle. And on the nearest shelf, on top of the nearest stack, a single index card in Vera's slanted handwriting:

*If you're reading this, then it worked, and I'm sorry, and I need you to be brave for about ten more minutes.*

Ulyana's hands were shaking. They noticed this the way you notice rain — distantly, as a fact about the world rather than the self. They picked up the card. The paper was soft at the edges, handled often. Vera had held this card. Vera had written it and then, apparently, kept holding it, turning it over, worrying it, the way she used to worry the corner of her movie ticket until the whole stub disintegrated.

*Ten more minutes,* the card said. Ulyana looked at the shelves. It was going to take considerably more than ten minutes.

They carried the first armload of notebooks down to the kitchen table, because the kitchen had the good lamp and, more importantly, the kettle. If there was one lesson Grandma Dravenmoor had imparted that had nothing to do with secrets, it was that no crisis should be faced without tea.

* * *

The earliest journal was dated the summer the twins were fifteen. Ulyana recognized the year the way you recognize a scar — by what it cost. That was the summer before Vera left. The summer of the fight. The summer of the last words, which Ulyana had carried for fifteen years like a stone in the shoe: *You always were the coward, Vera. You'd rather run than fix anything.*

Vera's first entry, in rounder, younger handwriting, was dated June 9th.

*Something is wrong with the house and nobody will say what. I heard it again last night — the humming. It comes up through the floor in the dining room, right under the table. Mom says it's pipes. I checked. There are no pipes under the dining room. I crawled under there with a flashlight and the crawlspace is just dirt and one of Grandma's old chair legs, which is weird, because why would there be ONE chair leg?*

Ulyana smiled before they could stop themselves. That was so exactly Vera — the flashlight, the crawlspace, the single forensic detail seized and worried like a terrier with a sock. Fifteen years dead and she could still make Ulyana smile, and the smile hurt worse than the crying had.

*Asked Grandma about the humming at dinner. She dropped her fork. Not dropped like clumsy — dropped like her fingers stopped working. Then she said "old houses settle" and left the table and washed a dish that was already clean. I counted. She washed it four times.*

*Ulyana thinks I'm making it up. Ulyana thinks everything is fine here because Ulyana wants everything to be fine here. Which is its own kind of scary, honestly.*

"Rude," Ulyana said aloud, to an empty kitchen, and turned the page.

The entries came faster as the summer went on. Vera had a scientist's patience and a poet's habit of personification, and the two together produced observations that Ulyana read with the tea going cold beside them:

*July 2. The humming has a pattern. I recorded it on my Walkman. It's eight notes. It never changes. It's not a song, it's more like — you know how a dial tone isn't music but it means the line is OPEN? It's like that. The house is holding a line open. Waiting for someone to pick up.*

*July 14. Went to the library. Mrs. Pell (librarian, terrifying, secretly nice) let me into the local history section. Found a photo of the founding families, 1847. Nine founders. Nine families. But the ledger in the display case lists only eight family names. I asked Mrs. Pell where the ninth name went and she looked at me like I'd asked her where the bodies are buried, which, ha ha, apparently is a real question in this town.*

*July 15. Ulyana is being weird. Keeps asking why I'm "obsessed." I tried to explain about the ledger and got the coward speech. Fine. FINE. I'll do it alone. I've been doing it alone. That's kind of the whole point of a secret.*

Ulyana had to set the journal down for a moment. The stone in the shoe turned over, and it was not the shape they had carried. *You'd rather run than fix anything* — they had said that. And all this time, Vera had not been running *from* anything. She had been running *at* something, alone, at fifteen, because her twin had called her obsession a character flaw instead of asking to come along.

"I'm sorry," Ulyana said, to the kitchen, to the journal, to the girl on the stairs who hopped the fourth step. The house said nothing, but the refrigerator hummed, eight notes that never changed, and Ulyana made themselves listen to it all the way through before turning the page.

* * *

The middle journals were the hardest and the most ordinary, which was somehow worse. Vera had left Vesper Hollow at sixteen, gone to live with the aunt in Duluth, and the journals traveled with her. For pages and pages she was just a person: bad roommates, a community college chemistry course she loved, a job at a print shop where she learned to develop photographs and wrote three delighted pages about the darkroom ("it's a room where you make TRUTH APPEAR using CHEMICALS AND PATIENCE, which is my entire personality"). She dated people briefly and dismissed them kindly. She sent money home to Grandma. She came back every Christmas and every Christmas she checked the dining room floor for the humming.

*Dec 26. Still there. Eight notes. Grandma still won't talk. But this year she looked at me when I asked, and she looked tired, and she said, "You're too much like him." I don't know who HIM is. Great-great-grandpa Elias? The one whose portrait is in the church? I'm going to find out.*

The research deepened through her twenties. Ulyana read entries about county archives, about a ledger in the church basement, about a column headed *Given* — and stopped, cold, because that was a thread they had pulled themselves only yesterday, and the convergence felt less like coincidence than like two people digging toward the same body from opposite ends of the yard.

*The ledger is real. Dravenmoor appears twice. Once, 1847 — "Given: Elias Dravenmoor, by consent of the table." The second entry is BLANK. A name space, waiting. A date space, waiting. I touched the page and the paper was warm. Paper should not be warm. I have decided to be the kind of person who notices when paper is warm.*

*Theory: the founders made a bargain. The frost keeps the town alive — and I mean that literally, I've been tracking it, the harvests here don't fail, ever, not once in 170 years, not through the Dust Bowl, not through anything — and in exchange, the frost takes one from the founding lines. Not every year. When it wants. When the table is short a guest. Grandma's photo albums — I counted the chairs. Eight chairs, eight founders. But there's supposed to be nine. The ninth name was scraped off the ledger. I think the ninth founder was the one the bargain was made WITH.*

*Or the one it was made ABOUT.*

And then, dated the winter before last, in handwriting that had gone small and careful, like someone writing on ice:

*I think I know why the humming stopped being background noise for me and started being a message. I think the frost chooses. Not the town. Not the elders. Not a lottery. The frost CHOOSES its own vessel, and I think it's been choosing me since I was fifteen, since I was the one who crawled under the house and listened. It doesn't take the willing. It takes the ATTENTIVE. It takes the one who picks up the line.*

*I'm going home for good in the spring. If something happens to me, it's in the walls of Grandma's house, because that's the one place I trust the lock. Ulyana, if you're reading this — you were right that I run. But I was running TOWARD, you absolute disaster. I was always running toward.*

Ulyana laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle and became the other thing, and they let it, sitting in their grandmother's kitchen with the good lamp on and the frost ferns creeping up the window glass outside, in a house that had held its appointments for fifteen years, waiting for the twin who ran toward to be joined, at last, by the twin who stayed.

* * *

It was near midnight when Ulyana found the last journal — or what was less a journal than a file folder, thick with loose pages, the most recent dated six weeks before Vera's death. The handwriting was rushed now, the careful smallness abandoned.

*I have it. The rite, the whole rite, transcribed from the chapel beneath the church — yes, there's a chapel beneath the church, there's a stair from the crypt, nine alcoves, and in the center a dais shaped like a CHAIR, because of course there is, because the whole town is a dining room and we are what's for dinner.*

*But here's the thing. Here's the thing I keep reading and re-reading. The rite is written as an invocation. Founders' names, called in order, asking the frost to take. And Vera, you idiot, you brilliant idiot — read it BACKWARDS. A bargain read backwards is a confession. The same words, reversed in order, are an accusation. The frost doesn't just take. The frost KEEPS ACCOUNTS. It has been keeping accounts for 170 years, waiting for someone in the founding lines to stand up and read the bill aloud.*

*I don't think I'm the vessel. I think I'm the witness. The frost chose the one who would find this and understand it. And if it's not me — if the frost takes me before I can — then it will choose again. It will choose the other twin. It always intended both of us, I think. One to sit, and one to read.*

The last page was dated six weeks ago. The handwriting had gone calm, which frightened Ulyana more than the rushing had.

*Going down to the chapel tonight to see it with my own eyes. If I'm right about the backwards reading, I won't need to sit in the chair at all. I'll just need someone to keep reading after me. Someone stubborn. Someone who stays.*

*Someone like Ulyana, who always was the brave one, no matter what we said to each other in the summer we were fifteen.*

Ulyana sat for a long time. The kettle had long gone cold; the frost on the window had grown a garden. And it was only when they finally rose, joints aching, to carry the folder back to the sealed room, that they noticed the last item — tucked inside the folder's back pocket, a photograph they hadn't seen Vera slip in among the pages.

It was a picture of the dining room. Grandma's dining room, the table, the eight chairs.

And in the center of the table, pulled up close, a ninth.

On the back, in Vera's handwriting: *It moves. I've watched it move. It's getting closer to the camera. Ulyana — it isn't a chair at the table. It's a seat being saved. And I think it's facing the door now. I think it's facing whoever walks in.*

Ulyana looked down the dark hallway toward the dining room, where the good lamp did not reach.

The humming had started again. Eight notes, patient, holding the line open — and this time, unmistakably, it was coming from the dining room.
