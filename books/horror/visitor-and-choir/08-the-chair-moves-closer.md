## 8. The Chair Moves Closer

The darkroom had been grandma's church.

That was how she'd put it, anyway, when Ulyana was small enough to be carried up the back stairs on her hip. *The rest of them pray in the building with the steeple,* she'd say, unlocking the door at the end of the hall with a key she wore on a ribbon around her neck. *I do my confessing in here.* She'd said it with a smile, but Ulyana remembered now — remembered the way her hands never quite stopped moving when she said it, the way she checked the lock twice behind them on the way out.

Ulyana stood in that room for the first time in fifteen years, holding a flashlight between their teeth while they hunted for the light switch, and thought: *she locked it from the outside. Every time.*

The switch, when found, did nothing. Of course it did nothing. The power company had cut service to the house in March, according to the final notice Ulyana had found on the kitchen table, addressed to a woman four months dead. So Ulyana worked by flashlight and by the gray mercy of the window — a small square of glass painted over from the inside with what looked like decades of careful black lacquer, because a darkroom's darkness is not an accident but a discipline.

The room smelled the way it always had: fixer and vinegar and dust, and underneath it all something faintly sweet that Ulyana could not name and did not want to. The counters were still lined with trays. Grandma's enlarger sat under its cloth like a sleeping animal. On the shelf above the sink, rows of paper boxes stood in their black plastic sleeves, each labeled in her small, upright hand: *Vera, age 6. Vera, age 7. Kitchen, spring. Kitchen, fall. The table, various.*

*The table, various.* Four boxes of it. Grandma had photographed that dining room the way other people photographed sunsets.

Ulyana hadn't come up here to develop anything. That was the honest truth, and they held onto it the way you hold onto a railing in rough water. They had come up because they couldn't stay downstairs anymore — because the footsteps had started again the night before, crossing the ceiling above Ulyana's bedroom at 3:14 in the morning, Vera's old uneven gait, left foot landing harder than right, and Ulyana had lain there counting the steps until they reached the number that meant Vera was standing at the top of the stairs, and then the counting stopped, and Ulyana had not slept since.

So: the darkroom. A room with a lock. A room where a person could be busy instead of listening.

They were sorting through the paper boxes — mostly to keep their hands occupied, mostly to avoid the albums still waiting downstairs with their impossible arithmetic — when they found the box at the back of the shelf with no label at all.

It was heavier than it should have been. Inside, wrapped in a cloth that had once been white, were two dozen sheets of photographic paper, exposed, undeveloped, stacked face to face like leaves pressed in a book. Ulyana turned one toward the flashlight and saw nothing — blank white, or nearly. But when they tilted it, a ghost rose up out of the emulsion: shapes, a room, the suggestion of a table.

Exposed paper doesn't wait forever. But it waits longer than you'd think, sealed away from light, and grandma's house had always been very good at keeping things.

Ulyana looked at the trays. Looked at the enlarger. Heard, distantly, in the part of the mind that never stops narrating, the voice of every true-crime documentary they'd ever half-watched: *don't go into the basement, don't read the diary, don't develop the mysterious photographs.*

"Right," Ulyana said aloud, to nobody, in the voice of a person who has already decided. "Well."

* * *

Developing photographs turns out to be like riding a bicycle, if the bicycle is haunted. Ulyana's hands remembered everything — the sequence, the timing, the gentle rock of the tray, the way you lift the paper by its corner and let the chemical sheet off it in a slow curtain. Grandma had taught them both, one summer, Ulyana and Vera side by side at this same sink, bickering about who got to agitate the tray. Vera had been better at it. Vera had patience; Ulyana had only curiosity, which is patience's louder, stupider cousin.

The first print came up out of the developer like something surfacing through dark water.

It was the dining room. Of course it was the dining room. Eight chairs against the walls, the long table under the window, the wallpaper with its fading pattern of birds — all exactly as the albums showed it, all exactly as Ulyana remembered it. Taken from the doorway, apparently, at standing height. Nothing wrong with it at all.

Except that Ulyana had no memory of this photograph being taken, and neither, as far as they knew, had anyone else, because the image was sharp and recent-looking in a way that the old prints weren't — the blacks deep, the whites clean, the dust of decades conspicuously absent from a room that had been shut up and rotting gently since March.

And there, at the far edge of the frame, just inside the door: the ninth chair.

Ulyana set it down on the drying rack with fingers that wanted very much to shake and were refusing, on principle. They pulled the second sheet from the stack and fed it into the developer.

Same room. Same angle. The chair had moved. Not much — a foot, perhaps less — closer to the camera. Closer to the viewer. It sat at a slight angle now, as though someone had risen from it recently, or was about to sit down.

The third print: nearer again. In this one Ulyana noticed the chair itself for the first time, really noticed it, because in the albums it had always been a blur at the periphery, a thing your eye slid off. It was a plain wooden chair, spindle-backed, the kind that came in sets of six from mail-order catalogs and outlives everyone who orders them. But its seat was worn pale in the center, worn the way wood wears when one body sits in it over and over across years, and there was a small dark stain near the front edge that Ulyana's mind supplied a history for and then immediately begged to un-supply.

Fourth print. Fifth. Sixth. Ulyana developed them in a rhythm now, tray to tray, and the chair advanced with the patience of weather. Between the fourth and fifth prints the angle changed — subtly, impossibly — so that the camera seemed to have drifted off the doorway and into the room itself, drifting toward the head of the table, and the chair turned to meet it, the way a person turns to watch someone cross a room toward them.

By the ninth print the chair faced the camera directly.

Ulyana laid all nine out on the drying rack in order and stepped back, and the flashlight beam moved across them one by one, and it was — there was no honest word for it except *cinematic*, and the word arrived with a little hysterical laugh attached. Nine frames. A slow push-in. Whoever — whatever — had arranged these exposures had understood composition. Had understood that horror lives in the approach, not the arrival. Had understood, with a fluency that made Ulyana's stomach drop, that the viewer would stand exactly where the camera stood.

Where *they* were standing.

The remaining sheets in the stack numbered fifteen. Ulyana counted them twice. Fifteen more frames, and a chair that had, in nine frames, crossed maybe a third of the room.

They did not develop the tenth.

Not out of courage — let the record show it was not courage — but because it was past noon and Mrs. Aldous was due at two, and because some animal part of Ulyana's brain had begun insisting, quietly and reasonably, that the photographs were a sequence, and sequences have ends, and they were not going to stand alone in a locked room and watch this one arrive.

They pinned the nine prints to the line above the sink with wooden clothespins, in order, like evidence, like a storyboard, like an invitation printed in nine installments, and went downstairs to make tea for a neighbor who had told them not to unpack.

* * *

Mrs. Aldous arrived at two o'clock precisely, carrying a covered dish and the expression of a woman arriving at a funeral that has not been announced yet. She was a small, neat person built entirely of angles and wool, and she had been bringing covered dishes to this house since before Ulyana was born, and she surveyed the kitchen — the unwashed mugs, the county records spread across the table from Ulyana's morning's work, the general atmosphere of a life interrupted — with the resigned air of someone who has seen every possible way a Dravenmoor can fall apart and finds this particular variety almost nostalgic.

"You've been upstairs," she said. It wasn't a question. She set the dish down — something with dill, by the smell — and unwound her scarf one loop at a time, like a woman defusing herself.

"I found the darkroom," Ulyana said. "I didn't know she kept it stocked."

"She kept everything stocked." Mrs. Aldous lowered herself into a chair with the care of someone whose joints file monthly complaints. "Your grandmother believed in being ready. For what, she never said. But ready." Her eyes went to the ceiling, tracking something, then came back down. "You heard her last night."

"The footsteps, you mean."

"I mean yes, the footsteps." She said it the way you confirm a diagnosis. "Third night now?"

Ulyana didn't answer, which was an answer. Mrs. Aldous nodded slowly and poured herself tea without asking, in the manner of a woman who had earned informal custody of this kitchen decades ago.

"When I was a girl," she said, "your grandmother and I used to sit where you're sitting, and I'd ask her about the house. All children asked about the house. And she'd say—" Mrs. Aldous's mouth did something complicated— "she'd say the house keeps appointments. That's all. *The house keeps its appointments.* We thought it was a joke. She had that dry way." She looked into her tea. "It wasn't a joke."

"Mrs. Aldous." Ulyana leaned forward. "There are photographs developing themselves in that room. There's a chair in them that isn't in the room. It's getting closer to the camera in every frame. I need you to tell me what you know, and I need you to stop deciding in advance what I can't handle."

Something flickered across the old woman's face — Ulyana would spend a long time afterward trying to name it, and would eventually settle on *grief wearing politeness as a coat* — and for one moment Ulyana thought the wall would crack, that the whole buried story would come up out of her like groundwater.

Then the clock in the hall chimed the quarter hour, and Mrs. Aldous flinched — actually flinched, shoulders jumping, teacup rattling against saucer — and whatever had loosened cinched tight again.

"I knew a boy," she said carefully, "who sat in a chair like that. Once." She smoothed her skirt. "He was my brother."

The words landed in the kitchen and stayed there, radiating cold. Ulyana did not move. Did not breathe, if they were honest.

"He was Given," Mrs. Aldous said, "in 1974. And the town prospered. And my mother set a place for him at every meal for the rest of her life, and no one ever, ever told her she shouldn't." She stood, abruptly, gathering her scarf. "Don't develop the rest of those photographs, Ulyana. Whatever else you do this week — and I can see you're a person who will do everything else — leave the last ones in the box."

"Why?" Ulyana asked. "What happens when the sequence ends?"

Mrs. Aldous paused at the kitchen door with her hand on the frame, and for a moment she looked every one of her eighty-some years, and older, older than the house, older than the birds on the wallpaper.

"It arrives," she said. "And then it expects you."

She let herself out. The dish with the dill remained, steaming gently, abandoned like a hostage.

* * *

Ulyana washed the tea things. This deserves recording, because it was an act of pure psychological self-defense: there is nothing like washing dishes to convince the nervous system that the world still contains ordinary verbs. They washed and dried and put away, and straightened the county records into a neat stack, and ate three forkfuls of the dill thing, which was excellent, which somehow made it worse.

Then they went back upstairs.

Because here was the thing Ulyana had figured out, drying dishes, staring at nothing: Mrs. Aldous had said *don't*. And every single thing in this town that anyone had said *don't* about had turned out to be the exact thing that mattered. Don't say her name. Don't unpack. Don't open the closet — well, Ulyana had opened the closet, and found Vera's journals, and found in Vera's handwriting the sentence that had rearranged the furniture of their entire grief: *the frost chooses its own vessel, and it has chosen me, and I ran, and running only chose me faster.*

Vera had run from this. Vera had spent years researching it in secret, filling notebooks with the founders' names and the ledger's columns and the arithmetic of chairs, and she had run, and the frost had followed her to a city four hundred miles south and taken her anyway, in a rented room, alone, in February, and put her — put *her*, wearing Ulyana's face — into a casket in Vesper Hollow.

Ulyana stood in the darkroom doorway looking at the nine prints hanging on the line, and understood, with the calm that arrives after fear has exhausted itself, that the chair was not approaching the camera.

It was approaching *them*. The camera was a courtesy. The sequence was addressed. Every frame was a letter, and the address was written in the only language the frost spoke: geometry, distance, patience.

*You are being invited,* Ulyana thought. *Everything here is an invitation. The footsteps are an invitation. The casket was an invitation. Even Vera's journals might be an invitation, and God forgive me, even her death.*

Their hand was already in the box before they finished deciding. That was the humiliating part — the body had voted first. Fifteen sheets left. Ulyana carried them to the trays.

The tenth print surfaced slowly, reluctantly, like something being questioned. Same room. The chair now stood at the table's head. In the eleventh, it had come around the table's end. In the twelfth, the camera had drifted low — knee height, child height — and the chair loomed, spindle-backs rising like the bars of something.

In the thirteenth print, Ulyana appeared.

Small, in the corner of the frame, at the age of maybe ten, sitting on the floor of the actual dining room with a coloring book, visible only because the photograph had been taken from inside the wall, or from wherever such photographs were taken, and Ulyana stood in the red-lit dark holding the wet print with both hands and heard their own heartbeat and thought, distantly: *grandma took this. Grandma must have taken this. Except grandma never took a picture of me in that room, I remember, I was never allowed in there during dinners, why would she—*

Except the print was new. The blacks were deep. The whites were clean. The dust of decades was absent.

The fourteenth print showed the empty room again, the chair close enough now that its worn seat filled the lower third of the frame, and the stain on its front edge was clearer, and Ulyana made themselves look at it properly this time, because Vera had taught them that: *look at the thing, Uli, look straight at it, it's the looking-away that kills you.* The stain was small and dark and shaped — and here Ulyana's breath went thin — shaped like a handprint. A small one. A child's, or a young woman's, pressed flat, fingers spread, as if someone had gripped the chair's edge to steady themselves while sitting down, or while being sat down.

One sheet left.

Ulyana held it over the developer for a long time. The red light hummed. Somewhere below, the house ticked and settled, and the wind did its work on the eaves, and Vesper Hollow went about its evening with its lanterns lit and its smiles oiled and its ledger balanced.

"If you're her," Ulyana said to the empty air, to the chair, to the frost, to whoever was listening — and someone was listening; the house had the held-breath quality of a room where someone is listening — "then you know I'm not going to stop. You know that by now. She knew it too. It's the one thing we ever had in common besides a face."

They lowered the last sheet into the developer.

It came up fast, eager, the image blooming across the paper in seconds as though it had been waiting fifteen years and one afternoon for permission. And Ulyana looked at it, and the cold that went through them was not the room's.

The fifteenth print showed the dining room from a distance of perhaps three feet. The chair filled the frame, facing the viewer dead-on, close enough to touch, close enough to *sit in*. Its worn seat caught the light. Its stain was sharp. And draped over its back, hung with the casualness of a coat or a cardigan or a shroud, was a garment Ulyana recognized with a lurch that began in the stomach and ended somewhere behind the eyes:

Vera's gray scarf. The one she'd been wearing the summer she left. The one Ulyana had helped search the house for, fifteen years ago, the morning she vanished — turning out drawers, tipping over the hamper, while grandma stood in the dining-room doorway saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing, with her hand resting on the back of the eighth chair.

The scarf was in the photograph. The scarf was in the *house*. It had to be — the photograph was new, the photograph was *now* —

Ulyana was moving before thought caught up, out of the darkroom, down the hall, into the dining room, flashlight beam swinging across the eight chairs against the walls, the long table, the birded wallpaper —

The ninth chair stood at the table's center.

It had not been there an hour ago. It was pulled out slightly, angled, the way a chair sits when someone has just risen or is about to sit. And folded over its back, gray wool gone soft with years, smelling faintly of cigarettes and winter and a city four hundred miles south, hung Vera's scarf.

Ulyana did not touch it. They stood in the doorway with the flashlight shaking, and behind them, faintly, from the darkroom at the end of the hall, came the sound of the enlarger's timer clicking softly on — though there was no power in the house, had been no power since March — and the smell of fresh developer, and beneath it, patient as snowfall, the sense of a sixteenth exposure already beginning.
