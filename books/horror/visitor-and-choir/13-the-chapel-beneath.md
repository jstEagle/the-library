## 13. The Chapel Beneath

Vera's journals had a system. Of course they did. Vera alphabetized her spice rack and color-coded her grudges, so it should not have surprised me that she'd indexed the town's oldest secret like a term paper: *Founders' rite — see Aldous, see Brannic (senior), see chapel, see CHAIR (do not sit in it, Uly, I mean it).*

That last entry was dated four years ago. She had known, even then, that I would come looking. Or she had known herself well enough to know she'd want someone to be told not to.

I sat on the floor of the sealed closet I'd pried open two nights before, surrounded by eleven notebooks, a shoebox of photographs I hadn't dared develop, and a thermos of coffee that had gone cold out of sheer neglect. The house creaked around me in the old familiar way, and somewhere upstairs — or possibly in the walls, or possibly in the part of my brain that had stopped distinguishing — came the soft, arrhythmic tap of Vera's walk. Left foot landing harder. She'd broken it at eleven, jumping off the garage roof because she was convinced she could fly if she believed hard enough. She could not. But she never quite fixed the limp, and now the house kept it for her, faithfully, like a dog waiting by a door.

"Okay," I told the ceiling. "Chapel. You could just tell me where."

The house, in the manner of houses and sisters, declined to be direct.

* * *

The journals laid it out in pieces, the way Vera did everything — cautiously, then all at once. The earliest notebook, its cover soft as cloth from handling, was from her first winter back in Vesper Hollow at nineteen. She'd come home from college for Christmas and heard the humming.

*There is a note the pipes make when the furnace kicks on,* she wrote. *Except tonight the furnace was off, and the pipes were still humming, and the note was a B-flat, and it was coming from under the kitchen, which is impossible, because under the kitchen is dirt.*

Later entries grew more methodical. She'd mapped the church. She'd measured the crypt. She'd befriended — the word she used was *recruited* — the sexton, a stooped man named Mr. Pell who had held his position for forty years and who, according to Vera, "knows everything and pretends to know nothing, which is the local dialect."

*Pell says the crypt is just the crypt,* she wrote in the third notebook. *Pell also says that when he sweeps it, he starts at the north wall and finishes at the north wall, having swept in a circle, and has never once been able to make himself walk the pattern the other direction. He says it feels like walking upstream. He laughed when he said it. It was not a laughing kind of sentence.*

And then, in the fourth notebook, in handwriting gone small and pressed-hard, the way she wrote when she was frightened and didn't want the fear to have enough room:

*There's a stair. Behind the Hollis marker — the one with the frost fern carved wrong, eight points instead of six. The stone isn't mortared, it's set on a pivot. Pell showed me. He made me swear on grandma's name, which he said was the only oath the ground down here respects. Uly, if you're reading this, it's because I couldn't stop, and I'm sorry, and also I'm not sorry, because someone has to look at it with open eyes, and you were always the one who could do that. You looked at the thing under the porch steps when we were kids and you didn't flinch. I flinched. I flinch constantly. But I can't stop looking, so I suppose between the two of us we make one whole brave person.*

I read that sentence four times. Then I put the notebook down, went to the kitchen, and stood at the sink for a while watching the frost climb the window in slow ferns, doing the breathing exercise my therapist in the city had taught me for panic attacks, which I had not had in three years and was now having on a schedule.

The thing under the porch steps, for the record, had been a possum. It had been dead for some time. I had been seven, and I had looked at it with open eyes because I hadn't understood it was sad yet. I was thirty-four now and I understood everything was sad, and I went down to the church anyway.

* * *

Vesper Hollow's church sat at the top of Founders' Hill like a black tooth in a gray gum, its steeple leaning two degrees east, which the historical society's plaque cheerfully attributed to "settling" and which Vera's journals attributed to "the congregation leaning toward the crypt during services, structurally, over two hundred years, which is a sentence I hate."

I had been inside twice since the funeral — once for the service itself, once to return a casserole dish Mrs. Aldous had pressed on me with the gravity of a state gift. Both times the building had felt less like a place of worship than a mouth held politely closed.

I came at four in the afternoon, when the light goes long and gold and forgiving, on the theory that haunted places are easier to face with the sun's help. The doors were unlocked. Of course they were. Nothing in this town locked anything; the town itself was the lock.

The nave smelled of candle wax and cold stone and, underneath it, faintly, of the humming. I stood in the aisle and listened until I caught it — a B-flat, Vera had said, and Vera had perfect pitch, and the walls were holding the note the way a struck bell holds its tone long after the clapper stops. Not loud. Just *present*, the way a person is present in a room after they've left, if you loved them enough.

"Right," I said, to the B-flat, to Vera, to nobody. "Behind the Hollis marker."

The crypt stairs were behind the pulpit, down a spiral of steps worn into shallow bowls by two centuries of mourners. At the bottom, the crypt opened into a low stone room lined with the town's dead: Hollises, Brannics, Aldouses, Dravenmoors. My grandmother's name was on a slab near the east wall, and beside it, uncut, unlettered, a second slab of blank granite waiting with the patience of stone.

Two. Grandma had bought *two*.

I put my hand on the blank one. It was colder than the others. Not frost-cold. *Expectant*-cold.

"Not today," I told it, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, and the B-flat in the walls hummed a half-tone lower, like something taking note.

The Hollis marker stood in the north corner. The frost fern carved into its face had eight points instead of six — I counted twice, because Vera had taught me that you count twice when the answer is supposed to be impossible. The stone was set into the wall without mortar, and when I pressed my palms against it and pushed, it gave with a grinding sigh, pivoting on some hidden axis, exhaling a breath of air from the dark beyond that was *warm*.

That was the first wrong thing. Not cold — warm. Everything in Vesper Hollow was cold; the town ran on cold the way engines run on diesel. And here was a door breathing out warmth like an animal's flank.

I took out my phone. One bar. I turned on the flashlight anyway, because Vera had gone down here with a candle and a notebook and I refused to be less equipped than my dead twin.

The stair went down.

* * *

It was not a long stair, but it was a *deep* one — the kind of descent that makes your ears pop and your sense of the surface above become theoretical. The walls changed as I went: fieldstone gave way to cut granite, granite to something smoother, darker, veined with white lines that might have been mineral and might have been script. I tried not to look at the script too closely. The one time I did, I got the impression of repetition — the same phrase, over and over, descending with me — and the phrase had the shape of a name, and the name had the shape of mine, and I decided the walls and I were not ready for that conversation.

The humming grew as I descended. Not louder, exactly. *Closer.* As if the note had been in the walls all along and I had finally walked into the room where it lived.

At the bottom, the stair opened into the round.

I stood on the threshold for a long time before I could make myself catalog it, because some part of me understood that once I had seen it clearly, I could never unsee it, and the child in me who had once looked at a dead possum with open eyes was begging the adult to look away just this once.

The chamber was circular, perhaps forty feet across, cut from the same dark veined stone. The ceiling arched overhead into a shallow dome, and set into the dome were nine small openings — skylights, or vents, or eyes — through which the last gray light of afternoon fell in nine pale columns that reached the floor and stopped, each column ending precisely at the mouth of an alcove.

Nine alcoves, spaced evenly around the wall. Eight of them held a stone bench, a shelf, a basin — the furniture of waiting. The ninth, directly opposite the stair, was empty. Not vacant — *empty*, in the way a stage is empty before the play, in the way a chair is empty before the guest arrives. The air in front of it looked, if I let my eyes unfocus, slightly thicker than the rest of the air. Slightly more *attended*.

And in the center of the floor, where the nine columns of light would meet if the sun were ever directly overhead — which, I understood with a slow cold certainty, it never was, because the openings were cut to prevent exactly that — stood the dais.

It was shaped like a chair.

Not a throne. Thrones are for kings, and this was older than kings. It was shaped like a kitchen chair — the plain, humble, four-legged kind, the kind you pull to a table. It rose from the living rock as if the whole chamber had grown around the memory of one, the stone flowing upward into a seat, a back, two arms. The arms were worn smooth. Worn *bright*, actually, in two small patches where hands would rest.

Two patches. Two hands. Generations of them.

I walked the circle. I couldn't help it; my body did it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Eight alcoves, and in each one, carved small into the wall above the bench, a family name. Hollis. Brannic. Aldous. Mercer. Pell. Coyle. Frost — of course Frost; the town had never been subtle. Vesper. And the eighth: Dravenmoor.

Above the ninth alcove, where a name should have been, the stone was carved with a single word, and the word was not a name at all.

It said: *WILLING.*

I stood in front of it for a long time, and the warmth that came off the empty alcove was gentle, and that was the worst part. It was the warmth of a room where someone has just gotten up. It was the warmth of a bed still holding a shape. It was, God help me, the warmth of my grandmother's kitchen on a baking day, and I understood that the thing down here had studied warmth the way a forger studies a signature, and it had gotten very, very good.

"You're not her," I said quietly. "Whatever you are. You're not her, and you're not me, and you're not going to get to be."

The humming shifted. Just slightly. A B-flat easing toward a B. The sound a chord makes when it's deciding.

* * *

I did what Vera would have done, which is the highest compliment I know how to pay anyone: I sat down on the floor, opened her fourth notebook to the last written page, and took notes.

*The dais is the ninth chair,* Vera had written. *The alcoves are the eight families. The rite needs a body in the chair, but the chair isn't in the alcoves — it's in the middle, where everyone can see. That's the point, I think. It was never meant to be private. It was meant to be witnessed. A town that sacrifices in the dark is a town with a guilty conscience. A town that builds a theater for it — a round room, nine lights, a stage — is a town that wanted an audience.*

*Grandma's name is on the eighth alcove. There are scratch marks under it. I rubbed charcoal over them. It says, in her handwriting, or close enough: "We were promised it would be a stranger. It was never going to be a stranger. It was always going to be one of us. — E.D."*

E.D. Eunice Dravenmoor. Grandma. Who had refused, and whose refusal had left the debt open, accruing interest for fifty years, until the frost came collecting with my sister's face on.

I looked up at the ninth alcove, at the word *WILLING*, and I thought about the ledger in the church basement, the column headed *Given*, the blank line under DRAVENMOOR. And I thought — with the strange, cold clarity that had been arriving lately like a visitor who no longer bothers to knock — that a bargain is a kind of sentence, grammatically. It has a subject and an object and a verb. And sentences can be read backwards.

I didn't know yet what that meant. But Vera had underlined something in the margin of this very page, twice, in ink gone brown:

*A bargain read backwards is a confession.*

I copied it into my own notebook, because some sentences you don't trust to memory. Memory can be frostbitten too.

* * *

It was full dark when I climbed back up — up the deep stair, past the veined walls and their patient script, through the pivoting Hollis stone, into the crypt where my grandmother's blank second slab waited with its expectant cold. I put my hand on it again on my way past.

"Not today," I said again. "But soon. And not the way you want."

The B-flat in the walls had risen to a B while I was below. When I emerged into the nave, the congregation's absence was deafening — no, that's not right. The nave wasn't empty. I could feel it the way you feel a full room behind a closed door: the *pressure* of people, the held breath of a congregation. The pews were bare. The sanctuary lamp burned. And from the balcony organ loft, where no one stood, came the first three notes of Sunday's opening hymn, played slowly, as if by someone practicing for an audience that was already seated.

I stood in the aisle with Vera's notebook against my chest and listened to the empty church rehearse, and I understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first: whatever happened Sunday, it would happen with the whole town watching, and they would call it a service.

The second: I had three days to learn the hymn — not the words, the *note* — because the thing beneath the chapel had been humming it to me my whole life, and I was finally, finally beginning to hear what it was actually saying.

It was saying my name. And it was singing it in Vera's voice.
