## 15. Brannic's Offer

The knock came at nine in the evening, and it was polite in the way everything in Vesper Hollow was polite — three measured raps, evenly spaced, like a man who had practiced knocking until even his knuckles had manners.

Ulyana opened the door to find Sheriff Brannic standing on the porch with his hat in his hands and a paper bag of oranges tucked under one arm. He was a broad, gray-templed man with the kind of face that seemed designed by committee: nothing about it memorable, nothing about it unkind. That was the trick of him, Ulyana had decided. He looked like every uncle at every Thanksgiving table in America, the one who carved the turkey and asked about your job and never once mentioned the thing everyone knew he'd done.

"Evening," Brannic said. "Brought you some fruit. Groceries run low this time of year, and Aldous mentioned you've been living on toast."

"That's generous of you."

"It's oranges." He said it as if correcting a misapprehension. "Generosity would be grapefruit, and nobody's ever been grateful for grapefruit." He waited until Ulyana stepped aside before coming in, which was its own kind of courtesy — the courtesy of a man who wanted witnesses to know he'd been invited.

He set the bag on the kitchen counter, examined the room with a slow professional sweep that took in the cold radiators, the frost ferns creeping up the inside corners of the windows despite the thermostat's honest efforts, and the stack of Vera's journals on the table, hidden under a dish towel that fooled no one.

"Cold house," Brannic observed.

"It has its opinions."

"Mm." He pulled out a chair without asking, sat down heavily, and rested his hat on his knee. For a while neither of them spoke. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, there came the sound of footsteps crossing a floor — Vera's old gait, quick and light on the left foot where she'd always favored her right. Brannic did not look up. That was somehow worse than if he had.

"You hear that?" Ulyana asked.

"I hear a house settling."

"Houses don't favor their left foot."

Brannic turned his hat slowly on his knee, once around, twice around, like a man winding a watch that had stopped keeping time years ago. "Dravenmoor," he said — and it was the first time anyone in Vesper Hollow had used the whole name out loud since the funeral, "I'm going to say some things tonight, and I'd take it as a personal kindness if you'd let me get all the way through them before you decide what I am."

"And what are you?"

"Tired," he said simply. "Thirty-one years tired. But I'll get to that."

* * *

Sheriff Dale Brannic had been twenty-two years old when he'd taken the oath, fresh off two years of community college and a summer working the cannery, and he had believed — genuinely, stupidly, the way only young men in small towns believe things — that the badge meant something clean.

His father had held the same badge before him. His grandfather too. The Brannics were law in Vesper Hollow the way the Aldouses were harvest and the Merricks were timber, and when Dale pinned the star to his chest, his father shook his hand and said, "You'll have one hard night, son. Everybody gets one. After that it's just paperwork."

Dale had assumed he meant a drunk with a shotgun. Something with blood in it. Something a man could understand.

The hard night came in his fourth winter, when old Man Corliss went missing and Dale found the man's boot at the tree line and tracked what he swore was a trail of frost through three feet of new snow — frost that led *toward* town, not away from it, frost that ended at the church basement door, which was locked, which had always been locked, which his own father stood in front of holding a lantern and an expression Dale had never seen on him before.

"Go home, Dale."

"Dad, there's a trail—"

"There's snow," his father said. "There's always snow. Go home, sign the report tomorrow, and write *wandered off*. Write it neat. And then, son—" and here his father had gripped his shoulder with a hand that trembled, which frightened Dale more than anything else that night, "—then you do your job for thirty years and you never open that door. You keep the peace. That's what the badge is. Not justice. Peace. They're different animals, and only one of them keeps everybody alive."

Dale had gone home. He had written *wandered off* in his neatest hand. And for thirty-one years he had kept the peace, and the town had prospered, and no child had gone hungry, and no store had been robbed, and every autumn the harvest came in so heavy the grain elevators groaned, and once a year, give or take, somebody wandered off into the snow and Dale wrote it down neat.

He told himself it was arithmetic. One against eight hundred. He told himself his father's version: that peace and justice were different animals. He told himself a great many things over thirty-one years, and he got very good at telling them, and the terrible discovery of middle age was that he could no longer tell whether he believed any of them or merely recited them, the way a choirboy recites Latin.

And now there was a Dravenmoor in the old Aldous house with a dish towel thrown over a stack of journals, and the frost was writing on windows again, and Dale Brannic lay awake at night doing arithmetic that wouldn't balance.

Because the last time the ledger had come due and the family had refused, he'd been a boy — 1974, Tommy Aldous, fourteen years old, who used to sell him crayfish off a string by the creek for a nickel apiece. Tommy had been Given because the Aldouses had no other children to spare and the town had needed its harvest. Dale remembered Mrs. Aldous at the spring social afterward, smiling, serving lemonade, her hands steady as stone, and how he'd thought even then, twelve years old and already learning the local grammar: *that smile costs more than mine ever will.*

He wasn't going to watch it happen twice. Not to a stranger. Not to a child.

Which was why he was sitting in a cold kitchen at nine o'clock at night with a bag of oranges, preparing to commit the first honest act of his career.

* * *

"I'm not going to insult you," Brannic said, back in the present, back in the kitchen. "You've read the journals. You've seen the ledger. You know what 'staying through the frost' means, even if nobody's said it to your face. So I'll skip to the part that matters."

Ulyana sat down across from him. "The part where you ask me to leave."

"The part where I tell you how." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time the committee-designed face cracked along some fault line, and underneath it Ulyana saw something exhausted and old and, unexpectedly, ashamed. "Tonight. There's a county road cut through the north woods — logging spur, unmaintained, doesn't show on most maps. Plow comes through at midnight when the weather allows. Weather allows tonight; I made sure of it. You drive out, you don't stop, you don't look back, and by morning you're in Cedar Falls, and by noon you're anywhere that isn't here."

"Just like that."

"Just like that."

"And my sister's grave?"

Brannic's jaw worked. "We'd see it kept."

"We. Meaning the people who dug it."

"Meaning me," he said. "I'd see it kept. I'm offering you my word, and I know what my word's worth in this town, which is exactly nothing, but it's the only currency I've got left."

Ulyana studied him. Upstairs, the footsteps had stopped, and the silence they left behind felt attentive, like a held breath. "And the name? You said renounce it."

"Legally it's nothing — a form, a notary, I know a woman in Cedar Falls who owes me. But it's not the paper that matters." He met Ulyana's eyes, and his voice dropped, went almost gentle. "It's that you stop being a Dravenmoor. The ledger doesn't follow a name that's put down. It can't. It's a contract, and contracts need parties. You walk out of here Ulyana Marsh, or Reyes, or Smith, or whatever you fancy, and the frost has no claim on you. None. You'd be the first person in a hundred and seventy years to walk away clean."

The fire ticked in the stove. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with that long sibilant sound Ulyana had come to think of as the town breathing.

"And the claim goes somewhere else," Ulyana said quietly.

Brannic didn't answer.

"Sheriff. The claim goes somewhere else. That's the arithmetic, isn't it? That's the whole beautiful machine. Somebody stays Given or the harvest fails or the frost takes the town — I've read enough journals to know the shape of it. So if I drive out that logging road tonight, clean and free and carrying your oranges, who sits in the chair next winter?"

"Nobody," Brannic said. "Not next winter."

"But eventually."

"*Eventually*—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands, and when they came down he looked ten years older than he had on the porch. "Eventually the town finds another way. That's what I'm authorized to tell you."

"Authorized." Ulyana laughed, and it came out with an edge that surprised them both. "By whom? The Rotary Club?"

"By men older than the Rotary Club." He stood, walked to the window, looked out at the dark as though the dark might be taking notes. When he spoke again his voice was flat, cop-flat, the voice he must have used on a hundred reports. "There's a girl. Sixteen. Lives with her great-aunt out past the creek — Aldous girl, grandniece. Name of Poppy. She does homework at the kitchen table with the radio on and argues about curfew and thinks the worst thing in the world is her algebra grade." He turned around. "If the ledger comes due and there's no Dravenmoor, that's who they'll bring to the chapel. Maybe not this year. Maybe not next. But the founders' descendants aren't patient men, and they're not creative ones either, and there are only so many families left with roots deep enough. She's one of maybe three candidates in the whole town."

The cold in the room seemed to gather itself, listening.

"So that's the offer," Brannic said. "I won't dress it up. You can be free tonight, truly free, cleaner than anyone's ever been — and somewhere down the line a sixteen-year-old who likes the radio too loud pays for it. Or you stay, and it's you, and she grows up arguing about curfew until she's old enough to argue about bigger things. Those are the doors. I hate both of them. I've hated them for thirty-one years. But I'd rather hand you a true choice than a pretty lie, and nobody in this town has handed anybody a true choice since before I was born."

* * *

Ulyana looked at the oranges.

It was such a small thing to fix on, in the middle of everything — a paper bag of oranges on a cold counter, brought by a tired man who had driven out to the orchard himself, probably, because the stores closed early and he hadn't wanted to arrive empty-handed. Vera would have liked him, Ulyana thought, and then the grief of that thought arrived right on schedule, punctual as everything else in Vesper Hollow.

They tried, honestly tried, to weigh it the way Brannic had laid it out. Their life against a stranger's. Their freedom against a girl's algebra grades and radio songs and whatever else sixteen-year-olds carried around in their pockets like treasure. And the horrible seductive arithmetic of it — *one against eight hundred, peace against justice, different animals* — rose up in them, wearing their own voice.

But underneath the arithmetic was the casket. Underneath everything was the moment the lid came up and their own face had stared back at them, and the understanding that had been growing in them ever since, quiet and cold and certain:

This town had spent a hundred and seventy years paying its debts with other people's faces. First the twins of strangers. Then the twins of the founding lines. Then Tommy Aldous, fourteen, crayfish and nickels. Then Vera — Vera, who had fought it alone in these walls, researching in the dark while the whole town smiled at her across checkout counters. And now the machine, denied, reached for a child.

Every generation, someone had been offered a true choice and chosen the pretty lie instead. Chosen the arithmetic. Chosen *peace.*

"I'm not leaving," Ulyana said.

Brannic nodded slowly, as if he'd known, as if he'd driven out here mostly to hear it said aloud so he could carry the sound of it home. "I figured. Wanted you to have the door anyway. A choice you don't know you're making isn't a choice."

"No," Ulyana agreed. "It's a ritual."

Something flickered across his face — pain, or respect, or both wearing the same coat. He picked up his hat, settled it on his head, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "your grandmother refused too. 1961. Hardest winter this town ever recorded, and she never gave them the satisfaction of a yes. People still talk about her like she was made of granite." He glanced back, and there was the ghost of something that was almost a smile. "Granite runs in the family, looks like."

Then he was gone, boots crunching down the frozen walk, taillights swelling red in the dark and shrinking away toward town.

Ulyana stood alone in the cold kitchen. Upstairs, softly, the footsteps started again — pacing, restless, the way Vera used to pace when she was working up to saying something important.

"All right," Ulyana said to the ceiling. "I heard you."

On the table, under the dish towel, Vera's journals waited. And beside them, brought home from the church archive in a moment of defiance Ulyana hadn't yet understood, lay the founders' ledger — open, as it had been all day, to the page bearing the blank entry, the waiting column, the name *DRAVENMOOR* written in ink that had faded but never, ever dried.

Ulyana pulled the towel off the journals. Then, slowly, they looked at the ledger, and at the iron stove with its banked orange heart, and an idea began to bloom in them — warm, reckless, entirely their grandmother's granddaughter.

"A bargain read backwards is a confession," Vera had written in the margin.

Ulyana reached for the matches.
