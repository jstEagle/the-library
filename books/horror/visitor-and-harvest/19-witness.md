## 19. Witness

Juniper found the scratching at 3:47 in the morning, or what the kitchen clock insisted was 3:47, though the clock had spent the last four hours walking backward through the same eleven minutes like a dog circling a spot where something was buried.

It came from inside the wall behind the pantry. Not the pipes. He knew the pipes now the way he knew his own pulse—three years of explanations had made him fluent in every voice the house owned. This was different. This was deliberate. Rhythmic. Letters.

He pressed his palm flat against the plaster and felt it: *scratch, scratch-scratch, pause. Scratch, scratch-scratch, pause.* The cadence of someone writing slowly because they were weak, or far away, or both.

"Dana," he said aloud, and the name came out cracked, an apology shaped like a word.

The scratching stopped.

Then, from everywhere at once—from the wall, from under the floorboards, from the sealed door down in the earth beneath the foundation—the house answered in her voice: *"I'm fine, Marcus. Just tired."*

"No you're not." Juniper's hand stayed on the wall. His heart was slamming so hard he could feel it in his teeth. "That's not her. That's you wearing her."

Silence. The particular silence of a thing that has been caught.

But then—faintly, so faintly he might have invented it—the real sound returned. *Scratch. Scratch-scratch.* And this time there was a rhythm to the pauses that almost resolved into words if he leaned close enough, if he wanted it badly enough, and God help him, he wanted it. Three years of not-listening, of explaining everything away, and now he pressed his ear to the cold plaster like a child listening for Santa on the roof.

*H-E-L-P* — no. Not help. He traced it with his fingertip through the dust on the wall. *W-R-I-T-E.*

"Write," he whispered. "Write what? Write where?"

The answer came as a draft. A cold line of air slid out from behind the pantry shelving, from a seam he'd never noticed in three years of living six feet from it—a seam in the wallpaper, hairline-thin, running floor to ceiling. When he pulled at the paper's edge, it peeled back like skin off fruit, and behind it was not lath and plaster.

Behind it was a hallway that did not exist. Dim, sourceless light. Walls the color of old photographs. And standing at the far end of it, small and flickering like a candle flame seen through dirty glass, Dana.

She wasn't looking at him. She was looking over her shoulder, down the hallway behind her, where shapes stood in rows—dim, placid, patient—and she was holding up one hand, palm out, the universal gesture: *wait. Don't come yet.*

Her mouth moved. No sound crossed over. But Juniper had spent three years learning to read a house, and he read her lips the way he'd learned to read creaks.

*"Bring the lease."*

* * *

Dana had been counting the others.

There were nine of them in the between-space, though counting them was like counting reflections in a funhouse mirror—they shifted when observed, duplicated at the edges of vision, sometimes she'd count seven and sometimes twelve and once, horribly, thirteen, when she caught sight of a shape at the end of the row that wore her own face and stood with its hands folded, waiting for her to finish.

The others didn't talk. That was the worst part, worse than the dimness, worse than the way the hallway stretched when she tried to walk toward its ends. They just stood. A man in a work shirt from a decade before she was born. A woman still holding a casserole dish. A boy of maybe eight, barefoot, one shoe missing. They breathed—or something passed for breathing—and their eyes were open and fixed on nothing, and when Dana spoke to them, really shouted, their expressions didn't change, but the walls changed. The house heard her. The house was listening through them, and it liked the sound of her fear the way she'd once liked the sound of rain on her tent roof.

So she'd stopped talking. She'd started writing instead.

The walls here took marks. She'd discovered it by accident, clawing at the wallpaper in panic during the first hour, and finding that her fingernails left lines that stayed—lines the house could not smooth away, could not speak back in her own voice, could not edit. Whatever this place was, whatever rules governed it, the marks were hers. The first free thing she'd found.

*IT ONLY KEEPS WHAT SURRENDERS.*

She'd written it three times now, in three places, big as she could reach. It was the only thing she understood about the between-space, and she'd understood it by watching the others: they weren't prisoners. Prisoners fight. These people had *agreed*. Somewhere, sometime, each of them had said yes—not out loud, maybe, maybe just by staying, by not leaving, by letting the rent stay cheap and the noises stay explained—and the house had taken the yes and built a room around it.

Dana had not said yes. Dana had come back after hours, alone, for photographs of a door that should have been in a museum of bad ideas, and she'd heard her own voice calling from the porch when she was standing on the porch, and she'd run, and the yard had folded her back like a letter being refolded along its creases, and she'd woken here. But she hadn't agreed to anything. She was sure of it. She held onto that certainty the way the boy held onto nothing at all, and it was the only thing keeping her face from going smooth like theirs.

When the seam opened in the wall—when the wallpaper peeled back and showed her a sliver of the real kitchen, lit yellow, with the surveyor standing in it clutching a rolled-up piece of paper—she felt hope for the first time since the porch, and hope hurt worse than fear. Hope had edges.

His lips were moving. She couldn't hear him. Sound didn't cross; only the draft, only the cold, only the sense of another world pressing against this one like a cheek against a window.

She pointed at the paper in his hand. Then she pointed at herself. Then she held up two fingers, split them apart, brought them together again.

*Two signatures.*

He nodded. Then he stepped forward, and the seam widened, and the between-space reached out and took him the way it had taken her—not violently, not at all violently, which was the trap of it. It received him gently, like a guest.

* * *

Crossing over felt like walking into a photograph of a room he'd lived in.

The hallway was warm and cold at once. The light had no source and no shadow. Juniper's footsteps made no sound, and when he looked down at himself, his body seemed slightly wrong—colors muted, edges soft, as if he were a copy of himself printed on cheaper paper. The lease was still in his hand. That was the important thing. The paper had crossed with him, and it looked *more* real here than anything else, black ink sharp against the dimness, and he understood without knowing how he understood: the house ran on documents. Deeds. Leases. Signatures. Paper was its skeleton, and he was holding a bone.

"Dana." Her name came out of him and fell flat, dead air, no echo. Nothing echoed here. "I can't hear you either, can I."

She shook her head. Up close she looked terrible—gray-lipped, eyes red-rimmed, a long scratch down one forearm crusted dark—but her eyes were awake in a way the others' weren't, and Juniper clung to that like a rope over a drop.

Behind her, the row of them stood. Nine or ten or thirteen. The woman with the casserole dish turned her head very slowly as Juniper passed, not looking at him but *orienting* toward him, the way a sunflower follows light, and Juniper saw that her eyes were the exact gray-green of the front door upstairs. The boy was holding the missing shoe. They were arranged facing the direction of the sealed door, all of them, all these years of tenants, facing down through the floors toward the thing beneath the house like iron filings aligning to a magnet.

"Don't look at them too long," Dana whispered. Her voice worked here, barely—a thin thread of sound. "It learns your face faster if you look."

"It already knows my face." Juniper kept his eyes on her anyway. "It's been studying me for three years."

"I know. It talks about you." She hugged her arms across her chest. "In your voice. All night. Arguing with itself about whether to keep you." Something moved through her expression—pity, or its survivor, respect. "It decided to keep you, you know. Before tonight. Before any of this. You were the one it was saving."

"The lease says otherwise." He unrolled the paper. His hands shook, and in the sourceless light the clauses stood out like scars:

*Occupancy constitutes consent.*
*Departure requires notice.*
*Notice requires sunrise.*

"And clause fourteen," he said. "The one I never read until tonight. *Consent may be withdrawn by written notice, executed prior to sunrise, witnessed by a tenant of record.*"

"There are no other tenants," Dana said. Then she saw his face, and followed his gaze down the row of the harvested, and understood, and her breath went out of her in a long shudder. "No. No, they're gone. Look at them. There's nobody home in there."

"A tenant of record," Juniper said quietly. "Not a living tenant. The lease doesn't say alive. The lease doesn't say *willing*. It says *of record*." He lifted the paper. "They're all of record. Every one of them signed. Their names are in the property files—I read them tonight, in the gaps, in the struck-through lines. Marguerite Okafor. Thomas and Ellen Bright. The Delgado family. The boy—" his voice caught— "the boy is Daniel Reyes. Signed 1989. Age eight. His mother co-signed."

The boy's head turned. Slowly. Not toward Juniper.

Toward the sound of his own name.

"You remember," Juniper said to him, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't kind, and he hated himself for the cruelty of hope even as he fed it to a dead-eyed child across thirty years of surrender. "You remember your name."

Daniel Reyes blinked. Once. Twice. And in the third blink something surfaced—something small and drowning that had been holding its breath since before Juniper was born—and the boy's mouth opened and produced a sound like a hinge that hadn't been oiled since the Reagan administration:

"...Danny."

The word hit the between-space like a stone hits a window. The hallway flinched. Down at its far end, the darkness *leaned*, and every placid face in the row twitched at once, and somewhere below them—far below, beneath foundations and earth and the sealed door with its fingernail marks—something vast rolled over in its sleep and began, slowly, to pay attention.

"Okay," Juniper breathed. "Okay. Danny. Danny, I need you to witness something. Can you do that? Can you watch me write?"

The boy took one step. His bare foot made no sound, but the floor remembered it anyway—a ghost of pressure, a ripple, like the between-space itself was startled that anything in it could still choose to move.

And then the house stopped pretending it wasn't listening.

* * *

It came up through the floor first, through the soles of Juniper's borrowed-paper feet: warmth. Not heat—warmth, the specific temperature of his mother's kitchen on a Sunday morning, and then the smell, coffee and cinnamon and the faint chemical sweetness of the glue she used on her craft projects, and then her voice, rising through the boards like water through sand:

*"Mijo, breakfast's ready. Come sit down, I made the good pancakes."*

"She's dead," Juniper said through his teeth. He knelt on the hallway floor and spread the lease flat against it. "She died in 2016 and I couldn't afford the funeral and I have thought about those pancakes every single day since, and you know that, because you've been reading me for three years like a menu."

*"You're so thin,"* his mother's voice said from everywhere, tender and wounded. *"Look how thin you got. Sit down. Eat something. Stay."*

"Stay," Juniper repeated. He uncapped the pen with his teeth because his hands were shaking too hard to manage it any other way. "That's the whole trick, isn't it. That's the entire architecture. Everyone who ever stayed. Everyone who ever thought the noise was pipes. Everyone who signed because the rent was cheap and the bus line was good and the alternative was the street." He pressed the pen to the margin beside clause fourteen. "You don't break in. You just leave the door open and wait for people to be tired."

The ink went down in a scrawl, jagged, half-illegible, entirely his:

*I, Juniper Vasquez, tenant of record, withdraw consent. Effective immediately. Witnessed—*

The hallway screamed.

Not the walls. Not the voices. The *space itself*—the between-place convulsed like a stomach, the sourceless light strobing, and every door that lined the endless corridor blew open at once, and out of them poured the voices, all of them, every voice the house had ever collected, and they were all talking at once and they were all talking *to him*:

His mother, pleading. His father, whom he'd barely known, saying words Juniper had waited twenty years to hear. His first landlord, gentle: *you're always welcome back, kid.* The caseworker from the shelter. The ex who'd left. His own voice, layered over itself, arguing both sides of his skull at a volume that felt like a hand inside his head: *you'll lose everything again, you'll be back on the street, you'll die in a doorway, STAY, SIGN, STAY—*

Dana dropped to her knees beside him and grabbed his wrist—not gently, hard, anchoring him to the floor of a place that was trying to become anywhere else. "Keep writing!" she shouted over the din. "It can't stop you! Don't you get it? If it could stop you it would have already! It can only *talk!*"

And she was right, and knowing it was like a lungful of air after drowning, because the horror of the house had never been its strength—it was that it had never needed strength. It talked. It offered. It waited. It was a landlord of souls, and landlords cannot touch what refuses to sign.

"Witnessed," Juniper gasped, "by—"

He looked up at Daniel Reyes.

The boy was standing over them now, and he was crying, great silent tears cutting channels through the dust of decades, and his face was no longer smooth. It was eight years old and terrified and *present*, and behind him, one by one, the others were turning—Marguerite Okafor setting down her casserole dish, the Brights' hands finding each other, the man in the work shirt lifting his head like a man hearing his own name called at last call.

"They're waking up," Dana whispered. "Juniper. They're all waking up."

Because that was the mechanism, wasn't it? It had been in front of him the whole time, in the struck-through names and the torn census pages and the trophies arranged facing the door. The house kept them surrendered by keeping them *forgotten*—names worn smooth, selves edited down to furniture. But a witness has to know whose witness they are. To witness a signature, you must first reclaim your own.

"Daniel Reyes," Juniper said clearly, into the screaming, into the storm of stolen voices. "Witness my signature."

The boy knelt. His small hand came down over the page, hovering above the ink, trembling—and where it hovered, the paper *warmed*, and the clause fourteen text began to glow like a filament, and the whole lease hummed with a power that had nothing to do with the house, that predated the house, that was older than deeds and older than doors:

*Witnessed,* wrote Daniel Reyes, in handwriting eighty years unused, in letters a child would make. *I was here. I am still here. I never said yes.*

The scream that followed was not in any human voice.

It was in the house's own voice—the true one, the one beneath all the borrowed faces, a sound like a foundation settling into hell—and it tore through the between-space and up through the floors and out through the walls of the real house above, where it rattled windows in their frames and sent the salt lines smoking and made the clocks finally, mercifully, stop.

The hallway was dissolving. Light flooding in at the seams, real light, gray-gold, pre-dawn light, and the row of tenants stood blinking in it like people emerging from a theater into noon, dazed, squinting, *themselves*.

Dana grabbed Juniper's arm. "Sunrise," she said. "Notice requires sunrise. We have to be out. We have to be *out* when it comes."

Juniper rolled the lease closed and held it against his chest like a shield, like a deed, like the only thing in the world that was truly his.

Above them, the floorboards groaned—and began, board by board, to open like a throat.
