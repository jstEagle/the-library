## 6. Sealed from the Inside

The excavator's arm hung dead over the pit like a hanged man, and Odile Brann stood at its base with her hands in her pockets and her jaw set against the morning cold, listening to the engine tick as it cooled for no reason anyone could name.

"Third time this week," said Marek, spitting into the dirt. "Hydraulics just—quit. Like somebody pulled a plug."

"Somebody did pull a plug," Odile said. "You did. Check your own lines before you blame ghosts."

But she didn't believe it was the lines. She'd been running crews for twenty-two years, since she was nineteen and lying about her age to get onto the bridge jobs upstate, and machines had a language all their own—a vocabulary of groans and shudders and small honest failures—and what was happening to her equipment here at Hollowmere Academy wasn't failure. It was refusal. The excavator didn't break down near the northeast corner of the site; it *stopped*, the way a horse stops when it smells something on the wind that its rider can't. Diesel engines don't have instincts. Hers had developed one.

She looked at the spot again. Just soil, really. A shallow depression in the ground behind the school's old east wing, ringed with birch trees that grew too close together, their trunks pale and smooth as candle wax. Grass grew there in a color of green she couldn't quite place—not the tired green of New England lawns but something deeper, older, like the green you saw in photographs of places where nothing had ever been built.

"Brann." One of the new hires, a kid named Torres with a scanner wand held out at arm's length like it might bite him. "You're gonna want to see this."

Odile walked over. The ground-penetrating radar display showed a hard return about nine feet down: a flat plane, regular, rectangular. Not bedrock's chaotic signature. Something with edges.

"How big?"

"Big." Torres swallowed. "Bigger than big. It reads like—it reads like a floor. Except we're reading it vertical."

She took the wand from him and swept it herself, slow, the way her father had taught her to sweep for pipes back in Fall River, patience in the wrist, let the machine talk. The rectangle resolved. Nine feet down, oriented true north-south, perfectly plumb: a surface roughly four feet wide by seven tall.

A door.

Nobody said anything for a while. The birches moved without wind, or seemed to.

"Blueprints say gymnasium foundation," Marek offered finally. "Nothing about—"

"Blueprints say a lot of things." Odile handed the wand back. "Get me the school's records office on the phone. And mark it. Nobody digs past six feet until I know what we're looking at."

* * *

The records office sent over a courier by noon: a thin folder containing a survey from 1954, a soils analysis from 1971, and a single typewritten sheet, undated, on paper gone the color of weak tea. The typewritten sheet was a memo. It read:

*RE: Northeast quadrant. No subsurface work is authorized in the northeast quadrant under any circumstances. This directive supersedes all prior directives and shall not be superseded.*

No signature. No letterhead. Just the words, typed with such force that the letters had punched through the page, and beneath them, in different ink, in a hand so old-fashioned it looked like engraving, someone had added: *It is not empty down there.*

Odile read it three times. Then she folded it into her breast pocket, where it lay against her heart like a coal.

"Well?" Marek asked.

"We dig."

He stared at her. "Boss, the memo says—"

"The memo says a lot of things too." She picked up her hard hat. "We've got a contract, a deadline, and a penalty clause. Whatever's down there has been down there a long time, and it hasn't come up yet. That's the whole history of buried things, Marek. They stay buried because nobody makes them come up. We're going to make it come up, look at it, write a report, and pour concrete over it. That's the job."

She believed every word when she said it. That was the thing she'd remember later, turning it over in the dark like a stone with something underneath: she had believed it, completely, right up until her hands told her otherwise.

* * *

They dug by hand past six feet. That hadn't been the plan—the plan was the excavator—but the machine died twice more at the edge of the depression, once with a sound Odile could only describe as a flinch, and after the second time she'd stood in the cab breathing diesel exhaust and made the call. Shovels. Slow work. Eight men rotating through the pit in pairs, cutting a clean square down through clay and gravel and then through clay again, clay that shouldn't be there, clay the color of old bruises, until at four o'clock on the third day Torres's shovel rang against something that wasn't soil.

The sound carried. Every man on the site stopped. Somewhere behind them, on the school grounds, a bell rang for end of classes, and the two sounds overlapped for a moment—the shovel's bright clang and the bell's dull bronze—and Odile would swear until the day she died that they were ringing in time.

"Everybody out," she said. "Torres, Marek, with me. Rest of you, break early. Paid."

They went down the ladder two at a time. The pit was deep enough now that the afternoon light only reached partway, and they worked the last few feet by floodlamp, brushing clay away with hand trowels and then with bare fingers, and what emerged from the earth was a door.

Not a doorframe. Not a doorway. A door—oak, black with age, iron-banded, set flush into living bedrock as though the mountain itself had grown around it, or as though the door had been planted and the rock had obliged. There was no wall around it. The stone face continued above and below and to either side, seamless, unbroken, a shelf of granite rising out of the pit floor, and the door sat in it the way a coin sits in ice. As if the rock had closed over it like water.

And the bolts were thrown.

There were four of them, heavy iron slides, one at each corner, and every one of them was shot home into sockets cut in the stone. Odile crouched and put her lamp close to the metal. No rust worth mentioning. No corrosion. The bolts gleamed dully, oiled-looking, maintained.

"Marek," she said quietly. "Which side are the bolt handles on?"

Marek didn't answer. He was staring, his lamp trembling slightly, and Odile followed his light and felt the cold arrive in her stomach and spread outward, professional and total, the way dread came to people who spent their lives trusting their hands.

The handles were on this side. On the outside. And the bolts were thrown toward the inside.

"Could be a mechanism," Torres said. His voice had gone thin. "Could be—counterweights, springs, some old automatic—"

"It's an oak door in solid granite, kid." Odile didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; it was why men dug for her. "There's no mechanism. Somebody threw those bolts from the other side."

"Maybe there's another side," Marek said. "Maybe it opens into a cellar, a tunnel, some old—"

"Into what? Look at the stone. Look at it." She ran her palm along the granite face beside the door. Unbroken. Seamless. The rock didn't stop anywhere; it simply continued, vast and indifferent, in every direction the lamplight reached. "There's no other side. There's no tunnel. There's nothing back there but rock, except rock doesn't throw bolts."

The pit was very quiet. Above them, faintly, the birch leaves moved.

"Cover it back up," Odile said at last. "Tarp, plywood, clay. Nobody touches it tonight. First thing tomorrow I call the historical commission, the state archaeologist, whoever picks up. We document, we photograph, and then we find out what the hell the school knows that their own blueprints don't."

"And if they don't know anything?" Torres asked.

Odile looked at the door a moment longer. In the floodlight the oak grain had a pattern she almost recognized, whorls and knots arranged in something too regular to be chance and too organic to be carpentry, and standing that close she became aware of a smell rising off it—under the clay and the mineral stink of the pit—faint, sweetish, familiar in a way she couldn't place. Beeswax. Old beeswax, and under that, something like bread.

Somebody had polished this door. Recently enough to smell.

"They'll know something," she said.

* * *

She should have left it there. She knew that even then, standing in the pit with the tarp half-draped, and she stayed anyway, and she touched it anyway, and later—much later, in the years when she allowed herself to think about Hollowmere at all—she would understand that the touching was the point. That the door had been waiting, patient as a spider, for exactly one kind of person: the kind who couldn't leave a locked thing alone.

The others had climbed out. The floodlamps were being packed. And Odile Brann, twenty-two years a builder of things, stood alone in a hole in the ground in front of a door that had been locked from the inside, and she reached out and laid her bare palm flat against the oak.

The wood was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not machine-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warmth with a pulse in it, slow and deep, and beneath her hand she felt—not heard, *felt*, the way you feel a train through a station platform—a humming.

It was a tune. That was the worst of it, the detail she would give anything to unknow: it wasn't a vibration, wasn't machinery, wasn't resonance off the bedrock. It was a melody, simple and patient, four notes rising and falling, rising and falling, the kind of thing a mother hums without knowing she's humming it, the kind of thing you hum to something you're keeping safe. Or keeping. The distinction arrived in her mind unbidden and unwelcome, and with it the smell of beeswax strengthened, and the warmth under her palm increased by a degree, the way skin warms when whatever lies beneath it begins to wake—

Odile snatched her hand back.

The humming stopped. Not faded—stopped, cleanly, mid-phrase, the way a person stops singing when they hear the floorboard creak outside their room. And in the silence that followed, in the clay-smelling dark of the pit, Odile stood with her hand pressed against her own chest and waited for her heart to slow down, and it did not, not for a long time.

When she climbed the ladder, the last light was going off the birches, and the school windows behind them glowed yellow and cheerful, and she thought she saw faces in one of them—students, probably, watching the construction, kids being kids—and then the curtain moved and there was nothing.

Her right hand ached all the way home. Not injured—ached, deep in the bones, in the metacarpals, a dull sympathetic pain like the ache you get in a lost limb. She flexed it under the truck's steering wheel at every red light. Twenty-two years those hands had built foundations and poured footings and never once lied to her, and now they were telling her something she didn't want to hear.

Dread, she thought, driving through the dark with her aching hand at ten and two. Dread doesn't come through the ears first. Ears you can argue with. It comes through the hands. Through the skin. The body knows what the mind is still making excuses for.

* * *

At seven the next morning, two of her crew were gone.

Not absent—gone. Danny Petrakis and Luis Ochoa, twelve and nine years on her crews respectively, neither of whom had ever quit a job in his life, both of whom had been in the pit yesterday brushing clay off iron bolts. Their tools were coiled and racked. Their lockers were empty. The trailer's coffee urn was full and cold, untouched since the night before.

The foreman's log had a note in Danny's blocky handwriting, dated yesterday, 11:47 p.m.: *Sorry Odie. Can't do it. Don't ask me why because I don't have the words. Tell Luis—*

The sentence stopped there. Below it, in Luis's smaller, slanted script: *Tell Danny I already know.*

Neither man answered his phone. Neither man came to collect his final pay—eleven hundred dollars each, sitting in envelopes in Odile's desk drawer for a week before she finally mailed them, and the envelopes came back marked *addressee unknown*, which was impossible, because she'd had both addresses in her books for years.

By eight she had the rest of the crew gathered at the tailgate of her truck, eleven men drinking bad coffee and not looking at each other, and she gave them the speech she'd prepared on the drive in: hazard pay, a safety officer on site, the state coming to inspect, anybody who wanted off the job could walk today with no black mark, she'd personally make the calls.

Nobody walked. That surprised her more than the quitting had. Eleven tired men stood in the cold and looked at her with a stubbornness she recognized because she'd put it there herself over years of shared winters and shared paychecks, and Marek spoke for all of them: "We finish the job, boss. But we do it your way. Slow. Careful. And nobody touches that door."

"Nobody touches the door," Odile agreed.

She was lying, and she suspected they knew it, and she suspected they understood, the way crews understand things about their foremen that foremen don't say aloud. Because all night her right hand had ached, and all night the humming had lived in it—not in her ears, in her *hand*, four notes rising and falling, rising and falling, patient as water finding its level—and she had lain awake cataloguing the reasons to walk away and found, one by one, that none of them weighed anything against the plain animal fact of her own curiosity.

She was fifty-one years old. She had raised two sons and buried a husband and outlasted every crooked contractor and every collapsing scaffold the trade had thrown at her. She did not scare.

But her hands scared, and her hands had never been wrong.

At noon the school called. A pleasant voice, a woman's voice, identifying itself as the office of Vice-Principal Grieves, wondering whether the construction schedule might be discussed at Mr. Grieves' earliest convenience, and would Ms. Brann care to come by for coffee?

Odile looked across the site at the plywood and clay covering the pit, at the birches standing too close together, at the school's yellow windows shining in the flat gray light.

"Tell him tomorrow," she said. "I'll bring the memo."

She hung up, and in the sudden quiet her right hand hummed, soft and content, four notes rising and falling, like something that had just been promised a visitor.
