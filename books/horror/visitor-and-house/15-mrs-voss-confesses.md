## 15. Mrs. Voss Confesses

The archive smelled the way old paper smells when it has been weeping: damp, sweet, faintly sour, like a room that remembered being a body. Miriam Voss had tended that smell for thirty-one years. She knew every drawer of the card catalog by the sound its handle made, every shelf by the particular sag of its wood, every box of student records by the weight of what had been removed from it.

She was re-shelving the 1974 yearbooks when she heard his footsteps on the stairs.

She had known he would come. They always came back to the archive eventually, the ones who noticed. It was a law of the building, older than the building. The ones who noticed could not stop noticing, and noticing led downward, always downward, into the basement levels where the records lived, where the truth was filed away in boxes marked with cheerful, anodyne labels like *ATHLETICS, INTRAMURAL, 1958–1963* and *COMMENCEMENT PROGRAMS (DAMAGED)*.

Felix Nakamura. The new one. The one with the notebook.

She had watched him from behind the circulation desk his first week, the way she watched all of them, cataloguing his cataloguing. He held his pen the way a man holds a knife he hasn't decided about yet. He photographed pages instead of trusting his memory. He counted things—windows, chairs, students—under his breath, moving his lips like a man praying in a language he was ashamed of.

And he had written down the girl's name. Wren Ashcombe. She had seen him do it, seen the way his pen hesitated over the *W* and then committed, the way a man steps off a ledge.

*That one will go down,* she thought. *Or he'll go down instead. Either way, he'll go down.*

She slid the yearbook into its place and did not turn around when the footsteps reached the landing.

"You're not supposed to be down here after four," she said. "The archive closes at four."

"I know," Felix said. "I knocked. You didn't answer."

"I never answer." She turned then, and looked at him properly for the first time in the good light of the reading lamp. He was thinner than he'd been in September. The term had been eating him. She knew that hunger. It was a hunger the House cultivated, patiently, the way a farmer cultivates a field he intends to strip. "What do you want, Mr. Nakamura?"

"You know what I want."

"I know what you *think* you want. Those are different things." She took off her glasses and set them on the cart, and without them the reading lamp became a halo behind her and her face became a soft, blurred country. "You want confirmation. You want someone older than you to say the thing you've been afraid to say, so it becomes real and you can stop doubting yourself. That's what you want. What you *need* is a different question entirely."

"Then tell me what I need."

"Sit down."

He didn't sit. He stood there with his notebook in his hand like a shield, and she thought: *yes, that's how I stood, forty years ago, in this same room, under this same lamp.* The lamp had been replaced twice since. The light was the same light. That was the thing about Hollowmere that no one ever understood until too late—the building changed its skin constantly, but the light stayed. The light was the House looking at you.

"Sit down," she said again, gentler. "What I have to tell you takes longer standing up."

* * *

He sat in the green reading chair, the one with the worn arms, the one where generations of students had fallen asleep over open books, and she sat across from him with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral she had been dreading for four decades.

"Her name was Colette," Voss said.

Felix's pen came up. She watched him catch himself, watched him lower it.

"You can write," she said. "I'd rather you did, actually. Someone should have written it down a long time ago. That was my sin, Mr. Nakamura. Not the singing. The *silence*."

"Colette," he repeated. "Your daughter."

"My daughter." The word came out of her like a tooth being pulled—clean, whole, with a little root of blood still attached. "She was fourteen. It was 1985. You weren't born yet, or you were a baby, I don't know which, and it doesn't matter. She was in the second form. She had a laugh that—" Miriam stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "You have to understand what Hollowmere was in those days. What it has always been. The missing students weren't a secret. They were a *policy*. There was a rotation. There was a list. The senior faculty kept the list, and the list was kept in balance like a ledger, because the House—" she said the word flatly, without drama, the way you'd say *the boiler* or *the board of trustees* —"the House requires payment. It has always required payment. And the faculty have always paid it, because the House pays back. Tenure that never ends. Health that doesn't fail. Careers that bloom like hothouse flowers. Grieves is ninety-one years old, Mr. Nakamura. Did you know that? Look at his hands sometime. Look at his *face*. He's been 'young' for sixty years."

"I've seen the records," Felix said quietly. "The redactions. The handwriting that's older than the school."

"The Founders' hand." She nodded. "You found the ledger."

"I found part of it."

"Then you know the shape of it. What you don't know is the texture. What it feels like to be on the inside of it." She unfolded her hands and looked at her own fingers—old woman's fingers, spotted, knuckles swollen, but steady. Steadier than they'd been in years, actually. That was the other thing about the House's payments. They kept coming whether you wanted them or not. "Colette got sick. That's where it starts, in the stories. It always starts with something you can't fight with money or prayer. Leukemia. The kind they didn't have a name for yet in the way they do now, the kind the doctors at the hospital talked about in that careful, gentle voice they use for things they've already given up on. And I was a young librarian with no money and no power, and I had spent eleven years in this building pretending not to notice what the building was, and I knew—I *knew*, the way you know the load-bearing walls of your own house—that there was a door in the basement that opened onto something that could fix her."

The lamp hummed. It was not an electrical hum. Felix heard it too; she saw it in the way his head tilted, the way a dog tilts its head at a frequency it can't place.

"Who told you the song?" he asked.

"No one told me. That's the trap of it. The song isn't taught. It's *recognized*. You hear it once—hummed by a teacher in the stairwell, sung under the breath of the groundskeeper, drifting up through the floorboards on a night when the boiler's off—and it lodges in you like a seed. And when you're desperate enough, when you're down in the dark in front of the door with your daughter dying three towns over, the verse simply... arrives. Complete. As if it had been waiting in you the whole time, the way a word waits on the tip of the tongue." She smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile, because it was warm. "Verse two. *To Be Kept.* Do you know what it promises?"

"I've heard the first verse. Wren sang it for me."

"The finding verse. Yes. Verse one finds you. Verse two *keeps* you." Her voice dropped, and the archive seemed to lean in around them, the boxes on the shelves settling like sleeping animals. "It's a bargain verse. It's the verse the faculty sings, or sang, the ones who made the old arrangement. You sing it, and you offer something, and the House keeps it, and in exchange the House keeps safe whatever you've asked it to keep safe. I sang it in the basement at three in the morning with my coat over my nightgown, and I offered—" she laughed, a small dry sound with nothing in it, "—I offered the only thing I had that the House wanted. I didn't know that's what I was doing. I thought I was offering a favor. A service. Time. The House is very generous about time; it has so much of it. But the verse doesn't take what you offer, Mr. Nakamura. The verse takes what you *value*. And what I valued, more than my own name, more than my own face in the mirror, more than every memory I had of my mother—" her voice did not break; it had been broken for forty years and had simply learned to speak around the break, "—was Colette."

"So the House took—"

"Nothing. That's the horror of it. It took nothing. It *kept* her. The leukemia went into remission in nine days. The doctors used the word 'miraculous' and wrote it in her chart with a little flourish, the way doctors do when they've witnessed something they've decided not to think about. She grew up. She married. She has three children and a garden in Vermont and she calls me every Sunday, and she is *kept*, Mr. Nakamura. She has been kept for forty years like a flower pressed in glass, and she doesn't age right, and her children don't quite look like her, and sometimes at Christmas she'll stop mid-sentence and stare out the window with an expression I can only describe as *inventory*, as if something inside her is being counted, and then she'll smile and ask me to pass the gravy." Miriam's hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. "And every year, the interest comes due. Not money. The House doesn't deal in money. It deals in *presence*. Every year, on the anniversary of the singing, I lose something. A year of my memory, at first. Then a friendship—I simply stopped being able to remember a woman I'd known for twenty years; her face was a blank to me, and everyone told me we'd never been close. Then my sister stopped writing, and when I wrote to her, the letters came back *addressee unknown*, and I checked, and as far as the world was concerned my sister had never existed. The House is paying down my debt by unmaking the collateral. And one day, Mr. Nakamura—and I know this the way you know weather—one day the debt will reach Colette herself, and the keeping will become a keeping-in, and she will be collected."

Felix was writing. She could hear the pen moving, fast, and the sound of it was the most hopeful sound she had heard in this building in forty years.

"You said you'd rather I wrote it," he said. "Why? Everyone else wants the records blank."

"Because blank pages are how the House wins." She looked at him, and took her glasses off the cart and put them on, and saw him sharp and young and terrified, and loved him a little for it, the way you love the young of any species that's about to do something fatal. "The House erases. That is its whole nature. It is a *hole* that eats. And the only thing a hole cannot digest is testimony. It can burn the paper, but if the words have passed through another mind—if they've been *read*—they leave a residue. A scar in the reader. That's why it hates your notebook. That's why it will offer you anything for it. Not because the notebook has power, but because it has *witness*."

"The House offered to buy it," Felix said. "Already. In the halls under the school."

"And you refused."

"I ran."

"Running is refusing with your legs." She almost smiled. "Good. Then you're ready for what I have to give you, because it will make the running much harder."

She rose, slowly, joints announcing themselves, and went to the far wall—the one behind the card catalog, the one with the framed photograph of the class of 1961, all those bright confident faces, half of them blank to history now. She reached behind the frame. Her fingers found the recess in the plaster that her fingers had found ten thousand times, and drew out a small cloth bundle, tied with waxed thread.

She carried it back and set it in Felix's hands.

It was heavier than it looked. He felt that immediately—she watched him feel it, watched his eyebrows draw together, watched him shift it from hand to hand as if testing whether the weight was a trick. It was not a trick. The thing inside weighed like a promise.

"Untie it."

He did. The waxed thread slithered away, and the cloth fell open, and there in his palm lay a key.

It was four inches long, maybe five, and it was made of two things that had no business being joined. The shaft was brass, old brass, green-black at the edges, and it was warm—warm as skin, warmer than the room. But the bow of it, the part you grip, the part that becomes the handle—the bow was a tooth. A human molar, whole, root and all, fused to the metal as if it had grown there, as if the brass had been poured around the root and the root had accepted it, and the enamel was yellowed and crazed with fine lines like a map of somewhere cold.

Felix did not ask whose tooth it was. She watched him decide not to ask, and she respected him for it, and she told him anyway.

"It's mine," she said. "The House took it the first year. Pulled it in my sleep, clean, no blood, no pain, no gap. I woke and my mouth was whole and there was no tooth missing and no one at the dentist could find anything wrong. But the tooth exists. The House keeps what it takes, even the small things. Especially the small things. It made me this key from the keeping, because a key has to be made of what it opens. Brass for the door. Tooth for the *keeper*." She looked at him steadily. "It opens the lone doorway in the field. The off-site door. And it opens the inner doors, the ones past the trophies. It will work for you exactly once, Mr. Nakamura. The House honors one use. After that, it will know your hand, and it will never open for you again—only against you."

"Why give it to me?"

"Because I am done paying interest," she said, "and I want to pay principal." She sat back down across from him, and for a moment she was not an old librarian in a cardigan; she was a young woman in a basement at three in the morning, singing to a door. "You're going to go in there. I've watched you for a term. I've watched you refuse the offers. You're going to go in, and you're going to try to take her—Wren—back, and you are going to fail, because everyone fails, because the House is patient and you are not. And then you are going to do the thing I couldn't do. You're going to finish the song."

"The song doesn't have an ending. Wren said—"

"Wren was *in* it. The kept don't get the ending. Neither did I, and I've had forty years to look." She leaned forward. "But I know something the House thinks I don't know. I know why the song has no ending in any throat that sings it. It's not that the ending was lost, Mr. Nakamura. It's that the ending was never *given*. It has to be made. Out of what the House kept apart." She tapped the cloth bundle, the tooth, the brass. "I don't know how. But I know the House is afraid of the possibility, and the House is never afraid of anything that isn't real."

Outside the high narrow window, the light was going the color of old brass, and the shadows of the shelves lay across the floor like bars.

"One more thing," Miriam Voss said. "And then you should go, because after four o'clock the archive doesn't like visitors, and neither, after tonight, will I."

"Anything."

"Colette calls on Sundays." Her voice was very quiet now. "And last Sunday, for the first time in forty years, she got my name wrong. She called me by a name I've never had. And then she laughed—her old laugh, the one I sang the verse to save—and the laugh went on a little too long, and came out a little too low, and at the end of it there was a sound underneath, a hum, four notes of it." She closed her eyes. "The House isn't waiting for my debt to come due anymore, Mr. Nakamura. It's *collecting*. And when it finishes collecting me, it will want its key back, and it will take it from wherever I've hidden it, because it can find everything it has ever made."

"Then I should take it now."

"You already have." She opened her eyes. "Go. And Mr. Nakamura—if you find my daughter's laugh in there, in a jar, on a shelf—leave it. Don't waste your one use on mercy. Mercy is how it gets you. Take the girl. Take the girl and *sing*."

* * *

He went. She heard his footsteps on the stairs, quick at first, then slowing on the landing, then stopping—and she knew, without seeing, that he had turned back toward the reading room, that he was standing in the doorway looking at her, wanting to say something, some word for what you say to a woman who has just handed you a key made of her own body and her own guilt.

He didn't say it. He was learning. That was good. The ones who learned not to say the unnecessary thing lasted longer.

The footsteps went up and away, and the archive settled around her, and Miriam Voss sat alone under the lamp with her folded hands and did the thing she had done every night for forty years: she listened to the building.

And the building listened back.

The hum came up through the floorboards, faint, patient, four notes, the first four notes of the song, over and over, like a hand testing a lock. It had never done that before. In forty years it had hummed at her only twice—once the night she sang, and once the night it took her sister's name.

It was humming the *finding* verse.

It was looking for something. Or someone. And she understood, with the calm of a woman whose accounts are finally in order, that it was not looking for the key.

The key had already left the building.

It was looking for the hand that carried it.

She rose, turned off the lamp, and stood in the dark of the archive she had tended for thirty-one years, and began, very softly, to sing verse two—the keeping verse, the paying verse—one last time, not to the House, but *against* it, throwing her old voice down through the floorboards like a stone into a well, to confuse the water, to buy the boy one night, one day, whatever a worn-out librarian's last coin could buy.

The humming stopped.

Then, from everywhere beneath her at once, in a voice made of every voice it had ever kept—children, teachers, groundskeepers, a sister whose name no one remembered—it answered her.

It knew her account was overdrawn.

It was coming to collect.

* * *

Miriam Voss did not sleep that night. She sat in the reading room with all the lamps lit, which she had never done, because the lamps were the House's eyes and you did not ask the House to look at you—but tonight she wanted to be seen. She wanted the last of her to be *witnessed*, by something, even by it.

Near dawn, the hum changed key.

It began, very faintly, very far below, to sing verse three.

*To be given.*

She had never heard it sung. No one she had ever known had heard it sung. It was the verse for those who enter willingly, the old books said, the verse the House demands as the price of the inner doors—and the House did not sing verses. The House *received* them.

Unless.

Unless someone was already inside. Unless someone had already used a key. Unless someone was walking its halls right now, in the dark before dawn, with a notebook chained to nothing yet and a key made of tooth and brass in his pocket, and the House was not singing to him—

It was teaching him.

And Miriam Voss, librarian, mother, debtor, sat up very straight in the green chair, and felt the last of her fear turn into something else, something with an edge on it.

"Then hurry," she said to the floor, to the House, to the boy in the dark beneath the world. "Learn fast. Before it finishes teaching you what the price is."

The hum rose through the boards one final time—and this time it was not four notes, and it was not five.

It was the whole song, all the way to the edge of the ending, and then it stopped, deliberately, the way a sentence stops before the word that changes everything.

Waiting for someone to supply the last line.
