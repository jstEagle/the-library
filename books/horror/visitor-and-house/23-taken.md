## 23. Taken

The morning of the eleventh of March began with frost on the inside of my window.

That detail matters. I have learned—was taught, in the hardest classroom there is—that at Hollowmere the small wrongnesses arrive first, like tremors before a quake. The frost formed on the *inside* of the glass while the outside stayed clear, as though the cold were not coming from the world but from somewhere beneath it, rising through the foundations, through the floors, into the panes. I sat on the edge of my bed and watched it bloom in fern-patterns and told myself: old building, bad insulation, pipes in the walls.

I no longer believe my own reassurances. I write them down anyway. That is the discipline. That is what separates the documentarian from the madman—or perhaps only documents the difference.

I dressed in the dark because the radiator was ticking and I did not want to hear it. In the hallway mirror my face looked like a man's face. I checked for that specifically now. Since the House offered me things, since Grieves' smile wore another smile underneath it, I check. Eyes the right color. Teeth the right number. A scar on the left knuckle from a bicycle fall in 2009 that my mother had bandaged with a dish towel because we couldn't find the first-aid kit. All present. All accounted for.

Attendance is a kind of prayer. I had written that in the notebook weeks ago and underlined it twice, and every morning since, when I stood before my third-period class with the roll sheet in my hand, I understood it literally. Each name spoken aloud was a small act of witness. *You exist. I see you. I will say so on paper.* At Hollowmere, where names vanished from yearbooks and seats stayed warm for students nobody remembered, the roll call was not bureaucracy. It was a ward.

Wren Ashcombe always answered on the second beat. Not immediately—she liked to let the name hang a moment, as if checking whether the air would try to swallow it—but always on the second beat, her voice low and slightly rough, "Here," with a little downward inflection that made it sound less like an answer and more like a vow.

I want to be precise about all of this. Precision is all I have left.

* * *

Third period. American Literature, eleven students, room 114, the radiator ticking its arrhythmic tick. The light came through the east windows in long amber bars and laid itself across the desks like something spilled. Outside, past the parking lot, the excavation site sat behind its chain-link fence, the hole in the earth covered now with plywood sheets weighted by cinder blocks—the district had ordered work paused, officially due to "permitting issues," unofficially because Odile Brann had been fired and her crew scattered and no one wanted to be the one standing over that door with a shovel.

Odile herself had come to me two nights ago, smelling of cold air and cheap coffee, and told me she'd dreamed of a hallway of doors with her name on one of them. She'd said it flatly, the way you'd report a flat tire. Then she'd said: *"Whatever you're going to do, Nakamura, do it soon. The ground's getting impatient."*

I hadn't asked how ground could be impatient. There are questions whose answers arrive whether or not you ask them.

Eleven students. Wren made twelve. No one else could see her—I'd stopped testing this months ago, after the day I asked Marcus Delgado to pass a handout to "the girl by the window" and Marcus had looked at the empty desk, then at me, then at the handout, with the particular careful blankness of someone deciding not to know something—and yet she took up space the way real things do. Her coat dripped actual meltwater onto the floor. Her pencil shavings smelled of cedar. When she laughed, which was rare and always sideways, like something escaping through a crack, the other students turned toward the sound without knowing why, their faces puzzled, their bodies remembering what their minds had been made to forget.

We were reading *Beloved*. I had chosen it deliberately, because Morrison understood something about houses and hunger and the debt of the past that no faculty handbook would ever teach. We were halfway through Chapter 7, the one where Sethe tries to explain to Paul D what it means to have a thing taken out of you and a thing put in its place.

Wren had her hand raised. This was unusual enough that I marked it—she preferred to speak uninvited, low-voiced, from the side of her mouth, as though participation were a crime she committed quietly. But her hand was up, straight and certain, and when I nodded to her she stood.

She stood, and I remember thinking: *she never stands.*

"I think I finally understand what the House wants," she said.

Not Beloved's house. Our house. Her voice carried down the rows of desks and the amber light seemed to lean toward her, and the radiator went silent mid-tick, the silence arriving so completely that I heard my own pulse in my ears.

"It doesn't want obedience," Wren said. "Obedience is cheap. It can get obedience from anyone—it got it from the whole faculty for a hundred and thirty years. What it wants—" and here she turned and looked directly at me, and her eyes were doing something I had never seen them do, something bright and frightened and resolved all at once, like a person standing at the edge of a diving board who has already decided, "—what it wants is to be *finished*. A house isn't finished until someone lives in it willingly. Everything else is just storage."

"Wren," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, as if the room were pressing on it.

"That's why it keeps lending me back," she went on, faster now, words tumbling. "It's not done with me. I'm a message it hasn't finished sending. Felix—Mr. Nakamura—" she corrected herself, formal, almost laughing, "—listen. Verse four isn't the last cost. Verse four is the *doorway* to the last cost. There's a fifth. There has to be. Every song ends or it isn't a song, it's just a trap with a melody, and the House is—"

The chalk dust in the light bars stopped moving.

I need you to understand what that looked like. Dust motes do not stop. They drift, they tumble, they ride currents too small to feel. But at 10:47 on the morning of the eleventh of March, the dust in room 114 hung absolutely still, each mote pinned in the amber light like insects in resin, and Wren Ashcombe stood in the middle of it with her mouth still moving—

—and no sound coming out.

Her lips shaped the word. I read it off her. I have read it ten thousand times since, in the notebook, in the dark, on the back of my eyelids.

*"—unfinished."*

Then the light went out of her.

Not dimmed. *Left.* It poured out of her the way water pours out of a tipped glass—all at once, in every direction, sinking into the floorboards—and what remained standing in her clothes was thinner than she was, translucent at the edges, a Wren-shaped absence wearing a Wren-colored coat. Her eyes found mine one last time and they were not afraid anymore. They were apologetic.

*I'm sorry,* her mouth said. *Don't follow the voice. Follow the warmth.*

And the room exhaled.

Every student blinked at once—a single synchronized contraction of thirty eyelids, the kind of unified motion that should be impossible and that I had seen once before, in the faculty meeting, in forty smiling faces—and when their eyes opened they were looking at the front of the room, at me, attentive, mild, waiting for the lesson to continue. Eleven students. Twelve desks. One of them empty.

No. Not empty.

Warm.

* * *

I crossed the room without deciding to. Later, reconstructing it, I cannot find the interval between seeing the empty desk and having my palm flat against its surface; the moment simply refuses to be documented, a gap in the record, and gaps in records are the House's native language.

The desktop was warm. Not sun-warm—the amber bars lay everywhere equally, and the other desks were cool as stone. This was body-warm, the specific animal heat of skin recently lifted away, the temperature of a handprint fading. I pressed harder, stupidly, as if I could hold the warmth in place by force, and the wood gave back nothing but its own slow cooling, degree by degree, the way everything at Hollowmere eventually cooled.

On the desk, precisely centered, lay her pencil. Cedar-shaved, tooth-marked at the end—she chewed it when she was thinking, a habit she claimed dated from before her first taking, one of the few possessions of her former self she trusted. It had not fallen. It had been *placed*.

Beside it, folded once, a page torn from her notebook. I knew her notebook; I had seen it a hundred times, its cover soft with handling, its pages dense with her slanted, urgent script. This page was blank except for four lines, written in that script but hurried, the letters leaning like runners:

*Verse four is not the end.*
*The House fears completion, not resistance.*
*Follow the warmth, not the humming.*
*If I don't come back to class tomorrow, I was never enrolled.*

The last line broke something in me that I suspect does not grow back.

Because I understood it. Oh, I understood it with a clarity that felt like falling. If Wren did not return—if the House decided this time to keep what it liked rather than lend back what it didn't need—then within a day, maybe two, the enrollment records would revise themselves, the photographs would blur, Mrs. Voss's key would open onto nothing, and I would stand in room 114 teaching *Beloved* to eleven students beside twelve desks, unable to say why the twelfth made my chest hurt. I would remember her the way you remember a dream: vividly, uselessly, with the full knowledge that no testimony survives waking.

Unless it was written down.

I am writing it down. I am always writing it down.

* * *

The attendance sheet was on my podium where I'd left it, clipped to its board, my fountain pen resting in the groove. I picked it up with hands that shook so badly the paper rattled like a small trapped animal, and I ran my finger down the column of names.

Aldous, M. — present.
Brann is not a student; skip. Delgado, M. — present.

My finger reached the bottom of the list, below the printed names, below the eleven souls the district believed occupied my room. And there, in handwriting that was almost mine but not quite—the same slant, the same pressure, missing some essential tremor of doubt—two words had been added in ink I did not own:

*Ashcombe, W. — present.*

Present.

She was gone. Gone the way the others had gone, gone the way Aldous had gone, gone into the warm dark under the school where voices waited in jars and years sat corked in bottles—and the ledger said *present*. The prayer had been said and the prayer had been answered and the answer was a lie with perfect penmanship.

I laughed. I want to confess that: I stood alone in an empty classroom holding an attendance sheet and I laughed, one short ugly bark of a sound, because the alternative was screaming and screaming would bring the faculty running with their synchronized smiles and their offers of help and their warm handshakes, and I could not survive being welcomed just then.

The students had filed out during all of this. Of course they had. The bell had rung—I never heard it—and they had gathered their books and departed in their mild, untroubled way, and one of them, Marcus, had paused at the door and looked back at me with an expression I couldn't parse, something flickering behind his eyes like a fish under ice, and then he'd gone too.

Room 114 held me and the warm desk and the attendance sheet and the radiator, which had resumed its ticking as if it had never stopped, as if the whole machinery of the world had merely paused to swallow.

* * *

I went to the archive. Instinct, or inheritance—Voss's lessons had sunk deeper than I knew. The old woman met me among the stacks before I'd taken three steps into the periodicals room, materializing out of the shelf-shadow the way she did, small and dry and smelling of paper dust and something faintly medicinal, and she read my face in one glance the way she read spines.

"She's gone," Voss said. Not a question.

"Mid-sentence." My voice sounded borrowed. "She was telling me about the fifth verse. She was telling me there *is* a fifth verse."

Voss closed her eyes. For a moment she looked every one of her eighty-some years, older, a woman made of accumulated interest payments. "Then it's moving faster than it ever has. Taking her in daylight. In front of you." She opened her eyes and they were hard as brass. "It's frightened of you, Mr. Nakamura. Frightened things grab."

"It left a note. It left her *pencil*, it left—" I stopped, steadied myself against the shelving. "Voss, the attendance sheet says present. Her name is on it. In my handwriting."

"In the House's handwriting." She took my arm—her grip stronger than her frame suggested, fingers like roots. "Listen to me. That mark is not a clerical error. It's a claim. When the ledger says present, the House considers her *in residence*. Which means she's not destroyed, not yet, not fully. Kept things can be returned. I'm proof. You're speaking to proof." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "But kept things can also be *spent*. And it has never spent one this quickly. Whatever she knows—whatever verse is rattling around inside her—it wants it somewhere it can't be sung."

"The door under the school."

"The door under the school." Voss released my arm and reached into her cardigan pocket and drew out the key—tooth and brass, warm-looking, wrong-looking—and pressed it into my palm and closed my fingers over it with both of her hands, and her hands were trembling now too, and I realized with a lurch that the librarian was terrified, had perhaps always been terrified, had sung her verse forty years ago and been paying interest ever since and was now, at the end of her account, making a withdrawal. "The excavation entrance is sealed with plywood and fear. Use the field instead. The lone doorway. It opens for the key and it opens for the marked, and you, Felix, are very marked."

"And if I go in after her?"

Voss smiled, and it was the saddest expression I have ever seen on a human face, the smile of someone watching a younger self walk toward a mistake she made decades ago and cannot regret because it bought her daughter's breath.

"Then you'll finally learn what the third verse costs," she said. "Go home first. Sleep if you can. Eat something. Write down everything you remember about her voice, because you're going to need it, and memory is the first thing the House taxes." She turned back to her shelves, dismissal and blessing in the same motion. "And Nakamura—when you find her, tell her the interest on my loan is called due. Tell her Voss said: *sing loud enough to be heard from inside.*"

* * *

I did not go home.

I went to my classroom and I sat at my desk and I opened the notebook to a fresh page, and my hand, steadier now than it had any right to be, wrote at the top in letters I recognized as entirely my own:

**March 11. Wren Ashcombe, taken 10:47 a.m., room 114, mid-sentence. Subject of sentence: the fifth verse.**

Below that, the facts. The stopped dust. The poured-out light. The warm desk. The placed pencil. The note. The attendance sheet and its lie. I documented until the page was full and then I started another, and somewhere in the second page the shaking stopped and something else began, something colder and cleaner, a resolve assembling itself in my chest the way the frost had assembled on the window that morning—not from the world, but from underneath it, rising.

They had taken a girl who had already been taken once. They had taken her while she was giving me the truth. They had marked her *present* in a book meant to prove she existed, converting witness into custody, prayer into predation. And they thought—Grieves with his warm handshake, the House with its thousand reasonable mouths—they thought that was the end of it. Another entry. Another jar. Another year corked and shelved.

But they had made an error. The same error every predator makes about the ones who document.

They thought the record was a memorial.

The record is a summons.

I clicked the cap onto my fountain pen, and the small sound of it rang through the empty room like the first note of something. On the desk before me lay Voss's key, tooth and brass, catching the last of the amber light. Tomorrow I would walk out past the parking lot, past the fence, into the empty field where a doorway stood alone in the grass, and I would open it, and I would go in after her.

Tonight, I practiced breathing.

Tomorrow, I would learn the third verse.
