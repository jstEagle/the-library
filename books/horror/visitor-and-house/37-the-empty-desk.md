## 37. The Empty Desk

The morning the school resumed, it resumed the way water closes over a drowned thing.

Wren stood on the lawn at 7:40 a.m., her backpack straps cutting into her shoulders, and watched the buses arrive. Ordinary buses. Ordinary children spilling out in clumps of color and noise, someone's headphones leaking a tinny rhythm, someone else laughing so hard they had to stop walking. The frost was burning off the grass in silver threads. The flagpole's rope clinked against the metal in the wind, a small patient percussion, and everything about the scene insisted—gently, insistently, the way a lullaby insists—that nothing had ever happened.

She knew better. She knew better the way a burn scar knows better.

Three weeks. Three weeks of the House, of the halls that rearranged, of singing verses she hadn't wanted to know, of fading like a photograph left in the sun. Three weeks of being *kept*, and then one impossible dawn on the lawn with dew soaking through her clothes and a name in her throat that wouldn't come.

She walked up the front steps. The double doors were heavy and warm, the way doors are when the building behind them has been breathing all night, and she made herself not think about that, made herself push through into the smell of floor wax and chalk dust and coffee from the faculty lounge.

The halls were full. That was the first wrongness, or maybe the first rightness: the halls were *full*.

Marcus Okafor was at his locker, arguing cheerfully with Dev Patel about a chemistry problem. Priya Chandrasekaran sat in the window seat by the east stairwell with a book, exactly as she had sat every morning since September, before she'd gone off-site, before her name had become a redaction in a ledger older than the school. She looked up as Wren passed and gave her the small polite nod of a girl who had never been missing a day in her life.

"Priya," Wren said. Her voice came out strange, too careful.

"Hey, Wren." Priya smiled. "You look tired. Big weekend?"

*You were in a jar,* Wren wanted to say. *You were mid-laugh on a shelf in a hall that doesn't exist, and a man with a notebook chained to his wrist sang you out of it.* Instead she said, "Something like that," and kept walking, because what else was there? You couldn't hand someone back their captivity. They hadn't asked for the memory. Maybe the not-remembering was the last kindness the song had done them, though Wren suspected the song hadn't been kind at all. The song had simply been thorough.

Her classroom was the same. Third period, the desks in their tired rows, the radiator ticking its winter tick. And there, third row from the window, second seat back: the empty desk.

Not *an* empty desk. The empty desk. The one whose occupant she could not name.

She sat down behind it before the bell, the way she'd been doing for days now, and rested her fingertips on its surface. Cool laminate. A groove where someone had once carved something and the school had sanded it smooth but never quite managed to erase the *shape* of it. She had decided, privately, that the groove was a letter. She had decided it was an F, though when she looked directly at it, it was only a groove, only a scar in the laminate, only nothing.

The teacher arrived. She was a substitute, or she had always been the teacher, or the position had been open all year; the schedules in Wren's binder disagreed with one another in small, polite ways, like witnesses who'd agreed on a story. The teacher took attendance.

"Okafor?"

"Here."

"Patel?"

"Here."

"Ashcombe?"

"Here." Wren's voice, steady, surprising her.

The teacher read down the list and paused at a name Wren couldn't hear—couldn't hear the way you can't hear a note outside your range—and then, without any change of expression, marked something in the book and moved on. Wren stared at the attendance sheet later, when the teacher stepped out, and found the line. It wasn't blank. It wasn't redacted. It simply said *present*, in the teacher's own handwriting, and Wren had no memory of the name being spoken, and the desk in front of her was empty, and the groove in the laminate was only a groove.

*If someone is reading this,* the notebook had said, in handwriting almost familiar, *the prayer worked.*

She was reading it. She was reading it every night, under her blankets, by flashlight, and every night she understood a little more, and every morning she understood a little less, because the erasure was not a wall but a tide. It came in while she slept. Names she'd recovered on Tuesday were gone by Thursday. The face she'd reconstructed—dark hair going gray at the temples, a habit of tapping his pen twice before writing, a way of looking at you as though you were a sentence he intended to finish—would not hold still. It kept sliding. By Friday she could remember the pen-tapping but not the face. By the following Wednesday she could remember that she had once remembered, and that was all, and it was the cruelest thing the House had ever done to her, worse than the keeping: it had taught her the exact weight of what she'd lost, and then taken the loss itself, leaving only the outline of the loss, a shadow of a shadow, an ache with no tooth.

She wrote everything down. She wrote it down the way he had taught her—without knowing he'd taught her—documenting everything, schedules and faces and the smell of chalk dust, because a record was the only weapon left and the only grave.

* * *

Odile Brann was smoking behind the gymnasium, which was not a gymnasium, which was a fenced rectangle of raw earth and a foundation pour that had gone in smooth as poured milk and now stood half-built and unremarkable, as if it had always been half-built, as if no crew had ever unearthed a door set into bedrock with its bolts thrown from the inside.

Wren found her there on a Thursday, following the smell of cigarette smoke the way you follow a thread through a labyrinth.

"You're not supposed to smoke on campus," Wren said.

Odile looked at her. She was a big woman with forearms like dock rope and a face that had been weathered by things other than weather, and she looked at Wren for a long moment with an expression Wren recognized because she'd been wearing it herself for weeks: the look of someone checking whether the other person is *still there*, still real, still holding the same fragment of the broken mirror.

"You're the Ashcombe girl," Odile said. "You came to the site once. You told me—" She stopped. She ground the cigarette under her boot with unnecessary force. "You told me the door would open for me because part of me already belonged. I told you to get lost."

"You held a door open," Wren said. "With your arms. Until they broke."

The silence between them was enormous. Somewhere beyond the fence, a compressor chugged at the construction site, and a crow said something bitter from the roof of the gym-to-be, and neither of them moved.

"I have dreams," Odile said finally. "About a hallway. Doors on both sides. Every door's got my name on it." She flexed her hands, and Wren saw that they trembled, and saw too the faint white scarring across both forearms, like the marks of a pressure that had been enormous and was now only a story the skin told. "Doctors say it's stress. I say doctors can go—" She stopped again. She was a woman who kept stopping, Wren realized, a woman whose sentences kept arriving at edges she couldn't name. "The crew. Two of my boys quit that first week, no pay. The rest of us finished the foundation. And now I drive past this school and my hands shake, and I don't know why, and everybody tells me nothing happened here, and the thing is—" her voice dropped, went rough, "—the thing is, some part of me *believes* them. Some part of me thinks I made it up. And that part gets bigger every week. Like water getting into a wall."

"I remember you," Wren said.

"You remember wrong, probably. Everybody remembers wrong." But Odile had come a step closer, and her eyes were wet and furious. "What do you remember?"

"Everything," Wren said. "And less every day. That's the trick of it. That's the fourth verse working itself out of the world." She hugged her backpack to her chest like a shield. "He sang all five. He unmade it. And the price of singing all five is being unmade—out of every record, every memory, every mind. Including—" her voice caught, "—including mine. I'm forgetting him on purpose, in a way. The song is doing it. But I'm the one who has to notice it happening."

"Who's *him*?"

And there it was: the empty space, the socket where the tooth had been. Wren reached for the name and found the groove in the desk, the pen tapped twice, a voice singing in a hall of kept years, and no name, no name, no name.

"I don't know," she said, and it was the truest sentence she had spoken in a month, and it nearly broke her. "I don't know, and I *did* know, and I wrote it down, and I'm going to show you what I wrote down, because you're the only other person in this town whose arms remember."

Odile looked at her scarred forearms. Then she nodded, once, the way you nod at a load you've decided to lift.

"Okay," she said. "Show me."

* * *

They met on Sundays, at first in Odile's truck at the far end of the reservoir lot, later in the booth at the far end of the diner on Route 9, where the coffee was bad and the waitress was deaf and indifferent, which made it the safest room in the county.

The ritual was always the same. Wren would read aloud from the notebook, and Odile would listen with her big scarred hands wrapped around her mug, and every so often something Wren read would strike a flint in Odile's memory—a smell, a sound, the exact weight of a crowbar—and Odile would say it out loud, and Wren would write it down, and the two fragments would fuse into something sturdier than either.

"The door was bolted from the inside," Odile said, one gray Sunday in early spring. "I keep trying to make that mean something else. A prank. A mechanism. But I touched it, and it hummed. Under my palm. Like a throat."

"Like a throat," Wren repeated, writing. "He wrote that the halls hummed. He wrote that the walls sang when the House was happy."

"The House." Odile said the words the way she'd say the name of a man who'd cheated her on a job. "I dreamed about it again. The hallway of doors. But this time—" she hesitated, embarrassed, this woman who feared nothing with a body, "—this time one of the doors was open. And behind it was just a wall. Bricks. And I woke up *happy*, Wren. I woke up happy about a bricked-up door, and I didn't know why until I read your notebook."

"Because it's dead," Wren said. "The House. He unmade it. Your dream is just the shape the absence left."

"Then why do I still shake?"

Wren didn't have a good answer. She had an honest one. "Because it was real," she said. "And because everyone else got to forget, and you didn't, and that's not a wound that closes just because the thing that made it is gone."

They agreed on rules, the way survivors of anything agree on rules. They would meet every Sunday. They would read the notebook aloud, cover to cover, start to finish, no skipping, even the parts that hurt, especially the parts that hurt. When one of them remembered something the notebook didn't hold, they would say it aloud and the other would write it down. When the tide came in—when a name went soft, when a face slid, when Odile woke up convinced she'd invented the humming—they would call each other, no matter the hour, and read one page, just one, just to hold the line.

"We're not going to get it all back," Wren said. "That's not what this is."

"Then what is it?"

"It's—" Wren looked out the diner window at the parking lot, at the frost coming up off the asphalt, at the ordinary world going about its ordinary business on top of a grave it had agreed to pave over. "The school has its version. The school's version is that nothing happened. The archive is clean. The yearbook pages aren't blank anymore. Priya Chandrasekaran has never missed a day. That's their memory: official, complete, and false. And then there's forgetting, which is what the song did to everybody else, which isn't a version at all, it's just an absence." She turned back. "What we're doing is neither. We're remembering *on purpose*. Imperfectly. Knowing we'll get it wrong. Choosing the wrongness anyway. Because the alternative is letting the official version be the only version, and I—" her hand flattened on the notebook, "—I can't do that. He didn't do all that so that the only record left in the world was a lie."

Odile turned her mug slowly on its saucer, a quarter turn, like someone zeroing a dial.

"My grandmother was from County Mayo," she said. "They had a word. Not a word—a practice. When the English burned the records, the families would sit up at night and tell everything aloud, and the children were made to repeat it back, and whatever got garbled got garbled, but it got *said*. She used to say: the paper forgets, the stone forgets, the mouth is the only archive that fights back." She looked up. "So we're the mouth."

"We're the mouth," Wren agreed, and wrote it down, because everything got written down now, everything, always.

* * *

Spring came on the way it always did at Hollowmere, which is to say beautifully and without apology. The elms along the drive leafed out. The track team ran laps in the long evening light. The foundation behind the school got its walls, its roof, its windows, and by May it was unmistakably, boringly, a gymnasium, and the students complained about the smell of paint, and the vice-principal—a new one, or an old one, the records disagreed—gave a cheerful speech at assembly about school spirit, and everyone smiled, all at once, warmly, and Wren felt the cold go through her like a blade of lake water and made herself smile back.

She had learned to smile back. That was part of the practice too. You could not walk these halls with your teeth bared; they would notice, and whatever noticed now was small, was vestigial, was a hum in the walls that never got past four verses—but it noticed, and she had no interest in being noticed.

She kept her attendance honest. That was the other part. She'd taken it upon herself, quietly, without anyone asking, to know—*really* know—where every person in her classes was, every day. Present meant present. Absent meant absent, and absent meant a phone call home, and a phone call home meant a voice on the line, a confirmation, a *body accounted for*. The teachers thought she was odd. The office thought she was overzealous. Let them. Someone had to count, and count truly, and she had learned from a man whose name she could not hold what happened when nobody counted.

The notebook was more than half read now, in the Sunday ritual. She knew its shape the way you know a piece of music: the intake form, the attendance prayer, the excavation, the blank pages, the girl who shouldn't be there. *He wrote about me,* she thought, not for the first time, *before I ever sat in his class. He wrote down my name when I told him not to.* She had been furious, once, reading that entry—furious and then, turning the page, undone by it, because the next line said: *I wrote it down because a name on paper is a hook in the world, and I intend to hold on.*

She held on. Page by page, Sunday by Sunday, she held on.

But there were entries she could not read. Not because they were damaged—because they were *about her*, and the girl in them was fading, and the teacher in them was already more gone than the notebook could reach. In one entry he described her humming four verses and refusing to finish. She had no memory of that. In another he wrote that she had begged him to stop, told him about a teacher named Aldous who was now a trophy on a shelf. *Aldous,* Wren thought, testing the name. It rang faintly, like a bell in a house she'd once visited. She wrote it down. Odile would hear it on Sunday. It would be said aloud. It would be kept.

That was the whole religion of it, she supposed. Say it aloud. Write it down. Come back next week and say it again. Imperfect, garbled, human—the mouth fighting back.

* * *

On the last day of school, the buses left at noon, and the building emptied the way a lung empties, and Wren walked the halls one final time before summer took the place away from her for three months.

She stopped, at the end, in the classroom. Third row from the window, second seat back.

The empty desk.

All year it had stayed empty. Not assigned, not filled, not removed. She had watched janitors work around it, had watched the seating charts quietly accommodate it, had watched the school's official forgetting bend itself around this one small piece of furniture like water around a stone. Maybe it was nothing. Maybe it was a broken desk nobody had gotten around to fixing. Maybe it was the last small stubborn fact the unmaking couldn't smooth over—a groove in the world shaped like a person.

She sat down in the desk behind it, the way she always did, and put her hand flat on its surface.

"Sunday," she said out loud, to the empty room, to the empty desk, to the mouth and the archive and the tide. "We're on chapter nine. What the ground gives back. Odile's bringing the badge."

The radiator ticked. The window threw a long gold parallelogram across the floor. Somewhere down the hall a door closed, and the building settled into its summer silence, and Wren could have sworn—would write it down that night, would read it aloud on Sunday, would make Odile say it back—that from somewhere below the floor, below the foundation, below the bedrock, faint as breath, the walls hummed.

Four verses. Never more than four.

But four was already too many, and she sat in the gold light with her hand on the desk and did not leave until the humming stopped.

Then she opened the notebook to a fresh page, and wrote the date, and wrote: *Still here. Still counting. Still the mouth.* And beneath that, because the last entry she'd ever read aloud had taught her how a record should end, she wrote the only prayer she had left:

*If someone is reading this, keep saying it back.*
