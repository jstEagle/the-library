## 3. What Grandma Kept Under the Linoleum

My grandmother's house had a smell, and the smell had layers, like sediment. Top layer: lavender and old carpet, the smell of a woman who believed in airing things out and had recently lost the war against dust. Beneath that: the particular staleness of a house that had been shut for two months while solicitors argued about probate. And beneath *that* — I only noticed it now, kneeling in the attic with a torch between my teeth — something mineral. Cold stone and iron. The smell of a cellar in a house that didn't have one.

I'd come up here to avoid Priya.

Not avoid, exactly. My cousin had appointed herself Foreman of the Estate Clear-Out, and she ran it the way she ran everything: with a clipboard, a colour-coded labelling system, and the serene certainty that any object not obviously useful was obviously rubbish. I'd watched her hold my grandmother's good gravy boat over the charity bag and say, "Well, it's chipped," in the tone of a judge passing sentence. The gravy boat had survived on a technicality. I hadn't the heart to fight for anything else.

So: attic. The one room she'd agreed, with visible reluctance, I could "have a poke about in," because it was full of boxes labelled in my grandmother's handwriting and therefore, by some legal fiction, mine to sort.

The linoleum came first.

The attic floor was covered in it — ugly, mustard-yellow stuff, the kind of flooring that outlives empires. Except it didn't cover the floor. It covered *most* of the floor, and where it stopped, near the north gable, the boards were bare, and the boards had grooves in them. Five parallel lines, evenly spaced, running the length of a single plank like someone had dragged a very patient fork through wet wood.

I told myself: woodworm. I told myself: settling. I told myself, in the voice I'd been using for twenty years, the reasonable voice, the voice of a man who crawls through storm drains for fun and has never once seen anything down there that didn't have a municipal explanation: *old houses do things, Tamsin, that's what old houses are for.*

Then I noticed the linoleum had been cut. Not worn through, not torn — cut, with a blade, in a neat rectangle the size of a ledger, and the flap had been glued back down with such care that you'd only find it by kneeling on the floor with your face six inches from the mustard yellow, which is apparently the kind of man I am now.

I peeled it up.

Underneath, in a shallow depression between the joists, wrapped in a tea towel gone stiff with age: a ledger. A proper one, cloth-bound, the kind with a ribbon bookmark and a spine that cracked like a knuckle when I opened it.

*1974* was written on the first page. Just the year, in my grandmother's hand — I knew her hand the way you know a face, the little forward lean on the *7*, the *9* with the closed loop she'd learned in some postwar penmanship class and never abandoned.

I want to say I hesitated. I want to tell you that some part of me, some decent part, felt the shape of what was coming and put the book back under its mustard-yellow lid. But grief had already done its work on me by then. Grief makes burglars of us all. I'd spent two months going through her drawers, her cupboards, her letters, looking for — I don't know. Her. Some residue of her that wasn't a charity-shop gravy boat. If she'd left a ledger under the attic floor, then a ledger under the attic floor was a letter to me.

I read it.

* * *

It was an accounts book. That's the mundane horror of it — it looked like what it pretended to be. Columns ruled in pencil: a date, a sum, a word. Monthly, without fail, on the first Saturday.

*2 Feb — 4s 6d — maintenance.*
*1 Mar — 4s 6d — maintenance.*
*5 Apr — 4s 6d — maintenance.*

Five names across the top of each page, five columns, five shares of four-and-six, which by 1974 was already an absurdly old-fashioned sum, the kind of money that existed only in ledgers and in the minds of women who remembered rationing. My grandmother, Edith Whitlock. Then: Harold Bexley. Then: Marjorie Osei. Then: Ruth Callow. And the fifth column —

The fifth column was destroyed.

Someone had scratched out the name at the head of it so hard, so many times, that the paper had torn through in a ragged vertical wound, and the scratch-marks continued down the entire column, month after month, year after year. Every entry. Every date. Whatever sums had been recorded under that fifth name had been obliterated with a ferocity that had nothing to do with bookkeeping. You don't scribble out a misprint like that. You scribble out a misprint once and initial it. This was decades of scribbling, the same name, the same violence, over and over, like someone trying to kill a word.

Except — and this is the part I keep returning to, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone — the scratching stopped. In the last quarter of the book, 2021, the final year, the fifth column simply went blank. Not scratched out. Blank. As though whoever had been maintaining the maintenance had finally, at ninety-three, run out of either ink or fear.

And in that blank column, in the last entry, December 2021, three weeks before she died, my grandmother had written a single word in her small forward-leaning hand:

*owing.*

I sat on the bare boards with the grooves in them, in a house with no cellar that smelled of cold stone, and I did the arithmetic that grief does when it curdles.

Five names. Five friends. My grandmother had had exactly five close friends — the photograph on the sitting-room mantelpiece proved it, five young faces squinting into a 1974 summer, and I'd looked at that photograph two nights ago and counted six chairs at the table behind them. The chair. The chair that was in every photograph and no one's memory.

Four-and-six a month, split five ways, for forty-seven years. Maintenance. You maintain a boiler. You maintain a hedge. You maintain, if you are five ordinary people who have done something extraordinary and unforgivable, whatever it is you've got shut under your house.

And the fifth name — the name that had to be destroyed, that couldn't even be *written* without tearing the page —

I got a pencil and held it at a shallow angle and shaded lightly across the worst-torn entry, the first one, February 1974, where the indentations might survive even where the ink didn't. Graphite ghost-writing, the trick every schoolchild knows. Most of it was gone. But the indentations were deep — whoever had scratched had pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages — and under the pencil's grey dust, letter by letter, like a face surfacing through ice:

E — d — m — u — n — d.

And below it, in a different, rounder hand, not my grandmother's, one word that had escaped the scratching entirely because it sat outside the column's ruled edge:

*Vail.*

* * *

Priya found me at dusk, sitting in the attic dark with the ledger on my knees and the torch off, which I hadn't noticed I'd done. The grooves in the plank were about a foot from my shoe. I had, at some point, apparently decided not to sit near them.

"You've been up here four hours." She stood in the doorway with two mugs of tea, and I watched her eyes go from my face to the book and change. Priya has a face that does its business in public, always has — she was the child who couldn't lie about the broken vase because her forehead filed a report first. "What's that?"

"Accounts book. Grandma's. From 1974."

"Accounts." She set the mugs down on a box marked *KITCHEN — MISC* with the care of someone buying time. "Tamsin. It's an accounts book."

"It has five names in it. Monthly payments. Labelled maintenance."

"So she ran a savings club with her friends. Women did that. They did it for decades — it's called a *pardner*, my mum was in one—"

"Four of the names are her four best friends."

"Which is exactly what a savings club *is*—"

"And the fifth name," I said, "is Edmund Vail. And it's been scratched out so hard it tore the page, every month, for forty-seven years. And the last thing she ever wrote in the book is the word *owing*."

The attic went quiet in the way attics do, which is to say the house's small noises — pipes, wind, the settling of old timber — suddenly sounded like they were being made *on purpose*.

Priya sat down on a box across from me. She looked at the ledger for a long time. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into a register I'd never heard her use, and it took me a moment to identify it, because I'd never heard my cousin be afraid of anything except wasps and our grandfather's driving.

"Where did you get the name? The Vail part."

"Under the pencil. It survived outside the column."

"Show me."

I showed her. She read it the way you'd read a diagnosis. Then she closed the book — gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping thing — and put both hands flat on its cover, and said:

"Put it back."

"Priya—"

"Under the lino. Where you found it. Then we finish the house, we sell the house, we get you a very good holiday somewhere with no basements, and we never—"

"You *know* the name." It came out of me before I could soften it. "You went still. You know it."

"I know it's not our business."

"Edith Whitlock is my grandmother. Whatever she was doing for fifty years with four-and-six a month is—"

"*Exactly as much your business as it was mine when I found it three years ago,*" she said, and then stopped, and I watched her realise what she'd said, and I watched her decide, visibly, in that honest forehead of hers, whether to double down or retreat.

She doubled down. Priya always doubles down. It's her best and worst quality and I love her for it and in that attic I could have wrung her neck.

"Three years ago," I said. "When?"

"The week after her first fall. She was in hospital, she was — she kept asking me to fetch things from the house. Specific things. And one of them was 'the green book from under the attic floor,' and I'm not an idiot, Tam, I looked at it before I took it to her." Her hands were still flat on the cover, as if the book might get up. "And she made me promise — she made me *promise*, with her hand on my wrist, and Edith Whitlock had never once in her life held anyone's wrist — she made me promise that whatever I saw in it, I'd put it out of my mind, and that if I ever saw frost inside the house, in a pattern, I should leave and not come back and take you with me."

The mineral smell. Cold stone and iron. The grooves in the plank, five of them, patient as a pulse.

"Frost," I said.

"Forget the frost."

"*Priya.*"

"She was ninety-three and concussed and she'd had a lifetime of whatever this is," Priya said, standing, taking the ledger off the box and holding it against her chest like a shield or a child. "Grieving women in 1974 did all kinds of things — séances, ouija boards, half the country was at it, they'd all lost someone in the war and then they lost people to the war's long tail — and then they grew up and got on with it, and the ones who didn't put it behind them ended up like her. Afraid of weather. Paying rent on a sin for fifty years. That's not a mystery, Tam, that's just what guilt looks like when it's been kept in a shoebox."

"Then why does the book say *owing*?"

She didn't answer. She was at the attic hatch, lowering herself onto the ladder, the ledger tucked into her cardigan, and she paused there with her head below the level of the floor, which meant she had to look up at me to say the last of it, and I will give her this: she didn't look away.

"Because she was dying," she said, "and dying people settle accounts. It doesn't mean the account was real. It means she thought it was." A beat. The house made its small deliberate noises. "Burn it or bury it, Tam, but stop *reading* it. Some things are only dangerous when you look directly at them. Ask any photograph in this house."

She went down. I heard her feet on the ladder, the hatch closing, her footsteps crossing the landing — pausing, just once, outside the third bedroom, the one with the painted-shut door that nobody ever mentioned, before continuing down the stairs.

I sat alone in the dark with the grooves a foot from my shoe and did the last of the arithmetic.

Edmund Vail. Not in the family albums. Not in the Christmas-card lists. Not in a single story my grandmother ever told me in twenty-six years of Sundays — and she told *stories*, she was a woman who could make buying a stamp sound like an epic. A man whose name had to be destroyed monthly, in her own handwriting, for longer than I've been alive. A man who owed four-and-six a month and had, apparently, stopped paying.

And underneath all of it, under the ledger, under the lino, under the mustard yellow, under everything my reasonable voice had spent twenty years papering over — the word she'd left me, the only word in the blank column, in the hand that leaned forward like a woman walking into wind:

*owing.*

I went down the ladder. On the landing, the third bedroom's door stood in its frame, painted shut, painted over so many times the panels had gone soft and featureless, like a face smoothed by frost.

I put my palm flat against it.

It was cold. Not attic-cold, not old-house-cold. Cold like the tunnel walls in February, when the water stops moving and the dark starts to feel *occupied*.

Tomorrow, I told myself. Tomorrow I'd look up the name.

I want it on the record that I meant to sleep on it. That I was, for approximately one more night, still a man who rationalised.

Then, from behind the painted door — faint, patient, five parallel lines of sound, like something dragging a fork through wet wood — the house began, very quietly, to scratch.
