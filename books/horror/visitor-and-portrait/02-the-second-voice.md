## 2. The Second Voice

The recorder was a gift from a listener who worried about me. That should have been my first clue. Nobody worries about a night-shift radio host out of generosity; they worry because they've noticed something wrong, and the gift is their way of saying so without having to say it.

It arrived in a padded envelope with no return address, third week of October. A Sony handheld, still in its box, and a note on lined paper: *You talk in your sleep. You might want to know what you say.*

I did what any reasonable person would do with an anonymous recording device from a stranger: I used it. Every night since, I've set it on the nightstand beside the glass of water I never drink and pressed record before the light went off. Not because I believed the note. Because I'm a documenter by trade and by affliction. If a thing happens, I want it on tape. The tape doesn't have to mean anything. It just has to exist.

For two weeks it recorded nothing but the radiator ticking and me breathing like a woman being paid by the breath.

Then, on the fourteenth night, it recorded a conversation.

* * *

I found it at nine in the morning, sitting cross-legged on my kitchen floor with the recorder between my knees like a Ouija board you operate alone. I'd developed a ritual by then: come home from the station, make coffee I wouldn't drink, pull up the obituaries for the people who had called the night before, confirm what I already knew, transcribe the call into the log, close the laptop, sit very still until the stillness stopped feeling like screaming.

The log lived in a composition book now. Forty-one entries. Forty-one callers who described their own deaths with the calm of people reading their own weather reports. Forty-one obituaries that matched. I told myself I kept hosting because the show was contractual obligation, because the station needed someone overnight, because quitting mid-sweep would look bad on a resume. All true things. None of them the true thing. The true thing was that if I stopped taking the calls, I'd have to decide what they were, and I wasn't ready to decide anything except to keep writing them down.

So there I sat, playing back fourteen nights of my own unconscious, expecting radiator noise, and instead I heard this:

**TRANSCRIPT — NIGHT 14 — 3:12 A.M.**

> **WENDELL:** How many are left?
>
> **VOICE:** Nine. After tonight, eight.
>
> **WENDELL:** And then quorum.
>
> **VOICE:** And then the signing.

My own voice sounded the way it always does on playback — flatter, smaller, slightly embarrassed to exist. But I knew the cadence. It was my interview cadence, the one I use when I'm drawing a caller out, patient, unhurried, a little warm. I was asking follow-up questions. In my sleep. To somebody.

And the somebody answered in a voice I didn't know. A woman's voice, low and dry, with a flatness to it that wasn't boredom. It was the flatness of a recording of a person rather than a person. Like the voice had been played so many times the edges had worn smooth.

I sat on the kitchen floor and listened to myself conduct an interview I had no memory of conducting, and the professional in me — God help me, the professional in me took notes.

* * *

**TRANSCRIPT — NIGHT 14 (cont.) — 3:19 A.M.**

> **WENDELL:** Say your name again.
>
> **VOICE:** Ada Voss.
>
> **WENDELL:** And when did you die?
>
> **VOICE:** March the ninth, 1974. In the house. They wrote pneumonia on the paper but the house had been cold for years before that. Cold is what they put when they don't want to write the truth.
>
> **WENDELL:** What truth?
>
> **VOICE:** That the house keeps a ledger, Wendell. Everyone who ever slept under its roof is written in it. Living and dead both. And every so often — not often, we're patient — the last living name gets asked to sign over the rest.
>
> **WENDELL:** Sign over to whom?
>
> **VOICE:** To us. To the ones waiting for a warm room. You know how it is on the air. You give the dead a line and they'll talk all night. We only want what your callers want. A little time inside a living thing.

There was a long pause on the tape after that. Long enough that I checked the counter twice. Ninety-one seconds of nothing but the radiator and two women breathing — or one woman breathing and one woman doing something adjacent to it.

Then my sleeping self said, cheerfully, horribly, in the tone I use to throw to break:

> **WENDELL:** We'll be right back after these messages.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I felt, because what I felt was shameful. I was not primarily afraid. I was *interested*. Somewhere under the dread, the host in me heard a guest with a story nobody else could get and thought: exclusive.

That's the complicity, I think. That's where it starts. Not with signing anything. With wanting the interview.

I played the tape through lunch. I played it while the afternoon sun moved across the kitchen floor and made the whole thing feel less possible, which is the trick daylight plays on us — it makes the impossible merely embarrassing. By dusk I had transcribed six nights' worth of conversations. My sleeping self had been busy. We had been busy. There was no "we" yet, not honestly, but the pronoun kept arriving anyway, uninvited, like a caller on hold.

What we had established, Ada Voss and I, across fourteen nights of my unconscious hospitality:

That she died in 1974 in a house on Corve Street, number 11, a house I had driven past maybe a hundred times without once seeing it, which she assured me was deliberate on the house's part. That the ledger held two hundred and six names as of the first of November. That forty-one of those names had called my show. She said it without accusation, but I felt accused anyway — my callers were her congregation, or her evidence, or her choir, depending on how she framed it that night, and she framed it differently each night, testing which version would move me.

And that the count was running toward something. Toward a night when enough names would be present — present, she said, meaning *accounted for*, meaning *listening* — and the last living signatory would be asked to pick up the pen.

"Who's the last living signatory?" I asked her out loud that evening, sitting on my kitchen floor in the dark, addressing a cassette. "Ask her tomorrow," the tape said, in my own voice, from three nights prior. "She'll tell you. She likes being asked."

* * *

**TRANSCRIPT — NIGHT 15 — 2:47 A.M.**

> **WENDELL:** Who's the last living signatory?
>
> **ADA:** You ask that so politely. Like I'll be offended either way.
>
> **WENDELL:** Who is it?
>
> **ADA:** You were born Wendell Anne Quintana. That's the name on your birth certificate, the one your mother signed in the county hospital in April of '81. But before April of '81 there was another paper. A private adoption, arranged through a family attorney, filed under seal. The family name on that paper was Voss.
>
> **WENDELL:** That's not funny.
>
> **ADA:** No. It isn't. It's arithmetic. Two hundred and six names, and two hundred and five of them are finished. You're the ink that hasn't dried, sweetheart. You're the last warm room in the building.
>
> **WENDELL:** I don't believe you.
>
> **ADA:** You will. You document everything. Go check the seal. Court records, probate division, file 74-V-118. Bring your little recorder. I'll wait. I'm good at waiting.

Then my sleeping voice, and this is the part I have played eleven times now, the part that lives behind my sternum like a swallowed stone:

> **WENDELL:** What do you want from me?
>
> **ADA:** A warm room. That's all. Any body will do, but yours is already half mine — you've been letting me answer your questions for two weeks, haven't you? That's a kind of tenancy. And in exchange I'll give you everything. Every name in the ledger. Every death before it happens. Your callers, Wendell — you could warn them. You could save them. Think of the show you'd do.

And my sleeping self — my traitorous, professional, insomniac self — said:

> **WENDELL:** Keep talking.

* * *

I went to the courthouse on Monday. I'm telling you this out of order because telling it in order would require me to explain why I waited two days, and the explanation is that I spent those two days doing the show, taking the calls, logging the dead, and asking each of them, casually, off-script, whether the name Voss meant anything to them. Eleven of them said yes. One of them hummed. One of them said, in a child's voice, *she's the one who waits by the door*, and hung up, and the line stayed open for a full minute afterward with a sound like a house settling.

File 74-V-118 existed. Of course it existed. The clerk retrieved it with the bored efficiency of a man who hands out sealed fates hourly, and I read it standing at the counter because sitting down had stopped feeling safe.

Petition for private adoption. Petitioner: Edith Voss, widow, of 11 Corve Street. Child: female infant, born April 1981. The signature block at the bottom held two names. Edith Voss, deceased 1991. And beneath it, in the space reserved for the adopted party upon reaching majority — a consent line, a transfer-of-name line, a line that read, in language older than the form itself, *the undersigned acknowledges residence in perpetuity.*

The line was blank. Waiting for me. It had been waiting thirty years, and the paper hadn't yellowed. That was the detail that undid me. Everything around it aged; the line didn't. Paper knows what it's holding.

I photocopied it. I documented it. I stood at the copier watching page after page emerge and understood, with the clarity that only arrives too late to be useful, that documenting a trap is not the same as escaping one. The log, the tapes, the transcript, the copy — I was building a case for a court that didn't exist, against a defendant who was already inside my sleep.

That night I set the recorder on the nightstand, pressed record, and lay down facing it, awake, determined to catch the handoff between waking and whatever came after.

I lost. Whatever comes after won. But the tape caught the moment of surrender, and on it, my voice — my waking voice, fraying at the edges — asks one more question before going under:

> **WENDELL:** If I get the others out — the living ones, the Vosses still breathing — what happens to the papers then?

And Ada, patient as winter, answers:

> **ADA:** Then there's no quorum, and the signing dies on the vine. But you'd have to find them all, Wendell. Scattered birds, my family. And you'd have to do it before the count fills. Eight nights left. Seven, if you sleep badly.

A pause. The radiator ticks.

> **ADA:** Which you will. You always do, before a big broadcast.

Eight nights. I played it back and counted on my fingers like a child, like a woman doing arithmetic she already knew the sum of, and outside my window the city went on making its small nighttime noises, and somewhere across town a house on Corve Street held two hundred and six names and one empty line, and I lay in the dark rehearsing the next night's show — *callers, we have a special topic tonight: family reunions* — and understood that I had already said yes. Somewhere between the fourteenth night and this one, between *keep talking* and *who's the last living signatory*, I had already said yes.

The only question left on the tape, the last thing recorded before morning, was my own voice, barely audible, asking the dark:

> **WENDELL:** Ada. When you get your warm room. What happens to me?

And the answer, sweet as a dial tone:

> **ADA:** Oh, sweetheart. You'll still have your show.
