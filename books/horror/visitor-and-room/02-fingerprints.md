## 2. Fingerprints

The Crawshaw house smelled like every house I've ever worked: old carpet, lemon cleaner deployed too aggressively over something the owners didn't want to name, and underneath it all, that faint mineral tang of a basement that's been damp longer than anyone's been willing to look at it.

I've been doing this job since I was nineteen, which means I've been doing it long enough to know that ninety percent of pest control is psychology. The mice are easy. It's the people who are work. People want their problem to be either nothing at all or a catastrophe — anything but the boring middle ground where a professional pokes around for an hour and charges them two hundred dollars. Mrs. Crawshaw had called me twice in one week, and when I pulled up to the house on Delver Street — a solid brick two-story with a porch swing that hadn't swung since the Clinton administration — she was waiting on the steps like I was delivering a kidney.

"Mr. Thackeray. Thank God." She wrung her hands the way my grandmother wrings dish towels, which is to say thoroughly and without any actual hope of getting them dry. "I know how this sounds."

"Try me," I said. "I've heard everything. Raccoons in the walls, bees in the attic, a man in Kettle Creek who swore a woodpecker was mocking him personally."

"It's not animals," she said. "It's *prints*."

"Prints are animals. Prints are exactly animals."

"Not these."

She led me inside, and I'll give her this: she didn't hover. She pointed me down the hallway toward the back bedroom, told me her husband was at his brother's because he refused to discuss it anymore, and then went to stand in the kitchen like a woman waiting for a verdict.

The hallway was narrow, papered in a floral pattern that had gone the color of weak tea, and the light came in sideways through lace curtains, doing that thing afternoon light does where it makes ordinary dust look deliberate. And there, on the wall, about chest height, were the fingerprints.

I want to be precise here, because precision is my whole religion. Grease prints. Human-shaped, five fingers each, pressed into the paint the way a child presses into fresh frosting — not smears, not drags, but *prints*, placed one after another up the wall and along it, a trail of them spaced at intervals like someone walking on their hands. They were dark against the wallpaper, brownish, waxy. Candle soot and skin oil, is what I thought first. Kids playing with matches, then touching the walls. Happens all the time.

Except these were on the *inside* of the exterior wall too. Mrs. Crawshaw showed me the bedroom, where the same trail emerged from behind a heavy oak dresser — a dresser she and her husband had moved themselves, she said, three weeks ago, and there had been nothing on the wall then but paint. The dresser sat flush against the outside wall. Behind it, sealed plaster, no vents, no gaps, no access of any kind. And on that sealed plaster: handprints. Dozens. Coming out of the wall the way footprints come out of a doorway.

"Rats can't do that," she said.

"No," I agreed. "Rats cannot do that."

"Squirrels?"

"No."

"Because Harold says raccoons—"

"Mrs. Crawshaw." I crouched by the dresser and looked at the prints properly, close up, the way you look at anything you're trying not to be afraid of. Five fingers. Thumbs. The proportions were right for a human adult, maybe slightly long in the fingers, the way hands look wrong in dreams. No knuckle creases showing in the print itself — just smooth pads of grease, as if whoever made them had been wearing gloves of rendered fat. "Harold's raccoon theory requires a raccoon with hands, opinions, and a grudge. What it doesn't require is a raccoon that can walk through drywall."

"So what does it require?"

"That's the question I get paid to answer." I stood up, brushed off my knees, and gave her the smile I keep holstered for exactly these moments — confident, a little rueful, the smile of a man who has never once met a problem he couldn't trap, poison, or seal out with hardware cloth. "Let me do a full walkthrough. Alone, if you don't mind. Animals behave differently with an audience, and so do people."

She minded — I could see her minding — but she went back to the kitchen anyway, and I heard her put the kettle on, which struck me as the most optimistic act I'd witnessed all week.

* * *

Here's my creed, the one my father taught me and his father taught him, back when Thackeray & Son was just Thackeray and a truck that only started on Tuesdays: *everything has an explanation.* Not everything has a *nice* explanation. Not everything has an explanation you'd want to tell a client over dinner. But everything that happens in a house happens because something physical did something physical, and your job is to find the something and the something else until the story closes. Ghosts are drafty windows. Hauntings are carbon monoxide. The devil is almost always a possum.

My grandfather built the business on that creed and my father died still believing it, and I have repeated it to myself so many times it functions less like a belief and more like a heartbeat. You don't notice it until it skips.

So I walked the Crawshaw house the way I always do, counterclockwise from the front door, checking baseboards with my flashlight, running my thumb along window sills for grit, reading the house the way some men read stock tickers. Droppings: none. Gnaw marks: none. Grease trails along the usual rodent highways — along walls, behind appliances: none. For a house with an "infestation," it was remarkably uninfested. No evidence of anything living except the Crawshaws and a spider in the bathroom who had clearly given up on the place months ago.

But the prints kept coming.

They were in the hallway. They were in the bedroom, trailing out of the sealed wall. They were fainter in the hall bath, on the tile, where they'd been partially wiped away by somebody's feet — Mrs. Crawshaw's, presumably, before she stopped walking barefoot in her own house. They went up the staircase in a neat diagonal, right side only, like a person ascending with one hand on the rail. At the top, they turned into the master bedroom and ended at the closet.

I opened the closet. Coats, boxes, a vacuum cleaner bag that needed changing, and on the back wall of the closet — which backed onto the chimney chase, which was itself sealed, which was itself impossible — more prints. These were different. The others had been spaced out, exploratory, a surveyor's pace. These were clustered, overlapping, dozens of impressions stacked on top of each other in the same spot, as if something had stood there pressing its palm to the wall over and over and over again.

Like knocking. Like knocking with the flat of its hand, patiently, for a very long time.

I stood in that closet with my flashlight beam on those prints and felt the first cold thread go down my spine, and I want to be honest about what I did next, because it matters later: I explained it. Out loud, to nobody. "Condensation and soot," I said. "Chimney seepage. Somebody leaned against the wall with greasy hands." My voice sounded fine. Professional. The creed held. The heartbeat didn't skip.

Then I heard the sound from downstairs.

Not a noise, exactly. A *motion*. The particular creak-and-settle of a floorboard taking weight — the third stair from the bottom, the one that announces visitors whether you want it to or not. I'd catalogued it on my way up; pest controllers memorize houses the way burglars wish they could. Third stair: creak. Someone had just stepped on it.

"Mrs. Crawshaw?" I called down. "You expecting company?"

The kettle whistled in the kitchen, thin and domestic, and under it I heard her voice, muffled, answering someone — not words, just the shape of conversation, pleasant, unhurried. Then the front door opened and closed, and a car engine started in the drive, and I went to the landing window in time to see a gray sedan pull away with Mrs. Crawshaw in the passenger seat, waving at the driver, a man in a suit I couldn't see clearly through the lace.

Which was strange, because I hadn't heard a car arrive. And stranger, because when I got downstairs — taking the stairs fast, flashlight up, adrenaline doing its dumb animal thing — the kitchen was empty, the kettle screaming on a stove that had been switched to high, and two teacups sat on the counter. One full. One empty, washed, and drying upside down on the rack, already spotless.

I turned off the stove. I stood in that kitchen for a good minute, listening to the house settle around me, telling myself the obvious explanations: neighbor gave her a lift. Friend called round. Harold came back early. All plausible. All fine. Except that Mrs. Crawshaw had told me Harold was at his brother's and refusing to discuss the matter, and except that the empty cup was dry, and except —

Except the prints.

There was a new one on the kitchen doorframe. Chest height. Fresh enough that the grease still shone.

And I knew — I knew with the certainty you feel in your sternum before your brain gets a vote — that it hadn't been there when I walked through that doorframe twenty minutes ago. I'd checked that doorframe. Checking doorframes is literally my profession. There had been nothing on it but forty years of paint.

I touched it. Waxy, cool, faintly sweet, like candle smoke and lanolin. My fingertip came away dark.

* * *

The cellar door was at the end of the back hallway, behind the laundry, and I found it the way you find things in houses that don't want to be found: by walking into the smell. Cold air, wet stone, and that mineral tang I'd noticed at the front step, concentrated now, flowing along the floor like water finding its level. The door itself was painted the same white as the walls, unremarkable, except that it was set into a wall that had no business containing a door. I'd walked past that stretch of hallway twice. It had been continuous paneling. I would swear it on my grandfather's grave, which is a figure of speech, since my grandfather is alive and would disown me for swearing at all.

I knocked. Solid. Old wood, older hinges.

"Mrs. Crawshaw?" I called, though I knew she wasn't home. The word came back to me off the door with a faint delay, like the space behind it was bigger than the space in front of it should allow.

I tried the handle. Locked. I got out my tools — because a locked door in a client's house is a locked door, and I have opened a hundred of them for rats and squirrels and one memorable honeybee colony — and while I worked the pick, I did what I always do: I talked.

"Routine inspection," I said to the door, to the house, to whoever kept books on such things. "Nothing personal."

The lock was old but simple, and it gave in under a minute, and I stood there with my hand on the knob and did not open it. That's the part I keep returning to, afterward. I had the door. I had the light. I had two hundred dollars' worth of professional obligation sitting in my palm, and I stood in that hallway for a long moment feeling the cold air push gently against my face like breath, and every instinct I had — not the trained ones, the *old* ones, the ones from when I was six years old and certain there was something in Grandma Ruth's back room, something patient, something that waited for the lights to go out — every one of those instincts said *don't.*

I'm a third-generation pest controller. I don't listen to instincts. I listen to evidence.

I opened the door.

Stairs went down into blackness, stone treads worn concave, and the cold rolled up over me like something uncrossing its arms. My flashlight found the walls: stone, old, mortared by hand. And on those walls, descending into the dark at exactly my shoulder height, matching my descent step for step —

Handprints. Hundreds of them. Going down.

Going down, and — I made myself look, made myself follow the beam all the way to where the stairs curved out of sight — none coming back up.

I stood at the top of those stairs for what felt like a year and was probably eleven seconds. Then I did the most professional thing available to me: I closed the door, locked it, wiped my prints off the knob with my sleeve, and wrote in my report — later, in the truck, with shaking hands I blamed on the coffee — *"Structural anomaly, rear hallway. Recommend contractor evaluation. No evidence of pest activity."*

No evidence of pest activity. Technically true. Whatever lived in that house, it wasn't a pest. Pests are afraid of you.

I was halfway down Delver Street before I made myself admit the other thing, the thing that had been scratching at the back of my skull since the kitchen. The fresh print on the doorframe. The washed teacup. The car that arrived without arriving.

Those prints hadn't been made by something hiding in the walls.

They'd been made by something that had been standing in the rooms with me. Watching me work. Following me, politely, at a respectful distance, the whole time I was explaining everything to myself in a calm professional voice.

And the last thing I saw in my rearview mirror, before Delver Street bent the house out of sight, was the upstairs window — the closet window, the one backing the chimney chase — where a shape stood with one hand raised against the glass. Not waving. Pressed flat. The way you press your hand to a window when you're the one inside, and it's everyone else who's outside.

That night I drove to Grandma Ruth's for Sunday dinner a day early, because I didn't want to be alone in my apartment, and I told myself it was because her pot roast was better than mine.

Her album was already open on the coffee table when I got there. She was going through it the way she always does, narrating, laughing at Dad's haircut in '84. And I looked down at a photograph taken in 1978 — Ruth and Grandpa on the porch of the old farm, young, squinting into the sun — and against the wall behind them, plain as anything, stood a wooden chair.

A chair that has never been in any photograph my family ever took.

"There's a chair in this picture," I said.

Ruth glanced down and smiled, warm and easy, and said the sentence that has not left me since:

"Of course there is, sweetheart. It's always been there."
