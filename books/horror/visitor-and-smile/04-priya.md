## 4. Priya

The letter had come in a cream envelope with no return address, and Priya Chandra had read it eleven times before she let herself believe it.

*Dear Ms. Chandra, we are pleased to inform you that you have been named sole beneficiary of the estate known as Hollowmere House, per the wishes of an anonymous donor.*

She'd looked up *beneficiary of an anonymous donor* on three different legal advice forums, and all three had told her the same thing: this is either a scam or a trap, and either way, consult a solicitor before you sign anything.

She signed everything.

Because she was twenty-six years old, four months behind on a flat in Croydon she shared with a mold problem and a landlord who texted at 2 a.m., and because when you have nothing, a free house doesn't look like a trap. It looks like a door. She hadn't had a door of her own since she was nineteen, since her mum's last year ate through their savings like water through plaster, since the hospice bills and then the funeral bills and then the long gray arithmetic of being alone with a degree in art history and no market for it.

Hollowmere House sat at the end of a lane that the council had stopped maintaining sometime in the nineties, behind a gate that groaned like something waking up. It was three stories of gray stone with too many windows, and every one of those windows had been dark the day she first saw it, which should have been a warning, but instead it looked to her like a face waiting to be smiled at.

The solicitor — a dry man named Pell who kept his office at a temperature that suggested he distrusted both heat and comfort — explained the terms twice.

"No conditions?" she asked.

"None."

"No requirement that I live there? Renovate it? Open it to the public?"

"None whatsoever." He slid the papers across. "Though I will note, off the record, that I've handled this estate's affairs for some years, and you are the first beneficiary it has ever produced."

"*It* has ever produced," she repeated.

"The estate," said Pell, in the tone of a man correcting nothing at all.

* * *

That was eight weeks ago. Now Priya stood in the front hall of Hollowmere with a paint scraper in one hand and a mug of tea going cold on the windowsill, and she had learned what the solicitor's careful phrasing had been hiding: the house cost money the way a sick relative costs money. Slowly at first, then all at once. The roof needed pointing. The boiler was a museum piece that ran on spite and kerosene. The wiring made the lights dim in unison whenever she ran the kettle, as if the house were flinching.

And she couldn't leave.

Not couldn't in the romantic sense. Couldn't in the arithmetic sense. The inheritance came with the house and only the house; there was no cash attached, no trust fund, no tidy annuity. Pell had been very clear about that. Whatever the anonymous benefactor had wanted, they had wanted her *here*, and they had arranged matters so that here was the only place she could afford to be. Selling would take months and lawyers and probably fail anyway — who buys a derelict pile at the end of a dead lane? Renting rooms meant making rooms rentable, which meant money she didn't have.

So she stayed, and she scraped paint, and she told herself — every morning, in the mirror in the downstairs loo, the small oval one with the silvering worn away at the edges like frost — *this is fine. This is a project. This is yours.*

Except lately, the mirror had started disagreeing about the timing.

* * *

It began, or she noticed it beginning, on a Tuesday. She was brushing her teeth in the loo, watching herself do it, and she thought: *I blinked and my reflection didn't.* Not quite. More like her reflection blinked and she hadn't yet, a half-beat out of phase, the way two clocks in the same room can fall into near-sync and drive you quietly mad.

She spat, rinsed, watched. Her reflection rinsed. A heartbeat late. Maybe less. Maybe her imagination.

She stood there until her legs got cold, staring, and the reflection stared back, and whatever gap there was closed again, or she stopped being able to see it, and she went upstairs and did not think about it for six days, which was its own kind of skill. She'd gotten good at not thinking about things. Grief had taught her that. You put the unspeakable in a box and you label the box something boring — *paperwork*, *the loft*, *later* — and you get on with scraping paint.

But the house kept handing her boxes.

The tall mirror in the drawing room, where her own reflection seemed to arrive after she did, like an echo with a limp. The wardrobe mirror in the master bedroom, which she'd covered with a dust sheet on principle and which somehow always had the sheet slipped halfway down by morning. And the nursery.

* * *

The nursery was on the second floor, north-facing, and it was the one room in Hollowmere Priya actively avoided, which was ridiculous, because it was also the one room she most needed to deal with. It had been sealed — actually sealed, wallpapered over the doorframe at some point in the house's past, so that finding it had meant following a draft and a smell of old milk and tapping along walls like a ghost-hunter in a documentary. Behind the paper: a door, and behind the door: a room where someone had once loved a child very much and then, judging by the layer of dust like gray snow, stopped coming in all at once.

A cot. A rocking chair with a cushion gone flat as a pressed flower. A mobile of wooden birds hanging motionless over the cot even though the window was cracked open enough to move the curtains. And on the wall opposite the window, a mirror. Big, round, framed in white-painted wood with little carved stars around the rim. A nursery mirror. The kind a parent holds a baby up to.

Priya had uncovered it her first week, wiped the dust off with her sleeve, and immediately wished she hadn't, because the room felt *watched* afterward. Not menacing. Attentive. Like a dog that had been kicked but still hoped.

Now she stood in the doorway with the pest controller, because Vesper Eastwick had come back.

* * *

Vesper Eastwick was not what Priya had expected from a pest controller, though she couldn't have said what she had expected. Something more weathered, maybe. But the woman who'd shown up that morning in a van with EASTWICK PEST SOLUTIONS stenciled on the side was maybe thirty, sharp-eyed, with the kind of posture that suggested she expected to be underpaid and was ready to prove it. She'd walked the ground floor with a torch and a clipboard, found claw marks above the kitchen doorframe that she'd clearly seen before — "your builder friend's right, these are old, whatever made them is gone or hibernating" — and taken samples of dust with the brisk tenderness of someone who respected evidence more than people.

Then Priya had said the thing she'd sworn not to say.

"And the mirrors lag."

Vesper had stopped writing. Looked up. "Sorry?"

"The mirrors. They're... delayed. My reflection comes late. It's worse upstairs. It's worst in the nursery." She heard her own voice doing the thing it did when she was frightened — going light, reasonable, almost cheerful, a voice designed to make other people comfortable so they wouldn't look too closely. "Which sounds insane. I know it sounds insane. Old glass does weird things, probably. Distortion. But I thought — you deal with the weird stuff, don't you? You're the weird-stuff person."

"I'm the rats-and-wasps person," Vesper said. But she capped her pen. "Show me."

So now they climbed the stairs together, and Priya talked, because talking was how she kept the fear at arm's length, and she could hear herself doing it and couldn't stop.

"—inherited it, which nobody believes, and honestly I barely believe it myself, some anonymous benefactor, the solicitor wouldn't say who, wouldn't say why me, and I know how that sounds—"

"How does it sound?"

"Like a trap." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, too thin. "But you don't turn down a house when you've got four hundred pounds to your name and a landlord who changes the locks for fun. You just — you take the door that opens. Even if—" She stopped herself. *Even if it's a trap* was not a sentence you finished out loud in a house like this.

"Even if," Vesper said, noncommittal, and Priya glanced at her and caught something flicker across the woman's face — recognition, maybe. Or its cousin.

At the nursery door, Priya hesitated. The wallpaper over the old frame was torn where she'd ripped it away weeks ago, and the door stood open onto dimness and dust and that faint sour-sweet smell, like milk remembered rather than smelled.

"It's the worst in here," she said. "Watch."

She stepped inside, crossed to the round mirror with the carved stars, and lifted her hand. Wave one. Waited. Her reflection waved back — a beat behind, unmistakable now that she knew to look, the way a dubbed film is unmistakable, mouths not quite matching words. Two seconds, maybe three. When she'd first noticed it, it had been a blink. It was getting worse. She hadn't told anyone that part, mostly because there was no one to tell, and partly because saying *it's getting worse* makes a thing real in a way noticing it doesn't.

Behind her, Vesper had gone very still.

"That's not glass," Vesper said quietly. "Glass distorts. Glass doesn't *wait*."

"You believe me." Priya turned. She hadn't realized, until the words were out, how much she'd needed someone to believe her — how heavy the last six days of not-telling had been, the solo gaslighting of watching your own face arrive late and deciding you were tired, or ill, or mad. "You actually believe me. Everyone else would say old house, tricks of the light, love, have you considered a holiday."

"I haven't decided what I believe," Vesper said. "I've decided what I'm looking at." She moved closer to the mirror, and Priya watched her watch it — watched her raise her own hand, hold it there, wait for the echo of it. Watched her jaw tighten.

"What is it?"

"Nothing." Too fast. Then, grudgingly, as if the honesty cost her money: "Mine's late too. Same delay. Which means it's not keyed to you. It's the mirrors. Or—" she glanced around the nursery, at the cot, the birds hanging dead-still in moving air, "—it's the room."

"The room," Priya repeated.

"Rooms remember things," Vesper said, and then looked almost embarrassed, as if the sentence had escaped custody. "That's not — I mean, structurally. Materials hold impressions. Temperature. That's all I meant."

But she didn't sound like she'd only meant that, and Priya, who had spent a year learning to hear what people didn't say while her mother died politely around her, heard it.

* * *

They ended up sitting on the nursery floor, because there was nowhere else to sit and because neither of them seemed willing to be the first to leave the room. Vesper had unpacked a thermos without being asked and poured tea into the lid, and handed it over, and Priya drank it even though it was the kind of strong brown brew that could strip varnish, because it was hot and someone had made it for her and that was rarer in her life than a haunted mirror.

"Why pest control?" Priya asked.

"Why not?"

"It's just — you talk like someone who went to university. You say *structurally*. You hold your torch like an instrument, not a tool."

Vesper snorted. "You hold a torch like a tool, you drop it down a wall cavity. Ask me how I know." She wrapped both hands around her own cup. "My dad did it. His dad did it. It's a family business the way a curse is a family business. You don't choose it, you just find yourself holding the torch." A pause. "What about you? Art history, yeah? Saw the books in the hall."

"Guilty."

"And you ended up owning a haunted nursery."

"Allegedly haunted. Allegedly a nursery. Allegedly mine." Priya pulled her knees up. Outside the cracked window, the light was going amber and long, and the wooden birds hung in it, motionless, patient. "You want to know the stupid part? I'm not even sure I want to fix it. The house, I mean. Not the — whatever the mirrors are. The house. Everyone who's seen it says strip it, gut it, modernize. But someone loved a child in this room. Someone hung those birds. Someone painted stars around a mirror so a baby would have something to look at." Her throat did something inconvenient. "If I gut it, it's like they never happened. And I know how that feels, being erased. My mum's whole last year, there's no record of it anywhere except in me. No photos near the end — she wouldn't allow them. It's like..." She gestured vaguely, helplessly, at the dust, the cot, the held breath of the room. "Like a year that happened to nobody."

When she looked up, Vesper was staring at her with an expression Priya couldn't read — something between alarm and grief, quickly filed away behind professionalism.

"Right," Vesper said, standing, brushing off her knees. "I'll need to come back. With proper equipment. Light meters, that kind of thing. There's a — supplier. Up north. Parts take time."

"You said that last time," Priya said. "About the claw marks. That you'd need parts."

"I need a lot of parts. It's a parts-heavy profession." Vesper was already packing her kit, movements quick, efficient, evasive. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame — the frame that had been wallpapered over, hidden, sealed — and said, without turning around:

"Don't cover the mirrors. Whatever you do. However bad it gets. Don't cover them, and don't break them, and don't be alone in this room after dark."

"Why not?"

Vesper's shoulders rose and fell once. "Health and safety," she said, and went down the stairs two at a time, and the front door closed behind her with a sound that was almost exactly like a held breath released.

* * *

Priya stood alone in the nursery as the light died.

She should go down. Make actual dinner instead of toast. Do the sensible things, the surviving things. Instead she crossed to the round mirror with the carved stars and stood in front of it, and raised her hand, and waited for her reflection to catch up.

One. Two. Three seconds now. She counted on her heartbeat, and it was three, and yesterday it had been two, and the day she'd first noticed it had been a blink.

Her reflection lowered its hand a full moment after she lowered hers. Then it did something else.

It tilted its head — slightly, gently, the way you look at a child asleep — and its eyes went past her, past the doorway, to the corner of the room where the rocking chair sat empty under the dust.

And in the glass, in the reflection of the chair, the cushion was dented. As if someone small had just been lifted out of it, and carried toward the mirror, and was looking back.

Priya did not scream. She had learned that much from her mother's dying year: you don't scream at the thing in the room, because the thing in the room is already sad enough.

"Hello?" she whispered.

Three floors below, the front door, which Vesper had latched behind her, unlatched itself with a soft click — like a house remembering it had once been polite — and somewhere beneath the floorboards, in a corridor that was not on any plan of the building, something that had been waiting fifty years for its name to be spoken began, very slowly, to climb the servants' stair.
