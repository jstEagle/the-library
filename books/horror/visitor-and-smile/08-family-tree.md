## 8. Family Tree

The county records office smelled like pencil shavings and other people's patience.

Vesper had been in a hundred buildings like it—municipal, underfunded, staffed by one woman at a desk who'd clearly been interrupted from a crossword and intended to make the interruption cost something. Vesper slid her pest control license across the counter like a passport.

"Property history," she said. "Hollowmere House, off the Marlowe Road. I need the ownership chain. Deeds, probate, anything on file from the seventies forward."

The woman looked at the license, then at Vesper, then at the license again, as if the connection between the two might explain itself.

"Pest control," the woman said.

"Woodworm. Structural survey. My client wants to know what she bought."

"What she bought is public record. It's what she *is* that costs twenty-five pence a page."

Vesper put a five-pound note on the counter and didn't smile. The woman took the note and pointed down a corridor that smelled of damp cardboard, and Vesper understood that she had just been dismissed by someone better at it than she was.

The archive room was a long table under fluorescent light, a rolling ladder against shelves of gray boxes, and a radiator that ticked in the corner with a rhythm Vesper chose not to think about. She pulled the box for the Hollowmere parish, sat down, and started at the back, because she'd learned a long time ago that if you want to know what's wrong with a building, you read its paperwork the way you'd read a body: injuries first.

She was three folders deep when she found the name.

Not Wren. She'd expected Wren—Priya had mentioned him, the previous owner, the one the solicitor's letters kept circling like a wasp around a jam jar. Aldous Wren, deceased, estate in trust, beneficiary unknown. Fine. Dead men don't lag mirrors.

The name she found was *Eastwick*.

Margaret Eastwick, née Wren. Witness.

Vesper sat very still. Somewhere down the corridor, the crossword woman laughed at something on her phone, a short bright sound that had no business in a records office.

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's maiden name, printed in a clerk's careful 1974 hand on a document Vesper hadn't read yet, in a file she hadn't known existed, in a town she'd driven into on a routine call about rats.

She read the document.

It was a deed of transfer, or something wearing a deed's clothes. The language was legal the way a scarecrow is a man—the shape of it, none of the substance. *In consideration of certain improvements to the temporal estate of the undersigned.* Vesper read it twice. Three times. The words didn't get better. They got worse, because on the third read she stopped trying to make them mean nothing and let them mean exactly what they said.

*The temporal estate.* Time. Treated like acreage. Bought, sold, improved.

Four names were listed below Aldous Wren's signature. Not witnesses. Not signatories. *Sources.*

Edith Marlowe, housemaid.

Samuel Okafor, groundskeeper.

Ruth Callow, cook.

Thomas Innis, chauffeur.

And beside each name, in the same clerk's hand, a figure. Not a sum of money. A duration. Three hundred and sixty-five days, allocated in portions—Vesper counted, because counting was the only thing keeping her hands steady—allocated in portions across the four names. Ninety-one days each. Ninety-one days each, with one day held back, *in reserve*, the document said, *against future need.*

A year. Taken from four people, a quarter each, and Aldous Wren had kept a day for himself like a man skimming the till.

Vesper's grandmother had witnessed this. Had stood in a room in 1974 with her father and a solicitor—or something solicitor-shaped—and watched him sign away a year of four servants' lives, and had put her own name on the paper to say: *I saw this. This happened. I agree.*

Vesper thought about her grandmother. Margaret Eastwick, who'd died when Vesper was nine, who'd smelled of lavender and cigarettes, who'd once grabbed Vesper's wrist hard enough to bruise when the girl had leaned too close to the hallway mirror at the old house, and said, *never look at yourself too long, love, glass remembers.*

Vesper had thought it was one of Gran's sayings. Gran had a hundred of them. *Never trust a door that opens too easily. Salt in the corners. Don't answer the second knock.*

They hadn't been sayings. They'd been instructions.

She put the document down and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes until she saw green static. The radiator ticked. Down the corridor, the phone rang and was ignored.

* * *

She requested the probate file next, and then the parish registers, and then, when the crossword woman had gone to lunch and the office was hers, she went through the boxes like a woman searching a burning house for one specific photograph.

Here was what the paper trail said:

Aldous Wren bought Hollowmere in 1961. He lived there with his wife, Constance, who died in 1969—quickly, the file said, though the death certificate Vesper found in a separate folder said something else entirely, a cause of death written in a hand that shook, *wasting, unexplained*, with a marginal note from a coroner who had clearly been told to mind his business: *no organic pathology evident.*

Here was what the paper trail said next: in 1974, Aldous Wren executed the document Vesper had already read. In 1975, his daughter Margaret Wren married a man named Eastwick—a man who appears in exactly one record, the marriage register, and then nowhere, no birth record that matched, no death record, as if he'd been assembled out of spare paper for the purpose of giving Margaret a new surname. In 1976, Margaret Eastwick left the county. In 1978, Aldous Wren was declared missing. The estate passed into trust. Nobody claimed it. Nobody claimed it for fifty years, until a letter arrived at a flat in Manchester belonging to a young woman named Priya Chandra, informing her that she had inherited a house she'd never heard of from a benefactor she'd never met.

Vesper sat back and looked at the ceiling, at the water stain shaped like a hand, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the first folder.

Aldous Wren had no other children on record. His wife was dead. His daughter had one child—a son, Vesper's father, dead ten years now, a man she preferred to remember as a phone number that stopped answering. And her father had one child.

One.

The line ran: Aldous Wren, to Margaret, to her father, to Vesper. Four generations, and at the end of it, one person. One heir. One set of hands for the debt to land in.

And Priya—Priya had inherited the house from an *unknown benefactor*. Vesper had assumed it was a solicitor's euphemism, some distant cousin of Wren's, some administrative ghost. But sitting in the damp cardboard smell with her grandmother's signature drying in front of her, Vesper understood that there was no benefactor. There was a mechanism. The house had needed an owner—had needed someone living inside it, someone whose days could be measured—and it had reached out and picked a woman who couldn't afford to leave.

The way it had reached into Vesper's family and picked her.

*No,* she thought. *Different. Priya's a stranger. I'm the blood.*

The thought landed in her stomach like swallowed glass, because she realized, sitting there, that she'd been relieved. For one whole second, reading Priya's name in the trust correspondence, some animal part of her had been *glad*—glad the house had someone else, someone uninvolved, someone the debt couldn't properly reach. Glad the bill might go to somebody else's account.

That was how it started. That was exactly how it started. A man in 1974 had looked at four servants and thought, *they're not really people the way I'm a person, their days are just sitting there, unimproved.* And a girl in a records office in the present had looked at a stranger and thought, *she's not really family the way I'm family.*

Vesper stood up so fast the chair went over backward. The sound cracked down the corridor. She didn't pick it up. She stood with her fists on the table and breathed, and hated—hated with a clean, clarifying heat—Aldous Wren, and the solicitor-shaped thing, and the clerk who'd written *sources* like it was a word that could be applied to human beings, and her grandmother most of all, because her grandmother had *known*. Had watched it happen, signed the paper, run, and then spent fifty years turning the knowledge into lavender-scented superstitions and pressing them into a child's hands like heirlooms, and had never once said, *the debt is coming for you, love, and here is what I did.*

*Glass remembers.* That was the apology. That was the whole confession. A warning with no explanation, passed down like a recipe.

Vesper picked the chair up. Set it straight. Sat back down, because there was one more thing to check, and she was a professional, and professionals finish the inspection before they burn the building down.

The servants. She wanted the servants. Edith Marlowe, Samuel Okafor, Ruth Callow, Thomas Innis—she wanted their files, their exits, their ends. If the document was nonsense, if it was some rich man's occult roleplay with a compliant clerk, then the four of them would have gone on to ordinary lives. Pensions. Gravestones. Grandchildren.

She found Edith Marlowe's file first.

Dismissed, March 1974. *Reason: misconduct.* No further detail. No forwarding address. Vesper turned the page and found a reference letter Aldous Wren had written—written, she noted, the same week as the dismissal—and it was the most chilling document in the entire box, because it was *kind*. *Edith has served this house with diligence and warmth. Her departure is a matter of household economy and no reflection on her character.* Warm. Generous. A man writing a good reference for a woman he was, by his own signature, robbing of ninety-one days of her life.

Vesper checked the others. Samuel Okafor, dismissed April 1974, *household reduction*. Ruth Callow, dismissed May 1974, no reason given. Thomas Innis, dismissed June 1974, *position eliminated*.

Four servants. Four months. Spring into summer, one dismissal a month, like a man drawing down an account in careful installments. And then in July, the document. The *improvement to the temporal estate*. The year, assembled.

None of them had pension records. None of them appeared in the parish register of deaths—not one, not in fifty years. Four people, employed in a house of that size for years, and the county had simply... lost them. The way you lose something you never really looked at.

Vesper thought of the handprints in the dust, the first day, the fingers too long, the prints facing inward. She'd catalogued them as evidence of an animal. Something nesting in the walls, something with a body she could trap or exclude or bill for.

Four somethings. Maybe. Four people with ninety-one days each, held somewhere, waiting.

And one day in reserve. Aldous Wren's day. *Against future need.*

* * *

She photocopied everything. The crossword woman came back from lunch and found the pest controller at the copier, feeding it page after page, feeding it twenty-pound notes, and had the good grace not to ask.

In the car, in the car park, with the copies in a folder on the passenger seat and the rain starting, Vesper didn't drive. She sat with both hands on the wheel and let the rage come, because she'd been holding it at arm's length since the folder marked *Eastwick* and it deserved to be looked at properly.

She was angry at Aldous Wren, but it was a distant anger, the anger you feel toward a headline. He was fifty years dead and probably deserved worse than her anger.

She was angry at her grandmother, and that was close and hot and specific. Margaret had known. Margaret had stood in that room. And Margaret's answer to complicity had been to run and marry a man made of paper and rename herself into a family that had never existed, and then to raise her son on silence, and to hand her granddaughter a pocketful of superstitions and a bruised wrist, and to die in 1993 leaving nothing behind but a mirror in the hallway that Vesper's father had sold within the week, no questions asked, because he'd inherited the silence along with the furniture.

The debt hadn't passed down like money. Money you can spend. It had passed down like a gene—dormant in one generation, expressed in the next, waiting for the right body. And Vesper's body was the last one available. The end of the line. The final signature on a document she'd never signed.

*That's not how it works,* she told the windshield. *You can't inherit a crime. There's no law—*

But there was a house. And the house had its own law, and the house had been very, very patient, and the house had watched her walk in with her spray cans and her disbelief and had thought, in whatever way a house thinks: *there she is. The witness's blood. She'll do.*

The rain thickened. Vesper's phone buzzed on the dashboard. Priya.

She almost didn't answer. She looked at the name on the screen and felt the animal part of her wake up again, the part that had been *glad*, the part that wanted to let the call ring out and drive back to the city and take the next three call-outs and never think about glass again. Priya Chandra was not her problem. Priya Chandra was a stranger the house had picked up the way a flood picks up a car.

Vesper answered.

"Hi." Priya's voice was careful, the way it had been careful since the mirrors. "You said you'd call when you had the parts. It's been four days. And—" a breath. "It's three seconds now. The lag. I timed it. I've been timing it. And Vesper, last night it wasn't just the mirror in the hall. It was the window. My reflection in the kitchen window was—" Another breath, and Vesper heard what was under it, what Priya had been carrying alone in that house for four days while Vesper hid in an archive. "It was smiling. Before I was."

Vesper looked at the folder on the passenger seat. Her grandmother's name on the top page, through the rain-blurred plastic.

"I found something," Vesper said. "I'm coming back. Don't go near the mirrors, and don't—Priya, if anything in that house asks you for anything, the answer is no. No matter how it asks. No matter who it sounds like."

A pause. "Who would it sound like?"

"Anyone," Vesper said, and started the engine. "It's had fifty years to practice."

She pulled out of the car park with the wipers going and the folder wedged against the gearstick, and it wasn't until the third junction that she admitted what she'd already known in the records office, under the fluorescent light, with the chair tipped over behind her: she hadn't gone there for the house's history.

She'd gone there for an out. A loophole. Some line in some deed that would prove the debt belonged to a dead man, or a trust, or the county, or anyone—*anyone*—whose name wasn't hers.

There was no out. There was only the line, running straight and unbroken from a room in 1974 to a car park in the rain, and at the end of it, her.

And one other thing—the thing she turned over as the Hollowmere Road narrowed and the hedges closed in, the thing that made her grip the wheel tighter than the weather required. The document had listed four *sources*. Four servants, four portions, one year.

But the house had shown her one handprint too many.

She'd counted them that first day, in the dust, before she'd known to count. Nine prints at ceiling height, small, inward-facing. She'd assumed overlap. She'd assumed the thing had walked the same wall twice.

Four servants. Nine prints.

Somebody else had been in the walls. Somebody the document didn't name. And Vesper was about to drive back to a house that had been keeping a fifth secret for fifty years, and her grandmother's signature was in the folder on the seat beside her, and the rain was coming down like the sky was trying to wash the road away before she could reach it.

The house came into view through the windshield, gray and patient, every dark window a mirror waiting for her.

Priya was standing at the front door. She'd been watching for the car. And as Vesper pulled up the drive, she saw Priya raise one hand—not a wave, Vesper realized. A warning. Pointing, back over her shoulder, into the hall behind her.

At the mirror.

Which was, even at this distance, even through the rain, even before Vesper stopped the engine—smiling.
