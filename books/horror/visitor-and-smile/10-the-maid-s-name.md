## 10. The Maid's Name

The county archive smelled of cold paper and other people's patience.

Priya had been there since nine, when the doors opened, and it was now past two, and the woman at the reference desk had stopped making eye contact with her somewhere around noon. Priya understood. She was the kind of visitor archivists dreaded: no academic affiliation, no clear research question, just a young woman in a wool coat asking for everything the county held on a house that most people pretended wasn't there.

"I'm not writing a book," she'd said, for the fourth time. "I live there."

That had gotten her a longer look than anything else she'd said all day. *I live there.* As if Hollowmere were a diagnosis.

Now she sat at a scarred oak table with a microfilm reader older than she was, scrolling through the *Ashcombe Vale Gazette* from 1974, page by yellowed page, looking for a name she couldn't yet describe to herself. She only knew what she was looking for the way you know someone is standing behind you — by the shape of the attention on your back.

The house wanted her to find this. That was the thought she kept circling and refusing to land on. Houses didn't want things. Houses settled. Houses developed damp and subsidence and dry rot, and you called a surveyor, and you paid too much, and life went on. Houses did not rearrange their staircases overnight. Houses did not have corridors that appeared between one Tuesday and the next, lined with mirrors that showed you figures waiting with the stillness of people who had learned that rushing never helped.

And houses certainly did not mouth your pest controller's name at you through glass.

*Vesper.*

Priya had told no one about the servants' stair. Not Vesper, though Vesper was the only person alive who might believe her — Vesper, who had gone grey around the mouth after the corridor incident and then lied about needing parts for equipment that clearly didn't exist. Not her sister in Manchester, who already thought moving into an inherited derelict house alone was a cry for help. Not anyone. Because saying it out loud would make it a thing that had happened, and Priya had become, over the past three months, a connoisseur of not-happened things.

But she had started researching anyway. That was the compromise her mind had struck with itself: she wouldn't say it aloud, but she would treat it as real in private, the way some people pray without believing in God, just in case the habit itself is the point.

So: the archive. So: 1974.

The Gazette was mostly harvest fairs and a scandal about the water rates. Aldous Wren appeared twice that year — once photographed at a charity dinner, silver-haired and handsome in the way of men who have never been told no, and once in a single line: *A. Wren of Hollowmere House announces the closure of the estate's domestic service, citing modernisation.* No names. No thanks. Fifty years of hands that had cooked and cleaned and carried, dismissed in eleven words.

Priya wrote down the date anyway. March 14th, 1974.

Something about the date itched. She sat with it for a while before she understood: it was the same week the lag had started growing faster. Vesper had said so, in her flat, defensive way — *it's worse this month, don't ask me why* — and Priya had checked her own notes, because she kept notes now, a spiral notebook hidden under a floorboard like a teenager's diary, and yes. The mirrors had slipped from two seconds to nearly three in the second week of March.

Anniversaries mattered here. She was starting to build a whole cosmology out of that single fact.

* * *

The parish records were kept in a side room that required the archivist to fetch a key shaped like something from a church door. Inside, the air was drier and colder, and the registers lay in acid-free boxes labelled by decade in faded copperplate.

Baptisms, marriages, burials. The three doors the old church opened for you, and nothing else. Priya had learned enough in the last hour to feel the violence of that — how a person could exist for decades inside a parish and leave behind only three marks, if they were lucky, and often fewer. The gentry got newspapers. The poor got ink in a ledger, if the vicar remembered.

She was looking for servants. Hollowmere's staff, 1970 to 1975. There wouldn't be a servant register at the parish level, but baptisms were different. If any of them had children in those years —

Her finger stopped halfway down a page of March 1974 baptisms.

**Marlowe, Edith Anne, mother. Father's name: withheld at mother's request.**

No father listed. A child baptised — she checked the date — on the 17th of March, 1974. Three days after Wren closed his household.

Priya read the entry four times. The ink was brown and certain. Somewhere in 1974, a woman named Edith Marlowe had stood in a church with a newborn baby and refused to give a name, and three days earlier her employer had dissolved his entire staff, and neither fact meant anything on its own, and together they meant everything.

"Excuse me," Priya said, and her voice came out strange, and the archivist looked up despite herself. "Is there a way to find out what happened to someone after this? A baptism record — is there anywhere else she'd appear?"

"That depends," the archivist said, "entirely on who she was."

"A servant." Priya heard herself say it and felt the word sit wrong in her mouth, too small for what it was carrying. "A housemaid, probably. At Hollowmere House."

The archivist took off her glasses. It was, Priya would think later, the gesture of a woman deciding whether to spend effort on a stranger, and something in Priya's face must have tipped the balance, because she came around the desk.

"Hollowmere," she said. "You're the one living there now, aren't you? The Chandra girl."

"You know the house?"

"Everyone my age knows the house." The archivist's name, according to her lanyard, was Mrs. Okafor, and she was perhaps seventy, and she looked at Priya with an expression that was not quite pity and not quite fear. "We used to dare each other to touch the gate. In the sixties, before Mr. Wren bought it. After, nobody dared at all." She paused. "What are you looking for, exactly?"

"A maid. Edith Marlowe. She had a baby in March 1974."

Mrs. Okafor went very still, in a way that reminded Priya — horribly, precisely — of the figures in the mirrored corridor. Stillness as a survival skill.

"Come and look at the electoral rolls," Mrs. Okafor said finally. "And then come and have tea, because you've got the look of someone who's going to faint at a filing cabinet."

* * *

Edith Marlowe existed in the records the way a footprint exists in snow: present, detailed, and vanishing.

The electoral roll of 1971 put her in the servants' wing at Hollowmere — a building Priya had walked past a hundred times without registering it as anything but a collapsed barn. The 1972 wage books, which Mrs. Okafor unearthed from a box of deposited estate papers Priya hadn't known existed, listed her as *E. Marlowe, housemaid, £9 per fortnight*, with a note in the margin in a clerk's hand: *recommended for under-housekeeper, 1973.*

Someone had believed in her. Someone had written it down. And then —

The 1974 roll: nothing. The wage books ended in February. The parish baptism: March. And after that, Edith Marlowe simply stopped appearing, as if the county had agreed, all at once, to look slightly to the left whenever her name came up.

"No death registration," Mrs. Okafor said, working through the indices with the efficiency of long practice. "No marriage. No emigration papers — those we'd see, the port records usually surface. She's just..." She made a small gesture, closing a book. "Gone."

"People disappear."

"Not from paperwork, dear. Paperwork is the one place people can't manage to disappear. Even the drowned get a coroner's line." Mrs. Okafor looked at her over the rims of her glasses. "Unless someone made sure there was nothing to find."

The tea room attached to the archive was a converted vestry with radiators that clanked like something pacing. Priya wrapped her hands around a cup she didn't drink and laid out what she had, because Mrs. Okafor had earned it: the mirrors, the lag, the corridor, the faces. She left out the part where the maid had mouthed Vesper's name, mostly because saying it would require explaining who Vesper was, and that explanation had a way of ending conversations.

To her credit, Mrs. Okafor didn't laugh. She stirred her tea for a long time.

"There was a story," she said at last. "My mother cleaned for the Hendersons up the lane, and the Hendersons' cook had a cousin at Hollowmere — this would be the early seventies. The story was that Wren was a man who collected things. Stamps, clocks, grudges. And that near the end he'd taken to collecting something else." She set the spoon down. "Time. That's the word that came down the lane, anyway. That he'd found a way to take time off people. Off the ones who couldn't refuse him."

"That's not —" Priya began, and stopped, because she had watched her own reflection arrive late to her own smile, and there was no sentence that started with *that's not* that survived contact with that memory.

"He dismissed the whole staff in March '74," Mrs. Okafor went on, as if Priya hadn't spoken. "All at once. Paid them off through a solicitor so none of them ever came back to the house. Except—" she frowned, reaching "—there was one they talked about. The one who didn't take the money. My mother said the girl came back to the gate three times, and the third time the police were called, and after that nobody spoke of her, because speaking of her made the Hendersons' cook cry."

"What was her name?"

"I never knew it. But she'd have been the one with the baby." Mrs. Okafor's eyes came up, sharp and old and sorry. "You said March '74."

"Baptised the seventeenth."

"Then she was dismissed within days of giving birth." Mrs. Okafor said it plainly, the way you'd state a weather condition, and somehow that plainness was the worst thing Priya had heard all year. "Pregnant, unmarried, in 1974, employed by a man like Wren. He'd have called it modernisation. He'd have written it down in his little book and felt tidy about it."

Priya's notebook was open on the table. She realised she'd written *Edith Marlowe* at the top of a fresh page and underlined it twice, and that her hand had pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

"She came back to the gate," Priya said. "Three times. Why would she come back?"

Mrs. Okafor looked at her for a moment with an expression that suggested the question answered itself.

"For her life, dear. Whatever he'd taken from her. People know when something's been taken, even when they can't name it. She'd have felt it like a missing tooth — the tongue keeps going back to the gap." She gathered her glasses and stood. "Fifty years, you said. Waiting fifty years in a house like that." At the door she paused, hand on the frame. "My mother always said the saddest ghosts aren't the angry ones. They're the ones nobody will admit to having known."

* * *

Priya drove back through a sky the colour of wet slate, and the closer Hollowmere came, the more the facts arranged themselves into a shape she didn't want to look at directly.

A maid, erased. A baby with no father on record. A dismissal timed to the week the house's sickness accelerated. An echo in a maid's uniform, standing in a corridor of mirrors, mouthing a name — and not Priya's name. Vesper's.

Why Vesper?

She pulled into the drive at dusk. The house rose against the last light with its windows lit by reflection only, every pane holding a copy of the burning sky, and Priya stood by her car in the gravel and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing for a week:

The house isn't haunting us. It's *asking* us. It has been asking, patiently, for fifty years, and it has run out of patience, and it has started pulling people in — first Vesper with her claw marks and her invoices, then me with my inheritance I never asked for — because it needs something done, and it cannot do it alone.

And the maid in the corridor, the one who mouthed Vesper's name — she hadn't looked menacing. Priya had been too frightened to parse it at the time, but now, replaying it, she saw it plainly: the figure's expression had been urgent. Pleading. The face of someone trying to pass a message through thick glass, knowing the words weren't getting through, mouthing harder.

Not *help me*. Priya was almost sure. Something more specific. Something that needed a particular person to hear it.

Vesper's car wasn't in the drive, but the mudroom light was on — she'd given Vesper a key weeks ago, in one of those practical gestures that had quietly become something else — and Priya let herself in through the kitchen, where the lag was worst, where the window above the sink held a reflection of the garden that arrived a beat after the garden did.

On the kitchen table, Vesper had left her instruments laid out in their foam case, and beside them, weighted down with a spanner, a sheet of paper torn from a legal pad. Vesper's handwriting, blocky and unapologetic:

*Found something in the attic. You need to see it before you sign anything or talk to anyone. Ledger. 1974. Names in it. One of them might be yours. Call me. — V*

*Might be yours.*

Priya stood in the cold kitchen holding the note, and outside the window her reflection stood holding it too, a half-second behind, and in the glass beyond her own delayed image — faint, at the edge of the pane, where the garden darkened into the lawn — a figure in a maid's uniform stood among the flowerbeds, perfectly still, watching the house with the posture of someone who has waited so long that waiting has become the whole of who they are.

Priya didn't scream. She was past screaming, and besides, the figure wasn't looking at her.

It was looking at the note in her hand. At the ledger. At the attic stairs.

And slowly — with the deliberate clarity of someone who has rehearsed this for half a century — it raised one hand and pointed upward, and its lips moved, and this time, through fifty years of glass and silence, Priya heard it as clearly as a bell:

*"Edith. My name is Edith Marlowe. Tell her I kept count."*
