## 22. Visitor and Smile

The knock came at nine in the morning, which was late by pest-control standards and early by Vesper Eastwick standards, because she had spent forty-one years believing that nothing worth catching moved before noon.

She was in the mudroom when it happened—her mudroom now, though the word still fit strangely in her mouth, like a shoe she hadn't broken in. She'd hung her tools on the pegboard the week before: the UV torch, the borescope, the traps she no longer needed for anything with more than four legs but kept anyway out of professional sentiment. Edith had reorganized them twice, alphabetically, then by frequency of use, then back to alphabetical, because fifty years of waiting on other people's households did not simply evaporate; it settled into the hands like flour.

"Someone's at the door," Edith called from the kitchen, where she was learning to use the kettle Priya had bought her—a proper electric one, not the stove-top relic Vesper had fished out of a charity shop. "It does that knocking thing. I remember knocking."

"People knock," Vesper said. "That's generally how it works."

"I know how it works." Edith appeared in the mudroom doorway, wiping her hands on an apron she'd sewn herself from fabric she'd picked out with the fierce deliberation of a woman making up for five decades of having no opinions honored. She was sixty-eight years old now, or looked it, having spent her returned year the way she'd chosen to spend it—growing older, growing realer, filling in like a photograph developing. Her hair had gone fully silver in the months since the mirrors let her through. She said it was worth it. She said it every time anyone asked, which was often, because everyone asked.

Vesper set down the screwdriver she'd been using to fix the cabinet hinge—the same cabinet, she noted, that had been loose since before Aldous Wren ever drew breath, some things being eternal—and went to answer the door.

Hollowmere House had changed in ways that were hard to explain to people who hadn't watched it happen. The paint was the same peeling gray. The gutters still sagged. But the light came through the windows differently now, arriving when it was supposed to, leaving when it was supposed to, and the front door—which had always had the particular heaviness of a door that didn't entirely want to open—swung free under her hand.

The woman on the step was maybe thirty, dressed in the particular armor of someone who had rehearsed this conversation in the car: sensible coat, folder clutched to her chest, the expression of a person about to say something ridiculous and bracing for it.

"Hi. Sorry. Is this—is this Hollowmere? I saw online there's a, um. A pest service here?" She held up the folder like credentials. "I'm Dana Okafor. I live over in Marlow Green, past the church? And I have a problem I don't—I know how this sounds."

"Mrs. Okafor," Vesper said, and stepped back, and opened the door wide. "Come in. You're going to want tea for this. Everyone does."

Dana blinked. "You haven't even heard what it is."

"You have something that lags," Vesper said. "Something that's running behind itself. A watch, probably. A clock, a phone camera, a mirror. Something small enough that you've been telling yourself it's your eyes."

The folder lowered an inch. "My grandfather's pocket watch," Dana whispered. "It started last Tuesday. It—it ticks after it should tick. I counted. Half a second. And this morning it was a full second, and I thought I was losing my mind, and my husband said take it to a jeweler, and the jeweler said—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He said the mechanism is fine. He said he could hear it ticking *late*. He laughed. He thought he was joking."

"They usually do," Vesper said. "The ones who laugh first are the ones who sleep best afterward. Come through."

* * *

The kitchen smelled of toast and the lemon polish Edith insisted on, and Priya was already at the table with her laptop, because Priya was always at the table with her laptop these days, building the archive—every servant, every name, every record the house had eaten. The project had a name now, *The Hollowmere Register*, and a modest grant, and a waiting list of families who had lost someone to a house like this one and never known why the loss felt so specifically shaped.

"This is Dana Okafor," Vesper said. "Marlow Green. Lagging watch. Second-generation, I'd guess—grandfather's piece means the attachment runs in the family."

"Sit down, love," Edith said, and poured the tea without asking, and Dana sat the way people sat in that kitchen now, which was to say all at once, like a marionette whose operator had finally been paid.

Priya looked up. "Marlow Green. Is your family from around here originally?"

"My gran grew up near Ashcombe. Why?"

"Because there were four houses doing this in the valley," Priya said gently. "We think. We've confirmed two. Yours might be the third." She turned the laptop around. On the screen was a scanned page, brown as old tea, covered in the cramped ledger-hand Vesper had come to know better than her own signature. "Does the name Wren mean anything to you?"

"No. Should it?"

"It shouldn't," Vesper said. "That's rather the point."

She explained it the way she'd learned to explain it—plainly, without apology, in the voice she'd once used for describing termite damage. There was a debt. It ran through objects and bloodlines and sometimes just through places. The lag was the sound of time leaking, time that someone had taken and never given back, and the taking left fingerprints, and the fingerprints eventually found their way to a door.

"And you can—you fix this?" Dana's hands were wrapped around her teacup like it was a railing over a drop. "The jeweler couldn't even—"

"The jeweler was looking at the wrong thing," Vesper said. "The watch isn't broken. The watch is *hungry*, or something near it is, and hunger like that doesn't get fixed with a screwdriver. It gets fed, or it gets answered, and those aren't the same thing." She paused. "I'll come out tomorrow. Bring the watch, don't wind it, don't let anyone else handle it, and if it starts ticking before it should instead of after—that's worse, and you call me immediately, day or night."

"Before it should," Dana repeated faintly.

"It happens. That's when it's not leaking anymore. That's when it's decided where it lives." Vesper smiled, and was aware, distantly, of how strange the smile must look on a face built for skepticism. "I know exactly how mad this sounds. I billed a woman three hundred quid for saying less than this, once, and slept fine. Then I met a house that taught me better."

Edith made a small sound—not quite a laugh. "She charged me nothing," Edith told Dana, "and gave me a year. Make of that what you will."

"What do you charge?" Dana asked, fumbling for her bag. "For—for whatever this is. I don't have much, but—"

"Nothing," Vesper said.

"That's not—there must be a fee schedule, there must be—"

"There's a fee," Vester admitted, and caught herself, and heard Edith's quiet huff of amusement at the slip, and pressed on. "There's a fee. It's just not money. Sit tight and I'll tell you what it costs, and you can decide if you want to pay it, because most people don't, and that's allowed. The fee is that you have to believe it. All the way down. No half measures, no 'well, it could be wiring.' Belief is the currency. Everything else is theater."

Dana stared into her tea. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious with relief. "Half a second," she said. "I timed it with two phones against each other. My husband filmed me doing it. He thinks I need a doctor." Her voice cracked. "I thought I needed a doctor."

"You need a cup refilled," Edith said, "and a good night's sleep, and us at your door tomorrow at ten. Drink your tea."

* * *

After Dana left—walking lighter than she'd arrived, clutching the dead-silent watch like a sleeping child—Vesper stood in the hall a while, looking at the coat hooks.

Her coat was on the third hook. Had been for weeks. Before Hollowmere, she had never hung a coat anywhere she wasn't actively working; coats lived in the van, van lived nowhere, nowhere was safe. She'd been invisible on purpose for twenty years—no lease longer than six months, no bank account with her middle name on it, no emergency contact until Priya had filled in the form herself, in pen, without asking, and Vesper had let it stand because correcting it would have meant admitting she'd read it.

"You're staring at the hooks again," Edith said, coming up beside her.

"I'm inventorying."

"You're marveling. It's all right to marvel. I do it daily." Edith touched the sleeve of Vesper's coat, straightening it a quarter inch, the gesture of a woman who had spent a lifetime keeping other people's houses and had finally been given one to keep people in. "Fifty years I waited in the glass, and do you know what I thought about? Not revenge. Not even freedom, really. I thought about doors. About who answered them. In 1974 nobody answered for me. Mr. Wren took my year and the vicar took my name off the register and my own family took the road south and there was no door anywhere in England that opened when I knocked." She patted the coat sleeve once, briskly, done. "So when I see you stand there and open yours wide for a frightened stranger with a haunted watch, I take a moment. Indulge me."

Vesper cleared her throat, which had gone unreliable. "The kettle's whistling."

"The kettle has been whistling for ten seconds. You're lagging." Edith's grin was quick and silver. "Kidding. Mostly. Go on, I'll see to it."

* * *

That evening Vesper walked the grounds, because she still walked the grounds, because some habits were load-bearing. The garden Priya had coaxed out of fifty years of neglect was going autumnal, the dahlias collapsing in their beautiful dying way, and the study window glowed warm where Edith had claimed the old Wren room for the Register's paper files—poetic justice, Priya called it; Edith called it *putting the furniture to honest work*.

Behind the study wall, where the clockwork device had once ticked its thefts, there was nothing now but plaster and pipe. Vesper had smashed it—or rather, she had offered her year and the device had shattered on its own, unable to survive being refused, and the shards had turned out to be ordinary brass and glass, and Priya had swept them up, and that was that. Some horrors ended not with a battle but with a broom.

Her phone buzzed. Priya: *Dana texted. Watch ticked ON TIME all afternoon. She says thank you. She also asks if we do weddings.*

Vesper snorted. *Tell her we do exorcisms, christenings, and moderately priced hauntings. Weddings are Edith's call.*

*Edith says yes.*

Of course she did.

Vesper put the phone away and stood a moment in the blue dark, listening. The house breathed around her—not the hungry, holding-breath silence of that first call-out, when she'd found claw marks at ceiling height and handprints facing inward and told herself *dust, settling, old glass, faulty wiring.* Just a house being a house. Pipes ticking on time. Wind finding the eaves it was supposed to find.

Somewhere inside, a mirror reflected her, instantly, faithfully, a second behind nothing.

She thought about the woman she'd been eleven months ago, driving out here with a van full of poisons and a heart full of arithmetic, billing the world for every hour and trusting none of them. She'd believed in nothing she couldn't spray, trap, or invoice, and the belief had kept her alive the way a locked room keeps you alive—perfectly, and alone.

Now there was a lease with her name on it, signed in ink, witnessed. Now there was an emergency contact. Now there was a woman in the kitchen who had waited fifty years to teach someone how to make bread properly, and a woman at the table who archived griefs for a living and laughed at Vesper's jokes even the bad ones, especially the bad ones. Now there was a door, and it was hers, and it opened.

Tomorrow, Marlow Green. Tomorrow, a grandfather's watch and whatever had fastened itself to it, hungry and patient and mistaken if it thought this household wouldn't notice. Tomorrow, another family learning the shape of what they'd inherited.

Tonight, dinner.

Vesper Eastwick turned from the dark, climbed the steps, and opened the door wide—smiling, believing, answering—and the light inside reached out to meet her, right on time.
