## 4. Miss Ashdown

She did not come down to him. That was the first thing Ivo noticed, and the first thing he noted against her, unfairly or not: a client of ninety-one who made the surveyor climb to her.

Nell led him up the back stair — the one that groaned in thirds, he thought, a chord of complaint — past a window whose glass had gone so old it poured the light in slow, greenish waves, as though the morning itself were underwater. The landing smelled of lavender and, beneath it, something colder. Stone. Or the memory of stone.

"She's had her breakfast," Nell said, stopping at the door. Her hand rested on the knob without turning it. "She's better in the mornings. Sharper. By evening she — " A small hesitation, a recalibration. "She gets tired. Don't let her keep you past four. And don't —" Nell looked at him then, directly, and Ivo had the uncomfortable sense of being measured by someone a decade his junior and twice as certain. "Don't tell her about the well."

"The well is part of the property, Miss Marsh. It'll be in the report regardless of what I tell her."

"I know. Just — not today."

She knocked, once, and opened the door without waiting, which told Ivo something about the household's architecture of habit.

The room was large and almost empty. A bed with an iron frame, a washstand, a wardrobe with one door hanging slightly ajar as if it had been interrupted mid-thought. And before the long window, a wheelchair — no, a chair, ordinary, high-backed — and in it Miss Verity Ashdown, upright as a plumb line, hands folded on a blanket the color of weak tea.

She was smaller than he'd expected. That was the trick of very old people, he supposed: the world wore them down the way water wore a stair, patiently, and you arrived expecting a ruin and found instead something worn smooth and hard as river stone. Her face was all planes and decision. Her eyes were pale grey, nearly the color of the light, and they went to him the moment he crossed the threshold — not to Nell, to him — and they did not blink.

"You're the third," she said.

"Miss Ashdown. Ivo Okonkwo, Halloran and —"

"I know who you are. I know who sent you. You're the third." She said it the way a doctor says *you're the third opinion*, with neither hope nor despair, only arithmetic. "Sit down, Mr. Okonkwo. The chair by the window. Mind the loose castor; it's been loose since 1974 and I see no reason to fix what only complains."

The chair did complain, a small brass whine under his weight. He opened his leather folio on his knee — the gesture was professional liturgy, the opening of the book, the click of the pen — and looked up to find her watching his hands with an expression he could not place. Not disapproval. Recognition, maybe. As if she had watched a hundred men open a hundred folios in this room, and knew exactly how the story went.

"You'll want to ask me questions," she said. "Ask them. But first I'll ask you two, and then we'll know whether this morning is worth either of our time."

Ivo kept his pen poised. "I'm at your disposal."

"Can you carry a tune?"

The question arrived with no context, no apology, the way a stone arrives in a window. He heard Nell, still by the door, go very slightly still — he couldn't have said how he knew; there was no sound to it, just a change in the room's attention.

"I'm afraid I have no ear at all," Ivo said. "I'm told I hum when I work. Badly. My colleagues have complained."

Something in the old woman's face eased — a fraction, a millimetre of tension leaving the muscle around her mouth. "Badly is fine," she said. "Badly is safe." And then, before he could ask what that meant: "Second question. Have you ever been mourned?"

He laughed. He was aware, even as it left him, that the laugh was the wrong instrument — too quick, too bright, a man's laugh deployed to fill a hole where an answer should be. "I'm thirty-four, Miss Ashdown. I hope to be mourned eventually, but the market's thin so far."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he admitted. The laugh had died in his throat and left something rawer behind. "No, I don't suppose it is." He considered the question properly for the first time, turning it over the way he'd turn a title deed, looking for the clause underneath. His father had died when he was nine; there had been a funeral, well attended, his mother weeping beautifully in a black hat. But mourned — *him*, Ivo, the specific boy who bit his nails and memorised bus timetables for pleasure — he had never had the sense that anyone had stood at a graveside grieving *that*. They had grieved a son, a husband's child, a shape. "I've been buried," he said at last, surprising himself. "I'm not sure I've been mourned."

Verity Ashdown nodded, slowly, the way one nods at a figure that finally totals. "That will do," she said. "Nell, you can go. He'll be safe till four."

"He'd better be," Nell said, and left. The door did not quite close behind her. Ivo noted it — the gap, an inch of dark hallway visible — and noted his own noting of it, and was irritated by both.

* * *

For an hour they worked, and it was, Ivo had to concede, the most productive client interview of his career. Verity Ashdown's mind was a ledger. She gave him dates without hesitation: the slate roof re-laid in 1962, the east chimney taken down in the war — "they said it might fall on a child; it never fell on anything" — the damp course, the rewiring, the year the kitchen floor was flagged over the old well-mouth ("1948. My husband insisted on the iron cap. He was a sensible man. It was the only sensible thing he ever did in this house, and he did it too late, or not in the right spirit, or something. I've never known which.").

She dictated. He transcribed. Her voice was dry and even, a metronome, and Ivo felt his professional self settle into it like a foot into a familiar boot. This was the work as he loved it: facts, dates, the world agreeing to be counted.

It was she who broke the rhythm.

"You'll find the rooms won't sum," she said, without looking at him. She was watching the garden through the window, where the white flowers — the ones he could not name, the ones that seemed to grow in every bed in Marrow's End — moved in a wind he could not feel from indoors.

"I'm sorry?"

"The rooms. When you measure them. They won't add up. They never do. You'll be a foot short, every floor, every time. Mr. Pell in 1979 measured for three days and got the same answer and drank himself stupid over it in the Fox and Whistle. The one before him — the one who cried — he tried to make it come right by shaving a little off each room. Clever. It didn't work. The house took the difference out of somewhere else." She turned her pale eyes on him. "Don't shave the rooms, Mr. Okonkwo. Write down what they are. Write down that they don't sum. Honesty is the whole of your employment here."

Ivo kept his pen still and his voice level. "You're telling me the house has a discrepancy of approximately one foot."

"I'm telling you the house is a foot short," she said. "There's a difference, and you'll understand it before you leave, though I hope not too well." She folded her hands again. "I've had three surveyors, Mr. Okonkwo, and each of them wanted to finish. That's the trouble with your profession — you're all in love with the total. The sum at the bottom of the page. The neat little figure that says the world has agreed to be counted. I will sign nothing — nothing — until the appraisal is honest. Not favourable. Not tidy. Honest. Can you do that? Think carefully. Most men can't. Most men would rather make the world add up than report what it does."

"I report what I find," Ivo said. "That's the job."

"Mm." She looked at him for a long moment, and he had the distinct sensation of being appraised — of the surveyor surveyed, the assessor assessed, some interior valuation being made of him with a precision he could not audit. "The last man said the same thing. He was lying, but he believed he wasn't, which is the most dangerous kind of liar. We'll see which you are."

* * *

She dismissed him at half past three — "go on, before the light goes" — but at the door he paused, and it was not professional curiosity that turned him back, or not only. It was the question she'd asked, the second one, still lodged in him like a splinter working toward the surface.

"Miss Ashdown. May I ask you something in return?"

"You may ask. I may not answer."

"Why did you ask if I could carry a tune?"

The light through the old glass had gone amber and slow, and it lay across her face in waves, so that for a moment she seemed to be seen through water — a face at the bottom of a clear, cold pool. When she spoke, her voice was still dry, still even, but something underneath it had shifted, the way ground shifts before it admits it's sliding.

"Because in this house," she said, "the only thing that matters about a man is what he does with his voice. Everything else — your sums, your signatures, your clever little figures — all of that is paper, and paper burns, and paper floats, and paper is eaten by damp. But a voice goes into the walls. A voice stays." She looked down at her folded hands, and Ivo saw that they were not folded in composure at all, but gripped, one over the other, hard enough that the knuckles had gone the colour of the flowers outside. "You'll hear something soon, Mr. Okonkwo. Everyone does. When you do — and I say this as your client, and I will say it only once — when you do, keep your mouth shut. Whatever it sings. However it asks. Keep. Your mouth. Shut."

"The appraisal —"

"Will be honest," she said, "if you're still here to make it honest. That rather depends on your mouth, doesn't it?"

He left her then. In the corridor, the light was failing at the wrong speed — too fast for the hour, the amber draining out of it like blood from a face — and Nell was waiting on the landing with a lamp already lit, though it was not yet four, though it was June.

"You asked her about the tune," Nell said. It wasn't a question.

"I asked why she asked."

"And?"

"She told me to keep my mouth shut."

Nell nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising, and held the lamp higher so its light reached him. In its glow her face was young and old at once, the way the house was, the way the whole village was — something living wearing something ancient, or perhaps the other way round.

"Good," she said. "That's the first honest thing anyone's told you here."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, from the kitchen where the iron cap sat warm over its black throat, came a sound Ivo would spend the rest of the evening refusing to name: not a knock, not a creak, not water. A hum. Three notes, rising, patient as a question — and then stopping, precisely, at the place where a fourth note ought to begin.

Nell's hand closed on his sleeve. "Come away from the stair," she said quietly. "It's learning your voice."
