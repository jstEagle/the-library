## 7. The Drowned Parish

The kitchen of Well House held its heat like a held breath. Ivo sat at the scrubbed pine table with a mug of tea going cold between his hands, and across from him Nell Marsh pulled the iron cap's warmth out of her mind by force, the way you'd pull a splinter, and set about making more tea she didn't drink.

She had not wanted to tell him. He could see that in the set of her shoulders, in the way she kept her back half-turned to the stove. But the dusk had come and gone an hour ago, and the bell had rung, and he had seen what he had seen through the crack of the pantry door — and there was only so long a woman could stand in a kitchen with a man who had watched a procession of the dead cross her yard and still pretend it was foxes.

"Say it," he said. "Whatever you're deciding whether to say. Say it, and then we can both stop pretending this is a survey."

Nell set down the teapot harder than necessary. "You'll think I'm mad."

"I already think the house is mad. You'd just be providing provenance."

That almost got a smile out of her — a flicker at one corner of her mouth, quickly banked. She sat down opposite him, wrapped both hands around her own mug, and looked at the iron disc set into the floorboards between them as if checking whether it was listening.

"It was 1911," she said.

* * *

"Everyone always assumes it was the war that broke Marrow's End," Nell said. "It wasn't. The war was just the excuse. There was a fund — the parish raised money for the new hospital in Ashford, or the widows' relief, or whatever cause was fashionable that season. And the vicar, a man called Ellery Shaw, stood up one Sunday and said God would rather have a hospital than a bell. That the bronze could do more good melted than ringing."

Ivo turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table. "And they sold it."

"They sold it to a foundry in Rye. Eleven hundred pounds, which was a fortune then. The whole village signed the receipt — every household, every hand, even the children made their marks. Verity was two years old; her father pressed her thumb into the ink." Nell's eyes went to the ceiling, as though the ledger of it were stored somewhere above them. "The bell came down on a Tuesday. My great-great-grandmother wrote that the rope went slack in the sexton's hands and everyone felt it — felt it *go* — like a tooth coming out. Like something leaving the body."

"And the tower?"

"Came down the next spring. Bricks sold for the road works. That's your scar of brick you saw on the church." She nodded toward the window, toward the dark where the churchyard lay. "They meant it to be tidy. A clean amputation. Village gives up its voice, village gets a hospital wing with a brass plaque. Very modern. Very 1911."

"But it didn't take."

"No," Nell said. "It didn't take."

She drank, finally, and set the mug down with a small decisive click, a woman arriving at the part of the story she had been circling all evening.

"The singing started the first Sunday after. Not the congregation — well, yes, the congregation, but not *in* the church. In the lanes. In the gardens. Old Mrs. Tredwell was heard humming the evensong hymn while she hung washing, and when her daughter asked her to stop she said, quite pleasantly, that she hadn't started. It was already going. Like the tune had been left behind with the tower and didn't know it was supposed to be finished."

"That's not how sound works," Ivo said. It came out gentler than he intended. He was a surveyor; he believed in load paths and water tables and the way a building tells you its age in its cracks. Sound did not linger in villages for decades out of sentiment.

"No," Nell agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

He should have gone back to the inn. He knew that even then, sitting in the yellow circle of lamplight with his cold tea and his notebook full of measurements that refused to sum. Every professional instinct he owned said: document, withdraw, report anomalies from a safe distance, let some other fool stand in this kitchen.

But Nell had begun talking again, quieter now, the way people talk in hospitals, and he found he could not leave while she was still speaking. It was nothing so noble as compassion. It was closer to the pull he felt toward a cracked foundation — the need to see the whole failure, to trace it to its source.

"People stayed," she said. "That's the part nobody puts in the county history. The village didn't empty because of economics, whatever the archives say. It emptied one voice at a time. Mr. Prowse the blacksmith, 1919. His wife followed within the year. The Adeyemi sisters — no, wait, they were earlier, before my time, but Gran had them in her book. They'd be walking home from the shop and simply... turn off. Toward the church. And they wouldn't come home. Not dead — the graves are all accounted for, Gran checked, she spent thirty years checking. Just *stayed*. Walking, singing, tending the churchyard, and never again answering to their names."

"You're describing dementia in a closed community," Ivo said. "Grief cults form in parishes that lose something central. It's documented. The bell was the village's identity; losing it produced a mass hysterical—"

"Ivo." She said his name the way you'd say *stop* to a horse about to step in a hole. "You watched them tonight. Through the pantry door. Tell me about dementia."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. The figures came back to him unbidden — the slow procession across the yard, forty shapes in clothing fifty years out of fashion, moving with the unhurried unity of a tide. He had told himself moonlight, told himself fog, told himself the strange acoustics of a hollow valley. But one of them had turned its head as it passed the window, and he had seen the face, and the face had been *singing*, mouth open, eyes shut, serene as a child at a carol service, and no fog had ever done that.

"There were four verses," Nell went on, mercifully letting him off the hook. "The parish hymn. Written for the village, about the village — the well, mostly. The old well. The one under your feet." She tapped the floorboards with her toe, once, softly, the way you'd tap a wall to find a stud. "Three verses everybody knew. The fourth—" Her jaw worked. "The fourth was never written down. Ruth Adeyemi composed it, taught it to the choir by ear, and then the bell went and everything went wrong, and she died before anyone thought to ask her for the words. Or maybe she refused to give them. Gran couldn't ever decide which."

"So the congregation sings three verses and stops."

"Every dusk. Three verses, and then silence, and then they walk back wherever they walk back to." Nell looked at him directly for the first time in several minutes. "Except they don't stop, Ivo. That's the thing. They're waiting for the fourth. They've been waiting a hundred years for someone to finish it. And whoever does—" She stopped herself, shook her head. "Whoever finishes it joins them. That's the shape of it. That's what Gran wrote, and Gran was the least fanciful woman who ever lived. She kept bees and did her own taxes and she wrote in her ledger that the last verse is a door, and doors open from the singing side."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved.

"And Miss Ashdown?" Ivo asked, because his voice needed a job. "Where does she fit?"

"Verity was two when the bell came down. She learned the hymn in her mother's arms, the way you learn a language. She's the last one alive who carries all three verses whole — the true tunes, not the garbled things in the hymnals. The congregation knows it. They sing *to* her, every dusk. Have done for ninety years." Nell's throat moved. "They're not haunting her. They're keeping her. Like you'd keep a tuning fork."

* * *

The tea had gone fully cold. Ivo became aware of it as a small solid fact he could hold onto — tea cools at a predictable rate, convection, Newton's law, the world still obeying itself — and he gripped the fact like a rail.

"Let me make sure I have this," he said. "A village sold its bell in 1911. The bell's absence began singing. Residents who wandered toward the church were absorbed into a chorus of the dead, who are dead in no sense I can verify, and who have been performing a truncated hymn nightly for over a century while awaiting a final verse whose author took its completion to her grave. The last living original singer is my client, aged ninety-one, and you're telling me all of this over tea as though it were the drainage arrangements."

"When you put it like that—"

"It sounds exactly like what it is. A folk memory wearing the costume of a haunting. Grief, isolation, inbreeding of legend, a century of retelling." He said it kindly. He even meant it, mostly. "Nell, I'm not mocking you. I've appraised churches in Cornwall where the pews move. There's always a spring underneath, a thermal draft, a resonance frequency. Buildings hum. People hear what they need."

"Do you know what you need?" she asked.

The question landed somewhere below his sternum.

"I need to finish this appraisal," he said. "I need eleven rooms measured and a valuation filed and to be on the 7:42 to Paddington by Friday. That's what I need."

"Then you'd better measure fast." She rose and gathered the mugs, and for a moment her hand rested on the back of his chair — briefly, warmly, an accident of geometry — before she carried them to the sink. "Because they sang to you tonight too. Did you notice? When you were behind the pantry door. The third verse dropped out, all of them, all at once, mid-line. For about ten seconds the yard was silent."

"I noticed the silence."

"They never drop out. A hundred years, and they've never once lost their place." She turned the tap; water drummed into the sink. "They stopped because they were listening to you breathe."

* * *

He stayed the night. He told himself it was practicality — the inn was four miles of unlit lane, and the lane ran past the churchyard, and whatever else he believed, he was not a man who enjoyed being wrong in the dark. He took the blue room at the back of the house, the one Nell aired for him without being asked, and he lay on top of the covers with his notebook open on his chest, reading his own figures by torchlight.

Eleven rooms. Ten of them summed correctly. The eleventh — the long gallery — came up one foot short, every time, in every configuration, as though the house kept a foot of itself in reserve the way a miser keeps a coin.

Below him, through two floors and a capped iron disc, something held its breath.

He did not believe her. He wanted that written plainly in the record of himself: Ivo Okonkwo, BSc, MRICS, did not believe in singing congregations of the dead, in doors that opened from the singing side, in wells that listened. What he believed in was the 7:42 to Paddington and a valuation defensible before any tribunal in England.

But when the first grey light came, and he heard Nell moving in the kitchen below — kettle, cups, the soft domestic percussion of someone who has decided you are staying whether you admit it or not — he got up, and shaved, and went down to her.

"Verity takes breakfast at eight," she said, not looking up. "If you're going to ask her about the hymn, ask gently. She hasn't sung it aloud for anyone since my grandmother died."

"I wasn't going to ask her about the hymn."

Nell glanced at him then, and there was something in her face — pity, or patience, or the particular kindness of people who live inside a story and watch newcomers arrive at its first page. "No," she said. "You weren't. Nobody ever is. That's how it gets you — it starts as a question about drainage."

She slid a fresh mug across the table to him, and above the iron cap in the floor, faint as breath on glass, the boards were warm.
