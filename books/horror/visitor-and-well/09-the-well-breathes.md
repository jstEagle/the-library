## 9. The Well Breathes

Ivo woke at four in the morning to a sound like a held breath.

Not the bell — that came at dusk, and he had learned its schedule the way one learns the schedule of a neighbor's dog. This was smaller, closer, domestic. A tick of the house settling. A pipe. He lay in the narrow bed of the guest room and listened the way he listened to everything now, cataloguing, assigning, filing. The house had eleven rooms he had measured and one he had not, and the one he had not was directly below him.

The sound stopped. Then it started again, and he understood it was not a sound at all but a rhythm — the rhythm of condensation. Of something weeping slowly onto stone.

He went down in his dressing gown with the torch from his survey bag, because a professional does not investigate with his phone like a civilian; a professional investigates with instruments, and instruments can be trusted.

The kitchen was the coldest room in the house, which was itself wrong, because the kitchen held the range and the range held last night's embers, and heat rises, and the kitchen was where the well was. The iron cap sat in the middle of the floor like a great flat coin, and it was sweating. Not damp — sweating. Beads of water stood on the black iron in a fine uniform crop, as though the metal were a cold glass in July, and they were running in slow rivulets toward the rim and down into the seams of the flagstones, where the white flowers had begun, in the last two days, to press their buds up through the mortar like fingernails through skin.

Ivo crouched beside it and put his palm flat on the cap.

Cold. Not stone-cold, not iron-cold. Cold the way the inside of a church is cold — a cold with intent behind it, a cold that had been maintained. He had put his hand on this cap his first morning in Well House and found it warm, blood-warm, faintly unpleasant in the way a stranger's handshake is unpleasant, and he had noted it in his log as *thermal anomaly, unexplained, possible geothermal*, because noting things was what he did and because *possible geothermal* was a phrase that kept the world orderly.

Now it was cold. The anomaly had reversed itself overnight, without consultation.

He fetched his thermometer anyway. Twelve minutes of waiting for the reading, during which he sat on Nell's grandmother's chair with his knees up like a schoolboy and watched the beads of water form. The reading came back: 4.1 degrees Celsius. The cellar of a church. The temperature of the soil at the depth where things stop decomposing properly.

He wrote it down. His handwriting was not as steady as usual.

* * *

The plumb line was, strictly speaking, for measuring depth of drop shafts and stairwells — for establishing whether a wall was true, whether a floor had sunk, whether the bones of a building had begun to lean toward some private conclusion. It was a brass weight the size of a thumb on thirty metres of braided line, and it was the single most honest tool Ivo owned. A plumb line does not estimate. A plumb line does not negotiate. It goes down until it stops, and it tells you the truth about vertical, and everything else is interpretation.

He told himself it was for the report. The well's depth was a material fact of the property; a capped well of unknown depth affected value; a surveyor who did not attempt a measurement was a surveyor who had not done his work. The reasoning was sound. He examined it from several angles the way he examined a wall for damp, and it held, and he was grateful to it, because the other reason — the one that lived lower down, in the same cold stratum as the well — was that he had to know, and knowing was the only prayer he had ever said.

The cap was held by four bolts at its rim. He had checked on the first day, professionally, and found them rusted to decorative uselessness, and had noted *cap not designed for removal; access point decorative* in the log. But when he put the socket wrench to the first bolt now, it turned as easily as a tap. All four did. The cap lifted on a hinge he had never seen, a small iron flap no wider than a dinner plate, hinged at the edge nearest the wall, and beneath it was a hole the diameter of a dinner plate, and beneath that was darkness with a smell coming up out of it.

Not the smell he expected. He expected well-smell: mineral water, stone, the green breath of standing things. What came up instead was frankincense. Old frankincense, the ghost of it, the way a closed church smells on a weekday — resin and smoke long gone, the memory of smoke, smoke remembered by stone. And under the incense, faintly, something floral. White flowers. The ones no one could name.

Ivo knelt at the rim and did what he had done at the top of a thousand buildings: he checked the anchor point. The hinge was solid. The line was new, thirty metres, rated to fifty kilograms, and he had used perhaps four metres of it in his whole career. He tied the brass weight with a double fisherman's knot and said, aloud, to the kitchen, to the house, to whatever was sweating on the other side of the iron:

"Depth of well, unknown. Commencing measurement."

His voice sounded very small and very reasonable, and the house did not answer, which was somehow worse than if it had.

He lowered the line.

* * *

The first five metres were ordinary. He counted them by the knots he had tied at every metre mark, and the weight turned slowly at the end of the line with the small patient motion of a thing hanging, and the darkness took it without comment. Five metres was a sensible depth for a village well. Six. Seven. Seven metres was the water table; he had checked the geological survey for the district before he'd come, because he checked everything, because checking everything was the armor he wore. Seven metres down, in the chalk of the downs, there was water. The line should have come up wet.

At eight metres the line was still dry and still going.

At ten metres his hands were steady but his jaw had set, and he was counting knots with the attention of a man defusing something. Eleven. Twelve. Twelve metres was deeper than any domestic well in the county records, and the line went on paying out through his gloved fingers with the smooth indifference of thread through a needle's eye. The incense smell thickened. It was not unpleasant. That was the obscene thing about it — it was *nice*, it was the smell of Christmas and childhood and the one funeral he had ever cried at, and it rose out of the hole in his client's kitchen floor like a welcome.

Fifteen metres. The spool was half empty. He stopped lowering and held the line still, feeling for a bottom, feeling for water, feeling for anything, and the line hung there in the dark of the earth and transmitted nothing at all — no weight, no drag, no swing. It hung as though it hung in a room. As though the well were not a shaft but a space, a chamber, a nave, and his plumb weight dangled in it like the rope of a bell that had not yet been rung.

He thought, with a clarity that arrived cold and complete: *it is waiting to see what I do.*

And then, because he was Ivo Okonkwo, and because the whole apparatus of his life — the numbers, the levels, the honest brass of it — was the only religion he had, he kept lowering.

Twenty metres. Twenty-five. The spool turned and turned. His knee ached against the flagstone. The flowers at the cap's rim had opened while he knelt — he was nearly sure they had not been open when he began — white and five-petalled and turned toward the hole as though toward a lectern. At twenty-eight metres the line ran out. The knot at the last metre mark sat at the lip of the cap, and the brass weight hung at the end of thirty metres of line in the dark under Marrow's End, and had found nothing.

No bottom. No water. No wall.

Ivo sat back on his heels and looked at the little iron door and felt the specific vertigo of a man whose instruments have begun to lie to him — no. No, that was the coward's version, and he was not a coward, he was a professional, and he made himself say the true version in his head: *the instruments are not lying. The instruments are reporting accurately. It is the world that has stopped being the one my instruments were built for.*

Thirty metres of dry air under a drowned village. He thought of the church without its tower, the scar of brick where the bell had been, the old man who had watched him pass without blinking, the measurements that came up one foot short in every room as though the house were keeping something back, holding one foot of itself in reserve, and he thought: the well is the opposite of the rooms. The rooms are one foot short. The well is thirty metres too deep. The house is not miscalculated. The house is *unbalanced* — and everything it withholds upstairs, it pays out downstairs, and the ledger balances perfectly, and it has been balancing for a hundred years.

The line moved.

Not swung — moved. A slow lateral drift, the weight traveling through the dark as though carried, or as though following, the way a man follows a verger down an aisle. The line paid out a further few centimetres from the spool with a sound like a sigh, and Ivo's whole body went cold from the cap outward, and every hair on his arms stood up and pointed, uselessly, down.

He hauled it up.

He hauled fast, hand over hand, no longer a surveyor taking a reading but a fisherman who has felt something on the hook, and the line came up and up and the knots passed his glove — twenty, fifteen, ten — and the smell of incense poured up the shaft after it, thickening, warm now, almost kind, and at five metres the line went briefly, gently, *heavy* — a weight at the end of it that was not his brass weight, a weight with patience in it — and then it was light again, and the weight broke the surface of the cap's little door, and he let it fall clattering on the flagstones and stood there breathing.

The brass plumb weight was dry. It was also, he saw when he picked it up with the torch, warm — warm as a hand, warmer than the room, warm the way the cap had been warm that first morning. And it smelled of incense. It would smell of incense, he understood, for a long time. Brass does not forget.

He wrote it down. He made himself write it down, kneeling there on the cold flags with the flowers nodding at the rim of the world's most honest hole: *Depth attempted 30m, line exhausted, no termination encountered. No water table at 7m (contradicts survey). Ambient scent: resinous/floral, persistent. Instrument affected.* And then, after a moment, in the small precise hand he used for things that would ruin him if they were ever read aloud: *The house is measuring me back.*

* * *

He did not sleep again. At seven he heard Nell come down the back stairs, and he was in the kitchen when she came in, sitting at the table with the log open and two cups of tea already made, because making tea was a thing hands could do while a mind reassembled itself.

Nell stopped in the doorway. She looked at the cap — shut now, bolts turned, though he could not remember turning them — and then at him, and then at the cap again, and her face did the thing he was beginning to be able to read: the flicker of *not again* crossed with *of course again*.

"You opened it," she said.

"I measured the well. It's a material fact of the property. Depth of a well affects value." The sentence came out in his professional voice, and he heard how it sounded — how it had always sounded, maybe, to everyone who had ever been frightened in front of him — and he stopped, and started again. "It's thirty metres deep. At least. There's no water. There should be water at seven."

Nell came and sat down. She took the tea he'd made her without looking at it, wrapping both hands around the cup as though it were something that might need holding down.

"Grandad tried a tape measure," she said. "Nineteen eighty-four. He got to forty feet and the tape came back marked." She looked up. "Marked how?"

"Marked," she said, "like it had been used for something else. He wouldn't say. He stopped surveying after that. Took up bees." She drank. "The cap was warm when you got here, wasn't it. First morning."

"Yes. It's cold now. It was sweating at four this morning."

Nell set the cup down very carefully, the way people set things down when their hands want to shake and they are refusing to let them. "It sweats when it's listening," she said. "That's what my nan called it. She said the well listens with its skin, like a frog." She looked at him across the table, and her eyes were steady and unhappy. "What did it listen to, Mr. Okonkwo?"

"Thirty metres of plumb line," he said. "And me counting."

The kitchen was quiet. The range ticked. Somewhere upstairs, Verity's floorboard spoke once, the small dry voice of a house holding a very old woman, and then went silent.

"You should know," Nell said at last, "that you're not the first one to put a line down there. And the ones who did—" She stopped. She looked at the cap, and then at the window, where the white flowers in the yard had turned, all of them, toward the house, though there was no wind and no sun yet, only the grey beginning of the day. "There's a book," she said. "A ledger. Nan kept it, and her mother before her. It's got every surveyor in it who ever came to Well House. What they measured. What they found." Her thumb moved on the table, tracing a line only she could see. "And what happened to them after."

Ivo looked at his log, open on the table, at his own small precise handwriting — *the house is measuring me back* — and heard himself say, in a voice that was almost steady and entirely curious:

"I'd like to see the ledger."

Nell stood, and did not quite meet his eyes, and said, "I know. That's what worries me."
