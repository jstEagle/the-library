## 11. Choir Practice

Ivo told himself it was professional diligence.

That was the phrase he used, standing at the window of his rented room above the Marrow's End inn, watching the light go long and amber across the churchyard. *Professional diligence.* The words had a clean, procedural sound to them, like the click of a steel tape measure retracting into its housing. He had been in the village six days. He had measured every room of Well House twice, and both times the numbers had refused to behave, and he had written in his field notes, in his neatest hand: *Discrepancy consistent throughout — 1 ft aggregate shortfall. Possible historical alteration; recommend structural investigation.*

He did not write down what he actually suspected, which was nothing, because he suspected nothing, because there was nothing to suspect. Houses did not lie. Tape measures did not develop opinions. A foot was a foot was a foot.

But Nell Marsh had said, that morning, over the eggs she'd made him without being asked — she cooked for him now, apparently, with an offhand domesticity that unsettled him more than any of the house's arithmetic — "Evensong's at seven. If you're going to keep poking at things, you might as well poke at the right thing."

"I don't poke," Ivo had said. "I survey."

"You survey." She'd dried her hands on her apron and looked at him with that expression he was beginning to catalog against his will: half amusement, half warning, like weather coming over water. "Six days, Mr. Okonkwo. You've heard it ring four times. And you still think if you just get the numbers right, it'll all add up."

"It will all add up," he'd said. "Numbers always do."

She'd laughed — not unkindly, which was worse somehow — and gone out to hang washing that never seemed to dry no matter how many days it hung.

So here he was, at ten to seven, walking up the lane toward a church with no roof, out of what he insisted to himself was diligence and what some quieter, less flattered part of him knew was spite. He wanted to see it. He wanted to stand in the middle of whatever this was — the bell that wasn't, the figures Nell had pulled him behind a pantry door to avoid, the hymn with its missing verse — and look at it directly, with his eyes open, and find it small. That was the method. That had always been the method. Fear shrank under observation. His whole career was built on the principle that a thing examined honestly became a thing manageable, priceable, puttable-in-its-place.

The church of St. Aldhelm's — or what the Ordnance Survey map, 1902 revision, optimistically called St. Aldhelm's — sat at the top of the village like a tooth knocked out and left in the gum. The tower was gone. Not ruined: *gone*, cut off clean at the roofline, the stump capped with slate as though someone had decided the building would simply stop being a church from the neck up. Where the spire should have risen there was only sky, and the scar of brick below it, and a few weeds finding purchase in the mortar. The nave walls stood. The east window stood, its glass long since replaced with boards, so that the evening sun came through the gaps between them in stripes and lay across the floor like the slats of a cage.

The pews were old, dark oak, worn smooth along the tops where generations of hands had gripped and slid. Ivo chose one near the back, third from the aisle, and sat down with his notebook on his knee like a man attending a planning committee. He clicked his pen. He wrote the date. He wrote *7:00 p.m., evensong (informal), attendance: TBD.*

The village drifted in.

That was the only word for it. They came up the lane in ones and twos, unhurried, nodding to each other, taking their places in the pews with the ease of people who had done this all their lives — because they had, most of them, their families before them. The old man who watched him pass without blinking on his first day took a pew two rows ahead. A woman with a headscarf. Two teenagers who slouched and elbowed each other and then, when the first note sounded, went still as everyone else. Perhaps thirty people. Perhaps forty. He counted them twice and got different numbers both times, and told himself the light was bad.

Nobody spoke. Nobody needed to. There was a quality to the silence that Ivo had never encountered in a room full of living people — a settledness, a patience, as though the silence were not empty but full, holding its breath around something they could all hear and he could not yet.

Nell slipped in beside him at five past seven. She smelled of soap and line-dried cotton, and she didn't ask why he'd come, which he appreciated, because he didn't have an answer that would survive being spoken aloud.

"Verity?" he asked quietly.

"Asleep. She sleeps early now." Nell folded her hands in her lap. "You can leave whenever you want. Nobody minds. Nobody ever minds."

"That sounds ominous."

"It's not meant to." She glanced at him sideways. "It's just true."

At seven o'clock precisely — not struck by any clock, but known, the way birds know dawn — the service began.

There was no vicar. There was no book, no order of service, no organ. An elderly man simply stood at the front, turned to face the congregation, and raised his hands slightly, palms down, the way a conductor quiets an orchestra that isn't playing. And then they sang.

They sang evensong. Not a modern hymn, not anything Ivo recognized from a childhood spent being dragged through chapels by a mother whose faith he had traded, somewhere in adolescence, for mathematics. This was older. Plainchant, maybe, or something shaped like plainchant the way a river is shaped like the valley it carved — the same form worn into something older and stranger. The melody moved in long, patient arcs, rising and settling, and the voices carried it together without effort or rehearsal, forty throats breathing as one set of lungs.

And the sound filled the roofless church and went up through the missing tower into the darkening sky, and Ivo understood, sitting very still with his pen uncapped and motionless, why the villagers had sold their bell in 1911 and kept singing anyway.

Because the singing *was* the bell. It rang in the same place in the chest. It tolled and settled and tolled again, and the tower's absence didn't matter because the tower had only ever been a throat, and the throat was still here, seated in these pews, wearing cardigans and headscarves and Sunday shoes.

He wrote nothing. His pen hovered over the page and produced nothing, because there was nothing to measure. Sound was not a quantity he had instruments for. He could have described the frequency, the reverb time of stone walls open to sky, the decibel falloff at distance — he could have reduced it to data, and the data would have been true and would have contained none of it, none of the thing itself, which was—

Which was that it felt like being *included*.

That was the horror of it, and he identified it as horror even as it worked on him, even as it did exactly what it was designed to do. The hymn had holes in it. Spaces left deliberately, the way a round leaves space for the entering voice. And the spaces were shaped like him. Shaped like anyone. Any listener. The melody rose and paused, expectant, and something in Ivo's chest leaned toward the pause the way a plant leans toward light, and he caught himself—

He caught himself mouthing it.

Not singing. Not yet. But his lips were moving, shaping the vowel of the second phrase, his breath timing itself to the gap in the melody, his body — his rationalist, well-regulated, professionally skeptical body — preparing to join a congregation he had attended for ninety seconds. His throat had already begun the harmony. He could feel it there, a hum below speech, a tuning fork struck against his sternum, and the terrible thing, the thing that made cold walk up his spine on slow feet, was that it felt *good*. It felt like coming home to a house he'd never lived in. It felt like the exact opposite of loneliness, and he hadn't known until that moment how lonely he was, how long he'd been lonely, alone in flats and hotels and survey offices, competent and unclaimed, measuring other people's houses and belonging to none of them.

His mouth opened.

Nell's hand closed over his wrist.

Her grip was not gentle. Her fingers were cold — colder than the evening justified — and she pressed down hard, once, a punctuation mark, and Ivo bit the sound back. Bit it literally, teeth meeting tongue, swallowing the half-born note whole. It went down like a stone into deep water, and the water closed over it, and somewhere in the nave the melody paused in its hole-shaped space, waited a moment, patient, unsurprised, and moved on without him.

The cold arrived next.

It came up through the pew, through the soles of his shoes, through the wood that had held generations of warm bodies and should have remembered warmth. It came in along the aisle like a tide filling a channel. The woman in the headscarf sang on. The teenagers sang on. The old man two rows ahead sang on, and Ivo saw, with a clarity he would have paid money to be rid of, that the air around the congregation had begun to shimmer faintly, the way air does over tarmac in summer — except this was cold-shimmer, a distortion, and in the distortion the pews were fuller than they had been. Denser. Shoulders overlapping shoulders where there had been gaps. A row that had held eight bodies now held eleven, and three of them cast no shadow in the striped light from the boarded window.

No one else seemed to notice. No one else needed to. The living sang with the dead the way a family sets extra places at table without comment, because the places have always been set, because the dead are simply members who arrived early.

Ivo sat with Nell's hand locked around his wrist and did not sing and did not leave, because leaving would mean admitting what he'd almost done, and admitting it would make it real, and if it was real then everything was real — the bell, the figures in the yard, the one-foot-short rooms, the well — and he was not ready. He was a man clinging to the gunwale of his own worldview with both hands while the sea came in, and he knew it, and he hated knowing it, and he stayed anyway.

The hymn ended. Not concluded — *ended*, mid-phrase, cut off at the precise point where a fourth verse would begin, the way Verity stopped when she hummed it in the kitchen, the way a record lifts its needle. The silence afterward was enormous. It pressed on his eardrums. In that silence Ivo heard, very distinctly, from beneath the floor — beneath the foundations, beneath the chalk and clay and whatever lay under a churchyard two hundred years deep — a single low tone. Sustained. Expectant. The congregation's silence had a floor to it, and the floor was listening.

Then the old man at the front lowered his hands, and the congregation rose, and the service was over as simply as it had begun. People turned and murmured to each other about weather and knees and someone's grandson. Ordinary. Devastatingly ordinary. The shimmer was gone; the pews were sparse again; the cold drained out of the woodwork like water finding a drain.

"Walk me back," Nell said. It wasn't quite a question.

They went down the lane in the last grey light. The white flowers stood in every garden, luminous in the dusk, and Ivo found himself looking at them properly for the first time — really looking — and realizing he still could not name them. Four petals, a throat of faint gold, a stem that seemed to lean very slightly toward Well House regardless of where they were planted. He had surveyed gardens for fifteen years. He knew plants the way he knew brick bonds. These he did not know.

"You almost sang," Nell said.

"I didn't."

"Your mouth moved."

"My mouth moved," he conceded. "People mouth along to music. It's a documented phenomenon. Auditory-motor coupling. Mirror response."

"Mm." She walked a few steps. "My mum said the same sort of thing, after her first evensong. She was a schoolteacher. Very scientific about it." A pause, the length of a heartbeat too long. "She stopped coming after that. Said she'd stay away till she'd thought it through. Then one dusk she heard the bell from the garden, and she said later it was like hearing her own name called by someone who loved her, and she went to the gate, and—" Nell's voice did something small and controlled. "Well. She's in the ledger now. Second-to-last page."

Ivo stopped walking. The lane was empty. Somewhere downhill a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Nell—"

"Don't." She said it gently. "Don't do the thing where you explain it to me. I've lived here my whole life. I just need you to understand what you brushed against tonight." She turned to face him, and in the dying light her eyes were very steady and very tired. "It doesn't grab people, Mr. Okonkwo. That's the thing nobody understands about it. It doesn't chase, it doesn't drag, it doesn't need to. It just makes room. It makes a space exactly your shape and it waits, and waiting is all it ever has to do, because everybody is lonely, and everybody wants to belong somewhere, and the singing feels like belonging." Her hand rose — hesitated — and touched his sleeve, briefly, where she'd gripped his wrist. "You bit it back tonight. Good. But it'll be easier next time to bite it back, and easier after that to not want to, and one day you won't want to, and that's the day."

"The day what?"

"The day you're theirs." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing he'd seen in Marrow's End, and he had seen the pantry door procession. "Come on. Gran will have the kettle on. And Mr. Okonkwo —"

"Yes?"

"If you go back to evensong — and you will, I can tell, you've got the look of a man who goes back to the thing that frightens him — sit next to me. Every time. My hand works better than your teeth."

They walked down toward the lights of Well House, and behind them the roofless church stood open to the sky, and in the tower that wasn't there, in the dark above the scar of brick, something that had waited patiently since 1911 noted, without surprise, that the new voice had almost joined — and that it would be back.
