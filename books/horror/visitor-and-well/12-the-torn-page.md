## 12. The Torn Page

Ivo woke on the fourth morning with the conviction that he had been dreaming about arithmetic.

Not numbers exactly — the *feel* of numbers, the clean satisfaction of a column that closed, a sum that came home to roost. In the dream he had been adding up the house again, room by room, and every time he reached the total it slid out from under him like a fish, one foot short, always one foot short, and somewhere in the dark behind the dream a voice had said, patiently, almost kindly: *you're counting the wrong thing.*

He lay in the narrow bed in the blue room and listened to Well House wake around him. Pipes ticking. A rooster two fields off that always sounded slightly offended. Below, the kitchen — and beneath the kitchen, under its iron cap, the well, which he had not gone near since the plumb line incident, and which he thought about more or less constantly.

Three days ago he had lowered a surveyor's plumb line through the gap in the cap and it had dropped forty feet into air that should have been water at twelve. It had come back up smelling of incense, of old stone and something sweeter underneath, like lilies left too long in a sealed room. He had told himself: old wells play tricks. Limestone acoustics. A ledge. A false bottom. He was a rationalist; he had a whole vocabulary for this.

The vocabulary was wearing thin. He could feel it going threadbare at the elbows.

He dressed, went down, and found Nell already in the kitchen, sleeves pushed up, scrubbing the flagstones with a ferocity that suggested the flagstones had personally wronged her. The iron cap sat in the middle of the floor like a sleeping animal she was cleaning around rather than cleaning. She'd spread white cloths over the dresser and the table — Verity's doing, he suspected. The old woman liked her house prepared, even for things no guest would ever see.

"Miss Ashdown's asking for you after breakfast," Nell said without looking up. "She wants to go through the hymn again. She says your ear's better than you let on."

"My ear is professionally neutral."

"Mm." Nell wrung the cloth. "That's what my mum used to say about sums."

Ivo let that sit where it landed. He ate his toast standing at the window, watching the white flowers along the garden path do their slow, breathing sway in wind he couldn't feel on his own face. He still couldn't name them. He'd photographed one and run it through three identification apps on his phone; all three had returned errors, then, on the fourth try, a single result so implausible he'd deleted it and cleared his cache. Some things only grew here. Some things only grew *for* here.

"I'm going to do the pantry and the back stairs today," he said. "Finish the service rooms. Then the measurements should—"

"They won't," Nell said. Not cruelly. Just as fact. "They never do. There's always a foot missing."

"And you've never wondered where it goes?"

Nell stood, draped the cloth over the pail, and looked at him for a long moment. She had her grandmother's eyes — grey-green, unhurried, the eyes of someone who had grown up watching weather.

"I know where it goes," she said. "I just don't say it out loud." And she carried the pail out to the yard, leaving Ivo alone with the well and the sound of his own pulse.

* * *

The pantry was a cold little room off the kitchen's north wall, shelved in elm, smelling of beeswax and dried lavender and, faintly, of the sea — though Marrow's End sat eleven miles inland, and had sat there, according to every map Ivo owned, for as long as maps had bothered with it. He measured it twice. Fourteen feet by nine. He measured the back stairs: thirteen treads, each seven and a quarter inches, rising eight feet exactly. He wrote the figures in his book in his small, disciplined hand, and when he added the day's totals against the floor plan, the deficit stood at one foot, four inches now. It had *grown*. The house was eating its own measure, an inch at a time, and some animal part of Ivo's brain — the part that flinched before the conscious mind knew why — kept whispering that the house wasn't losing a foot at all.

It was *gaining* one. Somewhere else.

He was crouched at the bottom of the back stairs, checking the string-line against the skirting, when his torch caught it: a gleam of brass in the hinge of the door at the stair's foot, a door that opened onto nothing but the pantry's side and had probably been bricked shut before either of them were born. The hinge was green with age, crusted, half-swallowed by paint layers. But between the leaf and the jamb, wedged deep where no casual eye would ever fall, something pale showed. Paper.

He worked it loose with his penknife, gently, the way he'd extract a fossil from matrix. It came free in two reluctant stages, and when it finally slid out into his palm he understood immediately why it had been hidden, and why it had been hidden *there* — pressed into the iron-dark join between wood and wall, as close to the well's axis as the geometry of the house allowed. Whoever had put it there hadn't been hiding it from people.

They'd been hiding it from the *house*.

It was a page torn from a hymnal. He knew the paper stock instantly — the same heavy, cream-laid stock as the mutilated copy in the drawing room, the one whose final leaves had been burned away to ragged stubs. But this page was older than burning. It had been folded in eighths until the creases were soft as cloth, and the ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, and the handwriting —

The handwriting stopped him.

It was a child's hand. Round letters, earnest, laboriously ruled against nothing, drifting uphill across the page. Certain words were written twice where the first attempt had wobbled. At the top, in the same hand but larger, careful as a title on a school exercise:

*THE LAST VERSE*

Below it, verse. His eyes moved down the lines and his breath did something complicated in his chest.

Half of it was legible. Half was lost — water-stained, or rubbed, or deliberately blotted, the ink smeared into grey ghosts where meaning had drowned. He read what remained, silently, the way you'd read anything, and got perhaps a third of the way down before he realized he was reading it *aloud in his head* — actually voicing it, the inner voice that narrates — and he snapped the book of his mind shut so hard he tasted copper.

Because he remembered Nell's tea-soaked exposition, delivered in this same kitchen with the light dying: *they sing it, and they stay.* Because he remembered Verity humming three verses and stopping at the cliff-edge of the fourth with the precision of a woman stepping back from a platform edge she'd stood on too long. Because he remembered, with a lurch, the shape of the surviving lines — and they weren't a verse at all, not really. They were a *question*. Addressed downward. Addressed below.

*Who will carry us, when the rope is cut*
*and the water takes the names we gave —*

The rest blurred. He turned the page toward the window-light and the stains swam, and for one vertiginous second he thought he saw the missing words moving *underneath* the stain, legible if he only tilted—

He tilted the page back flat. Held very still. Counted to ten, the way he counted everything, because counting was load-bearing in his life.

What survived, he could read. Roughly six lines of perhaps fourteen. A child had copied out the last verse of the hymn of Marrow's End — copied it carefully, lovingly, the way children copy things that matter to them — and then someone had folded it into eighths and driven it into a hinge beside the well, and the house had kept it safe for a hundred years, or kept it *prisoner*, and Ivo Okonkwo, BSc (Hons), MRICS, was holding it in a pantry that smelled of the sea.

He should take it to Nell. The thought arrived fully formed, obvious, moral. She'd shown him the ledger. She'd earned the truth; she'd bled for it across generations of Marsh women whose names ended in that book like sentences without full stops. She'd want to see it. She'd need to see it.

His hand closed around the page instead.

He didn't have a word for why. Later — much later, in the bad hours — he would try to find one, and the honest answer would be this: it was the first thing in Marrow's End that had belonged to *him*. Not to the house, not to the parish, not to the dead. A piece of evidence. A datum. His profession, his method, his whole self had been failing here for a week — measurements that lied, figures that fled, a plumb line dropped into a throat — and here, at last, was an artifact. Physical. Portable. *Provable.* If he handed it over, it became theirs, part of the village's wet dark story, another thing that happened to other people. If he kept it, it stayed a fact, and facts were the only raft he had.

Also — and he admitted this to himself with distaste, the distaste itself a kind of proof he was still sane — also, he wanted to read it alone first. All of it. Before anyone else's fear could tell him what it meant.

He folded it back into its eighths, feeling the creases give like old joints, and slipped it inside the cover of his measurement book, flat against the cardboard, where a page of figures wouldn't think to look for it.

Then he stood in the cold pantry for a long moment, listening.

The house made its ordinary sounds. Pipes. Wind finding the chimney's throat. From the kitchen, the tick of the clock Nell wound on Sundays. Nothing else. No hum under the floorboards, no warmth rising off the iron cap, none of the listening quality the well sometimes had, the sense of a vast ear turned upward through the earth.

But as he stepped out of the pantry and pulled the door shut behind him, the hinge — the hinge he'd robbed — gave a single soft click, like a tongue against the roof of a mouth.

* * *

Verity was waiting in the drawing room with the hymnal open on her lap and the fire built high, though the afternoon was mild. She looked, today, less like ninety-one than like a portrait of herself at forty that had been left out in weather — the bones enduring, the surface thinned. Her hands lay on the open pages the way a pianist's rest on keys.

"You've found something," she said.

Ivo's stomach performed a slow, professional somersault. "I've found several things. The house is short a foot and growing."

"Not in the house." Her eyes, pale and clear as shallow water, held his without effort. "In yourself. You came down the passage differently. Guilt walks on the outside of the foot." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, which made it worse. "Never mind. Old women say things. Sit, Mr. Okonkwo. Sing with me."

"I don't sing."

"Everyone sings. Some people just insist on calling it something else." She patted the page. "Verse two. You know it now. Hum, if you must. Humming is how the soul takes notes."

And because refusing her felt suddenly like a cruelty with no professional justification, Ivo sat, and Verity Ashdown began to sing the second verse of the hymn of Marrow's End in a voice like thin paper over wire, and — hating himself, monitoring himself, telling himself this was ethnographic, archival, *surveying* — Ivo hummed.

It was worse than singing. Singing would have been a decision. The hum simply happened, rising out of him on the third bar as if it had been waiting in his chest all week for permission, finding the interval below her melody without being taught, and Verity's eyes filled — not with sorrow, he thought, but with something closer to triumph, or relief, or grief for a thing not yet lost — and she reached across and laid two fingers on the back of his hand, and the touch was cold as the pantry hinge.

"There," she whispered, on the last note. "You see? It remembers you already."

"The tune is four bars long," Ivo said. His voice came out steady, which he counted as his finest professional achievement to date. "Anyone can remember four bars."

"Mm," said Verity, in exactly Nell's tone, and turned her face to the fire.

* * *

He waited until dusk was safely past — until the bell had rung its five tolls through a village with no bell tower, until the procession had come and gone beyond the pantry crack where he did not look this time, because looking felt like spending something he'd need later — and then he climbed to the blue room with his torch and his measurement book and locked the door, which was absurd, and did it anyway.

Under the covers, torch between his teeth like a burglar, he unfolded the page.

The child's writing swam up into the light. He read the legible lines again, slowly, letting each one land, and this time he let himself understand their shape. Each line named. *Old Thomas at the rope. Ruth at the slate. The twins who went down laughing.* Names braided into the verse like reeds into a basket — and he thought of chapter sixteen of his own future, though he didn't know it yet; he thought only of what Nell had said: *every surveyor who finished signed something.* Every verse a name. Every name a soul held. The hymn wasn't a song about the congregation.

The hymn *was* the congregation. Sung forward, it gathered them. And the last verse — the verse no one would write down, the verse burned out of every book, the verse a child had copied in secret and hidden in iron — the last verse held *all of them*, together, forever, and the trap was not in the singing.

The trap was in the answering.

Because the legible lines weren't instructions. They were a question, put to whoever remained above ground, asked in the voices of everyone who had ever gone down: *who will carry us?* And the only possible answers were silence — which the well did not accept twice — or the verse itself, sung back, which was acceptance, which was joining, which was the whole congregation pulling you gently, patiently, kindly through the iron.

Unless there was a third answer. Unless the child who wrote this — and Ivo found himself certain, with no evidence whatsoever, that the child had been a girl, and had written it for someone specific, someone she loved, someone the water wanted — unless the child had known an ending the adults never sang. Something folded into the illegible half. Something the stains had eaten.

He read the legible portion once more, lips strictly still, breath shallow, and committed it to memory the way he committed figures: frontwards, backwards, by heart. Then he refolded the page along its soft old creases, returned it to the spine of his measurement book, and lay in the dark listening to the house count its missing foot.

Guilt arrived on schedule, punctual as the bell. It sat on his chest and pointed out, reasonably, that Nell had shown him her ledger, her mother's name, her whole inheritance of dread; that Verity had sung to him with her fingers shaking; that he was sleeping under their roof, eating their bread, cataloguing their souls for money — and hoarding the one object in Marrow's End that might save them, in a locked room, under a torch, like a thief.

*I'll tell her,* he told the guilt. *When I understand it. When I can give her more than half a page and less than an answer.*

The guilt considered this, found it contemptible, and stayed anyway.

Downstairs, so faint he might have invented it, the iron cap ticked once against its seating — the sound of metal settling, or of a lid being tested from beneath — and Ivo Okonkwo, who did not believe, lay awake until morning with his hand pressed flat against the floorboards, counting his own heartbeat, and losing count, and starting again.
