## 7. The Night of Rising Water

The water came at moonrise, and it came politely.

That was the worst part, I think. No roar, no wall of black flood. Just the low fields filling like a bath drawn by someone considerate enough not to wake you—except I was awake, because I'd been awake for three nights running, listening to my tapes and hating them, and so I heard the first sluice-gate creak open down by the mill. Then the second. Then a sound I had no name for: a long wet exhalation from the direction of the founder's house, as if the valley itself had been holding its breath since 1911 and had finally decided to let it out.

I stood at Edith's window in my coat, notebook forgotten on the chair behind me, and watched the pasture turn to glass. The sheep had already moved themselves uphill with the unbothered competence of animals who have read the same chapter before. Nobody else was out. Every window in Marrow's Rest was lit, and every lit window was empty, and I understood that the whole village was standing just inside its own glass, watching the fields drown, waiting to see if I would do something about it.

I made tea. It seemed important, somehow, to be doing something small and human while the dead got organized.

* * *

They started singing around two in the morning.

Not from the well. From the lanes. That was the detail that undid me—not the voices themselves, though God knows they were bad enough, but their geography. The founders' chorus had come up out of the ground and gone walking, spreading through the village like spilled ink finding every seam in the paper. I could hear them on Church Lane, on the path past Tam's cottage, down where the old tithe barn leaned into its own shadow. They sang the missing verse—the one no living throat in Marrow's Rest had finished in a hundred years—and they sang it in rounds, patient, overlapping, the way you'd sing to teach a child.

And they sang it in my mother's intonation.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. Not her voice. Her voice died with her when I was nineteen, and whatever walked the lanes that night had never owned lungs or a mother or a name. But the intonation—the way she'd lift the third syllable of a line and let the fourth fall away like something set down carefully rather than dropped; the faint Norwegian flatness she'd never lost; the way she turned questions into statements so you couldn't argue with them—that was hers, reproduced with the fidelity of a machine that had been studying the recording of my life for weeks. Because that's what I'd given it. Every tape. Every interview. Every time I'd said *my mother used to sing* into a microphone, warm and confiding, an archivist sharing provenance.

It learns you if you listen back, Tam had told me. What I'd never understood until that night was that I hadn't needed to listen back. I'd been broadcasting the whole time.

I went out into the lane because someone had to, and because fury is braver than fear and easier to carry.

The fog had come up off the flooded fields, and the singers were shapes in it—tall, wrong-jointed, dressed in the cut of clothing I'd only ever seen in the parish photographs, the ones where everyone's face has gone soft as wet paper. They didn't stop when I came out. One of them turned its head toward me, and where a face should have been there was the suggestion of one, the way a well suggests water: darkly, and at a depth.

"You don't get her," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, which was mortifying, but I pressed on. "You don't get to wear her. She never set foot in this valley. Whatever you pulled her out of, put it back."

The shape regarded me. And then—it did put her back, or folded her away, and took up instead the voice of old Mr. Petchy who'd interviewed me my first afternoon about hedgerows, and then the voice of the vicar, and then, horribly, briefly, mine. A sampler. A wardrobe. It wasn't mocking me. That was the thing I finally grasped, standing in the cold with my fists clenched. It wasn't even threatening me. It was *demonstrating*. Look what we can hold. Look how much of you we've already got. The verse rose again over the rooftops in a dozen borrowed mouths, and under it, far below everything, the well hummed like a struck bell that had decided not to stop.

* * *

They came for me at dawn—or rather, they came out at dawn, all of them, wading up the lane through ankle-deep water that should not have been there, in their nightclothes and their good coats, carrying lanterns they didn't need because the sky was going grey and gold behind the fells. Edith walked beside me without being asked. Tam arrived at a run and planted himself at my left side with his chin up, and I put my hand on his shoulder and felt him shaking and felt him refusing to move anyway, and thought: *this is what belonging costs, and this is what it buys,* and hated myself for how much I wanted it.

Vicar Aldous stopped three paces from me. His hands were steady now. That frightened me more than the shaking had.

"It's time," he said.

"Time," I repeated.

"The water's at the churchyard wall. By tomorrow it'll be in the low cottages. We've had floods before, miss, but not—" He gestured at the lanes, at the fog, at the shapes still faintly visible in it, singing. "Not accompanied."

"And you want me to go down the well."

The word went through the crowd like a current. Not horror. Relief. I watched it move from face to face—Petchy, the Hendersons, the young couple from the shop who'd always been kind to me in the distant, unfurnished way of people being kind to a temporary guest—and I watched relief curdle into embarrassment when they saw that I saw.

"We're asking," Edith said loudly, "not demanding." But her voice had the strained quality of a woman holding a door shut against something, and I knew which side of the door she was on.

"No," I said. "You're not asking. Asking means I could say no." I looked around at all of them, these people who had fed me and sung to me and never once asked me to stay for supper. "Do any of you know what the ritual actually costs? Has anyone besides Edith read Maren's papers? Because I have. The keeper doesn't die. Dying would be tidy. The keeper goes *down*, and stays down, and wears what's down there like a glove, forever, and the village gets its quiet and its dry fields and its complete ballad, and nobody has to think about it again except once a year with flowers." I heard my own voice climb and didn't rein it in. "You didn't gather a savior. You gathered a sacrifice. You've been fattening me on kindness for six weeks and calling it welcome."

Silence. Water lapped against someone's gatepost. Somewhere in the fog the founders' chorus reached the end of the verse and began again, and this time they left a gap after the last line—a held breath, a rest, the exact length of the missing words—as if inviting the village to fill it in. As if inviting *me*.

Tam's hand found mine. Small, cold, gripping hard.

"They're frightened," he said, to me, not to them. Defending them even now. Maren's blood in him, I supposed. Keeper's blood runs stupid and loyal.

"I know they're frightened," I said. "So am I. Frightened isn't the problem." I turned back to the vicar, and I made sure my voice was level, because the next part mattered. "Here's what I need you to understand, all of you. If I do this—if I go down that well—I'm not doing it because you asked. I'm not doing it for Marrow's Rest. Marrow's Rest spent a century letting a boy's grandmother drown alone in a hole and called it tradition." Tam flinched; I squeezed his hand. "I'll do it for him. For Edith. Maybe—for the place itself, the valley, the actual ground, which never asked anyone for anything. But not for you. And I want that on the record. I'm a folklorist. Records are the only sacrament I've got."

Nobody spoke. The vicar bowed his head, whether in shame or agreement I couldn't tell and decided not to care about.

Edith stepped forward. In her hand was Maren's ledger, swollen with damp, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, something I recognized with a lurch: the founder's covenant, the original, the paper Hazel Iversen had crossed an ocean hoping some version of it existed. She held both out to me.

"There's one more thing you should know before you decide," she said. "Something Maren wrote at the end. About what happens to the keeper's records."

The fog had begun to thin. Down the lane, the singers had stopped, all of them at once, mid-verse, heads tilted toward us in the grey light—listening, the way they always listened, the way everything in this valley listened—with the patience of something that had waited a hundred years and could hear, in my silence, that I was already saying yes.

I took the ledger from Edith's hands.

"What does it say?" I asked.
