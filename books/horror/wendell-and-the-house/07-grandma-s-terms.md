## 7. Grandma's Terms

The journal did not want to be read.

Lena understood this the way she understood load-bearing walls — not by study but by pressure, by the way her hands hesitated at the cover as if the cardboard itself had opinions. It was a ledger, really, one of those green accounting books with the marbled spine, bought in bulk sometime in the seventies and filled with a handwriting that started neat and ended like a seismograph of a life coming apart.

She sat at the kitchen table with every light in the house burning. She had counted them twice: fourteen bulbs, four lamps, one flashlight on the table beside her like a sidearm. Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old pewter, and the storm drain behind the house breathed its warm, dry breath through the floorboards, patient as a sleeping animal.

*Day 1,* the earliest entry read, in her grandmother's young hand, *Mama says I am to keep the book now. She says the counting is not superstition. She says the counting is the fence.*

Lena turned pages slowly, afraid of tearing them, more afraid of what she'd find between them.

The rules emerged the way figures emerge from fog — first a shape, then a certainty. Her grandmother had written them out in full only once, early, maybe 1974, underlined three times, and then referred to them for thirty years as if they were scripture she no longer needed to quote because she had become it:

**THE TERMS.**

**1. Keep the lights burning. Every room, every night. The dark is not empty and it is not ours.**

**2. Count the doors at dusk and again at dawn. Ten doors. If you count eleven, do not open the eleventh. Do not knock on it. Do not say its number aloud.**

**3. The chair stays empty. Whatever sits down in it, you do not look at its face until it has been fed.**

**4. Once a year, on the longest night, renew. Say the words. Sign the line. Do not be late.**

Below that, in smaller letters, almost apologetic:

*The family lives.*

Lena read the paragraph four times. Her tea went cold beside her untouched. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard ticked — the ordinary sound of an ordinary house cooling in ordinary weather, she told herself, and told herself again, because the second telling didn't take either.

*Renew.* Renew what? There was no contract in the book, no copy, nothing to sign. But the entries around each winter solstice grew strange, formal, written in a hand that stiffened like someone reciting rather than remembering:

*December 21, 1981. Said the words. Signed. He seemed satisfied. He asked after Ruthie's schooling. I did not answer. You don't answer questions that aren't questions.*

*December 21, 1990. Renewed. He stood in the doorway of the kitchen for two hours afterward just watching me wash dishes. Not menacing. Worse than menacing. Hopeful.*

*December 21, 2003. Renewed. I let him set the table this once. Mama would have skinned me. But he arranged the forks so carefully, and he hummed while he did it, and for one whole minute this house felt like a house.*

Lena's throat tightened. She knew that humming. Everyone in the family knew that humming — the little tune Grandma sang while cooking, the one nobody could place, the one Mom said came from *her* mother, and her mother before. A lullaby with no origin. A song that had entered the family the way water enters a foundation: slowly, invisibly, everywhere.

She kept reading. The years stacked up like sandbags against a flood. And then the handwriting began to fail.

The hospice had taken Grandma in October. Lena remembered the room — the antiseptic sweetness, the machine that breathed for her in the last week, the way her grandmother's hands, even folded and still, seemed to be counting something. At the end Lena had leaned close and heard her murmuring numbers. *Nine. Nine. Nine.* They'd all assumed it was morphine. Lena had held her hand and said, "It's okay, Grandma. You can rest."

*You can rest.* God. She had given her permission to stop counting.

The last entry was dated December 20th of last year — the day before the solstice, the day before the renewal. The handwriting was barely legible, letters collapsing into each other like tired men leaning on one another:

*Cannot get there this year. Cannot hold the pen. Lena will come. Lena knows how to find things. I have left the book where she will find it. Pray she reads before—*

The sentence stopped. There was no dash, no trailing ink. Just a stop, mid-word, as if the pen had been taken from her hand. Or as if the hand had finally been allowed to rest.

Lena sat very still.

The refrigerator hummed. The storm drain breathed beneath the floor. And she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the first page, the way you avoid the total on a bill you already know you can't pay.

Grandma had died eleven months ago. Eleven months of entries missing — no, not missing. Impossible. A woman in hospice could not count doors or burn lights. Which meant that for eleven months the terms had been... what? Suspended? Forgiven?

No. Lena flipped back through the book, hunting, and found it — a single entry from decades earlier, wedged between two solstice renewals, written small and urgent:

*Nearly missed it in '88 — flu, fever, three days in bed. Thought the lapse would end us. It didn't. He waited. He said (and I hate that I remember his exact words): "A door stays open longer than you'd think." But he was disappointed. I could feel the house being disappointed, the way you feel weather change. There is a grace period. There has always been a grace period. One year. One year and then the terms are void and the house collects what it was owed.*

One year.

Grandma died eleven months ago.

Lena's mouth went dry. She looked at the calendar on the wall — Grandma's calendar, still hanging, still turned to the right month because nobody had the heart to take it down. She counted backward from today with her finger hovering over the squares, touching each one like a landmine.

Nine days ago, the year had closed. Nine days ago, the grace period ended. Nine days ago, the terms became void, and the house became entitled to collect whatever it had been promised across sixty years of counted doors and burning bulbs and one deliberately empty chair.

And nine days ago — she remembered this with a cold, sliding feeling in her stomach — was the morning she'd crawled into the storm drain. The morning the tunnels had been warm. The morning her compass spun and her flashlight dimmed *politely*, as if asked.

As if invited.

"No," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the journal, to whoever might be listening. "No, I'm not — I didn't sign anything. I didn't say any words."

But she thought of the photograph. The newest one, taken last week, the chair occupied by a figure soft with blur. She thought of the eleventh door she hadn't opened. She thought of the way the dark in the hallway had felt attentive, the way a dog feels attentive before it decides whether you're friend or food.

The house wasn't haunting her. Hauntings were echoes. This was a claim being processed. Sixty years of payments, one missed renewal, and now the debt had come due — and she was the only account holder left in the building.

Her phone buzzed on the table. She flinched hard enough to knock the flashlight sideways.

*MARCUS:* *Ferry's booked. Be there Thursday to help sort the estate. Can't wait to see the old place. Weirdly miss it??*

Thursday. Two days. Her brother, driving into a house that had just come into its inheritance — of them.

Lena typed with shaking thumbs: *Stay at the motel. Seriously. Don't sleep here.*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared.

*MARCUS:* *lol why*

She stared at the screen. How did you explain a ledger? How did you tell a grown man that their grandmother had spent forty years paying protection money to something under the floorboards, and that the payments had stopped, and that the collectors were already inside?

*It's got mold,* she typed. *Bad. Whole downstairs.*

*MARCUS:* *yikes ok fine motel. you're so dramatic, you know that?*

*Yeah,* she wrote. *I know.*

She put the phone face-down. Then she picked up the journal again, because there was one more thing nagging at her, one thread loose in the weave. The words. *Say the words.* Every solstice entry mentioned saying the words, but nowhere — not once in sixty years — did her grandmother write down what they were.

Lena went through the book again, slower, tilting pages to catch the light. And near the middle, pressed flat between the pages of a February long past, she found a slip of paper. Thin as onion skin, yellowed, folded into eighths. On the outside, in a hand she didn't recognize — heavier, older, a man's hand, confident the way only careless men are confident — a single line:

*For the keeping of the house and the keeping of the family, one seat at the table, one light in the window, one name never spoken above a whisper.*

Wendell. The name never spoken above a whisper. Except Grandma had spoken it, eventually, in these pages, over and over, until it stopped being a whisper and became a wound.

Lena unfolded the paper the rest of the way. The rest of the text was dense, formal, written in that same heavy hand, and it was worse than the rules. The rules were a fence. This was the deed. She read the opening clause and felt the temperature of the room change:

*...the undersigned does hereby grant unto the Keeper full right of dwelling within these walls, and the household granteth unto the Keeper that which is most precious to a dweller: acknowledgment, provision, and place. In return the Keeper shall ward the household from that which walks below. This bond renews yearly by word and mark, and should the bond lapse, the household oweth the Keeper that which was promised in perpetuity — namely, a place, and persons to fill it.*

*A place, and persons to fill it.*

Not protection money. A trade. Safety in exchange for seats at the table. And the seats had been empty — deliberately, faithfully empty — for sixty years, which meant the debt had been accruing interest the whole time, deferred, deferred, deferred, until a dying woman's hand slipped off the pen.

Lena looked up at the kitchen doorway.

She didn't see anything. That was the worst part. She didn't see anything, and the doorway saw her anyway — she could feel the attention of it, the particular quality of a space that was being stood in without being occupied. Warm air moved faintly at the threshold, though there was no draft. And beneath the floor, far down, the tunnels exhaled, slow and satisfied, like something settling in for a long-awaited meal.

"Thursday," Lena whispered. "He comes Thursday."

The warmth at the doorway deepened, almost pleased.

She gathered the journal, the slip of paper, the flashlight. Fourteen bulbs burning. She checked each room on her way to the guest bedroom, counting doors as she went — ten, ten, ten — and at the top of the stairs she paused, because the hall stretched away into the part of the house where the lights reached least, and somewhere in that dimness a chair scraped softly across hardwood.

Once. Politely. Like someone making room.

Lena locked her bedroom door, pushed the dresser against it, and lay down on top of the covers with the journal open on her chest and every light blazing, doing the only math that mattered now: two days until Marcus arrived, and a house below her that had waited sixty years to set the table —

— and had already begun pulling out the chairs.
