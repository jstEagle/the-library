## 6. Frost Notation

The first morning, Cyrus thought it was a leak.

She came into the kitchen at 4:40 p.m., eleven hours off the air and still half in the studio's blue light, and the window over the sink was furred white from corner to corner. Condensation, she told herself. The apartment's heat was a losing argument against October; the radiator clanked like something trying to get out of the wall, and the windows wept every winter. She'd lived here six years. She knew what frost looked like.

Except frost didn't look like this.

She stood at the sink with her coffee going cold in her hand and studied the glass. The pattern wasn't the usual ferning, those branching feathers ice makes when it's just being weather. This was lines. Straight, parallel lines running horizontally across the pane, five of them, evenly spaced, and along the lines sat small dense clusters of frost—ovals, mostly, some with little stems rising or dropping away from them, some stacked two high like they were leaning on each other.

Cyrus had taken four years of piano before her mother decided discipline was cruelty. She knew what musical notation looked like.

"Okay," she said to the window, because saying things out loud had become a habit lately, a way of proving her own voice was hers. "Okay."

She touched the glass. It was so cold it burned, colder than the room, colder than outside—the forecast said thirty-eight degrees, and this felt like the inside of a meat locker. She pulled her hand back and watched the spot where her fingertip had been. The frost there didn't melt. It closed over her fingerprint like water closing over a stone, and one of the oval clusters drifted, very slightly, a quarter inch down the staff line, as if adjusting its seat.

She did not clean the window. She wanted to—her hands actually ached with the wanting, the whole animal need to wipe the thing away and call it condensation—but she made herself leave it. She'd spent two weeks now pretending the callers weren't dead, and pretending had gotten her a sleep app full of a stranger's voice and a library card she was afraid to use. She was done pretending about things she could photograph.

So she photographed it. Phone flat against the glass, flash on, three shots. Then she opened her notebook—the cheap spiral one she'd started keeping after the Listener Log, because the log itself lived at the station where management could read it—and wrote the date, the time, *window over sink, nine ovals, five lines*, and underneath that, because her hand seemed to know something her head didn't want to: *nine.*

Nine callers would have been too neat. There had been four. Four dead people calling a graveyard shift in Halloway, which was already four more than statistics allowed. But the number nine kept surfacing anyway, like a body in a pond. Nine faces scratched out of a photograph she hadn't found yet but knew existed. Nine of something. She could feel the number waiting for her somewhere in 1974, patient as a debt.

She worked the evening shift, slept badly, and woke at dawn because the radiator died and the silence woke her like a hand shaking her shoulder.

The second bar had grown overnight.

It sat to the right of the first, same five staff lines continuing unbroken across the glass, new ovals settled onto them—a longer note this time, one oval with a stem and another without, and above the line, floating free of everything, a single small cluster that had no business being there, sitting in the space where a note would go if the scale climbed past the top of the staff. Cyrus stood in her pajamas with her breath fogging and counted the ovals twice. Sixteen now. The first group had held nine.

"It's growing," she said. Her voice came out wrong, flattened, the voice she used on-air when a caller said something that made the producer's headset go quiet. Professional voice. Armor voice. "You're growing."

The frost didn't answer. Frost never answered; that was almost worse than if it had. The second voice in her sleep recordings answered. The frost just *was*—inexorable, unhurried, doing whatever it did while she was unconscious, and she understood with a clarity that felt like falling that she was being shown something on a schedule. Not haunted. *Invoiced.* Something was presenting her with terms, and the terms arrived one bar per night, and she had no idea how many bars there were supposed to be.

She started sleeping on the couch so she could see the kitchen doorway.

* * *

By the fourth morning she had a system, because systems were how Cyrus Underhill stayed sane on the graveyard shift, and if her life was going to be a horror story she was at least going to run it like a broadcast: tight board, clean transitions, no dead air.

Coffee first. Then the window, photographed before she touched anything, timestamped. Then the notebook, dated entries, measurements—she'd bought a plastic ruler at the drugstore on Route 9 and held it up to the glass like an idiot at six in the morning, because data was a talisman and she needed all the talismans she could get. Staff lines ran edge to edge now, top to bottom of the pane, and the bars marched leftward from the frame in a slow procession. Bar one: nine ovals. Bar two: seven. Bar three: five, and one of them had a sharp sign in front of it, a tiny lattice of frost angled like a cage door.

Bar four appeared on Thursday and it was different, and the difference was what finally broke through the professional voice and got under her skin to the wet living part underneath.

The other bars had looked composed. Deliberate. Whatever intelligence arranged the frost understood notation better than she did—beams connecting stems, rests rendered as small blank gaps, the spacing between notes narrowing where the phrase presumably quickened. But bar four was crowded. Ovals pressed against each other, overlapping, some smeared sideways as though written by a hand moving too fast, and at the end of the bar the staff lines themselves frayed, dissolving into ordinary ferning frost before regrouping at the start of bar five's empty stretch.

It looked like someone singing while the building burned.

Cyrus put her palm flat against the glass and held it there until the cold went through her skin and into the bones of her hand, and she thought: *they're not writing this for me. They're writing this because it's the only way left to be heard.* Marla Tetch had called her, voice dry as paper ash, asking about a song. The woman in the sleep recording had said *she kept it in the hymnal.* And now the song itself was arriving on Cyrus's window one measure at a time, patient, legible, addressed to the one person in Halloway whose family name was on whatever had happened.

"You could just knock," Cyrus whispered. Her breath bloomed on the glass and the frost drank it, the pane clearing in a perfect oval around her mouth before whiting over again. "You could knock like a normal—"

She stopped. Laughed, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. Normal. Nothing about this had ever once considered being normal.

* * *

Friday night on air, she almost talked about it.

That was the danger of live radio nobody warned you about—that the microphone is a confessional booth with a transmitter attached, that at 3 a.m. the distance between *I'm fine* and *frost is writing sheet music on my kitchen window* is about four seconds of bad judgment. She introduced a Weather Service advisory instead, read the overnight lows—*thirty-one degrees, clear skies, frost likely*—and heard her own voice catch on the word *likely* like a record skipping.

The request line lit up. She stared at it through two songs. She knew who it would be. It was always someone from the obituary page now, and she had stopped telling herself otherwise sometime around the third caller, and the not-telling had built up inside her like carbon monoxide, colorless, odorless, structural.

She let it ring through. Took the next hour music-only, back-announcing in a monotone, and when her shift ended she sat in the parking lot in the gray pre-dawn with the engine ticking and called the one person she'd sworn not to call, and hung up before it rang twice, because the person she'd sworn not to call was Deen Okafor at the county archive, and she wasn't ready to owe anyone the truth yet.

Instead she drove home and measured the window.

Bar five had come in during her shift. Clean again, composed, the frantic crowding of bar four resolved—as if the writer had steadied their hand, or as if bar four had simply been the hard part, the part where the fire reached the choir loft, and the melody had come out the other side quieter. Five ovals. One of them sat below the staff on its own ledger line, deep down where the piano exercises of her childhood had never bothered to go.

She counted total notes: thirty-two. She wrote it down. Then, because dread was becoming arithmetic whether she liked it or not, she did the math she'd been avoiding since Tuesday.

Four bars in four nights. If the piece ran a conventional length—if it was, say, a hymn, verse and refrain, the kind of thing a chapel choir would sing on an ordinary Sunday in 1974 while a woman named Ruth Underhill walked out the back door with the sheet music in her coat—it might run sixteen bars. Twenty-four. Thirty-two.

At one bar per night, she had somewhere between eleven and twenty-seven days.

Or it ran nine bars. Nine singers. Nine ovals in the first measure, and she'd known it the moment she saw them and had spent four days refusing to write it down.

She wrote it down. *Nine bars. Four complete. Five nights remaining.*

The number sat on the page looking exactly like what it was: a countdown. She had spent her whole adult life filling dead air, and now the air was filling itself, and the deadline was written in ice on her own window in a language she could read but not speak.

* * *

Saturday she slept until noon and dreamed of the chapel.

She'd never seen Bethel Road chapel—not the ruins, not even a photograph; she'd gotten as far as the microfilm drawer at the library and then lost her nerve, which was its own kind of confession—but the dream knew it anyway. White clapboard gone gray. A steeple like a raised finger. Inside, pews, and dust motes hanging in light that came through windows that were somehow both intact and shattered, and nine people standing in the loft with their mouths open, holding one note, holding it for fifty years, their coats beginning to smolder at the cuffs while they kept the pitch because nobody had told them they were allowed to stop.

She woke with the melody in her head.

Not the frost version—something older, fuller, harmonized, four voices moving under a melody line that climbed toward the top of the staff and hung there, suspended, unresolved, aching to come down. She lay on the couch with her heart slamming and hummed it before she was awake enough to stop herself, three bars' worth, and the radiator chose that moment to clang back to life and she flinched so hard she bit her tongue.

But she'd caught herself doing something important, and she made herself look at it straight: she could hear it. The dead had spent two weeks trying to reach her through phone lines and sleep apps and now her own kitchen glass, and somewhere in the process the song had gotten *into* her, laid down tracks in her head like a cover version recorded over her own static. Which meant the frost wasn't just a message. It was practice. Rehearsal. Someone teaching her the piece one measure at a time because eventually—five nights from now, eight, eleven—someone was going to have to sing it, and the frost's arithmetic had already done the math on who was left.

"One singer left," she said to the ceiling, testing the words, and hated how well they fit.

She got up and went to the kitchen and the sixth bar was already there, early, arrived in daylight, which broke every rule the pattern had established and scared her more than anything so far. Six ovals. And beneath the staff, below the lowest ledger line, in frost finer than the rest, smaller, like a footnote or a signature or a whisper: a single word.

She leaned close enough that her nose nearly touched the glass, close enough to feel the cold coming off it like breath.

The word was *hurry.*

Cyrus stood in her kitchen in the noon light with her breath fogging the pane and her reflection ghosted over six bars of a dead choir's last song, and she understood that the schedule had changed. The countdown wasn't counting down anymore. It was counting *out*—and somewhere across town, in a cedar chest or a courthouse basement or a locked drawer in a house on Creed land, a hymnal with pages cut out was waiting for her to stop measuring and start moving.

She grabbed her keys, her notebook, and the jacket she'd stopped wearing because it had been Ruth's, and she headed for the door to go find Deen Okafor and tell him everything.

Behind her, on the window, the frost began, gently and without hurry, to write bar seven.
