## 7. Deen Asks Questions

Deen Okafor had worked at the Halloway Public Library for eleven years, and in that time he had learned that the most interesting people were the ones who tried not to be interesting at all.

The woman in the microfilm room was one of those. She'd come in three days running now, always at the same hour, always with the same careful casualness of someone who had rehearsed not looking like she was looking for something. She sat at the far machine, the one with the wobbly leg, and she fed the 1974 reels through the reader like a surgeon threading a suture—slow, precise, and not once glancing up when the door opened.

He knew who she was. That was the thing about Halloway. You knew everyone, or you knew everyone's grandmother, which amounted to the same thing. Cyrus Underhill. The night radio voice. Her grandmother Ruth had been in this library a hundred times, back when Deen was still the new hire everyone forgot to introduce. Ruth Underhill had a voice like a struck bell and a way of asking questions that made you answer before you'd decided to.

The granddaughter had the same way of sitting. Straight-backed. Listening even when nothing was being said.

Deen stood at the microfilm cabinet with a drawer half open, holding a reel he had no intention of filing, and watched her read a newspaper older than she was.

*The Halloway Courier*, March 1974. He knew exactly which page she was on, because he'd been on it himself, more times than he could count. Page three. The fire at the Bethel Road chapel. Nine dead. The photograph of the chapel's blackened ribs against a gray sky. And the sentence—the sentence he had underlined in his own working copy, the sentence that had kept him at this job long past the point where a sensible man would have moved to a city with an actual archive—

*Choir members present at the Wednesday evening rehearsal included Ruth Underhill, soloist, who was unavailable for comment.*

Unavailable for comment. Fifty years, and Ruth Underhill had been unavailable for comment.

Deen slid the drawer shut and made a decision he'd been circling for three days, the way you circle a dog you're not sure is friendly.

* * *

He waited until she came up for air. That was the archivist's patience in him—wait for the document to end, wait for the breath, then approach. She was squinting at something, her lips moving faintly, counting. He'd seen her count before, on the first day. Counting names in the casualty list, maybe. Counting something.

He set a cup of coffee on the table beside her machine. The good stuff, from the tin he kept in the back room for days when the town got to him.

"You've been on that reel for two hours," he said. "Your eyes are going to file a grievance."

She startled—actually startled, hand to her chest, and he felt bad about it immediately. He'd meant to be gentle. He was usually better at this.

"Sorry," he said. "I should have coughed or something."

"No. No, it's fine." She looked at the coffee like it might be a trick. "Thanks."

"Cream's in it already. I took a guess."

"You guessed right."

He pulled a chair from the next table and sat down across from her, not too close, at the distance you'd sit from a stranger at a bus stop. She watched him do it. Her eyes went to the microfilm cabinet, then to his face, then back to the machine, where the film had advanced to a page of grocery advertisements. She didn't rewind it. That was a tell, he thought. Someone deep in research would have paused it. She was letting it run because she wanted something to look at that wasn't him.

"Deen Okafor," he said. "I run the local history collection. Well—'run' is generous. I'm the local history collection."

"Cyrus." She didn't offer a last name. Another tell. Everyone in Halloway offered their last name; it was the town's native currency. "Underhill," she added, a half-second later, and he watched her hear herself say it and wish she hadn't.

"I know," he said, and smiled, to soften it. "I mean—I know the name. From the records."

Something moved behind her face. Not fear, exactly. More like a door being held shut by someone leaning their whole weight against it, and Deen had spent enough years around locked collections to recognize the posture.

"Which records?" she asked.

He'd rehearsed this part. He'd rehearsed it in his car in the parking lot that morning, actually, which was embarrassing, but there it was. He'd rehearsed three versions: the casual one, the professional one, and the honest one. He'd promised himself he'd start with the honest one, because he was fifty-three years old and he was tired of the other two.

"The Bethel Road fire," he said. "March thirteenth, 1974. The choir of the Bethel Road chapel was rehearsing for the spring service. There was a stove in the back room—the chapel never got the gas line run, they were still on oil heat. Something went wrong with it. The fire moved through the sanctuary in under four minutes. Nine people died. They were found in the choir loft." He paused. "They were found in the choir loft, Cyrus, and the coroner's report says most of them were still standing. Holding the rail. Like they'd kept singing."

The microfilm kept scrolling. Grocery ads. Eggs, forty-nine cents a dozen. Cyrus's hand had gone still on the table.

"That's a terrible story," she said.

"It is."

"Small towns have terrible stories. That's not news to anyone who lives in one."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on the table, a fidget he hated in himself. "Here's what's news. Or what would be, if anyone in this town still cared about its own history, which they don't, which is why it falls to me. Nine people died, and the official record has a hole in it the size of a person. The Courier lists the choir roster—eleven names. Eleven singers at that rehearsal. Nine died. One survived, and the paper never names her. Just says a choir member escaped through the vestry door. And the eleventh—" he watched her face, watched the door behind her eyes strain on its hinges— "the eleventh name on the roster is Ruth Underhill. Soloist. And the article says, and I quote, 'unavailable for comment.'"

The film ran out of the reel with a soft mechanical clatter and the reader's screen went white.

Neither of them moved to reload it.

"So," Deen said. "I've spent eleven years, on and off, asking one question. What happened to Ruth Underhill on March thirteenth, 1974? And for eleven years the answer has been nothing, because Ruth Underhill never gave one. And now her granddaughter is sitting in my microfilm room for the third day in a row, reading the same coverage I've read a hundred times, and jumping out of her skin when I say hello." He kept his voice level. Gentle. He'd learned gentleness from his mother, who'd learned it from grief, and it was the only tool he had here. "I'm not accusing you of anything, Cyrus. I'm asking if you know something. Because if you do, you're the first person in fifty years who might."

* * *

He watched her decide to lie.

It happened in real time, and it was almost painful, the way a bad audition is painful—her chin lifting a fraction, her shoulders settling into a shape she thought looked casual. He'd seen this exact performance before. Witnesses did it. Kids did it. Once, an eighty-year-old man had done it to him over the disposition of a church bell, and Deen had let him, because some lies were kind. This one didn't look kind. This one looked like a woman carrying something heavy and insisting it was a handbag.

"I don't know anything about it," Cyrus said. "My grandmother didn't talk about— I mean, she didn't talk much about anything, at the end. She was old. I'm just—" a gesture at the blank white screen— "I heard about the fire growing up, everybody did, and I got curious. That's all. It's not a big deal."

"Sure," Deen said.

"It's not."

"I believe you're telling me it's not."

"I am telling you it's not." Too fast. Too flat. He could hear the rehearsed sentence underneath, and worse, he could hear the rehearsal cracking. "My grandmother's dead. I didn't know her that well. I'm doing a— a piece. For the station. Local history segment. That's all this is."

"A piece for the station."

"Local history. It's a slow news cycle."

He nodded slowly and let the silence do what silence does. He'd learned that from depositions, of all things—he'd sat through two days of them as a young man, over a property dispute involving his father's land, and the lawyer for the other side had taught him more about truth in two hours of silence than any sermon ever had. People fill silence. People fill it with the truth when they're honest and with more lies when they're not, and either way, they tell you which one they are.

Cyrus filled it with her hands. She reached for the coffee, drank, set it down, aligned the cup with the table edge.

"Which station?" he asked.

"What?"

"Which station is the piece for?"

A pause that was a half-second too long. "The one I work at. WKHL. The night show. I do the graveyard shift."

"Right. I've heard you. My wife used to fall asleep to you." He hadn't meant to say that—*used to*—and he watched the word land on her and saw her decide, generously, not to ask. "You have a good voice for it. Calm. You could read a grocery list and people would feel safe."

"Thank you."

"So here's my confusion," Deen said, and he leaned forward, elbows on his knees, because the gentleness was spent and what was left was just the truth, and the truth deserved a level playing field. "If this is a piece for your station—if it's curiosity, a slow news cycle—then why, on Tuesday, did you ask the front desk whether we had the coroner's report? Not the newspaper coverage. The coroner's report. Which we don't have, by the way, because it was never deposited here, which is its own strange thing. And why did you ask for the chapel's deed records? And when Marta told you we had a photograph of the choir—there's one, in the historical society's collection, I can show you—why did you go pale and say never mind?"

Cyrus said nothing.

"I catalogued this fire before you were born," Deen said. "Not literally. But it feels that way. I have three binders on it. I have the fire marshal's sketch. I have interviews with two surviving witnesses, both dead now, both of whom said the same strange thing and neither of whom would say it on the record." He stopped. He hadn't planned to say that part. It came out because she was looking at him now, really looking, the radio voice behind her eyes doing the math on whether he was safe, and something in him wanted—needed—to be worth the risk.

"What strange thing?" she asked.

He hesitated. Then: "Both of them said the singing didn't stop when the fire started. That's it. That's the whole strangeness. One of them, a woman named Etta Marsh—she was outside, she'd stepped out to smoke, God rest her—said the choir kept going through the whole thing. Through the screaming, if there was screaming. Through the windows breaking. She said they sounded, and this is her word, *steady*. Like a record. Like the fire was the thing out of step, not them." He turned his cup another quarter turn. "The other witness was a boy at the time. He said the same thing and then he said something Etta didn't. He said the song kept going for almost a minute after the roof came down. And then it stopped all at once, mid-note. Like someone lifted the needle."

The room's heater ticked. Somewhere in the stacks, the old building settled the way old buildings do, a long wooden sigh.

"That's a story people tell," Cyrus said quietly. "About fires. About any tragedy. The mind makes it—"

"I know what the mind makes," Deen said. "I've heard every rational version. I've made the rational versions myself, at two in the morning, in this room, with the lights off. I'm not asking you to believe a ghost story, Cyrus. I'm asking you to believe that I've been carrying this for a long time, alone, and that when you walked in here Tuesday and asked for the coroner's report with your hands shaking, I recognized something." He let that sit. "I recognized someone carrying it too."

* * *

She almost told him. He would swear to that, afterward, in the notes he kept—the private ones, the ones that weren't for any collection. She almost told him. Her mouth opened, closed, opened. Her eyes went bright and stayed that way, held at the brink by pure will, and he thought: *she's not deciding whether to tell me. She's deciding whether I can be trusted with it, and she's deciding no, and I don't know yet whether that's wisdom or fear.*

"My grandmother was a good woman," Cyrus said finally.

"I'm sure she was."

"She was. Whatever you think you know, whatever hole you've found in the record—she was good. She gave to the church her whole life. She sang at every funeral in this town for forty years, for free, in every weather. She sat with dying people so they wouldn't be alone. So whatever happened in that chapel, whatever the paper didn't say—" her voice did something then, a small fracture, quickly mortared over, the professional calm sliding on like a coat— "it wasn't what it looks like. People survive fires. It's not a crime."

"No," Deen said. "It's not a crime."

"And even if it was, she paid for it. Whatever it cost, she paid it. You didn't know her at the end." Cyrus stood, gathering her bag, her coat, her composure, in that order. "She didn't sleep. Did you know that? Forty years and she didn't sleep. She had a machine that recorded her at night because she was afraid of— I don't know what she was afraid of. She'd leave the radio on all night, low, every night, like she was listening for something. That's not a woman who got away with something. That's a woman doing time."

She stopped. He watched her hear her own words come back to her—*a machine that recorded her at night*—and watched the color leave her face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"Cyrus," Deen said carefully, "why did you say that?"

"I didn't— it's not— " She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth for a moment, and when she lowered it, the lie was back, but thinner, worn through at the knee. "Family stories. That's all. Look, Deen, you seem like a decent man, and I'm sure your binders are very thorough, but this is a dead story. Everyone in it is dead. There's nothing to find. I'm sorry I bothered your microfilm."

She was almost to the door when he said it. He hadn't planned this part either. He'd had a whole careful exit prepared—*my card's on the table, my door is always open*—but what came out instead was the thing underneath eleven years of careful cataloguing, the thing he'd only ever said aloud once, at his aunt's grave, to a headstone, in the rain.

"My aunt was in that loft," he said.

Cyrus stopped with her hand on the door.

"Comfort Okafor. Second alto. She was twenty-six." His voice came out lower than he intended. "My mother's older sister. My mother never went to a choir practice again in her life. She never sang a note, not in church, not in the car, not humming. Fifty years, Cyrus. I grew up in a house where music was a wound. And the official record says my aunt died in a stove fire, and the record has a hole in it, and the one person who could fill that hole spent her whole life being *unavailable for comment*." He stood. "So no. I'm not going to drop it. I'm sorry. I've been polite about it for a decade and it hasn't gotten me anywhere, and now the granddaughter of the only witness is in my reading room with shaking hands and a lie she can't finish, and I would have to be a dead man not to notice."

Cyrus turned around. Her eyes were wet now, fully, no brink about it, and her jaw was set the way Ruth's used to set—Deen remembered it from a hundred reference desk conversations, that Underhill stubbornness, the bell-voice family, the family that held its notes.

"I can't help you," she said.

"Okay."

"I mean it. I can't. Not yet. Maybe not ever."

"Okay," he said again, softer.

"But if I could," she said, and here her voice did the thing that radio voices do, the thing that made strangers call her at 2 a.m. and tell her their secrets, the thing that was, he suddenly understood, the family inheritance in its purest form— "if I ever could. Would it matter? After all this time. Would knowing actually give anyone anything, or would it just be one more weight for this town to carry?"

Deen thought about his mother's silent house. About Etta Marsh, who'd asked him, near the end, whether the fire was ever going to *let go of her*. About the boy witness, who'd grown into an old man who wouldn't sit in any room with a piano in it.

"It would give them back their names," he said. "The nine. Right now they're a paragraph and a photograph of a burned building. They were people. They had parts to sing—alto, tenor, my aunt stood second from the left and she laughed through her whole nose, it drove the director crazy. Knowing would give them that. It wouldn't fix anything. But it would *name* them. And around here, Cyrus, an unnamed thing doesn't rest. It just waits."

She stood there a long moment, hand still on the door, and he watched the door behind her eyes—the one she'd been holding shut since Tuesday—shudder once, hard, like something on the other side had pushed.

"Your binders," she said. "The three of them. Could I see them?"

Hope went through him like a struck bell. "Tonight, if you want."

"Not tonight." She opened the door. Cold air came in off the street, and with it, faintly, from somewhere up the block—a sound he couldn't place at first, and then could: a car radio, someone's car parked outside with its windows down, playing a hymn. Something old. Something in a minor key he almost recognized. "Tomorrow," Cyrus said. "Tomorrow I'll come by, and you'll show me everything, and I'll—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. "I'll see what I can tell you."

She went out. The bell over the door gave its small tired ring, and the hymn from the car radio swelled for a moment as she passed through it, and Deen—standing alone in his reading room, with his cold coffee and his eleven years—realized two things at once.

The first was that Cyrus Underhill was lying, badly, and that the lie had a shape, and the shape was protection. Not of herself. He'd have bet the binders on it. She was protecting a dead woman's name, and losing, and she knew she was losing, and she'd asked to see his binders anyway.

The second thing was the hymn.

He crossed to the window in time to see her old sedan pull away from the curb, and the car radio next door was still going, still playing that slow minor-key hymn, and Deen stood very still in the fading light and tried to place where he knew it from. Not from church. Not from any hymnal he'd catalogued. From the transcripts—one of them. Etta Marsh's interview, page four, the sentence he'd read so many times he'd stopped hearing it:

*It wasn't a hymn I knew. I asked around after. Nobody knew it. Etta Marsh said the choir was singing something she'd never heard before, and never heard again.*

Except, apparently, someone was playing it in a parked car on Corbett Street, outside the library, at dusk, on a Tuesday.

Deen watched the car. Its windows were down. Its driver's seat was empty.

He reached for his coat, and his hands were the ones shaking now.
