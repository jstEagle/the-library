## 2. Documentation Is a Kind of Prayer

The house smelled the way it had always smelled — woodsmoke and beeswax and something faintly medicinal that no one had ever been able to identify, though Mother insisted it was the lavender sachets and everyone else privately suspected the plumbing. Juniper stood in the doorway with two suitcases and a camera bag and let the smell do what smells do, which is to say: it reached back through eleven years and closed its hand around their throat.

"Right," they said aloud, to nobody, because saying things aloud was how Juniper Eriksen kept from coming apart at the seams. "Inventory first. Grief later."

They had a system for this. They had a system for everything, which was either a virtue or a symptom depending on who you asked — Wendell had always said *both*, cheerfully, while Juniper alphabetized the spice rack for the third time in a month. The system was simple: photograph everything before touching anything. Catalogue everything before moving anything. If an object was documented, then it existed somewhere outside of time and loss, and if it existed outside of time and loss, then losing it — or being lost to it — was not entirely the end.

It was not a religion, exactly. But Juniper had stopped pretending, around age nineteen, that it wasn't a kind of prayer.

*Here is the thing. Here is where it sat. Here is whose hand last held it.* Say it enough times and it almost sounds like grace.

They started, sensibly, with the kitchen. The kettle was on the stove where it always was, limescale blooming inside like some pale coral reef. There were two mugs by the sink — one chipped blue, one whole and white — and Juniper photographed them both before allowing themselves to understand why there were two. One was Wendell's. Of course it was. Wendell had owned exactly four possessions he cared about: the blue mug, a fountain pen with a cracked nib he refused to replace, a wool scarf the color of wet slate, and a pocket watch that didn't run but which he wound every morning anyway, out of what he called "courtesy."

Juniper photographed the mug from three angles. Then they made tea in it, because it seemed worse somehow to leave it empty, and drank tea out of their dead brother's mug while standing at the counter where he'd stood, and did not cry, because crying would come later, scheduled, after the inventory. This was the arrangement. This was the covenant Juniper had made with themselves somewhere over the North Sea, somewhere between the airport and the ferry and the long bus ride into the hills where Hallowmere sat in its bowl of grey weather like a stone in the bottom of a cupped palm.

*Documentation is a kind of prayer,* they wrote in the notebook, underlining it twice. *And I am going to be so devout.*

* * *

By mid-afternoon they had catalogued the sitting room (forty-one objects of note, including a taxidermy trout of uncertain provenance that had hung over the sideboard since before either twin was born and which Wendell had named Gerald), the hallway (nine), and the downstairs washroom (three, all of them embarrassing). The light came and went through the windows in the manner of Hallowmere light generally, which never quite committed to being daylight, as if the sky here had signed some old agreement to only ever offer its best work grudgingly.

It was when Juniper carried the camera bag up the stairs that the house went quiet in a new way.

Not silent — houses are never silent; they tick and settle and mutter in their joists — but quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when someone has just stopped talking about you. Juniper stood on the landing with their hand on the banister and felt the upstairs listening.

"Wendell's room," they said aloud, and their voice came out smaller than intended.

The door was shut. It was never shut. In thirty years of shared childhood, Wendell's door had been shut perhaps a dozen times, and each of those occasions had been catastrophic — the flu of 2009, the Great Frog Incident, the week he'd tried to teach himself the accordion. An open door was Wendell's entire philosophy of life. He'd once propped open the bathroom door with his foot while brushing his teeth purely, he claimed, "on principle."

Juniper turned the handle. It wasn't locked. It simply hadn't been opened in a while, and the hinges said so, one long low note like a church organ warming up.

Dust.

Dust everywhere — not the polite film of a room left alone for a few weeks, but a genuine settlement, a soft grey snowfall across every horizontal surface, thick on the desk, thick on the bookshelves, thick on the model ships Wendell had built and named after women in Greek myths he'd gotten slightly wrong. Dust lay on the pillow in a smooth unbroken sheet, and Juniper found that detail lodged in them like a splinter: no one had slept here. Not recently. Not for a long time.

But the dust told a stranger story than that.

From the bed, a path — clean, swept bare, a corridor of exposed floorboard maybe eighteen inches wide — ran from the bedside to the hearth. Not to the door. Not to the window or the wardrobe or any place a person might reasonably go. To the hearth, where the old family portrait hung above the mantel, and where the floorboards gleamed faintly, as if polished by feet.

Juniper stood in the doorway for a long moment, camera hanging forgotten at their side.

People walked in their sleep sometimes. That was a thing. Wendell had talked in his sleep constantly — full sentences, mostly complaints — and once, memorably, had walked the entire length of the garden in his pajamas to have a stern word with the compost heap. Sleepwalking happened. Grieving people paced. A man pacing his own room at night, wearing a track between his bed and the fire, was sad, not strange.

Except the path wasn't worn. Worn paths took years. This one was *clean*, as though someone had swept it, deliberately, recently, with care — a neat channel through the dust, edges crisp as a paper cut.

And except that Wendell had died in the hospital in the city, forty miles away, with Juniper holding his hand and a nurse quietly mispronouncing both their names.

Juniper raised the camera. Their hands were steadier than they expected. They photographed the room in a grid, methodical, corner to corner — the dust, the path, the bed, the ships (Andromeda, Cassandra, and one labeled *Helen?* in Wendell's handwriting with a question mark, because even his models doubted themselves) — and then, last, the hearth.

The portrait looked down at them the way it had looked down at every Eriksen for a hundred and fourteen years: a stern man in a dark coat, painted in 1911 according to the brass plate, standing in front of a church with his hands folded and his face set in the expression of someone who has agreed to something and regrets it already.

Juniper had hated the portrait as a child. Wendell had loved it. He used to stand beneath it as a boy and hold conversations with it, and when Juniper teased him he'd said, with the grave certainty of an eight-year-old, "He listens better than you do."

"He looks like he's waiting for the bus," Juniper had said.

"He looks like a man who knows how the story ends," Wendell had said, "and isn't happy about it."

Click. Click. Click. Juniper photographed the portrait too, because the portrait belonged to the room and the room belonged to the inventory, and the inventory was the prayer, and the prayer was the only thing keeping their hands from shaking.

Then they noticed the dust again — on the mantel this time. On the mantel, the dust lay thick everywhere except directly below the portrait, in a rectangle exactly the painting's width and height, where the wood was clean. As if the portrait had recently been lifted down and put back up. Recently. Very recently.

Or as if the portrait had been taken down often. Regularly. Nightly, perhaps, by someone walking a swept path from the bed to the fire, in a room where no one had slept.

Juniper lowered the camera and became aware, all at once, of the hour. The light through the curtains had gone the color of weak tea. Downstairs, somewhere, a clock they'd forgotten to catalogue began to strike five, and the sound of it moved through the house like a finger dragged along piano strings.

Five o'clock. Dusk came early here, especially in autumn. Astrid Vold had mentioned something about dusk on the walk from the bus stop — something about the bell, something Juniper had filed away as village eccentricity, the way you file away a neighbor who talks to her hedge.

"Well," Juniper said to the empty room, in the bright, brittle voice they used for difficult phone calls. "That's fine. All of that is completely fine."

Nothing answered. But the portrait, in the fading light, seemed to lean very slightly forward on its nail.

* * *

Juniper did what Juniper always did with fear, which was to make tea and a list.

The list, written in the notebook under the heading *ANOMALIES — W'S ROOM*, read:

1. Dust consistent with months of vacancy. No one has slept there.
2. Swept path, bed → hearth. Edges too clean for wear. Deliberate.
3. Mantel dust disturbed in rectangle matching portrait dimensions.
4. Door closed. (Wendell never closed doors. See: entire childhood.)
5. I am tired and grieving and rooms are dusty and people are strange. Consider mundane explanations FIRST.

Item five was important. Item five was the item Juniper wrote at the end of every anomalous list they'd ever compiled, going back to the summer they were twelve and became briefly convinced the postman was stealing their thoughts. *Consider mundane explanations first.* It was the scientific method applied to a nervous disposition, and it had served them well for twenty years.

The mundane explanations were available and generous. The house had been empty for weeks while Wendell was ill; dust accumulates; doors drift shut in old houses; a draught could move dust in patterns; grief makes shapes out of nothing, the same way clouds make shapes out of nothing, and nobody calls an exorcist about a cloud shaped like a rabbit.

Juniper repeated these to themselves while the kettle heated, and mostly believed them, in the way you mostly believe a rope bridge will hold.

Mother was asleep in the back room — sleeping at five in the afternoon, which was new, though the doctors had warned them her rhythms would be strange for a while. Strange was the operative word for everything now. Juniper had last seen her at the funeral, upright and grey-faced and gripping Juniper's arm with a strength that had frightened them more than the coffin had. She'd looked at Juniper during the service with an expression Juniper still hadn't decoded — not grief exactly, or not only grief. Something closer to a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

She was sleeping now, breathing slow and even, and Juniper stood in her doorway for a moment watching the rise and fall of the blanket and thinking: *she knew him for sixty-three years and I knew him for thirty-one and neither of us got enough, and that is the whole of it, that is the entire crime, there is no mystery here worth having.*

Then, from the direction of the village, thin and far off, came a sound.

Juniper went still.

It took a moment to resolve into sense — it was so improbable that the mind kept trying to file it elsewhere, wind, water pipes, a truck reversing somewhere over the hill. But it was none of those things. It was a bell. A single, deep, unhurried toll, rolling up from the church at the bottom of the village and through the walls of the house and into the bones of anyone standing in a doorway at dusk.

One toll. Then silence, complete and enormous.

Juniper stood in the hallway with their heart going hard and their notebook in one hand and thought, absurdly, of Wendell winding his broken pocket watch every morning. *Courtesy,* he'd called it. Keeping faith with a thing that no longer worked.

"That's not possible," they said to the hallway, quietly. "That bell hasn't existed since 1911. I read the plaque. I photograph plaques. It's what I *do*."

The house said nothing. Upstairs, faintly, through the ceiling, came the small particular creak of a floorboard taking weight — one step, from the direction of the bed toward the hearth, and then nothing more.

Juniper looked at the stairs for a long time.

Then they did the bravest thing they knew how to do, which was to write it down. They opened the notebook to a fresh page, dated it, and wrote, in their smallest, most controlled handwriting:

*Dusk. Heard one toll of a bell that was melted for scrap 113 years ago. Heard one footstep overhead in a room where no one is. Documented anyway. Because documentation is a kind of prayer, and tonight, God help me, I need someone to be listening.*

They underlined it twice.

Above them, the house settled, sighed, and waited — patient as paint — for dark.
