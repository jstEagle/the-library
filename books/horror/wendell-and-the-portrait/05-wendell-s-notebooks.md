## 5. Wendell's Notebooks

The floorboard in Wendell's room was the only one that didn't creak.

I want to be clear about how strange that is. Every board in that house had a voice. The third step of the stairs sang a low B-flat when you stepped on the left side and a slightly offended C when you stepped on the right — I know this because our mother used to time us coming down for dinner by it, and Wendell had spent an entire childhood perfecting a silent descent, which she called cheating and he called *applied physics*. The hallway boards groaned like old men asked to move furniture. Even the kitchen floor had opinions, mostly about damp.

But the board at the foot of Wendell's bed said nothing. Ever.

I'd noticed it on my second night in the house, cataloguing his things. I was working through the room methodically — photographing everything before touching it, then photographing it again after, because if documentation is a kind of prayer then redundancy is a kind of devotion — and every time I crossed from the door toward the hearth, one plank beneath me stayed perfectly, unnaturally quiet. Not loose. Not repaired. Just *quiet*, the way a person goes quiet when they're hiding something.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself a lot of things that week. That the dust path from bed to hearth was just where Wendell had walked most often, though why a grown man would walk the same route four hundred times a week was a question I kept folding up and putting in a drawer. That the portrait's eyes were exactly where oil paint leaves them, which is to say following you, which is what eyes do. That the bell I sometimes thought I heard at dusk was wind in the empty tower, even though Astrid Vold had shown me the parish record with her own hands — *Bell removed and melted for scrap, Michaelmas 1911* — and Astrid Vold did not make mistakes with records. She made mistakes with casseroles, once, memorably, but never with records.

So on the fifth morning I knelt at the foot of Wendell's bed with a flathead screwdriver and apologized out loud to the house, the way you do, and pried up the quiet board.

Underneath there was a cavity lined with an old wool blanket, gone grey with age, and inside the blanket: notebooks. Eleven of them. School exercise books, the cheap marbled kind we'd both been issued at Hallowmere Primary, except these were filled edge to edge in Wendell's handwriting — small, upright, patient, the handwriting of someone who expected to be read carefully and intended to deserve it.

I sat down on the floor with my back against his bed frame, which still smelled faintly of him — cedar and engine oil and the peppermints he was never without — and I began to read.

* * *

Wendell numbered his notebooks the way librarians number volumes, and dated every entry twice, which I understood immediately because I do it too: the calendar date, and then the count. He counted days the way some people count money or sins. When I got to the last notebook I would understand what the count was counting, and I would wish very hard for a few minutes that I hadn't.

But the first notebook started innocently enough. *Mum's bad again,* it began. *Dr. Praagh says her heart is "managing." I looked up what doctors mean when they say managing. They mean losing.*

Our mother's illness had been the weather of our childhood — always present, sometimes storming. Ten years of it, off and on. Bad winters, worse springs. I had moved away for work; Wendell had stayed, and I had told myself that staying was its own kind of gift and not thought much beyond that, because thinking beyond that meant thinking about what I'd chosen instead, and I wasn't ready, and then there was no more time to be ready.

*I sat with her Tuesday while she slept,* the second notebook went on. *She woke up confused and asked me if I was Juniper. I said no. She seemed relieved, which stung, and then she said "good, Juniper shouldn't see me like this," which stung differently. I'm keeping notes on all of it. If I write it down it belongs to me and not to the fear.*

That was so exactly him that I laughed, alone on the floor of a dead man's room, and the laugh came out wrong, like a hiccup with ambitions.

He documented everything. Her medications, her pulse, the exact words Dr. Praagh used and the exact pauses between them. He photographed her pill bottles the way I photograph everything, and I felt a strange vertigo seeing my own habits in his hand — we'd never discussed it, the documenting, never named it as anything, and yet here it was in triplicate across two lifetimes: two twins who had each independently decided that the way to survive an uncontrollable world was to write it down until it held still.

Then, halfway through the third notebook, the entries changed.

*I found something in the attic.*

I remember reading that line three times. My thumb had gone cold on the page.

*It's the old portrait. The stern one, the 1911 man. It was wrapped in sailcloth behind the water tank. I don't know who put it there. Mum says it's always hung over the hearth and she's right, it has, but I have a memory — maybe a dream — of Dad carrying something heavy up the attic stairs the winter before he died, and telling me not to follow. I think he put it up there. I think he knew.*

*I brought it down. I don't fully know why. It wanted to come down. That's a stupid thing to write. I'm writing it anyway.*

Below that, the handwriting changed. Not dramatically — Wendell's hand was too disciplined for drama — but the letters leaned a degree or two further forward, like a man walking into wind.

*I've been sitting with it in the evenings. Talking to it, God help me. It listens better than most people. And last night it answered.*

*Not in words. In pictures. I'll describe them as precisely as I can, because precision is the only weapon I've ever owned:*

*A picture of Mum, well. Walking in the garden. Carrying washing. Laughing at something I couldn't hear.*

*A picture of me, older. Grey at the temples.*

*A picture of a church bell, whole and ringing.*

*And underneath all three, like a signature, a picture of a ledger with a long column of figures, and a blank space at the bottom waiting for a name.*

*I said yes.*

*I don't know yet what I said yes TO. I know that's insane. I know I should have come to you, June, I should have called, I should have — but you'd have talked me out of it, and I couldn't be talked out of it, because when I saw her laughing in that garden I understood that I would have said yes to anything, to anyone, to the devil himself holding a clipboard.*

*Mum was better within the month. The doctors keep using the word "remarkable." She planted tomatoes. She hasn't planted tomatoes since I was nine.*

There was a gap of several pages here — torn out, deliberately, the stubs still visible — and when the entries resumed, they had changed again. Quieter. Sadder. A man writing from inside something he could no longer see the edges of.

*It's taking payment. I understand that now. I thought the bargain was mine to give. It isn't. The bargain was an opening. What comes through it sets its own price.*

*People in the village are dying. Small deaths, quiet ones. Old Halvard at the mill, and everyone said it was his heart, and maybe it was, but his face — Praagh wrote "peaceful" in the report and I wanted to shake him. There's a difference between peaceful and EMPTY. I've seen both on the dead. You don't forget the difference.*

*The bell rang last night. I heard it. Nobody else did. Or nobody else admitted it.*

*It does not care which twin. It only cares that the count reaches one hundred.*

I read that sentence and then I read it again and then I set the notebook face-down on my knee and looked at the hearth, where the portrait hung, and the portrait looked back at me the way it always did, patiently, the way a spider looks at a fly that has stopped struggling out of mere exhaustion rather than acceptance.

Because here is what I had not let myself understand until that moment, kneeling in the dust of my brother's hidden life: the final entries were not about him anymore.

They were about me.

* * *

The later notebooks were harder. Not because the handwriting deteriorated — Wendell's hand held steady to the end, that terrible discipline — but because the content of it became a map of a man reasoning himself, rung by rung, down a ladder he could see was standing over an abyss.

*Day 41 of the count. I've worked out the arithmetic. Astrid Vold keeps the parish death registers and she'll show them to anyone who asks, because nobody ever asks. I asked. Ninety-four unexplained deaths in Hallowmere since 1911. Ninety-four. All of them quiet. All of them "peaceful." All of them with faces like wet paint smoothed flat.*

*Ninety-four plus Mum's illness, which should have killed her six times over — that's ninety-five debts paid. The portrait wants one hundred. It has wanted one hundred for a hundred years. The priest in 1911 opened the account; I can feel him in it somehow, a thin anxious presence, like a smell of candle wax. He traded the bell. I traded — I still don't know exactly what I traded. I think I traded a twin. I think the portrait accepted the currency and is now deciding which coin to take.*

*It does not care which twin. It only cares that the count reaches one hundred.*

*I've tried to warn you without warning you. I know how that sounds. But every time I pick up the phone to call you, I see the ledger again, and your name is already half-written in it, in my handwriting, and I hang up. Cowardice, probably. Or love. With me those have always lived in the same house and argued about the rent.*

I had to stop there. I pressed the notebook closed against my chest and breathed the way our mother had taught us to breathe during her worst attacks — in for four, hold for seven, out for eight — a child's trick that had become a family inheritance, grief passed down as respiratory technique.

Betrayal is a strange word for what I felt, because betrayal requires a traitor, and Wendell had not betrayed me. He had *tried*. Page after page, notebook after notebook, he had tried to build me a ladder out of the same pit he was standing in, and he had failed, and the failure was not cowardice, whatever he called it. The failure was that some pits are dug faster than ladders can be built.

But heartbreak doesn't check its paperwork. Heartbreak arrived anyway, on schedule, and sat down beside me on the dusty floor, and put its arm around my shoulders like a twin would, and said: *he knew. He knew and he let you walk back into this village blind, to bury him, to stand at a grave with the wrong name on it, and he never sent the letter, never made the call.* And then another voice — quieter, steadier, his voice — said: *read the rest. He left you the rest for a reason. He always finished what he started. Read the rest.*

So I read the rest.

* * *

The tenth notebook was almost empty. Three entries.

*It offered me a choice today. First time it's spoken clearly. It showed me two pictures. In the first, I go into the paint and the count stops at ninety-nine and holds, as long as I hold it. In the second, I run — it says I can run, it says running is permitted, it says that almost kindly — and the count resumes, and the next coin it takes is the other twin, and it will not stop at one hundred, it will stop whenever it is full, and it is very hungry.*

*I asked it what happens at one hundred. It showed me the garden again. Mum, laughing. Everyone laughing. The whole village warm and fed and lucky forever, the way it was supposed to be in 1911 before the famine ate the harvest and the priest got desperate. One hundred souls buys a century of luck. That's the exchange rate. That's always been the exchange rate.*

*I asked it what happens to the hundred.*

*It showed me the varnish. Close up. I won't write down what's in the varnish. Some things should only exist in one head at a time, and I am apparently the current custodian.*

*June — if you're reading this, and you are, because you're reading this, you're the only person alive who reads things properly — I need you to know that I held the phone. Every night. I held it until the plastic went soft in my hand. I want that in the record. Whatever else is true, I want THAT in the record.*

I put my hand flat on the page, over the words, the way you'd press a hand to a wound or a forehead. The paper was cool. Everything in that room was cool except the air near the hearth, which had a warmth to it that had no business existing in an unused house in October, a warmth like breath, like attention, like something leaning close over my shoulder to see how far I'd read.

"Almost done," I said aloud, to it, to him, to the room. My voice sounded younger than I wanted it to.

The eleventh notebook was the thinnest. One entry, undated, the double-date line left blank — and that absence frightened me more than anything written in the previous ten, because Wendell Eriksen did not leave lines blank. Wendell Eriksen dotted his i's like a man signing testimony.

*Final entry. I've decided. Tomorrow at dusk I go to the hearth and I offer myself in place of the count — not INTO the count, that's the trick of it, I finally see the trick — I offer myself as the DOOR. If I hold it shut from inside, the hundredth soul can't be taken, and a count that can't finish is a debt that can't collect. It agreed. It agrees too easily. I don't care. Mum lives. You live. The village limps on.*

*Rules I've learned, for whoever finds these:*

*One. It cannot take what is freely refused. It will rage about this. It rages quietly, in paint.*

*Two. It feeds on unspoken debt. Say things out loud. Confess everything. It hates daylight and honesty, which makes it the opposite of us, which is the only comfort I have.*

*Three. Do not look at it after dark. (Mum is right. Mum is always right. Tell her I said so, she'll be insufferable.)*

*Four. If the bell rings and you hear it, you're already part of the ledger. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I chose this with my eyes open and my heart full and I would choose it again, and I know that's not forgiveness, I'm not asking for forgiveness, I'm asking for the record.*

*Five. June. Juniper. Listen. It does not care which twin. It only cares that the count reaches one hundred. So be the twin it cannot count. Be the refusal. Be the door it can't open. I'll hold it from my side. Hold it from yours.*

*Don't come after me.*

*— W.E., who was here, and was glad, and was sorry, and loved you past the edge of the page*

The house made a sound. Not a creak — none of its usual opinions. Something lower, and closer, and pleased.

I looked up. The light through the window had gone the color of brass, which meant dusk was coming, which meant the hour when our mother had begged me — gripping my wrist with her new-strong, impossible, tomato-planting hands — *not to look at it after dark.*

The portrait hung above the hearth, and the painted man's face was my brother's, and my brother's painted mouth was smiling, and Wendell had never smiled like that, not once in thirty years, not even at his own jokes, especially not at his own jokes.

And beneath the smile, so faint I might have invented it, the canvas was moving. Breathing. Counting.

Somewhere below the floorboards, under eleven notebooks and a dead man's careful hand, the house began — very softly, very patiently — to tick.
