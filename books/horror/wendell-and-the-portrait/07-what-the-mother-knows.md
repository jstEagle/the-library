## 7. What the Mother Knows

My mother was humming when I came downstairs.

I want to be clear about what that means in the Eriksen house. For eleven years, Maren Eriksen had not hummed. She had wheezed, and whispered, and on her better days produced a thin, papery voice suitable for asking whether someone had remembered to close the gate. Humming was a sound from another century of our family — from photographs where she stood with her hands on Wendell's shoulders, both twins squinting into a sun that seemed to belong entirely to her.

Now she stood at the stove in her dressing gown, stirring porridge, humming something I almost recognized. A hymn, maybe. Or half of one. The melody kept stopping at the same place, as if it had forgotten how it ended, or as if it had never been taught.

"You're up," she said, without turning. "Good. You've been sleeping like the dead, Juniper, and given the circumstances that's an unfortunate expression."

"It's seven in the morning."

"It's nearly ten." She tapped the spoon against the pot's rim, twice, a small bright punctuation. "You were up all night with those notebooks of yours. Don't look surprised. The floorboards in this house have opinions, and they share them freely."

I had hidden Wendell's notebooks under my mattress, wrapped in a pillowcase, like contraband. Apparently the house disagreed about what counted as hiding.

She ladled porridge into two bowls — two, when for eleven years there had only ever been one — and set them on the table with the particular ceremony of a woman who has decided that today will be a normal day and will brook no argument on the matter. There was honey. We had not had honey since before her diagnosis. Honey, in Hallowmere in November, was either a miracle or a message.

"Sit," she said. "Eat. You've gone thin. Grief is no excuse for poor nutrition; your grandmother buried three husbands and never missed a meal."

"Grandmother Eriksen buried three husbands?"

"Two husbands and a brother, but she always said the third husband didn't count because he ran off to Hull." She sat across from me, folded her hands, and looked at me with an expression I could not place until I did, and then wished I hadn't: it was patience. The deep, settled patience of someone waiting for weather to change.

I ate the porridge. It would have been rude not to, and besides, my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

Here is what I knew by morning light, laid out in my head like documents on a desk:

Wendell had made a bargain. One twin's life, traded for our mother's recovery. The portrait did not care which twin. The count was meant to reach one hundred. And my mother — who had been dying so long that dying had become simply what she *was*, the way some houses are drafty and some dogs are old — was stirring porridge and humming and worrying about my appetite.

The arithmetic was not complicated. It was, in fact, the simplest arithmetic I had ever done, and I hated every digit of it.

"Mother," I said. "When did you get better?"

She smiled into her bowl. "Tuesday."

"Tuesday."

"The doctor came Tuesday. Dr. Pram, with his little bag and his little stethoscope he keeps warm in his pocket, bless him. He listened to my chest for a long time and then he sat down on the edge of my bed and cried a bit, which embarrassed us both. He said my lungs were clear. He said it twice, as though the first time might have been a clerical error." She laughed — laughed! — a small round sound. "He asked me what I'd been eating. I said porridge. He wrote it down. Doctors are just documentarians with better handwriting than you, my love."

I let that pass. It deserved better than to be a weapon, and I was saving my weapons.

"And before Tuesday?" I asked. "What do you remember of before Tuesday?"

The humming stopped. Not abruptly — it thinned out, note by note, the way a bell stops ringing, which is a comparison I was by now making far too often and would make for the rest of my life.

"I remember being tired," she said carefully. "I remember the winter, and the spring, and the doctor's face doing that thing faces do when they are delivering numbers. And then—" She turned her spoon over in the bowl. "There's a gap. A soft gap, like a page torn out. I remember Wendell sitting with me. Reading to me. He read me that dreadful book about the whaling ship, all four hundred pages, doing the voices. And I remember thinking his voice sounded strange, and I remember being cold, and then it was Tuesday and I wanted honey on my porridge."

"Wendell died Wednesday," I said. "The day after Tuesday."

"Yes." She said it steadily. Too steadily. Steadiness is a thing people build, plank by plank, when the alternative is falling down. "Yes, I know. Isn't that the way of it. He held on just long enough to see me well. That's very like him. Your brother never could leave a job half-finished."

"Mother—"

"Eat your porridge, Juniper."

"Mother, I found his notebooks."

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, a crow said something rude to another crow, and even the crows seemed to regret the timing.

She did not ask what notebooks. That was the thing I noticed most, later, when I replayed this morning the way I replayed everything: she did not say *what notebooks*, or *Wendell kept notebooks?*, or any of the sentences an innocent woman reaches for. She set down her spoon, aligned it parallel to the bowl's edge with a small precise click, and looked at me with the patience again, the weather-waiting patience, and said:

"How much did you read?"

"All of it."

"Then you know."

"I know what he wrote." My voice came out lower than I intended. "I know he went to the portrait. I know he offered himself. I know it took him instead of — no. No, that's not right, is it? It didn't take him. It took *me*. The certificate says Juniper Eriksen. The stone says Juniper Eriksen. Father Aldous prayed over a grave with my name on it while I stood in the back row holding flowers, and everyone looked at me like I was the one who should apologize for attending." I heard my own voice climbing and made myself come down. "So tell me how it works, Mother. Tell me exactly how it works, because Wendell's handwriting gets very small at the end, and small handwriting is where people hide the things they can't bear to write large."

* * *

She rose, then. She went to the dresser — the good dresser, the one with the locked drawer that had been locked my entire childhood, the drawer we children had once hypothesized contained either silver or a human skull — and she took a small iron key from around her neck, where it had hung against her skin for years beneath her nightgown, and she unlocked it.

Inside: no silver, no skull. A bundle of letters tied with green ribbon, and beneath them, a photograph.

It was the portrait. Photographed, decades ago, in black and white. The stern ancestor, painted 1911, the year the bell went to the scrapyard. But in the photograph the painted man's face was different — softer, younger, a face I had seen in the parish records Astrid showed me, the face of the ferryman who had sat for it. And on the back, in my mother's hand, faded brown ink:

*M. asks what we owe. E. says nothing we haven't already paid.*

"Maren," I read aloud. "Maren and E. Who are they?"

"My grandmother," she said, "and her brother Edvard. Edvard drowned in the ferry accident the winter the famine ended. The winter after the bell was melted." She set the photograph down between us like a card played in a slow game. "The family survived that winter, Juniper. All of us. Every Eriksen under that roof, and the Volds next door, and half the village that ate from our table because we had somehow, impossibly, enough. My grandmother used to say we were rich in a currency she wouldn't name. She said it with her mouth smiling and her eyes somewhere else."

"You're telling me this has been going on for a hundred years."

"I'm telling you it has been going on longer than the word *going*." She sat back down. Her hands were steady. Mine were not, and she reached across and covered one of them, and her hands were warm — warm, when for eleven years her touch had been cool as river stones, and I flinched, and hated myself for flinching. "Let me tell it properly. You're a documentarian. So document."

And she told it.

How her own mother had told her, at sixteen, by the hearth, in the tone other mothers used for explaining menstruation or death: plainly, briefly, with a warning attached. How the portrait was not a picture of a man but a *doorway* shaped like a man, and doorways do not care who walks through them, only that traffic continues. How the family's luck — the harvests that came in, the fevers that passed over our roofline like weather around a hill, the money that never quite ran out no matter how badly anyone managed it — was not luck but *balance*. Something paid in. Something owed.

"When I got sick," she said, "I knew what it meant. I thought — this is the bill coming due. Our line's account is empty. And I made my peace with it, mostly. I got the good sheets out. I wrote letters. I told Wendell not to be silly about it, and he agreed with me in that way he agrees with people, nodding along, doing absolutely the opposite."

"He was twenty-six."

"He was mine." For the first time her voice cracked, one clean hairline fracture, and then she repaired it in front of me, quickly, the way you'd patch a wall before company came. "He was my son and he was dying of watching me die, and one night in October he sat on my bed and read me the whale book and did the voices, and afterward he kissed my forehead and said, *Sleep, Mam, I'll sort it.* And I lay there knowing — *knowing*, Juniper, the way you know weather, the way you know when milk's about to turn — knowing what he'd gone to do. And I let him."

"Why?" It came out of me like something breaking. "Why would you *let* him—"

"Because he asked me to!" And there she was, my mother, all the patience burned off at last, leaning across the table with her eyes wet and furious. "Because he came back from the parlor at dawn and he knelt by my bed and he said, *It's done, and don't you dare undo it, and don't you dare tell her,* and he looked at me the way your father used to look at me when he'd spent the grocery money on something foolish and wonderful, daring me to be angry, begging me to be angry, and I couldn't. I couldn't. He gave me Tuesdays, Juniper. He gave me honey and mornings and the doctor crying on my bed, and you want me to call that a crime?"

"It *is* a crime. It's a crime with a ledger, and the ledger isn't finished!"

"No," she said softly. "No, it isn't."

And there it was. The silence after it was worse than anything she could have said, because I watched her arrive at it ahead of me — watched her walk calmly into the sentence I had been circling all morning — and she did not look away.

"The count," I said. "Ninety-seven souls, Astrid says. Ninety-eight now, with the blacksmith's boy. Wendell's bargain left it short. One twin was promised, and one twin was taken, and the portrait counts everything, and it is still hungry, and there is one twin left."

"Juniper—"

"Don't." I stood up. The chair legs shrieked against the flagstones. "Don't say it. Whatever you're about to say, whatever you've been building toward all morning with your porridge and your honey and your *eat, you've gone thin* — don't."

She stood too. She came around the table, and she took my face in her warm, warm hands, hands that had been cool for eleven years and were warm now because my brother had bought the warmth with his life, and she said it anyway, gently, terribly, the way you'd say a grace:

"One twin for the family. That was the terms. That was always the terms. Your grandmother's brother understood it, and Wendell understood it, and they went willingly because they loved us, and I am asking you — I am *begging* you, my darling, my last child — to understand it too. Because I cannot lose this house. I cannot lose the luck. I have been dead for eleven years and I have only just gotten back, and I will not go back into that gray country, and I will not watch the harvests fail and the fevers find us and the money run out like it does for everyone else, like it does for people who are merely *people*." Her thumbs moved along my cheekbones, tender as bandaging. "You think I don't know what I'm saying? You think I don't hear it? I hear it. I hear every word and I'm saying it anyway, and God forgive me, I mean it."

"Then God should forgive both of us," I whispered, "because I mean it too. I'm not going into that paint, Mother. I'm going to burn it."

Something crossed her face then — quick as a fish under ice. Not fear. Something older and sadder than fear. Pity, maybe. The look you give someone who has announced they will fight the tide with a teacup.

"Oh, my love," she said. "You can't burn what owns the fire."

Outside, far off toward the church, a crow lifted from the tower and the whole grey sky seemed to lean a little closer to the house. And beneath the floorboards, beneath the kitchen, beneath the hearthstone where the family had warmed itself for a hundred paid-for years, I felt — or imagined I felt, though I have stopped trusting that distinction — something turn over in its sleep, and begin, patiently, to listen.

* * *

That night I photographed the photograph of the portrait, front and back, and the letters, and my mother's face while she slept — documentation as prayer, prayer as armor. And when I developed the print of her sleeping face, in the red dark of the bathroom, I stood over the tray a long time before I understood what I was looking at.

In the photograph, my mother's expression was peaceful.

Her mouth was moving.

Three words, silent on film, caught mid-formation by the shutter's blind eye. I read them off the glossy paper under the red lamp, over and over, until the words stopped looking like words.

*I'm sorry, Juniper.*

Behind her sleeping head, on the parlor wall, out of focus, the portrait's frame hung slightly crooked.

As if something inside it had leaned forward to listen too.
