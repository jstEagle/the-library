## 21. A Name Written in Ashes

The hearthstone was the only thing in the house that had not burned.

Everything else had gone to smoke and cinder — the stairs, the table where Mother had eaten her whole life, the photographs Juniper had spent three days cataloguing like a rosary. The fire had taken the portrait and then, with a kind of terrible thoroughness, taken everything the portrait had ever touched. But the hearthstone itself, the great grey slab that had sat at the center of the Eriksen house since before anyone alive could remember, remained exactly as it was. Cool to the touch. Patient. Waiting, perhaps, for someone to tell it what it had witnessed.

Juniper knelt before it on the scorched floorboards with Wendell's pocketknife in their hand.

It was a small knife, the kind sold to boys who wanted to feel grown, with a horn handle worn smooth by a hundred years of thumbs. Juniper had found it in the ashes of Wendell's room, or rather — they had reached into the ash and their fingers had simply closed around it, the way you find a coin in a riverbed, without looking, because some part of you already knew where it was. The blade was still sharp. Of course it was. Wendell had never let anything of his go dull.

"You'd have laughed at me," Juniper said aloud, to no one, to him. "You'd have said, *June, you can't carve your way out of a problem.*"

But there was no one left to say it.

They set the blade against the stone and pressed.

*W.*

The first stroke was the hardest. Not physically — the stone gave surprisingly easily, as if it had been waiting to be written on, as if all these years it had been holding its breath. It was hard because Juniper understood, with the cold clarity that had settled over them since the fire, what the carving meant. This was not a memorial. Memorials were for things other people could verify. This was testimony. This was one person standing up in an empty courtroom and saying: *I was there. I saw. It happened, even if I am the only one left who knows.*

*E.*

Their mother was asleep in the front room, which had survived better than most of the house, though the fire had licked its doorway and left the wallpaper curling like old lettuce. She slept more now. She slept the way people sleep after a long illness finally ends — not restfully, but completely, surrendered, as if her body had decided that whatever debt it had been carrying was paid and it might as well stop pretending otherwise. Sometimes she woke and looked at Juniper with an expression Juniper had come to dread: polite puzzlement, the look you give a stranger on a train who has addressed you by name.

*N.*

"Dell," she had murmured once, two nights ago, half-dreaming. And then she had frowned, because the name had nowhere to land. There was no Dell in her life. There had never been a Dell. When she woke she asked Juniper why the kitchen smelled like someone else's childhood, and Juniper had said, "Burning," and she had nodded, satisfied, and asked for tea.

*D.*

The letters came slowly. Juniper was not a carver; they were a writer, a photographer, a keeper of lists and ledgers and receipts. Their whole life had been an argument with forgetting. Every birthday documented, every meal photographed, every conversation with Wendell recorded in notebooks that now existed only in the space behind Juniper's ribs, because paper burns and so does film and so does everything except the thing itself, the raw unerasable fact of having loved someone.

*E.*

*L.*

*L.*

Juniper sat back and looked at what they had made.

**WENDELL ERIKSEN**

Fourteen letters, crooked as a fence line in wind, cut deep into the hearthstone of a house that had tried to forget him. The stone dust glittered in the morning light coming through the broken window. It looked, Juniper thought, like frost. Like the first frost of a year that might, if everyone worked very hard, be ordinary.

"Good," Juniper said. Their voice cracked on it. "Good. That's — that's step one."

Because there was a step two. There had been a step two since the moment Juniper had stood in the ruins holding a camera that no longer held any pictures of him, a heart that no longer held any witnesses.

Step two was the notebook.

* * *

Astrid Vold brought it.

She arrived at nine in the morning, which for Astrid constituted a lie-in, carrying a parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with the particular aggressive knot of a woman who did not trust string. She was thinner than she had been a month ago. Everyone in Hallowmere was thinner. Ninety-nine souls had passed through the village as light, and something of the village had gone with them — a brightness, a fatness of spirit, burned away like fog off a field. But Astrid's eyes were clear, and her hands were steady, and when she set the parcel on the kitchen table she did it with the ceremony of a woman laying a foundation stone.

"Parish stationery," she said. "Well. Former parish. The church is a shell now, but the storeroom didn't burn, and God knows nobody else is going to use the ledgers." She untied the knot with two efficient tugs. Inside was a notebook — thick, cloth-bound, its cover a deep blue faded almost to grey, the kind of book built to outlast its owner. Beside it she set a pen, a bottle of ink, and, inexplicably, a small tin of peppermints.

"The mints are for stamina," Astrid said, catching Juniper's look. "Writing is harder than people think. My grandfather kept the flood records of 1903 and he chewed through a pound of peppermints a week. Said sweet things kept the bitter ones down."

"Astrid."

"Don't." She held up a hand. Her voice, always brisk, wobbled once and then firmed itself like a soldier straightening his back. "Don't thank me yet. Thank me when you've finished the first page, because I know you, Juniper Eriksen, and I know you've been sitting on this floor for two days trying to figure out how to begin, and beginning is the part that kills people."

Juniper looked at the notebook. It was blank in the way that only truly empty things are blank — not waiting, but open. Willing.

"How do you remember?" Juniper asked quietly. "Any of it? You were there. You counted. But the count is gone too, isn't it. The ledger pages."

"Gone." Astrid pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, the way she sat, as if chairs should be grateful. "I went back to my records the morning after the fire. Ninety-seven names, ninety-eight, ninety-nine — all of them just... smudged. Ink gone grey, like rain got at it. And the spaces where the names used to be don't even look empty. They look *content.*" She shuddered. "That's the worst part. The record isn't grieving them. The record has made its peace. Only we haven't."

"And me?"

Astrid looked at them for a long moment. Outside, a bird sang — actually sang, the first proper birdsong in weeks, some small brown thing declaring ownership of a hawthorn hedge as if the world had not nearly ended.

"You," she said, "are the last page he was ever written on. So write him again. Write him so hard that forgetting chokes on it."

Then she stood, patted Juniper once on the shoulder — Astrid's version of an embrace, worth roughly six normal hugs — took one peppermint for herself from the tin, and left.

Juniper opened the notebook.

The first page was clean white and slightly rough, good paper, honest paper. Juniper uncapped the ink. Their hand hovered.

And then, because there was no other way to begin, because every document they had ever made began the same way, they wrote:

***Entry the First. His name was Wendell Eriksen.***

The pen moved, and the words came, and they did not stop for a long time.

*He was born eleven minutes before me, and he never once let me forget it. He said the eleven minutes proved he was the responsible one. He was not the responsible one. He was the one who climbed the water tower on a dare and the one who fed the vicar's dog sausages until it followed him home and the one who —*

Juniper stopped. Pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. Kept going.

*— who made our mother laugh when the fever had her, really laugh, the ugly snorting kind, and the doctors said laughter was medicine and I believe them, because I watched it work. He loved badly and loyally. He kept his promises the way other people keep secrets: absolutely, and to the death. Especially to the death.*

*I am writing this because no one else will. The certificate in the church office says Juniper Eriksen died. The grave marker says it. Our mother cannot say who we buried. The photograph album holds pictures of an empty chair beside me at every birthday, and the empty chair does not trouble anyone but me. As far as the world is concerned, I was an only child who grew up alone in a house with a cold hearth.*

*But the hearth remembers. I carved his name into it this morning and the stone took the letters like it had been saving room.*

*Here is what happened. All of it. Even the parts that will make whoever finds this think I lost my mind. Especially those parts. Because I did not lose my mind. I lost my brother, and I intend to be extremely precise about the difference.*

And then Juniper wrote the story. The bell that rang at dusk though it had been melted in 1911. The face in the oil paint that shifted between sittings. The notebooks under the floorboards, Wendell's careful hand documenting his own damnation in the tidy shorthand he'd used for grocery lists. The bargain — one twin for the family, one soul to save their mother, and the portrait's patient arithmetic: *it does not care which twin, it only cares that the count reaches one hundred.* The blacksmith's son. The priest. Astrid's grandson, taken from a locked room while Astrid screamed at a door that had never once in forty years failed to lock. The road that looped. The ferryman with his century-old face and his price no one could pay twice.

Juniper wrote about the mirrors. That was the hardest part, and they saved it for the middle of the day, when the light was strong, because they needed strong light to write it. How the glass had begun showing Wendell instead of them. How in the deepest reflection — the old mirror in the hall, the one with the foxed silver — he had spoken, or seemed to speak, his mouth moving a half-second behind his eyes: *I'm holding it, June. Ninety-nine. I won't let it take the last one. Don't come after me. Please. For once in your life, do as you're told.*

"I have never once done as I'm told," Juniper wrote, "and I am not going to start being obedient now that you're dead. Forgive me. Or don't. You never listened to me either."

They wrote about cutting their palm and pressing it to the canvas. About the grey village inside — ninety-nine souls in streets the color of old dishwater, and Wendell at the center of it, arms spread, holding shut a door that breathed. About offering to take his place. About his refusal — furious, immediate, the most Wendell thing he had ever done, refusing freedom itself rather than let his twin pay for it — and how the refusal broke the bargain open, because the loophole had been there all along, waiting: *the count can be broken not by a soul given, but by a soul freely refused by the one it was taken from.* The hundredth toll had rung and found no one willing. The portrait had burned from the inside out, and the souls had gone up through the roof as light, and Astrid had watched from the lane and sworn afterward that the light had paused, just briefly, over the garden where the brothers used to dig for worms.

And then Juniper wrote the price.

*The fire took him twice. Once into the paint, and once out of memory. Every record of Wendell Eriksen is gone. Every photograph shows empty air beside me. Our mother looks at the second bedroom and sees a sewing room. The villagers nod to me kindly and ask if I'm settling in well, as if I hadn't lived here all my life, as if I hadn't buried — what? Who? Even they can't say. I am the sole custodian of a human being. If I die carelessly, he dies entirely.*

*So I will not die carelessly.*

*I will write him every day. I will write his terrible handwriting and his worse singing. I will write the scar on his chin from the ice, age nine, and how he cried and then made me promise never to tell, and I am telling, Wendell, I am telling everyone, because a secret kept for one person is love and a secret kept for no one is just a stone in your shoe. I will write the joke about the ferryman that I cannot put down here because Mother sometimes reads over my shoulder and some things are sacred and stupid in equal measure.*

*His name was Wendell Eriksen. He was born eleven minutes before me. He held a door shut against hell for as long as his arms could bear it, and then his brother came and made him let go, and letting go was the bravest thing either of us ever did.*

*This notebook is his grave. Visit often.*

Juniper set down the pen. The ink was still wet on the final line. Through the window, the afternoon had gone gold and long, and somewhere down the lane a dog barked at nothing, ordinary as bread.

Their hand ached. Their chest ached worse. But underneath both, faint and unfamiliar, something else stirred — something that felt almost like purpose wearing grief's coat.

* * *

They found their mother at the kitchen table.

Not the burned kitchen — the surviving one, the front room's little annex where the kettle still worked and the window still had most of its glass. She was sitting with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, staring at the middle distance with an expression Juniper had never seen on her face before. Not confusion. Not the polite stranger's puzzlement of the past weeks. Something older. Rawer.

"Mother?"

She didn't turn. "There was a song," she said. "I've been trying to think of it all morning. A lullaby. Someone used to sing it — badly, terribly, all the wrong notes, and I'd tell them to stop and they'd sing louder." Her voice thinned. "I can't remember who. I can't remember the tune. I just remember that I loved hearing it, and that I told him to stop every single time, and that he knew — he always knew — that *stop* meant *again.*"

Juniper's throat closed.

"Him," they managed.

"Him." She frowned at the word as if it had appeared on her tongue uninvited. "I keep doing that. Saying *him,* saying *he,* and there's no — there's nothing attached. It's like reaching for a stair that isn't there. Juniper, did we..." She turned at last, and her eyes were wet and utterly lost. "Did we used to be more people? In this family? Were we ever four instead of two? Because my heart keeps setting a place for someone and I cannot for the life of me think who."

Juniper crossed the room and knelt beside her chair, the way they had knelt at the hearthstone, and took her hands — hands that had signed nothing, remembered nothing, chosen comfort over confession and lost anyway, because the portrait had never honored bargains, only collected them.

"There was," Juniper said gently. "There were four of us once. Father. And someone else. And I'll tell you about them whenever you want. Every day, if you need. I know things, Mother. I'm keeping them safe."

She looked at them for a long time. Then she pulled Juniper's head down against her shoulder, the way she had when they were small, and wept — not the sharp weeping of fresh grief but the slow, bewildered weeping of a woman mourning a hole shaped exactly like a son, with no name to pour into it.

Above them, faint through the wall, the hearthstone held fourteen letters in the dark.

And outside, far off toward the empty church, the bell tower stood silent against the evening — but as Juniper rose to make fresh tea, they noticed something that stopped their breath: on the sill of the broken window, in the last of the light, someone had left a single peppermint, unwrapped, placed dead center, the way you leave a coin for a ferryman.

Or the way a friend leaves one for a writer who has a very long book ahead of her.

Juniper picked it up. It was cool, and sweet-smelling, and real.

"Well," they said softly, to the room, to the stone, to the boy born eleven minutes early. "Best get back to work, then."
