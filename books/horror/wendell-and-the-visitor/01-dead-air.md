## 1. Dead Air

The red ON AIR light had a hairline crack in it that Xanthe Dunmore had been staring at for eleven years, and in all that time it had never once seemed like an omen. Tonight it looked like one.

He leaned back from the board at 2:07 a.m., the way he did every night between the news block and the open lines, and let the song do the work — some slow, waterlogged ballad from the seventies that the syndicator fed him without comment. WXRL broadcast out of a converted dental office above the laundromat on Corliss Street, and at this hour the building hummed around him like something digesting. The dryers downstairs thumped their arrhythmic heartbeat through the floor. Outside the glass, Harrow Bend lay flat and dark under sodium lights, the reservoir a black absence at the edge of town that you could feel more than see, the way you can feel a tooth that isn't there anymore.

"Two-oh-seven," he said into the mic when the song faded, because his mother had taught him that radio was a conversation and conversations needed timestamps. "You're awake, I'm awake, somebody's got to be. Lines are open. If you can't sleep, you're in good company — half this town's been up since the water table started doing whatever it's doing. Talk to me."

He killed the mic and sipped cold coffee. Twenty years of overnight radio teaches you a kind of patience that isn't patience at all; it's a low-grade hypnosis. The callers came in waves. Insomniacs, truckers, the chemically lonely, the woman who called every Tuesday to describe her dreams about the governor. He knew them by voice before they said their names. That was the whole trick of the job, if it was a job: you became the place people put the things they couldn't say in daylight, and you learned not to flinch.

Line two lit up at 2:14.

He glanced at the screen. No caller ID — the board was older than some of his listeners' parents — but there was something odd about the way the light sat there. Steady. Not blinking the way line lights did when someone was holding. It just glowed, patient as a cat outside a mouse hole.

Xanthe punched it up. "WXRL, you're live. Who's this?"

Static first. But not the ordinary kind. Xanthe had spent two decades listening to static the way a piano tuner listens to pitch, and this was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name — a thin, silvery hiss underneath the caller's breath, like the sound a frequency makes when nobody's broadcasting on it. A carrier wave with nothing riding it. He knew that sound intimately. Every station has its dead frequencies, the slots between licensed channels where the atmosphere murmurs to itself, and WXRL's own dead spot lived at 1610 AM, a sliver of nothing where you could sometimes hear, faintly, the ghost of the station's old signal from before they'd moved frequencies in '89. Engineers called it bleed-through. Xanthe had always thought it sounded like the station remembering itself.

The woman spoke through that hiss.

"Is this the man who remembers things?"

Her voice was sixty-ish, maybe older, gravelled at the edges but warm underneath, the voice of someone who'd done a lot of talking over kitchen tables. Rural vowels. Local. He placed her within ten miles before she finished the sentence.

"That's what I try to do," Xanthe said, easy, professional. "Who am I talking to?"

"You're talking to Marla Ketch."

"Evening, Marla. What's keeping you up?"

The pause that followed was long enough that he checked the board to make sure the line hadn't dropped. It hadn't. The light still glowed. And beneath her silence, that silver static kept running, steady as a held note.

"I wanted to ask you," she said finally, "to remember a name."

"Well, names are my business. Give it to me."

"No." Another pause. "That's not how it works. You have to remember it yourself. That's why I'm calling you. They said—" the static swelled, swallowed half a word, receded— "they said you were the one who remembers."

Xanthe smiled at the cracked ON AIR light out of habit. "Marla, I hate to disappoint you, but I don't have any special memory. I've got a microphone and bad sleep habits. What name are we talking about?"

"Toby Vance."

The name went through him like a draft through a house with a broken seal — a small cold movement in a room he'd thought was shut. He didn't know it. That was the strange part, the part he would turn over later, again and again, looking for the seam. It wasn't that the name rang a bell or failed to ring one. It was that hearing it produced a sensation of *almost* — the feeling of reaching for a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. Somewhere in him, something braced for a memory that wasn't there.

"Toby Vance," he repeated, buying time, keeping his voice level. "Should I know that name?"

"Yes," Marla Ketch said simply. "You should. Everybody should. That's the whole trouble."

"Where's he from? Harrow Bend?"

"He's from here." A soft, terrible laugh. "He's from right here, Mr. Dunmore. He never left."

Xanthe reached for his notepad — an actual paper pad, a habit from a previous station manager who distrusted anything with a battery — and wrote the name down. Toby Vance. The letters looked ordinary on the page. Two words, nine letters, ink on pulp. He stared at them and felt that same vertiginous almost, like a word on the tip of the tongue that refuses to arrive no matter how you circle it.

"All right," he said. "Tell me about Toby Vance. Who is he to you?"

And here was where the night turned, though Xanthe wouldn't understand until much later how far back the turning actually went.

"Oh, honey," the woman said, and her voice changed — softened, went somewhere private, "Toby was my boy."

Something in the studio shifted. Not physically. Nothing fell, nothing moved. But the hum of the building seemed to drop away for a second, the dryers downstairs going silent mid-thump, and Xanthe had the distinct impression of being listened to by more than his audience meter indicated.

"Your son," he said carefully. "And you want me to remember him."

"I want *somebody* to remember him. You're on the air. People listen to you." The static rose again, and now he could hear something inside it, threads of other sound woven through the hiss — a murmur, almost choral, too indistinct to be words. "I've been gone four days, Mr. Dunmore. Four days, and already it's getting hard to hold on to things. Names go first. Then faces. I need somebody down there with a voice to say it out loud, keep it moving, keep it alive. That's what you do, isn't it? You say things out loud so they stay real."

"Gone four days," Xanthe repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "What does that mean, exactly?"

"It means what it means."

"Marla — are you telling me you've passed away?"

The laugh again, softer. "My obituary was in the *Courier* on Sunday. Page six, with the photograph from the church picnic, the one where my hat's crooked. My daughter picked it out. She always hated that hat." A pause, and the static beneath her voice deepened, took on texture, like wind moving across the mouth of a cave. "You can look it up. I'd rather you did, honestly. I'd rather you checked and found I'm telling the truth, because then you'll have to think about the rest of it."

Xanthe looked at the notepad. Under *Toby Vance* he wrote *Marla Ketch — obit??* and the question marks looked childish even as he wrote them.

Professional skepticism was not just his temperament; it was his armor. Twenty years of overnight radio means twenty years of cranks, drunks, performance artists, and once, memorably, a man who claimed to be broadcasting from inside the county courthouse walls. You developed a protocol. You stayed warm, you stayed vague, you never gave them the reaction they were fishing for. Reaction was oxygen to these callers.

But the static. The static was the problem. Because Xanthe knew that sound, had known it since he was a boy fooling with a shortwave kit at his mother's kitchen table, tuning through the dead band below 1620 and finding that silver whisper that engineers could never quite explain away. It was the sound of a frequency with no one on it. And Marla Ketch's voice was riding that sound the way a rider sits a horse — perfectly, naturally, as if it were the only medium a voice like hers had ever traveled on.

Live callers sounded like telephone lines. Dead ones, apparently, sounded like the space between stations.

"Here's what I'll do," he said, and heard his own voice take on the gentle firmness he used with the ones who were losing their grip. "I'm going to read your name on the air, and I'm going to ask our listeners — and it's three people and a dog, Marla, but they're loyal — to help me out. If anyone knows a Toby Vance from Harrow Bend, I'll ask them to call in. Fair enough?"

"Fair," she said. "But it won't work."

"Why not?"

"Because they don't remember either. That's what I'm trying to tell you, sweetheart. Nobody remembers. That's not an accident. That's the arrangement." The static surged, and for one clear second — one second he would replay for the rest of his life — Xanthe heard beneath her voice a chorus of others, dozens maybe, all murmuring the same two syllables over and over, soft as rain on a roof: *To-by. To-by. To-by.* Then it was gone, folded back under, and Marla Ketch was alone on the line again, an old woman's voice fraying at the edges. "It's coming due now. That's why I could get through. The door doesn't latch so good anymore. You'll see. You'll start hearing us, one after another, and you'll think you're going crazy, and you won't be. You'll be the only one who isn't."

"Marla—"

"You knew him." Her voice sharpened suddenly, urgent, close, as if she'd leaned toward a microphone on her end of wherever she was. "You played with him. Down by the creek, the summer you were nine. Think, Mr. Dunmore. Think about the summer of '74. Think about who led you down to the water."

"I was nine in—" He stopped. He had been nine in the summer of 1974. He had grown up in Harrow Bend, in the green house on Delano Street with the crooked porch rail, and he had spent that whole endless summer outdoors, the way children did then, unsupervised from breakfast until the streetlights came on. He tried to reach for it — the creek, the woods, the other kids — and found the summer strangely smooth, like a stone worn featureless by water. He remembered being nine. He remembered the heat, the smell of cut grass, the ice cream truck's warped jingle. He remembered *being*, that year, vividly. He just couldn't remember anyone else. No faces. No names. As if he'd spent the entire summer alone in a town full of people.

Except—

Except there had been someone. There was a shape rising in him now, unbidden, wearing a striped scarf in July for no reason anyone had ever questioned: a tall boy, a laughing boy, a friend who was always there and whom no one else ever seemed to—

"Wendell," Xanthe said aloud, without deciding to, and the name landed in the studio like a dropped glass.

The line clicked. Not hung up — *clicked*, a mechanical sound, a relay somewhere opening or closing — and Marla Ketch's voice came back thinner, stretched, already receding into the silver hiss as though she were walking away from a microphone in an enormous empty room.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's the door he used. Oh, you sweet boy. He's waiting for you. He never stopped waiting. Ask him what he's warden *of*—"

The light on line two went dark.

Not faded. Dark, all at once, the way a bulb dies, and the studio's hum rushed back in — the dryers, the ventilation, the tick of the board — so ordinary and so loud that Xanthe realized he'd been holding his breath.

He sat there for a long moment. On the pad, in his own handwriting, three lines:

*Toby Vance*
*Marla Ketch — obit?? Sunday Courier p.6*
*WENDELL*

He looked at the third line longest.

Wendell. He hadn't spoken that name in forty years. Hadn't needed to. Children lost imaginary friends the way they lost teeth — painlessly, without ceremony, replaced by the next thing growing in. Except Xanthe had never thought of Wendell as imaginary, even at nine, even when his mother asked, gently, over dinner, *who are you talking to out there, sweetheart?* and he'd said *Wendell* and she'd gone quiet in a way he'd noticed even then, a quiet with weight to it, a quiet like a hand pressed flat over something.

He remembered the scarf. Red and white stripes, wool, too warm for summer. He remembered a laugh like a crow's, delighted and rough. He remembered being led places. He remembered — dimly, distantly, the way you remember a dream only after stepping in the same footprint twice — being led *away* from somewhere, once, on an afternoon that mattered, though the afternoon itself was smooth as river stone, featureless, worn blank.

Xanthe set down the pen. Then, because the ritual of the job was the only solid thing left in the room, he leaned into the microphone, keyed it, and heard his own voice come out steady, which felt like watching someone else perform courage.

"That was Marla Ketch, folks, asking after a Toby Vance from Harrow Bend. If that name means anything to you — family, classmate, neighbor — give me a call. Line's open till four."

He released the key. The silence afterward had a texture he'd never heard in eleven years of dead air: expectant, like a held breath, like a town leaning in.

At 2:31 a.m., Xanthe Dunmore opened the *Harrow Bend Courier* archive on the station's ancient computer, navigated to Sunday's edition, and scrolled to page six.

There was the obituary. MARLA JEAN KETCH, 71, beloved mother and grandmother, preceded in death by her husband Dale, survived by her daughter Erin, services Thursday at First Methodist. And beside the text, slightly pixelated from the scan, a photograph of a smiling woman in a sun hat knocked charmingly askew.

Four days dead. Calling him by name. Knowing his childhood. Telling him, in a voice wrapped in the station's own dead frequency, that a boy named Toby Vance had been taken out of the world so thoroughly that even the shape of the absence had been erased — and that someone named Wendell was waiting for him.

The ON AIR light flickered in its cracked housing. Downstairs, the dryers thumped on. And Xanthe noticed, with a calm that frightened him more than panic would have, that he had already decided: he was going home to Harrow Bend. To the green house on Delano Street. To the porch.

Because some part of him — the nine-year-old part, the part that had never fully believed Wendell was made up — already knew he'd find someone sitting there, patient as a cat outside a mouse hole, wearing a striped scarf in any weather, waiting forty years to collect.
