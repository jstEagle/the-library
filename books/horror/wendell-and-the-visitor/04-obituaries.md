## 4. Obituaries

Xanthe didn't sleep. He told himself it was the schedule — twenty years of 2 a.m. broadcasts had rewired his circadian rhythm into something closer to a bat's than a man's — but he knew the shape of the lie even as he spoke it into the dark of his apartment. Marla Ketch had died on a Tuesday and called him on a Sunday, and between those two facts lay a gap no amount of professional skepticism could paper over.

He'd pulled her obituary from the *Harrow Bend Courier*'s website before dawn. MARLA KETCH, 71, BELOVED AUNT AND RETIRED SCHOOLTEACHER, PASSED PEACEFULLY AT HOME. Services private. In lieu of flowers, donations to the Lake Meridian Restoration Fund. The photograph showed a woman with a firm jaw and reading glasses on a chain, the kind of face that had spent decades telling children to sit down.

He read it eleven times. Then he did what he always did when reality misbehaved: he prepared for air.

* * *

The station at 1:40 a.m. was a held breath. KRLD-AM occupied the ground floor of a building that had been a furniture store in some previous economic era, and its studio smelled of burnt coffee and carpet glue and the faint ozone tang of old electronics. Xanthe ran the board himself now; the station couldn't afford a producer for the graveyard shift, which suited him. *Night Lines* was his show, his name, his small kingdom of insomniacs and truckers and grief-struck widowers who needed a voice in the dark that wasn't their own.

He laid out the night's materials like a surgeon laying out instruments. Phone log. Coffee. And beside them, printed off that afternoon, the obituaries page — seven notices from the past week, each with a name, an age, a date of death.

He stared at them for a long time before the red ON AIR light bloomed.

"Good morning, Harrow Bend and the wide awake world," he said, and heard his own voice do the thing it always did — drop half a register, go warm, go smooth, the voice of a man entirely in control. "It's two o'clock, and the lines are open."

He let the first twenty minutes run normal. A man named Denny called about the potholes on Route 9. A woman wanted to know if anyone else had seen the fox that had been living under the Food King loading dock. Ordinary voices, ordinary static, the comfortable hum of a town talking to itself through him.

Then, at 2:23, he said: "I want to try something a little different tonight. Some of you may have heard last week's broadcast. Some of you may have heard something you can't quite explain." He kept his tone light, curiosity-show light, the register he used for UFO sightings and haunted barns. "I've got this week's death notices here in front of me. Seven names. I'm going to read them. If anyone listening knew these folks — really knew them — I'd love to hear about them. Line one is open."

He picked up the page.

"Harold Beamish, eighty-four, retired millwright. Passed Wednesday at County General."

The board lit up so fast the console buzzed.

He looked at line one. Then line two. Both blinking. He took line one.

"Night Lines, you're on."

"Harold liked his eggs over hard," said a man's voice, and the static underneath it was wrong — not the crackle of a bad connection but a low, layered hiss, like wind moving through a place wind shouldn't reach, like the sound the dead frequency made when the station's transmitter idled down at sign-off and the channel went to carrier silence. Xanthe knew that sound. Every overnight host knows it. It's the sound of the station talking to itself when no one's there.

"I'm sorry — who is this?"

"Over hard. Not scrambled, not sunny side. His wife fixed them scrambled for forty years and he never once complained, and the week after she passed I asked him why he never said anything, and he said—" the voice paused, and the pause had texture, had weight, "—he said, *she made them, didn't she.*"

"Sir, are you—"

"You're reading us," the voice said, gentle as a nurse. "Keep going. We've been waiting all week for you to keep going."

Xanthe took his hand off the fader and looked at it. Steady. Twenty years of broadcasting had given him hands that didn't shake on air, whatever they did off it.

"Edna Pruitt, seventy-nine," he read. "Passed Friday at home. Survived by two daughters."

Line three.

"She hid the cigarettes in the flour canister," said a woman's voice with the same subterranean hiss beneath it. "Fifty years. Everybody knew. Nobody ever told her we knew. That was the kindness, you understand? That was how we loved her."

"Ray Delgado, sixty-one. Passed Monday. No services listed."

"He coached little league even after his boy stopped playing. Even after—" the voice wavered, or the static did, hard to tell where one ended and the other began. "Even after Toby stopped playing."

The word went through the studio like a current through water.

Xanthe's hand hovered over the fader. "Say that again?"

"Toby," the voice said. "You'll hear it again. We all say it. It's all any of us can—" The line dissolved into pure carrier hiss and clicked dead.

He sat in the sudden quiet of the booth, the ON AIR light burning red against the glass, and felt the vertigo open up beneath him like a trapdoor. Because here was the thing — here was the thing his mind kept circling back to like a tongue finding a chipped tooth: Harold Beamish's eggs could be a prank. Edna Pruitt's flour canister could be a prank, someone with access to the obituaries, someone cruel, someone bored. But Ray Delgado's caller had said a name Xanthe had never heard, in a context that meant nothing and everything, and the static —

The static was the dead frequency. He was sure of it. He'd logged it once, years ago, a technical anomaly at 3:07 a.m., a hiss with structure in it, almost syllabic, that the engineer had shrugged off as crosstalk from a decommissioned line. KRLD had been broadcasting since 1958. Its frequencies had been used by other stations, other voices, other shows, all of them gone now. Dead air wasn't empty. It was just unattended.

"Let's keep going," he heard himself say, and his voice on the monitors sounded like a stranger doing an impression of him. "Gil Marsh, seventy-three. Passed Thursday. Veteran, Army, 1968 to 1970."

"Gil remembered every name," said a new voice, older, female, patient. "Every soldier in his company. He'd recite them at the kitchen table. But there was one name he reached for sometimes, near the end, that wasn't on any list. He'd get halfway to it and stop, like a man walking into a wall he couldn't see. *To-* he'd say. Just *to*. And then he'd cry without knowing why."

Xanthe wrote the fragment down. *TO-.* His pen pressed hard enough to dent the pad.

"Doris Ann Wexler, ninety-one. Passed Saturday. Preceded in death by her husband and, the notice says, 'all who knew her.'"

"That's wrong," Doris's voice said immediately, and there was something almost amused in it, a great-aunt's dry humor surviving the grave. "That's backwards. She preceded *them*. She went first so she could hold the door." A pause, the static breathing. "She used to watch a boy swim at the quarry, before the lake. Before they filled it. He swam like the water owed him money. Toby Vance. She thought about him her whole life and could never have told you why, because she couldn't have told you who. Isn't that something? Grief with the name filed off. It still weighs the same."

Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. He drank cold coffee anyway, mechanically, because the ritual of it was a rope to hold.

One name left. He read it.

"Walter Ossman, fifty-eight. Passed Tuesday. Beloved husband. The family asks for privacy."

"They won't get it," Walter's voice said, and this one was different — younger, ragged, angry the way only the recently dead can be angry. "They want privacy because privacy is where the thing lives. Ask them about the summer of '74. Ask any of them. Watch their faces do the trick faces do. There was a boy, Dunmore. There was a boy and they took him twice — once out of the world and once out of the telling. Toby Vance. TO-BY VANCE. Say it. Somebody has to say it or we're all just—"

The line cut. All four lines went dark at once, and then, absurdly, line five lit up.

He took it because his hand moved before his judgment could stop it.

"Night Lines, you're on."

"Don't stop," said a woman, and this voice was alive — you could hear it instantly, the breath in it, the wet click of a throat with saliva in it, the small imperfections of a body still running. She was whispering, though, whispering like someone hiding in her own house. "Please. Please don't read any more names. I know what you're doing. I heard last week and I heard tonight and I need you to stop."

"Ma'am, can I ask—"

"My name is Carol Ossman." Walter's widow. "My husband died Tuesday and I buried him Saturday and he called me Thursday night. On my cell phone. From his own number. He said—" her whisper frayed, re-gathered itself. "He said he couldn't rest until somebody said the name. I asked what name. He said *you know the one, everybody knows the one, we just can't say it.* And I hung up on my dead husband, Mr. Dunmore, and I have not slept since, and tonight I turned on the radio because the house was too quiet, and I heard you reading the obituaries, and every single one of them — every one — they all started saying it. Toby. Toby Vance. I don't know a Toby Vance. I've lived in Harrow Bend my whole life and I don't know a Toby Vance, and I checked, I spent today at the library and the courthouse and the historical society, and there is no Toby Vance anywhere. Not in a census. Not in a yearbook. Not on a gravestone. Nothing. So either my dead husband and six other dead people are lying to me—"

She stopped. When she came back, her whisper had dropped so low the board nearly lost her.

"—or the town ate somebody, Mr. Dunmore. Whole. And I think it's starting to be hungry again, because Walter said one more thing before I hung up. He said, *they're going to come for the one who remembers.*"

The line clicked dead.

Xanthe sat motionless in the red light. The studio clock read 2:51. Somewhere in the building, the HVAC cycled on with a sigh, the most mundane sound in the world, and it made him flinch.

He should kill the mic. He knew that. Standard practice, standard instinct: unverifiable claim, distressed caller, end the segment, take a break, play the weather. Twenty years of training said *smooth it over, move along, nothing to see.*

Instead he leaned into the microphone, and heard his own voice come out low and level and more honest than it had been in years.

"You're listening to Night Lines. My name is Xanthe Dunmore. And I want to know if anyone out there remembers a boy named Toby Vance."

He let the silence run. Five seconds. Ten. Long enough that a program director would have cut his throat.

Then every line on the board lit up at once — all seven — and beneath the blinking lights, beneath the console, beneath the floor, the station's old dead frequency opened like an eye, and the hiss coming up through the board had structure in it now, unmistakable, rhythmic, patient.

It sounded like chanting.

It sounded like a name.

And on line one, in a voice he recognized with a lurch that dropped through his chest like an elevator cable snapping — a voice he had last heard four days ago asking him to remember — Marla Ketch said:

"Now you're getting it, sweetheart. Now we can talk about what your mother did."
