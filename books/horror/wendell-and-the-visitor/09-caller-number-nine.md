## 9. Caller Number Nine

The board lit up at 2:14 a.m., and Xanthe had learned to read the lights the way a fisherman reads weather. There were the ordinary calls — insomniacs, drunks, the lonely — and there were the others, the ones that came in on a frequency that didn't technically exist anymore, carrying that specific static he'd first heard in Marla Ketch's voice four nights ago. This one was different. The light glowed amber instead of the dead callers' gray-green, and when he punched it up, he heard breathing. Living breathing. Wet, ragged, human.

"Night Lines, you're on with Xanthe."

Silence for three seconds. Then a woman's voice, fifties maybe, pitched low as if someone might be listening through the walls of her kitchen.

"I shouldn't be calling this late. My husband's asleep upstairs."

"That's what this hour is for," Xanthe said, falling into the cadence he'd used for twenty years, the voice that made strangers believe their secrets were safe. "Who am I talking to?"

"Doreen." A pause. "Doreen Prewitt."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Prewitt. Aldous Prewitt, the councilman who'd met him at the shoreline two nights ago with his warm, empty smile and his warnings dressed as hospitality. *Stay away from the water, son. Some things settle better left alone.*

"You're related to Aldous Prewitt?"

"He's my father-in-law." A dry, nervous laugh. "Which is why I almost didn't call. Which is why I've been sitting in my kitchen for an hour with the phone in my hand, telling myself I'm crazy."

"What made you call, Doreen?"

Another silence, longer. He could hear her deciding. He'd spent two decades helping people across that threshold, and he knew every hesitation's shape — but this one was new. This was a woman standing at the edge of something she couldn't see the bottom of.

"It started Tuesday," she said finally. "I was doing dishes, and I got this — pain. Not like anything else. Like a wire pulled tight behind my eyes. And with it came—" Her breath caught. "I remembered a boy teaching me to skip stones on Miller's Creek. And I don't have a brother. I'm an only child. My mother told me that my whole life. Only child, just us girls, three generations of them. But I remember his hands showing me how to hold the stone flat, and I remember his name, and when I try to say it out loud, the wire pulls tighter."

Xanthe's hand went still on the fader. Outside the studio glass, the parking lot lay empty under the sodium lights, and the night felt suddenly thinner, like ice over deep water.

"Doreen," he said carefully, "what was his name?"

"I can't—"

"Try. Right now, on the air. Try to say it."

He heard her inhale. Heard the effort of it, the way you hear someone lifting something far too heavy. And then, beneath her breathing, faint as a radio heard through a wall, another sound rose in the line. Voices. Plural. Soft, overlapping, chanting something that wasn't quite words yet — a low tide-sound of syllables that Xanthe recognized with a lurch in his stomach. The dead callers. They were on the line with her. Or she was on the line with them.

"Toby," Doreen whispered.

The wire snapped.

She screamed — not long, but raw, the scream of a woman whose skull had been pried open from the inside — and then there was a wet sound, and Xanthe was on his feet before he understood why, pressing his palm flat against the console glass as if he could reach through it.

"Doreen. Doreen, are you hurt?"

"There's blood," she said, and her voice had changed, gone strange and calm, the calm after rupture. "My nose is bleeding. It's all over the phone. All over the—" She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "Oh God. Oh God, I remember him. I remember *everything*. He fell off the rope swing in June and I ran to get his mother and I remember his dog, a brown mutt named Biscuit, and I remember he wanted to be a diver, he wanted to see what was under the creek, and I remember—" her voice climbed, frantic with recovered years, "—I remember the day they took him. I was eleven. I watched them walk him into the water and I forgot I watched and I have been *not watching* for fifty years—"

"Doreen, listen to me. Are you safe? Is someone with you?"

"No one is ever with me," she said, and then, quieter: "That's not true. That's what I always thought. But he was. Toby was with me. In the part of me they cut out."

The chanting beneath her voice had grown clearer. Xanthe leaned toward the monitor, and now he could distinguish it — seven voices, maybe more, layered like sediment, each one murmuring the same two syllables over and over. *To-by. To-by.* Not menacing. Almost tender. The way a congregation says an amen. The way mourners say a name at a graveside because saying it is all that's left to do.

And underneath the tenderness, Xanthe heard something else. Hunger. Patient, enormous, coiled at the bottom of the sound like a shape at the bottom of a lake.

"Doreen, I need you to hang up now. Gently. And I need you to write down everything you remember before you sleep. Everything. Don't trust it to stay."

"Why?" Her voice sharpened. "Why would it go? It's *mine*. Fifty years they kept it and it's mine—"

"Because they took it once," Xanthe said. "And the people who took it are still in Harrow Bend."

The line went quiet except for the chanting, which seemed, impossibly, to swell slightly — as if the dead had heard him and approved.

"My father-in-law called tonight," Doreen said at last. "Before I dialed you. He said the family was worried about me, that I'd been acting strange since Tuesday, that Dr. Halloway could give me something to help me rest." A pause. "He said it the way you talk to someone you're deciding about."

"I know that tone," Xanthe said. "I've been getting it all week."

"Then you know what I know now." She breathed out, long and shaking. "They're going to do it again. Whatever they did to Toby. There's a boy — my husband's sister's boy, Danny, he's nine — and Aldous has been having him out to the lake every evening this week. Teaching him to fish. Being *kind* to him." Her voice broke on the word. "Aldous has never been kind to anyone in his life unless he wanted something."

The studio around Xanthe seemed to recede. He was aware of the ON AIR sign buzzing its red buzz, aware of the dead frequency humming in the board like a struck tuning fork, aware that somewhere across town an old man was walking a nine-year-old boy along the shoreline in the dark, teaching him the shape of a bargain.

"Doreen. Get Danny away from that lake. Tonight if you can."

"How? Who do I say I am? A woman who remembers a brother nobody's ever heard of?" She laughed again, bitter and wet. "They'll have me committed by Friday."

"Then don't tell them anything. Just get him out of town for a while. Say he needs a dentist. Say anything."

"And then what?"

Xanthe looked at the board. Seven gray-green lights burned steady, patient, waiting their turn. Behind them, the amber light where Doreen's living breath still crackled. He thought of Marla Ketch asking him to remember a name he'd never heard. He thought of Wendell on the porch, striped scarf, forty years unchanged, speaking of obligations as casually as the weather. He thought of the Morse-light flickering under the black water of Lake Meridian, and the riddle Wendell had given him: *What the water holds, the water keeps, until a mouth it loved speaks free.*

"Then I find out how to give him back," Xanthe said. "All of him. To everyone."

The chanting swelled — and for one moment it resolved into clarity so sharp that Xanthe's own nose began to sting, a hot copper thread trickling over his lip. Through the blood and the static, the dead voices spoke together, no longer chanting but *asking*, and the question was the same one Marla Ketch had asked him five nights ago:

*Remember him.*

"I will," Xanthe said aloud, to the dead, to the living, to whoever was listening on either side of the signal. "I promise. I'll remember him."

Doreen hung up first — or the line simply ended; he couldn't tell. The amber light died. The seven gray-green lights held for ten seconds more, their murmur fading down through the frequencies like a choir sinking beneath water, and then the board was dark except for the ordinary lights of the ordinary lonely, and the clock above the glass read 2:41 a.m.

Xanthe wiped his nose with the back of his hand and stared at the blood there, bright and undeniable in the studio's cold light. Proof. The memory had a physical cost, and he had just paid a down payment on air.

His cell phone buzzed against the console.

Unknown number. Local prefix. He answered, and a voice he hadn't heard since he was nine years old — cheerful, patient, terribly familiar — said:

"You're making progress, Xanthe. He likes that. But you should know the old man's already picked the next one." A pause, and Xanthe could *hear* the smile in it, the awful unaged smile. "Danny Prewitt turns ten on Sunday. The water's been very patient. Have you?"

* * *

Xanthe drove to the Prewitt house without deciding to. His hands made the turns; his foot found the gas; the town slid past his windows asleep and lamplit and utterly complicit. He told himself it was to warn Doreen. He told himself a lot of things at four in the morning.

The Prewitt house sat on Crestline Drive like a statement — brick and white columns, older than everything around it, one of the nine original houses the founding families had never let leave the family. Every light was off except one on the ground floor, and behind that lit window Xanthe saw a silhouette he recognized: Aldous Prewitt, seated, unmoving, facing the front door as if he'd been expecting company.

Xanthe parked across the street and sat with his engine ticking. He should go to the door. He should pound on it. Instead he watched, and after a while he understood that watching was the point — that the old man had put himself in the window deliberately, a message rendered in posture: *I see you. I know where you are. Come in and be civil, or sit out there and be afraid.*

His phone buzzed again. Not Wendell this time. A text from an unknown number, and the words on the screen made the copper taste rise in his throat:

**DOREEN K. — ADMITTED TO ST. LUKE'S, 3:12 AM. NOSEBLEED. LISTED AS PSYCH HOLD. FAMILY CONCERNED.**

Family concerned. Aldous's phrase, wearing a hospital's clothes. They had taken her memory and now they would take her credibility, seal it in a chart, file it under unwell. The erasure didn't just delete the past, Xanthe realized. It had procedures. It had follow-through. It was, in its way, professional.

He looked back at the lit window. The silhouette had risen. The porch light came on, yellow and welcoming, and the front door opened, and Aldous Prewitt stood framed in it in his cardigan, one hand raised in a small wave — an invitation, or a summons, delivered with that practiced warmth that had never once reached his eyes.

Behind the old man, at the top of the stairs, small and barefoot and half-asleep, a boy stood holding the banister. Nine years old. Looking down at Xanthe's car with the unguarded curiosity of a child who does not yet know what he is worth.

Danny Prewitt raised one hand too, mimicking his grandfather, and waved.

And from the darkness of the back seat — though Xanthe had checked the mirrors twice, though the doors were locked, though nothing moved — came a voice he knew better than his own, close as breath on his neck:

"Don't wave back, Xanthe. He counts waves."
