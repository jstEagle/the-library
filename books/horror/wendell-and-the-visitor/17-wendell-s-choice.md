## 17. Wendell's Choice

Xanthe found him on the porch swing, the way he'd found him a lifetime ago and a week ago both — sitting with his hands folded in his lap, striped scarf neat against his throat, watching the street as though Harrow Bend were a play performed for his benefit alone.

The porch light was dead. It had been dead since Xanthe arrived, one more thing on the list of things to fix about this house, and yet Wendell sat inside a small pool of illumination that had no source Xanthe could name. The light didn't fall on him so much as agree to be near him.

"You took me away," Xanthe said.

He hadn't planned to open with it. He'd rehearsed rage on the drive over — rage was easier to carry than what he actually felt — but standing on his mother's sagging porch steps, looking at the thing that had been his only friend for three summers, the words came out flat and terrible and true.

"That afternoon. August ninth, 1974. You said there was something down by the creek you wanted to show me. A heron, you said." His voice cracked on the bird's name like a boy's voice cracking. "You kept me in the water catching crawdads until it was dark. Until it was *done*. And I laughed, Wendell. I came home soaked and happy and I told my mother about the heron that never existed, and she looked at me like I'd handed her a gift."

Wendell did not move. The swing did not creak under him, because nothing about him weighed anything at all.

"I know," he said.

"You *know*?"

"I was there, Xanthe. I'm always where I'm sent." He turned then, and for the first time since the porch, since 1974, Xanthe saw something behind the pleasant fixed features that wasn't pleasant at all. It took him a moment to identify it, because it was so ordinary, so human: it was shame. "I would tell you I had no choice, but you've heard enough lies from this town. So instead I'll tell you the truth, and you can decide what it's worth. Which is probably nothing. Which is probably less than nothing."

"Then talk fast."

Wendell patted the swing beside him. Xanthe stayed standing. Something flickered across the not-quite-boy's face — hurt, maybe, or the memory of hurt — and then he folded his hands again and began.

* * *

"There was a boy before me," Wendell said. "That's the part nobody remembers, because remembering me is the one thing I can't allow, and I've had fifty years of practice at being unrememberable. But before I was your friend, I was a friend. A real one. Flesh and scraped knees and a bicycle with a bent front fender."

"A boy from here."

"From the lowland district. From the streets they flooded." Wendell's gaze went out past the porch rail, past the dark lawns, toward the reservoir that lay beyond the town like a held breath. "My father worked the church. Sexton. We lived two doors from the Vance family. Toby and I used to dare each other onto the church roof. He always won, because Toby Vance never once declined a dare in his life. That was the whole shape of him. He couldn't leave a thing alone. Not a locked door, not a deep pool, not a question somebody told him was rude."

The name landed in Xanthe's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the word to dissolve on his tongue the way it had in the historical society, when the founders had taken his voice — but Wendell had said it, easily, the way you'd say any name, and Xanthe understood suddenly that Wendell existed outside the rite's jurisdiction. You cannot forbid the collector to collect.

"The drought was bad that summer," Wendell went on. "Wells going dry. Cattle dying. My father stood up in church and prayed for rain and the rain didn't come, and the nine families got together, and what they got together *with* —" his mouth twisted — "that isn't mine to describe. I wasn't at the table. I was ten years old. What I know is that they needed a first payment. A proof of concept, if you like. Something to show the thing beneath the water that Harrow Bend was serious. And they chose me."

"Why you?"

"Because my father hesitated." Wendell said it simply, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "When the covenant was drawn up, every family signed and gave their token. Hair, blood, whatever the terms required. My father signed too — he was one of the nine, don't ever mistake him for innocent — but when they asked whose child, he paused. One breath. Maybe two. And in that pause, the others decided for him. A hesitating man's son. A boy nobody would fight hard enough for." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the sunny one, and it was the most horrifying thing Xanthe had ever seen on a face. "They were right, as it happened. Nobody fought. Not even me."

"What did they do?"

"They walked me down to the water. The creek, then — the reservoir wasn't finished. There was a place where the springs came up cold out of the limestone, and the thing was already there, already waiting, because it had been waiting under Harrow Bend longer than Harrow Bend. They gave me to it. And it —" For the first time, Wendell faltered. His small hands opened and closed in his lap. "It didn't kill me. Killing would have been a mercy it doesn't have. It *kept* me. It unmade the parts of me that were inconvenient and kept the parts that were useful, and what was left over, it dressed up and sent back to town wearing a scarf my mother knitted me, because it had seen the scarf and thought it was a nice detail. A convincing detail. Children trust children who look loved."

Xanthe's legs wouldn't hold him anymore. He sat down on the top step, three feet from the swing, as far from Wendell as the porch allowed.

"It sent you to me."

"It sent me to everyone, at first. Every child in the lowland district saw me that summer. I was a game, a rumor, a boy on a bike with no fender. But most children forget an imaginary friend within a season. You didn't." Wendell looked at him directly now, and the shame in his face had deepened into something older and more worn. "You remembered me for forty years, Xanthe. Do you understand what that made you? The only mind in Harrow Bend that held me and held *him* — held Toby — and refused, all that time, to let either of us go. Your mother fought them in '74. She hid the tape. She wrote things down and burned them and wrote them again. But she could be threatened, and she was threatened, and eventually she chose to keep you alive rather than keep the truth alive. Any mother would. No court in heaven would convict her."

"But me—"

"You were nine. You were *safe*. You were the one person they never had to threaten, because you'd do the work yourself. All they needed was for you to be led away on the crucial afternoon, laughing, thinking about herons." Wendell's voice dropped. "And I did it. I waded into that creek with you and I made you laugh and I watched the sun go down and I knew — I knew exactly what was happening up at the Vance house while we were splashing around after crawdads — and I kept you there anyway, because keeping you there was the task, and disobedience, for something like me, isn't courage. It isn't even possible. It's like asking the tide to feel guilty."

* * *

The night had gone very quiet. Even the crickets had stopped, as though the whole town were leaning in to listen through the walls.

"So you're telling me," Xanthe said slowly, "that you're a victim."

"I'm telling you I'm *both*." Wendell stood, finally, and the sourceless light moved with him, and up close Xanthe could see what he'd never let himself see as a child: that the boy's edges weren't quite edges. That the skin was a suggestion. That underneath the striped scarf and the freckles there was a darkness with a shape held around it like a hand around a moth. "Victim and instrument. The drowned and the drowning hand. I've had fifty years to work out which one I am and I still can't make the arithmetic come out clean. But here's what I've decided, in the last week, watching you refuse to stop." He stepped off the porch, onto the grass, and turned to face Xanthe, and for a moment the mask slipped entirely — and beneath it was not a monster, not a void, but a ten-year-old boy's terror, preserved like a fly in amber, screaming silently behind the freckles. "I've decided that if I get one act in this world that's mine — one — it will be this."

He reached up and pulled the scarf loose from his throat.

"No," Xanthe said, without knowing why. "Wait. What does that cost you?"

"Everything, and nothing." Wendell held the scarf in both hands, and it looked suddenly like what it had always been: a knitted thing, a mother's gift, fifty years old and still bright. "The lock they put on your voice is keyed to me. I'm the collector; the silence is collected through me. If I stop being the collector — if I give up this form, this shape, this *duty* — the lock opens. You'll be able to say his name. Tobias Ellery Vance. You'll be able to sing her lullaby at the water and finish what your mother started." His eyes glistened, and Xanthe realized with a lurch that the thing was crying, or doing its best imitation of crying learned from a hundred children across five decades. "But the form is also the cage, Xanthe. It's the only thing holding me together. Without it, I don't become free. I just become... a man. Ordinary. Grieving. With fifty years of borrowed memories crashing in like the lake coming home. I'll remember being two boys at once. I'll remember my mother knitting. I'll remember what they did to me in the spring water. And I'll have to carry all of it with nothing to hold me up but a body."

"How long have you known you could do this?"

"Since the day you recognized me on this porch." Wendell almost smiled. "A warden who gets remembered starts to come apart at the seams. You did it just by looking at me and saying my name. Isn't that funny? After everything, all it ever took was someone saying my name and meaning it."

Xanthe stood. His throat ached. Eleven days ago he'd have called this a script, a bit, a radio stunt. Now he looked at the small figure on his mother's lawn — the imaginary friend who had been a murdered boy, the murderer who had been a hostage, the surveillance that had been, God help them both, the closest thing to friendship his childhood had contained — and what rose in him was not hatred. Hatred had nowhere left to stand.

"Wendell," he said. "Before you do it. Tell me one true thing about the summer. Anything. Something only the real boy would know."

Wendell considered. Then: "Toby Vance was afraid of the dark, which he'd have denied with his last breath, so he made up a rule: whoever went into the dark first got to pick the game. He always went first. Always. Fifty dares, fifty times first into the dark." His voice broke clean through, and for a moment the voice was not a boy's voice at all but a middle-aged man's, ragged and wet. "He went first into the dark for all of us. And we let the dark keep him."

He looped the scarf once around his fist.

"When I let go," he said, "you'll hear water. Don't be afraid of it. It's just me, draining out." He looked up, and the last thing in his face was not fear but gratitude, which Xanthe knew he would never fully forgive himself for accepting. "Go to the church, Xanthe. Go tonight. Because Prewitt's people felt the covenant start to slip the moment your mother's voice reached you on that line, and they won't wait for the seventh day. They're choosing a new child right now."

"Whose child?"

"Prewitt's grandson." Wendell's knuckles whitened around the scarf. "The rite begins at moonrise. Now stop wasting time feeling sorry for me and go save somebody."

And he let go.

There was no explosion, no light, no sound of tearing. There was only a soft rush, like a river heard through a wall, and the porch light — the sourceless, agreeing light — guttered and died, and where Wendell had stood there was a man. Middle-aged, heavyset, balding, in a cardigan buttoned wrong, kneeling on the dead grass with the striped scarf clutched to his chest, weeping the way men weep when they have fifty years of grief arriving all at once: horribly, gratefully, without any grace whatsoever.

Xanthe crouched beside him. He didn't touch him. Some drownings you have to let finish.

"Can you walk?" he asked.

The man — Wendell, still Wendell, would always be Wendell — shook his head, then nodded, then managed, in a voice like gravel settling: "Go. *Go.* Moonrise."

Xanthe ran for his car. Behind him, on the lawn of the house where his mother had once hidden a tape inside a hymnal, the stranger who had been two boys knelt in the dark, holding a scarf, learning how to cry with a face that finally fit him.

Above the eastern hills, low and fat and merciless, the moon was already lifting itself toward the water.

And along the shoreline of Lake Meridian, in nine houses arranged in a ring as old as the covenant, lights were coming on.
