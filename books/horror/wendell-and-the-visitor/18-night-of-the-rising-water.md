## 18. Night of the Rising Water

The water came up Main Street at nine minutes past eleven, and it came the way a memory comes — all at once, from underneath, without any sound of arrival.

Xanthe saw it first from the window of his mother's house, where he had been sitting in the dark with the reel-to-reel machine, listening to her young voice argue with ghosts. The streetlights along Meridian Avenue flickered in unison, a slow pulse, like something breathing through the town's electrical system. Then the gutters began to run backward.

He stepped onto the porch and watched black water slide up the asphalt in a sheet, smooth as poured glass, covering the road without turbulence, without spray. It did not rush. It *arrived* — the way fog arrives, the way dread arrives — climbing the curbs, kissing the wheels of parked cars, and then, impossibly, continuing to rise while remaining perfectly still. No current. No waves. Just depth accumulating out of nothing, as if the lake had finally decided that the lowland district it drowned in 1974 deserved company.

His phone buzzed against his hip. The screen said UNKNOWN, and he answered because for two weeks every unknown number had been either dead or about to be.

"It's starting," Prewitt said. The old man's voice was stripped of its practiced warmth; what remained was thin and dry as paper left too long in sun. "They're doing it tonight. The rite. They have a child."

Xanthe's stomach dropped through the porch boards, through the soil, all the way down to the drowned church. "The grandson."

"Silas. Eight years old." A breath that might have been a sob swallowed whole. "I told them no. I told them fifty years was enough, that we'd let the debt lapse like decent men. But the others—" His voice cracked and reassembled itself into something colder. "The others remember what the drought felt like. And they're afraid. Fear is the one thing this town has never learned to forget."

"Where, Aldous."

"The historical society. They moved the covenant down to the archive room an hour ago. Xanthe — listen to me. Whatever you're going to do, do it before midnight. After midnight the water stops being water."

The line went dead. In the silence after, Xanthe heard the lake.

Not lapping. Not rushing. *Humming.* A low sustained tone rising out of the floodwater in the street, the same note he'd heard underwater at the steeple, amplified now until the windows buzzed in their frames. And threaded through it, faint but unmistakable, came voices. Dozens. Hundreds. The dead callers from his show, all speaking at once, not words yet — just the shape of words, the way a crowd sounds from three streets away.

He got in his car and drove into the rising water.

* * *

Harrow Bend at night had always looked to Xanthe like a town holding its breath, but tonight it exhaled.

The water was knee-deep on Elm Street when he turned onto it, and it was full of light. Not reflected light — *emitted*. Soft gold glimmers rose through the black surface like bubbles, and each one, when it broke against the air, opened briefly into a picture: a kitchen, a birthday cake, a man's hands fixing a bicycle chain, a woman laughing in a dress printed with poppies. Memories, rising off the drowned district below and maybe off the living town above, popping free like corks after fifty years of pressure.

And the people were standing in it.

An old man stood waist-deep in front of the hardware store, arms hanging loose, staring at one of the golden lights with his mouth open. As Xanthe slowed the car, the light burst against the man's chest and he made a sound — a small, wounded, wondering sound — and said, clearly, to no one: *"Dad?"*

A woman in a bathrobe stood on her porch steps with water swirling around her ankles, both hands pressed over her face, and between her fingers Xanthe could see she was smiling and crying at the same time, the way people cry at weddings or funerals, the way you cry when something lost comes home.

He rolled down his window and the smell hit him — not lake smell, not mud and algae. It smelled like his mother's kitchen. Like pencil shavings and cut grass and the inside of the Harrow Bend public library in summer. The water smelled like everything the town had ever forgotten, and it was giving it all back.

A boy of perhaps twelve ran past the car, barefoot through the flood, shouting — not in fear, in *joy*: "Mom! Mom, there was a school! There was a whole *school* down there, I can see it!" And behind him, following the waterline, other children were emerging from their houses, drawn out of sleep by the humming, standing in the shallows with their faces tipped up toward lights only they could fully see.

Xanthe understood then what Prewitt had meant. The founders had built their prosperity on a forgetting so total that even the ground had agreed to keep it. Now the ground was reneging. Every drop of this water was a returned memory, and the town was drowning in its own recovered past — gently, at first, the way grief drowns you, the way truth does.

Then he passed the Ketch house, and it stopped being gentle.

Marla Ketch had died four days ago, and her widower stood in his doorway with the flood swirling around his knees, screaming. Not at anything visible. At the light bursting around him — dozens of them now, faster, insistent, Marla's whole life surfacing at once — and with each burst the man flinched as though struck, whispering *stop, stop, I can't, it's too much,* and Xanthe realized with a lurch that remembering could kill you if it came all at once. That some forgetting was mercy, even stolen mercy. Even *bought* mercy.

He pressed the accelerator. The historical society sat on Courthouse Hill, and the water was climbing faster than he drove.

* * *

Courthouse Hill, when he reached it, was an island.

The flood had risen thirty feet in under twenty minutes — impossible, hydrologically absurd, and utterly beside the point, because this water obeyed no law but its own. Below him the town glowed like a submerged lantern, streets of gold light under black glass, and the humming had resolved into something almost musical. He knew the melody. It took him three bars to place it and when he did his hands went cold on the wheel.

It was his mother's lullaby. The one from the tape. The one she'd sung to two boys in the summer of 1974, and the lake was singing it back.

The historical society's doors stood open. Of course they did. They wanted him to come; they'd always wanted him to come; the whole fortnight had been a corridor narrowing toward this room. He climbed the steps, soaked to the thigh, and followed candlelight down the back stairs into the archive.

Nine chairs had been arranged in a circle around the open covenant case. Eight of the nine founders were alive — he counted them, matching faces to names he'd read in the ledger: Halloway, Brandt, the Voss sisters, old Cutter Meecham who owned half the marina. They stood with joined hands, and in the center of the circle, on a table draped in white cloth, sat a brass basin of lake water.

Silas Prewitt stood barefoot in the basin.

The boy was calm in the way children are calm when adults have told them this is important and special and their part matters. He wore pajamas with rockets on them. Someone had combed his hair. Around the rim of the basin, someone had written words in a language Xanthe didn't know and, looking at it too long, felt begin to *un-know* itself, letters sliding off his comprehension like rain off wax.

"You shouldn't be here," Aldous Prewitt said from the edge of the circle. He wasn't in it. Xanthe registered that — the old man stood outside the ring of joined hands, and his face was gray, and his eyes were wet, and his fists were clenched at his sides. "I called you to warn you, not to—"

"Aldous," said Halloway, without turning her head, "you are either in the covenant or you are nothing. Choose."

"I chose fifty years ago," Prewitt said, "and I've been choosing wrong ever since."

Xanthe stepped forward. "Let the boy go. You don't need him. The debt is coming due regardless — Wendell told me, the taker is dying, your bargain ends whether you renew it or not. All you're buying is another fifty years of lying."

"We're buying *water*," Cutter Meecham snapped. His voice was a rusted hinge. "You think this town survives a second drought? You think the county doesn't come in and carve us up? We fed one child to the lake and Harrow Bend ate for fifty years. One child, Thackeray. Your generation drinks clean and calls it luck."

"He wasn't food," Xanthe said, and his voice came out lower and more dangerous than he'd intended. "He was eight years old. His name was Toby Vance, and you razored him out of a yearbook, and you let his mother go mad with grief for a son no one would admit she'd had, and you've been drinking him ever since."

The circle tightened. Candle flames leaned toward him, all in the same direction, like grass bending under wind.

"You can't say that name," Halloway said, and there was something almost gentle in it, almost pitying. "We took it out of your mouth weeks ago. Say it again. Go on."

He tried. He opened his mouth and reached for the name and felt it dissolve on his tongue — the syllables sliding apart like wet paper, *Tob—* becoming nothing, becoming a cough, becoming air. Rage flooded him so hot his vision swam. They smiled. Eight elderly faces, kind and terrible, watching a man discover the shape of his own silencing.

"That's why the boy," Halloway continued, nodding at Silas. "The covenant requires a voice that loves the taken one. Yours loved Toby — we couldn't use yours once we'd hollowed it. But a new voice, a fresh one, a child who will grow up loving the lake the way we taught our children to love it—" She spread her hands. "The bargain renews. The water stays clean. Everyone forgets this night, including you. It's painless, Xanthe. We designed it to be painless. That was the *kindness* in it."

"The kindness," Xanthe repeated.

Above them, the building shuddered.

Plaster sifted from the ceiling. The candles guttered. Through the small basement windows, the gold light of the flooding town pulsed brighter, and the humming rose in pitch, and beneath the lullaby Xanthe now heard words — hundreds of voices, the dead callers, chanting softly, patiently, a name he could not speak but could hear perfectly, the way you can hum a song you're forbidden to sing.

Silas looked up at the ceiling. The boy's calm cracked, just slightly. "Mimi," he said — his grandmother, Xanthe guessed, or someone else already gone — "there's somebody in the water. He says he's cold."

Every founder in the circle went rigid.

"He always says that," whispered one of the Voss sisters, and her joined hands began to shake, and the circle's geometry wavered like a reflection disturbed.

That was when the water came down the stairs.

It did not pour. It *rose* — climbing the steps in a silent column, a walking pillar of black water studded with gold lights, memories churning visibly inside it like fish. It filled the doorway behind Xanthe and stopped, waiting, and the temperature in the room dropped ten degrees, and Silas began, very quietly, to cry.

"The taker knows," Prewitt breathed. "God help us, it knows what you're doing. It's coming to collect early."

"No," Xanthe said.

Everyone looked at him. Water stood in his shoes; his clothes clung; his heart slammed against his ribs like something trying to get out. But his fear, in this moment, was strangely quiet — because his mother's voice was in his ear from the ruined phone line, from two nights ago, patient and certain: *it needn't be spoken by you, darling. Only returned by love, aloud, at the water.*

"Not early," he said. "*On time.* Fifty years, to the week. You built your clock; it kept it better than you did. And you're right about one thing — the name can't come from me. So I'm going to the one place in this town where a voice that loved him is still kept. Where it's been waiting since 1974."

"The church," Prewitt whispered. His face had gone the color of candle wax. "The steeple. Oh, you fool, the water's over the rooftops by now—"

"Then I'd better swim."

Xanthe turned for the door, and the column of black water turned with him — not blocking him, he realized. *Escorting.* The taker wanted him in the church. The taker had always wanted him in the church. Fine. Let it want. His mother had left him a song, and Wendell owed him a voice, and somewhere in the drowned dark below the reservoir a boy named Tobias Ellery Vance had waited fifty years for someone to walk down and say hello.

Behind him, the covenant circle broke apart into shouting. Halloway's voice rose above the rest, cracking with real fear for the first time in fifty years: "Stop him — Aldous, *stop him*, if the name gets spoken the whole—"

The rest was swallowed by thunder, or by the lake, or by both.

Xanthe went up the stairs into the flooded night, and the water carried him.

* * *

It should not have been possible to move through those streets, and yet the flood bore him along like a hand — swift, sure, almost tender, sweeping him downhill through intersections gone to rapids of light. On every side the town was coming apart into remembering. Windows blazed gold from within. People stood in doorways and yards, soaked, arms raised, weeping or laughing or both, calling out names — names of parents, names of pets, names of streets that hadn't existed since 1974. An entire municipality hallucinating in perfect unison, except none of it was hallucination. That was the horror and the grace of it. It was all true. It had all been true the whole time, held under sixty feet of clean water and a signed page.

He passed the elementary school and saw teachers leading children to high ground, and one little girl turned to watch him pass and said, conversationally, over the roar: "He's glad you're coming. He's been practicing saying *thank you*."

At the bottom of Depot Hill the water deepened past wading, past swimming, and lifted him entirely. He did not fight it. The lake carried him down Meridian like a pallbearer, past the glowing wreck of the radio station — KVHB's tower still standing, transmitter burned out, dead air at last meaning something honest — and out across what had been, until tonight, the northern shore of Lake Meridian.

Ahead, breaking the surface like a black tooth, stood the steeple.

The water set him down against it, gentle as a nurse, and held him there while he caught his breath. The church roof lay six feet below, a shadow under shadow. Somewhere beneath it was the nave, the altar, the place where a congregation had gathered in 1974 to trade a child for rain — and somewhere beneath *that*, coiled in the oldest dark, the taker itself, rising, rising, the humming now so vast he felt it in his teeth, in the bones of his skull, in the scar tissue of his childhood.

He put his lips close to the water and spoke, and though the name dissolved on his tongue, the intention carried, the way a melody carries when you've forgotten the words:

*I brought the song. Bring me my friend.*

The lake went still. Every light in the flood, every golden memory burning across miles of black water, dimmed at once — and then, far below, at the edge of hearing, something answered.

Footsteps. On the submerged roof. Climbing.

And a voice he had known since he was nine years old, worn thin as wire, saying: *"You came, Xanthe. I was afraid you wouldn't. I was afraid right up until now — and oh, you have no idea how good it is, to be afraid of something small."*

The water parted, and Wendell came up out of the church.
