## 8. The Last Verse

The house stopped pretending at dusk.

Felix knew it the way you know a friend has stopped smiling—by what went missing. The drafts stopped explaining things. The floorboards quit their creaking apologies. Even the hum, which had been a constant companion for days now, a tuneless roommate he'd learned to work around, drew itself up into something deliberate.

He was standing in the kitchen with Marisol's map of the house spread across the table—the one they'd drawn together two nights ago, her handwriting looping and confident beside his cramped surveyor's script—when the walls breathed.

Not figuratively. He watched the plaster swell outward an inch, hold, and release, like the flank of something enormous asleep on its side. A hairline crack above the window opened and closed with each breath, a slow wink.

"Okay," Felix said aloud, because saying things aloud had become his way of refusing to be a character in someone else's story. "Okay. I see you."

The house, apparently, had been waiting for exactly that.

* * *

It started with the chairs.

There were eleven of them now in the front parlor—he'd counted that morning, there had been four—and they arranged themselves while he watched, scraping across the boards in short, eager movements, like dogs settling before a meal. They faced the center of the room. They faced him. Every seat angled toward where he stood, patient as an audience before the curtain, and every seat was empty in a way that felt less like absence and more like held breath.

Felix did not sit down. This felt important. He had the sudden, animal certainty that if his knees bent even slightly—if he so much as leaned against the doorframe with the weight of a tired man—the chairs would consider it consent.

Instead he took out his phone and photographed them, because documentation was the only prayer he knew.

The photo showed twelve chairs.

"Oh, come *on*," he said, and the nearest chair rocked forward onto its front legs like a schoolchild raising its hand.

The song began at 3 a.m., because of course it did. It had kept grandmother's hours all week, and Felix had begun to suspect the house didn't so much keep time as *hoard* it, saving its best performances for the hour when the living were weakest.

But tonight the song was different. Tonight it had structure. He lay in his childhood bed—his childhood bed, which still smelled faintly of lavender and camphor and fifty years of a woman sleeping alone and unafraid, or pretending to be—and listened as the melody assembled itself verse by verse, like a hand laying out cards.

Verse one he knew. He'd been mouthing along to it in his sleep until he'd bitten his tongue bloody; the taste of copper was still his morning greeting. Verse two had surfaced during the cellar, during the coats, during the names embroidered inside collars like labels on preserved fruit. Verse three had arrived last night, and it had been beautiful, and that was the worst part—it was genuinely, achingly beautiful, a melody shaped like being carried somewhere safe, and Felix understood now that this was the whole mechanism. The song wasn't a trap you fell into. It was a door you *wanted* to walk through.

And then the fourth verse began, and stopped.

One line. A single rising phrase, the musical equivalent of a stairway ending in open air. And then silence—not absence, but a silence with a shape to it, a silence like a chair pulled out and waiting.

The house wanted him to fill it in. The house had been building toward this for fifty years, maybe longer, maybe since before the house had been a house at all, back when it stood in a village that was now a shipping lane. It had sung three verses and left the last one hanging, and every soul who'd ever heard it had done what souls do: they'd hummed along, filled the gap, invented an ending, given the collector the only thing it couldn't make for itself.

A name freely given is a name freely owned.

Felix lay in the dark with his heart going like a trapped bird and thought: *Grandma never sang.*

Fifty years. She'd lived here fifty years with this thing breathing around her, and she had never once hummed in the bath, never crooned to her plants, never done any of the small unconscious singings that people do without knowing. He'd thought, as a boy, that she simply wasn't musical. He'd thought she was a little sad about it.

She hadn't been sad. She'd been *armed*.

* * *

"You have to show me," Felix said to the empty kitchen at dawn, and his voice cracked on the last word, and he let it, because Marisol had taught him that cracking was allowed. "You spent your whole life not singing. You must have written it down. People like us write everything down."

People like us. He heard himself say it and realized he meant it. His grandmother had been a woman who made herself unnoticeable—who had survived something enormous by being small and quiet and meticulous—and Felix had spent thirty-four years perfecting the same art without ever knowing it was an inheritance. Not cowardice. Curriculum.

The house breathed around him. The chairs in the parlor shifted, resettled, waited.

Nothing answered. But something *led*. He felt it as a pressure behind his sternum, gentle as a hand between shoulder blades, steering him down the hall toward the study—the room he'd been avoiding all week because it was where the ledger lived.

* * *

The ledger sat in the bottom drawer of the writing desk, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string that had gone brittle as old cheese. Felix's hands shook getting it open, and he laughed at himself—a thin, terrified sound—and the laugh seemed to offend the house, because every door upstairs slammed at once.

"Noted," he said. "You don't like jokes. Join the club."

The ledger was an appraisal book. Of course it was. His grandmother had been an appraiser too, in her way—she'd catalogued this house for half a century, every repair, every season, every strange event rendered in her small upright hand. *March 1974. Repointed the chimney. The east wall warm again though no fire lit. Did not sing. Did not sing. Did not sing.*

Page after page. Decades of *did not sing*, entered like rainfall totals, like a farmer tracking weather that could kill him.

And then, near the end, in ink darker than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*If anyone reads this, it will be Felix, because he is the only one who looks closely at things nobody wants looked at. Listen to me, boy. The ending cannot be sung. That is the whole trick of it. It can only be NAMED. Singing is offering. Naming is power. I wrote it backward so that no mouth could stumble into it, so that no sleepy midnight voice could read it aloud by accident. You must turn it around yourself. You must choose it. That choosing is the lock.*

*It is in the columns. Where else would I put it?*

Felix turned back through the ledger with suddenly clumsy fingers, to the accounting pages, the neat grids of figures his grandmother had maintained for fifty years—costs of shingles, costs of coal, costs of a sister's funeral in 1976, entered without comment, paid in full.

He read the numbers forward. Shingle prices. Coal tonnage. Nothing.

Then he held the page up to the window light, the way you check a watermark, and read the columns backward, bottom to top, right to left, letting the digits resolve into letters the way a magic eye picture resolves into a sailboat.

And there it was.

Not a word in any language he knew—but a name, unmistakably a name, the way a face is unmistakably a face even in a crowd of strangers. Letters that predated the drowned village, that predated English, that his grandmother had copied out digit by digit over decades, hiding the collector's true name in plain sight in the most boring document in the most boring room of the house, because she had known—she had *known*—that the thing in the walls could take songs and faces and memories and names freely surrendered, but it could not take what was written backward by a woman too stubborn and too careful to give anything away.

The collected keep only one power, she'd written. The naming. Spend it well. Spend it once.

At the bottom of the page, smaller, in handwriting gone wobbly with age or feeling:

*I am sorry about your mother's photographs. I am sorry about the chair. It was always coming for you, love. You were always its kind. So be its opposite instead. Be seen. Be loud. Be counted.*

*And Felix—when you read this, I will already be part of the house. Don't grieve the coat on the hook. Grieve fast, and then go home.*

* * *

The sun was setting when he finished copying the name—not singing it, not whispering it, just setting it down in his own surveyor's hand on the back of Marisol's map, letter by careful letter, the way you transcribe evidence. His pen scratched. The house held its breath. Somewhere below, in the cellar, he could hear the coats swaying on their hooks, a soft dry rustling like applause rehearsed and withheld.

When the last letter was down, the house exhaled, and the song came back all at once—full chorus, every wall, every pipe, every hollow space in the building joining in, and under the melody he could hear voices now, dozens of them, hundreds, the drowned village and the forgotten residents and his grandmother among them, singing the three verses they'd been made to learn.

The song reached the penultimate line. Rose. Stopped.

Silence, shaped like a chair.

The parlor doors swung open on their own. Down the hall, the audience of chairs waited, and in the middle of them stood one that hadn't been there before—taller than the rest, older, its wood dark as deep water, its seat worn smooth by no one. It turned toward him slowly, the way a host turns to greet the guest of honor, and Felix understood with perfect clarity that this was the collector's whole body: an empty chair, vast and patient, the furniture of being overlooked, waiting for one more person to sit down and be nothing forever.

The silence leaned toward him, offering him the missing verse like a pen offered for a signature.

Felix Almeida stood in the doorway of his grandmother's house, holding a piece of paper with a dead woman's name on it and a living man's name on the other side, and for the first time in his life he did not make himself small.

"No," he said. "I'm not filling in your blank. I'm reading yours."

He unfolded the map. He found the name. And he opened his mouth—not to sing, never to sing—to *speak*, and the first syllable of the collector's true name came out of him like a nail driven into a coffin lid, and every chair in the house turned to face him at once.

The silence broke like glass.
