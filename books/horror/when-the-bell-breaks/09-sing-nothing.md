## 9. Sing Nothing

The chorus woke him at three minutes past three, which was fitting, because Felix had always been a man of precise and unremarkable habits, and the house had learned his schedule the way a spider learns the vibrations of its web.

He came down the stairs without turning on the lights. There was no point. The house was lit from within now—a low, honeyed glow seeping through the wallpaper seams, as though the walls themselves had swallowed a sunset and were digesting it slowly. The song poured through everything: floorboards, banister, the fillings in his teeth. It was beautiful. That was the worst part. It was genuinely, achingly beautiful, a lullaby in no language he knew, sung by no throats he could count, and every note landed in Felix's chest like a hand settling on a shoulder that had been waiting years for someone to touch it.

*Come along,* said the song, without words. *You've been so quiet for so long. Come be quiet with us forever.*

In the hallway, the coats were swaying.

All of them—the surveyor's oilskin, the child's duffel, the woman's cardigan with the moth-eaten elbow—hung on their hooks and rocked gently back and forth, back and forth, like a congregation swaying at hymn time. Their empty sleeves lifted slightly on a draft that did not exist. Inside each collar, Felix knew, a name was embroidered in thread gone soft with age. Names nobody remembered. Names the world had let go of, one careless decade at a time.

"Not tonight," Felix told them. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which he chose to interpret as growth.

The coats swayed on, patient. They had all the patience in the world. They were, after all, mostly patience now.

He walked toward the cellar door, because Grandma's ghost—or memory, or whatever stubborn residue of Almira Ravensworth-Almeida refused to be filed under *vacant*—had shown him where she'd hidden the ending. The ledger. Written backward, mirror-wise, in her cramped schoolteacher's hand, because she had understood something Felix was only now beginning to grasp: that naming is power, but only if you do it yourself. Only if you say it rather than sing it. A name offered in melody belongs to the melody. A name spoken plainly, in your own dry, unmusical voice, belongs to you.

The cellar stairs creaked their familiar complaint. At the bottom, the room had changed.

The chairs had come down to meet him.

They stood arranged in rows facing the far wall where the thin door—the throat-colored one, the one Marisol had nearly walked through—waited with its paint glistening faintly, like something swallowing. And before the chairs, filling the space the way weather fills a valley, stood the collector.

Felix had expected teeth. He had expected vastness with appetite in it, hunger given architecture. What he got instead was an empty chair.

It was a chair the way the ocean is a puddle. It had the shape of one—seat, back, four legs—but scaled wrong, impossibly, its seat rising higher than the ceiling should allow, its legs rooted through the concrete floor into whatever the house was actually standing on. It was made of the same honeyed light as the walls, or perhaps the walls were made of it. It did not move. It did not need to. It simply *was*, with the enormous, unhurried presence of something that has never once been late for anything.

And it was looking at him. Being looked at by an empty chair should have been impossible. Felix felt it anyway: attention, ancient and gentle and absolutely total, settling over him like snowfall.

*Felix Almeida,* it said, and its voice was the song, slowed down until the words could form. *Surveyor. Grandson. Ghost-in-waiting. We have kept your chair warm.*

"I noticed," Felix said. "Nine chairs in every photograph. Very thorough."

*We are thorough.* Something like pride moved through the light. *We found you when you were six years old, hiding behind your mother's coat at your own birthday party, praying no one would call on you. We thought: there. There is one who has already begun leaving himself. We only had to wait for him to finish.*

"That's the thing," Felix said, and here his voice did waver, because this part was true and true things are heavy. "I did finish. I spent forty years making myself so small that people literally forgot my face by lunchtime. My own firm struck my name off the roster while I was still drawing a paycheck." He took a breath. "I know exactly what I am. I'm the easiest meal you've ever caught."

The collector's light warmed. *Yes.*

"But," Felix said, "you're missing something."

*Nothing is missing. We are complete. You will be complete too. No more measuring rooms that won't hold still. No more being overlooked. Here, you will be the most attended-to thing in existence. Every coat on these hooks was once someone who wanted exactly what you want. Rest, Felix. Sit. Be held.*

And God help him, Felix wanted it. That was the trap's whole genius—not fear, not force, just an offer so precisely tuned to the shape of his loneliness that refusing it felt like refusing water in a drought. Total peace. Permanent belonging. To be seen, finally, ceaselessly, by something that would never look away.

Marisol's voice surfaced in his memory, unimpressed and wonderful: *You know you're allowed to just tell people your name, right? Like, out loud? To their faces?*

"See, that's the difference," Felix said. "You offer belonging. She offers introductions."

He walked forward—not to the chair, but past it, to the workbench against the cellar wall where Grandma's ledger lay open under a film of dust. His hands shook. He let them. Hands that shake can still turn pages.

The final verse waited where she'd left it, written in reverse, each letter flipped like a reflection:

**NAMED BE THE NAMER, NOT THE SONG.
I SAY YOU, THING OF EMPTY SEATS.
I SAY YOUR NAME: HOLLOWMEN, HOLLOWMEN,
WHO FILL YOUR HOUSE WITH FORGOTTEN FEET—
VESSEL-THAT-WAS-NOTHING-FIRST,
CHAIR-BEFORE-THERE-WERE-CHAIRS,
I NAME YOU LACKING. I NAME YOU LACKING.
AND WHAT IS NAMED MUST ANSWER.**

Below it, in her normal handwriting, small and fierce: *Say it. Don't sing it. Singing is joining. Saying is commanding. Forgive me, Felix. I fed it Ruthie so I could die in my own bed, and I have regretted it every morning since. You are braver than I was. Be seen going out.*

The chorus surged. The coats rose on their hooks, sleeves spread wide, and the whole house leaned in like an audience at the moment before the aria, and the collector's voice wrapped around him, sweet and vast: *Sing with us, Felix. Just the once. Just the last line. You needn't mean it. You need only hum.*

"No," Felix said.

And then, in the flattest, most tuneless, most gloriously ordinary voice he possessed—the voice of a man who reads terms and conditions, who orders the same soup every Thursday, who has never once been asked to sing karaoke and would decline—Felix Almeida read the verse aloud.

Not sang. *Said.*

"I name you, thing of empty seats. Hollowmen, Hollowmen, who fill your house with forgotten feet—"

The song stumbled. Actually stumbled, the way a choir stumbles when the organist falls off the bench. The honeyed light flickered.

"Vessel-that-was-nothing-first. Chair-before-there-were-chairs." His voice cracked on the next line and he pushed through the crack, because Grandma hadn't written *be brave*, she'd written *be seen*, and those turned out to be the same instruction. "I name you lacking. I name you lacking."

The collector did not scream. That was somehow worse. It *dimmed*—the vast patient light contracting, folding inward, the way a crowd goes quiet—and for one naked instant Felix saw what it actually was: not a monster, not a god, just an emptiness so old it had learned to dress itself as comfort. An absence wearing upholstery. A nothing that had spent centuries persuading forgotten people to climb inside it and call the silence peace.

"And what is named," Felix finished, "must answer."

Silence.

Then the house answered.

Every wall convulsed at once. The honeyed light drained downward like bathwater, and the chairs toppled in rows, domino-slow, and the coats dropped from their hooks and lay flat on the floor, suddenly just coats, just wool and moths and embroidered names that were finally, finally only names. The thin door—the throat-colored one—shrieked along its hinges, swung itself shut with a slam that Felix felt in his sternum, and then, with a sound like a held breath released after fifty years, collapsed into a drift of gray ash that settled gently across the cellar floor.

The song was gone. In its place: the tick of cooling pipes. The drip of an honest, ordinary leak. A house, doing nothing, which was the most beautiful sound Felix had ever heard.

He sat down on the bottom step and laughed until he cried, which he noted, wiping his face on his sleeve, was also probably the first time anyone had heard him do that.

At the top of the stairs, a knock. Three raps, brisk, familiar.

"Felix?" Marisol's voice, muffled through the door. "The porch light just came on by itself and I swear if this house is starting something—"

"It's done," he called back, and his voice carried, clear and audible and unmistakably his. "You can come down. Mind the ash."

A pause. "There's *ash*?"

"Long story. It ends well. Mostly."

Her footsteps came down the stairs, and Felix Almeida stood up to meet them—standing straight, in full view, entirely visible—and began, for the first time in his life, to tell someone what happened to him in his own words.

Behind them, unnoticed by both, the last of the ash stirred once in a draft from the open cellar door, and settled, and was still.

But three streets away, in a house that had stood empty for eleven years, a pipe hummed a single low note—and paused, as if listening for someone to hum it back.
