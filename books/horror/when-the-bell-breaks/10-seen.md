## 10. Seen

The house, in the end, was just a house.

That was the strangest part. Felix had expected—had braced himself for—some residue. A hum under the floorboards when the wind came off the marsh. A door that didn't quite match the others. The smell of salt and old hymnals. But three weeks after the night the walls convulsed and the thin door went to ash between one breath and the next, Number 9 Latchmere Row stood on its lot like any other tired Victorian on a tired street: sagging porch, gutters full of last autumn's leaves, a garden gone to seed in a way that suggested neglect rather than malice.

He'd walked it twice that morning with his clipboard and his laser measure, and the numbers had come back the same every time. He'd measured the parlor four times anyway, standing in the middle of the room with his thumb hovering over the button, waiting for the readout to change. It didn't. 14.2 by 11.8 meters. Fourteen point two by eleven point eight. He almost laughed. Rooms that lied to you were terrifying; rooms that told the truth over and over were almost embarrassing, like a bully who'd forgotten why he'd started.

There were ten chairs in the parlor now. He'd counted them the first day back, heart in his throat, and found them exactly as the house had arranged them on that final night—an audience, frozen mid-attention—and then he'd spent an entire afternoon carrying nine of them out to the garage, one at a time, because some things you don't leave for the new owners to find.

One chair he kept. Not from the house—he'd bought it, actually, forty pounds from a secondhand shop on Fenwick Street, oak with a wobble in the left leg that he'd fixed himself with a shim and more patience than the job deserved. It sat in his flat now, in the corner of the living room, and he ate dinner in it most nights. It was an ordinary chair. That was the point. You don't give the thing that collected you the satisfaction of keeping the seat warm.

* * *

The appraisal took him two days, and he wrote it honestly, which felt like a kind of prayer.

*Property: 9 Latchmere Row. Detached dwelling, c. 1898, relocated from the drowned village of Marrow's End circa 1931.* He'd found that last detail in the parish records, buried in a margin note about "resettlement of structures above the flood line," as if the house had simply been carried up the hill on someone's shoulders, humming all the way. *Condition: fair. Deferred maintenance throughout. No structural defects observed.*

He sat with that sentence for a long time. *No structural defects observed.* True, as far as it went. What had been wrong with the house had never been structural. It had been appetite.

In the section for *notable features*, he wrote: *Original pine floors; cast-iron fireplace, cellar; cellar contains historical artifacts of previous occupancy (coats, footwear, personal effects), recommended for museum assessment prior to clearance.* He did not write down the names embroidered inside the collars. He'd copied them into his own notebook instead—all eleven of them, plus his grandmother's sister, whose name had been Edith and who had been twelve years old when Grandma Almeida, nineteen and terrified and alone in a house that sang, had made the arithmetic of survival that Felix still couldn't forgive and couldn't entirely condemn. He'd looked up Edith in the records. Drowned, 1931, Marrow's End. The journal hadn't been lying about that either. The house hadn't taken her so much as *accepted delivery*, and Grandma had spent fifty years paying off a debt she never named aloud, never singing, never humming, teaching herself to whistle only through her teeth on days the walls got loud.

Felix had donated the coats to the county historical society. A woman there named Dr. Okafor had wept over the embroidery, said these were people nobody had ever gone looking for, and asked him, twice, how on earth he'd found them. He'd told her the truth, or the shape of it: "My grandmother kept them safe until someone could see them."

It was the first time in his life he'd said something like that without flinching.

At the bottom of the appraisal, in the signature block, he wrote his name. Not the abbreviated scrawl he'd used for fifteen years of reports—the *F. Almeida* that let clients squint at it and call him "Fred" or "Alan" or nothing at all. He wrote **Felix Tomas Almeida**, each letter deliberate, the crossbar on the T slightly too long, the way he'd written it in primary school when he'd still believed names were things people wanted to know.

His supervisor, Mr. Hendricks, reviewed the report on Thursday and frowned at it for a long moment.

"Almeida," he said. "You're the one who did the Latchmere job?"

"I'm the one who did the Latchmere job," Felix agreed.

"Huh." Hendricks tapped the signature. "Thought we had someone named Felix. Roster says—" He checked his screen, and whatever he saw there made him sit up straighter. "Huh. Says you're back on. Since three weeks ago. Funny, I could've sworn..."

"Paperwork error," Felix said. "It's been corrected."

"Well." Hendricks signed off on the report, then, unexpectedly, extended his hand. "Good work. Thorough. Come by the Christmas do this year, yeah? We never see you at those."

"I'll be there," Felix said, and meant it, and watched Hendricks carry the report away with the small, private astonishment of a man who has just realized a colleague has a face.

* * *

Marisol picked him up at seven on Friday, leaning on the horn outside his flat like they'd known each other for decades instead of a month and one shared nightmare.

"You're late," she said when he got in, which was a lie, which was her way of saying hello.

"There's traffic."

"There's always traffic. That's not lateness, that's physics." She pulled out before he'd fully closed the door. "Anyway, you can't be late tonight. Tonight is sacred. Tonight I am introducing you to my friends, and if you're late it looks like I made you up."

"You did make me up," Felix said. "For about a week there, so did the firm."

"Don't joke about that." But she was grinning, and her hands were steady on the wheel, and the streetlights came through the windshield in long gold bars, and Felix thought: *this is what belonging feels like from the inside. It feels like being teased about punctuality.*

Dinner was at Priya and Tomás's flat, above a bakery that made the whole stairwell smell like Sunday morning. There were six people around the table, and Marisol stood at the head of it with a glass of wine and said, "Everyone, this is Felix Almeida. He's a surveyor, he saved my life, and he makes terrible jokes about property lines. Be nice to him or answer to me."

"That's a very generous summary," Felix said.

"It's accurate. Sit down, Felix. You're next to me. Obviously."

And here was the thing he kept turning over afterward, walking home through the cold bright streets: nobody at that table forgot him. Not once, not even a little. Priya asked about the historical society donation twice over the course of the evening because she'd genuinely been thinking about it. Tomás argued with him about whether the house on Latchmere Row should be landmarked ("It's got history!" "It's got dry rot, Tomás") and remembered the argument the next time Felix refilled his wine. At one point Marisol's friend Dev leaned across the table and said, "How do you do it? Surveying old houses. Isn't it grim? All those lives layered up in the walls?"

"Sometimes," Felix said. "But mostly it's just people trying to live somewhere. And somebody ought to pay attention to that."

Dev raised his glass to that. So did everyone else. And Felix Almeida, who had spent thirty-four years perfecting the art of being passed over, sat in a warm room full of people who knew his name and let himself be seen, and discovered that it didn't burn. It didn't even sting. It was just light.

He kept one photograph from the estate inventory. The rest went to the archive boxes with the coats, catalogued and honored, but the last one—the family portrait from 1987, the one where every face smiled at a camera and behind them, angled toward the lens with terrible patience, stood ten chairs instead of nine—that one lived on his bookshelf now, propped against a stack of novels. Marisol had asked him once why he kept it.

"So I remember what it costs," he'd said. "Being overlooked isn't neutral. Somebody's always collecting the difference."

She'd looked at him for a moment, then nodded slowly, the way you nod at a truth you're glad you didn't have to learn firsthand. "And the tenth chair?"

"Bought my own," he said. "Forty pounds. Wobbly leg. Fixed it myself."

* * *

On Sunday he drove out past the marsh one last time, to check the sale listing photos before they went live. The house stood empty in the gray-gold light, windows reflecting clouds, porch steps swept. Ordinary. A young couple had already made an offer—a nurse and a teacher, the agent said, first-time buyers, planning to put in a garden.

Felix stood at the gate for a while, listening.

Nothing. Wind in the dead grass, a dog barking two streets over, the tick of the house cooling in the afternoon air. Just a house, holding its silence the way houses do—not hungry, not patient, simply quiet. He touched the gatepost once, the way you'd touch a gravestone, and said, "Rest," though he wasn't sure to whom, and walked back to his car.

He was halfway down the lane when his phone buzzed. Marisol: *Priya wants to know if you're coming to trivia Thursday. I already said yes. This is called having friends, look it up.*

He laughed out loud, alone in the car on an empty road, and typed back *I'll be there,* and signed it with his whole name, because it was becoming a habit, and because habits are how you keep a promise to yourself.

The road curved past the old parish boundary, and beyond it the land dropped toward the flats where Marrow's End had drowned, and where other villages had crowded uphill over the centuries, resettling, rebuilding, carrying their timber and their slate and whatever else came along with them.

In one of those villages, in a house at the end of a lane where the streetlight doesn't quite reach, a floorboard settled in the cooling evening with a sound almost like a note.

Then another, a half-step lower.

And in the kitchen, where no one was sitting, an empty chair turned—slowly, gently, the way a listener turns toward music—to face the room.
