## 5. Doors Counted

Rosalind had counted doors since she was six years old.

Not casually—not the way people count stairs or streetlights, half-attentive, forgetful. She counted the way other people prayed. Every doorway in every place she slept, touched with her fingertips and named aloud in a whisper: *front, back, kitchen, hall, hall, bath.* The number written on her wrist in ballpoint pen each night before bed, checked against the morning's count before her feet touched the floor.

She had never told anyone why. There wasn't a why, not one she could say out loud without sounding like something that should be medicated. There was only the memory of her mother, kneeling in the doorway of their apartment in Duluth with a jar of salt in one hand and Rosalind's small hand in the other, saying, *Count them, Rosie. A house will lie to you about anything except what you make it admit.*

Her mother had died when Rosalind was nineteen, and by then the counting was so deep in her it had become indistinguishable from breathing.

Hollowmere House had eleven doors.

She knew this because she had counted them four times on her first day, walking the halls with her tape measure and her clipboard, touching each frame—*front, back, kitchen, pantry, cellar, parlor, study, stairs, upstairs hall, primary bedroom, bath*—and writing **11** on her wrist in blue ink before she let herself sleep in the surveyor's cot she'd set up in the east bedroom. Eleven was correct. Eleven matched the blueprints, matched her own measurements, matched the house's confession of itself.

On the second morning, there were twelve.

* * *

She found it at 6:40 a.m., standing at the top of the stairs with her wrist already raised, counting out of habit before her mind was fully awake. *Front, back, kitchen, pantry, cellar, parlor, study, stairs, upstairs hall—*

The twelfth door stood where the upstairs hall met the west wall, between the linen closet and the window that looked out over the orchard. It was a plain door, painted the same tired white as all the others, with a brass knob gone dull as an old coin. It fit the wall the way a word fits a sentence. It looked like it had always been there.

It had not been there last night. Rosalind would have staked her license, her savings, her entire careful gray life on that. Last night the wall between the linen closet and the window had been eleven feet of plaster and wallpaper faded to the color of weak tea. She had measured it twice, because she measured everything twice now, because the numbers in this house refused to hold still like numbers were supposed to.

She stood at the top of the stairs for a long time, one hand on the banister, watching the door the way you'd watch a dog that might be friendly and might not.

Then she did what she always did. She walked toward it slowly, touched the frame with two fingers, and whispered, "Twelve."

The wood was warm under her fingertips. Not sun-warm—the window across the hall was cold, the whole west side of the house cold in the early light—but warm the way a body is warm. Warm like something had been leaning against the other side of it, waiting.

She wrote **12** on her wrist over the fading **11**, and told herself, in the flat professional voice she used for clients who wanted to hear that cracks were character, that old houses settle. That walls get moved. That some previous owner, decades ago, had closed off a doorway and some later owner had opened it again, and her own memory of the blank wall was simply wrong, simply tired, simply the ordinary unreliability of a human brain at the end of a long day in a big empty house.

She almost believed it. Believing things was a skill she'd practiced her whole life, the way other people practiced piano.

But she didn't open the twelfth door. She noted its position on her floor plan—a small square penciled in with hands that were steadier than she felt—and she went downstairs to make coffee in a kitchen where the tap water ran warm no matter how long she let it, and she began her day.

That was the deal she made with herself, and the deal held for exactly one day.

* * *

On the third morning there were thirteen.

The new door was in the cellar, at the far end of the coal room, where yesterday there had been a wall of fieldstone sweating cold mineral smell. This one was oak, dark, iron-hinged, older than the others—it looked like it had been pried out of some earlier century of the house and installed overnight by patient invisible carpenters. No handle this time. Just a keyhole, round and black, and from behind it, when she pressed her ear close enough that her breath fogged the wood, a sound like air moving through a long throat.

She didn't put her ear to it again.

She went back up to the kitchen and sat at the table with her clipboard and did the thing she was best at in the world, better than measuring, better than being forgotten: she made a list.

**Doors — Hollowmere House**
- Day 1: 11 (matches blueprints)
- Day 2: 12 (+1, upstairs hall, west wall)
- Day 3: 13 (+1, cellar, coal room)

She stared at the list until the numbers blurred. Then she added a fourth line, because she was a surveyor and surveyors extrapolate:

- Day 4: ?

And beneath that, in smaller handwriting, the question she'd been circling since the first morning, when the rooms wouldn't measure the same twice:

*What is it growing toward?*

Because that was the thing about a pattern—one data point is noise, two is coincidence, but three is a direction. The house wasn't decaying. Decay loses things. This house was *adding*, patiently, at the rate of one door per day, and if the rate held then by closing day—by the day her firm had penciled in, the day the paperwork would need her signature—there would be twenty-two doors in a house that had confessed to eleven.

Eleven more openings than the house was built to have. Eleven more ways in. Or out. Or through.

Rosalind got up and walked the whole house, touching every frame, whispering the count, and when she arrived back at thirteen she took out her phone and photographed every single door, front and frame and knob, timestamped, geotagged, filed into a folder she named DOORS in capital letters because lowercase felt too casual for whatever this was.

The photos came out fine. That somehow made it worse. In the pictures the thirteenth door looked completely ordinary, completely real, a door any inspector would sign off on—and Rosalind understood, with a cold sliding feeling in her stomach, that she had no evidence anything strange was happening here at all. Only her word against a house's. And nobody, anywhere, had ever taken her word over a building's.

* * *

That evening she called her sister.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to call the county historical society about the Voss family papers, and then she'd meant to call her firm's office line and leave a message about structural anomalies, and instead her thumb had found the contact labeled *Nadia*—last called fourteen months ago, last spoken to warmly nine years ago—and pressed it before the thinking part of her could intervene.

Nadia picked up on the third ring. "Rosie?"

"Nadia." Her sister's name in her mouth felt like a key to a house she'd moved out of. "Hi. Sorry. I know it's late."

"It's eight." A pause, and Rosalind could hear her shifting somewhere, a couch creaking, a child—no, not a child, a television—murmuring in the background. "Is everything okay? You never call."

"I'm working. Upstate. An old house." Rosalind looked at the kitchen doorway, counted it without meaning to. *Kitchen.* "I had a question. About Mom."

Another pause, longer. When Nadia spoke again her voice had changed, gone careful, the way you talk near a sleeping animal. "Okay."

"Did she ever—" Rosalind stopped. Started over. "When we were kids. The counting. Did she ever tell you why we counted doors?"

"She told *you* why. I got the salt." A short laugh, not quite amused. "You got the counting, I got the thresholds. Divide and conquer, right? Rosie watches the doors, Nadia keeps the lines drawn." Her voice did something complicated. "God, I haven't thought about that in years. Why? What happened?"

"Nothing happened. I just—" Rosalind pressed her palm flat against the table, feeling its honest grain. "I found myself doing it here. Counting. And I realized I don't actually remember her explaining it. I remember her showing me. Kneeling down in the doorway. But the words are gone."

Nadia was quiet for a moment. "She said houses keep accounts," she said finally, reciting it like scripture recovered from a flood. "That's the part I remember, and I don't even know if it's hers or something her mother said. *A house keeps accounts, and if you don't count first, it counts you.*" A breath. "Rosie, why are you asking me this? What kind of house is it?"

"A colonial. Eighteen-forties. Big." Rosalind watched the kitchen doorway. Watched the hallway beyond it, where the shadows pooled at the exact depth where electric light gave up and older darkness started. "It has more doors today than it did yesterday."

The silence on the line went on so long she checked whether the call had dropped.

"Nadia?"

"I'm here." Her sister's voice had gone very quiet and very level, the voice of someone standing very still. "Say that again."

"The house has thirteen doors. The plans say eleven. I've measured every room twice and the measurements won't repeat. There's a space between the walls on the second floor that isn't on any blueprint and it's warm, Nadia, it's warm like—" She stopped herself before she said *like skin*. "I'm probably overtired. Old houses do weird things acoustically, thermally, there are rational explanations for—"

"Rosie." Nadia's voice cracked the sentence open. "Mom turned around three times before she'd go back into any room she'd left. You remember that? And she never, ever slept in a house where she couldn't see the front door from the bed. And the year before she got sick, she tried to buy back Grandma's place in Two Harbors, and when the sale fell through she cried for a week, and when I asked her why she said—" Nadia's breath caught. "She said, *better it stays empty than it gets a new tenant while I'm still alive to miss her.*"

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know! I was fifteen!" Now Nadia was almost shouting, and somewhere behind her a door closed and her voice dropped. "I don't know what any of it means, Rosie, I spent thirty years pretending our childhood was normal, and now you're calling me from some house that's *growing doors* and asking me to remember, and I can't—Rosie, listen to me. Listen. If the count is going up, you leave. You get in your car tonight and you leave, and you call me from a motel, and you don't go back. Promise me."

Rosalind looked down at her wrist. **13**, in blue ink, beginning to smudge.

"I can't," she said. "The appraisal closes in two weeks. If I walk off it, they send someone else, and—" She didn't finish the sentence, because finishing it meant saying *and whoever comes next won't count*, and saying that out loud would make it a fact instead of a fear, and facts had weight, and this house seemed to be listening for weight.

"That's not a reason. That's a job."

"It's the only thing I'm good at."

"Rosie—"

"I have to go. I'll call you tomorrow." And she hung up, which was a coward's move, and she knew it, and she did it anyway, because the alternative was letting her sister hear her cry, and Rosalind Marchetti had not let anyone hear her cry in nineteen years, and she was not going to start in a house with too many doors.

* * *

She did not sleep well, and when she did sleep, she dreamed of her mother kneeling in a doorway that kept getting farther away, salt running through her fingers like sand in an hourglass, saying words Rosalind couldn't quite catch—*count them, count them, count them*—except that in the dream the words were wrong, they were *counting you, counting you, counting you*, and Rosalind woke at 3 a.m. with her heart slamming and lay in the dark of the east bedroom doing the thing that calmed her, the inventory, the litany: *eleven doors, four exterior walls, two floors, one cellar, one Rosalind.*

At 6:40 she stood at the top of the stairs, raised her wrist, and counted.

*Front, back, kitchen, pantry, cellar, coal room, parlor, study, stairs, upstairs hall, west hall, primary, bath—*

Fourteen.

The new door was in the east bedroom. Her bedroom. It stood in the north wall, six feet from the foot of her cot, and it was not old, and it was not plain, and it was not like the others.

It was a brand-new door, pine still bright as fresh-cut lumber, unpainted, unhung hardware glinting, and it was sized exactly, precisely, to the dimensions of the space between the walls—the hidden room that existed on no blueprint, the narrow warm gap she had found and measured and filed away as an anomaly.

Someone—or something—had built a door from the inside of her bedroom to the inside of the house's body. And it had done it in one night, quietly, within arm's reach of where she slept.

Rosalind stood there a long time, in her wrinkled clothes, holding her clipboard against her chest like a shield. Then, because she was who she was—because measurement was the only prayer she knew—she crossed the room, knelt, and laid her tape measure along the door's height.

Seventy-nine inches. Standard.

Its width was twenty-eight inches. Narrow. A door for someone thin. A door for someone who had learned to take up as little space as possible.

A door her size.

She wrote **14** on her wrist, and her hand shook so badly the numerals came out doubled, ghosted, like a lagging reflection of themselves. Then she packed her instruments, loaded her car, and drove into town to find the county records office—because if the house was keeping accounts, she was going to read its ledger, and she needed to know the names of everyone who had ever signed for it before her.

Four names, she'd find. Four signatures, four closings, four buyers who vanished off the face of the earth the day the ink dried.

But that discovery was still ahead of her. All she knew, driving down the orchard road with the house shrinking in her mirror, was the number on her wrist and the arithmetic underneath it: the house grew one door per day, and closing was twelve days away, and twelve plus fourteen was twenty-six—

—and she had the sudden, sick certainty that the house was not building doors at random.

It was building a way out. For something that was currently inside.

Or a way in—for whoever it was building them *for*.
