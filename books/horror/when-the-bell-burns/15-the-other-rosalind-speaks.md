## 15. The Other Rosalind Speaks

The house stopped pretending on the ninth night.

Rosalind knew because the silence changed. For weeks Hollowmere had creaked and settled like any old building, a soundtrack of ordinary decay she'd used to steady herself—*just wood, just weather, just an old house holding its breath.* But that night the silence was total. No settling. No wind against the east windows. Even her own breathing sounded borrowed, as if the house were listening to it before letting her keep it.

She sat at the kitchen table with Mirabel's note flattened under a water glass: *It keeps the ones who half-choose.* She had read it maybe two hundred times. She could recite it in her sleep, and suspected she did.

The hallway mirror was visible from where she sat, at the end of the long sightline through the kitchen doorway—a pale rectangle in the dark, catching what little light leaked from the lamp beside her. She had covered every mirror in the house during the first week. Sheets, towels, a coat of painter's drop cloth taped over the big vanity upstairs. But the sheets came down each morning, folded neatly on the floor beside them, corners squared with a precision she didn't possess. The house unwrapped its eyes while she slept.

So she had stopped covering them. That was the trap of it, she was learning. Every defense she mounted became one more thing the house could undo, one more small humiliation, until the only move left was to stop defending and simply watch back.

She watched the rectangle of glass now.

"Say it," she said quietly. Her voice sounded thin in the dead air. "Whatever you've been rehearsing. Say it."

For a long moment, nothing. Then the reflection moved.

Not the lag—that old one-second delay, the hesitation she'd catalogued and tested and nearly made peace with. This was different. The figure in the glass turned its head toward her *before* Rosalind turned hers. It looked at her across the distance of the kitchen doorway with an expression Rosalind recognized because she'd spent forty-one years practicing the opposite of it: open, unhurried, certain of its welcome.

"Hello, Roz," the mirror said.

Her own voice. Warmer than she ever let hers be.

Rosalind's hand found the edge of the table and held on. "That's not my name."

"It is." The woman in the glass rose—Rosalind rose a half-second later, her body obeying some reflex older than thought—and came forward until they stood three feet apart, glass between them. Up close, in the dark, the differences were easier to see and harder to bear. The same face, but the mouth unclenched. The same eyes, but looking *at* things instead of past them. She wore the green dress Rosalind had tried on in a shop in Albany eleven years ago and put back on the rack because the color drew attention, because someone might say something, because someone might look.

"You kept the tag," the mirror-self said, touching her own sleeve. "From that dressing room. It's still in your coat pocket. You couldn't throw it away."

Rosalind's hand went to her coat on the hook by the door before she could stop it. Stopped. Came back.

"That's not proof of anything," she said. "You're in my head. You know my head."

"I'm not in your head." The other woman smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was the worst thing yet. "I'm in your *wake*. Everything you decided not to do, everything you swallowed so no one would see you do it—it doesn't disappear, Roz. It goes somewhere. It went here." She pressed her palm flat to the inside of the glass. "I'm the sum of your restraint. And I have been waiting a very long time for you to run out."

* * *

Rosalind walked away first. She heard the other woman laugh softly behind her—not mocking, worse, *understanding*—and climbed the stairs with the lamp held high like a weapon that had never once worked.

In the master bedroom she did the inventory. Doors: five, correct. Windows: four, latched, correct. Mirror over the vanity: uncovered, as expected, and empty—showing only the room, only her, standing there rigid with her lamp, arriving exactly on time for once, as if the other one had stepped out of the timeline entirely to make room.

She checked the second room's wall anyway. Sealed. She'd known it would be; it had been sealed since the night after the dinner, the hairline seam in the plaster gone smooth as poured wax. Abigail Voss's half-carved circle, the folded clothes of four vanished owners—all of it behind stone now, or something like stone. The house had closed that door when Rosalind started asking questions at the dinner table. It opened doors for her these days only when they led where it wanted.

She sat on the bed and put her face in her hands.

"Let me guess," she said to the room. "You want to talk about the signature."

When she looked up, the vanity mirror wasn't showing the bedroom anymore.

It showed the kitchen—the table, the lamp, the water glass pressing flat Mirabel's note. And the other Rosalind sitting at the table, reading the note with a slight frown, as if grading it.

"The old woman means well," the mirror said, without looking up. "But she's arguing from a position of loss. Of course she told you not to choose. She chose wrong—for herself. Refusing to choose isn't noble, Roz. It's just the slowest way to lose."

"How are you doing this?" Rosalind asked. Her voice came out level, and she was absurdly proud of that. "You're not in the hall anymore. You're everywhere the glass is."

"I'm wherever you aren't," the other woman said. "That's been true your whole life. The house just gave me a place to stand."

She set down the note—in the mirror-kitchen, the paper slid out from under the glass—and looked up directly at Rosalind through the vanity, through the dark, through whatever the glass actually was.

"So here's my offer. And I want you to hear all of it before you decide I'm the monster."

* * *

"I know what tomorrow is," the mirror said.

Rosalind stood in the middle of the bedroom, arms crossed, refusing to sit because sitting felt like staying. "Closing is in six days."

"Closing is whenever you sign. The date is theater. The buyer likes theater; it makes people feel like events are happening *to* them instead of *because* of them. You know how that works. You've built a whole life on feeling like things happen to you." The other woman tilted her head. "But yes. Six days. Nine days since the call. He'll bring papers, you'll sign, and one of us walks out of this house a person and one of us stays in the glass. That part is fixed. That part is the founders' arithmetic, and even I don't get to change it."

"But the rest," Rosalind said slowly, "is negotiable."

"The rest is entirely negotiable." The mirror-self stood, and in the glass she crossed toward the vanity until their faces nearly touched, separated by silvered dark. "Here's what he wants. Here's what the town wants. A resident who signs *willingly*. Not coerced, not tricked—willing. The ritual runs cleaner that way. The valley's luck runs deeper. They've had thin years, Roz. Did you notice? The Hendersons' orchard, the mill's second shift. The house has been running on fumes since the last exchange, and everyone's too polite to say why. They need you to mean it."

"And if I don't mean it?"

"Then he'll spend six days making you mean it. Dinners. Kindness. Pressure wearing the face of hospitality. He's very good. He was good at this when Abigail Voss was alive." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Or—"

"Or."

"Or I sign."

The word hung in the room. Rosalind realized she'd stopped breathing and made herself start.

"You can't," she said. "You're a reflection. You can't hold a pen."

"In the glass, I can do everything you can. And on closing day, the boundary gets thin. Thin enough that if you *step aside*—really step aside, not fight me, just stop insisting—I can reach the pen. I can write your name. My version of it. It will be close enough. It's always been close enough; that's the whole problem, isn't it? Nobody can tell us apart. Your firm couldn't. Your sister couldn't." The other woman's voice gentled. "*You* barely can."

"And then what happens to me?"

"You go back to being no one."

Rosalind flinched. The mirror caught the flinch and held it up gently, like something broken being shown to its owner.

"No—listen. Listen to me, because this is the part you've never let anyone say out loud. Being no one is not death. I know you've been telling yourself it's a trap, that erasure is the horror, that the four owners in the county records were destroyed. Were they? Think about it. No bodies. No grief. No obituaries with the wrong details. They just... stopped being *looked at*. Which—" the smile again, soft, devastating— "is the life you already live. You've been rehearsing vanishing since you were nine years old, Roz. Since your mother taught you the door-counting and the light-leaving and the art of entering rooms like an apology. The house isn't offering you something strange. It's offering you the thing you've been practicing for. Permanently. Safely. Without the dying part."

"I'd be trapped in a mirror."

"For a while. Until the next exchange, fifty years from now, when some other tired person stands where you're standing. And in between?" The other woman spread her hands, and Rosalind saw the gesture belonged to a life she'd never lived—someone comfortable being watched. "In between, I live yours. I sign the sale. I drive your car to a city where nobody knows you. I wear the green dress. I answer to Rosalind Eriksen loudly, in restaurants, in meetings, badly and beautifully. And you rest. No more measuring rooms twice. No more counting doors. No more being the woman who is almost present. Just quiet. Just the warm dark behind the glass, where nothing is ever asked of you again."

The temptation arrived the way floodwater arrives—not dramatically, but everywhere at once, rising around her ankles before she'd noticed the river had left its banks.

Because God help her, it sounded like peace.

* * *

She made herself walk. Downstairs, through the kitchen, out onto the side porch where the night air smelled of cut fields and cold stone, where there were no mirrors, where the only reflection available was her own dim ghost doubled in the black window glass—and even that, she realized, might be listening. She sat on the porch steps and hugged her knees like a child and let the argument happen inside her, because it was going to happen whether she permitted it or not.

*He can't force you,* she told herself. *Mirabel said it keeps the ones who half-choose. So don't half-choose.*

*But what if I full-choose the wrong thing?*

The other woman's voice hadn't lied. That was the terrible part. Rosalind went back through everything she'd said, hunting for the hook in the bait, the distortion, the tell—and found none. Only precision. The dressing-room tag. The mother's wards. The way Rosalind entered rooms. Whoever stood in the glass knew her with an intimacy no one alive had ever bothered to attempt, and that intimacy was itself the seduction. Thirty years of marriage wouldn't teach a man what one week in this house had taught her own reflection.

And underneath the fear, quieter, shamefuller: *she's right about the practice.* Rosalind had been rehearsing disappearance her whole adult life. The forgettable haircut. The gray coats. The job measuring other people's property because appraisers were supposed to be transparent, invisible, a neutral instrument—she had chosen a profession of not existing and called it a career. If some part of her had been preparing for the glass all along, then the house hadn't corrupted her. It had merely recognized her.

*That's what it wants you to think,* said a smaller voice, Mirabel's voice, scratchy and stubborn. *It keeps the ones who half-choose. And a woman who believes she was always meant to vanish? That's the most half-chosen creature there is.*

"Then what's the play?" Rosalind asked the dark. "Refuse to sign, and he starves the town until they carry me to the table themselves. Sign myself, and I'm the one who stays. Let her sign, and I get the warm dark forever." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Every door leads somewhere bad. This whole house is a hallway of bad doors."

Behind her, through the window, the kitchen light flickered once.

She turned. In the black glass of the porch window, faint as breath on cold, the other Rosalind stood in the yard behind her—out of the mirrors now, or maybe the window counted, maybe everything reflective counted, maybe the whole world was becoming glass. The figure raised one hand. Not threatening. Waving. The way you wave to yourself in a store window, checking.

"One more thing," the reflection said, and though the window muffled her, Rosalind heard it perfectly, heard it *inside*, the way you hear your own thoughts. "You should know what happens if you try the circle. The counter-ritual. Abigail's little unfinished project—you think I don't know you've been piecing it together? To finish it, you have to stand in that ring and be *seen*. Completely. Every name, every failure, every cowardice, spoken aloud, witnessed, written into the record of the world. No armor. No gray coat. No forgetting." The figure leaned closer to the glass, and her eyes were kind, and that kindness was the cruelest weapon in the house. "You'd rather die than do that, Roz. We both know it. I'm not even tempting you, really. I'm just the first person to offer you the exit you've been looking for since childhood. Everyone else called it cowardice. I'm calling it mercy."

The figure faded. The yard was just the yard.

Rosalind sat on the cold steps a long time, shaking, and understood at last that the offer had done its work regardless of her answer. Because now the question was in her, planted, watering itself. *Would you rather be seen, or be gone?* And every hour she spent not deciding, the house grew one degree warmer around her, patient as soil.

She went back inside. She checked the doors—five, correct. She left the hall light burning, out of habit that was maybe a ward, maybe just fear with better posture.

On the kitchen table, Mirabel's note had been moved.

Still under the water glass. Still legible. But beneath it, in handwriting identical to her own—which was somehow worse than any stranger's script—someone had added a line:

*Six days. Then I sign, with or without your permission. Sleep well, Roz. One of us should.*

Rosalind picked up the note with a steady hand, carried it to the sink, and burned it over the burner flame, watching the words curl and blacken.

Then she stood in the dark kitchen, smoke stinging her eyes, and admitted the truth she'd been circling for hours: she didn't feel horrified.

She felt tempted. And somewhere behind the walls, something felt her feeling it, and began, very softly, to count down.
