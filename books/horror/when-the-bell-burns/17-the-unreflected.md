## 17. The Unreflected

Mirabel's house sat at the end of Corliss Lane like a held breath.

Rosalind had walked past it a dozen times since arriving in the valley—she'd catalogued it without thinking, the way she catalogued everything: clapboard gone silver, garden gone to seed, porch light burning at noon because some habits outlive their reasons. It was the kind of house she would have appraised and forgotten in the same hour. She had assumed its owner was the same.

Now she stood on the sagging porch with the old woman's note crushed in her fist—*It keeps the ones who half-choose*—and understood that she had never once actually seen this house. She had looked through it, the way people looked through her.

The door opened before she knocked.

"You took long enough." Mirabel was small and dry as a winter apple, her cardigan buttoned wrong, her eyes the only living thing about her—dark, quick, unblinking. "Come in before the street sees you. The street tells."

The interior smelled of cedar and burnt coffee. Every surface that could hold a mirror did not: no glass over the mantel, no framed photographs, the bathroom door shut with a towel jammed beneath it. Where mirrors should have been there were squares of painted-over wall, dozens of them, layered like scars.

"You've been covering them," Rosalind said.

"For fifty-one years." Mirabel poured coffee without asking, into two mismatched cups, and pushed one across the table. Her hand shook until it touched the cup's handle, then went still, as if the tremor were something she could set down when she needed to. "Paint works for a while. Then the paint starts lagging too."

Rosalind sat. The chair creaked under both of them, though only one of them had weight she could account for. "You were at the dinner."

"I'm always at the dinner. They like having me there. Proof of hospitality." Mirabel smiled without any warmth reaching it. "An old woman with no reflection, toasting the new girl's fresh start. They think I don't notice what I am. Fifty years in this valley, dear, you learn what everyone else is careful not to look at."

"Your note said—" Rosalind unfolded it, smoothed it on the table between them. "It keeps the ones who half-choose. What does that mean? What are you?"

Mirabel looked at her for a long moment, and Rosalind felt the look the way she felt the house measuring her—a slow inventory, item by item, deciding what she was worth.

"I'm the fifth owner," Mirabel said. "Nineteen seventy-three. Before you, there were four. After you, God willing, there will be no more, but I stopped believing in God willing around 1974." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "You found the records office. Four sales, four signatures, four women and one man who walked into Hollowmere and walked out of existence. The county has nothing on them because the county has nothing *to* have. But the records office keeps deeds going back further than deaths, further than disappearances. There's a fifth deed they filed wrong. Wrong name. Mine."

Rosalind's throat tightened. "You signed."

"I signed everything. That's what you do at closings—you sign what they put in front of you, and you're grateful someone wants your name at all." The bitterness in her voice was old enough to have fossilized. "I was thirty-one. I'd come from nowhere, married a man from nowhere, and Hollowmere was our chance to be somewhere. I remember standing in the parlor with the pen in my hand and feeling—for the first time in my life—like the world had made room for me." She laughed, a dry sound with no floor beneath it. "That's how it gets you. Not with fear. With *welcome*."

"What happened?"

"The ritual started the way it starts for all of them. The mirror-self. The lag widening. My husband thought I was losing my mind, which was convenient for the house, because a woman losing her mind signs whatever her husband hands her." Mirabel's eyes fixed on the painted-over square above the sink. "I got as far as you've gotten. I found the second room. I read Abigail Voss's letters. I stood in that circle with the chisel in my hand, and I learned what finishing it costs."

Rosalind leaned forward. "Being seen. Fully. Name, history, every shame spoken aloud."

"So you've read the last letter." Something flickered across Mirabel's face—not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "Then you know why I stopped. You know what I couldn't do."

"No," Rosalind said quietly. "I know what *Abigail* couldn't do. I don't know what you chose instead."

Mirabel set down the cup. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reciting damage she'd memorized so she wouldn't have to feel it fresh.

"I refused to choose. That's the thing nobody understands about the ritual—it doesn't need your yes. It needs your *decision*. Either decision. Give yourself to the circle and be seen, or give yourself to the glass and be kept. What it cannot abide—the one thing it cannot digest—is a woman standing in the middle saying *neither*. So that's what I said. Neither. I put down the chisel and I walked out of the room and I told the house no in every way a person can say no, and I kept saying it for nine days, until closing came and went with my signature still mine."

"And the house let you?"

"The house *kept* me." Mirabel rose, crossed to the window, and pulled back the curtain an inch. Late afternoon light fell across her face—and passed through it, or seemed to; there was something unfinished about her outline, like a sketch the artist abandoned. "There's always a price. The exchange demands collateral even from the ones who escape. It took the only part of me the valley ever valued." She turned. "It took my reflection."

Rosalind had guessed it, had half-guessed it since the dinner, since she'd watched Mirabel raise a toast and seen the candlelight do strange things behind her—but hearing it said plainly landed differently. Plainly was worse. Plainly was true.

"Fifty-one years," Mirabel said, "I have not seen my own face except in spoons and dark windows and other people's descriptions. Do you know what that does to a person? You begin to suspect you aren't there at all. You begin to do things just to confirm you cast a shadow. I stood in front of the covered mirrors for the first decade and talked to them, hoping. Hoping." She straightened her crooked cardigan with sharp little tugs. "The house holds it. Somewhere in that glass, there's a woman who looks like me, frozen at thirty-one, and every year the house owns more of her and I own less. One day—I can feel it—one day she'll stop being my reflection and start being theirs entirely. And then I'll finish fading. That's what 'half-choose' means, dear. The house doesn't lose. It only negotiates the terms."

The coffee had gone cold in Rosalind's hands. She drank it anyway.

"Why tell me this?" she asked. "Why now? You've watched four owners vanish since you. Why break fifty years of silence for me?"

Mirabel came back to the table but didn't sit. She stood over Rosalind, and up close her eyes were wet and furious and terribly tired.

"Because the others *wanted* something. The man in '81 wanted money. The sisters in '94 wanted each other forgiven. The last one, in 2011—" Mirabel's jaw worked. "She wanted to be loved by this town so badly she'd have signed herself away for a dinner invitation. They all wanted something the house could pretend to give. But you—" she pointed one thin finger at Rosalind, almost accusingly, "—you don't want anything. I've watched you for weeks. You walk through this town like a woman doing an inventory of exits. You want *out*, and you want to remain *yourself* on the way out, and you haven't figured out yet that those might be the same wish. That's new. That's the first new thing in fifty years."

"That's not an alliance," Rosalind said. "That's a diagnosis."

"It's both." Mirabel finally sat, and the chair received her with relief. "Look—I'm not offering friendship. Friendship died in this valley before you were born. I'm offering information, and information here is worth more than blood. You'll need it, because the buyer will come for you soon—he always comes early now, he's getting bolder, the house is hungry and the valley's luck is running thin and they all feel it, even the ones who won't say it. And when he comes, he'll offer you the trade. He'll dress it up as mercy. And you'll be so tired, by then, that mercy will sound like sense."

"How do you know what he'll offer?"

"Because he offered it to me." Mirabel's mouth twisted. "In 1973, standing in my own kitchen, wearing my husband's face for the last three minutes of my marriage. *Let her take the signature,* he said. *Let her take the life. You can go back to being no one.*" She repeated it in a whisper, the words worn smooth from fifty years of handling. "And God help me, it sounded like heaven. To disappear without dying. To be released from the burden of being perceived. I said neither to him too. It's the only reason I'm sitting here with a face nobody can see and a story nobody believes."

Rosalind set down her cup. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, something was moving like water finding cracks.

"The counter-ritual," she said. "Abigail's circle. If I finish it—if I stand in that room and speak myself whole—does it free yours?"

Mirabel went very still.

"I didn't come here to recruit you," Rosalind said. "But I've been doing the math since the dinner. The house feeds on concealment. The lag widens when I lie and narrows when I tell the truth. Your reflection isn't destroyed—it's *held*. Collateral. And collateral gets returned when the debt is settled." She met the old woman's eyes. "If the exchange reverses—if the house is denied—if everything it borrowed goes back where it came from—"

"Then Mirabel Okafor, née Voss—yes, *that* Voss, Abigail's great-granddaughter, another thing this town forgot on purpose—gets her face back." Mirabel's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, Rosalind could see her hating it, could see fifty years of practiced indifference failing at the seams. "Yes. That's the arithmetic. That's why I'm telling you. Not because I like you. Because you're the first owner in a century who might actually finish it, and if you finish it, I get to see whether I'm still in there."

* * *

They talked until the light failed.

Mirabel talked the way a drowning woman throws rope—fast, tangled, desperate to land any length of it on solid ground. She drew the second room from memory on the back of a grocery receipt: the circle's dimensions, the order of the carved lines, the place where Abigail's chisel had stopped mid-stroke. She described the buyer—*he wears different faces but the pause is always the same, that beat before he arrives, like the world holding its breath*—and warned her about the townspeople, naming which ones knew and which ones only suspected and which ones had stopped asking questions so long ago they'd forgotten questions existed.

And she asked, once, quietly, near the end: "What will you speak? In the circle. Do you know yet?"

Rosalind stared at the cold coffee, at the painted-over mirrors, at the small fierce ruin of a woman across the table.

"My mother taught me wards," she said. "Lights on. Doors counted. Never be the loudest thing in a room. I thought they were habits. I thought they were *mine*." She laughed, short and hollow. "I've spent forty years making sure no one could describe me accurately. Even my sister. Especially my sister. There's a version of me that stayed loud, that stayed *seen*, and the house grew it in a mirror like a pearl around my cowardice. If I speak myself whole, I have to say all of it. Out loud. To a room full of carved stone and listening walls."

"Yes," Mirabel said simply. "That's the price."

"It's not a price. It's a demolition."

"It's both," Mirabel said again, and this time it sounded almost gentle. "Everything worth surviving is."

At the door, in the blue dark, Mirabel caught Rosalind's wrist. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested—grip of a woman who had spent five decades holding on to things no one could see.

"One more thing, and then we're done trusting each other for tonight. The house knows you came here. It knew before you left your driveway. From tonight on, it will stop courting you and start *herding* you—walls, doors, weather, the girl in the glass walking where you walk. Whatever it shows you, whatever it offers, whatever shape your own face takes in the dark—" her grip tightened, "—remember that it has never once told the truth about what you want. Only about what you fear. Those are different rooms in the same house, and you, dear, are the surveyor. Measure twice."

Rosalind walked home through streets that had gone unnaturally quiet, past windows glowing with the warm amber of borrowed fortune, and every lit window held a family that would sleep soundly tonight because someone else had agreed, or almost agreed, or half-chosen.

Hollowmere rose against the last of the light, and even from the lane she could see it: the upstairs hallway lamp burning in the window, though she had left it off that morning.

She had counted.

She always counted.

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke she hadn't built. On the hall table, neatly squared, lay a document she hadn't brought home—an amended closing statement, dated tomorrow, her name already printed in the seller's line in type that looked, up close, like handwriting.

And in the hallway mirror, hanging perfectly still, her reflection waited—smiling, patient, dressed in clothes Rosalind would never dare—

—except the reflection wasn't waiting anymore.

It raised one hand. A greeting. Or a summons.

Behind Rosalind, at the far end of the hall, a door she had counted shut swung slowly open onto darkness, and from the darkness, in a voice like her own worn smooth, something began to hum the song her mother used to sing while counting doors.
