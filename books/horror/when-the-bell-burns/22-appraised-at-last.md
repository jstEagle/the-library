## 22. Appraised at Last

The county clerk's office smelled the same as it always had—toner and old carpet and someone's reheated coffee—and Rosalind Eriksen walked in carrying a folder she had rewritten eleven times.

She knew the number because she'd counted. Counting was what she did. But the counting had changed since Hollowmere. It used to be armor. Now it was just a habit, the way some people drum their fingers or hum in elevators. She counted because the world was worth counting, not because she was afraid it would rearrange itself if she looked away.

"Appraisal report for the Hollowmere property," she said, and set the folder on the counter.

The clerk—Dale, fifty-something, a man who had signed off on four Hollowmere transactions in his career and had never once thought about what happened to the buyers afterward—took it without ceremony. He flipped to the summary page. His eyebrows did a small, involuntary dance.

"Unsellable?"

"Structurally compromised," Rosalind said. "Foundation settlement beyond economic repair. The house has a room that doesn't exist on any blueprint and a foundation that doesn't exist under any of it. I'd have to certify the square footage, and I can't, because the square footage changes depending on what the house wants." She paused. "You can quote the first part in your file. The second part is between us."

Dale looked at her over his glasses for a long moment. He was a man who had worked in a county office for thirty years, and men like that develop a very specific survival instinct: the ability to hear something true and file it under *not my problem*. She watched him do it now, watched his face perform the little shrug of a man choosing the shallow end.

"Compromised foundation," he repeated, stamping it. "That'll kill the sale."

"That's the idea."

He slid her a receipt copy. "Buyer's going to be disappointed."

"The buyer," Rosalind said, "was never going to be anything but disappointed."

She signed the receipt—*Rosalind Anne Eriksen*, full name, no hesitation, the letters even and hers—and walked out into a morning so ordinary it felt like a gift she hadn't earned yet and intended to keep earning anyway.

* * *

The town of Hollowmere had been beautiful the way a stage set is beautiful: everything in the right place, everything slightly too vivid, everything lit from an angle no sun would choose. The orchards had hung heavy. The diner had never had an empty table. The hardware store owner had bought a boat he had no lake for, and everyone had smiled about it in that soft, glazed way of people who are being paid and know it and have decided not to know it.

Weeks later, Rosalind drove back through on her way to nothing in particular and saw the town exhaling.

The orchards were still there. The apples were still growing. But the heavy, unnatural abundance was gone—the branches held a normal crop, an honest crop, the kind that required weather and labor and luck of the ordinary kind. The diner had two tables occupied at lunch. The hardware store owner's boat sat in his yard with a tarp over it, and when she passed him at the gas station he looked tired in a way she recognized: the tiredness of a man whose life had stopped being given to him.

He nodded at her. He didn't smile the glazed smile. He just nodded, the way one person nods at another, and something in his face said he knew—or was beginning to know—and that the knowing was going to be hard and that he was going to survive it.

Rosalind had expected to feel something about this. Guilt, maybe. Or vindication. The town had eaten four people that she could document and possibly more that she couldn't, and every prosperous dinner table in the valley had been set with what they'd left behind.

What she felt instead was something quieter and harder to name. Not rancor. Not forgiveness, either—she wasn't in the forgiveness business, and she doubted the vanished were taking applications. It was more like watching a fever break. The town had been sick with a luck it hadn't earned, and the sickness was passing, and now the long, ordinary work of being a town would begin. Some of them would resent her forever. Some of them would never understand what had changed. A few—Mirabel said the old ones, the ones whose grandparents had signed the founding papers—would wake up some night with the whole bargain suddenly legible in their memory, the way a word you've misread your whole life suddenly resolves into its true spelling.

They would have to earn it now. The valley, the harvest, the small good fortunes. They would have to earn it the way everyone else did, which was to say imperfectly, and together, and without a house doing the arithmetic for them.

Rosalind pulled over at the overlook above the valley and looked down at Hollowmere House.

From here it looked like what it was: an old colonial with good bones and a bad history, its windows dark, its grounds gone slightly wild in the way of a place no longer tended by anything. The house had gone dark the moment she signed—she had felt it go, a great held breath released, a century of borrowed luck exhaling out of the walls and up through the valley and gone. She'd stood in the foyer with the pen still warm in her hand and listened to the house become, simply, a house.

The mirrors were the strangest part. She'd checked every one of them, all eleven, because of course she'd counted. They showed her a woman standing in a dim foyer holding a pen, and the woman arrived when she arrived, moved when she moved, left when she left. No lag. No second self. Just glass doing what glass does, which is nothing, which is everything it was ever supposed to do.

She'd left the front door unlocked on her way out. It seemed only fair. A house that had spent two hundred years as a vessel deserved to spend whatever it had left as a house—cold in winter, dusty in summer, full of the small honest sounds of an empty building settling. Old houses settle. She'd told herself that once, in denial, and now it was just true.

She got back in the car and drove down into the town she had, in a way, unmade.

* * *

Mirabel had stayed.

Not in Hollowmere House—she'd taken one look at Rosalind's face on closing day and said, "I'm not setting foot in that place while there's still a signature drying," and had driven them both to her cottage on the far ridge, where she made tea that tasted like bark and refused to discuss anything important until the second cup.

But she'd stayed in the valley. Rosalind had asked her why, once, expecting an answer about roots or history or unfinished business, and Mirabel had shrugged with the economy of a woman who had spent forty years with no reflection and had stopped performing for anyone.

"Because I left once," she said. "Mid-ritual, no choice made, and it cost me my face in every mirror for forty years. You finished it, and it cost you—" she'd waved a hand at Rosalind, at all of her, "—whatever it cost you. We're the only two people alive who know what that house was. If both of us leave, it's just a story again. Stories get retold wrong." She'd sipped her tea. "Somebody has to stay and remember it right."

So Rosalind stayed too. She rented a small house in town—two bedrooms, one bath, a kitchen with a window over the sink—and told her firm she was relocating for "regional coverage," which was a lie so boring no one questioned it. She kept taking appraisal jobs. Ordinary houses, ordinary numbers, foundations that stayed where foundations were put. She was good at her work. She had always been good at her work. The difference was that now, when she measured a room, she believed the tape measure, and the tape measure, as far as she could tell, believed her back.

Her new house had four mirrors. She'd counted on the first day—of course she had—and then she'd done something that would have been unthinkable a year ago. She'd hung a fifth. A full-length mirror in the hallway, bought at the hardware store from the man with the tarp-covered boat, who had asked no questions and given her a small discount, which she had chosen to read as the town's first installment of a very long repayment.

She passed that mirror every morning on her way to the kitchen, and every morning she arrived exactly on time.

It never stopped being a small miracle. She didn't expect it to. Some things you don't get used to; you just get grateful.

* * *

The lights were the last thing.

She kept them on. All of them—the porch light, the kitchen, the hallway, the lamp in the spare room that she never otherwise used. Her mother's wards, rebuilt in a new house: doors tallied, lights burning, thresholds respected. For the first few weeks she'd done it at night with her heart going hard, checking each bulb the way you check a lock in a neighborhood you don't trust yet.

Then one evening in October she was standing in her kitchen making tea—Mirabel's bark tea, which she'd developed a genuine taste for, or at least a genuine loyalty to—and she caught herself reaching for the hallway light switch and realized she had no idea why.

Not fear. She ran the inventory, the way she ran inventories of everything now, honestly, out loud if she had to. The house was a house. The doors were the doors she'd counted. The mirror in the hallway showed a woman making tea, on time, every time. There was nothing in the dark. There had never been anything in the dark; the dark had only ever been where the house kept its patience, and the house was done.

But she turned the light on anyway.

Because the light made the kitchen warmer. Because the hallway looked friendly lit, the way hallways are supposed to look. Because her mother had taught her those wards in a voice tight with fear, and Rosalind had carried them for thirty years like a stone in her chest, and she was allowed, now, to set the stone down and keep the habit the way you keep a grandmother's recipe—changed, lightened, made yours.

She kept the lights on out of habit now. Not fear. Habit. It was, she thought, the whole difference between a ward and a ritual: a ward keeps something out. A ritual reminds you who's in.

* * *

The call took her three weeks to make and eleven seconds to dial once she finally picked up the phone.

Her sister lived in Ohio. Had lived in Ohio for nineteen years, ever since the fight Rosalind had never fully spoken aloud—the fight that was really about Rosalind's talent for vanishing inside her own life, for being so unremarkable, so un-seeable, that the people who loved her eventually gave up looking. Claire had said, at the end, *You'd rather be nobody than be my sister*, and Rosalind had said nothing, which was the same as yes, and that had been that.

Nineteen years. In the circle, in the second room, with the walls warm around her and the mirror-self waiting to take her place, Rosalind had spoken that truth along with all the others—her mother's wards, her cowardices, the sister she let vanish from her life rather than be seen failing—and she had felt the lag in the mirrors shrink with every word, and she had understood, finally, that concealment was the house's food and that she had been seasoning herself her whole life.

She'd survived by being seen. It followed, she'd reasoned in the weeks after, that she ought to keep doing it.

Claire answered on the second ring. "Hello?"

"Hi," Rosalind said. "It's Roz."

The silence on the other end lasted three seconds. She counted. She counted everything; it was what she did; it was, now, a form of attention rather than a form of armor. Three seconds of a sister deciding whether this was real.

"Roz," Claire said, and her voice did something complicated—cracked and warmed and steadied itself all at once. "Is everything okay? Is Mom—"

"Mom's fine. I'm fine. Everything's—" Rosalind looked out her kitchen window at the valley, at the town with its honest dim orchards, at the ridge where an old woman with a returned reflection was probably watching the sunset and remembering it right. "Everything's actually fine. Which I know is not how these calls usually go."

"You haven't called in nineteen years. Fine is not a word I associate with these calls."

"I know." Rosalind sat down at the kitchen table, in the light she'd turned on out of habit, and told the truth, because she was out of practice at nothing else. "I did something cowardly a long time ago and I let it become a personality. And then I spent the last two months in a house that eats people like me, and I got out, and getting out required me to say a lot of things I'd spent my whole life not saying, and one of them was your name." She breathed. "I'm not calling to fix nineteen years in one phone call. I'm calling because I'd like to try. If you'll let me."

Another silence, but a different one—the silence of someone putting down something heavy.

"You sound different," Claire said finally.

"I am different. I'm appraised." She smiled, and there was a mirror over the kitchen table, a small one, and the woman in it smiled when she smiled, at the same time, the same smile, and it was hers. "It's a long story. Do you have a long story's worth of time?"

"For you?" Her sister's voice, and it was warm, and it arrived exactly when it arrived, and there was nothing behind it. "Roz. I have the rest of my life."

* * *

That night, Rosalind walked the house before bed. It was not a ward; it was a pleasure. She checked the doors because she liked knowing they were hers. She left the lights on because the house looked kind lit. She passed the hallway mirror and the woman in it passed with her, punctual as a heartbeat, and Rosalind paused—just for a moment—and looked at herself.

A woman of no particular fame, in a small rented house, in a valley learning to earn its luck. Tired around the eyes. Steadier around the mouth. Someone who had gone into a house unremarkable and come out signed, named, seen—someone the dark would have to work very hard to swallow ever again.

"Goodnight," she told her reflection, and meant it, and the reflection said nothing, because reflections don't, and that was exactly right.

She turned off the hallway light last—habit, not fear, the small nightly liturgy of a woman who kept her own counsel and finally had counsel worth keeping—and in the dark she heard it: faint, from the ridge road, a car climbing toward Mirabel's cottage with its headlights sweeping the trees. Two headlights. Mirabel didn't drive at night.

Rosalind stood in her dark kitchen, listening, and felt the old counting rise in her out of pure instinct—one car, two people, an hour past the time Mirabel answered her door—and she reached for the phone, and outside, far up the ridge, the headlights stopped moving.
