## 10. What Loyalty Costs

The archive closed at five. I stayed until the archivist's keys started jangling at a quarter to, and then I did what I had learned to do in twenty years of exploring places that didn't want me found: I waited for the building to forget about me.

The reading room after hours was a different country. The overhead fluorescents clicked off in sequence, aisle by aisle, like a tide going out, and the dust in the slanted light from the high windows turned slow and golden and indifferent. Somewhere below us — below all of us — the cannery district sat on its sealed secret, and somewhere above my grandmother's stove, the cellar door held its warmth like a hand pressed flat against wood.

I had the founders' ledger open in front of me under a lamp I'd brought myself, because the reading room lamps were on motion sensors now and I intended to be very still.

I had read it three times already. Front to back, back to front, the way you read anything when you're hoping the words will rearrange themselves into something kinder. They hadn't. Words never do. That's the first thing documentation teaches you: the record doesn't care what you want it to say. It just says.

*Completed this 14th day of October, 1911,* the final entry began, in a hand that started confident and deteriorated by degrees, as though the writer were being interrupted, or as though his arm were remembering something older than writing.

I read it a fourth time anyway. Because reading it was still better than knowing it.

*The binding requires a keeper. Not a sacrifice — the word is wrong and has always been wrong, and we ask forgiveness of whoever finds this for using it even once. A keeper. One who remains below, who takes the shape of the thing we have bound, who wears its hunger and its patience and its long attention as a garment wears a body. It will not die. This is the mercy and it is also the horror and we no longer have the language to separate them. It will be aware. Every hour of every year, aware, serving, holding shut the door we opened because we were proud and impatient and thought a city could be founded on a foundation it never paid for.*

*It must be one who loves what stands above. That is the whole of the requirement and the whole of the cruelty. A stranger would let go. Only love holds forever.*

My lamp buzzed. In the dark beyond its circle, the shelves stood in their rows like headstones for books nobody mourned.

*We four have drawn lots. Elias drew the short straw and has gone down smiling, which is more than any of us deserved from him. We seal the ledger with his name so that someone, someday, knows there was a man under the cannery district before there was a cannery district, and that he went willingly, and that we who remained topside lived our whole lives in the shadow of what we asked him to do, and that we never once stopped hearing him.*

*If you are reading this, the ritual is unfinished. We could not finish it. Finish requires descent — the witness must go down accompanied by those who love them, and must complete the last line in their own voice, and one of those who love them must remain. We tell ourselves we will come back and do it properly. We tell ourselves many things.*

*Do not finish it. Or finish it knowing. Those are the only honest instructions left.*

Below the signature block, four names. Below the names, a fifth, written smaller, in a different ink that had gone brown with age: *Elias Okonkwo.*

I sat with that for a long time.

Okonkwo.

Not a coincidence. Of course not a coincidence. There had been no coincidence since the day my camera refused to focus past ten feet of storm drain, and probably long before that — since 1974, since the chair first appeared in the corner of a photograph of my mother's christening, patient and wooden and empty. Since my grandmother's mother stood at the top of cellar stairs she refused to descend, and chose her own survival over the completion of a debt, and thereby converted a single terrible payment into an installment plan stretched across fifty years and three generations.

We hadn't been harvested slowly because the thing below was greedy. We'd been harvested slowly because the ritual was *waiting*. Waiting for a witness who loved what stood above. And every Okonkwo born since 1911 had been another candidate submitted for consideration, another name added to a list nobody knew they were on.

And now here I was. Witness. Documentarian. The one who prayed in photographs. The one who loved what stood above so much it ached in my teeth — Priya with her spreadsheets and her armor of skepticism, Dez with his laugh that filled stairwells, Marisol with her quiet ferocity, Grandma Adaeze with her Sunday rice and jollof and her hands that smelled like nutmeg. My whole architecture of a life. Everything the ledger meant by *what stands above.*

One of us stays below.

Forever aware. Forever serving. Wearing the hunger like a garment.

I closed the ledger and put my palms flat on its cover, as if I could hold the knowledge down, keep it from getting up and walking into the world where the others lived.

* * *

They were waiting at grandma's house when I got there, which wasn't unusual anymore. Since the pull — since the night gravity changed its mind and delivered all four of us to the cellar without our consent — we had stopped pretending we could be apart. We gathered the way people gather around a fire they're afraid will spread but can't bring themselves to leave.

Priya had colonized the kitchen table with printouts. Dez was peeling plantains with a competence none of us questioned anymore; he'd absorbed himself into Adaeze's kitchen months ago and Adaeze had simply accepted the annexation. Marisol sat on the floor with her back against the radiator, sketching tunnel cross-sections from memory, which was either preparation or exorcism, possibly both.

"Where've you been?" Dez asked, not looking up. "Archive closes at five."

"Had some things to copy."

"You've been weird since the ledger," Priya said. She said it lightly, but Priya's lightness was load-bearing; I'd learned to hear the rebar in it. "You get this look. Like you're buffering."

"I always look like I'm buffering."

"Yeah, but usually it's charming."

Adaeze came in from the garden with rosemary and that particular evening serenity she wore like the ledger's writers must have worn their guilt — smoothly, practiced, over something enormous. She kissed the top of my head on her way to the sink. Her lips lingered half a second longer than usual. Grandmothers know. I don't know how, but grandmothers know, and hers had known since 1974.

I helped with dinner. I chopped onions until my eyes ran for reasons I could attribute to chemistry. I listened to Dez argue with Priya about whether the chalk symbols constituted a language or a notation, and I laughed in the right places, and the whole time the ledger's last page sat folded against my sternum like a coal.

*One of those who love them must remain.*

Here is what I decided, standing at my grandmother's counter with onion burn in my eyes: I decided it would be me.

It was the easiest decision of my life and I want to be honest about that, because everything afterward was hard, and the hardness came entirely from the hiding, not the choosing. The choosing took four seconds. Four seconds of arithmetic: four people I loved, one seat below, and I was the witness — the ritual needed me regardless, so the extra weight should land on the person already carrying weight. Besides, I had spent my whole adult life converting experience into record. I was the one who believed a thing documented couldn't consume you. Let me test that belief at the only depth that mattered. Let me find out if my prayer worked both directions.

Four seconds. Then I dried my hands and carried the rice to the table and lied to everyone I loved for the rest of the night.

* * *

Lying, it turns out, is a skill, and I was bad at it, and the only reason I survived the evening was that my family's love made them credulous. Love wants to believe you. That's its beauty and its vulnerability and, tonight, its usefulness to me, and I hated myself a little more with every successful deception.

Priya laid out the rules she'd reconstructed, tapping her printouts: willing descent, accompanied witnesses, completion before the new moon. Nine days. "Which means we prep," she said. "Salt lines. Recordings. Backup documentation of everybody — faces, voices, the works. If someone doesn't come back, there'll be a record that they existed."

"That's grim," said Dez.

"That's *archival*," said Priya. "Grim is a side effect."

Marisol looked at me across the table, and I looked at the candle flame between us, and I felt the lie settle into place like a bone setting wrong — functional, but you'd feel the weather in it forever.

"What does the ledger actually say happens?" Marisol asked. "At completion. Nobody's actually read me the ending."

"The ending's damaged," I said. "Water damage. Half the page's illegible." I heard myself say it and marveled at the smoothness. Water damage. The archive kept that ledger in a climate-controlled case. "Something about sealing. Binding. The usual."

"And the price?" Priya again, sharp as ever, because Priya was always sharp as ever, and I understood in that moment why the founders' generation had burned so much and hidden so much — not because truth is dangerous to enemies, but because it is nearly impossible to conceal from people who love you and pay attention.

"There isn't one listed," I said. "That's the creepy part. It just ends."

It didn't just end. It ended with a man's name in brown ink and a description of eternity that I could recite now with my eyes closed: *aware, serving, holding shut the door.* But I said *there isn't one listed*, and Priya nodded, and Dez went back to his plantains, and Marisol watched me for two seconds too long before looking away, and I filed that look away where I file things I can't afford to examine yet.

After dinner we pushed the furniture back and rehearsed. That was Priya's idea — walk the ritual steps in the living room, assign positions, drill the responses until they lived in our mouths instead of our notes. Dez stood where the altar would be. Marisol marked the floor with painter's tape. I read the liturgy fragments aloud from my copies, and my voice did something strange on the final line — the unfinished line, the line the ritual had been waiting a hundred years for someone to speak — it thinned, went almost transparent, like the words recognized that I already knew how I meant to rewrite them.

"Bram." Adaeze stood in the doorway with a dish towel over her shoulder. "Walk with me."

* * *

We walked to the end of the garden, past the rosemary, to the fence where the neighbor's security light didn't reach. Moths ticked against the bulb somewhere behind us. The city hummed its big low hum, and beneath the hum, if you'd learned to listen the way I'd learned, the fainter hum of the deep places, patient as digestion.

"You read the whole ledger," she said. Not a question.

"Grandma—"

"I'm not asking what it says." She turned to face me. In the dark her eyes were mostly absence, but I could feel them, the way you feel the cellar door's warmth through your palm. "I'm asking if you're going to carry it alone. Because I know that face. Your mother wore it. Her mother wore it. It's the face of someone doing math with other people's lives and deciding the answer is themselves."

My throat closed. I managed: "The ledger's incomplete. That's all."

"Mm." She reached up and touched my cheek, and her hand smelled of rosemary and dish soap and fifty years of feeding people, and I nearly broke right there, nearly gave her the whole of it, the brown ink and the name Okonkwo and the garment of hunger. Nearly.

Instead I said, "Go inside, Grandma. It's cold."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, in Igbo, a sentence I didn't fully understand but whose tone I did — the tone of a woman laying something down at a grave — and she kissed my forehead and went inside.

I stood alone in the garden with the moths and the hum, and I told myself the lie one more time, practicing it, making it smooth: *Nobody else carries this. That's the whole point. That's what love is for.*

But the ledger's words had gotten into me the way tunnel water gets into stone, widening everything they touch, and in the widened spaces a question had taken root that I could not stop watering:

What would they do when they found out? Not that I'd hidden the price — that I'd *decided*. That while they were printing backup photographs of each other's faces, I had already spent mine. That I had looked at the people who became my home and answered the ritual's question — *who loves what stands above?* — by volunteering to become the thing beneath it, without asking them, without letting them vote, without granting them the same terrible dignity of choice I was about to grant the dark.

Love expressed as a lie of omission. The founders would have recognized it instantly. It was, after all, exactly what they'd done to Elias.

* * *

I got home past midnight. My apartment smelled of dust and developing fluid and the ghost of every basement I'd ever photographed. I hung my jacket, booted my laptop, and began the work I'd been circling for days: drafting the rewritten final line.

Because that was the other thing I hadn't told them. Not just the price — the plan. The ritual required a keeper, yes. Required something to remain below, aware, serving, wearing the hunger. But the ritual was a text, and texts could be edited, and I was the one member of this family who had built a life on the conviction that the record could hold what flesh could not.

Every photograph. Every map. Every hour of audio, every scan, every annotated survey, twenty years of documentation — the whole archive of my attention, poured into that chamber as the offering. Let the thing below wear my record instead of my body. Let it serve bound in paper and prayer. It was either blasphemy or inspiration and I wouldn't know which until nine days from now, at the bottom of a staircase that had been growing toward me since 1911.

I typed until three. The cursor blinked its small patient blink, like the chair in the photographs.

Then my phone lit up on the desk. A message from Marisol, sent forty seconds ago:

*Saw you typing. Couldn't sleep either. Whatever you're writing — whatever you copied out of that archive today that you think we didn't notice — you don't have to do it alone. That's the whole point of us.*

I stared at it for a long time.

Then I deleted the draft, closed the laptop, and sat in the dark listening to the hum beneath the city, counting the days — eight now — and wondering which would break first: my resolve, or my family's faith in me.
