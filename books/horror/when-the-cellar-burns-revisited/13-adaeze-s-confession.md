## 13. Adaeze's Confession

Grandma Adaeze's kitchen smelled like it always did on a Tuesday: ginger, scallion oil, the ghost of yesterday's rice. But the light was wrong. It came through the window over the sink at an angle that made everything look like evidence — the chipped blue teapot, the dish towel folded into a precise rectangle, my grandmother's hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had not yet sipped.

She'd called me alone. Not Priya, not Dez, not Marisol. *Come by yourself, nne m. Bring nothing. Especially bring nothing that records.*

So I sat there with empty pockets and empty hands for the first time in years, feeling like I'd left skin behind.

"You found the ledger," she said. It wasn't a question. Word travels in this city through channels older than the internet, and grandmothers are some of those channels.

I nodded. "1911. The cannery founders. A witness who loves what stands above."

"Mm." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a small ritual motion, like she was aligning something invisible. "And you came here to ask me why."

"I came here because—" My voice caught on something. "Because every answer I've dug up ends at your door, Grandma. The chalk marks match the symbols in Auntie Ngozi's wedding photos. The chair is in our pictures. The pull brought us to *your* cellar. I need to know if this is a family matter."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a bus sighed past, ordinary as anything.

"It has always been a family matter," Adaeze said. "Since before you were a word in anyone's mouth."

* * *

She got up then, slow in the way she was never slow, and went to the pantry shelf where she kept the things that weren't food: a tin of sewing supplies, a box of birthday candles, and behind them, wrapped in a cloth faded to the color of weak tea, a photograph album with a cracked spine.

She set it on the table between us like she was setting down something heavy.

"Your great-grandmother's name was Chiamaka," she said. "My mother. You were named for no one, Bram — you know this. Your parents wanted you to be new. But newness is a luxury this family stopped affording in 1911."

She opened the album. The first photographs were stiff and silvered, faces from a country I'd only known through her cooking and her occasional Igbo lullabies. Then Lagos, then the boat, then this city, gray and brick-toothed, the cannery district rising in the background of one shot like a row of dark teeth.

"Your great-grandfather worked the rendering floor," she said. "He came home in 1910 smelling of things he wouldn't describe. He said the drains sang at night. Everyone said he drank. Maybe he did. But he also said the owners were building downward instead of up, and no man builds down unless he's hiding something or feeding something."

The page turned. A group photo outside the cannery gates, 1911 by the banner. Five men in dark coats. Four of them looked at the camera the way people do. The fifth looked slightly off, slightly down, as if listening to something beneath the cobblestones.

"The founders sealed something under the district," Adaeze said. "You read their ledger, so you know they didn't finish. They built the table, they wrote the words, they chose the witness — and then they lost their nerve. Or their faith. Or their stomachs. The record doesn't say which. It says only that the work was left *open*, and an open door does not stay empty."

"What did they bind?" I asked. "What's down there?"

"A hunger with a shape it borrowed." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb pressed hard against the edge of the page, whitening the nail. "It was old before this city was a trading post. The founders fed it for years — animals first, then... it stopped mattering what. What matters is the binding. A thing like that can't be killed. It can only be kept. And keeping requires a keeper who loves what stands above. That's the whole terrible architecture of it. The ritual needs a witness whose love is real, because the thing below feeds on exactly that — attachment, devotion, the warm gravity that holds people to each other. A witness who loves makes the seal strong. A witness who loves makes the bait fresh."

My mouth had gone dry. "Chiamaka was supposed to be the witness."

"My mother was chosen in 1953. She was young, newly married, beloved by everyone on the street — the founders' descendants had waited a generation for someone with enough love in her to hold the door shut from below." Adaeze finally drank her tea. Her hand did not shake, which somehow frightened me more than shaking would have. "They told her what it meant. Descend willingly, with those who love you, take the fifth chair, become the keeper. Forever aware. Forever serving. Never again above ground, never again holding a child, never again tasting pepper or hearing rain. Just awareness, in the dark, doing the work of remembering the binding so the binding holds."

"And she refused."

"She refused." Adaeze closed her eyes. "Can you blame her? She had me. She had a life. She stood at the cellar door of the house we lived in then — this same house, Bram, this same cellar — and she heard the thing below say her name in her husband's voice, in her mother's voice, and she still said no. She slammed the door. She salted it. She never spoke of it again except once, on her deathbed, when she held my hand and said, *Adaeze, ada m, the debt doesn't die with the debtor. It waits for the blood.*"

* * *

The kitchen felt smaller now, the walls leaning in like interested listeners.

"So the family pays instead," I said slowly. "Slowly."

"Harvested," Adaeze said, using my word without flinching. "That's the honest word. An unfinished ritual doesn't cancel itself. It goes on collecting interest. Small withdrawals. A cousin who walked into the harbor in 1968 and was never found. Your great-uncle Emeka, who went down to check a burst pipe in '74 and—" She stopped. Her jaw worked once. "In 1974."

Something cold walked up my spine. "The photos start in 1974."

"Yes." She reached across the album and turned pages with sudden purpose, decades flickering past — birthdays, christenings, Sunday dinners, the same kitchen in six different wallpapers — until she reached a photograph from Christmas 1974. The whole family crammed around the dining table, mid-laugh, someone's hand blurred raising a glass. And against the far wall, half in shadow, a wooden chair. High-backed. Plain. Facing the camera the way furniture doesn't.

"That chair was never bought," Adaeze said. "Never inherited. No one carried it into this house. It appeared in the photographs after Emeka went below. We burned three copies of that picture. It came back in the negatives. We moved houses twice. It followed. Eventually my mother told me to stop burning things — *you cannot burn a receipt, Adaeze. It is proof of what we owe.*"

"Emeka," I said. "He's the occupant. The figure Priya enhanced. The hands."

"My uncle has been sitting in that chair for fifty years," she said quietly, "in every picture this family has ever taken since. Waiting. Because the ritual was interrupted mid-offering when he went down — he wasn't taken properly, he wasn't made keeper, he was just... collected. Collateral. The thing below keeps him the way a shopkeeper keeps a coin on layaway. And every year the family thins. Every year another withdrawal. It is patient because patience costs it nothing, and it knows eventually a generation will come along that loves hard enough to be worth the full price."

I thought of Priya's corrupted enhancement files. Of Dez going pale at dinner. Of Marisol saying *families don't let each other go down alone* without knowing how right she was.

"Why didn't you tell us?" My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Grandma, we've been documenting this thing for weeks. Marisol caught you at the cellar door at three in the morning reciting — what, maintenance? Prayers? And you let us walk into it blind."

"I let you walk into it *loved*," she said, and the word landed like a stone in still water. "Bram. Listen to me. The ritual requires a witness who loves what stands above, accompanied by those who love them. If I had told you the truth in the beginning, you would have been afraid. Fear is not love. Fear would have spoiled you as witnesses — the thing below would have rejected the offering and simply taken, the way it took Emeka, the way it takes cousins off piers. The only chance any of you have is to go down the way the ritual demands: willing, accompanied, loving. So I kept the cellar warm. I kept the dinners long. I taught you all to eat at my table until you loved this house the way blood loves blood." Her eyes glistened but did not spill. "I built the very thing that could destroy you, because it was also the only thing that could save you. Forgive me. Or don't. Both are fine. I'm an old woman who has been complicit for sixty years either way."

The teapot ticked. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked, twice, like a polite request.

"The pull," I said. "It brought all four of us to the cellar without our consent. That's not willing."

"No," she agreed. "Which is why the deadline exists. Before the next new moon, the witness must choose freely — truly freely, feet on stairs, heart open — or the choice gets made *for* them, and being taken is not the same as serving. Taken, you're food. Serving, you're the lock. My mother's refusal condemned Emeka to be food and condemned the rest of us to be slowly eaten. A proper completion — a willing keeper — closes the account. Ends the harvest. Frees whatever can be freed." She looked at me then, directly, and her gaze was the heaviest thing in the room. "You understand what I'm telling you."

"One of us stays."

"One of us stays. Willingly. Loved and loving. And the rest go free — the family debt dies, the chair empties, the tunnels close their appetite. Or none of you choose, and the new moon comes, and the thing below stops being patient and starts being thorough."

* * *

I don't remember standing up, but I was standing, palms flat on the table, the album open between us to the Christmas 1974 photograph, Emeka's blurred suggestion of a figure seated forever in his impossible chair.

"Then it should be me," I said. "I'm the documenter. I've spent my whole life believing recording a thing keeps it from consuming you. Let me be the record. Let me keep it."

Adaeze reached out and covered one of my hands with hers. Her skin was paper over iron.

"Nne m," she said softly. "Do you think I called you here alone to give you permission? I called you here alone because if I said this in front of your friends, one of them would offer first. The girl with no family left. The boy who ran from his brother. The archivist who trusts evidence more than God. They will all offer, Bram. That's what love does in a room like ours. And the ritual will accept whichever offering is most delicious." She squeezed my hand. "I am telling you the truth so that you can carry it better than I did. My mother carried hers in silence and it curdled into cruelty. Carry yours into strategy. Nine days. Decide who decides. And Bram—"

She paused, and for a moment she looked every one of her eighty-some years, a woman standing at the end of a very long corridor of refusals.

"—when you go down there, and you will go down there, look at the fifth chair carefully. Look at what sitting in it for fifty years has done to your great-uncle. And then decide whether love means giving it your body—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—or finding a third thing the founders never imagined. You are the one in this family who believes a record can hold what flesh cannot. Prove it. Because I am too old to dig, and too guilty to pray, and you — you were always the one who photographed the dark to keep it from eating us."

I picked up the album. In the last photograph, taken two Sundays ago at dinner — Priya laughing, Dez mid-story, Marisol reaching for the pepper, me with the camera's timer blinking — the fifth chair sat against the wall.

And for the first time, its occupant was leaning forward.

Toward us. Toward the table. Toward the warmth.

Like something that had been waiting fifty years and had finally, finally smelled the door opening.
