## 20. Above Ground

Dew soaked through Priya's jacket before she knew she was lying on grass.

She came back to herself in pieces, the way a photograph develops — first the cold green smell of morning, then the weight of her own body, then the sky, gray-pink and ordinary, stretched over the rooftops of the neighborhood like nothing had ever been asked of anyone. A sprinkler hissed somewhere down the block. A dog barked twice and lost interest. The world was performing its oldest trick: pretending to have continued while they were gone.

Priya sat up too fast and had to put a hand against the earth to keep it from tilting.

Marisol was ten feet away, curled on her side in Adaeze's flowerbed, one shoe missing, dirt ground into the knees of her jeans like she'd crawled. Dez lay flat on his back in the middle of the lawn with his arms spread wide, staring straight up, chest rising and falling in long deliberate breaths — the breathing he did when he was counting himself back into his body. She'd taught him that breathing, three years ago, after the storm drain collapse on Delmore Street. *Four counts in. Seven counts out. You're still here.*

They were all still here.

Three of them.

The number arrived without ceremony, the way the worst numbers do. Not three of four. Just three. Priya said Bram's name out loud because someone had to, because silence around a name is how you start losing it, and the name came out cracked down the middle. Dez's breathing stuttered. Marisol didn't move at all.

Nobody answered. Of course nobody answered. But the lawn stayed quiet in a way that felt less like absence and more like attention, as if the grass itself were listening to see what they would do next, and Priya understood — with the flat, archival clarity that had always been hers — that this was the first morning of the rest of a life none of them had planned for.

* * *

Adaeze found them there.

The screen door banged open at 6:14 — Priya checked her watch because checking things was how she kept the world bolted down — and the old woman came across the lawn in her housecoat and slippers, moving faster than Priya had ever seen her move, and then slower than Priya had ever seen her move, all in the same walk. She stopped between Dez and the flowerbed and looked at each of their faces one by one, counting them the way Priya counted everything, and when she reached the end of the count and came up one short, her mouth opened and closed twice before any sound came out.

"Where is my grandchild," Adaeze said. It wasn't quite a question.

Priya stood. Her legs held, which surprised her. "Gone," she said. And then, because she owed this woman more than a single syllable, more than she could ever pay: "Not dead. I don't think — we can't say dead. They're in the record now. In the photographs. In the maps." She heard herself talking like a person describing weather in a country she'd never visit. "It took the archive instead of a body. Bram offered it. It accepted."

For a long moment Adaeze just looked at her, and Priya braced for the grief to come off the old woman like heat off asphalt, to be blamed, to be told to leave and never come back. That would have been survivable. Blame has edges; you can grip an edge.

Instead Adaeze sat down on the wet grass — seventy-eight years old, housecoat soaking through without complaint — and began, very quietly, to laugh. It lasted maybe five seconds and contained no joy whatsoever, and then it broke apart into something else, and Dez rolled onto his knees and gathered her into his arms, and Marisol crawled out of the flowerbed to sit close, and Priya stood above all three of them like a lighthouse keeper whose light had gone out, holding her watch, holding her camera bag, holding the only thing she had left to hold.

"They rewrote it," Adaeze said finally, into Dez's shoulder. "My mother refused and paid for sixty years. My grandmother ran and paid longer. And that stubborn child—" her voice caught on the word *child*, though Bram had been thirty-one, "—that stubborn child sat down at the founders' table and renegotiated."

"It's what they'd do," Marisol said softly. "It's exactly what they'd do."

"I know," Adaeze said. "That's why I'm laughing."

* * *

The cellar door was cool under Priya's hand.

That was the first thing. After weeks of warmth radiating from the wood like something breathing behind it, the door was simply a door, painted white, chipping at the lower corner where the paint had always chipped. She pressed her palm flat against it and waited for the pull, the lurch, gravity changing its mind. Nothing. Just the hinge's familiar resistance and the smell of old paint and, beneath it, potatoes and onions and the mineral damp of an ordinary root cellar.

She went down the stairs one at a time, camera hanging unused at her hip.

The cellar was smaller than she remembered. That was the strangest part — not that the chalk symbols were gone, not that the walls were bare plaster instead of breathing stone, but that the room had *shrunk*, contracted back to its honest dimensions like a lung exhaling. Eight feet by ten. Shelving along the east wall. Jars of Adaeze's pepper sauce lined up in their neat regimented rows. A bare bulb on a pull chain. When she pulled it, the light came on yellow and steady and dumb, asking nothing of anyone.

In the corner where the chair used to stand there was nothing but a pale rectangle on the concrete floor, a ghost of discolored cement, the kind of mark a heavy thing leaves after decades. She crouched and touched it. Cold. Ordinary. Already beginning to gather dust, as if it had been empty for years rather than hours.

"Surveys are back," Dez said from the top of the stairs. He had his phone out, and his voice had the careful flatness of a man reading from a document he doesn't trust. "City GIS portal updated overnight. The whole cannery tunnel network — mapped, permitted, 1911, sealed 1934, structural survey 1987. There's even a PDF with a stamp on it." He laughed, one dry note with no floor under it. "There's a *flood mitigation report*. Somebody signed it. There's a signature."

"Let me see." Priya climbed the stairs and took the phone. The map filled the small screen: blue lines and gray lines, contour data, access points, every passage they had bled and sweated through rendered in boring municipal typography. She zoomed until the chamber should have appeared — the founders' table, the five chairs, the place where Bram had thinned into light and paper — and found a blank rectangle labeled STORAGE, NON-CONFORMING, DEMOLISHED 1934.

Demolished. As if it had never been finished. As if the ritual had ended the moment it was completed, retroactively erasing its own architecture, leaving only paperwork where the hunger used to be.

"The tunnels are mundane," she said. "They're *mapped*. Everything we documented — everything Bram documented — it's all just... infrastructure now." She scrolled. Storm drains. Utility conduits. A footnote about asbestos tile in the second-level gallery, which made her laugh in a way that frightened Dez enough that he put a hand on her arm. Because it was true, she realized. It had probably always been true underneath. The horror had been a second layer printed over the city's ordinary body, visible only to the unwitnessed, and now that the witnessing was done — now that the debt was paid in full by the only person who'd ever loved the dark places enough to pray over them in photographs — the two layers had collapsed back into one.

"We could go down," Dez said. "Right now. Walk the whole system with flashlights and a permit number and nothing would look at us twice."

"No," Priya said, and was surprised by the force of it. Then, gentler: "No. We don't go back down. Some doors you leave shut even when they're only doors."

He nodded slowly. But she saw him looking at the cellar stairs the way you look at a grave you know is occupied, and she knew he'd go down alone someday, when the grief got loud enough, and she decided she wouldn't stop him. Everyone deserved one private conversation with the dark.

* * *

It was Marisol who found the photographs.

By noon the three of them had migrated through the rituals of the shell-shocked: showers that ran too long, tea nobody drank, Adaeze insisting on feeding them eggs they couldn't taste. Marisol had been quietly going through the shoebox of prints from the scrapbook project — the gift they'd never finished, forty years of family photos sorted by decade — and she made a sound from the dining table that brought all of them running.

She had the box in front of her and both hands pressed over her mouth.

Priya picked up the top print. Christmas, 1998, judging by the tree and the fashion. Adaeze younger, mid-fifties, laughing at something outside the frame. The living room unchanged except for the wallpaper. And there, at the edge of the shot, half-cropped by the lens: a wooden chair. High-backed, plain, the wood dark with age. Empty.

She picked up another. Adaeze's backyard, 2004, a birthday party, children blurred mid-motion. The chair again, standing on the patio as if it belonged there, as if it had always belonged there. Empty.

Another. The kitchen, 2011. The chair tucked against the wall beside the refrigerator, patient as furniture. Empty.

Every photograph. Every single one, spanning the decades, the chair present in backgrounds where no one had ever noticed it — and Priya felt the old dread stir and then falter, because the chair was *empty*. All of them. Forty years of photos and the occupant was gone from every frame, evicted, unseated, and in its place—

"There," Dez whispered.

In the most recent photograph — taken six months ago, the four of them crammed on Adaeze's porch steps, arms around each other, Bram in the middle making the face they always made when a camera came out — there was a fifth figure. Faint. Warm-edged. Sitting slightly apart from the group in a blur of gold light that might have been lens flare, might have been sunset, might have been neither. It leaned toward Bram's shoulder the way a friend leans in when you've just said something funny.

Bram was leaning back.

Priya set the photo down with hands that shook only a little, because she was an archivist, and archivists know that evidence is sacred even when it breaks your heart. She went to her laptop and pulled the cloud library — eleven thousand images, six years of exploration, every tunnel and rooftop and Sunday dinner — and began to scroll.

Four chairs at dinner. Four chairs at the diner. Four helmets lined up on the tailgate. Four shadows on the tunnel wall, and a fifth shadow, soft and golden, standing a little apart, watching them work.

One empty frame. That was the pattern, once she saw it: wherever four appeared, a fifth space hovered nearby — a gap in a row, a blank square of wall, an out-of-focus rectangle of light shaped like a picture frame within the picture. Not menacing. Not hungry. Just *present*, the way a well-loved photograph is present in a wallet worn soft at the crease.

"It's in everything," Marisol said. Her voice was wrecked but steady. "Every photo we own. It's—"

"Don't," Dez said sharply. Then, softer: "Don't say *it*. Say *them*. Please."

Marisol looked at him, and something passed between them that Priya chose not to archive, some private settlement of terms. "Them," Marisol said. "Them is in every photo we own. Looking at us. Being *with* us." She laughed wetly. "You know what the worst part is? I'm glad. I'm so glad I want to scream. We didn't lose them to a hole in the ground. We lost them to the album. To the record. To the exact place they always said they wanted to live."

"Documentation is a kind of prayer," Priya quoted, barely audible. "They prayed more than anyone alive. Now they *are* the answer."

Dez put his face in his hands.

* * *

They tried removing the empty frames that evening, and that was the day Priya learned the shape of the new rules.

It was Dez's idea — practical, gentle, the kind of mercy a man offers when he can't offer anything else. "We can't keep seeing them everywhere," he said. "Every photo, forever. We'll go crazy. Let's just — take the empties out. Crop them. Put the originals in a box. Nobody has to look."

Priya volunteered, because handling images was her jurisdiction and her penance. She opened the porch photo in her editor, selected the golden blur at Bram's shoulder, and hit delete.

The file corrupted instantly. Not gradually, not the slow rot of the enhanced copies from weeks ago — this was immediate, total, the image collapsing into gray static with a sound her speakers shouldn't have been able to make, a sound like a held breath released. She tried a backup. Same result. She tried cropping instead of deleting, tried painting over the blur, tried simply saving a copy with the region untouched but the canvas resized — every version died on export, every time, within seconds.

But the original file sat in the folder, intact, serene, the golden blur leaning toward Bram's shoulder as if nothing had been attempted.

"It won't let us edit them out," Priya reported, and heard how strange her voice sounded — part scientist, part congregant. "Any alteration corrupts the whole image. The originals persist. Only the originals persist." She thought of the ledger, of the founders' careful ink, of rituals that survive precisely because no one is allowed to revise them. "They're protected now. Archivally. Permanently." She almost smiled. Almost. "Bram would find that hilarious. All those years fighting me about metadata standards, and now they've enforced their own preservation policy from beyond the—"

She stopped. The word *beyond* wouldn't come. There was no beyond. That was the whole point. Bram hadn't gone anywhere. Bram had become *here* — distributed across every frame, every map annotation, every recorded voice memo, present the way water is present in a sponge.

Marisol reached over and closed the laptop lid with terrible tenderness. "Then we keep them," she said. "All of them. Every photo, every empty frame. On the walls. In the albums. We hang them where we can see." Her chin trembled and her voice didn't. "Families don't hide their people."

So they hung them. That evening, the three of them moved through Adaeze's house with hammer and nails and museum-gloved care, rehanging forty years of photographs with their new resident fully visible. Adaeze supervised from the doorway, dabbing her eyes with the corner of her apron, occasionally pointing — *that one higher, mi vida, the 1998 one goes higher* — and by nightfall the walls of the house told a story with a new character in it, a warm golden presence at the edge of every memory, leaning in, keeping watch, impossible to remove and impossible, now, to imagine the pictures without.

The last nail went into the wall at dusk. Priya stepped back and looked at the porch photo on the living room wall, framed in black, lit by the lamp Adaeze had angled toward it, and she spoke to it the way she'd once spoken to hard drives she was formatting — formally, apologetically, with respect for whatever might be listening.

"We kept your backups," she said. "All of them. Redundant storage, three locations, like you always nagged us about." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "You'd better be updating your will. Digital assets clause. I'm not doing your estate paperwork twice."

The lamp flickered once — the bulb, just the bulb, old wiring, she told herself, she would check the wiring tomorrow — and in the glass of the frame, for half a second, the golden blur seemed to lean closer, and Priya could have sworn, could have sworn on every archive she'd ever built, that the shape of it was smiling.

* * *

Later, when Dez and Marisol had gone home and Adaeze was asleep, Priya sat at the kitchen table with her laptop open to Bram's documentation site — the urban exploration archive, six years of maps and photographs and field notes, the public record of a life spent praying in shutter clicks.

The site had updated.

She stared at the timestamp for a long time. 2:47 a.m., tonight. A new post, published while the three of them slept on Adaeze's lawn-turned-guest-rooms, while the city hummed its ordinary hum overhead. The title was simple, in Bram's unmistakable lowercase style:

*cannery district — final survey (read this before you go down)*

Her hand hovered over the trackpad. The cursor trembled. Outside, the sprinkler next door hissed through its arc, and the refrigerator cycled, and the house settled around its new silence, and Priya Ishikawa — skeptic, archivist, keeper of the evidence — clicked the link.

The page loaded. The map drew itself line by line across her screen, blue passages spreading through the cannery district like veins, annotated in a voice she would know anywhere, alive in every keystroke:

*hey. it's me. don't panic — okay, panic a little, it's good for the reflexes. here's what i need you to know about the deep places now that i live in them...*

Priya began, very quietly, to cry — and to read.
