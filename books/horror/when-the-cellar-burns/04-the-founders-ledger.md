## 4. The Founders' Ledger

The church of Hollowmere smelled of cold stone and tallow, a smell so old it had stopped being a smell and become a fact. Mara stood at the top of the stairs leading down to the archive, her breath fogging in the dark below, and told herself — for the third time that morning — that she was here as a scholar. Nothing more.

The frost on her grandmother's kitchen window had written *VILLALOBOS* overnight. Her own name, in the spidery hand the frost favored, followed by a date: the twenty-third of November. Nine days from now. She hadn't slept after that. She'd sat up with the photograph album open on her knees until dawn turned the windows grey, watching the seated man sharpen by degrees, like ink soaking into paper. He was still blurred, but the blur had structure now. Shoulders. A high collar. Hands folded in his lap with terrible patience.

*You're a folklorist,* she reminded herself, descending the stairs. *Folklorists don't flinch.*

Reverend Ansel Crane had given her the key without questions, which itself should have been a question. "Mind the damp," he'd said, pressing the cold iron into her palm. "And mind what you carry back up." She'd laughed. He hadn't.

The basement was long and low, its ceiling lost in shadow, shelves marching away into darkness on either side. Jars of preserves lined some of them — plums, damsons, things gone the color of bruises. But deeper in, past the last jar, the shelves held books. Ledgers, mostly, their spines cracked and furred with mold. Parish records. Tithe rolls. And one volume, bound in black leather gone green at the edges, with no title at all.

It was warm.

Not metaphorically warm. Mara's fingers registered actual heat when she lifted it from the shelf, as if the book had been sitting by a fire, or as if it were generating its own small fever. The leather clung to her skin for half a heartbeat before letting go.

She carried it to the single lamp on the reading table, sat, and opened it.

The first page bore a date older than the village charter: *In the year of the Long Hunger, we who remain do set down what we have done, so that those who come after will not have to do it blind.*

The handwriting was beautiful. That was the first thing that unsettled her — how beautiful it was, how calm, a script written by someone with steady hands and a settled heart. Six names signed beneath the line. One of them was *Edric Vale*, and Mara's mouth went dry, because she knew that name now. She'd seen it on the founder's portrait in the church hall upstairs, three days ago, hanging above the portrait of a man whose face the frost seemed determined to show her every night.

She read on.

*The covenant is simple, though it cost us much to agree upon it. Each of us gives a portion of what we remember into the common pool, and the pool keeps us. In famine winters, when the body starves, the memory feeds what the body cannot. A village that remembers together does not break together. We have seen what hunger does to houses that hoard. We will not hoard. Not our grain, and not our pasts.*

There were diagrams. Circles within circles, drawn with a compass and annotated in that same serene hand. Words she didn't recognize — or thought she didn't, until she caught herself mouthing them along with the page, pronunciations rising unbidden from somewhere below her scholarship, somewhere below her childhood, somewhere she could not name. She stopped. Pressed her palms flat against the table. The wood was cold; the book was warm; between them her hands trembled.

*The pool asks only this,* the ledger continued, pages turning under her fingers as if eager. *That nothing be taken that was not freely given. This is the law above all laws. What is given in love may be borrowed in need. What is taken in fear rots the vessel that holds it.*

Mara read that passage four times. Something about it snagged, like a burr in wool. *Freely given.* She thought of Tam Vey going pale mid-hymn. She thought of Dora Hale's casseroles appearing on her doorstep, of the Henderson boy calling her *cousin* before they'd exchanged ten words, of how quickly, how *hungrily*, Hollowmere had opened its arms to a stranger with a recorder and a notebook. Hospitality, she'd called it. Generosity.

She thought of the photographs. Of the chair that appeared in every image of her grandmother's house, and the man growing clearer in it night by night, patient as winter.

*What is taken in fear rots the vessel that holds it.*

"Someone broke the law," she whispered to the empty basement. Her voice came back flat and close, swallowed by stone.

The later entries changed. The handwriting remained lovely, but the content curdled by degrees, the way milk turns — slowly, then all at once. Entries about *harvesting*. About *tithes of remembrance* collected not in famine but in plenty. About outsiders — peddlers, surveyors, a schoolmistress who stayed one winter too long — whose memories were *pooled* without signature, without consent, their names recorded in columns like livestock. One entry, dated sixty years back, read simply: *The Villalobos woman has been most generous.* No first name. No explanation of what generosity meant, or what it had purchased.

Mara sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere overhead, the church creaked as the building settled against the cold, or leaned closer to listen.

Her grandmother. It had to be. *The Villalobos woman.* Her grandmother had come here once, decades ago — Mara had the postcards to prove it, the ones with the extra chair in them now. Had she visited? Had she been *harvested*? Was that why the house had called Mara back, why the frost wrote her name, why the man in the photographs looked at her with an expression she was beginning to understand as recognition?

She turned pages faster now, chasing the entries toward the present, toward answers. The ledger grew thinner in her hands. And then —

The final page was torn out.

Not removed cleanly. Torn, violently, the stub of it still glued deep in the spine, ragged fibers standing up like torn flesh. Whatever had been written there — the price, the ending, the terms of completion — someone had wanted it unread badly enough to damage the book itself. Mara ran her thumb along the stub. The paper was old. The tearing was not recent. Decades, at least. Someone had known, all that time ago, exactly what needed to disappear.

Except the frost was writing something on her window, night by night, line by line, toward a final pattern due on the twenty-third of November. Someone or something remembered the missing page perfectly.

She copied everything. Three hours of work, her pen scratching in the silence, transcribing entries and diagrams into her notebook while the lamp burned low. When she reached for the ledger to check a date, the warmth of it startled her again — warmer now than before, almost blood-warm, as if the reading had fed it somehow. As if it had enjoyed being read.

She put it back on the shelf. The shelf exhaled a breath of cold air across her wrist, and she did not stay to interpret it.

* * *

Sleep took her like a trapdoor.

She dreamed of the kitchen in her grandmother's house — but the kitchen was wrong, stretched too long, the table running away into darkness where walls should be. Every chair at the table was occupied. Dozens of chairs, dozens of figures, all of them her grandmother's neighbors, her grandmother's friends, faces from the photographs, and none of them turned to look at her. They sat facing forward, hands folded, waiting for something.

At the head of the table stood the extra chair.

The man in it was clearer than she'd ever seen him — clear as the portrait upstairs, clear as daylight. High collar, hollow cheeks, eyes the grey of ice over deep water. Edric Vale. He was trying to speak. His mouth moved with urgent, careful articulation, shaping each word as if words were fragile things that might shatter if handled roughly, and no sound came out at all. The silence pressed against her eardrums like deep water.

*Slower,* she tried to tell him. *Say it slower.*

His lips formed three syllables. Then five. Then, with excruciating care, one word at a time, a whole sentence — and she watched every shape his mouth made and understood none of it, because the dream had taken sound the way the ledger's final page had been taken from the book, torn out at the root.

He saw her fail. She watched him see it. And instead of frustration, his face filled with something worse: grief. The specific grief of a man who has been trying to deliver a warning for a very long time, to a great many people, and has learned exactly how it ends.

Then he raised one hand — not toward himself, but past her, toward the darkness at the end of the table — and pointed.

Mara turned. At the far end of the impossible kitchen, where the wall should have been, there was a door. Cellar-dark. Standing open. And from the darkness beyond it came a sound she could hear perfectly, the only sound in the whole silent dream: singing. Hundreds of voices, soft and layered, singing the founding hymn in perfect unison — the hymn no villager would sing twice — and underneath the melody, so faint she might have imagined it, another sound. Weeping. Or laughter. Or both, worn down to the same thing.

Edric Vale's hand closed around her wrist. His grip was cold and solid and real, and his mouth shaped one final word, slow, deliberate, holding each syllable like a man carrying water across ice:

*Burn.*

Mara woke with her own name in her throat and frost climbing the inside of the bedroom window — the *inside* — spelling out the next line in the countdown. Two more lines remained blank below it. Nine days.

But it wasn't the frost that kept her sitting upright until morning, blanket clutched to her chest, listening to the house breathe around her.

It was the memory of the door at the end of the table. Because she knew that door. She'd walked past it yesterday, in the church basement, without giving it a second glance — a low wooden door at the far end of the archive, padlocked shut, marked with a sign in Reverend Crane's careful hand:

*STORAGE. KEEP OUT.*

And this morning, when she'd left the church, she had seen fresh boot-prints in the frost on the flagstones outside it. Going down. Coming up.

Going down carrying nothing.

Coming up carrying jars.
