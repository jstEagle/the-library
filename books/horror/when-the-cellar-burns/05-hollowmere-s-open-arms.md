## 5. Hollowmere's Open Arms

The bread arrived on the third morning, wrapped in a cloth still warm from the oven, sitting on Mara's doorstep like an offering left at a shrine. There was no note. By noon there was a jar of honey beside it, and by evening someone had mended the sagging gate that had groaned against its hinge since her arrival.

She hadn't asked for any of it.

Mara stood in the lane with the honey in her hands and watched old Wren Cutler — a man who, two weeks ago, had crossed the street to avoid speaking to her — rehang her garden door with the unhurried competence of a man doing work he'd done a thousand times.

"You didn't have to," she said.

"Didn't I?" He smiled, and his eyes crinkled like paper held too near a flame. "Felt right, is all. Like something owed."

*Owed.* The word sat strangely in the cold air. She thanked him, and he touched his cap and walked away whistling — three notes of the founding hymn, she realized, the same three notes Tam Vey had sung before going pale as milk.

That night she catalogued the day's kindnesses in her field journal the way she'd once catalogued ballad variants: bread, honey, gate, door hinge oiled, chimney swept while she was at the archive. A list is just a list, she told herself. Villages are sometimes kind to strangers. But Hollowmere hadn't been kind to strangers. Hollowmere had been *watchful* of strangers, for two full weeks, with the flat patience of a trap left baited.

And now the trap had sprung shut around her, lined with wool.

* * *

By the fifth day, the children had named her.

"Cousin Mara!" they shouted from the frozen pond, where they skated in ragged circles under a sky the color of dishwater. "Cousin Mara, watch this!"

She watched. She laughed. And somewhere between one laugh and the next, she felt something *shift* — a small, painless loosening behind her ribs, like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was in her chest.

The girl who'd shouted — Ivy Thorne, eight years old, gap-toothed, fearless — skated over and caught Mara's mittened hand without asking. "You'll come to supper Sunday? Gran wants you. She says you've got the look of family about you."

"I only met your gran once."

Ivy grinned up at her, utterly certain. "That's not what she says."

Walking home through the blue dusk, Mara tried to reconstruct exactly when the village had turned toward her, and found the memory strangely soft-edged, like a photograph left too long in the sun. She could remember *that* the baker had been cold to her. She could no longer quite remember his face doing it.

She stopped in the middle of the lane, breath smoking, and made herself stand very still.

Her grandmother's face. Think of her grandmother's face. It came — mostly. The strong jaw, the crooked incisor, the scar over the left eyebrow from a fall off a hay wagon in 1961. All present. But the details arrived like items read off an inventory rather than a face rising unbidden behind her eyes. She had to *fetch* them. They used to come on their own, crowding, insistent, alive.

Something was taking them. Not stealing — taking the way a river takes silt, gently, continuously, without malice or even awareness. Every meal shared, every hand clasped, every time a child called her cousin and she answered — something flowed out of her and did not come back.

"The song remembers you back," Tam Vey had said.

It wasn't only the song. It was everything. Hollowmere remembered *her*, and the remembering cost.

* * *

She began testing it, because testing things was the only prayer she knew.

Each morning she wrote three memories in her journal in careful detail — sensory, specific, dated. Each evening she reread them and scored how much felt *hers*. The taste of her mother's orange cake: intact, vivid, 10/10. Her first kiss behind the university library: intact but distant, 7/10, the boy's name requiring effort. The smell of her grandmother's cellar — damp stone and dried lavender — intact, but when she closed her eyes to summon it, what rose instead was *this* cellar, the church basement, the founders' ledger, its smell of tallow and iron.

The pool was substituting. Filling the space it emptied with itself.

On the eighth morning she woke knowing the words to a lullaby she had never heard, in a dialect that predated the county. On the ninth, she caught herself answering to "cousin" without a flicker of surprise. On the tenth, she dreamed in Hollowmere's accent.

She sat up in the dark of her grandmother's house — *her* house now, the deed said, though the house disagreed nightly — and looked at the photographs spread across the kitchen table.

The chair-man was clearer again. He always sharpened after days when the giving ran heavy, as if her memories were feeding him through whatever thin channel connected them. His face was nearly resolved now: long, austere, patient as winter. She knew it from the founder's portrait in the church vestry. Edric Vale, the plaque said. *Died in service of the covenant.*

He was looking directly out of the photograph at her. He was always looking directly at her. But tonight — she leaned close, breath fogging the glossy surface — tonight his mouth seemed slightly open. Mid-word. Mid-*plea*.

"Don't eat what they give you," she whispered, inventing his counsel, needing him to be an ally. "Don't let them feed you."

But she ate what they gave her. That was the horror of it. The stew Dora Hale sent up from the inn was the best thing Mara had tasted in years, rich and dark and seasoned with herbs she couldn't name, and she ate every bowl down to the glaze at the bottom, and every empty bowl left her lighter, thinner, more *theirs*.

Belonging, she was learning, was a currency. And she was spending herself to buy it, at a stall where she'd never agreed to trade.

* * *

At the inn, Dora Hale counted spoons.

It was a habit from her mother, who'd counted them before her — forty-one spoons in the good chest, forty-one, because forty-one was the number of households in Hollowmere and each spoon was a household and if a spoon went missing then a household went missing, and this was the sort of arithmetic that kept a village alive through the hungry years. Dora's mother had taught her the counting the same winter she'd taught her which memories were safe to give and which must be walled up tight behind teeth and silence.

Dora counted the spoons. Forty-one. Then she poured herself a finger of plum brandy and sat in the inn's back parlor, where the fire was small and the shadows were honest, and she thought about Mara Villalobos.

The girl was dissolving nicely. That was the ugly truth of it, and Dora made herself look at it straight, the way you look at a wound before cleaning it. The pool had taken to her like warm water takes to sugar. Ten days of suppers and mended gates and children's voices, and already the girl's edges had gone soft — already she laughed at jokes whose context the pool had fed her, already she hummed tunes older than the church. Another fortnight and Mara Villalobos would be Cousin Mara entire, a daughter of Hollowmere in every way that mattered, her strange outside memories folded into the common stock like flour into dough.

And the elders would call it a triumph. *A new vein opened,* Aldous Crane would say at the winter meeting, in that voice like a well bucket descending. *The pool runs sweet again.*

Dora turned her glass. The brandy caught the firelight and held it, amber and trembling.

Because here was the thing Dora had never said aloud, not in forty years of counting spoons: the pool did not run sweet. It ran *thin*. Every harvest thinned it further, the way breeding from a shallow stock weakens the line. The elders took and took — from travelers, from drifters, from their own dissenters — and the protection the covenant bought grew weaker each generation, the frost script crept closer to its final pattern each winter, and the elders' answer was always the same: take more. Take deeper. Take the folklorist with the founder's blood in her veins, whose grandmother had escaped the pool fifty years ago carrying secrets in her head like stolen coins.

They thought Mara was a harvest.

Dora set down the glass. In the coals, a log shifted and collapsed, throwing sparks up the flue like escaping birds.

She had seen the girl's journal page once, through the window — the little scoring system, the numbers, the slow subtraction of self. Mara was fighting it. Cleverly, quietly, alone. Which meant the girl would either break soon, or do something worse than break.

Dora's mother had walled up one memory above all others, the night before she died, gripping Dora's wrist hard enough to bruise: *The ledger's last page, girl. The price that was torn out. You remember it, and you remember you remember, and when the time comes —*

But that memory was walled too, wasn't it. That was the cruelty of walls; they kept things from the pool, and they kept things from you.

Dora closed her eyes and pressed her palms flat against her temples, as if she could hold the shape of the thought through sheer pressure, the way you press a hand to a bleeding wound.

Outside, the frost was writing on the inn windows. She could hear it — a fine crystalline whisper, like sand pouring through an hourglass, like a pen scratching across the world's largest page. Line by line. Name by name. Counting down.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would carry the stew up to the girl's house herself, sit across the table, and start talking. Not the pool's talk — the other kind. The kind with walls around it.

Forty-one spoons. She counted them one last time, and blew out the candle.
