## 10. What We Choose to Remember

Spring came to Hollowmere eleven days early, and no one could say why.

Mara woke to birdsong — actual birdsong, the first she'd heard since arriving in the village — and lay still beneath her grandmother's quilt, listening to it the way you listen to a language you almost remember. The window above her bed was clear. No frost script. No names, no dates, no countdown crawling toward its final line. Just glass, and beyond it a sky the color of a rinsed-out bruise going blue at the edges, and the long white fields already weeping themselves into mud.

She had expected grief to hit her like a wave. It didn't. It came instead as a series of small absences, each one discovering itself at its own pace, like rooms in a dark house being opened one by one.

Her mother's face. She reached for it and found a shape where it should have been — warmth without features, love without a portrait. She knew she had a mother. She knew she had been loved. The knowing was intact; only the picture was gone, burned with everything else in the cellar's yellow light.

*That's the price,* Edric had told her, in the last hour of his unraveling, his voice already thinning into something like wind through reeds. *The pool held what they stole. But it also held what you gave. When it burns, it takes back everything it ever kept.*

"You didn't tell me it would take *you*," she'd said.

He had smiled then — the founder's face from the portrait, the man from the chair, finally wearing an expression that belonged to no painting. "I was never yours to keep, Mara. I was only ever borrowed."

Now she sat up in the empty house and tested herself the way you test a broken bone: gently, braced for the pain. Her name. Her work. The folk songs of the northern counties, verse after verse rising unimpaired. Her grandmother's kitchen, the smell of cardamom bread — there, that survived. But the summer she'd spent here as a child: gone. A whole season, a whole woman who had taught her to gut fish and swear in two languages, dissolved into smoke along with a hundred years of stolen Hollowmere memory.

And Dora Hale. She pressed on that absence hardest of all, the way your tongue returns to a missing tooth. She knew Dora had held the cellar door against her own kind while the archive burned. She knew Dora had confessed, had wept, had chosen Mara over three centuries of complicity. She remembered the confession itself, every word of it — but the friendship that must have grown around those words, the meals shared, the laughter, the slow accretion of trust: all of it had lived partly in the pool, and the pool was ash.

The house felt enormous around her. She got dressed anyway.

* * *

Hollowmere looked like a village recovering from a fever.

People stood in their doorways in the early light, blinking, touching their own faces as if confirming ownership. Old Tam Vey sat on his step with a fiddle in his lap he hadn't played in forty years, plucking out a tune and stopping mid-phrase, staring at his hands. Two children ran past Mara's gate chasing a dog that had, until yesterday, been nothing but a name in a ledger. Everywhere she looked, people were remembering — and grieving what they couldn't.

They saw her, and they stopped.

Not with hostility. With something worse and better: recognition without recollection. They knew her name. They knew what she had done. The details of how they'd come to love her — because she could see in their faces that they had loved her, that the love itself had survived even where its history hadn't — those details were ash too.

Dora came down the lane last, moving slowly, one hand pressed to her side as though something in there had torn loose. She was older than she'd been a week ago. Whatever years the jars had been holding back had come home to her all at once, settling into her shoulders and the deep lines around her mouth. She stopped at Mara's gate, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"I don't remember you," Dora said finally. Her voice was steady but her eyes weren't. "I remember the fire. I remember choosing. I don't remember why I—" She stopped, jaw tightening. "No. That's not true. I remember enough. I remember that when the elders came for you, I was not afraid to stand in that doorway. And I have spent my whole life afraid of exactly one thing." She looked up. "So whatever else burned, that part of me knows you."

Mara's throat closed. "I don't remember you either," she managed. "I'm sorry. I keep reaching for—"

"Don't apologize." Dora pushed open the gate and came up the path and took Mara's hands in both of hers, weathered and warm. "We were given to each other once by a machine. A covenant. A pool. None of it was ours." Her grip tightened. "So we start again. Plainly. On purpose. If you'll have it."

Behind them, doors were opening all down the lane. Tam Vey set down his fiddle. The children stopped running. One by one, the people of Hollowmere turned toward the two women standing in the thawing morning, and Mara understood that this — this awkward, unmagical, entirely human moment — was the first thing the village had ever chosen freely in three hundred years.

"Come inside," Mara said. "I'll make tea. I'll make all of it wrong, because half of what I know about this house is gone."

"Good," said Dora. "I'll correct you. We'll argue about it. That's how family works — I'm told."

* * *

They rebuilt the songs first.

It was Mara's idea, offered at the spring meeting in the church — the same basement where the ledger had waited, now swept clean, the shelf-scars of the jar racks still visible in the stone like old surgical marks. Half the village came. They sat in mismatched chairs — real chairs, ordinary chairs, chairs that appeared in photographs exactly as many times as there were people to sit in them — and they sang.

The founding hymn came apart easily once no one was compelled to protect it. Without the ritual's grip, it was just a song again, and a rather plain one: a harvest round, four lines, the kind of thing any village in the county might have sung. What made Hollowmere's version strange had only ever been the silence wrapped around it. Now Tam Vey sang it through twice, three times, daring anyone to go pale, and nobody did, and he laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

Then the villagers began bringing Mara other things. Songs their grandmothers had hummed and never explained. Lullabies in an accent that predated the county maps. A wedding tune with a hole in the third bar where a verse had been harvested from some dissenting family generations ago — the melody limping, incomplete, waiting. Mara recorded all of it, her grandmother's reel-to-reel turning faithfully, and wrote in her notebook the sentence she'd been circling since the frost first spoke to her:

*The song remembers you back. But only if you let it forget you first.*

Some gaps could not be filled. A dozen villagers had lost parents to the harvest over the decades — memories taken whole, stored, burned before they could be returned. Those people carried absences that would never resolve, and Mara learned to sit with them in the evenings, doing the only honest work left: helping them build memorials out of what remained. A name carved in the churchyard wall. A chair left empty at a table, on purpose, in honor rather than haunting.

Edric got his place among the founders' portraits — not the idealized canvas in the church hall, but a new carving, rough-hewn by Tam Vey's hands, set low where children could reach it. Beneath it Mara mounted the last photograph of him: the chair by the fire, the blurred figure sharpened almost fully into a man, caught between worlds. She could hardly look at it. She had known him for less than a week and lost him forever, and the grief had no proper shape because so much of what she'd felt about him had pooled and burned with everything else. But sometimes, washing dishes or walking the lane at dusk, she'd catch herself speaking aloud to an empty room — offering some observation about the weather, the state of the roads — and stopping, puzzled, warmed, unsure who the habit belonged to.

Maybe that was the truest memorial. A kindness with no remembered source.

* * *

On the first truly warm evening, Dora found her in the garden behind the house, turning the frozen earth for planting.

"They're asking what to do with the cellar," Dora said. She'd brought two cups of tea and handed one down without ceremony, settling onto the stone step like a woman who intended to stay. "Empty now. Swept. Some want to brick it up. Some want to forget it exists."

"What do you want?"

Dora considered. "I want a door on it that locks from the outside. And shelves. And I want the village to decide, together, every year, whether to open it." She sipped her tea. "Memory hoarded becomes a weapon. Memory thrown away becomes a wound. There's a middle road. We've never walked it. Might as well start."

Mara laughed — really laughed, the first time since the fire — and Dora's face did something complicated and pleased, a stranger learning the shape of a friend.

"When do you leave?" Dora asked, after a while. Not accusing. Just asking.

"My bus is Thursday." Mara leaned back against the cold fence post. "The book needs writing. And my mother—" she faltered on the word, on the faceless warmth behind it, "—my mother deserves to be remembered by someone, even if I have to reintroduce myself to do it."

"And after?"

"After, I think—" She looked down the lane, where lamps were coming on one by one in windows that stayed lit because someone wanted them lit, in houses whose doors stood open because open was allowed now. "After, I think I come back. In autumn. For the frost. To see what it writes when it's writing nothing at all."

Dora nodded slowly, and Mara watched her decide something. "There's a room upstairs," Dora said. "East-facing. Good light for your work. It's been sitting empty a long time." A pause, and then, plainly, deliberately, the way you hand someone a gift with both hands: "It'll be yours whenever you want it. Not because of anything we can't remember. Because of this. Right here. What we choose."

Mara's eyes stung. She looked away, at the garden, at the dark soil giving up its frost, at the first green shoots of something pushing through where nothing had been planted yet.

"Thursday," she said. "Ask me again Thursday. And every Thursday after that, for as long as it takes to make it true."

Above the village, the first star came out. Somewhere a fiddle started up — Tam Vey, playing the harvest round, playing it twice, playing it as many times as he pleased — and the song rose over the rooftops of Hollowmere and asked nothing of anyone, and remembered no one against their will, and was, at last, only a song.
