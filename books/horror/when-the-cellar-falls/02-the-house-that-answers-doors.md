## 2. The House That Answers Doors

The drive out to Hollowmere took forty minutes and cost me most of my good mood. Not because of the distance—distance is fine, distance is billable—but because the last ten minutes were all gravel and hedgerow, the kind of road that makes you feel like you're being swallowed by the county one field at a time. My van's suspension registered its complaints in a language I've come to understand fluently. The satnav gave up two turns back, which I logged mentally as *navigation failure, 6:41 p.m., rural route, no signal*. If nothing else, this job was going to produce excellent paperwork.

I like paperwork. I want to be clear about that up front, because people always assume pest controllers got into the business because we love killing things, or because we couldn't hack it in real estate, or some other insult dressed as small talk. No. I got into this business because a job with no documentation is a job that never happened, and I have spent my whole life making sure things happened. Photographs before treatment. Photographs after. Timestamps on everything. Coordinates for every entry point, every droppings trail, every smear mark along a baseboard. My logbook is not a notebook. It is a rosary, and I say it nightly.

The dispatcher had given me the address twice, which should have been my first warning. Dispatchers don't repeat themselves unless something about the call made them nervous, and Mrs. Okafor has been doing dispatch for eleven years without ever once being nervous about anything except her coffee supply.

"Hollowmere House," she'd said. "Old place, past Marlow Fen. Client says there's something inside the walls."

"Rodent activity, interior wall voids. Standard."

"No." She'd paused, and I remember the pause the way you remember a wrong note in an otherwise pleasant song. "The client says there's *something* inside the walls. Their word. They said—" she'd checked her notes, I could hear the paper— "they said it leaves fingerprints."

"Fingerprints."

"On the *inside*, Mr. Ishikawa. Of sealed walls."

I'd written it down anyway, because that's what I do. *Client reports impressions resembling fingerprints on interior face of sealed wall cavities.* And then I'd underlined it once, lightly, the way you touch a bruise to see if it still hurts.

Now here I was at 7:03 p.m., dusk coming down like a lid, and Hollowmere House rising out of the last of the light the way a whale rises out of dark water—slowly, enormously, and with the distinct suggestion that it had been down there the whole time, watching.

It was a big house. Georgian bones, probably, though the skin had been allowed to fail in places: shutters hanging by one screw, ivy doing the work of mortar along the east wing, a chimney leaning just enough to make you tilt your head. The windows were dark except for one on the second floor, where the glass caught the dying light and held it, orange and stubborn, like an eye that hadn't decided whether to close.

I parked, set the brake, and did what I always do. I photographed the exterior from four angles. Timestamped. Then I sat for a moment with my logbook open on the steering wheel and wrote:

*Arrived 19:04. Property: Hollowmere House. Structure: detached, two-storey plus cellar (assumed), approx. 8–10 rooms visible. Condition: neglected but structurally sound. No vehicles present. No lights observed. Client not yet visible.*

That last sentence felt strange in my hand. *Client not yet visible.* Usually by arrival time the client is on the porch, wringing their hands, asking me if it's rats, please tell them it's rats. Usually there's a dog, or a nervous teenager, or an old man who wants to show me his attic before I've even got my kit out of the van. Houses are full of people. That's the thing nobody tells you about pest control: half the job is managing the humans, and the humans are always worse than the mice.

Hollowmere House had no humans. It had a front door standing open six inches into the dark, like a mouth mid-word.

I sat with that for a while.

Here's the thing about me, the thing I've built my entire life around the way you build a house around a load-bearing wall: nobody remembers me. This is not self-pity; it's a professional observation, verified over decades. Waiters forget me mid-order. Neighbors of fifteen years greet me with the polite panic of strangers. At my mother's funeral—I'll come back to my mother, maybe, someday, in a chapter I haven't written—the vicar asked me twice if I was family. I am a man whose name slides off the world like water off waxed paper, and I have made peace with it. Made a career of it, even. Nobody remembers the pest controller. We are the ghosts who come when called and leave no trace but the absence of vermin. There's a freedom in it. There's safety.

So when I looked at that open door and felt—not fear, exactly, fear came later—when I felt *expected*, the feeling didn't fit. It snagged against everything I knew about myself the way a coat snags on a nail.

Because the door wasn't just open. Open doors happen. Drafts happen. What doesn't happen is the porch light being on, which it was, a yellow bulb burning steady above the frame, and what really, truly doesn't happen is what I saw when I finally got out of the van and walked up the cracked flagstones and looked through that six-inch gap into the entrance hall.

There was a table just inside. A hall table, dark wood, doily underneath whatever sat on it. And on the table, open, angled toward the door like a face turned to watch someone come in, was a guest book.

I'm going to be honest with you, since honesty is apparently the theme of my evening: I stood on that porch for four minutes. I know it was four because I checked my watch at the start and the end, and I wrote both times down afterward, because that's what I do, that's the armor, that's the ritual. Four minutes on the porch of a stranger's house, telling myself very reasonable things. *Clients leave doors open for contractors all the time. Clients leave notes. Maybe the book is for deliveries. Maybe the client stepped out. People step out.*

None of those sentences survived contact with the guest book.

I went in. Of course I went in. You can't invoice a job you didn't enter, and besides—and I want this on the record, whoever ends up reading this logbook, if anyone does—some part of me wanted to see. That's the crack I'm describing, the first hairline fracture in the load-bearing wall. For thirty-nine years I have arranged my life so that no one looks at me, and standing on that threshold I realized that some starving corner of me had been waiting my whole career for a door to already be open. For something in the dark to say, quietly, in the language of furniture and lamplight: *you. We meant you. Come in.*

I documented the entry anyway. Procedure is procedure, and procedure is the only prayer I know.

*Entered via front door, 19:11. Door unlocked and ajar approximately 15 cm. Porch light operational. No client present. Guest book open on hall table.*

Then, because the logbook demands corroboration and my hands wanted something to do besides shake, I photographed the guest book.

My name was written in it.

Not scrawled. Not guessed. Written in a fine, careful hand, copperplate-adjacent, the kind of penmanship they stopped teaching before I was born, in ink that had dried brown-black with age: **August Ishikawa.** Below it, in the same hand, the date. Today's date. And below that, in a column labeled *Purpose of Visit* in faded type, the same handwriting had filled in: *To finish what was started.*

I did not write that. I want to be extremely clear about that. I have never seen this book before tonight. The ink was old. The ink was *old*, is the point—it had that oxidized tone you get from decades in a drawer, not hours on a table. Whatever hand had written my name had done it long ago, in a year when I was possibly not yet born, and had known—not predicted, *known*—that on this date, at dusk, a man named August Ishikawa would stand in this hallway and read it.

There is a version of me—a smarter version, probably—who put the book down, walked out, drove home, and filed the whole thing under *not my department*. Fingerprints inside walls, pre-written guest books, houses that answer doors before you knock: none of that is covered by my certification. There's a hotline for that sort of thing. Probably. I've never needed it.

But the smarter version of me has never once won an argument, because the other version of me—the one holding the camera, the one logging timestamps—had noticed something else about the guest book, something that turned the dread into something worse, something almost tender.

Above my entry, the page was full. Dozens of names, decades of them, fading backward through time like footprints in receding tide. *E. Halloway, 1961. C. Halloway, 1958. M. Halloway, 1954.* A family, signing in season after season, year after year, and then—this was the part I had to photograph three times to confirm—stopping. Mid-century, the names simply end. The rest of the page is blank. Fifty years of blank lines. And then mine, at the bottom, patient as a seed.

A house doesn't stay occupied for fifty years on hope. Someone kept this book. Someone expected guests.

I closed it gently, the way you close a hymnal, and I told myself it was to protect the pages. It was to keep myself from reading it again.

* * *

The entrance hall smelled the way old houses smell when they've been empty but not abandoned—beeswax and cold ash and something green underneath, like a garden pressed between pages. Someone had swept recently. I noted that. *Floor: recently swept, no cobwebs in stairwell corners, dust disturbed in patterns consistent with foot traffic within past 48 hrs.* An empty house with clean floors is either inhabited or haunted, and both options fall outside my service agreement, and I logged it anyway, because the alternative to logging it was thinking about it, and I would much rather have data than thoughts. Data sits still. Thoughts move around.

I worked the hall the way I work any site: slow, systematic, left to right. Skirting boards first—no gnaw marks, no grease trails, which ruled out rodents, which I had privately known since the word *fingerprints*. Window frames—sealed, painted shut, intact. Then the walls themselves, and here I'll admit my flashlight lingered. I ran my palm along the plaster of the north wall, flat and cool and unbroken, and tried to imagine a hand pressing against the other side hard enough to leave a print. Plaster doesn't take prints. Plaster takes cracks, dents, the occasional fist-shaped hole from a bad night. It does not record touch. Skin leaves oil; oil needs a surface that holds it; sealed plaster holds nothing. The physics of the complaint refused to function, and yet the complaint existed, had been phoned in by a client with a voice like dry paper, and the door had been open, and the book had known my name.

In the corner of the hall, beneath the stairs, there was a coat hook with nothing on it except a faint pale rectangle in the dust of the wall behind it—the ghost-shadow of something that used to hang there. A coat, or a bag. I photographed the rectangle. I don't know why. It seemed like the kind of detail that matters later, in the way that all details seem like they might matter later, which is the occupational disease of careful men.

The staircase went up into the dark, and the dark up there had texture. You learn to read darkness in this trade—the flat dark of an empty room versus the grainy dark of a space something small and alive is moving through. The upstairs dark had grain. I stood at the bottom of the stairs with my pen hovering over the logbook and wrote: *Upper floor: acoustic anomaly possible. Recommend inspection.*

*Acoustic anomaly possible.* That's what I wrote instead of *something up there is breathing*, which is what my ears reported, a sound at the very floor of hearing, rhythmic, patient, less like breath than like a house remembering how.

I clicked my recorder on instead. Voice notes are faster than writing, and my handwriting goes to pieces when my hands do. "Site inspection, Hollowmere House, day one, 19:26 hours," I said, and my voice came out level, professional, the voice I use for clients, the voice that belongs to a man who is fine. "Exterior documented. Entry documented. Guest book anomaly documented. Proceeding to initial survey of ground floor, then upper rooms. No client contact at this time. Will attempt phone contact at"—I checked my watch—"19:30."

I dialed the number dispatch had given me. It rang the way empty houses ring: each tone too big for the room, bouncing off plaster, coming back changed. Nine rings. Ten. Then, on the eleventh, the line picked up—not to a voice, but to a sound. A room tone. A hiss of quiet with a shape to it, the acoustic signature of a large interior space, and underneath it, faint and far away, something that might have been a child laughing, or might have been water moving in pipes, or might have been nothing, might have been the network, might have been my own pulse finding its way into the microphone the way it finds its way into everything if you let it.

Then a click, and the dial tone, and I stood in the hall of Hollowmere House holding a phone that had answered itself.

I logged the call. *19:31. Attempted client contact. Line connects; no respondent; ambient audio consistent with interior of property. Duration 0:14.* My hand was steady enough. I was almost proud of it.

And then, because the itch under my sternum had graduated from *expected* to *awaited*, and because the only tool I have ever had against feelings is a checklist, I opened my logbook to a fresh page and drew the little box I draw at the start of every job. *Scope of works.* Underneath, in my smallest, tidiest hand:

*1. Survey ground floor.*
*2. Survey upper floor.*
*3. Identify entry points.*
*4. Identify source of impressions (walls).*
*5. Contact client.*
*6. Treatment plan.*
*7. Depart by sunrise.*

Item seven was not standard. I added it without deciding to, the way you flinch without deciding to, and I stared at it for a long moment—*depart by sunrise*—wondering which part of me had written it and what that part knew that the rest of me didn't. I did not cross it out. Some instructions you leave in place even when you can't hear who gave them.

Outside, the last of the light let go of the single lit window on the second floor, and the house went fully dark around me, all at once, like a held breath released. Somewhere above my head—up the stairs, down the hall, behind a door I hadn't opened—the grainy dark shifted its weight.

I picked up my case, clicked on my flashlight, and started toward the nursery, because the work order had said *nursery, sealed, priority one*, and because I am a man who does things in order, and because the alternative was standing in that hall until the house came down to get me.

The stairs took my weight one creak at a time, counting me upward.
