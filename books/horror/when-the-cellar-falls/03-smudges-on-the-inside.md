## 3. Smudges on the Inside

The nursery was at the end of the east corridor, behind a door that had been sealed with a conviction I usually only saw in people hiding bodies or hoarding cats. Two layers of paint over the frame, then a strip of lathe-and-plaster mudded across the jamb like a scar, then—because whoever did this wanted to be thorough—a row of finishing nails driven in at intervals so regular they looked machine-made. Somebody had not wanted anything getting out of that room. Or in. With houses like this, the distinction tends to collapse.

I stood in front of it for a good minute doing what I always do before a difficult entry: nothing. Just breathing and looking. People think pest control is about courage. It isn't. It's about patience wearing a uniform. The brave ones kick doors down and get bit. The careful ones stand still long enough to notice that the doorframe has been nailed shut from the *outside*, which tells you everything you need to know about who was afraid of whom.

*10:47 p.m.,* I wrote. *East wing, terminal room north side. Door sealed externally: paint, plaster, nails (approx. 14). No keyhole. Hinges interior-facing, painted over.*

My hands were steady when I wrote that. I want that on the record. Whatever happened after, my hands were steady at 10:47.

I photographed the door from three angles with the timestamp function on, because timestamps are the closest thing our profession has to a sacrament. A photograph without a time is just a rumor. Then I got out the pry bar—not to force it, not yet, but to sound it. You tap a wall and listen. Hollow means void. Solid means mass. A wall that answers back means you should have stayed in the van.

This one answered.

Not a knock-back, nothing so theatrical. But when I laid my palm flat against the plaster beside the seal—just resting it there, the way you'd rest a hand on a horse's neck to calm it—the surface was warm. Not sun-warm. It was nearly eleven o'clock on an October night in a house with no heating I'd found yet, and the plaster under my hand held the temperature of skin.

I took my hand away. Wiped it on my trousers. Told myself: old pipes. Old wiring. Houses hold heat the way grudges hold shape. All true statements, all reasonable, all of them the professional equivalent of humming loudly while walking past a graveyard.

*Wall temp anomalous,* I wrote. *Possible thermal event behind plaster. Recommend infrared scan.*

Then I pried the nails.

They came out with a sound like knuckles cracking, one after another, fourteen small confessions. The plaster strip broke along a seam someone had scored in advance—which meant it had been designed to come off again someday, by someone, for some reason. I filed that away under *questions I am not paid to ask* and kept going.

The door wasn't locked behind the seal. It was latched. Latched from the inside.

I want to repeat that, because my logbook entry at 11:02 p.m. understates it and I've had time since to find better words. The nursery door could only be secured from within the nursery. Whoever sealed this room shut did it knowing the latch was on the far side—as if the thing being kept in might also, at some point, be asked to keep itself in. As if the arrangement required cooperation.

I turned the handle. It gave with the softness of a well-oiled mechanism, which was somehow worse than rust. Rust means abandonment. Oil means maintenance. Someone had been oiling this latch recently enough that it swung like a hotel door, and the room beyond exhaled at me: dust and old milk-sweet rot and underneath both, faintly, something like crayons.

*

I clicked on the big flashlight and gave the room the full survey, because procedure is a rope and I was starting to suspect I'd need every knot of it.

Nursery. Obviously, unmistakably a nursery, even stripped bare. The crib was gone but its four indentations remained in the floorboards, ghost-sockets where the feet had pressed for years. A window seat with a cushion worn into the shape of a small body. Wallpaper—faded yellow, patterned with rabbits that had faded into something more skeletal, more suggestion than animal. A rocking chair near the cold fireplace, facing the corner.

Facing the corner. Not the room. Not the window. The corner.

I logged it. *Rocking chair oriented toward NE corner, 45° off room axis. Reason unknown.* And then, because my pen apparently had opinions my brain hadn't authorized: *Reason unknown, and I would like to keep it that way.*

The walls were what I'd come for. Client's note had said smudges, inside walls, sealed rooms. I'd done this dance before—condensation ghosts, thermal tracking, kids' fingerprints preserved in soot like cave paintings. There's almost always a boring answer, and the boring answer pays the same as the interesting one.

I started low, at the baseboard, working left to right the way you read. Nothing. Dust undisturbed, gray and even as felt. I worked up the wall in rows, flashlight raking sideways so any texture would throw a shadow. Row by row. Baseboard to waist. Waist to shoulder. Shoulder to—

There.

At roughly five and a half feet, dead center on the north wall, a smudge. Not a smear of dirt. A *print*. Five fingers, splayed, pressed hard enough into the plaster to leave an actual impression—not pigment on the surface but a deformation *of* it, the way dough remembers a thumb. The plaster hadn't cracked around it. It had yielded. Plaster does not yield. Plaster is not a material with manners.

And it was the size of a child's hand. Small. Four, maybe five years old, if children were the ones making prints in walls, which they were not, because children do not press their hands into solid plaster from the *inside* of a sealed cavity, and also because—

Because the print faced the wrong way.

I stood there a long moment making sure. Fingers point up, thumb to the left—that's a right hand, palm out, pressed from the room's side. Fine. Normal. Except the pressure gradient was inverted. The deepest compression was at the fingertips, tapering back toward the palm, which is what happens when someone pushes *outward* against resistance—from behind the wall, pushing into the room. From the room's side, pressing in, the heel of the palm takes the load. Always. Every print I've ever lifted off glass, off paint, off anything, follows that physics.

This one didn't. This one had been made by a small hand pushing out of the wall, into the nursery, hard enough to dent cured plaster.

*11:19 p.m.* My handwriting had begun to develop a slight forward lean, like a man walking into wind. *North wall, center, approx. 66 inches above floor. Impression consistent with juvenile right hand, approx. age 4–6. Compression vector indicates force applied from within wall cavity. Note: impossible.*

I wrote *impossible* and then sat on the windowsill for a moment, because my knees had submitted a formal request.

Here's the thing about me that clients never see and dispatchers never ask about: I am very good at being unbothered, and I have always understood that this is the same skill as being unnoticed. You learn it young if your life requires it. You make yourself smooth and procedural and slightly dull, and the world's terrible things slide past you looking for someone with a face. I have spent forty-one years being the man nobody remembers at the funeral, the technician whose name the client gets wrong on the survey call (*the nice Asian fellow, very thorough*), and I told myself for decades that this was armor. That invisibility was safety.

Standing in that nursery, I felt the first hairline crack run through that belief, because the wall had made a print of itself being touched, and I understood—with the dumb animal clarity that arrives ahead of thought—that this house noticed things. This house *kept* things. And I had walked in announcing myself with timestamps.

*

I did what I always do when the world misbehaves: I kept counting.

Left of the first print, eighteen inches: another. Same size, same hand, same inverted pressure. Right of it, two feet: a third. They weren't random. Once I stopped seeing individual prints and started seeing the sequence, the pattern assembled itself with horrible tidiness.

They were climbing downward.

Not descending—climbing downward, the way you'd climb down a ladder face-out, each print lower than the last, fingers up, grip after grip, a controlled descent measured in small-child increments. From the first print at sixty-six inches, the trail stepped down the north wall: fifty-eight inches. Fifty-one. Forty-five. Each one placed with the deliberation of something that knew exactly where the next hold was. Something that had been *up inside the wall* and was coming down it the way a child comes down a ladder—carefully, both hands, one rung at a time.

Except a child coming down a ladder leaves two prints per step, alternating hands. These were all the same hand. Right, right, right, right. As if the left was holding something else. As if the left was holding onto whatever was *above*.

I followed the trail around the corner onto the west wall, logging coordinates like a man taking EKG readings of a heart attack. Thirty-nine inches. Thirty-three. Twenty-eight. The plaster here was older, the impressions shallower, and I caught myself thinking in the clinical register—*degraded impression, reduced application force, subject fatiguing*—and then caught the word *subject* sitting in my head wearing a child's shape, and had to put the flashlight down on the windowsill and look out at the dark garden for a while.

The garden was the usual ruined grandeur: hedges gone feral, a stone birdbath holding a coin of black water that reflected nothing, which I noted and declined to think about. Ordinary darkness. I made myself look at it until my pulse agreed to rejoin the project.

Back inside, the trail continued. Twenty-two inches. Sixteen. Ten. At ten inches above the floorboard, the final print—smallest of the set, fingers barely dimpling the plaster, the touch of something arriving rather than escaping—and below it, at floor level, a scuff arc in the dust. The sweep of small feet landing. Standing up. Walking away.

The drag-marks of small feet crossed the dusty boards toward the rocking chair, circled it once, and stopped at the corner where the chair faced.

I did not follow them to the corner. I want to be honest about that in a way my logbook isn't. My logbook says *trail terminates at NE quadrant, floor level.* What actually happened is that I stood at the edge of the dust with my light pointed elsewhere and performed the oldest negotiation in my profession: *if I don't illuminate it, it hasn't been delivered yet.* Cowardice, technically. Also, arguably, restraint. In this line of work the difference is mostly whether you survive to bill for it.

*11:41 p.m.,* I wrote, and my hand was shaking now, fine tremor, the letters coming out like seismograph output. *Full descent documented: nine (9) impressions, N wall and W wall, 66" to 10", single right hand, juvenile scale, inverted force vectors throughout. Subject descended from wall cavity to floor between previous inspection and present. No exit point identified. No entry point identified. Seal on door intact prior to removal by undersigned.*

Then, beneath the official record, smaller, the way you write a thing you don't want even yourself to read easily:

*It came down while I was in the house. It waited until it heard me working.*

*

I packed up slow. Repacked, actually, twice, because the first time I put the pry bar away I couldn't remember whether I'd holstered the flashlight, and the second time I checked the door latch four times. Rituals. My whole trade is rituals with invoices attached. Photograph, log, timestamp, verify. Say the numbers and the numbers will hold the world's shape while you walk out of the room.

At the threshold I stopped. Behind me, the nursery sat in my flashlight's fading spill, rabbits dissolving on the walls, rocking chair keeping its patient appointment with the corner. And on the north wall, at sixty-six inches, the first print—the highest one, the one where the descent began—was no longer alone.

Beside it, fresh, sharp-edged, plaster dust still sifting from the finger-grooves, was a new impression. Larger. An adult hand. Left. Pressed from the inside, pushing out, placed precisely alongside the child's the way you'd place your hand next to a child's on a height chart. *Look how you've grown. Look how far down you've climbed.*

I did not photograph it. I have thought about that a great deal since. Every other anomaly in that house went into the logbook with coordinates and timestamps, catalogued, contained, converted into data I could carry. That one I left unrecorded, and I think—now, with everything I know now—I think some part of me understood that recording it would be answering it. That the print was not evidence. It was correspondence.

I backed out of the nursery, pulled the door shut, and set the latch.

From the inside, the latch turned smoothly, silently, well-oiled, cooperative. And as the tongue slid home into the strike plate, from deep within the wall—within the cavity, within the space between the lathe and the brick, the space I had just spent an hour documenting the contents of—something knocked.

Twice. Small. Knuckle-height, maybe four feet up. Patient. Almost polite.

Like a child knocking on a door, waiting to be let back in.

I wrote the timestamp standing in the corridor with my back already turned: *11:58 p.m. Acoustic contact, N wall, nursery interior. Two impacts, manual origin presumed. Duration of silence following: ongoing.*

Then I flipped to the work order page and added a line I'd been avoiding all night, the line that had been itching at me since the guest book downstairs, since the door that opened before I knocked:

*Query for client file: how did Hollowmere House know to request me by name?*

Down the hall, in the dark behind me, the knocking started again—but this time it wasn't coming from the nursery.

It was coming from the mirror at the corridor's end, where my reflection stood a half-second late, hand raised to knock on glass from the wrong side.
