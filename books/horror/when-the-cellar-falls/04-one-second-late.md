## 4. One Second Late

The upstairs hallway of Hollowmere House was the kind of hallway that made you feel like a guest at your own funeral. Long, dim, carpeted in a red so old it had gone the color of dried things, lined with doors that all looked like they had opinions about you. Seven doors on the left, six on the right, and at the far end a window with curtains drawn against a dusk that hadn't quite committed to being night yet.

I did what I always do. I started at one end and worked toward the other, logbook open, pen uncapped, photographing everything before I touched it. Door frames. Baseboards. The dust lines where furniture used to sit. A professional documents first and worries later, and I have built an entire life on the principle that worry deferred is worry denied.

The hallway smelled like cedar and something underneath it — not rot exactly. More like the memory of rot. The ghost of an infestation past, or the promise of one future. I wrote that down, then crossed it out, because *the promise of one future* is the kind of sentence that gets a man referred to a colleague who specializes in talking.

*4:52 p.m.,* I noted. *Second floor, east corridor. Ambient temp 61°F. No visible droppings, gnaw marks, or entry points. Client absent. Client has been absent for approximately forty years, per county records.*

That last part wasn't strictly procedure. But the work order had listed no contact name, just HOLLOWMERE HOUSE, and when I'd called the number provided, it had rung in an empty room somewhere — I could hear the echo of it, the particular hollowness of a phone ringing in a house with nobody home — until it stopped mid-ring, like someone on the other end had picked up the receiver and then thought better of it.

I'd told myself it was a routing error. Old switchboard, dead line, some telecom ghost. Houses have ghosts in their wiring all the time. It's the people inside them you have to worry about.

Except there were no people inside this one.

There was me.

And there was, as I was about to discover, something else wearing my timing.

* * *

It was the third mirror that betrayed it.

The first two had been ordinary — dusty cheval glass in the corner by the stairwell, a small oval thing above a table that held a vase holding nothing. In both, I looked exactly as I expected to look: a thin man of forty-one in coveralls the color of surrender, headlamp off because the hallway light still functioned, gray going early at the temples, face with the specific blankness of a man who has spent decades ensuring no security camera ever gets a good shot of him. I don't avoid mirrors out of vanity or its opposite. I check them the way I check corners. Situational awareness. You never know what's behind you.

The third mirror was a tall Victorian thing at the hallway's midpoint, dark wood frame carved with grapes or berries or possibly tumors, the glass itself spotted with age like the skin of an old man's hand. I passed it, glanced into it out of habit —

— and my reflection was still looking at the wall behind me.

Not turned away. Not gone. Just *behind*. Half a beat, maybe less. By the time my brain registered it and I stopped walking, the reflection caught up, snapped into place like a film finding its sprockets, and there I was, looking at myself looking at myself, everything apparently normal.

I stood very still.

Here is a thing about me: I do not spook easily, and when I do spook, I spook *quietly*, the way a cat does, all outward stillness and internal catastrophe. My heart rate went from sixty-something to a number I didn't care to log. My hands, however, stayed perfectly steady on the logbook, because hands are trainable even when the rest of you is screaming.

*Possible causes,* I wrote. *1. Trick of light. 2. Fatigue. 3. Faulty perception due to low ambient light and high-contrast glass. 4. [leave blank].*

I left number four blank. Number four was the cause I didn't want to write down, because once you write a thing in ink it becomes real in a way that the world can't take back. Ink is a commitment. I've always respected that about it.

Then I did what any reasonable pest controller would do, which is run a controlled test.

* * *

I took out my pen — the good one, the steel-bodied one I've carried eleven years — and I held it up beside my head, level with the mirror, my arm extended.

In the glass: a man holding up a pen.

I dropped it.

The pen fell. The sound of it hitting the hardwood arrived a fraction of a second after the sight of it hitting the glass — no wait. Let me get this right, because getting it right matters, because if I can't trust the order of events then I can't trust anything, and if I can't trust anything then I am just a man standing in a dark hallway holding a logbook full of fiction.

The pen fell. In the mirror, the reflected pen fell. And the reflected pen hit the reflected floor *after* the real pen hit the real floor. After. Not before, not simultaneously. After. The way a dubbed film lags the actor's mouth. The way thunder follows lightning, except here the lightning was me.

I picked the pen up. My knees made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, which was undignified but at least confirmed my body was still operating on standard physics.

*Test one,* I wrote. *Object drop. Observed delay between physical event and mirrored event: approximately 0.5–1.0 seconds. Repeat trials recommended.*

I ran it four more times. Drop, watch, listen, record. The delay varied — half a second, then nearly a full second, then back down — as though whatever was translating me into glass was doing so under load, or was distracted, or was *getting better at it.*

Because that was the other thing. On trial three, the delay shortened. On trial five, it shortened again. Whatever lag existed was closing. Like a signal acquiring lock. Like something calibrating.

I sat down on the hallway floor — procedure be damned, my legs had filed a formal complaint — and I wrote:

*Working hypothesis: the reflective surface is displaying recorded imagery rather than live imagery. Delay consistent with processing or playback. Delay decreasing over successive trials. Conclusion pending further data. Note: conclusion may be "run."*

I stared at that last word for a long moment. Then, because I am who I am, I didn't cross it out. Some notes deserve to stand.

* * *

Here's the thing I couldn't stop thinking about, sitting on the floor of a dead woman's hallway while her house learned my rhythm: I have spent my whole life being unremarkable on purpose.

This isn't false modesty. It's engineering. My mother disappeared when I was six, and after that I was passed through the foster system like a parcel nobody wanted to sign for, and somewhere in those years I learned the single most valuable survival skill available to a child nobody wants: become forgettable. Be polite but not memorable. Be clean but not striking. Answer questions correctly and offer nothing else. When teachers took attendance, give them a face they'd have to look down at their list to recall. When the bad thing came through the house — and in some houses it comes through regularly, on schedule, like a meter reader — be furniture. Furniture doesn't get hurt. Furniture doesn't get remembered, either, but that was the price and I paid it gladly for thirty-five years.

I became a pest controller because it was the perfect profession for a man like me. Nobody invites the exterminator to dinner. Nobody asks the exterminator his life story. You arrive, you do the work, you leave, and within a week the client remembers the absence of roaches and not the presence of you. I am a ghost who charges by the hour. I have eaten hundreds of meals alone in vans outside strangers' houses, and every one of those meals tasted like safety.

And now here was the joke, the punchline delivered with the timing of something that had been rehearsing: invisibility had never protected me at all. Because something in this house had known my name before I walked in. Had written it in a guest book in handwriting I'll describe later, when I have better words for it. Was watching me right now — not watching the hallway, not watching the room, watching *me*, through a second of borrowed time, learning me the way I learn an infestation. Entry points. Traffic patterns. Where I sleep.

You spend your whole life making yourself small enough to slip through the world's attention, and it turns out the world was never the thing you needed to hide from. The world barely looks at anyone. It's the *houses* you have to worry about. The houses see everything. The houses are patient, and the houses remember, and I was standing inside one that had been remembering longer than I'd been alive.

I laughed. It came out wrong — too loud in the empty corridor, bouncing off walls that seemed to lean in to catch it. Somewhere below me, or above me, or inside the walls themselves, I would have sworn something laughed back. One second late.

* * *

I got up. I tested the other mirrors.

The oval one above the empty vase: normal. Or normal-ish. There was a delay there too, once I knew to look for it, but it was smaller, a stutter rather than a gap, like a video buffering on bad wifi. The cheval glass by the stairs: delayed by maybe a quarter second, and — this detail I logged twice, underlined — the reflection was slightly *darker* than the hallway it depicted. Not much. The difference between a room lit by a bulb and a room lit by the memory of a bulb.

Every mirror in the house was a window into somewhere almost identical to here, running a beat behind, running a little dim, like the house itself was a recording of a house, and I was living slightly ahead of my own image.

Or — and I want to be honest, because honesty is the only procedure that matters tonight — like the images weren't recordings at all. Like they were *rehearsals*. Like something on the other side of the glass was practicing being me, running the choreography a half-beat late, getting the steps wrong in ways I could measure, and closing the gap trial by trial. Drop the pen. Watch how he drops the pen. Again. Again. *Again.*

By the time I finished the survey, the delay at the Victorian mirror was down to a quarter second. I logged it.

*6:11 p.m. Mirror survey complete. All reflective surfaces exhibit temporal lag. Lag decreasing uniformly across all surfaces regardless of distance between them, suggesting centralized source rather than individual malfunction. Source unknown. Source location unknown. Note: "centralized" implies a nervous system. Houses do not have nervous systems. Repeating this note does not make it more true, but writing it feels load-bearing.*

My hands were shaking now. I let them. There's no shame in it; shame is for men who have someone to hide from, and I had carefully arranged my life to contain no such audience. I shook, and I documented, and the documentation steadied me the way it always has — the rosary of it, timestamp, measurement, note, timestamp, measurement, note. As long as I was logging, I was working. As long as I was working, I was a professional. As long as I was a professional, I was not a six-year-old boy waiting for a mother who had written her name on a wall of a house I'd never seen and then walked out of the world.

I checked the Victorian mirror one last time before moving on to the bedrooms.

My reflection checked me back.

Not a trick of the light. Not fatigue. It looked at me the way I look at a wall with something inside it — patiently, professionally, with the calm certainty of a thing that knows the smudge will tell it everything eventually. For one full second — a full second, the longest second of my life, a second with rooms in it — my reflection did not copy me. I raised my hand. It didn't raise its. I stepped left. It stayed.

And then it moved. Not to match me. Ahead of me, or beside me, or independent of me entirely — it lifted its own hand, slow, deliberate, and pressed its palm flat against the inside of the glass.

From the other side.

The gesture was familiar in a way that made my stomach drop through the floorboards and keep dropping, past the cellar I hadn't found yet, past the foundations, into some older dark. Because I knew that gesture. Every pest controller knows that gesture. It's what you see when you pull a sheet of drywall off a stud and find the smudges on the inside — the prints of the thing that lived in the cavity, pressing outward, testing the boundary of its world, asking the only question anything trapped ever asks:

*Is anyone out there? Is anyone coming?*

My reflection stood with its hand against the glass, and its mouth began to move. No sound came through. The words were slow, shaped, deliberate — not frantic, not hungry. Almost gentle. I leaned close, close enough to fog the antique glass with my breath, and read its lips the way I'd read a thousand work orders, and what it said, syllable by silent syllable, was:

*You're — one — second — late.*

Behind me, at the far end of the hall, a door I had already inspected and photographed and logged as closed swung open onto darkness, and from somewhere inside that darkness — the nursery, I realized, the sealed nursery from the work order — came the small, warm smell of plaster dust, and the sound, faint and unmistakable, of tiny palms pressing flat against the inside of a wall.
