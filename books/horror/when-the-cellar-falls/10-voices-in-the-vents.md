## 10. Voices in the Vents

The recorder cost me two hundred and eleven dollars in 2019, and I have never once regretted it. It is a Sony, it has a windscreen shaped like a tiny gray mushroom, and it has never lied to me. In my line of work, that puts it in rare company.

I set it on the hallway floor at 11:47 p.m., directly beneath the vent grate outside what used to be the nursery, and I pressed record because that is what I do. You find a sound, you document the sound. You don't have to understand the sound. Understanding is for people with therapists and hobbies.

The sound had started as scratching.

Scratching, I want to be clear, is normal. Scratching is ninety percent of my professional life. Mice scratch. Rats scratch. Squirrels scratch with the entitled confidence of creatures who have never once been asked to pay rent. If you put your ear to enough walls, you develop a taxonomy of scratching: the dry skitter of roaches, the heavy drag of something with weight to it, the rhythmic gnaw of teeth that are, frankly, showing off.

This was none of those.

This was *movement* — deliberate, distributed, like footsteps in a room above a room that doesn't exist. And underneath it, faintly, something I refused to name for the first ten minutes I heard it, because naming it would mean admitting what it sounded like.

It sounded like dishes.

*

Let me back up, because procedure matters, and procedure is the only prayer I know.

At 11:31 p.m., I had been standing in the upstairs corridor doing what I always do when a house stops making sense: measuring it. My logbook now contained a floor plan of Hollowmere's second story that contradicted itself in three places. The corridor was forty-one feet long when I entered it and thirty-eight feet long when I tried to leave it. There was a door — plain pine, brass knob, entirely unremarkable — that had not been there at 10:15, and which opened onto a linen closet that smelled of lavender and, beneath the lavender, faintly of pond water. I documented all of it. Timestamps, measurements, sketches. My handwriting stayed level even when my hands didn't. That's the trick nobody tells you about fear: if you keep writing, you get to pretend you're the one holding the pen.

The house had been quiet since the mirrors. Quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Then, at 11:44, the scratching began above the ceiling — except there was no above the ceiling. I'd checked. The attic access in the hall closet opened onto four feet of blown insulation and a roof deck, no crawlspace, no joists wide enough for anything larger than a mouse. Whatever was moving up there was moving through architecture that existed only for its convenience, and I found this less frightening than I probably should have, because by then I'd already accepted the working theory I hadn't written down anywhere:

Hollowmere House was not infested.

Hollowmere House was the infestation.

So when the sound migrated — down through the ceiling, along the wall cavity, into the ductwork above the corridor vent — I did the only reasonable thing. I got out my recorder, knelt on the dusty boards like a man about to propose to them, and hit the red button.

11:47:03 p.m. Recording commenced. Ambient temperature 61 degrees. Subject sound: unidentified movement within HVAC ducting, origin unknown, material composition unknown. Note: ductwork in this section of the house appears original to 1920s construction; galvanized steel; should not carry sound with this degree of clarity.

That last note went into the logbook before I understood why I was writing it. The clarity. That was the thing. Ducts muffle. Ducts echo and rattle and turn every footstep into thunder or nothing at all. This duct carried sound the way a good parlor carries conversation — warm, close, intimate, like the walls wanted me to hear.

Like the walls had been waiting for someone to listen.

*

The first thing I heard clearly was a chair scraping.

Not a ghostly, reverberant scrape. A real one — wooden legs on hardwood, that specific short bark of friction, followed by the small settling creak of someone sitting down and getting comfortable. Then a second chair. Then a third, lighter one, dragged with the particular graceless enthusiasm of a child who has been told to come to dinner and intends to arrive at maximum speed.

I sat back on my heels.

"Okay," I said quietly, to the vent, to the recorder, to whatever compliance officer might someday review this footage. "Okay. Documenting."

A woman's voice came through the grate, and I am going to describe it accurately even though accuracy makes it worse: it was mid-conversation. Not beginning, not ending — *middle*. She was talking the way people talk when they've talked a thousand times before, the rhythm worn smooth, words half-swallowed with affectionate impatience.

"—and if you put your elbows on the table one more time, Thomas Halloway, I will take them home with me and you can eat through a straw."

Laughter. A man's laugh, low and easy, and the boy's laugh over it, bright and gap-toothed — you can hear a gap in a child's laugh, some acoustic signature of missing enamel, and this one had it, a little whistle on the s-sounds.

I did not move. I am not sure I breathed. Somewhere in my chest, under the sternum, in the place where dread usually sets up shop, there was instead something warmer and infinitely more terrible, and it took me a moment to identify it because I had so little practice.

It was longing.

Because here is the thing about eavesdropping on the dead: they aren't performing. There was no message in it, no warning, no cold hand reaching through the speaker to say *flee, fool, flee.* It was just dinner. Tuesday dinner, or Wednesday, some unremarkable evening decades deep in the ground, preserved in the ductwork of a house that had eaten everyone who ever loved it and kept their evenings like pressed flowers. The woman said something else — I caught the word "gravy" and the word "*finally*" — and the boy groaned theatrically, and the man said, "Ellie, let him have the drumstick, he fought the dog for it fair and square," and a dog barked twice, delighted, and I pressed my forehead against the cool metal of the vent grate and felt like the worst kind of trespasser.

Not the kind who breaks in. The kind who arrives too late.

*

I want to explain the guilt properly, because it wasn't simple, and I've had time since to take it apart.

It would have been easier if it were horror. Horror keeps you at arm's length; horror says *this is not your business, run.* But this wasn't horror yet. This was intimacy. This was a family being a family in the small, unguarded register that families only use when they're sure no one is listening — the teasing, the in-jokes, the mother's voice dropping into that specific tender exasperation that exists nowhere else in the world. And I was listening. I was crouched in their empty house with a two-hundred-and-eleven-dollar microphone, recording their last ordinary evening like evidence.

Except it wasn't their last ordinary evening. Not yet. I knew what came after — I'd read the father's unfinished letter, I'd seen the study, I knew how the Halloway file ended — and they didn't. They were passing the gravy. They had no idea.

And some ugly, honest part of me noticed that the guilt had a twin sitting right beside it, and the twin was relief. Because for eleven years I had built a life out of not being listened to — out of being the man whose name slides off a dispatcher's tongue without leaving a mark, the man clients forget by the time the check clears — and here, finally, was proof of what I'd always half-known: that being unheard doesn't protect you from anything. These people had been heard. They'd been heard by each other, thoroughly, daily, with love. And the house had taken them anyway.

Invisibility wasn't armor. It had never been armor. It was just a smaller room to die in.

I wrote that down, actually. Word for word. My handwriting wobbled on "armor," but it's legible.

*

11:58 p.m. The dinner continued.

I catalogued it, because cataloguing is how I love things — I've known this about myself for years and have decided not to examine it too closely. The clink of a serving spoon against a bowl. The dog's nails on hardwood, trotting a circuit. The boy — Thomas, presumably, elbow offender, drumstick victor — narrating something at length with his mouth full, and the father saying "swallow first, buddy," in a voice so patient it made my teeth ache. The mother, Ellie, laughing at something her husband murmured too low for the duct to carry, and then saying, "Don't say that in front of him, he'll repeat it at school."

A pause. Forks. The small percussion of a family eating.

And then Ellie said — clear, close, as if she'd turned toward the vent itself, as if she could see me kneeling there in the dark with my bleeding heart and my little gray-mushroomed microphone —

"—August, pass your plate."

I did not move.

I want that on the record: I did not move. Every hair on my body stood up and filed a complaint, and my hands went cold around the recorder, and somewhere in the rational department of my brain a clerk calmly noted that *August* is not an uncommon construction of syllables, that the acoustics of old ductwork distort, that there are a hundred explanations and most of them are better than the truth.

But my hands knew my name. My hands have written it ten thousand times in service tickets and sign-off lines, and they flinched at it like a struck bell.

The moment passed. The dinner rolled on. Either she'd said a name I misheard, or she'd said mine, or the house had reached into its inventory of sounds and selected the one calibrated to keep me exactly where I was, kneeling, listening, unable to leave. All three possibilities were unbearable. I recorded all three in the margin of my logbook and drew a box around them, the way I do with unresolved findings.

12:04 a.m. The plates cleared. The boy was sent to wash up — I heard the bathroom pipes knock twice, a sound so mundane it nearly undid me. The parents talked in the low register of people who have run out of things to hide from each other. I caught fragments. Mortgage. The cellar door sticking again. *Tom, we should really get someone out to look at it.*

"We'll call someone," the father said, and laughed, tired and fond. "We always say we'll call someone."

I looked at my hands. Pest controller, twenty-two years licensed. I could have fixed their cellar door. I could have fixed it in an afternoon, charged them fairly, left a card, been forgotten by Friday. That was the whole shape of my grief, right there: I had spent my entire career arriving at houses just after they stopped needing me, and now I had arrived at this one decades too late, and the only work left was the work of witnessing, and nobody had ever trained me for that.

*

12:19 a.m.

The laughter stopped.

I need to be precise about this, because precision is all I have to offer these people. It did not fade. It did not trail off into the natural diminuendo of an evening winding down. It *stopped* — mid-note, the boy's bright whistle-cut laugh severed clean, like a wire snipped with pliers. One instant: the dog barking, chairs shifting, life. The next instant: nothing.

And then, through the duct, from somewhere far down in the house — below the kitchen, below the foundation, from the direction of the cellar I had not yet found and already dreaded — a scream.

One scream. Long. It did not rise and fall the way screams do; it held its pitch like a bowed string, sustained past the point where lungs should have failed it, sustained past the point where I understood what I was hearing, sustained until my own throat closed in sympathetic refusal. It was a woman's voice. I believe it was Ellie. I believe it to this day, and I will believe it until I die, and I would give a great deal to be wrong.

Then silence. Not quiet — silence, the total acoustic absence of a house swallowing a sound whole, keeping it, filing it away in the sediment of itself with everything else it had ever been fed.

My hands were shaking. The recorder had bitten into my palms where I gripped it — I hadn't noticed until I saw the blood on the casing, two crescents from my thumbs, printed neatly on the Sony logo like a signature. Like fingerprints. I almost laughed. Almost.

12:23 a.m. Recording terminated. Duration: 36 minutes, 41 seconds. I wrote the entry with blood on the pen grip and did not clean it off. Some part of me — the part that has kept a logbook like a rosary for twenty-two years — understood that the blood belonged in the record. Evidence of the witness. Proof that someone had been here, listening, too late, and had bled a little for it.

*

I sat on the corridor floor for a long time after that. The house said nothing more. The ducts ticked as they cooled, ordinary metal doing ordinary contraction, and somehow that was the loneliest sound of the night.

Here is what I know now that I didn't know at midnight: the house had shown me that dinner on purpose. It herds. Chapter eight of my own logbook proves it — the doors that rearrange, the corridors that shorten, the architecture bending itself around some destination I'm being walked toward like livestock. And tonight it had opened the vents and played me the Halloways' last evening like a man luring a stray with food.

But knowing a trap is a trap doesn't tell you what to do when the bait is a family saying each other's names across a table.

Because that's the thing I couldn't stop thinking about, kneeling there in the dark with my bloody hands. They'd said each other's names all night. *Thomas. Ellie. Tom, buddy, sweetheart.* Names passed back and forth like dishes, like bread, like small warm objects you hand to the people you love. And the house had taken them anyway — had swallowed the whole table, gravy and laughter and gap-toothed whistling, and kept them, unnamed and unreleased, in its walls.

Keeps what is unnamed. Releases what is spoken.

I didn't know yet where I'd learned that sentence. I wouldn't learn until later, in the ledger, in her handwriting. But I felt it then, rising in me like water finding its level, and I looked down the long dark corridor toward the stairs, and past the stairs, toward the cold breath seeping up from the basement door I had been carefully, professionally not thinking about all night.

Thirty-six minutes and forty-one seconds of a dead family's dinner sat on my memory card. It was the closest thing to a grave marker they had. Someone should hear it. Someone should say their names — Thomas, Ellie, the father whose first name I still didn't know, the dog whose name I'd bet anything was something stupid and perfect like Biscuit.

Someone should go down to wherever the house kept them, and read the sediment.

I stood up. My knees cracked. The corridor stretched out ahead of me, forty-one feet or thirty-eight or however long it felt like being tonight, and at the end of it, the basement door stood very slightly open — I want to stress that I checked it earlier, and it had been locked, and I had documented the lock — exhaling cold air in slow, patient rhythm.

Like something breathing. Like something that had waited decades for a reader, and had finally, finally heard footsteps.

I picked up my recorder, logged the time — 1:02 a.m. — and went downstairs to meet the cellar.
