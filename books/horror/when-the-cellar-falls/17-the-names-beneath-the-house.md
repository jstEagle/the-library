## 17. The Names Beneath the House

The cellar stairs had gained three steps since I'd last descended them. I counted twice, because counting is what I do when the world starts lying to me. Fourteen going down before midnight. Seventeen now. The house was growing the way mold grows in a damp wall—slowly, patiently, and always toward the food.

I stood at the top with my logbook open to the page where I'd copied out every name from beneath the wallpaper, every name from my mother's ledger, cross-referenced against the scratched columns on the cellar walls themselves. Forty-one names. Some were barely legible, gouged into plaster by fingers that must have been bleeding by the third letter. Others were written with a strange, careful elegance, as if someone had all the time in the world and wanted the house to know it.

At the bottom of the stairs, the cellar waited. Every surface fingerprinted, layered like sedimentary rock—a geology of the forgotten. The child's chair sat facing its blank wall, patient as furniture, which is to say patient as a trap.

"Okay," I said, to no one, which is how I say most things. "Procedure."

My voice came back to me wrong. Not an echo—an *edit*. The cellar took the word *procedure* and gave it back half a beat late, softened, like it was tasting it.

I didn't let that stop me. Stopping is how houses like this win. You stop, you listen, you start wondering whether the walls are breathing, and then you're halfway to being wallpaper yourself.

I clicked my recorder on. Timestamped the entry. 3:47 a.m., cellar, commencement of name recitation, subject: forty-one individuals, purpose: eviction notice.

Then I read the first name aloud.

"Halloway, Edmund. Father of the household. Carpenter by trade."

Nothing happened.

I'd be lying if I said I wasn't disappointed. I'd built up to this. I'd rehearsed in the hallway like a man about to give a eulogy, which, in fairness, I was. And the cellar just sat there, fingerprints glistening faintly in the light of my flashlight, smug as an unpaid invoice.

So I kept going.

"Halloway, Margaret. Mother of the household. Keeper of recipe cards, forty-seven of them, filed by season."

The temperature dropped. Not dramatically—the way a room cools when a door opens somewhere far away. Somewhere above us, a door I hadn't opened swung wide, and cold air poured down the stairwell like water finding its level.

And then—

*Flour on her hands. Always flour, even in the evening, even after dinner, because she'd started tomorrow's bread tonight because tomorrow was never certain in this house.* The memory arrived whole, uninvited, warm as a hand on the back of my neck. It wasn't mine. I had never baked bread in my life; I once set off a smoke alarm making toast. But for three full seconds I knew the weight of Margaret Halloway's hands, knew that she hummed off-key when she kneaded, knew that she was afraid of the cellar and went down into it anyway, every week, because the family needed remembering and someone had to do the work.

I gripped the edge of the child's chair. My knuckles went white. When the memory released me, I was breathing hard, and there were tears on my face that I had no memory of authorizing.

"Noted," I said, voice cracking. "Margaret Halloway. Memory returned. Continue."

Because here's what I understood, standing in the dark with a dead woman's grief still dissolving off my skin: each name was a key, and each key opened something the house had locked away. The house fed on memory—that was the infestation, that was the whole rotten colony. And every name I spoke pulled a meal back out of its mouth.

It was going to hate me for this.

Good. The feeling was mutual.

* * *

"Halloway, Thomas. Son. Age nine at time of disappearance. Dental records on file, two fillings, lower left."

The wall beside me *shuddered*.

Not settled. Not creaked. Shuddered—the way a dog shudders when it shakes off rain. Plaster dust sifted down from the ceiling in a fine gray veil, and somewhere in the dark behind the walls, something long and jointed turned over in its sleep and did not wake, but dreamed harder.

The memory hit me mid-sentence. *Thomas, teaching himself to whistle in the stairwell, failing, trying again, the sound coming out flat as a dying battery—and his father laughing from the study, not mean laughter, the good kind, the kind that says come here, son, let me show you the trick of it.* I felt the boy's frustration. I felt the father's pride. I felt, underneath both, the thin cold current running through the whole family, the one they never named: the sense of being *watched*, of being *kept*, of living inside something that loved them the way a jar loves preserves.

I wiped my face with my sleeve and read the next name.

"Halloway, Abigail. Daughter. Age six."

The chair moved.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last thing I own outright. The chair did not slide. It did not tip. It rotated—slowly, on legs that made no sound against stone—until it faced me. Empty. Waiting. An invitation written in pine and dust.

"No," I told it. "Thank you. I stand while I work."

The house groaned around me, a deep structural complaint, like a ship taking on water. Above my head, the floorboards began to move—not footsteps, the boards themselves, flexing, rippling, the way a carpet looks when something enormous passes beneath it. The staircase I'd descended groaned and stretched, and I heard, distinctly, the sound of new steps being added. The house was building itself a longer throat.

It wanted me to run. Running meant leaving the names unread. Running meant staying swallowed.

I turned back to the wall and kept reading.

* * *

By the twelfth name, I had learned the rhythm of it.

Name. Pause. Memory. Convulsion. Dust. Next name.

The memories came faster now, less politely, arriving not one at a time but braided together, so that speaking a single name delivered a whole life in a single swallow. Edmund Halloway's hands on a saw, and Edmund Halloway's hands shaking as he wrote a letter he never finished, and Edmund Halloway's hands pressed flat against this very wall, adding his own print to the sediment, whispering *we remember you, we remember you* like a man feeding a stray cat, hoping to keep it tame.

That was the trick of the place. That was the whole architecture of it. The Halloways had remembered the house to keep it gentle, and the house had learned to need remembering the way an addict learns to need, and when the family finally ran out—when the last of them stopped speaking the names, or left, or died, or was taken—the house had begun to starve. And starving things don't stay gentle.

"Whitlock, Samuel. Groundskeeper, 1911 to 1946."

A window of memory opened onto a man mowing a lawn that no longer existed, whistling—*there's the whistling, Thomas, it runs in the groundkeepers too*—and I staggered, caught myself on the wall, and my palm landed flat against decades of fingerprints.

The wall was warm.

Warm as skin. Warm as a fever. And under my hand, faint as a pulse through a wrist, I felt it beating.

"Don't," I said to it. "Don't you dare make this intimate."

But it was already intimate. That was the horror I'd been circling since the guest book with my name pre-written: this house didn't want to eat me. Eating is simple. This house wanted to *keep* me. It wanted me the way Margaret wanted bread on the table, the way Edmund wanted the letter finished, the way a lonely child wants a guest who cannot leave. All those fingerprints weren't marks of captivity. They were signatures on a lease. Everyone who touched these walls had agreed, in the only language the house could read, to belong.

I peeled my hand off the plaster. It came away reluctantly, with a sound like tape lifting from paper.

"Whitlock, Samuel," I said again, louder, because resistance is mostly repetition. "You are named. You are witnessed. You are free to go."

The cellar screamed.

There's no better word for it. The walls contracted, the ceiling buckled downward a foot and held there, trembling, and from every vent and seam and hairline crack came a sound like a thousand voices inhaling at once. Plaster rained down. My flashlight flickered, steadied, flickered. In the beam's strobe, I saw the fingerprints on the far wall *move*—the layers shifting, rearranging, pressing closer together like a crowd trying to hide one of their number.

The house was trying to shield some names from me. Trying to tuck them behind the others.

I found them anyway. I'm a pest controller. Finding what things hide is the entire job.

Behind a shelf of collapsed masonry, low near the floor, a column of names in handwriting I recognized with a violence that stopped my heart. Small, careful, slanting left. The same hand that had filled half a ledger in the study. The same hand that had signed a ritual and left it unfinished.

*M. Ishikawa.*

My mother had been down here. Had knelt on this stone, in this dark, and cut her name into the wall—or been made to, or chosen to, and I would learn which eventually, and dread learning it either way. Her name sat uncrossed among the crossed-out, waiting, the way a seat waits at a table for someone who said they'd come.

"You left me," I said to the wall. My voice came out steady, which surprised me, because nothing else about me was. "Twenty-six years ago. A suitcase, a bus ticket, a neighbor who 'offered to help.' You left me with strangers and a house that smelled like other people's cooking. And then you came *here*."

The wall said nothing. Walls are excellent listeners and terrible correspondents.

"But you wrote your name," I said. "You wrote it and you didn't cross it out. Which means part of you wanted to be found. Or part of you knew I'd come. Or—" and here my voice did finally break, quietly, like a branch under snow, "—or you needed your son to finish your sentence. Again. Like always. Cleaning up after you, Mom. Even now. Even here."

I put my flashlight between my teeth, found her name in my logbook, and read it aloud.

"Ishikawa, Mariko. Mother of August. Keeper of unfinished things."

The memory that came was mine.

Not borrowed. Not inherited. *Mine*—a thing the house had stolen from me without my ever knowing it was gone. I was five years old. There was a kitchen that smelled like ginger and dish soap. There was a woman kneeling to my height, and she had my chin, my stubborn cowlick, my habit of counting things when frightened—she was counting my buttons, right to left, four of them, murmuring numbers like a prayer. And she said, *August, if you ever can't find me, say my name. Names call people home. Say it every day until I come.*

I had said it every day. For a year. Two years. Until the saying wore smooth as a river stone and then wore away entirely, until I woke up one morning at eight years old unable to remember why the word *Mariko* made my chest hurt, and then, later, unable to remember her face at all—I'd assumed grief had blurred it, grief does blur things—but it wasn't grief. It was theft. This house had reached across twenty-six years and miles and taken her out of me, bite by bite, and I had called the emptiness healing.

I sank down against the wall and cried the way men cry alone in cellars: badly, efficiently, hating every second of it.

Then I got up, because the list wasn't finished, and procedure doesn't pause for weeping. Procedure is how you survive the weeping.

* * *

The house fought back properly after that.

The thirtieth name brought the temperature down to the point where my breath showed. The thirty-second brought the walls alive with movement, shapes pressing outward from within the plaster, faces surfacing and submerging like swimmers under ice. The thirty-fourth killed my flashlight, and I finished the column by candlelight from an emergency stub in my kit, reading names in a small trembling circle of flame while the darkness leaned in around me, listening, starving, furious.

Each name cost the house something. I could feel it weakening—the convulsions shorter, the groans thinner, the lag in my own echo stretching and fraying like old rope. The fingerprints nearest the candle had begun to fade. Not smudge. *Fade*, the way ink fades, the way memory fades, lifting off the plaster like breath off glass.

And each name gave something back. By the thirty-fifth, I carried a groundskeeper's knowledge of every tree that had ever shaded Hollowmere. By the thirty-eighth, I could have cooked Margaret Halloway's entire autumn menu from muscle memory alone. By the fortieth, I knew the Halloway daughter's laugh, her fear of thunder, the exact weight of her asleep against her father's shoulder. They lived in me now, all of them, crowded and warm and grieving. I was no longer a man alone in a cellar. I was a procession.

"Last one," I said, and my voice echoed back in real time for the first time all night. The house was too weak to edit me anymore. "Halloway, Abigail. Daughter. Age six. You danced in this room. Someone taught you to waltz. You counted the steps—one-two-three, one-two-three—and you laughed when you stumbled, and you were not afraid of the dark, only of being forgotten in it."

I closed the logbook.

"You are not forgotten," I said. "Any of you. I have you all in writing."

The cellar exhaled. The whole house exhaled—a long, shuddering sigh that moved through the walls like wind through wheat, and the fingerprints lifted from the plaster in slow spirals of pale dust, rising, swirling, drifting upward through the buckled ceiling like ash from a fire burning in reverse. Decades of held breath, released. The pressure in the room dropped so fast my ears popped.

And then the candle guttered, and in the dark, I heard it.

Scratching. Faint, deliberate, from the wall beside the child's chair. Letter by letter, the sound of fingernails—or something wearing fingernails—carving into fresh plaster.

I relit the candle and held it up.

New scratches, bright and raw against the old stone. Fresh writing, in a hand I almost recognized—slanted, careful, urgent:

**ONE NAME REMAINS.**

Below it, a single empty space. A blank line, waiting.

And below that, smaller, in handwriting I knew better than my own:

**Fill it in, August. The house has been so hungry, and it has offered you such a lovely chair.**

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no wind, pointing—like a finger—toward the chair, which had turned again, patiently, to face me.

Waiting for its final guest.
