## 22. The Man Who Remembers

The call came in on a Tuesday, which August Ishikawa had long ago decided was the universe's preferred day for other people's hauntings. Tuesdays were the day furnaces failed and raccoons moved into soffits and, apparently, dead houses remembered they were hungry. He let the phone ring twice more than necessary—not out of reluctance, but because he had learned that a man who answers too quickly sounds like a man with nothing else to do, and August had spent forty-one years cultivating the opposite impression.

He answered anyway. He always answered. That was the whole trouble with him.

"Meridian Pest Solutions, this is August."

"Hi, yes—" A woman's voice, tight with the particular exhaustion of someone who has stopped sleeping in her own bedroom. "I don't know if you're the right kind of company for this. It's going to sound insane."

"Ma'am, I once spent six hours negotiating with a squirrel over custody of an attic. Try me."

A wet, grateful laugh. Then the story, delivered in the halting cadence he'd come to think of as the confession rhythm: smudges on the inside of sealed walls. Fingerprints, she said, small ones, appearing overnight on plaster that hadn't been opened since the Eisenhower administration. Her husband thought it was condensation. Her daughter had started sleeping with the lights on and wouldn't say why.

August sat very still at his kitchen table, the receiver warm against his ear, and felt the old familiar cold walk up the back of his neck and settle there like an old friend taking its usual chair.

"Inside of the walls," he repeated. "You're certain they're not on the surface?"

"I wiped them off," she said. "They came back by morning. And Mr.—I'm sorry, what was your name again?"

And here was the thing. Here was the hinge of the whole morning, the small brass fitting on which everything else would turn.

Three months ago—the lifetime ago, the before—he would have said *Ishikawa* and nothing else. First name only, the way he'd done for two decades, the way a man does when he wants to be a function instead of a person. A voice on a phone. A van in the driveway. Nobody's memory burdened with him. He had believed, with the quiet devotion most people reserve for religion, that being unremembered was a kind of safety. He had built his entire life inside that belief the way a hermit crab builds itself inside a borrowed shell, and it had taken a house full of the dead, a cellar floor giving way beneath him, and his mother's handwriting in a ledger older than he was to teach him what the shell had actually been.

It had been a wall. Sealed from the inside. With his own handprints all over it.

"August Ishikawa," he said. "First name August, last name Ishikawa. A-u-g-u-s-t. You'll want the spelling for the invoice anyway."

He heard her write it down. He heard the small scratch of pen on paper, and something in his chest turned over gently, like a page.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it in a way people rarely mean anything. "Nobody ever gives me their full name anymore."

"No, ma'am," August said, looking out his kitchen window at the ordinary gray light of an ordinary October morning. "I've learned not to waste it."

* * *

He packed the van the way he always did, which was to say like a man preparing for surgery, a siege, and a funeral simultaneously.

Spray rig, excluded. This job wasn't going to need it, and they both knew it, the rig and him. Traps, excluded. Flashlight, included, out of respect for tradition. The digital recorder went into the left breast pocket of his work shirt, where it had lived so long it had worn a faint rectangular shadow into the fabric. And then, last, wrapped in a clean shop towel and laid flat across the passenger seat like a sleeping animal:

The ledger.

It wasn't the logbook anymore. The logbook—the green one, the one with four thousand meticulous entries, timestamps and coordinates and weather conditions, the rosary he'd told himself beads of—was still in the glove box, still dutifully recording the mundane infestations of the living. But the ledger rode up front now. Leather cover gone soft as ear at the corners. His mother's handwriting in the early pages, looping and sure, the handwriting of a woman who had come to Hollowmere House without her son and left something unfinished on purpose, because some doors can only be opened from the child's side.

And after her pages, his. Forty-one names in his own hand, each one spoken aloud in a collapsing cellar while the walls convulsed and the fingerprints lifted off the plaster like ash rising off a burnt page. Eleanor Halloway. Thomas Halloway. Wren Halloway, who had taught a boy to waltz in a room with no music, whose memory lived in August's feet now and always would. And at the bottom of the final page, uncrossed at last, the name he had carried his whole life without knowing it was a debt:

*M. Ishikawa.* Marian. His mother, who had named herself into a house and waited thirty years for her son to read her out of it.

He touched the ledger's cover once, the way you'd touch a shoulder. Then he started the van.

The address the dispatcher gave him was in Calloway County, ninety minutes north, a farmhouse outside a town so small the map app pronounced its name like an apology. August drove with the windows cracked despite the cold. He'd gotten into the habit. There were things you wanted to be able to smell coming.

Around mile forty, somewhere past the reservoir, he caught himself humming—a tuneless little three-note phrase, rising, falling, rising—and realized it was the melody Wren Halloway had hummed while teaching him to waltz in a room that no longer existed. He didn't stop. That was another thing the house had taught him: you don't get to choose which memories stay. You only get to decide whether you carry them like luggage or like guests.

He carried them like guests. He set another place at the table every day.

* * *

The farmhouse announced itself a half mile early, the way honest houses do: a lean of fence posts, a gate that hung with the particular despair of hardware nobody touches anymore. The woman—her name was Delia Marsh, and he'd written it down in the ledger under a fresh heading, because names went in the ledger now, all of them, that was the procedure—met him in the yard with her arms crossed against the cold and the guilt.

"I know how it sounded on the phone," she said.

"It sounded accurate," August said. "That's usually the part people apologize for."

She led him through the house room by room, and he documented as he went, because documentation was still armor even when you knew what it was armor against. Photographs with timestamps. Measurements. In the back hallway he found what she'd described: a sealed section of wall, plaster over old lath, and there on the inside face—visible through a hairline seam where the plaster had begun to breathe—smudges. Small ones. Fingertip-sized. Arranged not randomly but in a pattern he recognized with a lurch that started in his sternum and finished somewhere behind his knees.

They were climbing downward.

"They wipe off," Delia said behind him. "But they come back. And lately—" She stopped. Started again. "Lately my daughter says she can hear someone counting under the floor. Not saying numbers. Just... counting."

August crouched at the wall seam and did not touch it. He had learned about touching.

"Has anyone in your family lived here long?" he asked. "Generations?"

"Four," she said. "Why?"

"Because houses like this don't infest," he said, standing, brushing the dust from his knee with the care of a man buying time. "They inherit. And sometimes—" he looked at the seam, at the small warm prints pressing patiently outward from a century of silence, "—sometimes they advertise for new family."

Delia stared at him. "What kind of pest control company *are* you?"

August Ishikawa smiled, and it was a real smile, tired around the edges but real, the smile of a man who had walked into a cellar expecting to die and walked out at dawn carrying forty-one souls and his mother's forgiveness in a leather book.

"The thorough kind," he said. "I'll need until sundown. And Mrs. Marsh? Take your daughter and go stay somewhere with fluorescent lighting tonight. Nothing personal. It's just that what happens here next isn't a spectator event."

* * *

He worked through the afternoon the way he imagined his mother must have worked through hers, decades ago, in a different house with the same appetite. He photographed. He logged. He mapped the rooms against each other and noted where the geometry disagreed with itself—a closet deeper than the exterior wall allowed, a window that counted wrong from outside—and he wrote it all down in the green logbook first, because procedure was procedure, and then transferred what mattered into the ledger, because the ledger was for truth.

In the upstairs hall he found the mirror.

Of course he found the mirror. There was always a mirror. It hung between two framed samplers of faded embroidery, and when he stepped in front of it, his reflection arrived a beat late—a blink lagging, a breath trailing—and August stood there and watched the second-hand version of himself watch him, and felt not terror but something closer to recognition. Like running into an old coworker at a funeral.

"Yeah," he told it quietly. "I know. I remember you."

The reflection's mouth moved. No sound came. But he was fluent now in the grammar of lagging glass, and he read the shape of it easily, the way you read lips through a window.

*You came back.*

"Not on purpose," August said. "But yeah. Somebody's got to do the reading."

The reflection nodded—slowly, a full second behind, and then a full second ahead, and then, for just one frame, perfectly in time, which was worse—and August took out his recorder and logged the timestamp and the delay interval and the exact wording, because somewhere in the last three months he had understood that the notes were never armor at all. They were the opposite. They were the door, held open. You cannot witness a thing and keep it at arm's length. Witnessing *is* the embrace. His whole life he'd thought writing things down kept the world out.

It kept the world *in*. That was the ritual. That had always been the ritual. His mother had known it, and died before she could tell him, and left him a blank line in a ledger so he'd have to learn it the way she had: by hand.

By dusk, Delia Marsh was gone with her daughter, and August stood alone in the farmhouse kitchen with the ledger open on the table and the light going blue at the windows, and he did what he now did at the start of every job, the way other men check their equipment or say their prayers.

He read the names.

All forty-one. Aloud, slowly, in the empty kitchen, giving each one its full weight—Eleanor, Thomas, Wren, and the thirty-eight others, the generations of Hollowmere's kept and finally released—and as he spoke them he felt them settle around him the way family settles around a holiday table: not quiet, exactly, but present. Warm at the edges. A found family assembled not from blood but from witness, from the stubborn act of saying *you existed, I saw it, I'm telling it forward.*

Last, as always: "Marian Ishikawa." His mother's name, uncrossed, in his own hand. "My mother. She liked lilacs and she hummed off-key and she loved me in the only way she could, which was badly, and from far away, and enough."

The house around him sighed—or the wind did, through eaves that needed work—and August closed the ledger and picked up his flashlight and went to find the cellar.

There was always a cellar.

At the top of the stairs, he paused, and took out his phone, and called the dispatcher at Meridian Pest Solutions, because there was one more piece of procedure now, one more bead on the rosary, and it was the newest one and the most important.

"Meridian, this is Carol."

"Carol, it's August Ishikawa." Full name. Always the full name now. "I'm on site at the Marsh property, Calloway County. I'm going to be unavailable by radio until sunrise. If I miss my morning check-in, send someone—but send someone who's willing to listen, Carol. That's the qualification that matters."

There was a pause on the line. "...You okay, hon?"

August looked down the cellar stairs, into the dark where small hands had been pressing patiently against the inside of the world for a hundred years, waiting for somebody to come down and say their names.

"Yeah," he said, and found that he meant it. "For the first time in about forty years, Carol—I'm exactly who I'm supposed to be."

He put the phone away. He clicked on the flashlight. And August Ishikawa, the man who remembers, walked down into the dark carrying forty-two names—

—and stopped, three steps from the bottom, because the flashlight beam had found the cellar wall, and the wall was already covered in fingerprints.

Fresh ones. Small ones. Climbing upward.

And beneath them, scratched deep into the plaster in a hand he recognized with a jolt that dropped straight through the floor of him—in his mother's hand, in handwriting thirty years dead, in letters he had last seen in a ledger in a house that had fallen at dawn—

were two words:

*TOO SLOW.*
