## 3. One Second Late

The staff bathroom at Marrowfen Academy smelled of lemon polish and old pennies, which Xanthe had decided was the school's way of saying *we try, but we are very old*. She'd been there three weeks now, long enough to have a favorite mug in the staff room (the chipped one shaped like an owl, which nobody else wanted, thank goodness) and long enough to stop flinching when the radiators knocked like polite ghosts asking to be let in.

She was washing her hands after third period — the Year Nines had finally learned that "Miss Coldharbour" was not, in fact, a suggestion — when she noticed it.

Her reflection blinked.

She hadn't.

It was a small thing. The kind of thing you'd blame on fluorescent lighting, or tiredness, or the tail end of a cold. Xanthe stood with her hands dripping into the basin and watched the woman in the mirror watch her back, and for one heartbeat everything was perfectly ordinary, and then—

There. Again. A lag. Her reflection's smile arrived a half-beat after she felt it on her own face, like an echo in a canyon that had gotten its timing wrong.

"Right," Xanthe said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "You're just tired. You've been marking essays about *Wuthering Heights* until midnight. Your brain is doing a buffering thing."

The reflection said it too — but late. A full second late, maybe more. She counted. One-Mississippi. The mouth moved. Two-Mississippi. The sound caught up.

Xanthe dried her hands very slowly, the way you do when you're trying not to alert a bear, and left the bathroom without looking back. Which was, of course, impossible not to do. She looked back. The mirror held only the empty room, and even the empty room seemed to arrive slightly after the fact, like news from a distant country.

* * *

By evening, the boarding house had gone strange in the specific way old houses go strange: not menacing, exactly, but attentive. Xanthe's little flat above the east wing had three mirrors — the wardrobe glass, the vanity, and a round one in the hall downstairs that everyone called the Sentinel for reasons nobody would explain, which felt less like tradition and more like a dare.

She tested them all, scientifically, with a metronome app and the grim cheer of a woman who has decided that if she is going mad she will at least be *methodical* about it.

The wardrobe: one second behind. She raised her hand; the glass raised its hand as the second turned over, obedient but tardy, a student who knows the answer but won't say it until someone else does.

The vanity: one second behind.

The Sentinel: one second behind, and worse — the Sentinel seemed to be waiting for her. When she stepped into the hall, the reflection was already mid-turn toward the door, as though it had heard her coming down the stairs before she had. Then it corrected itself, snapping back into sync with a flicker like a skipped frame of film, and pretended it hadn't.

"Oh, I don't like that," Xanthe told it. "I want it noted that I don't like that."

The note was taken one second late.

She did what any reasonable person does. She covered the mirrors with scarves. She made tea. She sat with her laptop and typed *mirror lags one second behind* into a search bar and got results about screen refresh rates, dissociation, and a forum called GlassWatchers that she closed immediately, because the first post read *if yours is counting, stop letting it finish*, and Xanthe had a hard limit on how much dread she could absorb per evening.

It was nearly eleven when the knock came at her door — three soft raps, apologetic, the knock of someone who hoped she wasn't home.

Wren Ashcombe stood in the corridor holding a lantern, which was absurd and wonderful, because the corridor had electricity, but Wren carried the lantern the way other people carried opinions: constantly, and without apology. They were the caretaker, twenty-something and freckled and permanently smelling faintly of lamp oil and cut grass, and they had been watching Xanthe since her first day with an expression she'd privately catalogued as *pity with a work ethic*.

"You saw it," Wren said. Not a question.

"Saw what?" Xanthe said, because her mother had raised her to never confirm anything to anyone holding a lantern after eleven p.m.

"The mirrors." Wren glanced down the hall, both directions, the practiced sweep of someone checking for eavesdropping architecture. "They're late tonight. All of them?"

"All of them. Exactly one second. I timed it." Xanthe hesitated. "You say 'tonight' like this is weather."

"It is weather." Wren's voice dropped. "Can I come in? Your kettle's the only one in the building that doesn't whistle in B-flat, and I can't explain why that matters, but it does."

Xanthe let them in. Wren took in the scarved mirrors with a nod, like a doctor confirming a rash, and sat down at her little table without being asked, and for a moment neither of them spoke while the kettle gathered its courage.

"My sister," Wren said finally. "Maren. She was a student here. 1998." They turned the lantern down, though it didn't need turning down; some habits are prayers. "The autumn she disappeared, I was nine. And the mirrors went late. Every one in our house. My mother covered them with sheets and my father took the sheets down the next morning and said we'd imagined it, and he said it so many times that eventually we had."

Xanthe sat down across from them. "And Maren?"

"Maren walked out to the orchard on a Sunday and never came back. Eleven searchers, two weeks, nothing. No coat, no shoe, no—" Wren stopped. Started again, quieter. "The town says she ran away. The town says that about everyone who goes missing here. It's practically the town motto. *Marrowfen: Where People Run Away, Historically, In Groups.*"

"In groups," Xanthe repeated. "The class of '74."

Something moved through Wren's face — relief, she thought, the terrible relief of being known. "You found the register."

"I found a register with eleven names struck through in red and a note in my pigeonhole telling me to stop counting." She wrapped her hands around her tea. "I assumed the note meant stop counting students. Now I'm wondering if it meant stop counting seconds."

"They're the same count," Wren said. "That's the thing nobody says out loud. The house counts. Doors, names, seconds. My family built half of it and my family pretends the other half doesn't exist, and I grew up learning which floorboards to walk soft on." They looked up, and their eyes were steady and scared in equal measure, which Xanthe was beginning to understand was simply the Marrowfen expression. "I've watched those mirrors go late twice in my life. Once the year Maren vanished. And once—"

They stopped.

"Wren."

"Three weeks ago," Wren said. "The week you arrived."

The radiator knocked. Outside, wind moved through the orchard with a sound almost like humming, almost like a tune remembered from childhood, and Xanthe felt the hair rise on her arms in a slow wave, one arm and then the other, and hated herself for noticing the delay.

"So it's me," she said. "Whatever this is, it started when I came."

"Or it started *for* you," Wren said. "There's a difference. Things here don't happen *to* people. They happen *about* them. Like the house is rehearsing." They rubbed their face. "I shouldn't be telling you any of this. If my aunt knew I was up here past ten, she'd have me regrouting the chapel by dawn. But you stood in that hallway your first week and counted the doors out loud — I heard you, everyone hears everyone here, that's half the problem — and you got the number right, and nobody's gotten the number right in years, and I thought: either she's going to be eaten by this place, or she's going to be the one who finally asks it a question it has to answer."

"And which would you prefer?"

"Honestly?" Wren almost smiled. It was a good smile, crooked and unpracticed, arriving exactly on time, which in this house felt like a small miracle. "I'd prefer the question. I've been waiting twenty-five years for somebody brave enough or stupid enough to ask it. Preferably somebody who isn't a founding family. Somebody with no name in the ledger. Somebody the house hasn't already been paid for."

"That's a lot of conditions."

"You fit all of them. It's very annoying."

Xanthe laughed — really laughed, for the first time since she'd arrived, and the sound bounced off the scarfed mirrors and came back wrong, one second late, a ghost of her own gladness trailing her like a shy child. She decided, deliberately, to ignore it. There would be time for fear later. Right now there was tea going cold and a person across the table who had chosen, against blood and habit and obvious self-preservation, to sit in her small warm room and tell the truth.

"All right," she said. "Alliance. But I have terms. One: no more ominous partial sentences. If you start a sentence about your family, you finish it, even if it takes all night. Two: we test the mirrors together tomorrow, properly, every one in the building, and we write down what we find. Three—" she pointed at the lantern, "—you teach me why you carry that thing, because I refuse to believe it's aesthetic."

"It's partly aesthetic," Wren admitted.

"Partly."

"And partly because light that comes from something burning is older than light from a bulb, and some things in this school only respect the older kind." They said it lightly, but their hand had gone white around the lantern's handle. "Same time tomorrow night? I'll bring salt."

"Why salt?"

"Term four," Wren said, and then, catching themselves, held up both hands. "Sorry. Term four: I answer that question tomorrow, over the good biscuits, in a room with no mirrors."

They left at half past midnight, and Xanthe stood at her window watching the lantern bob away across the quad, a small stubborn flame moving through all that dark, and thought: *I came here for a fresh start. This is technically fresh.*

Behind her, under its scarf, the wardrobe mirror waited. And just before she turned away — just before, she was almost sure, she had decided to turn away — her reflection turned first.

One second early.
