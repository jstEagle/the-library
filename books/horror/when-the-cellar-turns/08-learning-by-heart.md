## 8. Learning by Heart

### Xanthe

The third verse could not be written down.

Xanthe learned this on the fourth stolen night, when she brought a pencil and a napkin from the dining hall into the reflected school and the eleven students looked at her with the particular pity reserved for someone who has suggested something charmingly stupid.

"You can't," said Odile Marsh, who had been thirteen in 1974 and was still thirteen now, in a way that made Xanthe's chest ache every time she saw her. "It's not that it's forbidden. It's that paper forgets."

"Paper doesn't forget."

"This paper would." Odile tapped the blank third page of the sheet music Xanthe had smuggled out of the sealed music room. "It tried already. Look."

Xanthe looked. Faint gray ghosts of notation swam across the page — a clef here, the suggestion of a rest there — like footprints in snow that kept filling themselves in. Whatever the verse was, the page had tried to hold it and given up.

"It lives in the singing or nowhere," said Tobias Fenwick, the eldest of them, though "eldest" meant he'd been fourteen longest. "That's why they never worried about us teaching anyone. They assumed no one would refuse to join long enough to learn it."

"And you're refusing," Odile said, studying Xanthe with frank, unnerving curiosity. "You keep almost humming along. Your mouth does this thing." She demonstrated: a small, treacherous opening of the lips, like a fish considering religion. "You catch yourself every time. That's why we can teach you. The song only passes to someone who keeps saying no."

The reflected school was the academy as the house remembered it — warmer than the real one, which should have been the first clue. The corridor lamps glowed amber instead of institutional green. The radiators clanked in friendly rhythm. Somewhere below, the cellar hummed its low patient note, the one Xanthe had begun hearing in her teeth.

They met in the reflected music room, because it was the only room where the walls didn't lean in to listen. Eleven desks arranged in a half-circle. Eleven children who had been rehearsing the same lullaby for fifty years without knowing they were the instrument.

"Rule one," Tobias said. "You never sing it alone until you mean to finish it."

"Rule two," said a small boy named Piers, who had died — no, *stayed* — at nine, "you don't sing it in front of the mirrors unless Wren is watching the door."

"Rule three," Odile said, "is that if you start crying, we stop. Not because crying is bad. Because the house collects tears. It uses them for tuning."

"That's horrifying," Xanthe said.

"We know," said all eleven, in unison, with the mildness of people stating the weather.

### Wren

Wren had become, in the space of two weeks, an accomplished criminal.

Not a dramatic one. No masked burglaries, no midnight safecracking. Just a caretaker walking the halls after lights-out with a wheelbarrow of cleaning supplies, except the supplies were candles, a sack of rock salt, matches, and — on Thursdays, because Piers had asked — a tin of lemon drops that Wren bought from the village shop with cash so there'd be no receipt.

Mrs. Aldery at the shop had raised an eyebrow. "Expecting a power cut?"

"Winter's coming," Wren said, which was true, and also the kind of answer that made people stop asking questions in Marrowfen. Half the town's conversations ended in shrugs that meant *don't look too hard*.

The salt was for the thresholds. A line across any doorway slowed the lag — bought the reflected school an extra minute of separation, gave the children time to scatter if the house stirred. The candles were simpler: the children wanted light they could watch flicker. Fifty years under the flat, patient glow of reflected lamps had left them starved for anything that behaved honestly.

Wren went down the cellar stairs first, always, and stood before the tall mirror at the bottom — the one that didn't show the cellar, but showed the music room — and waited.

It took between ten seconds and four minutes, depending on the house's mood. Then the glass would fog from the far side, and a small hand would press through — not breaking the surface, just dimpling it, the way a finger presses a drumskin — and Wren would pass the candle through, and the salt, and the tin.

"How are they?" Wren asked, the first night, and felt foolish immediately, because how do you ask that about children your grandmother helped bury in a mirror?

The hand withdrew. A moment later, Odile's face surfaced against the glass like a swimmer's.

"Loud," Odile said. "In a good way. We haven't been loud in years. She makes us laugh and then she catches herself doing it and looks guilty, and then we laugh harder, and the house gets very annoyed." A pause. "Your sister says hello."

Wren's throat closed. Their sister, Maren, taken in 1989, folded into the reflection before Wren was old enough to spell her name right. Wren had spent thirty years being carefully, professionally unaware of where she'd gone.

"Tell her—" Wren started, and couldn't find the end of the sentence.

"She knows," Odile said gently. "She watches you count the doors. She thinks you're very thorough." Another pause, and then, with the terrible directness of thirteen-year-olds: "She also thinks you're in love with the new teacher. Everyone thinks that. You're extremely obvious."

"I'm going to take back the lemon drops," Wren said.

"You won't."

Wren wouldn't.

### Xanthe

Learning the verse was like learning a staircase in the dark — by falling up it.

The children taught in fragments, one per night, because more than that and the house noticed the shape of what they were building. The first fragment was three notes that tasted like cold iron. The second was a rest held so long Xanthe thought they'd stopped teaching. The third required her to breathe wrong — to inhale through the pause instead of before it — and she got it wrong eleven times before Piers climbed onto a desk, took her chin in his small hands, and said, "You're breathing like a living person. Stop it. Breathe like a door."

"Breathe like a—"

"A door opens by making itself into nothing," Piers explained patiently. "That's the whole trick of the verse. You have to be willing to be nothing for exactly one second. One second. Any longer and you're theirs. Any shorter and the song doesn't believe you."

"One second," Xanthe repeated. The same length as the lag. Of course it was. The whole house ran on that missing beat; the verse simply asked her to stand inside it.

On the ninth night, she got the breath right, and the three notes came out sounding less like notes and more like a decision, and all eleven children went silent in a way that was different from their usual silence — hopeful instead of practiced.

"That's it," Tobias whispered. "That's the beginning of the end of it."

"Don't celebrate yet," Odile warned. "There's a middle part that's awful."

"How awful?"

"It's the part where you have to forgive them," Odile said, and nodded upward, toward the town, toward the founding families, toward the warm lit windows of people who had fed children to a house for seventy years. "The verse won't carry anger. Anger is heavy. It needs you empty. You can be furious again afterward — we'd insist on it, frankly — but during the verse, you have to let it go, and I don't know if you can, and honestly I don't know if I want you to."

Xanthe sat with that. Around her, the reflected music room breathed its amber light. Somewhere above, the real school slept, and the real town slept, and the founding families slept in houses built on interest payments of a debt paid in children.

"I can try," she said.

"That's all it ever asks," said Maren, speaking for the first time in weeks, from the back row. "Trying. The house never asks. That's how you know which one you're talking to."

### Wren

On the twelfth night, Wren found Xanthe asleep in the boarding house common room with the blank sheet music open on her chest and salt lines drawn around the armchair in a pattern that was either protective or decorative.

Wren stood in the doorway a long moment, watching the candlelight move on her face, and thought about the family ledger in the cellar office — the one with columns of names and dates and, beside each date, a figure. The Coldharbour fortune. The Fenwick mills. The Marsh shipping money. All of it accruing quietly, generation after generation, on the backs of eleven names struck through in red.

Their grandmother had kept the ledger. Their mother had dusted it. And Wren had spent thirty years walking past the cellar door twice nightly, counting it faithfully, keeping the books of a crime by pretending the ledger was furniture.

Xanthe stirred. "How long have you been standing there looking tragic?"

"About a minute. It's my specialty."

"It's very good." She sat up, wincing at the crick in her neck. "Progress tonight. I did the breath. Piers says I'm nearly a door."

"Piers is generous. He once told me I was nearly a good dancer."

"He's right about both things, probably." She smiled, then sobered. "Wren. There's a part of the verse I don't know if I can do. And there's a part of the plan I haven't told you, because telling you might make you complicit in a way you can't walk back."

"I've been smuggling contraband through a haunted mirror to the ghost children my family imprisoned," Wren said. "I think the complicity ship sailed, capsized, and was declared a maritime loss."

Xanthe laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind the house couldn't collect because it happened in a salted room — and then crossed to Wren and took both their hands.

"The equinox concert," she said. "They're going to add new names. I heard the headmaster rehearsing his speech. So it has to be that night. I go down to the cellar, I sing the last verse alone, and I hold the note while the chorus tries to swallow me." Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "And I need you to not save me. Even if it sounds like I'm dying. Especially then. Dying is the one second. If you break the salt line to reach me, I'll be nothing forever instead of nothing for a moment."

Wren looked at her — this woman who had arrived wanting a fresh start so badly she hadn't asked enough questions, and who was now volunteering to stand inside the house's missing beat and sing it shut.

"If it goes wrong," Wren said, "I'll be counting doors for the rest of my life knowing I counted you out."

"It won't go wrong."

"People say that right before it goes wrong."

"Then let me say something else instead." Xanthe squeezed their hands. In the candlelight, with salt at her feet and ghost-children waiting behind the glass and the whole rotten town sleeping off another comfortable century, she said it plainly, no poetry, no rehearsal: "I love you. I wanted you to hear it before the dark does. If the house takes my voice tomorrow, I want it to be the second-to-last thing I ever said out loud."

"What's the last thing?"

"'Hold the note.'"

Wren pulled her close, careful of the salt lines, careful of everything, and whispered it back against her hair — *I love you* — quiet enough that even a listening house would mistake it for a heartbeat.

Above them, faintly, the cellar began to hum.

The equinox was three days away.
