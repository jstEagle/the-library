## 3. Nineteen Desks

The basement archive smelled of cold paper and older things beneath it — a sweetness like apples rotting in the dark, though no one had stored fruit down here in decades. Isandro stood at the bottom of the concrete stairs, keys in hand, and let their eyes adjust to the single caged bulb that swung faintly on its wire, though there was no draft.

Principal Voss had given them the key the way a man hands over something he'd rather not touch. "Records from before '85 are down there," he'd said, already turning away. "Nothing much worth reading."

Which, Isandro had learned in three weeks in Hollow Creek, was exactly how people here said *don't look*.

They had come for attendance records. That was all — a teacher's ordinary diligence, an audit of enrollment numbers so the district funding forms could be filed correctly. Documentation as armor. If Isandro wrote everything down, catalogued every oddity, then the oddities stayed on the page and out of the blood. That was the theory, anyway. The logbook in their satchel — eleven days of entries now, each one noting the lag in the mirrors, the fraction of a second growing like a debt accruing interest — had begun as science and was becoming liturgy.

The archive was four rows of steel shelving under low pipes. Boxes labeled by decade in faded clerk's hand. Isandro found 1974 near the back wall, where the bulb's light gave up, and carried the box to the worktable beneath the stairs.

Inside: grade books, lunch program receipts, a PTA newsletter gone soft as cloth with damp. And the attendance registers — hardbound, maroon, their spines cracked like knuckles.

Isandro opened the fall term of 1974 and felt the breath go out of them slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere deep in a house.

Pages had been razored out.

Not torn — razored. Clean excisions, close to the spine, leaving only a pale seam of stubble where the paper had been. Someone had sat at this table or another like it, with all the time in the world, and cut these children out of existence one leaf at a time. Isandro turned page after page. September intact: names in a careful teacher's script, nineteen of them, checkmarks marching across the weeks. Then October began, and the register went to the blade. November: more seams. By December the book was mostly absence, whole gatherings of pages removed, so that the remaining leaves fanned wrong, loose, like a mouth missing teeth.

Nineteen desks in Isandro's classroom. Twenty-three names on the memorial plaque outside. Isandro had counted them the first day, standing in the cold with coffee going bitter in their hand, telling themselves the discrepancy meant nothing, that plaques accumulated errors the way towns accumulated silence.

Now they ran a finger along a razored seam and understood that someone had done arithmetic here too — and chosen the knife over the pen.

At the bottom of the box, wrapped in a 1973 newspaper whose headlines were about grain futures, was a journal. Cheap composition book, cover bloomed with gray mold in the corners. No name on the front. On the first page, in the same careful script as the registers:

*Sept. 4, 1974 — Nineteen students. All present. God forgive me for what I have agreed to.*

Isandro did not move for a long moment. The caged bulb hummed. Somewhere above, footsteps crossed the hallway floor and paused — just briefly — directly overhead, before moving on.

They read.

*Sept. 20 — The committee came again. They call it the tithe as if naming it plainly would make it worse than it is. Four, they say. From my class. I asked what happens to them and Mr. Aldous looked at me with such pity, such terrible patience, and said: the same thing that happens to all of us eventually, Miss Crane. Just sooner, and for everyone's sake.*

*Oct. 1 — First frost soon. The fields this year — you would not believe the fields. Corn past the eaves. Tomatoes in October. The Hollis farm next county over has blight and we have abundance. There is no God in this arrangement but there is certainly something.*

*Oct. 11 — I have written the names. I will not write them here again. I cannot stop seeing their faces at their desks. I have begun keeping a second register, hidden, because I will not let them be cut out. Whatever they do to the pages, I will keep the names. A name kept is a door left open.*

The final entry was dated October 31st, 1974, and consisted of a single line:

*They are taking them tonight. I am going down to say attendance one last time.*

There were no entries after that. But bound into the back cover, folded small, was a sheet of lined paper — a handwritten roster, twenty-three names, each one followed by a small, urgent checkmark, as if the writer had been marking them present even as the world marked them absent.

Isandro copied every name into their logbook. Their hand shook only a little. Outside, far off across the fields, something that might have been wind moved through the standing corn, and the sound it made was almost like children counting.

* * *

Marisol dreamed in handwriting she'd never learned.

She hadn't told anyone that. Not Dev, who'd have made a joke first and worried after; not Theo, who barely spoke; certainly not her mother, who went quiet in a specific closed way whenever 1974 came up on the news or in church. But it was true, and it was getting worse, and lately the dreams didn't stay dreams. She'd woken Tuesday with ink on her fingers — actual blue-black ink, the old fountain-pen kind nobody used anymore — and a page torn from her spiral notebook covered in looping, careful cursive that was not hers:

*Attendance. Say it every morning. Say it so they know they are not forgotten. Ruthie Calloway. Present. Daniel Okafor. Present—*

She'd burned it in the sink and scrubbed the pan twice and told herself it was sleepwalking, stress, the weirdness that had settled over Hollow Creek like weather since Ms. Blackwood arrived and started asking questions nobody wanted answered.

But Thursday night the dream came again, and this time she was *in* it — not watching, in it — sitting at a desk too small for her, in a classroom she knew was her own classroom except the light was amber and the windows showed October and outside the corn was taller than the sky. Nineteen desks, all full. All the kids looking forward, patient, the way you wait for your turn. At the front stood a woman with kind tired eyes and chalk dust on her sleeve, and she was saying names, and every child answered *present* in a voice like a bell rung underwater, and Marisol's mouth was moving too, answering to a name that wasn't hers, and it fit her mouth perfectly.

She woke at 3 a.m. with her heart slamming and her hand already writing.

This time she didn't burn it. She read it instead, by phone-light, huddled under blankets while the house ticked around her. The cursive marched down the page, unhurried, faithful:

*Ruthie Calloway. Present. Daniel Okafor. Present. Wren Hollis. Present. Thomas Aldous Jr. Present. Marisol Vega—*

Her own name. In handwriting fifty years dead. Marked present.

Marisol sat very still until the shaking stopped, and then she thought about the only person in Hollow Creek who might not look at her like she was crazy, might not go quiet and closed, might actually write it down and take it seriously. Ms. Blackwood, who kept that little logbook and asked about the gap year with a flat brave stubbornness everyone warned her against. Who'd started a lunchtime archive club that was really just the four of them — Marisol, Dev, Theo, and the teacher — eating burnt grilled cheese in the basement among the boxes while Isandro pretended it was about district paperwork.

Friday morning, before first period, Marisol stood outside the classroom door with the page folded in her pocket like contraband, and watched through the glass as Isandro Blackwood arranged nineteen desks into rows, pausing — as they did every morning, Marisol had noticed — at the four extra desks pushed against the wall. Touching each one lightly, like a headstone.

She knocked.

* * *

Isandro listened without interrupting, which was the hardest discipline they knew. Marisol Vega stood at the front desk holding a torn notebook page in both hands, sixteen years old and white-lipped, reciting a dream in which she answered roll call for a class that vanished before her parents were born.

"Say the names," Isandro said quietly. "The ones from the dream."

Marisol did. Nine of them. Isandro pulled the logbook from their satchel, opened it to the page copied from the hidden roster in the journal, and laid it flat between them.

Marisol looked down. Her face did something complicated — relief and horror arriving together, twins. "That's it," she whispered. "That's the same list. How do I know that? I've never seen that paper. I've never learned that handwriting." Her voice thinned. "Ms. Blackwood, why do I dream in a dead woman's hand?"

"I don't know yet." Isandro kept their voice level, their pen moving, recording. "But I found the journal yesterday. The teacher's name was Eleanor Crane. She hid a second register — the real one, with all twenty-three names — because the district razored the official copies." They watched Marisol absorb that. "You're not crazy. You may be remembering."

"That's worse," Marisol said, and laughed, a small cracked sound. "I've been having grief dreams, is what you're saying. Grief that isn't mine." She pressed her palm flat against the notebook page, over the looping cursive, the way you'd press a hand to a fevered forehead. "It doesn't feel like it isn't mine. That's the horrible part. It feels like mourning somebody I loved."

Outside, the October light lay long and gold across the parking lot, beautiful the way everything in Hollow Creek was beautiful, fed on something. Isandro thought of the razored pages, the clean seams, the town's tidy amnesia, and of Eleanor Crane going down into the dark on the last night of October 1974 to say attendance one final time.

"Marisol," Isandro said. "Theo draws faces he shouldn't know. Dev hears the corn saying numbers. Now you write in a dead teacher's hand. Do you understand what that means?"

Marisol shook her head.

"It means the door Eleanor Crane left open," Isandro said, closing the logbook gently, "was left open *through you*. Three of you. Maybe four." They glanced toward the wall where the extra desks waited. "And I think whatever's under those fields has noticed the door is open — because my mirrors have started waiting for me too."

Marisol hugged the notebook page to her chest. "So what do we do?"

Isandro looked at the roster, at the twenty-three small checkmarks, at the last line of a dead woman's courage, and heard themselves answer before caution could catch up:

"We finish her attendance."
