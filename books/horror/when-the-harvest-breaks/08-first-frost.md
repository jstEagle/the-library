## 8. First Frost

The frost came on a Thursday, and the town knew before the weather did.

Isandro woke to a silence with weight to it — the particular hush of a place holding its breath — and found the windows of the rented house furred white on the *inside*, as though the cold had come not from the sky but up through the ground, through the fields, through the foundations, looking for a door. The mirrors had gone quiet. That was the first wrong thing. For three weeks they had lagged, a half-second, then a full one, the house showing Isandro yesterday wearing today's face. Now every glass in the house reflected instantly, obediently, the way mirrors were supposed to, and Isandro understood with a lurch that the lag had never been a malfunction. It had been a countdown. The thing below the stubble no longer needed to look ahead. It was close enough now to see in real time.

The second wrong thing was the smell. Woodsmoke and cinnamon and, beneath it, faint and sweet as rot under blossoms, the smell of the harvest — that impossible abundance the neighboring counties whispered about. Corn heavy enough to split its own husks. Pumpkins the size of cradles. All of it ripened on a schedule no farmer had set, and all of it, Isandro knew now, was interest paid on a debt the town had been servicing for fifty years.

The ledger lay open on the kitchen table where Isandro had left it, weighted with a jam jar. Four names in the teacher's careful, decaying hand. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, but the last entry — made, impossibly, in handwriting fresher than the page it sat on — was not faded at all:

*Payment due: first frost. Four. As always. As agreed.*

Eleven days, the journal had promised. The thing below had not waited for arithmetic. First frost had come early because the thing below had *decided* it had, and the fields did not argue with what fed them.

Isandro dressed in the dark, took the ledger, took the journal, took the folded pages of Theo's drawings — nineteen faces rendered in a child's fierce graphite, accurate down to the freckles, down to the gap in the front teeth of a girl named Abigail Rook — and stepped out into a morning that glittered like broken glass.

* * *

Hollow Creek had never been a warm town, but it had been a courteous one. Mrs. Halloran at the corner house had left tomatoes on the porch rail all September. Pastor Enfield had shaken Isandro's hand at the church picnic and said, *You've been good for the young ones.* The Delgados had waved from their truck every morning like clockwork, Marisol's father lifting two fingers off the wheel in a salute that had come, over the months, to feel almost like belonging.

Now the porch rails were bare. Now the curtains hung drawn at every window Isandro passed, and behind the drawn curtains Isandro could feel them — the whole town, watching, the way a body watches a wound.

Mrs. Halloran's door had a new chain on it. Isandro could see the shine of the brass through the gap where the storm door didn't quite close. When Isandro knocked — three times, the way they'd knocked all autumn, the way that used to bring her to the door with preserves and gossip — the house went still in a way houses shouldn't be able to go still. Not empty-still. *Holding-still.* The stillness of a woman standing in her own hallway with her hand pressed over her mouth.

"Mrs. Halloran," Isandro said. "I know you're there. I just want to talk. About the ledger. About 1974. About what's due tonight."

Nothing. A floorboard creaked somewhere deep in the house, the sound of weight shifting away.

"They were children," Isandro said to the chain, to the drawn curtain, to the town. "They were *children*, and you fed them to it, and you're going to do it again tonight, and you can't even look at me while you do it."

The curtain in the front window twitched. Just once. Then it was still, and Isandro understood that Mrs. Halloran had not twitched it to look out. She had twitched it to check that Isandro was leaving.

The Delgados' truck was in their drive. Isandro crossed the road and knocked there too, harder, and this time there was an answer — Marisol's father opening the door only as far as the chain allowed, his face in the gap gray as porridge, his eyes going past Isandro's shoulder to the fields, to the stubble standing silver with frost all the way to the tree line.

"Mr. Delgado. Marisol is one of the four. You know that. You've *always* known—"

"You don't say her name." His voice was low and cracked and terrible. "You don't say it out loud. Names call things. You've been reading that ledger, teacher, you know how it works. You say a name on first frost and you've *signed* it."

"She's already signed. It's already written. The only question is whether you let them take her or whether you stand with me and we end it — fifty years, Mr. Delgado. Four children every generation. Your grandmother's generation started this. You can be the one who stops it."

For one moment — one long, trembling moment — something moved behind his eyes that might have been the man who'd lifted two fingers off the wheel every morning. Then it went out, the way a candle goes out, deliberately, a thumb and forefinger of fear.

"The harvest keeps the hospital open," he said. "Keeps the co-op. Keeps the lights. My mother's in that hospital, teacher, on a machine that runs on money that grows out of that dirt." His jaw worked. "I'm sorry. God help me, I'm sorry. But everybody pays for something. You just got here. You don't get it yet. *Everybody pays.*"

The door closed. The chain rattled. Isandro stood on the porch of a house that had waved every morning and felt the frost working through the soles of their shoes, and understood that the town had not turned against Isandro. The town had simply, finally, shown Isandro what it had always been: a congregation. And tonight was the service.

* * *

The harvest festival began at dusk, because it always began at dusk, because the thing below did its accounting in the dark.

Isandro had gone to the school first, to Marisol and Dev and Theo, and found the three of them in the archive room among the stolen pages, and had told them everything — the ledger, the early frost, the barred doors, the plan. *We go down before they collect. We go down tonight, ahead of the festival, and we return what was taken before it can take anything new.* Dev had packed a bag with the grim competence of a boy who'd been preparing for a war he couldn't name. Theo had brought the drawings, every one, and a box of matches, and said nothing, which from Theo was a speech.

It was Marisol who'd argued to go home first. *My mom made tamales,* she'd said, and laughed, and the laugh had cracked in the middle. *If tonight's — if something happens, I want — one normal hour. One. Is that so much to ask?*

Isandro had said yes. Isandro would spend the rest of whatever life remained learning to hate that yes.

The festival square glowed orange and gold, strung with bulb-lights and paper lanterns, and the whole town had turned out in its good coats, and the smell of cider and fry bread lay over everything like a blessing. A band played something old and minor-keyed on fiddle and accordion, a tune that sounded, if you listened too long, like a hymn played backwards. Children ran between the stalls. The corn-dollies hung from the pavilion rafters in bundles of four.

*Always four,* Isandro thought. *Even the decorations. Even the crafts.*

Isandro found Marisol near the cider press, and for a moment the festival was almost what it pretended to be — she was laughing at something Dev had said, cider mustache on her upper lip, breath steaming, alive in every ordinary way — and Isandro felt the whole terrible arithmetic of the town press down at once: this, all of this, the lights and the music and the abundance, bought at exactly this price, year after year, generation after generation, and the town dancing on top of the bill.

Then the band stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-phrase, all four musicians at once, the way a clock stops, and in the ringing silence Isandro heard the second sound — the one underneath, the one that had been underneath everything since the day they arrived. A long, slow inhalation. The fields, breathing in. The soil at the edge of the square, at the edge of every yard, at the edge of every grave, drawing breath the way a lung draws it, and the frost on the ground lifting in a fine silver mist and flowing *inward*, toward the pavilion, toward the four corn-dollies, toward the center of the town where the old root cellar had been bricked over and paved and forgotten by everyone except the ledger.

"Marisol." Isandro grabbed her arm. "We go. *Now.*"

But the town had already begun to move. Not running. Worse than running — walking, unhurried, in that terrible congregational way, the good coats and the drawn curtains and the chained doors all converging with the smooth inevitability of water finding a drain. Pastor Enfield was at the front of them, and his face was not cruel. That was the worst of it. His face was *grieving*, and *resolved*, the face of a man carrying a cross he'd helped build.

"The teacher," someone said, and hands — gentle hands, apologetic hands — took Isandro's arms, and there were so many of them, the whole town, everyone Isandro had eaten with and graded papers beside and waved to, and Mrs. Halloran was among them with tears running down her face, saying, "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry," in a voice like a hymn.

They held Isandro at the edge of the square with the terrible gentleness of people who were ashamed, and Isandro watched — could do nothing but watch — as Marisol's father took his daughter's hand. As Marisol looked back over her shoulder, once, and did not scream, because she had read the ledger too, because she had dreamed in a dead child's handwriting, because she had known since the basement what her town was and had stayed anyway, had stayed for tamales and one normal hour, and her eyes found Isandro's across the crowd and in them was not blame.

That was the unbearable part. There was no blame in them at all. Only a question, asked with the whole of her face: *Are you coming?*

The pavilion doors opened onto the old cellar stairs, bricked over in 1975 and bricked over no longer, the mortar crumbled to dust, the dark below exhaling the smell of turned earth and blossoms and something older. The town sang as the four children went down — a harvest hymn, minor-keyed, the one the band had been playing backwards — and the fields breathed in, and in, and in.

The hands let go. They had never needed to hold Isandro. The choice had always been the point; the ledger had a column for witnesses who walked away, and the town knew it, and Isandro knew it, and the thing below the stubble knew it best of all.

Isandro stood in the frost with the ledger under one arm and Theo's drawings against their chest and the journal's last instruction burning behind their eyes — *return what was taken; a debt paid in kind may be paid in kind; memory is a kind* — and watched the dark swallow the children, and felt the mirrors of the whole town, every glass in every house, turn in their frames like heads.

"Wait for me," Isandro said — to Marisol, to the dark, to the nineteen faces in the drawings, to the person they would be on the other side of this, if there was one. "I'm coming down. And I'm not bringing a payment."

Isandro took the first step into the dark, and the frost closed overhead like water, and the last thing the surface world heard was the sound of a teacher reading names aloud into the earth — *Abigail Rook, Thomas Vane, Eleanor Pruitt, Samuel Okafor* — like a prayer said backwards, like attendance being taken at last.
