## 9. Return What Was Taken

The dark beneath the fields was not empty. It was full.

Isandro understood this the way one understands drowning — not as an idea but as a pressure against every part of the body at once. The root cellar had swallowed them whole: Marisol's hand in theirs, Dev's flashlight beam trembling ahead, Theo pressed close with his sketchbook clutched to his chest like a shield made of paper and freckles. And below them, below everything, the soil breathed. Slow. Patient. A lung the size of a county.

Then the ground opened, and Isandro fell into 1974.

Not fell, exactly. *Sank.* Time down here did not move; it pooled. It stood in still brown water up to their knees, warm as bathwater, and when Isandro looked down they saw not their own legs but a schoolyard — asphalt cracked by frost heave, a hopscotch grid chalked in colors that had never faded because nothing here had ever been allowed to fade. Nineteen children stood in the shallows of that drowned year, facing away from them, reciting something in voices worn smooth as river stones.

Attendance. They were reciting attendance.

"Albright, Wren." "Present." "Barnes, Colby." "Present."

The names went on and on, twenty-three of them, each answered by that small clear *present*, and Isandro felt the sound enter through the soles of their feet and climb like cold water finding its level. Marisol made a noise beside them — half sob, half recognition — and one of the children turned her head. Just her head, too far, the way reflections turn wrong. Her face was dry as paper but her eyes were wet, and they were looking directly at Isandro with an expression no child should know how to make: hope, held so long it had calcified into something closer to hunger.

"You came," she said. "Nobody ever comes. They only ever send more."

"I know," Isandro said. Their voice sounded strange down here, flattened, as if the year 1974 were absorbing it. "I know what they sent you instead. I read the ledger. I read all of it."

"Then you know how it works." The voice came from everywhere now, from the soil itself, from the pooled time, wearing the missing teacher's cadence the way a coat is worn by someone else's shoulders. It had spoken to them at the threshold, offered its bargain once already, and Isandro had refused. It offered again anyway. That was the nature of the thing below: it had infinite patience and exactly one appetite. "A name for a name. Four children above for four children below. The harvest keeps. The town keeps. Everyone keeps, forever, except the ones who are eaten. You could walk out of here, teacher. You could even choose who stays. It's only arithmetic."

"It isn't arithmetic," Isandro said. "It's theft. Fifty years of theft, dressed up as bookkeeping."

Marisol's grip tightened on their hand. Dev's light swung wildly across the flooded year, catching faces — nineteen faces, and Theo's drawings had not lied about a single one, down to the gap in the front teeth of a boy named Colby Barnes, down to the constellation of freckles across a girl's nose that Theo had rendered without ever having seen her alive. Theo stepped forward, past Isandro, into the shallows, and held up the sketchbook open to its last page.

"I drew you," Theo said quietly. "All of you. So somebody would remember what you looked like. In case nobody else did."

The children stopped reciting.

For a long moment there was only the breathing of the earth and the drip of time pooling somewhere in the dark, and then the smallest of them — a boy no older than seven, standing hip-deep in 1974 — began to cry without any change in his face, tears simply falling from those dry-paper eyes as though his body had forgotten how grief worked and was relearning it badly.

"We want to go home," he said. "It's almost supper. Mom said be home before the streetlights."

Isandro knelt in the pooled year until they were eye-level with him, and the water soaked through their trousers and it was cold now, cold as October, cold as first frost, and they spoke gently, the way they would have spoken to any student in any classroom they'd ever stood in.

"The streetlights are a long way off," they said. "But I can get you there. All of you. If you'll let me try something the town never tried."

"There is nothing else to pay with," the thing beneath the fields said, and its patience frayed just slightly, showing the vast dull machinery underneath. "Only names. Only children. Only *present*."

"You're wrong." Isandro reached into their coat and drew out the ledger, swollen with damp, its pages soft as skin. "There's always been something else. You've just never been offered it, because the people above you never loved anyone enough to give it."

They opened the ledger to the last blank page and set it on the surface of the pooled time, where it floated, turning slowly.

"Memories," Isandro said. "Mine. Every one I have of Hollow Creek. Every one I have of the three students who followed me down here. Take them instead. Take all of them. Not a name for a name — a life for twenty-three lives. That's the trade. That's the only trade I'm offering."

"No," Marisol said. It came out of her like something breaking. "No, you don't get to — we found you, we *found* you, you're the first person who—" Her voice failed. She grabbed Isandro's sleeve with both hands, fourteen years old and furious and terrified, and Isandro covered her hands with their own.

"Listen to me," they said. "I spent my whole life writing things down because I was afraid of forgetting. Notes, logs, records. Armor against a world that prefers forgetting. But I finally understand what the ledger taught me, what your town never learned: a record isn't the same as a memory. You can write my name in a book. You can't make me remember why it matters. And if the price of you three walking out of here whole is that I wake up tomorrow not knowing your names — then it's cheap. Do you hear me? It's the cheapest thing I've ever been offered."

Dev was crying openly now, the flashlight shaking in his grip, throwing wild shadows across the drowned schoolyard. "We'll find you again," he said. "However long it takes. We'll—"

"Maybe," Isandro said, and smiled, and meant it to be kind. "Or maybe you'll just feel it. Like a room where furniture used to be. That's enough. That's more than these kids got for fifty years."

They turned back to the ledger, to the floating page, and began to speak.

Not names this time. Everything else.

"The smell of chalk dust and burnt coffee in the faculty lounge. The way the corn outside Hollow Creek stands two feet taller than anywhere in the county, and how wrong that looks at dusk. Marisol telling me her mirror waits for her, and how she laughed after she said it, like she'd confessed to something small. Dev arguing about the Cubs in the middle of an archive, dust in his hair. Theo drawing faces from a year he wasn't born in, accurate down to the freckles, and being embarrassed when I cried over it. Burnt dinners in the rented house on Kettle Road. The plaque outside the school with twenty-three names and nineteen desks. Principal Hargrove saying *don't ask about the gap year* with sweat on his upper lip. First frost coming over the stubble fields like a tide going out—"

With every memory spoken, something left Isandro. They felt it go — a warmth lifting out of the chest, a color draining from the interior landscape. The pooled time around them drank it in, and the water began to glow faintly, gold as harvest light, and the children began to rise.

That was the horror and the grace of it, happening together: as Isandro emptied themselves, the nineteen grew lighter than the year that held them. Their feet lifted from the drowned schoolyard. Their heads tipped back toward some ceiling none of them could see, and far above — impossibly far, up through fifty feet of packed earth and frozen stubble and October sky — glass began to break. Isandro heard it as a distant ringing, mirror after mirror shattering across Hollow Creek, in bathrooms and bedrooms and hardware stores, every reflecting surface in town bursting outward at once, opening doors that had only ever shown a lagging second behind.

The children rose toward the breaking mirrors like bubbles rising through dark water.

Wren Albright went first, and as she passed Isandro she touched their cheek with a hand cold as well-water and said, "Present." Then Colby Barnes. Then the small boy who wanted his supper, whispering *thank you thank you thank you* in a voice already thinning into morning. One by one, twenty-three souls — nineteen taken in '74 and four owed since, the debt the ledger had carried all these years — passed upward through the shattering glass, toward the pale seam of dawn cracking over the fields.

The thing beneath the fields screamed. It was not a sound so much as a subtraction — the whole underground year convulsing, the pooled time sloshing black, the teacher's stolen voice tearing down the middle. *You cannot buy them all. No one has ever paid that much. You will be nothing. You will wake as no one.*

"Yes," Isandro said, and kept speaking, kept giving, faster now, pouring themselves out onto the floating page. "I know. Worth it."

The last memories went hardest. Isandro saved them deliberately, hoarded them to the end the way one saves the best of anything: Marisol's laugh, sharp and sudden, in the basement archive. Dev's hand on Theo's shoulder in the dark. Theo's sketchbook, open, the faces of the dead rendered lovingly by a living boy. The three of them around a burnt dinner, laughing, a family assembled out of wreckage and choice.

*I love them,* Isandro thought, and said it aloud, and gave it away.

The light took it.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, and gray light, and cold air that tasted of frost and turned earth.

Isandro lay at the bottom of the collapsed root cellar, alone. The ledger was gone. The children were gone. Above, through the broken throat of the cellar, the sky was doing what October skies do at dawn — paling, hesitating, deciding to begin.

Somewhere up there, mirrors lay shattered in a hundred houses, and in a hundred houses people were waking to a grief they could not name, and in one small house on Kettle Road a woman named Marisol was waking whole, and hollowed, and free.

Isandro tried to remember why they were lying in a hole in the ground.

Nothing came. Only a habit older than thought: they reached into their coat pocket, found a notebook, and a pencil, and wrote, in handwriting that felt borrowed:

*Don't know where I am. Don't know who I am. There's dirt under my nails and I've been crying and I don't know why. Document everything. Start there.*

They climbed toward the light.

And in the pocket of the coat, folded small, a page they did not remember writing, in handwriting that was unmistakably their own, said only:

*worth it.*
