## 4. Grandma's Ledger

The house smelled like it had been holding its breath since she died.

Orsen stood in the kitchen doorway with a crowbar and a flashlight, and told themselves they were here to check the floor joists. That was the story. Rodents in the walls, soft spots in the boards, an old woman's house settling into itself the way old houses did. Rational. Billable. The kind of job that ended with an invoice and a drive back to a rented room where nothing watched from photographs.

But Orsen hadn't brought traps. Hadn't brought bait or gloves or any of the tools of the trade that had paid their bills for twelve years. They'd brought a crowbar, because three nights ago they'd dreamed of grandma kneeling at the edge of the kitchen, prying up a board with her bare hands, her knuckles split and bleeding, mouthing something over and over.

*Under. Under. Under.*

Grandma had never appeared in Orsen's dreams before. Grandma had been the one person in Hollow Creek who never seemed to want anything from anyone—just casseroles and card games and a hand on the back of the neck when the world got loud. If she was clawing at her own floorboards from beyond whatever waited past death, Orsen was going to look under those floorboards.

The chair was still in every photograph. Orsen had stopped looking at them, mostly. But passing the hallway wall on the way in, they'd caught it in peripheral vision—that pale wooden sliver at the edge of every frame, patient as a held breath—and had kept walking.

The kitchen floor was pine, wide-planked, older than anything else in the house except maybe the foundation. Orsen walked it slowly, tapping with the crowbar's handle, listening the way they'd learned to listen in crawlspaces: for hollow, for give, for the difference between wood laid over earth and wood laid over *something else*.

Near the northeast corner, beneath the window where grandma's fern had died two winters ago, the sound changed.

Not hollow. Not exactly. More like the board answered—a low, dense knock, as if the space below were shallower than it should be, packed tight with whatever grandma had hidden there. Orsen knelt. Ran a thumb along the seam. Found the hairline groove someone had cut decades ago, worn smooth by use.

*Use.* The word landed cold. This wasn't a hiding place. It was a drawer.

The crowbar fit the groove like it had been measured for it. The board came up with a groan of old nails surrendering, and dust breathed out of the gap, sweetish, like paper left too long in a closed room.

A tin box. Flour brand faded to a ghost on the lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: a ledger, bound in black leather gone soft as skin, and beneath it a smaller bundle Orsen didn't touch yet—fabric, and something that clinked, faintly, like glass.

They carried the ledger to the kitchen table. To *the* table—the one in the photographs, though the real one held only four chairs and always had. Orsen sat down where grandma used to sit, because it felt less insane than sitting anywhere else, and opened the cover.

The first page stopped their heart for one full beat.

Not because of what was written. Because of the handwriting. It was grandma's—the same cramped, forward-leaning script that filled sixty years of recipe cards still stuffed in the drawer by the stove. But the first entry was dated 1963, and grandma would have been nine years old.

At the top of the page, in that impossible handwriting, a heading:

**PAYMENTS DUE — THE WELL OF HOLLOW CREEK — KEPT BY THE HOUSE OF HALLORAN**

Below it, columns. Dates. Names. Amounts—not money. Orsen read the first entries three times before the pattern resolved, and when it did, they wished it hadn't.

*March 1963 — Paid: one year of rain, withheld from the Kettleman fields. Received in trade: the memory of Thomas Halloran (b. 1931), who will not be missed by those who never knew him.*

*Harvest 1963 — Paid: the fever that took the Pruitt children. Received: the name of Elsie Halloran, struck from the church rolls, from the census, from every mind that ever held it.*

Names crossed out in red ink so old it had gone brown. Not names of the dead—Orsen understood that slowly, horribly, the way you understand water is rising around you. Names of people who had *never been*. People the well had eaten backward through time until even the families who'd raised them couldn't say why a bedroom sat empty or a photograph showed six children where five should be.

Orsen thought of the farmer in Maren's archive photos. The schoolteacher. The woman holding the child no one could identify.

They weren't appearing in the pictures.

They were *coming back*.

* * *

Orsen turned pages until the light through the window went amber and then gray, and didn't get up once. Sixty years of entries. Some years quiet—a single line, *harvest renewed, all debts current*, in handwriting that aged across the decades until it became unmistakably, undeniably grandma's own. She'd taken over the ledger at nineteen. Her mother had kept it before her, and her grandmother before that, the script shifting generation to generation but the columns never changing, the payments never stopping.

And threaded through the whole book, like dark thread through pale cloth: Hallorans.

Hallorans paying. Hallorans received. Hallorans crossed out.

*Harvest 1971 — Paid: the drought. Received: Walter Halloran's face, from all photographs and all remembrance. His wife will grieve without knowing whom she grieves.*

Orsen's great-grandfather. There was a man in one family photo whose features Orsen had never been able to place—a blur where a face should be, which they'd always assumed was bad film. It wasn't bad film. It was a *balance paid*.

Then Orsen reached 1994, and their hands stopped working.

*Harvest 1994 — Paid: the collapse of the east bridge. Received: Daniel Halloran and Ruth Halloran, entire. Struck from all records, all memories, all bloodlines' knowing. Their son remains, unclaimed, as surety.*

Their parents.

Orsen had grown up with a hole shaped like parents and been told—gently, by grandma, by the town, by the soft conspiracy of everyone who loved them—that there'd been an accident, that some things were better not dug at, that Hallorans kept to themselves. Orsen had built a whole life on the foundation of that absence. Had learned to stop asking by age eight. Had learned to flinch away from their own reflection in family photographs, because something about their own face had always looked borrowed.

Because it was. They were the change that never came. The surety. The collateral on a debt their family had been servicing since before the town had a name.

Orsen laughed—one dry, cracked sound with nothing behind it—and it echoed wrong in the empty kitchen, and somewhere down the hall a photograph shifted on its nail, and Orsen chose not to think about that.

They kept reading. Anger made a better engine than grief. Grief wanted to lie down; anger turned pages.

The final entry was dated next month. Harvest night.

It wasn't written in grandma's hand. The letters were deeper than ink—as if someone had pressed them into the page from underneath, from the other side of the paper, the way you'd write on a desk and find your words carved into the wood below.

*Final payment due: ORSEN HALLORAN. Entire. The well remembers its own. The seat has waited sixty-one years.*

The seat.

Orsen looked up at the hallway wall without meaning to. Through the kitchen doorway, in the half-dark, the photographs hung in their neat rows, and in every single one—at every table, in every parlor, at every wedding and funeral and county fair across sixty years of Halloran life—there was the chair. Empty. Waiting. Carved along its backrest with a spiral pattern Orsen knew better than their own signature, because it matched the birthmark coiled on their wrist like a thread tied off.

They had thought the chair was a summons. A threat. Something coming *for* them.

Sitting there in grandma's kitchen, with the ledger open to their own name, Orsen understood it differently. The chair wasn't waiting to be filled.

It had been theirs the whole time. Reserved. Inherited. Every Halloran who paid into the well had been keeping it warm, generation after generation, and grandma—the last keeper—had spent her whole life making payments to postpone the day the account came due.

She'd died the night the chair first appeared. Orsen had checked the dates twice now, and both times the arithmetic had come out the same. She hadn't died and *then* the chair showed up.

The chair showing up was what killed her. The bill had come due early, and she'd refused to pay it, and the well had collected the only thing she had left to give.

Her being remembered.

That was why Orsen couldn't cry properly. That was why the grief felt secondhand, like mourning a stranger described in a letter. Somewhere in the last month, the well had already begun taking her—not her body, which lay in the ground past the tree line, but her weight in the world. Orsen could feel it: trying to picture grandma's face and finding it soft-focus, trying to remember her voice and getting only the shape of it, the rhythm, like a song hummed by someone who'd forgotten the tune.

They were eating her right now. Page by page. Memory by memory.

Orsen closed the ledger, and their hands were shaking, and the shaking wasn't fear anymore.

"Sixty years," they said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the photographs, to whatever listened from beneath the dry ground. "You let her pay for sixty years, and then you took her anyway."

The house said nothing. But the pressure in the room changed, subtle as a drop in altitude, and Orsen had spent enough years in crawlspaces and cellars to know what that meant. Something had heard. Something was pleased, or curious, or hungry—it didn't matter which. Attention had weight, and Orsen could feel it settle on them like snowfall.

They pried up the rest of the bundle from the floor cavity. Oilcloth, and inside it a mason jar of dark water—water that shouldn't exist, drawn from a well that had been dry for a century—and folded around the jar, a page torn from a book much older than the ledger. Latin, mostly. Diagrams of a circle of chairs. And at the bottom, in a different hand, a scrawled note in English:

*Seven steps. I broke the seventh so it could never be finished. God forgive me. Find it or make a new one. — E.C., 1849*

E.C. Edith Crane? Elias Crane? The mayor's surname sat in Orsen's chest like a swallowed stone.

Outside, full dark had come. And down the road, toward the center of town, Orsen could see lights on at the Crane house—every light, blazing, as if Aldous Crane were also awake at this hour, also unable to sleep, also standing at his window looking toward the Halloran place.

Or toward the well behind it.

Orsen wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth, tucked it under their arm, and picked up the crowbar—not to put it away.

Tomorrow they'd go back to the archive. Tomorrow they'd find out which founder's blood ran in their veins, and what E.C. broke, and why the seventh step mattered enough to damn a family for a hundred and seventy years.

Tonight, they walked out through the kitchen, past the table, and paused at the doorway where the photographs caught the moonlight.

In the nearest frame—grandma at twenty, laughing with sisters Orsen had never heard mentioned—the extra chair was no longer empty.

Something sat in it. Small. Still. Shaped almost like a child, if a child were carved from the same pale wood as the chair itself.

It raised its head.

Orsen turned off the kitchen light and walked out into the dark, and did not run, because some part of them understood now that running had never once been on the table.

The well had chosen its heir long ago. All that remained was to learn what it had chosen them *for*.
