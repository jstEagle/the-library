## 9. The Seventh Step

The water let go of Orsen the way a hand lets go of something it has decided to keep.

One moment they were beneath the flood, in the green-black hush where the entity wore the faces of everyone Hollow Creek had ever forgotten, and the next they broke the surface gasping, coughing black water into air that tasted of iron and cut hay. The festival grounds had become a lake. Strings of harvest lights still burned above the waterline, their reflections shivering below like drowned stars. Somewhere a fiddle was playing, untouched by the flood, playing a tune no one had written.

Orsen's hands found the roof of the well-house. The old stone structure had half-collapsed in the first surge, and now its remaining wall rose out of the flood like a rib. They dragged themselves onto it, shaking, and pressed the ledger to their chest. Grandma's ledger. Waterlogged but whole. And inside its binding, stitched into the spine beneath sixty years of crossed-out names, the seventh step:

*The seat is not given. It is taken, freely, by one who refuses to keep it.*

Orsen had read those words underwater while faces made of other people's memories smiled at them with grandma's mouth. *It eats being remembered,* she had said—or the thing wearing her had said it, or her actual voice had come up through the dark like a rope thrown down a well. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that Orsen finally understood what the founders had built in 1847, and what they had gotten wrong.

They had built a cage and called it a bargain. They had paid it in other people—paid it in names, in photographs, in neighbors who had never been born—and they had called that mercy because the payments kept the town alive. Six chairs around a table, six lines of descent, each generation passing down the debt like an heirloom nobody wanted but everybody kept polishing.

And every one of them had hoarded the truth. Kept it in ledgers under floorboards. Kept it behind librarian's warnings and mayor's smiles. Kept it so deep that when the bill came due for Orsen Halloran, not one living soul would say their name aloud and tell them why.

The seventh step wasn't a step at all. It was a door, and it only opened from the inside.

* * *

The water was rising still. Orsen could feel it climbing the stones beneath them, patient as arithmetic. Across the flooded square, the erased ones walked among the crowd—waist-deep now, unhurried, their faces smooth as eggs where memory should have been. The townsfolk looked straight through them. A woman holding a child that had never been born waded past the cider stall, and the man selling cider nodded at her, pleasant, empty, his eyes sliding off her like rain off wax.

And there was Crane.

The mayor stood on the steps of the meetinghouse, dry somehow, impossibly dry, in his good suit with the flag pin, shouting over the water that everything was fine. That the creek had crested early this year. That the festival would continue. People clung to the words the way drowning men cling to driftwood, because the alternative was to look at the water and see what was standing in it.

"Crane!" Orsen's voice cracked across the square.

He turned. Even from here, even through the rain beginning to fall, Orsen could see the calculation move behind his eyes—the quick weighing of whether acknowledging them cost more than ignoring them.

"You knew," Orsen shouted. "You've known your whole life. Your line signed first."

Something flickered across Crane's face—not guilt. Recognition of a debt he'd decided long ago wasn't his.

"The ritual needs six seats," he called back, and his voice was almost gentle, the voice of a man explaining taxes to a child. "Six lines. Yours ran dry when your grandmother died. There's nothing to be done, Halloran. Go home. Let the town do what it's always done."

"Which is lie?"

"Which is *survive.*" He spread his hands, magnanimous, a mayor to the last. "You think I wanted this? You think any of us wanted it? But the bargain holds, and the bargain is all that stands between Hollow Creek and—" he gestured at the flood, at the faceless walkers, at the fiddle playing its dead tune "—and *that*. One name a generation. One photograph. It's a small price."

"It's not a price if you make someone else pay it."

"That's exactly what a price *is.*"

The water surged. Orsen felt the well-house shudder beneath them, felt the thing below turn its attention upward like a lighthouse beam. And in that moment they understood Crane completely—not as a villain, but as a man standing hip-deep in a system he'd never once questioned, bailing with both hands, furious at anyone who suggested bailing wasn't the point.

Power corrupts when you hoard it. Crane had hoarded the truth for forty years, and the truth had rotted in him into something that looked like leadership and smelled like fear.

Orsen stood up on the ruined wall, the ledger open against their forearm, rain streaming down their face.

"Then watch," they said. "Watch what happens when nobody pays."

* * *

The seventh chair waited in the flooded meetinghouse.

Orsen waded through chest-high water between the pews, the ledger held overhead, following the pull in their wrist—that birthmark shaped like a wheat sheaf, burning cold now, pointing forward like a compass needle that had finally found its north. The doors of the meetinghouse stood open. Inside, arranged in a circle on the raised dais, seven chairs sat in the rising water: six old oak thrones carved with wheat sheaves, and between them, at the head of the circle, the phantom chair from the photographs. Grandma's table had been a rehearsal. This was the original.

The water reached Orsen's chin as they climbed the dais. Below, through the windows, they could see the crowd gathered on rooftops and truck beds, watching the meetinghouse light spill gold across the flood. Watching without understanding. Maren was there—Orsen found her face in the crowd, found her looking back, and Maren raised one hand. Not waving. Saluting. Keeper of records, bearing witness to the one entry no ledger would hold.

Orsen opened the binding one last time. The seventh step, in grandma's cramped hand, stitched where no one would ever think to look:

*A seat kept is a debt renewed. A seat refused is a door opened. Only the one who takes the chair and wants nothing from it closes the circle—for the thing below feeds on being remembered, and cannot eat a person who gives their memory away.*

Not sacrifice. Sacrifice was what the founders had called it, to make it sound noble, to make it sound like the victim's choice. This was simpler and worse and better: Orsen would sit down, and claim the seat, and give away every memory anyone held of them. Every photograph. Every recollection. Every trace. Not erased *by* the thing—erased *for* the town, handed over willingly, spent like currency until nothing remained but the chair itself, warm, waiting, permanent.

The trade was total. The trade was fair. And unlike Crane, unlike six generations of founders, Orsen would not keep anything back—not a secret, not a title, not a single grain of power.

"I don't want it," Orsen said aloud, to the circle, to the water, to whatever listened beneath. "I never wanted it. That's the point. That's always been the point."

They sat down.

* * *

The world went white and then went quiet.

Orsen felt it happen the way you feel a tooth come loose—a small wrongness, then release. Memories didn't vanish; they *drained*, flowing out of them like warmth out of an open hand. Grandma's kitchen. The smell of the ledger's paper. Twelve years of rational explanations cracking one by one. Their own name, said by their own mother, in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but here, in the last second of its existing.

Below the floor, something vast turned over in its sleep and did not find them. Could not find them. You cannot remember a meal you were never served.

The water began to fall.

It went all at once, the way a fever breaks—rivers running backward into the well, streets draining, the faceless walkers dissolving mid-stride into the ordinary dark. On the meetinghouse steps, Aldous Crane was still shouting that everything was fine, that the creek had crested, that the festival would continue—and then the well-house, its purpose ended, its bargain void, gave up the pretense of standing. The stones folded inward like a closing hand. Crane went down with it, suit and flag pin and all, and the last thing he said—witnesses swore to it later, though none of them could say why they remembered—was:

"There's nothing wrong here. There has *never* been anything wrong here."

The water closed over him gently, almost kindly, the way the town had closed over every truth for a hundred and seventy years.

* * *

Morning came to Hollow Creek clean and ordinary.

The mud dried. The fiddle player couldn't remember what song he'd played. The townsfolk woke in their beds with the vague, pleasant ache of a night they'd drunk their way through, and by noon the flood was a story about weather, and by evening it wasn't even that.

In the meetinghouse, six chairs stood in a circle, dusted and proud, awaiting a founding day celebration no one would schedule. No one counted seven. Counting had never once occurred to anyone.

And in a vacant house on the edge of town, in a kitchen where a family had lived and paid and died, a wooden chair sat at the table—carved with wheat sheaves, warm as if just vacated, waiting for someone who would never come, belonging to someone no one could name.

Somewhere, a keeper of records was about to open a box of photographs.

And find the one that didn't add up.
