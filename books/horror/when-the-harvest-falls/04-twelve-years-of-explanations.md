## 4. Twelve Years of Explanations

The scratching started the night after the first descent.

Marguerite had told the story so many times—to themselves, mostly, in the dark of their apartment with the radiator ticking—that it had worn smooth as river stone. Nineteen years old, a borrowed headlamp, a gap in the foundation wall behind the old Harrow cannery that no survey acknowledged. They'd gone down on a dare they'd given themselves. They'd come back up three hours later with a notebook full of measurements and a conviction that would eat the next twelve years: *nobody has mapped this. Nobody knows it's here. I will be the one who knows.*

They had never included the part about the sound.

Now, sitting at their kitchen table at 2 a.m. with the county's official tunnel surveys spread out beside twelve years of their own hand-drawn maps, Marguerite found themselves listening to the walls again. The apartment sat two blocks from the cannery lot. Close enough. Close enough for twelve years.

*Water in the pipes,* they'd told themselves, the first night. *Old buildings settle. Rats.*

The scratching came again—three strokes, a pause, three strokes—and stopped, as if it had heard them thinking.

Marguerite put both hands flat on the table and made themselves look at the maps instead.

* * *

The first descent, October, nineteen years old:

The tunnel behind the cannery foundation was brick, then cut stone, then something older that didn't have a name—a dark grainy rock that swallowed the headlamp beam instead of returning it. Marguerite had crawled through on elbows and knees, laughing a little, high on the smell of wet earth and the pure adolescent joy of being somewhere forbidden.

Then the laughter died, because the corridor kept going when the map in their head said it should end, and the air changed temperature, and somewhere ahead—below?—something moved.

Not moved like water moves. Moved like a body shifts its weight.

Marguerite had stood very still in the dark and listened, and what they heard was faint, rhythmic, patient: a soft dragging rasp against stone. Like fingernails. Like someone dragging fingernails down the inside of a wall, slowly, without urgency, the way you might knock on a door you knew would eventually open.

They had told themselves: *it's the water table shifting through gravel.*

They had written it in the notebook that same night, in their own handwriting, in ink: *Auditory phenomena consistent with hydrology.* Twelve words of deliberate fiction, dated and preserved, the first entry in an archive of lies.

And then—they could see it now, could see it with terrible adult clarity—they had gone back the next weekend. And the next.

* * *

At the kitchen table, Marguerite pulled the oldest map toward them. Survey One, drawn at twenty, ink still sharp. Every corridor measured by knotted string and counted paces. It was good work. It was obsessive, meticulous, beautiful work, and it had won them a small grant at twenty-three and a smaller reputation ever since, the local eccentric who mapped the under-Harrow nobody official cared about.

There was a corridor on this map—the long western run beneath the cannery lot—where they had noted, in the margin, in pencil, faded now to gray: *acoustic anomaly, recurring, source unidentified.*

Source unidentified.

Marguerite pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes.

The truth was that they had identified the source within six months. Maybe sooner. Maybe the first night. The truth was that *source unidentified* was a sentence constructed the way a dam is constructed, and behind it, all these years, the water had been rising.

* * *

Second year underground. Twenty years old, and already the tunnels were theirs in the way a secret becomes yours—by the weight of keeping it.

That winter they'd found the alcove.

It was off the eastern spur, behind a fall of rubble they'd cleared over three visits. A sealed niche in the older stone, mortared shut, and the mortar was wrong—not poured, not troweled, but packed by hand, hastily, in layers, the way you'd pack a wound. And low in the mortar, at knee height, there was a depression. A shape. Five fingers' width across, pushed outward from the inside, the plaster stretched thin as skin over a knuckle.

Marguerite had stood in front of it for a long time. Long enough for the headlamp to dim. Long enough to hear, from somewhere beyond the mortar, close, so close, a sound like breathing that wasn't breathing.

Then they had marked it on the map as *structural anomaly — void, filled* and climbed back into the daylight and eaten a sandwich on the cannery loading dock and gone home and slept eight hours.

They remembered sleeping well. That was the detail that undid them now, twenty-six years old in their kitchen at 3 a.m., remembering how *well* they had slept. How they had learned, without ever admitting learning it, to set the sounds outside the border of things that required response. There was a country called Explanation, and Marguerite had been its most diligent cartographer, and every rationalization was a road on the map, and every road led away.

*Rats,* when the scratching followed them along the western run, matching their pace, stopping when they stopped.

*Settling stone,* when something knocked twice from inside the alcove wall the day after the county crew first surveyed the cannery lot—knocked twice, distinctly, deliberately, like a question.

*My own pulse, echoed,* when the whispering started, third year, a sibilance just below intelligibility that seemed to try syllables and discard them, the way a child learns language.

Marguerite had stood in that corridor at twenty-two with their ear against the stone and heard a voice almost form their name—*Mar-gue-*—and had stepped back, and checked their watch, and noted the acoustic anomaly, and left.

They had come back the following week. Of course they had. That was the thing about the country of Explanation: you could only live there if you kept importing supplies, and the supplies were visits, and the visits were the whole point, weren't they? The thrill. The secret. Being the one who knew. Marguerite had spent twelve years telling themselves they went down for the mapping, for the science, for the record—but the record was locked in a fire box under their bed where no one would ever see it, and they knew that, and they went anyway, again and again, drawn down into the dark toward the thing that whispered, the way you press a bruise to check if it still hurts.

It always hurt. They always went back.

* * *

Fourth year. The night they'd brought a recorder.

This was the entry they'd never let themselves reread. Marguerite dug the old cassette out of the fire box now, because the excavation crew was coming Monday and the ground above their corridor was going to be opened whether they confessed anything or not, and some part of them understood, with the cold administrative clarity of 4 a.m., that the time for explanations was ending.

The tape hissed. Then: their own younger voice, breathless, narrating. *"Approaching the western junction. Acoustic activity audible. Recording."*

Forty seconds of silence. Then the sound.

Marguerite had listened to this tape exactly once before, at twenty-four, alone in this same kitchen, and had labeled it *unidentified acoustic phenomena* and put it away. Now they played it through headphones in the dark and heard what they had chosen not to hear.

It was a voice. Not a whisper, not wind, not water. A voice, thin and rusted from disuse, saying the same phrase over and over, and the phrase was: *"—still here, we're still here, we're still here—"*

And underneath it, fainter, more of them. Dozens. A chorus worn down to a monotone, reciting their own existence like a proof, like a prayer, like people who had been told no one was coming and refused to stop announcing themselves anyway.

Young Marguerite, on the tape, after ninety seconds of this: *"Acoustic phenomena persists. Source unidentified. Returning to surface."*

A pause. Then, quieter, almost tender, almost amused, the voice of a person falling in love with their own secret:

*"God, this place is incredible."*

Marguerite stopped the tape.

* * *

They sat in the dark kitchen and did the arithmetic they had spent twelve years refusing to do.

Every noise had an explanation. That had been the rule, the discipline, the craft. Water, rats, settling stone, pipes, pigeons in the flue, the neighbor's dog, the building's bones. And every explanation had been available *before* they needed it—arriving pre-made, fully formed, the moment the sound occurred, as if the explanations had been waiting in stock. As if some clerk deep in Marguerite's mind kept a shelf of them, dusted and ready, restocking nightly.

Because that's what complicity is. Not the act. The supply chain.

They thought of the alcove with the hand-shaped depression. They had gone back to it eleven times over the years. Eleven documented visits. Each time the depression was a little deeper, the mortar around it cracked a little further, and each time Marguerite had measured it, photographed it, logged it as *progressive structural degradation*, and each time they had crouched there in the dark knowing—knowing the way you know your own name—that on the other side of that mortar was a hand, attached to an arm, attached to a person, and that the person had been there longer than Marguerite had been alive, and that Marguerite's flashlight was probably the only light it had ever known.

On visit nine, something had changed. The depression had fingerprints now. Full prints, pressed hard, holding, the way you hold when you're trying to keep from being pulled back.

Marguerite had taken the photograph. It was in the fire box. In the photograph, if you looked correctly—and they had looked correctly, once, at twenty-five, and never again—you could see that the fingers were not pushing through the mortar.

They were gripping it. From the inside. Holding on.

*We're still here.*

Marguerite got up and turned on every light in the apartment, which did nothing, because the scratching wasn't in the walls of the apartment. It had never been in the walls of the apartment. It was in the walls of the world, and Marguerite had spent twelve years standing on the outside of those walls taking notes while something on the inside spelled out its situation letter by letter, patiently, hopefully, addressing its only correspondent.

And the correspondent had filed it all under *hydrology.*

"I'm sorry," Marguerite said aloud, to the wall, to the dark past the wall, and their voice cracked in the middle like a kid's, and the silence that answered was worse than any sound, because it was the silence of something that had stopped expecting apologies.

* * *

Dawn came up gray over the cannery district. Through the kitchen window Marguerite could see the lot itself, fenced now, orange cones scattered across the broken asphalt like pills spilled from a bottle. The county notice stapled to the fence read EXCAVATION — HAZMAT PRECAUTIONS ADVISORY — QUESTIONS: OFFICE OF PUBLIC WORKS. Beneath it, someone had already taped a flyer for a missing cat.

Monday, the machines would come. They would bite forty feet down into the lot directly above the western run—directly above the alcove, if the geometry held, and Marguerite's geometry always held—and they would find whatever was in there, and it would not matter anymore what Marguerite had explained or failed to explain. The ground was going to give its answer regardless.

Unless.

Marguerite looked at the maps spread across the table—twelve years of work, the only complete record of the under-Harrow, the thing that had made them special—and felt, for the first time, not pride but nausea. Because there was a use for these maps besides science. There was a use the founders of this town had apparently foreseen, if the old stories were true, if the door in the deep stone was real, if the thing the whispers circled around without naming had a name and a place and a lock.

If someone needed to go down—one last time, before the machines—to reach whatever had to be reached.

The maps would show them how.

Marguerite gathered the surveys into order, oldest to newest, and slid them into the fire box, and their hands were steady, and that steadiness frightened them more than the scratching did, because it was the same steadiness they'd felt at nineteen, at twenty-two, at twenty-five, every time they chose the explanation over the truth.

Outside, the first truck of the excavation crew turned onto Cannery Row, early, ahead of schedule, its beacon sweeping the gray morning like a slow searchlight looking for something specific.

Marguerite grabbed their coat.
