## 2. Wren on the Porch

The sun was coming up the wrong way.

That was Dominic's first thought, standing at the mouth of the storm drain with mud to the elbows and a headlamp dying against their chest — that the light was arriving from beneath them, seeping up out of the ground instead of spilling down from the sky. They knew it wasn't true. They knew the eastern ridge caught first light over Harrow Creek the way it had every morning of their thirty-four years, and that what they were seeing was exhaustion bending the world at its seams. But knowing a thing and believing it had always been two different rooms in Dominic's house, and this morning the door between them stood open.

They climbed out of the culvert on hands and knees, and the grass along the drainage easement was stiff with cold, each blade furred white at the tip though the forecast had promised nothing but rain. Frost in October. Early even for this town, where autumn came down off the hills like something with an appointment to keep. Dominic crouched and touched one whitened blade and the cold bit through their glove like a small deliberate teeth.

*Later,* they told themselves. *Document it later.* The camera bag hung heavy on their shoulder, forty pounds of evidence they hadn't finished collecting, couldn't finish collecting, because the tunnel had kept going after the survey said it should have ended, and the tally marks had kept going after sense said they should have stopped, and somewhere around hour six Dominic's instruments had begun disagreeing with each other about which way was down.

They walked home through streets still blue with pre-dawn. Nobody else was out. A dog started barking three houses before Dominic passed it and stopped the moment they were by, as if it had been barking at something behind them and lost interest when the thing behind them did whatever it did. Dominic didn't look back. Twenty-five years of professional habit said *always look back*, and for the first time in their career they overrode it, because some animal part of them understood that looking back would be an invitation, and they were not yet ready to learn what would accept it.

Their house sat at the end of Pell Street, a narrow gray clapboard thing inherited from a grandmother who had left it to them along with everything else she'd owned: furniture, photographs, a cellar full of preserves in jars so old the labels had gone the color of tea. Dominic had lived in it for nine years now and loved it the way you love a coat that fits — without thinking about it much.

Then they rounded the hedge, and stopped.

Someone was sitting on the porch steps.

* * *

The porch light was off. It was always off; the bulb had burned out in spring and Dominic kept forgetting to replace it, another entry in the long ledger of domestic neglect. So there was no light to see by except the sky going pale behind the water tower, and that was enough. That was more than enough.

The figure sat on the third step from the bottom, knees drawn up, chin resting on folded arms, facing the street. Facing Dominic. Small. Maybe four feet tall if it stood, though it never stood, it only ever sat or crouched or perched, in the way that certain things perch. It wore a shape Dominic's body recognized before their mind could object: the too-long arms, the bird-thin shoulders, the head tilted at an angle no neck should permit without complaint. Its skin, what the half-light showed of it, was the color of birch bark. Its eyes were dark and wet and patient as the eyes of things that wait under logs.

Dominic knew it. That was the horror of it, arriving ahead of any other feeling, ahead of fear itself: *I know you.*

"Wren," they said. Their voice came out cracked, unused for hours, scraped raw by tunnel air. Saying the name aloud felt like pressing on a bruise they'd forgotten they had and finding it still tender after all this time. "Wren."

The figure did not move. Not a blink, not a breath — though the morning air was cold enough now to show breath, and Wren's made none. It simply sat, the way it had sat on the footboard of Dominic's bed at age seven, the way it had sat in the crook of the crabapple tree while Dominic read aloud to it from books about caves and maps and the men who drew them. Patient as furniture. Patient as the porch step it occupied, as though it and the step had been milled from the same wood, installed on the same day, by the same indifferent builder.

"You're not real," Dominic said. The words tasted like a prayer recited backward. "You were never real. I made you up. I was a lonely kid and I made you up and then I grew up and I put you away, that's all, that is *all*—"

Wren tilted its head the other way. The motion was slow and fluid and wrong, like watching a plant move in time-lapse, like watching something that had learned motion secondhand and never quite gotten comfortable in it. And then it spoke, and its voice was exactly as Dominic remembered — high and dry and slightly sanded, a voice like wind through a keyhole — and twenty-five years collapsed between one syllable and the next.

"You found the door."

Four words. Dominic's knees nearly went. They gripped the gatepost, iron cold through their glove, and stared, and the staring did not help, because staring confirmed it: Wren was unchanged. Not aged, not weathered, not softened by a quarter century of being remembered less and less until memory wore through entirely. Children's imaginary friends were supposed to die of neglect. That was the natural order. You outgrew them the way you outgrew shoes, and they went wherever outgrown things went, and you forgot them so thoroughly that meeting them again would be impossible — not improbable, *impossible*, because there would be nothing left to meet.

But here Wren sat, whole and exact, down to the small tear in the left knee of its shapeless gray clothes that Dominic had given it at age eight because Wren had asked for one, had wanted to look like someone who'd been somewhere. Down to the way its fingers were too many-jointed, folding back on themselves like heron legs. Down to the smell — and God, the smell reached Dominic across ten feet of frosted lawn, faint but unmistakable, cold stone and turned earth and something sweet underneath, something like apples going soft in a cellar.

It smelled like the tunnel.

Dominic's stomach dropped through the morning. "You were down there," they said. "In the drain. With the marks on the walls." Their own voice sounded far away, a recording played in another room. "Those tallies. Did you make those?"

Wren said nothing. It only looked at them, and its looking had weight, had texture, like standing in shallow water that wants to pull you deeper. Behind it, the house waited with its dead porch light and its dark windows, and Dominic had the sudden vertiginous certainty that the windows were not reflecting the brightening sky correctly — that the glass held a different dawn than the one happening above the water tower, grayer, colder, older.

"I buried you," Dominic whispered. It came out before they could stop it, a confession to a priest who shouldn't exist. "When I was nine. I dug a hole behind the shed and I put my drawing of you in it and I said the words — I don't even remember what words, some kid liturgy, *you're done now, go away now* — and I never drew you again. I never said your name again. Until just now. Just now, for the first time in twenty-five years."

Something moved in Wren's face. Not an expression. An adjustment — the way a mask adjusts when the wearer shifts it a fraction of an inch. And for one terrible flickering moment Dominic saw that the face beneath the face was not a child's invention at all, was not anything invented, was old and smooth and attentive in the way of things that have been listening for a very long time.

Then it was Wren again. Bird-boned, birch-skinned, beloved. Worse, somehow, that it could do both.

"You found the door," Wren repeated, and this time the words landed differently, and Dominic heard what lived inside them: not praise. Pity. The tone of someone watching a neighbor walk confidently toward thin ice. "You weren't supposed to find it yet. But you always were quick with maps."

"What door?" Dominic's voice climbed. "What are you? What's down there, Wren? Because I measured that tunnel. I measured it three times with three instruments and it doesn't end where it ends. There's no record of it. No survey, no utility map, nothing. A mile of carved rock under a town that doesn't know it's there, covered in counting marks older than my grandmother, and now you're on my porch telling me I found a *door*—"

"Dom." Wren said their name — the childhood diminutive, the one nobody living used anymore, the one that existed only inside a nine-year-old's head — and the sound of it went through Dominic like a nail through paper. Gentle. Devastating. "Go inside. Lock the door. It won't matter, but do it anyway. Sleep if you can. When you wake up, look at your grandmother's things. She kept a ledger. She was careful with it. Careful people leave gaps, and gaps are where the truth keeps its coat."

Dominic's mouth opened. Closed. Every question they had queued up — and there were dozens, a career's worth, a lifetime's worth — jammed together behind their teeth and none of them would pass.

"How do you know about her ledger?" they managed finally. "Wren. How do you know my grandmother kept a ledger?"

Wren unfolded from the step. It rose the way smoke rises off ice, without effort, without quite using legs, and stood on the frosted lawn between Dominic and the gate, and up close its eyes were worse than dark — they were deep, they were the color the tunnel went at hour six when the headlamp failed and the dark stopped being an absence and became a substance with opinions.

"It knows what it's owed," Wren said softly. "And it sent me to tell you: the counting has started again. You saw the marks. Count them again when your hands stop shaking. Count them *carefully*." A pause, and in the pause the frost crept visibly across the grass toward Dominic's boots, blade by blade, like a tide deciding. "You have until the frost finishes writing."

"Frost finishes writing—" Dominic took a step forward despite every instinct screaming otherwise. "Wren, wait. Wait. Are you — are you still *mine*? Are you still — him? The friend I made? Is any of you—"

Wren smiled.

It was the smile Dominic had drawn a hundred times in crayon, gap-toothed and lopsided and kind, and it broke their heart clean in half to see it rendered in flesh that shouldn't exist, on a face that was wearing it like a borrowed coat over something vast and patient and agricultural, something that thought in seasons.

"Go inside, Dom," Wren said. "Eat something. Check the ledger. And Dom—" the smile faded, and what remained was almost tender, almost sorry, "—don't photograph me. It doesn't like being documented. And right now, you need it to keep liking you."

Then the light changed — the sun finally clearing the ridge, throwing long gold across Pell Street — and the spot where Wren had stood was empty. Not departed. *Empty*, the way a word is empty after being erased, the grass unmarked, the frost undisturbed, as though the figure had been subtracted rather than removed.

Dominic stood alone in their own front yard, shaking, camera bag heavy on their shoulder, and understood with total clarity that they were not going to sleep.

* * *

Inside, they did everything wrong, and knew it while doing it. They locked the door. They checked the windows. They put the kettle on because their hands needed a task, and while it heated they stood at the kitchen sink and watched the yard through the glass, and Wren was not there, and then — between one blink and the next, no transition, no movement, no arrival — Wren *was* there, seated cross-legged on the flat top of the stone wall at the property's edge, facing the house, hands folded, patient as furniture.

Domini did not scream. They set down the kettle with a hand that trembled only a little, and they picked up the field camera instead, because documentation was the load-bearing wall of their entire self, and Wren had told them not to, and some contrary engine in their chest said *that is exactly why we must.*

They raised the camera. Framed the wall. Wren sat in the viewfinder, small and gray and waiting.

Dominic pressed the shutter.

The mirror slapped up. The shutter fired. And in the instant between the two sounds, Wren looked directly into the lens — into *them* — and shook its head once, slowly, with an expression of such profound and gentle sorrow that Dominic lowered the camera without meaning to, the way you lower your voice at a funeral.

When they raised it again, the wall was empty.

The kettle screamed behind them. Dominic let it. They stood in their kitchen in the full gold of morning, in the house their grandmother had left them, and looked down at the camera's little screen, where the photograph should have been.

The frame was not empty. That was the worst part. The frame showed the wall, the frost, the crabapple tree gone rust-red at the edges — and on the wall, where Wren had sat, a faint pale mark like a handprint in condensation, already fading even as the screen refreshed.

And beneath it, burned into the image in letters of frost-fern white, small and neat and unmistakable, five words:

**COUNT THE MARKS AGAIN, DOMINIC.**

Outside, the last of the night's frost was melting off the grass. But on the north-facing stones of the well cap, in the shade where the sun wouldn't reach all day, new white lines were spreading — fine, branching, deliberate — and they were not melting at all.

They were spelling something. And they had all day to finish.
