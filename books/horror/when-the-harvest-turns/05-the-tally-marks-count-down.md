## 5. The Tally Marks Count Down

Sleep didn't come so much as it was interrupted, over and over, by the sound of a screen door that hadn't opened. By morning I'd stopped pretending I was going to rest. The light through the blinds was the gray of old dishwater, and Wren was still in the yard.

They sat on the flat rock by the fence line where I used to leave them when we played — a game of stations, of sentries, of *you wait here and watch for bad things*. Twenty-five years, and they sat exactly the way nine-year-old me had posed them: knees drawn up, chin on knees, face tilted toward the house. Patient as furniture. Patient as something that had been built to wait.

I made coffee I didn't drink. I stood at the kitchen window with my hands around a mug going cold and watched them not move, and somewhere in the third minute I understood that watching them was its own kind of documentation — that some part of me was already timestamping this, already filing it under *evidence of*, because that was the only way I knew how to hold a thing without it sliding through me.

The tunnels were still there. That was the thought that finally got me moving. Whatever was wrong with my porch, the tunnels were measurable. The tunnels had walls and water and tally marks cut into limestone, and tally marks could be counted, and counting was the closest thing to prayer I trusted.

* * *

The storm drain mouth was a half-mile from my house, down where Kettle Creek went underground beneath the old rail spur. I'd found it eleven days ago with a headlamp and a surveyor's instinct, following a concrete throat that every map in the county said should dead-end after four hundred feet. It didn't dead-end. It never dead-ended. It went down and back and down again, through hand-cut sections older than the town's charter, and at the junction where the concrete gave way to stone, someone had carved marks into the wall — five-bar gates, hundreds of them, thousands, marching into the dark like a fence line buried alive.

I'd photographed all of it. Eleven days ago the tallies had been a curiosity. A folk habit. Miners' marks, maybe, or kids with too much time and a chisel.

Today the air coming out of the drain mouth was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. October air doesn't come out of the ground warm. It came up against my face carrying a smell I couldn't place at first — green, vegetal, the smell of a greenhouse or a root cellar full of things still growing — and underneath it, faintly, the iron-and-copper note I'd learned to read as *old blood* even though I'd never actually smelled old blood in my life. Some knowledge you're born with. It's how the body votes before the mind has counted.

I stood at the mouth and did what I always do: I checked my gear out loud, recording it. "Headlamp, primary. Headlamp, backup. Batteries, six. Camera, charged. Notebook." I patted the notebook in my chest pocket. "Notebook."

From the yard behind me, half a mile away across the fields, I felt rather than heard Wren acknowledge this. There was no acknowledgment. That was the point. They simply existed in the direction of my house, the way a compass needle exists toward north.

I went in.

* * *

The concrete section was unchanged. Water ran in its channel, shallow and clear, making the small talk water makes. My boots rang on the invert. Four hundred feet, and the survey's promised dead-end arrived right on schedule — a poured-concrete bulkhead, rusted rebar showing through the cracks — except that the gap at its base was wide enough for a person who didn't mind crawling, and I'd minded, eleven days ago, and crawled anyway.

On the far side, the world changed materials. Cut limestone, blocks fitted without mortar, the ceiling low enough that I stooped. My lamp threw shadows that moved a half-second behind my hands. I told myself that was an artifact of the light, of wet stone, of my own tired eyes. I have a whole shelf in my mental library for explanations like that. Lately the shelf has been sagging.

The tally wall came up on my left at the first junction, and I stopped so fast my lamp swung and the dark lurched.

There were new marks.

Eleven days ago I had documented this wall exhaustively. Three hundred and forty-one five-bar gates in the visible section, the oldest blackened nearly to the color of the stone itself, the newest — I'd estimated — decades old, their edges softened by mineral film. I had a spreadsheet. I had forty-seven photographs with scale references. I could have testified in court about the condition of this wall.

Now there were fresh cuts beside the old ones, and they were wet.

Not damp. Wet. Glistening, as though the stone had been carved minutes ago and was still bleeding groundwater from the wound. The new marks continued the sequence without a break — the same hand-width spacing, the same slight downward slant on the fifth stroke that closed each gate — but they were pale where the old ones were dark, raw where the old ones were healed. And there were a lot of them.

I counted. Of course I counted. My hands shook and I counted anyway, touching each fresh gate with a gloved fingertip, and the number I arrived at was nineteen. Nineteen new five-bar gates, carved into stone that had been untouched for longer than I'd been alive, sometime in the last eleven days.

Nineteen. I turned the number over. Nineteen days until — what? I did the date math standing in the dark with my heart going hard against my ribs. Nineteen days ago would have put the first mark at roughly the day I entered the tunnel the first time. But these weren't nineteen days of marks. These were nineteen marks, and if they were counting *down* —

I photographed the wall. In the viewfinder, the fresh marks looked almost luminous, wet stone catching my lamp like the inside of a shell. Then I did the thing I will be thinking about for the rest of whatever life I have left: I touched the most recent gate, the one whose fifth stroke was still glistening, and I held my finger there, and I waited to see if it would finish drying.

It didn't dry. While I stood there, the cut *deepened*. Not much. A hair's width. The stone parted along the groove with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard, a sound like a stitch being drawn tight, and a bead of water welled up along the fresh edge and ran down the wall in a line that was almost, almost straight.

The twentieth mark was finishing itself while I watched.

I backed away. I'm not ashamed of that. I backed away with my lamp shaking and my breath loud in the low ceiling, and I did the coward's arithmetic out loud, because saying numbers makes them behave: "If one per day, and twenty is today, then —" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Then the count ends in —"

I didn't say the number. Saying it felt like signing something.

* * *

I told myself I was retreating to document. That's the story I filed, anyway: *subject withdrew to surface to review photographic evidence.* The truth is that my legs decided, and my mind followed them down the tunnel drafting justifications, which is more or less how my mind has operated since the porch.

But the tunnel didn't take me back the way I'd come.

I know how that sounds. I've spent twenty-five years training myself against exactly this kind of sentence — the sentence that begins with the narrator's certainty and ends somewhere impossible. But I mapped this junction on my first visit. I have the sketch. The return route runs south-southwest, past the seep wall, to the crawl gap under the bulkhead. What I walked instead was a passage running level and east, wider than any section I'd seen, its floor dry and dusted with something pale and fine that swirled around my boots like flour.

My compass agreed with my body and disagreed with my memory. South-southwest, the needle said, steady as ever. The passage kept going east. I know it kept going east because the passage had begun to slope gently downward, and water runs downhill, and the water in the channel beside me — there was no channel beside me. There was no water anywhere now. Just the pale dust, and the silence, and the growing sense that the tunnel was not lost but *choosing*.

"Wren," I said. My voice came out flat and small in the dry air. "Wren, if this is you."

Nothing answered, which is its own kind of answer.

I walked another fifty feet because stopping felt worse, and that's when the light changed ahead of me — not brighter, but *warmer*, a brown-gold glow like lamplight through a barn wall, sourceless, pooling in the passage ahead. And standing in the near edge of it, close enough that I could see the stitching on their coat, the frayed cuff, the exact scuffed toe of the left boot that I had personally imagined into existence when I was seven years old and afraid of the space under my bed —

Wren.

Deep inside. Miles deep, by any honest measure of what I'd walked. Closer than the dark should allow. They weren't waiting at the tunnel mouth anymore. They were here, in the warm light, and they had been facing me the whole time, the way furniture faces a room.

"You shouldn't count them," Wren said.

Their voice hadn't aged either. It was the voice from the games, from the forts, from the long summer evenings when we patrolled the fence line together — a child's voice, level and unhurried, with something underneath it that had never belonged to a child. Something patient. Something that had been lent to them, the way you lend your coat to a scarecrow.

"The marks," I managed. "The new ones. Who's cutting them?"

"They cut themselves." Wren tilted their head, and the gesture was so familiar it hurt — the same tilt, the same angle, from a thousand remembered afternoons. "When the debt gets close, the stone remembers it's owed. You've seen frost do the same thing. Patterns that write themselves."

"How many?" I asked, and then, because Wren only looked at me: "How many are left?"

Wren was quiet for a moment. In the brown-gold light their face was smooth and unchanged and terribly kind, and I realized I was gripping my notebook so hard the cover had bent.

"Four," they said.

The word dropped through me like a stone through still water. Four marks left. Four days. And I thought of the weather report I'd skimmed that morning without reading, the one I'd scrolled past on my phone — *first hard freeze possible late week* — and the two facts clicked together in my chest with a sound like a lock throwing.

First frost. The count ended at first frost.

"What happens," I said, and my voice wasn't a documentarian's voice anymore, wasn't a professional's, wasn't even fully an adult's — it was the voice I'd had at nine, talking to the thing under the bed, negotiating. "What happens at the end of the count?"

Wren took one step closer, and the distance between us did not decrease by the amount of the step. It decreased by more. The dark behind them folded, politely, like paper.

"That's not mine to tell," they said. "I was only sent to walk you in."

"Sent by who?"

And Wren — my friend, my invented boy, my stitched-together sentinel who had waited twenty-five years on a flat rock by the fence — Wren looked past me, down the passage, into the warm dark, and their face did something I had never seen it do in any year of my childhood.

It flickered. Like a flame in a draft. Like a picture losing signal.

"Down," they said softly. "By the one who keeps what it's owed. Dominic —" and hearing my name in that voice, in this place, was worse than everything else combined — "the stone finishes the last four marks whether you're here to see them or not. But it would rather you saw."

Behind me, far back up the passage, faint through miles of stone that should have been silent, I heard a sound like a chisel drawing a single short line.

The twenty-first mark.

Three left.

Wren turned, unhurried, and walked into the gold-brown glow, and the glow received them the way soil receives a seed, and I stood alone in the dry dark with my bent notebook and my useless compass and the absolute, animal certainty that I had four days to learn what this town had been paying for — and to whom — before the frost came down off the hills and finished writing.
