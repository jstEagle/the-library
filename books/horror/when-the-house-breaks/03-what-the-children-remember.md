## 3. What the Children Remember

The trouble with Hollowmere, Silas decided over his third cup of the inn's aggressively strong tea, was that everyone wanted to talk to him and nobody wanted to say anything.

They came by in ones and twos. Mrs. Tilly from the post office, who stayed forty minutes and told him everything about her late husband's allotment and nothing about anything else. Old Bram Hodge, who shook his hand with a grip like a bear trap and said, "You'll be wanting to ask about the song, then," and when Silas said yes, actually, he would, Bram nodded sagely and left. Just left. Walked out into the drizzle like a man who had completed an errand.

"It's not rudeness," the innkeeper's wife, Dora, told him that evening, refilling his cup without being asked. "It's kindness. They're deciding how much to tell you so it hurts you least."

"That's not how information works, Dora."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But it's how Hollowmere works."

He'd set up his recorder on the inn's back table each morning, a small silver thing with reels that turned like patient eyes. He'd been a folklorist long enough to know that the truth of a village rarely lived in its answers. It lived in the pauses. In what people did with their hands. In the way a room went quiet around a particular word.

And in Hollowmere, the quiet always gathered around the same place: the second bar of the lullaby. The one Dora had hummed four notes of before going white as milk.

* * *

His first real break came from an unexpected quarter: the vicar's daughter, Ruth Calloway, thirty-eight years old, sensible as a cardigan, who ran the bakery now that her father had passed. She arrived at the inn with a tin of shortbread she clearly hoped would buy her discretion, sat down across from Silas, and folded her hands like a woman preparing for confession.

"I want to preface this," she said, "by saying I don't believe in anything."

"A position I respect and share."

"Good. Because what I'm about to say is nonsense." She pushed the tin toward him as if sealing the bargain. "When I was six, I knew a song. A lullaby. My mother never sang it — she sang 'Lavender's Blue' and hymns and that awful one about the grand old Duke of York. But I knew this other one. Knew every note of it. And when I hummed it once at the supper table, my mother dropped a whole tureen of soup. Scalded her arm badly. And she looked at me — Silas, I've never been looked at like that since — and she said, *'Where did you hear that?'* And I said I didn't know. And that was true. I didn't know. It was just *in* me, the way knowing your own name is in you."

Silas kept very still. "Did other children know it?"

Ruth laughed, but it wasn't a laugh; it was a flinch wearing a laugh's coat. "That's the thing. That's why I'm here. Last Christmas, at the vicarage memorial service, there was a moment — candles, carols, all very lovely — and Peter Ashdown's boy started humming something between carols. Just under his breath. And I felt it go through the room like a current. Half the adults stopped talking. And I realized — I realized I was mouthing along. The words. Words I haven't thought about in thirty years." She looked down at her hands. "I never taught him. Nobody taught him. He says he doesn't remember learning it. Says it's just *there*, like a tooth coming through."

"What are the words?"

She stood up so fast her chair skidded. "No. No, I'm sorry, I've said too much already, I can feel it — " She pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and for a moment Silas saw genuine terror there, the animal kind, before she mastered it. "Ask Tam Linnet. The schoolteacher. She keeps records. She's the only one in this village who writes things down instead of just enduring them."

She left the shortbread. Neither of them mentioned it again.

* * *

Tam Linnet's schoolhouse sat at the crook of the lane below Ash Hill, a low stone building with ivy eating one wall and a bell tower that hadn't rung in living memory. Tam herself was perhaps fifty, sharp-eyed, ink-stained, with the specific patience of someone who had spent decades explaining things to children and adults interchangeably because the difference was smaller than people liked to admit.

"You're the folklorist," she said, opening the door before he knocked. "Dora telephoned. Come in before the hill sees you standing there."

"The hill sees—"

"Figure of speech," said Tam, in the tone of a woman filing it under *not entirely*.

Her classroom smelled of chalk and wood polish. Along one wall hung forty years of children's drawings, sun-faded, curling at the edges. Silas drifted toward them while Tam made tea on a battered hotplate, and it was Tam who finally said, quietly, "Third row. Second from the left."

It was a drawing in wax crayon, the kind every child produces by the ream: green hill, blue sky, a house with a smoking chimney. Except the hill had a door in it. Not on it — *in* it, a dark archway set into the slope itself, drawn with such heavy black pressure that the crayon had torn the paper in places. And beneath the door, drawn small and careful, were figures. Stick figures, but arranged wrong — not walking or playing but standing in a line, facing the door, holding hands. Above them, in the wobbling letters of a seven-year-old, a caption:

**THEY SING SO IT CAN SLEEP**

Silas stood there for a long time. The tea went cold in his hands.

"Who drew it?"

"Ellie Marsh, 1994. Her family moved away that spring. Not because of the drawing," Tam added quickly. Too quickly. "Because of her father's work."

"And the others?" Silas nodded at the wall. Forty years of drawings. "Is it always there? The door?"

Tam didn't answer at once. She crossed the room and began, methodically, to take down drawings — one, then another, then a third, laying them on the desk like cards in a patience game. Different children. Different years. Different hills, different skies. And in eleven of them, the same black door in the same green slope, and in nine of them, the same line of hand-holding figures.

"Children draw what they dream," Tam said. "I told myself that for years. I'm a teacher; rationalization is my pension plan. But dreams scatter, Mr. Ashgrove. Dreams are snowflakes. This—" she tapped the line of figures, "—this is a queue. Thirty years apart, children who never met drawing the same procession, the same posture, the same *order*. You don't get that from dreams. You get that from instruction."

"From whom?"

Tam looked at him then with an expression he'd spend the rest of the week trying to forget — pity and apology braided together, aimed slightly past him, as if at someone standing behind his shoulder.

"That's the question I stopped asking in 2003," she said. "Sit down. I'm going to show you my diary, and then you're going to wish I hadn't, and then we're both going to have tea about it."

* * *

*From the school diary of Tamara Linnet, entries transcribed by permission:*

*14 March 2001 — Asked Year 2 to write about their favourite songs. Six children, independently, wrote down verses of the same lullaby. None could say where they learned it. Called their parents. All six phone calls ended the same way: a long silence, then "Who else knows?" Not "where did they hear it." Who else knows. As if comparing notes.*

*2 May 2001 — Jamie Prewitt hummed it during silent reading today. Miss Ellery (supply teacher) asked him to stop. He looked genuinely confused and said, "But it likes it when we sing." Asked what "it" was. He pointed at the window. At the hill. Then carried on reading about Biff and Chip as if nothing had happened.*

*19 June 2001 — Kept Ellie Marsh behind at lunch. She'd drawn the door again. I asked her why the people were holding hands. She said, "So nobody gets lost on the way down." Asked where down was. She looked at me the way you look at someone being deliberately slow and said, "Miss, you KNOW where down is."*

*I do know. God help me, I do know.*

*30 September 2003 — New rule, unwritten, never spoken aloud: no singing in the classroom. Ever. Nursery rhymes, hymns, happy birthday, nothing. The children think I'm strange. Let them. Strange is survivable.*

*11 November 2003 — David Ashgrove's name came up today. The children were playing a naming game — who used to live in your house? — and one of them said "the man who got away" and the others all shushed her, fast and fierce, like she'd said a swear. Asked them about it after. Total blank faces. Perfect little masks. Whatever they know, they know together, and they aren't telling me.*

*Note to self: the new family moving into the old Ashgrove cottage should be warned. Note to self, later: warn them how? With what words? There are no words for this. There is only the song, and nobody will sing it twice.*

* * *

Silas set the diary down. His hands weren't quite steady, and he was grateful Tam pretended not to notice.

"David Ashgrove," he said. "My father."

"Yes."

"'The man who got away.'"

"Yes." Tam poured more tea neither of them wanted, filling the silence with something to hold. "You understand now why Dora telephoned ahead. Why everyone's been so terribly kind and so terribly useless. It's not that they don't want to tell you, Mr. Ashgrove. It's that the last time this village talked openly about the song, a child from your house went missing, and the ones who remember have spent their whole lives making sure they never speak of it clearly enough for *it* to hear."

"Hear what?"

"The pattern." She said it simply, without drama, which made it worse. "Every generation, the song comes back in the children's mouths. Every generation, some families leave, some stay, everybody agrees not to compare notes too loudly. And every generation, one child goes. Sometimes shoes at the bottom of the hill. Sometimes nothing at all. And the village holds its breath and does its arithmetic and tells itself: not mine, not this year, sing quieter."

"That's complicity."

"That's Hollowmere." Tam smiled, and it was the saddest thing he'd seen since arriving. "You wanted folklore, Mr. Ashgrove. Well, this is what living folklore looks like. Not costumes and festivals. Fear, inherited like furniture. Everyone owns a piece; nobody admits to the whole set."

Outside the schoolroom window, the light was going amber, and Ash Hill stood against it, green and rounded and utterly ordinary, the way a held breath is ordinary right up until the moment it isn't.

"One more thing," Tam said, as he gathered his recorder. "The children drew those doors years ago. But last week — last week, Ellie Marsh's old classmate, grown now, visiting her mother — she stopped dead outside this window and stared at the hill and said, 'It's opened before, hasn't it. I remember the draft.'" Tam's voice dropped. "Mr. Ashgrove. The children have started humming the *second* verse. Not the first. The second. And in thirty years of teaching here, I have never once heard them go past the first."

The walk home took him past the base of Ash Hill, because in Hollowmere every road eventually did. The grass on its slope was long and unmown, and the evening wind moved through it in a long, slow ripple, like something breathing under a blanket.

At the cottage, the porch lamp was already lit, though Silas hadn't lit it.

Wren sat on the top step, knees hugged to his chest, exactly as eight years old as he had been yesterday and would apparently be tomorrow, and he was watching the hill with an expression no eight-year-old face should have been able to make.

"You went to the school," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"She showed me the drawings."

"Mm." Wren picked at a splinter on the step, not looking at him. "Then you know it's starting again. And you know the children only ever sing two verses." He finally looked up, and his eyes in the lamplight were old and kind and terribly afraid. "So the only question left, Silas — the one you're going to lie awake asking — is who taught them the first one. And why it's waiting for *you* to learn the rest."

Behind the cottage, in the dark, the old well cover shifted — a soft wooden groan, like a throat clearing before a song.
