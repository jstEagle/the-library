## 5. Margaret Under the Yew

The directions Margaret Fenwick had given him—shouted once, years ago, to a journalist who never came back—still lived in the village's collective memory, passed along like a recipe nobody wanted to cook. *Follow the lane past the old smithy, and when the yew leans over the road like it's listening, that's hers.*

The yew did lean. It had been doing so for perhaps four hundred years, its trunk split and hollowed, its branches hanging low enough that Silas had to duck as he passed beneath them. The lane beyond was narrower than it had any right to be, hemmed in by hawthorn hedges gone feral, and the light seemed to arrive here reluctantly, filtered and thinned.

Margaret Fenwick's cottage sat at the end of it, and Silas stopped walking when he saw it, because his professional instincts and his childhood instincts finally agreed on something.

Every window had iron nails driven into the frame. Not two or three—dozens, hammered in at odd angles, rusted to the wood, some so old they'd been painted over and painted over again until they looked like growths. The doorstep was ringed with salt, renewed recently enough that the crystals still caught the grey light. Above the door, a horseshoe hung point-down, and beside it, a small glass bottle wrapped in red thread.

There were wards, and then there was *architecture*. This was architecture.

"Miss Fenwick?" he called, and immediately felt foolish for the *Miss*. She'd been a married woman once, he was fairly sure. "Mrs. Fenwick? My name is Silas Ashgrove."

Nothing. A jackdaw on the ridge of the roof regarded him with what he chose to interpret as professional interest.

"Ashgrove," he said again, louder. "Of Ash Hill. I grew up here. I've come about the song."

That did something. He heard it before he saw it—a shift behind the front window, the particular creak of floorboards that meant a person had been standing very still for a very long time and had just decided to stop.

"I know your mother's grave," said a voice, dry as paper and twice as brittle. It came from somewhere near the side of the house. "I know your grandmother's grave. I know your great-grandfather sold the Fenwicks their pig in 1954 and shorted us two stone, and I know you're standing on my path because you think the salt is decorative."

Silas looked down. He had, without thinking, stepped over the salt line. He stepped back with the careful deliberation of a man who has just realized the floor is thinner than it looks.

"May I come to the gate, then?"

"You may come to the gate. You may not come past the gate. And you may stop shouting, boy, I'm ninety-one, not deaf."

He found her around the side, in a scrap of garden walled by the yew's lowest branches. Margaret Fenwick sat in a wheelchair that had clearly not been new in this century, wrapped in a blanket the color of wet slate, her hands folded on her lap like two pale birds. Her face was a topography of wrinkles, and her eyes—the only part of her that seemed to move quickly—were a pale, washed-out blue that fixed on him with the unhurried precision of a hawk deciding whether something was food or not.

"You're Thomas Ashgrove's boy," she said.

"I am."

"Thomas Ashgrove was a fool, but he was an honest fool, which is more than most." She jerked her chin at the chair opposite her, a wooden thing with a cushion that had been mended so many times the original fabric was a rumor. "Sit. You'll want to sit when I tell you what I tell you. Everyone does. Some of them fall down."

Silas sat. The chair groaned but held. Up close, he could see the iron nails again—here too, hammered into the garden wall at intervals, and a line of salt poured along the top of the low stone border, a white seam like a scar.

"Who told you about me?" she asked.

"The innkeeper's wife. Mrs. Trelawny."

"Hah. Bess Trelawny wouldn't sing past four bars if you held a hot poker to her. Her mother was the same." Margaret's mouth did something that was almost a smile, in the way that a cliff face is almost a staircase. "You've heard the first verse."

"Last night. From—" He hesitated.

"From the dead boy on your porch," Margaret said. "Yes. I know what sits on the Ashgrove steps. I've known since before you were born. Go on."

The ease with which she said it knocked the breath out of him. "You know about Wren."

"I know about *a* Wren. There's always been a Wren, or a Meg, or a Tom-tom, whatever the child wants to call it. The friend who doesn't grow. Every family on this hill has one in the attic of their history, and every family pretends it was a phase." Her eyes didn't leave his face. "You're the one it stayed for. That makes you either the luckiest or the most damned creature in Hollowmere, and I haven't decided which."

"The song," Silas said. "I need to hear all of it. The whole thing. There's a collector's notes from the 1930s, and they break off in the middle of the third verse, and I need—"

"Stop." The word came out flat and hard as a dropped pan. The jackdaw on the roof startled into the air. Margaret's folded hands had not moved, but something in her stillness had changed, the way a pond changes when something moves beneath the ice. "You were going to say it. The third verse. You were going to ask me for the third verse, out loud, in my garden."

"I need to understand it."

"*Understand* it." She laughed, and the laugh was worse than the shout. It was a small, exhausted sound, like a door that had been opened and closed too many times. "Boy, I heard the whole song sung once. Once. 1931, and I was a girl of nine, and I stood at my window and listened to it come up out of the ground on Ash Hill, all three verses, sung by voices I knew. The Cooper girl. The two Merrick boys. Little Annie Prowse, who used to share her sweets with me." Her hands unfolded, just slightly, and he saw that the knuckles were swollen, the fingers twisted. "I was the last one they sang to. Do you know why?"

"Because you heard it."

"Because I *listened*." She leaned forward, and the blanket slid, and he saw that beneath her shawl she wore an iron chain, old and black, against her throat. "There's a difference, boy. Hearing is what happens to you. Listening is what you *do*. And when the third verse is sung, and you listen—truly listen, with your whole heart wanting to know what comes next—it listens back. That's the whole of it. That's the trap. It isn't a song, it's a conversation, and the third verse is the part where it learns your name."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the yew, the village went on with its afternoon—a dog barking, a tractor, the small ordinary noises of the living—and they sounded very far away.

"Tell me the second verse," Silas said.

Margaret studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, he couldn't name it, but she seemed to find it, or enough of it, because she sighed—a long, rattling exhalation, the sigh of a woman setting down something she'd carried for ninety years and discovering her arms had forgotten any other shape.

"Second verse only. And you'll not write it down. And you'll not hum it. And you'll not so much as tap the rhythm with your finger while I speak, because I'll know, and I'll stop, and you'll have learned nothing but how it feels to be shouted at by an old woman."

"I promise."

"Promises." She snorted. "The thing under the hill collects promises. It's got a whole drawer full. But very well."

She closed her eyes. When she spoke, it was not sung—she spoke it, deliberately, flatly, crushing all melody out of the words the way you'd crush a wasp—but the melody rose up underneath her voice anyway, like water finding its level, and Silas felt the hair on his arms lift.

*Sleep, my darling, dream of deep,*
*Down where all the old ones keep,*
*Count the stones and count them slow,*
*The hill remembers what you know.*

*Sleep, my darling, hold your breath,*
*Under hill there is no death,*
*Only singing, sweet and long,*
*And every voice becomes the song.*

The last word left her mouth and the garden seemed to tilt, very slightly, as if the ground had taken a breath. Silas realized his own lips had parted, that his lungs had filled without his permission, that some muscle in his throat had *prepared*—

He clamped his jaw shut. Swallowed. His heart was going like a hammer.

Margaret's eyes opened, and they were wet, and furious about being wet. "There," she said. "That's what it does, and that's only the second, and you nearly answered. I saw your mouth."

"I didn't—"

"You nearly. *Nearly* is how it gets them. Nobody leaps in. They *nearly*, and then they nearly again, and the third time the nearly does it for them." She reached down beside her chair and came up with a walking stick, and used it to point at him, the tip trembling. "Now you'll go away. You'll go away and you'll think about what you heard, and you will *not* come back here asking for the third, because if you do I'll have my nephew set the dogs on you, and I mean that with love, boy, I do."

"Mrs. Fenwick." Silas stood. His legs felt borrowed. "The children. They're singing the second verse already. In the schoolyard. None of them will say where they learned it. And last night I dreamed I was walking down stairs inside the hill, and I woke with dirt under my nails, and there's a boy—there might be a boy already—"

Margaret went very still.

"Which boy," she said.

"Edie Marsh's son. Danny. Nobody's seen him since yesterday evening."

The stick lowered. Margaret Fenwick looked, for the first time since he'd arrived, like a woman of ninety-one—small, and cold, and tired down to the marrow. She turned her face toward Ash Hill, visible over the yew's shoulder as a green mound in the middle distance, innocent as a sleeping animal.

"Ninety years," she said quietly. "I've poured salt for ninety years and hammered nails until my hands gave out, and it waited, and it *waited*, and the moment someone's fool grandson comes home with a tape recorder—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "The third verse is not a song, Silas Ashgrove. It's a *key*. And the reason it's never written down, the reason it dies with whoever hears it, is that a key doesn't care whose hand turns it. It only cares that it turns."

"Then teach me to turn it the other way."

"There is no other way. There's only the door, and what's behind it, and how fast you can run."

"Margaret." He crouched, bringing himself level with her, close enough to see the iron chain at her throat, the salt dust on her blanket, the ninety years of vigil written into every line of her. "I had a friend who stood on my porch for thirty years so I wouldn't have to stand where Danny Marsh is standing now. I'm not going to run. I never learned how. Ask anyone."

She looked at him for a long, long moment. The wind moved in the yew, and its shadow moved across the garden like something wading through shallow water.

"Get out of my garden," Margaret Fenwick said at last, and her voice cracked on the last word, and Silas understood that it was not a refusal. It was the sound of a woman slamming a door against something that had already gotten inside.

He was halfway down the lane, ducking under the yew, when her voice caught up with him—thin and hard and carrying, pitched to reach him and nothing else:

"The third verse has no melody, boy! It's spoken, never sung—that's the only mercy in it! Come back at dusk, and bring your own salt, and if you tell a single soul I taught you, I'll deny it to my grave and *laugh* while I do it!"

Silas stood in the lane with his heart slamming and the light going long and gold across the fields, and behind him, in the warded garden, an old woman began—very softly, very deliberately—to hum the first verse, the way you'd ring a bell to clear the air.

And ahead of him, down the lane toward the village, a small figure was waiting by the stile. Eight years old, unchanged, barefoot in the cold.

Wren wasn't looking at him. He was looking at Ash Hill, and his face was the loneliest thing Silas had ever seen.

"You shouldn't have gone there," Wren said, without turning around. "Now it knows you're asking."
