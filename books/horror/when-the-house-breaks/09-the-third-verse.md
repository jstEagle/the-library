## 9. The Third Verse

The dark inside Ash Hill was not the absence of light. It was a presence, thick as wool packed into the ears, and it breathed. Silas felt it against his face — slow, patient exhalations that smelled of turned earth and old milk and something sweeter underneath, something like honey left too long in a hot room.

He had come down through the barrow door with a torch, a coil of rope, and Edie's son's name written on his palm in ballpoint ink because he was afraid he'd forget it. *Tomas.* Seven letters. He kept reading them like a rosary.

The chorus was below him. He could hear it now without trying — dozens of voices, high and small, singing the second verse over and over in a round so perfect it made his teeth ache. Generations of it. He recognized phrases Margaret had hummed before slamming her door, phrases Wren had sung at the well with his hands pressed flat over Silas's ears as if that could help.

And beneath the children's voices, holding them up the way a riverbed holds water, was the other one. The listener. It did not sing. It *attended.*

Silas climbed back up the barrow passage on shaking legs, sat down hard in the wet grass outside, and took out his phone.

* * *

Margaret Fenwick answered on the fourth ring, and her voice came through thin and crackling, as if she were speaking from the bottom of a well herself.

"If this is you, Silas Ashgrove, I told you never—"

"I'm at the hill," he said. "I've been inside. Tomas Merrick is alive down there. Wren can feel him."

A long silence. In it he heard her wind chimes — iron nails strung on fishing line, clanking like a tiny, furious church.

"You went inside." Her voice had gone very quiet. "You sang."

"I didn't sing. I swear to God, Margaret, I didn't sing. But I'm going to need the third verse, and I'm going to need it now, and I need you to understand something first." He pressed the phone harder against his ear, as if he could push himself through the signal into her kitchen, into the smell of coal smoke and lavender she'd carried out of 1940. "I'm not going to say it back to you. Not once. You're going to teach me the way you'd teach someone who's deaf. I'll memorize it silent. And then I'm going to go down there and speak it — speak, not sing — at the threshold, and we're going to see what happens when somebody refuses the terms."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again, all the iron had gone out of her voice, and what remained was an old woman's tiredness, ninety-one years of carrying a secret nobody thanked her for.

"My mother taught me the whole song when I was six," Margaret said. "She made me promise two things. Never sing past the second verse where anyone could hear. And if the day ever came that someone needed the third—" Her breath caught. "She said the day would come. She said it always comes. Every generation, someone gets curious, or desperate, or brave, and the hill opens its mouth again."

"Margaret."

"I'm getting there. Don't rush an old woman, Silas Ashgrove; it's rude, and your mother raised you better, whatever else went wrong with you." He heard her moving, the creak of a floorboard, the scrape of something heavy — a Bible, maybe, or a box of things wrapped in oilcloth. "Now. The verse has nine lines. Nine, do you hear me? If you lose count, you stop, you climb out, and you wait for another night. A broken binding is worse than none. Do you understand?"

"Nine lines. I understand."

"And you will not hum it. You will not tap it. You will not let it settle into any part of you that keeps rhythm. Your heart counts as a drum, boy, so don't get excited."

Despite everything — the dark breathing behind him, the chorus seeping up through the roots of the hill like cold through a floor — Silas laughed. It came out cracked and half-mad, but it was a laugh. "My heart and I have an understanding. Go ahead."

* * *

She taught him by lamplight and landline, her voice tinny and wavering, and he learned it the way he'd once learned dead languages: as shapes, not sounds. He would not let the words become music. He broke each line into its plainest parts, stripped the melody off it like meat from a bone, and held the bare meanings up to the light of his torch.

*Close the door that has no wood.*
*Nine lines spoken, none of them sung.*
*What was given back is given good.*
*We do not want you. We are done.*

It was not beautiful. That was the point, he realized, somewhere around the fifth line, sitting in the grass with his eyes shut and his lips pressed together while Margaret's voice threaded through the dark. Lullabies are beautiful because they are invitations. This was not an invitation. This was a legal document, hammered out by some terrified grandmother three hundred years ago and passed hand to hand like a knife, and it was ugly and procedural and full of refusal — *we do not want you, we are done, go back, go back, go back* — and every time Margaret reached the end of it, her voice shook, and Silas understood that she had never once said these words aloud either. She had only ever known them. Carried them. Ninety-one years of holding a blade by the handle so no one else got cut.

"Again," he said, after the sixth repetition. "From the top."

"You've got it. I can hear you've got it — you're not mouthing along, good lad, good lad—"

"One more. Then I'll recite it back to you silently, and you'll tell me if my face looks wrong."

Margaret made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. "Your face. Lord preserve us. All right. One more. And Silas—" The wind chimes clanked behind her. "When you're down there, and it starts talking to you — and it will talk, it talked to my brother right up until the end — remember that it lies the way other things breathe. Without thinking about it. Whatever it offers you, whatever shape it wears — and it will wear Wren's shape, if it's clever, it wears what you love—"

"It won't be Wren," Silas said quietly. "Wren's up here. Wren's been keeping watch on the porch for thirty years, and tonight he walked me to the barrow door and wouldn't come closer, and he said, 'It can't wear me anymore. I gave that up.' So it'll have to try something else."

"What did it take from you, Silas? What does it have?"

He looked at the dark mouth of the barrow. Thought of his mother's voice, gone since he was nine. Thought of the cottage, sold and lost and crumbling. Thought of belonging itself — that warm, humming thing he'd been chasing through fifteen years of field recordings and footnotes, always just outside the circle of other people's songs.

"Everything," he said. "But it doesn't know that I already decided I don't want anything on those terms. That's the trick of it, isn't it? The binding works because someone refuses. Not because they're strong. Because they're *done.*"

Margaret was silent a moment. Then, softly: "Go get that boy. And Silas Ashgrove — when it's finished, you come to my kitchen, and I'll make you tea, and you'll tell me about my mother's voice, because I haven't heard anyone sing the first verse in seventy years and I find I miss it terribly."

* * *

The descent was easier the second time, which frightened him more than the first descent had. His feet knew the way. The hill wanted him comfortable.

The chorus rose to meet him, and this close he could pick out individual voices — a girl singing a descant no one had taught her, a boy whose verses lagged half a beat behind, tired, so tired, a child who had been singing for a very long time. And under everything, the listener's attention turned toward him like a lighthouse beam, and the air grew sweet, and the walls of the passage seemed to lean in.

*THERE YOU ARE,* said a voice that was not a voice. It came up through his boots, through the bones of his legs, resonant as a struck bell. *WE KEPT YOUR PLACE. ALL THESE YEARS. THE STAIRS REMEMBER YOUR FEET.*

Silas kept walking. He did not answer. Answering, Wren had warned him, was halfway to harmonizing.

*YOU CAME BACK. THEY ALWAYS COME BACK. AND WE ARE SO PATIENT, AND SO KIND, AND NO ONE UP THERE EVER LOVED YOU THE WAY WE LOVE OUR SINGERS.*

"That's true," Silas said aloud, and his voice sounded shockingly loud, human, rough. "No one up there loves the way you love. That's rather the point."

The passage opened.

The chamber below Ash Hill was not large, and it was not grand, and that was somehow the worst thing about it. It was the size of a village hall, low-ceilinged, its walls packed earth studded with small white things he refused to look at closely. And standing in rows, facing a doorway in the far wall — a doorway of nothing, a door-shaped absence that breathed — were the children.

Dozens of them. Clothes from every decade. A girl in a 1930s pinafore. A boy in bell-bottoms. Children in modern coats with their names still written in marker inside the collars. They swayed gently, all together, singing, and their faces were peaceful in the way of people asleep in a burning house.

Near the front, small and stubborn, stood a boy of seven with mud on his knees and no shoes. Tomas. His lips moved with the chorus, but his eyes — his eyes were his own, and they found Silas's torchlight, and they widened.

Silas stepped forward, and the sweetness thickened, and the melody leaned on him like a hand on the back of his neck, coaxing his breath into rhythm. *Come,* said the hill, in Wren's voice, in his mother's voice, in the innkeeper's wife's four bars, *come, you know this part, everyone knows this part, you've known it since you were small, just open your mouth—*

His lungs ached to join. That was the horror of it — it wasn't compulsion from outside. It was homesickness, weaponized. The song felt like belonging. It felt like coming home.

Silas thought of a porch in the evening light. Of a small figure aging year by year, sunset by sunset, spending himself down to nothing to keep one stubborn fool alive. Of Margaret's tea waiting. Of Edie's hands wringing a dish towel. Of a village that had finally, clumsily, started to make room for him.

Belonging chosen, not taken.

"No," Silas said.

And he spoke the third verse.

Not sang. *Spoke.* Flat, ugly, deliberate, each of the nine lines dropped like stones down a well, his voice cracking on the sixth and steadying on the seventh, and the moment the first line landed the chamber *screamed* — the children's chorus shattering into discord, the listener's attention whipping around like a wounded animal, the sweet air curdling into a smell of burnt hair and old graves.

*YOU CANNOT,* the hill roared, wearing his mother's voice now, wearing everyone's voice, *YOU ARE ONE OF US, YOU HAVE ALWAYS BEEN ONE OF US—*

"We are done," Silas said, and spoke the eighth line.

The door-shaped absence began to shrink.

The children stopped swaying. One by one they turned, blinking, like sleepers waking, and where they had stood there was slowly, gently, nothing — not death, Silas realized, watching them come apart into motes of grey light, but release, decades of borrowed time finally being handed back. The girl in the pinafore smiled at him. The boy in bell-bottoms saluted, cheeky even now.

The ninth line. Silas spoke it with everything he had, with fifteen years of loneliness and thirty years of grief and one evening of birdsong still owed to a friend on a porch:

"Close the door that has no wood. We do not want you. We are done."

The threshold snapped shut with a sound like a book closing, deep underground, final. The screaming cut off. The sweetness drained away. And in the sudden ordinary dark, lit only by his trembling torch, a small muddy boy looked up at him and said, in the imperious tone of seven-year-olds everywhere:

"Took you long enough. Mum's going to kill me."

Silas laughed until he cried, and picked him up, and climbed.

They came out of the barrow door into a sky going grey-gold at the edges, the first pale suggestion of dawn, and Edie Merrick was running across the field with her dressing gown flying and her arms already open, and behind her, scattered across the hillside like a congregation released, the whole of Hollowmere stood watching — and somewhere above them all, faint and clear, a bird began to sing the first verse of the lullaby, sweet and unafraid, and this time, nobody flinched.

On the porch of the old Ashgrove cottage, small and patient, Wren lifted one hand in greeting — and Silas saw, with a catch in his throat, that the hand was wrinkled now, spotted with age, and that Wren's hair had gone white as ash.

One sunset at a time, starting tonight. There wasn't much of him left.

Silas meant to make every hour count.
