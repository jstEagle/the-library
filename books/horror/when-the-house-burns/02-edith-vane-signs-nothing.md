## 2. Edith Vane Signs Nothing

The preliminary disclosure packet was eleven pages, and Edith Vane read none of them.

Rosalind had laid them out on the morning room table in his preferred order—disclosure of liens first, then the title abstract, then the standard seller's affidavit, each page squared to the table's edge with a precision he didn't bother hiding anymore. He'd brought two pens. One for her. One that never left his breast pocket, because a surveyor who lends his good pen learns what ink does to linen.

Mrs. Vane sat across from him with her hands folded on the table like a woman at a funeral she'd already made peace with. She was small. That was the first thing. He had expected, from the file photographs—a girl in a white dress on the east lawn, 1961, squinting into a sun that no longer seemed to visit this house—to find someone diminished by age. Instead she looked *concentrated*, as if everything she had ever been had been boiled down over ninety years into something dense and dark and patient. Her eyes were the color of tea left too long in the pot.

"Sign there," Rosalind said, "and initial there and there. Then I can begin the interior survey this afternoon."

She looked at the papers the way you might look at a stranger claiming kinship.

"No," she said.

He waited. In his experience, most people filled silence with agreement. Silence was a tool; he kept it oiled.

Edith Vane did not fill it. She let it sit between them until the clock in the hall—an old regulator with a pendulum that swung without any sound he could detect—had ticked through nearly a full minute.

"The survey comes first," she said finally. "All of it. Every room, every wall, every measurement written down true. Then I'll sign."

"Mrs. Vane, the affidavit is a formality. It doesn't affect the appraisal."

"It affects whether the survey is honest." She unfolded her hands and laid one flat on the table, and he noticed then how thin the skin was, how the veins beneath mapped themselves like river systems seen from altitude. "You've measured the outside already. Tell me what you found."

He hesitated. Not because he didn't know—he knew to the centimeter—but because something in her tone suggested the question was not small talk.

"Hollowmere House," he recited, "footprint forty-one meters by eighteen point six. Two full stories plus attic level. Thirty-one rooms by exterior count."

"And inside?"

"I haven't completed the interior count."

"No," she said softly. "You haven't."

* * *

He began where he always began: the foundation walls in the cellar, working upward, because a house told its truth from the bottom. The cellar was dry, which was wrong for a property this old in soil this damp—the drainage should have failed decades ago—and the stone was warm under his palm, which was wronger still. He noted it. He noted everything. That was the discipline: note first, explain later, and if explanation never came, the note remained honest anyway.

By late afternoon he had covered the ground floor. His clipboard held thirty-one entries in his narrow, upright hand, each room numbered, dimensioned, described. He worked the way other men prayed, which is to say completely, and without much thought about what he was saying.

At five o'clock he went looking for Mrs. Vane to report his progress and found her in the morning room still, sitting exactly where he'd left her, though the light had moved across the table and she hadn't shifted an inch to follow it or escape it.

"You've done the east rooms," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Ground floor complete. Tomorrow I'll do the upper stories and attic."

She nodded slowly, and there was something in the nod that frightened him before he understood why—something like grief, or pity, aimed at him rather than offered to him.

"Come here," she said, and led him—not waiting to see if he followed—to the foot of the main staircase. She stood before it the way people stand before graves. "How many steps?"

He counted them with his eyes, a habit so ingrained it cost nothing. "Fourteen."

"There were twelve when my mother died in this house. There were ten when I was a girl." She turned and looked up at him, and her face was perfectly calm, which made it worse. "You wrote down fourteen because fourteen is what's there today. But your clipboard doesn't say what the stairs are doing, does it. It says what they are. And what they are is *growing*, Mr. Quintana, and it has been growing since before either of us was born, and it grows faster when someone is watching."

"That's—" He stopped himself. He had been about to say *impossible*, and he realized, standing in that dim hall with the soundless pendulum swinging behind them, that he had no evidence for the word. Only expectation. Expectation was not evidence. Expectation was just a ledger you'd never audited.

"Your house reads its visitors," Edith said. "It's been reading you since you drove through the gate. I can hear it reading. It's very quiet when it works, but the house is old and I am old, and we've learned each other's sounds." She put out her hand—not for the pen, he saw with a lurch, but palm-up, open, empty. "Finish your survey honestly. Write down only what is true while it is true. And when the numbers stop matching, don't correct them. Correct yourself."

Then she walked past him toward the stairs, and climbed all fourteen steps without once touching the rail, and closed her door, and the house settled around him like a thing exhaling.

* * *

He did not sleep well, which was unusual. He slept badly perhaps four nights a year, and always for reasons he could name: caffeine, weather, a client's fraud keeping his mind chewing. Tonight he lay in the guest room—the east guest room, third door on the left, dimensions 4.2 by 3.8, window facing the lawn—and listened to Hollowmere make its small noises. Houses made noises. Contraction, expansion, wind in chimneys. He catalogued each one and filed it and felt his pulse come down.

Somewhere past two in the morning, the noises stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. All at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone enters it.

Rosalind lay in the dark and felt the silence press against the windows like a face.

He got up. He told himself it was to get water, and he even took the glass, and he stood in the hallway with the moon coming gray through the landing window, and he heard—from below, from the direction of the morning room—the small, wet sound of a pen moving across paper.

He went down. Of course he went down. A surveyor investigates discrepancies; that was the whole of his creed.

The morning room was empty. The moon lay across the table in a long pale parallelogram. His disclosure packet sat where he'd left it, squared to the edge, unsigned, and beside it—

Beside it lay his clipboard.

His clipboard, which he had carried upstairs. Which he had set on the nightstand at half past eleven, after entering the day's final measurements. He remembered setting it down the way he remembered every object he owned, which was completely.

He picked it up. The pages were cool, as if the room had been cold around them. He thumbed to today's entries, checking his hand against his memory, and found the ground floor intact, thirty-one rooms, his own upright script, everything true.

And then one more page.

One more entry, in his own handwriting—which was the part that stopped his breath, because forgery he could have survived, forgery was human, forgery had motives he could investigate. This was not forged. This was his hand, his spacing, his abbreviations, the little hooked crossbar he made on his t's when he was tired.

**Room 32. 3.1 × 2.4 m. Windowless. Contents: one chair, one table, one ledger, open.**

He had never entered Room 32. There was no Room 32. The house had thirty-one rooms by exterior count, and he had surveyed thirty-one rooms, and the arithmetic was clean and closed and correct.

He stood in the moonlit morning room holding evidence of a room that did not exist, written in a hand that was unquestionably his, and he became aware—as slowly as cold moves through boot leather—that somewhere above him, faintly, a floorboard had shifted. Not creaked. *Shifted.* The way weight redistributes. The way a house adjusts itself around a new fact.

He looked at the staircase. Fourteen steps, silvered with moonlight, leading up into the dark.

He counted them twice. Both times, sixteen.

Rosalind Quintana, who trusted ledgers more than people, who had built a whole life on the principle that what is written down cannot lie, stood at the bottom of those stairs holding a clipboard that had begun writing back—and understood, with the first real crack running through the foundation of his rational world, that the discrepancy was not in the house.

The house was keeping books too.

And tonight, it had opened an account in his name.
