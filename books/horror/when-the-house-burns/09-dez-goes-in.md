## 9. Dez Goes In

Tomas Okafor had been running excavation crews for nineteen years, and in that time he'd learned that the most dangerous thing on any site wasn't the equipment or the ground. It was the moment a man decided he knew better than the plan.

He saw it coming with Dez for three days before it happened.

It started small. Dez stopped eating lunch with the others at the top of the pit and started taking his meals down by the door—sitting cross-legged on the tarp they'd laid over the exposed lintel, eating his sandwiches like a man keeping vigil at a shrine. When Tomas told him to knock it off, Dez nodded and said, "Sure, boss," in a voice that was agreeable the way still water is agreeable. Then he went back down anyway, an hour later, when he thought nobody was watching.

The second sign was the humming. Dez had never hummed a day in his life as far as Tomas knew—he was a talker, a joker, a man who narrated his own work like a sports commentator—but now he hummed while he worked, low and tuneless, four notes cycling over and over. It took Tomas two days to place where he'd heard the pattern before, and when he did, it made the skin on his arms crawl: it was the same four-note phrase Rosalind had recorded on his survey audio, the sound that came up through the soil around the buried door when the wind died down. The house's sound. Or whatever lived under the house's sound.

The third sign was the dreams. Dez admitted them on Wednesday night, sitting in the trailer with the space heater rattling, while the rest of the crew played cards around the folding table. He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his hands were wrapped around his coffee cup like it was the only warm thing left in the county.

"I keep dreaming about stairs," he said. "Going up."

"It's a basement door," Marcus said, not looking up from his cards. "Stairs go down, genius."

"Not in the dream." Dez smiled. His smile was fine. His eyes were somewhere else entirely. "In the dream I open the door and the stairs go up, and there's dirt overhead, layers of it, like the whole lawn is stacked above me, and every layer has people in it. Standing in the walls of the dirt. And they're all doing inventory."

"Doing inventory," Priya repeated. She'd started joining the crew evenings after her days in the Vane archive, and she'd brought with her a notebook she was always writing in—the counter-ledger, she called it, though she didn't explain what it counted against.

"Naming things," Dez said. "Counting things. Like—" He snapped his fingers, searching for it. "Like your friend the surveyor. Like Rosalind does. That's all they do, all the way up. They count themselves so they don't get lost."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about Hollowmere now; nobody laughed anymore, not really. The jokes happened but the laughter was a formality, a ritual of its own, performed to prove they were still the kind of crew that joked.

Tomas shut it down the way he always did. "You're sleeping in the trailer with the radio on from now on," he said. "And you're off pit duty until this job closes out. Marcus takes your line."

"Boss—"

"That's not a discussion."

Dez looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind his face—not anger, nothing so simple. It was more like a man watching a door close and deciding, quietly, that he no longer needed doors.

"Okay, boss," he said. "Whatever you say."

* * *

Friday morning broke gray and windless, the kind of stillness that made the whole site feel held, like something cupped in a palm. The air smelled faintly of wax. It had smelled that way for two days, sweet and churchy, drifting up from the pit, and the crew had stopped commenting on it because commenting meant acknowledging it and acknowledging it meant thinking about it.

Tomas was up at the trailer going over load tickets when he heard Marcus shout his name—not angry-shout, not hurt-shout, but the specific flat alarm of a man who has just seen something impossible and needs a witness immediately.

Tomas came down the slope at a run.

Dez was already in the pit.

He'd gone in without a harness, without telling anyone, without even leaving his boots laced properly—one of them had come half off in the soft earth near the bottom—and he was standing at the door with both hands flat against its iron face, his forehead pressed between them, and he was talking. Not to them. Not to himself, either. Talking *into* it, the way you'd speak through a wall to someone in the next room.

"—I know, I know, I'm sorry it took me so long, I had to wait for them to sleep—"

"DEZ!" Tomas dropped into the pit beside him, gravel skidding under his boots, Marcus and the kid, Hollis, coming down behind him. "Step away from the door. Right now. That is a direct order."

Dez turned his head. He was smiling. Up close, Tomas could see that his lips were cracked and his eyes were red-rimmed and dry, the eyes of a man who hadn't slept so much as he'd been *kept*, and the smile was the worst part, because it wasn't Dez's smile. Dez's smile was crooked and too big and arrived a half-second late, like him. This one was symmetrical. Patient. It fit his face the way a fitted sheet fits a mattress—smoothly, completely, and with no warmth underneath.

"He sealed it from the inside," Dez said. His voice had changed too. It was still his voice, the same pitch, the same regional bend in the vowels, but the rhythm was wrong—measured, formal, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Did you know that? Whoever built it. They stood right here, on the inside, and they sealed themselves in. That's not a prison, Tomás. You don't build a prison with hinges."

"How do you know my middle name?" Tomas said.

Because it did. Tomás. Not Tomas—Tomás, with the accent his mother used, the name that appeared on exactly zero documents because his father had anglicized it on every form ever filed. There were maybe six people alive who called him Tomás, and none of them were standing in a pit in Vermont.

"The ledger knows everyone it holds," Dez said gently. "You've been in it since Tuesday. Page forty-one. 'The foreman, who counts loads and men and believes the counting keeps him safe.' That's you, isn't it? That's very you."

"Grab him," Tomas said, and hated how thin his voice came out.

Marcus took one arm. Hollis took the other. And Dez—Dez came easily, almost courteously, letting himself be pulled back from the iron—but as they hauled him toward the ladder he twisted once, not violently, just enough to look back at the door, and he said, loud and clear, in that borrowed cadence:

"I'll come back. I know the way now. It knows mine."

Then they were hauling him up into the gray morning light, and the smell of wax was stronger than it had ever been, and beneath their feet, through the soles of their boots, through the packed earth of the east lawn, Tomas felt—or imagined he felt—a single slow pulse of warmth, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

* * *

They sat him in the trailer and gave him coffee he didn't drink and watched him the way you watch a man who might have a concussion, except concussions don't recite your family history.

For twenty minutes Dez was mostly Dez again. He shivered. He asked stupidly what time it was. He let Hollis wrap the emergency blanket around his shoulders and complained about it being crinkly, which was such a Dez complaint that Tomas felt his chest unclench a little. Shock, he told himself. Sleep deprivation and obsession and shock. People did strange things. People heard voices in fan noise and saw faces in smoke. The mind reached for patterns; that was all.

Then Priya arrived, breathless, notebook clutched to her chest, and asked Dez what he'd seen inside, and Dez looked at her with those patient symmetrical eyes and said:

"You mean before or after?"

"After," Priya said. "After you opened it."

"There isn't an after yet," Dez said. "There's a before, and there's a during, and the during hasn't finished happening. But if you want the before—" He folded his hands on the table, and his voice slid fully into the founders' cadence then, the measured liturgical rhythm they'd all heard humming up through the ground, and nobody stopped him because stopping him felt suddenly, horribly rude, like interrupting a prayer. "The door opened inward, which surprised me, though it shouldn't have. Everything about this house opens inward. Behind it there was no dark. Dark would have been a mercy. There was a stairwell, and it went up, and the air coming down it was warm and smelled like bread baking and cut grass and, underneath, like the inside of a closed book. Old glue. Old paper. And the walls of the stairwell weren't stone. They were shelving. Floor to ceiling, both sides, and every shelf was full."

"Full of what?" Priya whispered.

"Lives," Dez said. "Bound ones. Each one labeled in a neat hand. I read three labels before they noticed me reading. The first said *Edith Vane, b. 1931, incomplete*. The second said *Marguerite Vane, 1934–1963, retained*. And the third—" He paused, and for one flickering instant something surfaced in his face that was purely, desperately Dez, some last native part of him looking out through the glass of his own eyes, and it mouthed *help me* before the calm closed over it again. "The third said *Rosalind Quintana, surveyor, appraisal pending*."

The trailer was silent except for the rattle of the space heater.

"His middle name is Ambrose," Dez added, conversationally. "He doesn't put it on anything. He thinks dropping it made him harder to file. Isn't that funny? Nothing is hard to file here."

Priya wrote the names down. Her hand was shaking so badly the letters came out jagged, but she wrote them, and Tomas understood why—understood it the way you understand things in nightmares, without wanting to. If the house kept records, then records were a battlefield. If the house remembered, then remembering was resistance.

"Dez," Tomas said slowly. "When you read those labels. What did they notice you *with*?"

Dez tilted his head, considering, and the gesture was wrong too—too smooth, too birdlike, a motion no part of Dez's body had ever made before.

"They didn't chase me," he said. "Chasing is for things outside the collection. They just... revised me. A little. An accession number, here." He tapped his temple. "A cataloguing cadence, there. A few facts about the staff, for context. I'm mostly me still. Mostly." The smile again, symmetrical, terrible. "They're very efficient. They don't take more than they need. They're not greedy, Tomás. They're *thorough*."

"Stop calling me that."

"Your mother is dead eleven years," Dez said softly. "She called you that. Nobody else living does. Somebody should keep saying it, don't you think? Otherwise it's just a fact in a drawer."

Tomas hit him.

He didn't decide to. His fist was already moving, and it caught Dez across the jaw and knocked him sideways off the bench, and Hollis shouted and grabbed Tomas's arm, and Dez lay on the trailer floor for a moment with blood at the corner of his mouth—and then he sat up, unhurried, and touched the blood, and looked at it with mild scholarly interest, and said:

"Sample noted. Thank you."

And that was worse than the punch. That was so much worse than the punch that Tomas stepped back like the floor had bitten him.

* * *

They drove Dez to the hospital in Rutland that afternoon, Tomas at the wheel, Marcus riding shotgun, Dez in the back seat between Hollis and Priya like a prisoner who didn't know he was one. Dez chatted pleasantly the whole way—about the weather, about the crew, about the correct depth for frost footings—and every sentence was perfectly normal and every sentence was delivered in that cadence, and by mile ten Tomas wanted to drive the truck into a reservoir just to make the voice stop.

The ER doctor found nothing. Concussion negative. Bloodwork clean. "Has he been under unusual stress?" she asked, and Marcus laughed—one short, broken bark—and said, "Lady, we dug up a door that was locked from the inside," and the doctor looked at him the way doctors look at you when they're deciding whether to order a psych consult for *you*.

They brought Dez back at dusk.

The site was empty when they returned—the crew sent home early, the machines parked and tarped—and the pit should have been empty too. They'd covered the door with plywood and two feet of fill on Thursday, standard containment, documented with photographs.

The plywood was stacked neatly at the rim of the pit.

The fill was mounded beside it, tidy as a grave digger's leavings.

And the door stood open.

Not forced. Open, its inward hinge swung wide, and the stairwell beyond it went up into the earth, and warm air breathed out of it smelling of bread and cut grass and old paper, and from somewhere up inside that throat of shelves and soil came a sound that made all five of them stand frozen at the pit's edge while the light died behind the trees.

Humming. Four notes, cycling.

And beneath the humming, layered under it like a figure under a watermark, a voice—dry, courteous, infinitely patient—reading aloud in the dark. Reading names. Working its way down a list with the unhurried pleasure of a man opening mail.

It got to the Q's just as the last light failed.

"*Quintana,*" it said, tasting the syllables. "*Rosalind Ambrose Quintana.*"

Beside Tomas, Dez exhaled happily, like a man hearing home announced at the end of a long journey.

"He's next," Dez said.
