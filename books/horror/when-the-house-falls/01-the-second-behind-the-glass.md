## 1. The Second Behind the Glass

The house made its noise at 2:14 a.m., same as it did every night.

Kestrel had stopped wearing a watch years ago because he didn't need one. The Pemberton house kept time better than any clock he'd owned—a groan in the west wall at 2:14, a tick in the stairwell at 3:00 sharp, the long settling sigh from the attic around dawn. Twelve years of night shifts and he'd mapped the whole vocabulary of it. Pipes. Framing lumber contracting in the cold. Old houses talked; you just had to learn not to listen too closely.

He was listening anyway. He always was. That was the trick of the job nobody warned you about: you spent so long pretending the sounds meant nothing that you got very, very good at hearing them.

Mr. Aldous was dying in the blue room, third floor, east side—the room with the good light in the morning that he'd never see again. Kestrel had been sitting with him since eleven, reading aloud from a western novel Aldous had already forgotten he'd heard twice, and the old man's breathing had the wet, hitching rhythm that Kestrel knew the way other people knew their own heartbeat. Hours, maybe. Not many more than that.

"Kestrel." Aldous's eyes opened, milky and searching. "You hear that?"

Kestrel set the book down. "Hear what, Mr. Aldous?"

"The counting." The old man's lips moved, shaping numbers without sound. *Nine for the nails, eight for the door...* His fingers twitched against the blanket in a pattern, like he was keeping time. "It's coming down the years. It always comes down."

"It's the pipes," Kestrel said, gently, the way he said it every time. "Old house. Old pipes."

Aldous looked at him for a long moment, and something in the look made Kestrel's chest go tight—not pity, exactly, not the fog of morphine. Something clearer. Something almost sorry.

"You've been here twelve years," Aldous said. "And you still say pipes."

"I say it because it's true."

"Sure." Aldous closed his eyes. His fingers kept counting. "Sure it is."

Kestrel gave him his meds at two, checked the morphine drip, and stepped out into the hall to give the man his privacy from being watched, which was a strange courtesy but one Kestrel had learned mattered to the dying. The hallway ran the length of the east wing, narrow, papered in a faded pattern of climbing roses that had been there since before Kestrel was born. At the far end, beside the window that looked out on the dark garden, hung the mirror.

It had been there all twelve years. Tall, dark-framed, the glass gone slightly amber at the edges like a photograph left in the sun. Kestrel passed it a dozen times a night, every night. He had long ago stopped seeing it, the way you stop seeing your own front door.

He saw it now.

The hallway behind him was empty. He knew that without turning around—he could feel the whole length of it, the way you can feel a room at your back, the particular pressure of empty air. The nightlight at the stairwell glowed its dim amber. The roses on the wallpaper climbed their faded climb. Nothing moved.

But in the mirror, at the far end of the reflected hall, just at the edge of the glass, there was someone standing.

Kestrel went still.

Not a shape. Not a shadow. A *person*—or the suggestion of one, tall and narrow, in what might have been a coat, standing where the hall met the window light. Standing in his hallway, at the edge of his vision, where nothing was standing.

His heart did something complicated—a lurch and then a strange, suspended quiet, the way it does in the moment before you decide whether to be afraid.

*Exhaustion,* he told himself. *You've been up since six. You've buried four residents this year. Your brain is doing what brains do.*

He turned around slowly.

The hall was empty. Of course it was empty. The nightlight hummed its small hum. The window held its rectangle of black garden. Nothing stood at the end of the hall, and nothing had ever stood at the end of the hall, and in twelve years nothing had stood at the end of the hall.

He turned back to the mirror.

The figure was still there.

That was the part that made the skin on his arms tighten—not the figure itself, but the fact that it had stayed. If his mind had invented it, the turning should have erased it. Hallucinations don't survive a change of angle. But there it stood, in the ambered glass, in the reflected hallway, patient as furniture.

And then it moved.

Not much. A shift of weight, the way a person shifts when they've been standing a long time and intend to keep standing. The motion had a lag to it—a beat behind, like a film out of sync with its sound. Kestrel watched the figure's head begin to turn, slowly, toward him—or toward the mirror's version of him, the Kestrel in the glass who stood at the near end of the reflected hall with his hand still on the doorframe of the blue room.

He looked down at his own hand. It was on the doorframe. He looked up. In the mirror, the reflected Kestrel's hand was still lifting toward the frame. Not there yet.

One second behind. Maybe less. Maybe more. Enough that he could see it, if he looked, if he let himself look: the glass showing him the hallway as it had been a breath ago, and himself a breath ago, and the figure standing in a breath that hadn't happened yet—or had happened and was only now catching up.

*It's a trick of the light,* he thought. *Old mirrors warp. The silvering goes bad in patches, and the light comes back at slightly different times—*

That wasn't how light worked. He knew that wasn't how light worked. He'd known it since he was a child with a flashlight and a science book, and knowing it didn't help at all, because the rational explanation he reached for was already dead on arrival and his hands were reaching for it anyway, the way Aldous's fingers reached for numbers.

The figure's head finished turning.

He couldn't see a face. The glass was too dark at that distance, too amber, and the figure stood just at the boundary where the window's light gave out. But he had the absolute, animal certainty that it was looking at him. Had been looking at him. Maybe for a long time. Maybe for twelve years, and tonight was simply the first night he'd let himself catch it.

"Hello?" he said, and hated how small his voice came out.

The figure didn't answer. The figure didn't move. The reflected Kestrel in the mirror finally put his hand on the doorframe, a second behind the real one, and Kestrel watched himself do what he had already done, and the wrongness of it went through him like cold water—not fear exactly, not yet, but the *ground* of fear, the substrate underneath twelve years of pipes and settling wood, the thing all his rationalizations had been built on top of like a floor over a cellar.

From the blue room, Mr. Aldous began to hum.

Kestrel knew the tune. That was the horrible thing—he knew it, had known it for years, had heard it from the mouths of the dying in this house again and again and had filed it under *end-of-life vocalization, common in dementia and terminal delirium*, a clinical little box to put it in so he never had to notice that it was always the *same* tune, that every dying resident in twelve years had hummed the same descending sequence of notes, that it wasn't random at all but a melody, a *song*, and that he had been hearing it his entire adult life in this house without once letting himself ask what song it was.

*Seven for the sorrow, six for the stair...*

The words surfaced in his mind unbidden, in a child's cadence, a skipping-rope rhythm, and he didn't know where they came from and he didn't want to know.

He made himself turn away from the mirror. He made himself walk down the hall, past the glass, not looking at it—feeling it at his back the whole way, feeling the figure in it, feeling his own lagged self trailing a second behind him like a shadow that had learned to be late. He stepped into the blue room and closed the door and stood with his hand on the knob, breathing.

The humming continued, soft and content, from the bed. Aldous's eyes were closed. His fingers counted against the blanket.

"Mr. Aldous," Kestrel said quietly. "What's the song?"

The old man smiled without opening his eyes. "You know the song."

"I don't."

"Everybody knows the song." The humming resumed, gentle as a lullaby. "You just have to be dying to remember it."

Kestrel sat down in the chair by the bed. He did his job. He checked the drip, moistened Aldous's lips, held his papery hand when the old man's breathing hitched. He was good at this. Twelve years good at this. He knew how to be calm at a deathbed, how to make the small hours bearable, how to keep the fear in a box in the back of his chest where it couldn't reach the person who needed him steady.

But his eyes kept going to the blue room's vanity mirror—the small oval one over the dresser—and in it he could see the door, and through the door's frosted pane, the dim amber glow of the hallway. And he could not stop watching the pane, waiting for a shape to cross it. Waiting for the figure to have moved closer. Knowing, with the certainty that lives underneath rationalization like water under ice, that when he finally opened that door, the hall would be exactly as empty as it had always been, and the mirror would show it exactly one second late, and the figure would be standing exactly where it stood, and he would say *pipes* again, and *exhaustion* again, and *old houses* again, and he would keep saying it until the day the house stopped letting him.

Aldous's breathing slowed toward dawn. The humming softened with it. And just before the sky went gray, just before the 3:00 tick in the stairwell, the old man's fingers stopped counting, and his voice, barely a thread, finished the line he'd been circling all night:

"...five for the family, kept behind the wall."

Then silence. Then breath again—shallower, but still there. Still holding on.

Kestrel sat in the gray light and did not move for a long time.

Then he stood, opened the door, and looked down the hallway at the tall dark mirror at the end of it, where the morning light was just beginning to reach, and where—for one clear, unguarded moment, before exhaustion could build its wall of pipes and explanations again—he saw the reflected hall standing perfectly, exactly, one second behind his own.

And in it, at the far end, the figure raised one hand.

Not a wave. Not a threat. Something almost like a greeting. The way you'd greet someone you'd been waiting a very long time for.

Kestrel closed the door, and stood in the blue room with his back against it, and listened to Mr. Aldous breathe, and told himself, one more time, in the voice he'd been using for twelve years:

*Pipes.*

But downstairs, faint through three floors of old wood, the house made its 2:14 sound again—two hours early—and for the first time since he'd come to Pemberton house as a boy, Kestrel Pemberton did not pretend he hadn't heard it.
