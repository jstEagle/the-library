## 2. Counting Backwards

The morphine clock said 3:14 when Aldous began to hum.

Kestrel had been in the room for six hours, doing the quiet arithmetic of the dying—pulse, breath, the shallow tide of it—and he knew the sound before he knew what it was. Not a moan. Not the wet rattle that came at the end of pneumonia cases. A tune. Small, deliberate, almost cheerful, rising out of a throat that hadn't managed a full sentence since Tuesday.

He set down the chart and leaned closer.

*One for the door, two for the stair,* Aldous hummed. *Three for the hall where nobody's there.*

The melody was simple. Nursery-simple. The kind of thing you'd expect from a jump-rope chant or a lullaby someone's grandmother had carried over an ocean. But Aldous was eighty-seven years old, born in Dayton, no grandmothers from anywhere Kestrel knew of, and the words had a shape to them that made the back of Kestrel's neck prickle—a shape like something being *counted*. Like each verse was a coin being set on a table, one by one, and the table was getting fuller.

"Mr. Aldous." He kept his voice low, nurse-voice, the voice that didn't startle. "You're dreaming, sir."

Aldous's eyes opened. That was the first wrong thing. Dying men didn't open their eyes like that—clear, focused, fixed on the ceiling above Kestrel's head as if reading something written there.

"It goes backwards," Aldous said. His voice was thin but steady. "Did you know that? Everybody thinks you count up. You count *down*. That's how you know it's real."

"Sir—"

"*Four for the window, five for the wall.*" Aldous's hand lifted off the blanket, fingers moving like he was pressing keys. "*Six for the ones who never left at all.*"

Kestrel had been a hospice nurse for twelve years. He had heard dying people speak to mothers dead fifty years, argue with sergeants long decommissioned, beg forgiveness from children who'd never existed. He had a whole taxonomy of terminal delirium filed away in his head, and he reached for it now the way you reach for a light switch in a familiar dark room.

But the song didn't fit any drawer he had.

Because he *knew* it. Not the words—he was certain he'd never heard the words—but the melody sat in him like a stone sits in a shoe. Familiar in the way the house was familiar. In the way the third step's creak was familiar, and the way the pipes knocked twice at 2 a.m., and the way the hallway mirror outside Room 4 held its images a half-second too long after you walked past, which was nothing, which was old glass, which was exhaustion, which was twelve years of night shifts finally collecting their tax.

"Where did you learn that?" he asked.

Aldous smiled. It transformed his face—made him look like a boy, made Kestrel's chest ache with a tenderness he usually kept holstered during shifts. "Learned it? Nobody learns it. You just find out you know it." His eyes drifted back to the ceiling. "It's the house singing through whoever's leaving. Cheaper than a bell."

"That's not—" Kestrel stopped. Recalibrated. Nurse-voice again. "Can I get you anything? More water?"

"*Seven for the kitchen, eight for the door.*"

"Aldous."

The old man's hand found Kestrel's wrist. His grip was stronger than it had any right to be. His skin was cool and papery and his pulse under Kestrel's fingers was a bird trapped in a wall, and he said, very clearly, very soberly:

"Don't learn the last one."

Then his eyes closed, and the humming went on without him, smooth as water finding a channel, and Kestrel stood there holding a dying man's wrist while the man counted down a house.

---

At 4:40, Aldous stopped between verses.

Not trailed off. Stopped. The way a person stops at the top of a staircase when they hear something below. His breathing continued—slow, mechanical, the body finishing its paperwork—but the song halted so cleanly that the silence had edges.

Kestrel waited. Ten minutes. Twenty. He checked the pulse, noted the rhythm, adjusted the drip. At 5:05, Aldous's lips moved again, and this time there was sound, barely:

"What comes after nine?"

"I don't know," Kestrel said, before he could stop himself.

"Nobody tells you." Aldous sounded almost aggrieved, a man complaining about a missing final chapter. "They all stop at nine. Every single one of them stops at nine, and then they look at you like—" His face worked. "Like they're waiting for permission."

"Who does?"

"The others." Aldous turned his head on the pillow. For a moment his eyes were wet and ordinary and terrified, an eighty-seven-year-old man in a failing body, and Kestrel could breathe again. "The ones who died here. They all hum it, son. Ask Marisol. She'll pretend she doesn't know, but ask her. Ask Dev. Ask anyone who's worked this floor longer than a winter." His grip tightened. "We're not raving. We're *rehearsing*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

The word landed in Kestrel's chest and stayed there, cold and specific.

*Rehearsing.*

Rehearsal implied performance. Performance implied an audience. And an audience implied—

Aldous exhaled, long and slow, and the monitor's tone flattened into the sound Kestrel knew better than his own heartbeat, and somewhere in the walls—or maybe just in Kestrel's ears—the house seemed to settle, satisfied, like a guest leaning back from a finished meal.

* * *

He called it in at 5:20. Time of death 5:12. He did the things you do: closed the eyes, folded the hands, straightened the blanket into a shape that looked like rest rather than stopping. He opened the window a crack because Marisol said souls were heavy and needed help leaving, and even though Kestrel had long ago filed that under kindness rather than belief, he still did it every time, because some rituals aren't about whether they work.

Dev arrived at 6 with coffee and red-rimmed eyes. Twenty-three years old, all elbows and earnestness, the closest thing Kestrel had to a little brother. Dev had cried at his first death and still cried at most of them, and Kestrel loved him for it in a way that embarrassed them both.

"He go easy?" Dev asked.

"He sang."

Dev's cup paused halfway to his mouth. "Sang."

"Hummed. Some kind of counting song. Kids' rhyme, maybe." Kestrel watched Dev's face the way he'd learned to watch faces in this house—for the flicker behind the expression, the thing people did instead of answering. "You've heard it."

It wasn't a question, and they both knew it wasn't.

Dev set the coffee down. Looked at the window, at the gray coming up over the garden. "Everyone's heard it," he said. "That's what I was going to say. Everyone who's worked here. It's—it's one of those things. Old houses have sounds. This house has a song. You get used to it."

"You don't get used to a song, Dev. You get used to pipes. You get used to floorboards. Songs have *words*."

"Yeah, well." Dev picked the coffee back up. Drank like the conversation owed him money. "Words are probably just—your brain filling in. Like when you think you heard your name across a crowded room. Auditory pareidolia. I read about it."

Kestrel let it go. That was the arrangement, the unspoken treaty of the Pemberton house: everyone noticed the strangeness, and everyone agreed not to notice it together. Twelve years of that treaty. Twelve years of covering mirrors at dusk without asking why, of not answering knocks after three, of walking past the sealed parlor on the second floor with his eyes forward like a good communicant. He'd told himself it was respect for an old building's quirks. He'd told himself a lot of things at 3 a.m.

But *rehearsing* was still sitting in his chest, going colder by the hour.

* * *

Marisol was in the kitchen at seven, as she had been every morning of Kestrel's working life, kneading bread dough with the forearms of a woman half her seventy years. She didn't ask about Aldous. She saw it in his face; she always saw everything first.

"He asked me to tell you something," Kestrel said. "He said you'd pretend not to know."

Marisol's hands kept moving. Fold, press, turn. "Old men say many things."

"He said the dying all sing it. All of them. Going back decades. He said we're not delirious—we're rehearsing." He watched her knuckles whiten inside the dough. "And he said they all stop at nine. Before the last verse. Every time."

The kitchen clock ticked. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice, and Kestrel watched Marisol's head tilt toward the sound and back, quick as a bird's, a reflex so practiced it had become invisible.

"Sit," she said finally. "Eat something. You've been on shift fourteen hours."

"Marisol."

"You are tired, and tired men hear songs in everything." She shaped the dough with sudden violence. "I will tell you what I tell everyone: this house is old, old houses make noises, and the dying reach for whatever comfort their minds can hold. A children's rhyme is comfort. That is all."

"That's not what Aldous said."

"Aldous is dead." She said it flatly, without cruelty, the way she said everything true. "Dead men are very certain and cannot be argued with. Eat."

He ate. Because she'd raised him, more or less—because she'd been there at six years old when his parents drove away and didn't come back, because she'd bandaged his knees and taught him Spanish lullabies and stood in this kitchen for fifty years like a load-bearing wall. Because refusing her breakfast felt like a kind of blasphemy.

But halfway through the toast, he heard himself say: "What's the last verse, Marisol?"

Her hands went still.

It lasted maybe two seconds. Long enough for the clock to tick three times. Long enough for Kestrel to understand that he had just asked the wrong question in the only house where questions had assigned seats.

"There is no last verse," Marisol said, and resumed kneading. "Songs end where singers stop. Now go sleep. You look like your father."

He climbed the stairs past the hallway mirror, and he couldn't help it—he looked. His own face looked back, on time, exhausted, ordinary. Just glass. Just old glass.

In his room, he lay down and did not sleep. He lay there turning over the morning's inventory: a dying man who sang a song nobody taught him, a brother-figure who'd read up on auditory pareidolia overnight-ready, a woman who'd gone still for exactly three ticks of a clock.

*Rehearsing.* Aldous had chosen the word carefully. Rehearsing meant the performance was scheduled. Meant there was a role to prepare for. Meant—

Meant that if the dying were practicing, someone had given them the material.

Kestrel got up, crossed to the small desk, and pulled out the notebook he kept for shift notes. On a fresh page, without quite deciding to, he wrote down the verses he could remember. His handwriting looked shaky, foreign.

*One for the door, two for the stair.*
*Three for the hall where nobody's there.*
*Four for the window, five for the wall.*
*Six for the ones who never left at all.*
*Seven for the kitchen, eight for the door.*
*Nine for...*

Nine for what?

He stared at the page. The line wouldn't come. It was like reaching for a word on the tip of his tongue—except the word wasn't on his tongue. It was somewhere else entirely, somewhere he'd apparently already been, because his pen had known to stop at nine without his telling it to.

He hadn't written that pause. His hand had.

Kestrel set the pen down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

Downstairs, faintly, through two floors of old wood, he could hear Marisol humming to herself as she worked. A lullaby, he thought at first. One of her Spanish ones.

Except the melody was climbing downward, verse by verse, patient as stairs.

And it stopped at nine.
