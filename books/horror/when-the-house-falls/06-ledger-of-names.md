## 6. Ledger of Names

The attic stairs were the only part of the house Kestrel had never learned to stop fearing.

Twelve years of night shifts taught a man which creaks meant nothing. The third step on the main landing, the loose board outside the linen closet, the groan of the east wall when the temperature dropped—all of it filed under *pipes, settling, old wood doing what old wood does*. He'd built a career out of that filing system. Built a life.

But the attic stairs made a sound he'd never been able to file: a slow, deliberate drag, like something being pulled across the floor above him whenever he stood at the bottom and looked up. Marisol said it was the wind in the gable vents. Dev said it was raccoons. Kestrel said nothing at all, because saying it out loud would have required admitting he'd heard it, and there were still rooms in his denial he kept swept and dark.

Tonight he climbed them anyway.

The hand mirror was in his back pocket. It had been in his back pocket for three days now, riding against his hip like a weapon he didn't know how to fire. Every few hours he'd take it out, hold it up, and watch the world arrive late—Aldous's empty bed already cold, the hallway lamp already swinging from a draft he hadn't felt yet. One second. Sometimes two. This morning, brushing his teeth, he'd watched his lagged self finish spitting before he did, and he'd had to sit down on the edge of the tub until his hands stopped shaking.

Exhaustion, he'd told himself. Grief. Aldous was dead four days and Kestrel hadn't slept properly since the humming started, and tired eyes did strange things with time.

But exhaustion didn't explain why the lagged room behind him, in the mirror's delayed second, always seemed to have one more piece of furniture than the real one. A chair where no chair stood. A coat rack by the door that wasn't there. And once—a figure, tall, in an old-fashioned coat, standing exactly where the coat rack should be, as if the two things were the same thing seen from different years.

So: the attic. Because Marisol left every morning at nine to shop, because Dev slept until noon after his overnights, and because if there was an answer in this house about what it was, it would be in the place where answers go when nobody wants them anymore.

The drag-sound came again as he reached the top, right beside his ear, though the attic stretched empty before him—rafters, dust sheets, boxes in ranks like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been postponed for a century.

"Okay," Kestrel said quietly, to the house, to himself, to whatever filed sounds for a living. "Okay."

* * *

The dust sheets were the first wrong thing.

They were draped over furniture he'd never seen despite twelve years in this house—and they were draped *recently*. No gray film of decades, no mouse-chewed corners. The folds were sharp. Someone had covered these pieces within the last year, maybe the last season, and Kestrel found himself lifting the nearest sheet with the particular dread of a man checking a wound.

Underneath: a rocking chair, walnut, its arms worn pale by hands. Not antique-worn. *Used*-worn. Beside it, a small table bearing a reading lamp with a fringed shade, and a romance paperback whose spine was cracked at page ninety.

He knew that book. His mother had read it aloud to him once, or he'd dreamed she had—he'd never been sure which, because he'd been six when she vanished and memory at six is a rumor you spend your whole life trying to verify.

He put the sheet back. His hands were very steady, which felt like someone else's steadiness, borrowed.

* * *

The ledger was in a trunk under the dormer window, beneath a quilt and a hatbox of photographs he couldn't bring himself to open yet. It was a fat, square book bound in green leather gone black at the corners, and the word **PEMBERTON** was stamped on the cover in gold that had dulled to the color of old teeth.

He carried it to the dormer seat, into the gray light, and opened it.

It was not a family Bible. It was not a photo album. It was a *ledger*—columns ruled in faded red ink, entries in a dozen different hands across a century, some careful copperplate, some hurried scrawl, one entire decade written in pencil by someone who pressed so hard the pages behind were braille.

At the top of each page, the same heading:

**RESIDENTS — PEMBERTON HOUSE — COMMENCING 1911**

And below it, in the oldest hand, the founding entry:

*Elias Pemberton, proprietor. Wife, Adeline. Sons, Cyrus and Nathaniel. Household sealed per agreement, all parties consenting, all parties remaining.*

Kestrel read it three times. *All parties remaining.* The phrase sat wrong in his chest, like a bone swallowed sideways. Nobody wrote *remaining* about a family unless leaving had been discussed.

He turned the page.

The entries ran year by year, and they were not family records. They were admissions. Dates, names, ages, and a final column headed simply **TERMS**. And the terms, page after page, decade after decade, were all variations on the same small set of phrases:

*Delivered per agreement.*

*Per arrangement with household.*

*Received in exchange for continued quiet.*

*Terms fulfilled. Resident satisfied.*

Nurses. Housekeepers. Cooks. A gardener named Ovid Rill, 1938, *delivered per agreement, terms: voice.* A handyman, 1952, *terms: hands.* Over and over, the house taking payment in body parts listed like line items, and Kestrel's nurse's mind catalogued it with horrible fluency—voice, hands, hearing, face—the inventory of a person, itemized.

He told himself it was a joke. Some morbid ancestor's accounting game. He got as far as 1970 on that theory.

Then the handwriting changed to a small, cramped script he almost recognized, and the entry read:

*Tomas Reyes, age 9, delivered per agreement. Terms: hearing. Note—he hears the counting. Resident pleased.*

The attic went very quiet around him, the way a room goes quiet when it's listening back.

Tomas. Marisol had mentioned him exactly once, eleven years ago, wine-drunk on New Year's Eve: a boy who'd lived here before Kestrel, who'd drowned, she'd said, in the creek, before she ever took the position. She'd said it looking at the window instead of at Kestrel, and she'd never said it again, and he had done what he always did with the house's wrongness—filed it, swept the room, turned off the light.

*He hears the counting.*

Aldous, humming in his last hours. The counting song, counting down. And a boy entered into a ledger as payment, his *hearing* listed as the purchased item—as if the ability itself were the thing the house wanted delivered. As if hearing the song weren't a symptom but a *qualification*.

Kestrel's mouth had gone dry. He turned pages faster now, past Tomas's terse follow-up entries—*terms fulfilled, 1979*; *replacement required*—and then the replacement, and then:

**K. Pemberton, age 6, delivered per agreement.**

His own name. His own age. Six years old, the year his parents drove through the hills and never arrived anywhere again.

He read the full entry the way you read a diagnosis, twice, because the first time doesn't take:

*K. Pemberton, age 6, delivered per agreement. Parents' terms discharged in full. Child retained as hearer. Note—responds to counting without instruction. Resident pleased. Long-term placement approved.*

*Parents' terms discharged in full.*

There it was. Not *lost*. Not *deceased*. *Discharged*—a debt settled, a transaction closed. His parents hadn't vanished. They'd been *spent*. And whatever was left over—the child, the ears, the boy who could hear the counting without being taught—had been retained. Filed. Kept.

Kept for twenty-six years in a house that paid its staff in body parts, fed by people he loved, tended by a woman who braided his hair when he was small and taught him to make soup and never, not once, told him he was livestock.

He set the ledger down on his knees because his arms had stopped wanting to hold things.

* * *

The light moved. He let it. He sat in the dormer window while the afternoon went gold and then copper, the ledger open in his lap, and he did the thing he'd done his whole life, which was to reach for the rational explanation and find the shelf bare.

Not a joke—the entries spanned a century and matched no single joker's lifetime. Not madness—madness didn't come with columns and terms and *long-term placement approved*. Not coincidence—because the entry for 1998 described a six-year-old brought to the house *per agreement*, and Kestrel Pemberton had arrived at this house at six years old with no explanation anyone had ever given him beyond *your aunt arranged it*, and he had no aunt. He'd checked, as a teenager. There was no aunt. There had never been an aunt.

There was a ledger, and he was in it, between a boy named Tomas whose hearing was purchased and a column of terms that read like a butcher's order form.

And underneath the horror, quieter and worse, something else was moving. Because the entry said *responds to counting without instruction*, and Kestrel thought of Aldous humming, and he realized—with the specific vertigo of a man finding a trapdoor under his own kitchen—that he had known the melody. Not recognized it. *Known* it. When Aldous hummed it that last night, Kestrel's lips had wanted to follow, had shaped the next note before his mind caught up, and he'd blamed grief and fatigue.

He hadn't been catching the song from the dying.

He'd been keeping it. All along. The way the house kept everything.

* * *

He heard the stairs when the sun was nearly down—not the drag-sound but footsteps, human ones, unhurried, and then Marisol's voice floating up through the floorboards, calling his name the way she'd called it since he was six, the way that used to mean *dinner* and *safe* and *you're wanted here*.

"Kestrel? You up there?"

He looked down at the ledger. At his own name in a dead stranger's handwriting. At the words *retained as hearer*.

Every instinct he had—the twelve-year instinct, the practiced one—said close the book, carry it down, say *found some old papers, isn't that something*, and file this too, sweep the room, turn off the light. That instinct had kept him functional in a house that ate people. That instinct was also, he understood now, exactly what it had been built to be: a lid. Something installed in him young, maintained lovingly, by people who needed their bait calm.

"Kestrel?"

He closed the ledger. Held it against his chest. Stood.

"Yeah," he called down, and his voice came out level, and he hated how good he'd gotten at that. "Yeah, I'm coming."

He paused at the top of the stairs, the green book heavy under his arm, and looked back once at the sheeted furniture—at his mother's chair, her lamp, her cracked paperback at page ninety—and made himself say the true thing out loud, quietly, to the listening dark:

"You chose me before I ever chose you."

The house said nothing. But somewhere below, faint and far off, in a register only he seemed able to hear, someone had begun—very softly—to count.
