## 12. Mrs. Okonkwo's Last Night

The morphine clock said it was nearly time, but Mrs. Okonkwo's body had its own opinions, and for three days now it had refused to agree with the chart. Kestrel had learned long ago not to argue with the dying about their schedules. They always knew better.

He sat in the blue chair by her window with a cup of tea going cold on the sill and the lamp turned low. The house was quiet in the way it only got after two in the morning—not silent, never silent, because Pemberton House had never once been silent in his twelve years there, but quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Pipes ticking somewhere below. The old radiator in the hall knocking out its arrhythmic code that Marisol swore was just expansion and contraction and that Kestrel had stopped believing was anything of the kind around the same time he'd started seeing the mirrors lag.

He'd covered hers, of course. The little vanity mirror on her dresser faced the wall now, a towel over it, per House Rules. But he'd left the window uncovered, because she liked to see the garden, and because the glass in the old panes didn't count as mirror. He'd checked. Twice.

"Kestrel." Her voice came out papery, amused. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The listening thing. You sit like a man at a train station waiting to hear something terrible come down the tracks." Her eyes opened—cloudy, but sharp underneath, the way they'd been sharp all month while the rest of her folded up like a letter being put away. "Sit like a man at a bedside instead. It's kinder to both of us."

He smiled and did as he was told, dragging the chair closer. Up close she smelled of lavender talc and the particular sweetness the body gives off when it's begun negotiating its own surrender. Her hands lay on the coverlet, dark and elegant, fingers curled loosely around nothing.

"I'm not afraid," she said. "I want you to know that first, before anything else tonight. Whatever you see or hear in this room before sunrise—I am not afraid."

"That's a strange thing to preface."

"It's a necessary thing to preface." She turned her head toward him. In the lamplight her face was all planes and patience. "You've been asking about the song."

His stomach dropped. He hadn't asked her directly since that afternoon in the garden when she'd taught him the fourth verse—the one about the winter the well froze from the bottom up—and then closed her mouth like a gate when he'd tried to ask what came next. *Not that one,* she'd said. *Never that one aloud.*

"I haven't—"

"You've been humming it wrong in the hallway," she said. "Verse two. You keep rushing the third line. The song doesn't like to be rushed." She paused. "Neither does whatever listens to it."

The radiator knocked. Once. Twice. Then, faintly, from somewhere under the floor, a third knock that hadn't matched the pattern.

Kestrel kept his voice level. "Mrs. Okonkwo. What happens if you sing all of it?"

"Ah." She exhaled slowly, and the exhale seemed to take some of her with it, deflating her against the pillows. "There it is. The question. Everyone who hears the song eventually asks the question." Her fingers moved on the blanket, tracing a small shape over and over—a circle, maybe, or a keyhole. "And everyone who asks is already halfway to answering it."

"I don't understand."

"No," she agreed gently. "But you will. Before dawn, I think. That's why I asked you to sit with me tonight. Not because I need nursing." A dry click that might have been a laugh. "I need a witness."

"A witness to what?"

"To my last verse," she said. "The one I get to choose."

* * *

The hours went soft after that. She dozed; he dozed in the chair with his neck at the angle that would punish him tomorrow. At some point he got up and changed her position, checked her pulse, did the small mechanical kindnesses of his trade, and she let him without complaint, watching him with an expression he couldn't read—something between gratitude and apology.

At four-something, the quality of the darkness changed.

He couldn't have explained how. The room was the same room, the lamp the same lamp, but the shadows pulled inward slightly, the way water pulls back from a shore before a wave. The cold spot he'd been pretending not to notice near the foot of the bed spread upward along the mattress like frost claiming a windowpane. And the sound—the sound he'd been hearing all his life in this house without letting himself name it—rose up through the floorboards.

Counting.

*One for the stone the house was raised on,* sang a voice, high and sweet and wrong, *two for the door that opens both ways—*

It was Mrs. Okonkwo's voice.

That was the thing that made his hands go cold on the arms of the chair. Not the melody—he knew the melody now, had it stitched into him since childhood, had hummed it in hallways without knowing what he was carrying. It was that the voice singing was unmistakably hers, the exact grain and timbre of it, the slight crack on the high notes—and Mrs. Okonkwo's lips were not moving. Her mouth was closed. Her chest barely rose. And out of her stillness poured the song, perfect, note-perfect, every rhythm exactly where it belonged, none of it rushed.

*Three for the children counted into walls, four for the wife who wouldn't stop her keening—*

"Stop," Kestrel whispered. He didn't know who he was talking to. "Stop it, leave her alone—"

Her eyes opened. They were fixed on the ceiling, and they were shining, and she was smiling.

*Five for the ledger with its patient columns, six for the boy delivered per agreement—*

His own name almost surfaced in the melody and slid past, a stone in a current, and he felt it go by like a hand brushing the back of his neck.

*Seven for the hearer taken in 'seventy-nine, eight for the dumbwaiter's hungry throat—*

Tomas. It was singing Tomas. The boy from 1979 whose face had frayed off him like wet paper. The song wasn't nonsense. It wasn't a nursery rhyme. It was a *record*. Every verse a year, a wound, a fact the house had swallowed and kept.

*Nine for the nurse who counts the seconds late, ten for the mirrors holding back their breath—*

The Resident. He understood it suddenly, wholly, the way you understand a fall only once you're falling: the song was not an invitation. It was a *leash*. Someone had made this thing wear a song like a collar, verse by verse, naming its crimes in order, binding it to the truth of what it had done. As long as the verses were sung true, the thing in the mirrors had to listen. Had to *stay named.*

And the last verse—

The last verse was the handle of the leash. The end of the rope. Whoever held it could pull—or could let go.

*Eleven for the vigil kept till morning, twelve for the witness at the bed—*

The voice faltered. Just slightly. Mrs. Okonkwo's chest hitched, and for a moment the song wavered like a radio losing signal, and beneath it Kestrel heard—faintly, horribly—another voice trying to surface through hers, older and drier and patient as dust, a voice practicing, rehearsing, learning the shape of a throat it intended to keep.

No. Not learning. *Correcting.* The way you correct a student.

She rallied. He saw her do it—saw her gather herself the way he'd seen a hundred patients gather themselves for one last act, one last word, one last goodbye. Her lips parted. This time they moved with the voice.

*Thirteen for the door that was never locked from inside.*

And then she stopped. Four verses' worth of counting, flawless, and silence where the fifth should be, and the cold spot at the foot of the bed drew itself up like a question denied an answer.

"Don't," Kestrel breathed. "Whatever it wants from you, don't—"

"Kestrel." Her voice was her own again, thin and spent, but hers. She found his hand on the blanket and gripped it with surprising strength. "Listen to me. It needed my voice because a dying voice can carry the verses true—it can't lie, we're too close to the door to lie. So it borrowed me. Fine. Let it borrow." Her smile widened, and there was triumph in it, actual triumph, blazing up through the gray exhaustion of her face. "But I chose which verses. I chose where to stop. Do you understand? It sang through me, yes—but *I* held the leash. All night. Right to the very edge of the last verse, and I did not give it the handle."

The cold in the room curdled. The lamp dimmed though the filament didn't flicker. And in the black window glass behind her head—just for a second—Kestrel saw the reflection of the room arrive late, half a heartbeat behind reality, and in that delayed image Mrs. Okonkwo was sitting up, and her face was not entirely her face, and it was looking at him.

Then it settled. The window showed only the garden, the dark lawn, the honest night.

"It's angry," she said, delighted. "Oh, it's been a long time since anyone made it angry."

"Mrs. Okonkwo—"

"Adaeze," she said. "Call me Adaeze. We're past the honorifics, you and I. I'm about to die in front of you; the least we can do is be on first-name terms." She coughed, wet and deep, and rode it out, and when it passed she was still smiling. "Now. The important part. Are you listening?"

"I'm listening."

"The song is a leash. You've seen it now. Four verses sung true and it has to stand still and be counted." Her grip tightened. "But a leash cuts both ways, Kestrel. Whoever sings the last verse doesn't summon the Resident. They *claim* it. Or—" her eyes glittered, "—they *unname* it. Take the collar off. Open the door that was never locked from inside. Thirteen. The thirteenth verse isn't a command. It's a mercy. And mercies are the most dangerous things in any house like this one."

"Which is it?" His voice cracked. "If someone sings it—does it free them or free *it*?"

"Yes," she said.

The lamp steadied. The cold receded, grudging, like a tide going out. Somewhere far below them, deep in the bones of the house, something slammed—a door, or a wall, or a jaw.

"And that," Adaeze said softly, "is why nobody has ever sung it. Not in a hundred years. Because no one could know which way the blade would fall until it fell." She released his hand and lay back, and the smile stayed on her face like something carved. "But you're not going to sing it. Are you. I've watched you for weeks, Kestrel Pemberton. You listen the way other people pray. You'll find the way to use the verse without giving it your voice. Mouth it. Write it. Think it into the walls. There's more than one way to hold a leash, and the ones who get eaten are always the ones who insist on using their mouths."

The sky outside was going the color of dishwater. Dawn, or the rumor of it.

"Stay," she said. "Witness. That's all I need now. Just—stay, and don't look away, and don't be afraid on my behalf. I've done my counting. I know exactly which year I am."

So he stayed. He held her hand through the last hour, and the sun came up thin and white through the garden window, and somewhere in that thin white light her breathing spaced itself out like footsteps walking away down a long corridor, and then stopped altogether, and the room was just a room, and the woman in the bed was just a woman who had died smiling, genuinely smiling, her face eased and young and *hers*, worn by no one but herself, right up to the final second.

Kestrel sat with her a long time after. He closed her eyes. He straightened the blanket. He did the things his hands knew how to do so that the rest of him didn't have to think.

And when he finally stood, legs stiff, eyes burning, he caught sight of himself in the darkened television screen across the room—his own shape, arriving a full second late, standing in a room that was already empty.

In the lagged image, his mouth was open.

Singing.

* * *

He found Marisol in the kitchen at six, kneading bread dough she had no intention of baking, her knuckles white, her back rigid. She took one look at his face and stopped.

"She's gone," Marisol said. Not a question.

"Gone." He braced his hands on the counter across from her. Flour dust hung in the light between them. "Marisol. It sang through her. Four verses, perfect, in her voice while her mouth was shut. And she stopped it. She *chose* where to stop, and it couldn't make her finish." His voice shook, and he let it. "It's not a summoning song. It's a leash. And the last verse—"

"I know what the last verse is."

The words landed like a dropped glass. Kestrel stared at her. "You—"

"Fifty years in this house, mijo." Marisol wiped her hands slowly on her apron, and her face was old in the morning light, older than he'd ever let himself see. "You think I scrub these floors and cover these mirrors without knowing what for?" She looked at him for a long moment, and something in her expression broke and reassembled itself into resolve. "Sit down. Both of us. There are things I should have told you when you were six years old, and God forgive me, I kept telling myself tomorrow."

From the direction of the dining room came a sound neither of them moved toward: the dumbwaiter, descending on its own, cables groaning in the wall, stopping at the ground floor with a soft, expectant clatter.

Waiting to be fed.

Marisol untied her apron with steady hands. "First," she said, "you're going to tell me everything she said. Every word. And then I'm going to tell you about a boy named Tomas, and about the agreement, and about why a six-year-old was brought to this house in the back of a car with tinted windows."

The dumbwaiter clattered again. Impatient.

Kestrel looked at the shaft, then at the woman who had raised him, and felt grief for Adaeze sharpening, hardening, cooling into something with an edge. Resolve, maybe. Or a blade.

"Start with Tomas," he said.
