## 16. The House Closes

The first door to go was the linen closet.

Kestrel noticed it at four in the morning, on his way back from checking on Mrs. Okonkwo's empty room—empty now, stripped of her presence but not yet of her smell, lavender and something mineral underneath, like wet stone. He'd been walking that hallway for twelve years. His feet knew it the way his hands knew a pulse: without looking, without thinking.

So when he reached for the linen closet handle and found flat wall instead, he stood there for a long moment with his hand pressed against wallpaper that had never existed before this morning.

He knocked. The wall answered with the dull thud of plaster over lath. No hollow sound. No seam. As if the closet had never been there at all—as if the house had simply decided, sometime in the last hour, that it had always been this way, and history had agreed to go along.

"Okay," Kestrel said quietly, to no one, because saying things out loud had become a way of proving he was still the one doing the talking.

He went downstairs to tell Dev and found the front door gone too.

Not locked. Not boarded. *Gone.* Where the door had been there was now an expanse of wall with a window in it—a window that shouldn't have been there, overlooking a street that didn't exist anymore. A 1954 street. Kestrel knew the year the way you know your own birthday, from the ledger, from Marisol's stories, from the photographs in the parlor he was never supposed to find. A Hudson Hornet parked at the curb, chrome bright as a held breath. A woman in a shirtwaist dress walking a dog that paused mid-stride, frozen, its head cocked toward the house as if it could hear something coming through the glass.

The woman kept walking. Only the dog looked.

Kestrel backed away from the window and turned around, and the hallway behind him was longer than it should have been. Longer by whole rooms. He counted doorways out of habit—nurse's habit, fire-safety habit—and got eleven where there should have been six. The extra doors were all closed, and behind them he could hear, faintly, the sounds of a house being lived in: a radio playing big band music, a kettle whistling, a child laughing once and then not laughing anymore.

*1954,* he thought. *It's not showing me the past. It's moving into it.*

The lag had started eating time instead of just borrowing it.

* * *

He found Dev in the kitchen, standing at the counter with both hands flat on the surface like a man bracing against weather. Marisol wasn't there. Her room upstairs was dark, and when Kestrel had passed it, the door had been open onto a room that was somehow larger than the footprint of the house allowed, shadows pooling in corners at angles the light couldn't explain.

"She took the dumbwaiter down," Dev said, before Kestrel asked. His voice was scraped raw. "After the bargain. She said—" He stopped. Started again. "She said if she's not back by dawn, don't come looking."

"It's not dawn for hours."

"I know." Dev finally looked up, and Kestrel saw that his eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which was worse than crying. "Kestrel, the kitchen window's been showing my grandmother's backyard since midnight. I watched myself rake leaves in it. I was eight years old. I was wearing a shirt she made me before she died." He laughed, a small broken sound. "The reflection raked leaves for twenty minutes and then it stopped and looked at me and smiled, and it wasn't my smile. It was too wide. It was practicing."

Kestrel pulled him away from the window, gently, the way you move a patient away from something upsetting. Old reflexes. "We need to stay together. We need to stay in rooms we can see each other in."

"That's not how it works anymore." Dev shook his head. "You haven't seen the second floor. The lag isn't a second now. It's—it's whole minutes. You walk past a mirror and the room in it is still doing what the real room did five minutes ago, except sometimes it does things the real room never did. There are people in those reflections, Kestrel. People standing in the delayed rooms, waiting for the present to catch up to them."

"The dead," Kestrel said. "Tomas and the others. They're trying to—"

"Or it's him." Dev's voice dropped. "Wearing the delay like a coat. Walking around in the seconds we've already spent."

As if summoned—or as if it had simply finished arriving—the lights dimmed.

Not flickered. Dimmed, evenly, all of them at once, down to a brown amber gloom, and in that gloom every reflective surface in the kitchen came alive with movement. The window over the sink. The dark glass of the oven door. The copper-bottomed pots hanging from the rack, each one holding a small warped world.

And in all of them, slightly out of sync with each other, slightly out of sync with reality, Kestrel watched himself walk into the kitchen.

But he was already in the kitchen. He was standing right here, holding Dev's arm.

The reflected Kestrel crossed the room at an unhurried pace, and the versions in the different surfaces moved at slightly different speeds, so that for a moment there were five of him, staggered across the kitchen like frames of film laid end to end. The one in the window reached the sink. The one in the oven door was still crossing. The one in the largest pot tilted its head—*his* head—at an angle Kestrel had never turned his own neck, studying the room with the patient curiosity of something learning how curiosity worked.

Its mouth was moving.

No sound came through the glass. But Kestrel could read lips; twelve years of hospice work, patients who couldn't speak above a whisper, patients who couldn't speak at all. And the thing wearing his face was mouthing words in a rhythm he recognized with a sick drop of his stomach.

Counting. Downward. Verse after verse after verse.

Practicing.

"He's learning it," Kestrel whispered. "In my voice. He's rehearsing the song in my voice so that when he sings the last verse, it'll count as *me* singing it."

Dev made a sound low in his throat. "Then don't let him finish."

"You think I'm going to stand here and outlast him?" Kestrel gestured at the windows, the pots, the whole silvered world closing in. "Dev, look at the house. It's not haunting us anymore. It's *digesting*. Every room it takes, every door it eats—that's not an attack. That's preparation. It's making the house into a throat."

* * *

They tried the stairs together, shoulder to shoulder, and the stairs let them up as if they wanted them upstairs, which was the most frightening thing yet. Twelve years of the house resisting him—sticking doors, cold spots, the sense of being tolerated rather than housed—and now it moved aside like a crowd parting for someone important.

The second floor was worse than Dev had described.

The hallway stretched under a ceiling that breathed, very slightly, rising and falling. Wallpaper patterns repeated with tiny variations, and in some of the variations the flowers had faces—not human faces, but the suggestion of them, the way clouds suggest things, except these suggestions were watching. Doors opened off the hall into rooms that showed 1954 through their windows: a summer afternoon, golden and still, a garden party on a lawn, women with victory-roll hair holding glasses of lemonade, men in suspenders laughing at something. None of them moved. All of them were smiling toward the house.

Toward him.

"They're not ghosts," Kestrel murmured, passing one window. "Look at them. They're not trapped. They're *waiting*. Like an audience."

"Kestrel." Dev had stopped at the bathroom door. His face had gone the color of candle wax. "Come look at this. Don't—just come look."

The bathroom mirror was lagging badly now. Three seconds, maybe four. In the glass, the bathroom showed itself a beat behind—but the beat was full. The lagged room contained the lagged Kestrel, standing at the sink, washing his hands. Water ran in the reflected basin though the real basin was dry. And the reflected Kestrel was talking to someone beside him, someone the mirror showed only from the shoulders down: a tall figure in an old-fashioned coat, standing close, familiarly close, the way family stands.

The figure's hand rested on the reflected Kestrel's shoulder.

The reflected Kestrel was nodding. Agreeing with whatever was being said. And then he turned his face toward the mirror—toward the present, toward the real Kestrel standing in the doorway—and smiled, and began to sing.

Sound came through the glass this time. Thin, warped, underwater—but unmistakably Kestrel's voice, singing the first verse of the counting song. Then the second. Perfect pitch. Perfect rhythm. The voice of a man who had spent years humming to dying patients to calm them, stolen and used as an instrument.

The real Kestrel clapped his hands over his ears and it didn't help, because the song wasn't only coming through the mirror anymore. It was coming through the pipes. Through the walls. Through the floorboards under his feet, in the deep register of the house's own bones.

"Third verse," Dev shouted over it. "It's on the third verse—Okonkwo said there's seven—"

"Five more," Kestrel said. "Five verses and then the last one, and if it sings the last one in my voice—"

The song stopped.

Mid-verse. Mid-word. The silence that followed was enormous, physical, pressing against their eardrums like depth. And in the mirror, the lagged Kestrel lowered his head and stood very still, and the tall figure leaned in close to the reflected ear, whispering something that made the reflected face frown—frown with Kestrel's exact frown, the one Marisol called his *stubborn face*, the one he made when a diagnosis didn't make sense.

Then the reflection pointed.

Directly at him. Through the glass. Through the seconds between them.

And every light in the house came back on at once, blazing, and the hallway beyond the bathroom door was no longer the hallway. It was the ballroom. The mirrored ballroom from the east wing that had been sealed since before Kestrel's time, that existed now as a doorway where a linen closet used to be, that waited at the end of a corridor of borrowed 1954 light with all its mirrors facing inward like a throat of silver.

At the far end of the ballroom, small with distance, something wearing his body raised one hand.

Beckoned.

"No," Kestrel said aloud, and the word felt absurd, inadequate, a paper shield. But he said it again, louder. "No. You don't get to call me. I'm not yours. I was never delivered to you—I was *born*, I had a mother, I had a name before this house—"

The house shuddered.

Not an earthquake-shudder. A swallowing-shudder. The floor rolled gently underfoot like the deck of a ship, and somewhere below them a door they would never find again closed with a sound like a held breath released. The windows along the hall went dark all at once—not night-dark, but *absence*-dark, the glass showing nothing at all, neither inside nor outside, as if the world beyond the house had been set aside for later.

The temperature dropped. Frost bloomed across the bathroom mirror in ferning patterns, and through the frost, faintly, the reflected Kestrel was still pointing.

"Marisol," Dev said suddenly. "The bargain—if it's closing the house, if it's sealing everything in for the renewal, then whatever it promised her—"

"Is happening right now." Kestrel grabbed his arm. "Which means she's still alive. Which means there's still something to save." He looked down the impossible hallway, at the ballroom door standing open like a patient mouth, at the small distant figure in his shape with its hand still raised, patient as winter.

Every instinct he had—the nurse's instinct, the caretaker's instinct, twelve years of *go toward the suffering*—pulled him down that hall. And beneath the instinct, colder and older, something else pulled too. Something that had been pulling since he was six years old and a car stopped in front of this house and a man carried him inside per agreement.

That was the trap, he understood now. It had never been the mirrors or the song or the doors. The trap was that some part of him had always belonged here, and the house was simply collecting what it had already been sold.

"We go down," Kestrel said. "Together. On our terms. And Dev—whatever you see in the mirrors down there, whatever wears my face and says my name and cries my tears—remember one thing."

"What?"

"It doesn't blink first." Kestrel started walking. "I do. It never learned that. It's been copying me for weeks and it still waits for me to blink."

Behind them, unhurried, the last door on the second floor quietly ceased to exist.

Ahead, the ballroom waited, and in its hundred mirrors, a hundred versions of him turned in unison to watch him come—some lagging, some leading, one of them already singing—and in the space between the glass and the world, in the widening second where anything might still be true, Kestrel Pemberton walked toward the thing that wanted to wear him, carrying the only weapon left: seven verses, one of them unspoken, and a name it had never managed to learn.

His own.
