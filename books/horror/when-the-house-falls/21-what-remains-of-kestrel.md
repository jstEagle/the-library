## 21. What Remains of Kestrel

The house was honest now, and honesty was its own kind of grief.

Dev noticed it first thing every morning: the pipes knocked when the pipes knocked, the floorboards settled when they settled, and nothing in the walls answered back. For twelve years—no, for as long as he'd lived there—the house had felt like a held breath. Now it exhaled, and the exhale was just dust and cold air and the ordinary rot of an old building that had never been loved properly. He should have found it comforting. Some mornings, carrying coffee up the back stairs, he almost did.

Almost.

Marisol sat in the kitchen window seat where the morning light came through, hands folded in her lap, watching the garden she'd tended for fifty years as if it were a stranger's yard. She still cooked. That was the strange mercy of it—her hands remembered everything. She could make pan de elote without looking, could fold sheets into the tight hospital corners Kestrel's patients needed, could find a bandage in the dark. But when Dev asked her what she wanted from the store, she looked at him with a polite, patient blankness, the look you give a new aide on your first day.

"And who are you, mijo?" she'd asked him the first week.

"Dev. I live here. I help you."

"Ah." She'd nodded, satisfied, and gone back to her kneading. Then, without looking up: "You should eat more. You're too thin. Kestrel was too thin too."

"You remember Kestrel?"

Her hands had stopped. Flour drifted off her knuckles. For a moment something moved behind her eyes—a fish under ice—and then it was gone.

"Should I?" she said.

That had been three weeks ago. She didn't ask anymore. She'd built herself a small, tidy present and lived in it, and Dev had learned not to knock on the walls of it, because when he did—when he said *you raised him, you kept him, you lied to him for his own good and then told the truth at the worst possible moment*—she would look at him with such raw, animal confusion that he had to leave the room and stand in the pantry until he could breathe again.

She'd given her fifty years to the house. The house had taken the years and the name that went with them. Dev had found her service photograph in the hall cabinet, the one from 1974, the young woman with the defiant chin, and written *MARISOL* on the back in pencil, because no one else was going to remember to.

Somebody had to keep the ledger now.

* * *

Kestrel was quieter. That was the word everyone settled on, though there was no one left to settle on words with—just the two of them and Marisol and the dying, and the dying didn't count words. Quieter. Gentler, even. He moved through the house like a man in a museum of his own life, pausing at doorways, tilting his head at staircases as though trying to place where he'd seen them before.

The doctors—Dr. Vane from the county, who came twice a month and asked too many questions—called it dissociative amnesia, secondary to acute psychological trauma, and wrote it in a chart, and that was the story the paperwork told. Dev knew a different story. Dev had stood in the mirrored ballroom doorway and watched a man refuse, absolutely refuse, to say four lines of a song, and had watched the refusal eat him alive from the inside, memory by memory, like frost eating a windowpane.

Kestrel didn't remember his mother's face. Dev knew this because Kestrel had told him, once, in the flat calm voice he used for facts that should have been screams: "There was a woman. I know there was a woman, because Marisol used to say *you have your mother's hands*. But there's no face attached. It's like a coat on a hook. Just the shape where a person was."

He didn't remember his birthday. He didn't remember arriving at the house at age six, though the ledger still sat in the attic with its entry in faded iron-gall ink: *K. Pemberton, age 6, delivered per agreement.* Dev had burned the ledger in October. It seemed like the decent thing. Kestrel had watched it curl in the incinerator out back with an expression Dev couldn't read—not relief, not loss. Recognition, maybe. The way you'd look at a scar and remember it used to hurt.

He remembered his nursing. That was the strange, salvaged thing. Twelve years of hospice work lived in his hands and his voice, in the way he turned a patient with a counted rhythm—*one, two, three*—and the way he could read a dying room the way sailors read weather. He remembered medications and dosages and the names of every hymn that helped. He remembered how to sit with the dying so they didn't feel alone, which is a skill some people study a lifetime and never learn, and Kestrel had it in his bones, and the bones had kept it when everything else was spent.

"You gave up a lot," Dev said to him once, in November, in the kitchen, while Marisol hummed over the stove—she hummed constantly now, old songs with the words worn off.

"I gave up what I didn't need," Kestrel said. "I think." He turned his coffee mug a slow circle on the table. "That's the thing, Dev. I don't know what I don't remember. You can't miss a room you've never seen. I just know the house is lighter now. I can feel that much." He looked up, and his eyes were clear in a way they'd never been in the old days—no dread behind them, no practiced denial, no counting. Just a man looking at his friend. "Is that selfish? To feel lighter?"

"No," Dev said, and it was mostly true.

* * *

The patients kept coming, because the dying always come, and the Pemberton house was still a licensed care home no matter what had lived in its mirrors. There were three residents now: Mr. Halloran, who was mostly asleep; Miss Iyer, who watched game shows and cheated at dominoes; and Mrs. Adeyemi, ninety-one, in the east room, whose heart was finishing its long argument with the rest of her.

Dev did the nights now. Kestrel took days. They'd divided the house between them like brothers dividing an inheritance—Dev got the dark hours, Kestrel got the light, and neither of them said out loud why the division made sense. Kestrel had enough dark in him. Dev had spent enough nights listening at vents.

It was Dev, on a Tuesday in early December, who heard the humming.

He was in the east room at 2 a.m., turning Mrs. Adeyemi, counting the rhythm—*one, two, three*—and when he settled her back against the pillows she looked up at him with the bright, unfocused eyes of the nearly gone and began to hum.

Dev knew the tune before his mind finished naming it. Everyone who'd lived in that house knew the tune. It was a children's counting song, the kind you'd skip rope to, the kind with a hundred regional variations, and it had drifted through the halls of the Pemberton house for a century like smoke finding cracks. The dying hummed it. They always hummed it. Dev had spent years telling himself it was a coincidence of dementia, some shared fragment of childhood radio, and then he'd spent one autumn learning that nothing in that house was a coincidence, and now the house was clean and the song should have been just a song.

Mrs. Adeyemi hummed the first verse. *One for the stone, two for the door—*

And the door of the east room opened, and Kestrel came in.

Dev hadn't heard him on the stairs. The stairs announced everyone; they were a house alarm of creaks, and Dev had navigated them blind for six years. But there Kestrel stood in the doorway in his flannel robe, hair flattened on one side, eyes half-shut—and yet not sleepwalking, not quite. He stood very still, head tilted, the way you'd tilt your head toward a sound you weren't sure was real. Toward the humming.

"Kes," Dev said softly. "It's two in the morning, man."

Kestrel didn't answer. Mrs. Adeyemi hummed on, sweet and threadbare, and Kestrel crossed the room like a man wading through water and sat down in the chair by the bed, and his face—Dev watched it happen and would spend a long time trying to describe it afterward—his face *changed*. Not into fear. That was the thing. In the old days, the song had put dread on Kestrel like a hand on the back of his neck. Now what Dev saw was something older and simpler: recognition. Homecoming. The look of a man hearing a voice he'd forgotten he knew.

The verse ended. Mrs. Adeyemi's breath rattled, evened, rattled. She smiled at the ceiling.

"She's not in pain," Kestrel said. His voice was rough with sleep. "Is she?"

"No. Morphine's holding."

"Good." He sat back. His hands were folded in his lap, and Dev saw that they were perfectly still. Kestrel's hands had never been still, in all the years Dev had known him. They'd drummed, and fidgeted, and counted things—buttons, stitches, floorboards—because some part of him had always been counting, always listening for the count to continue. Now they were just hands. "I heard her from my room," Kestrel said. "I didn't know what it was. I still don't, really. It's just—" He frowned, searching. "It's like when you smell bread and you're six years old again and you don't know why. Something in me stood up and walked here before I did."

"You don't remember the song," Dev said. It wasn't a question.

"No." Kestrel looked at Mrs. Adeyemi's sleeping face. "Should I?"

"No," Dev said, too fast, and Kestrel's eyes came up, and for a moment—the first moment in weeks—Dev saw something sharp move through all that gentleness. The old Kestrel, the one who'd found the dumbwaiter notes, the one who'd stood in the parlor full of hair and photographs and names and looked at his family like a man reading his own autopsy. That man was still in there. You could spend memories, Dev understood now, but you couldn't spend the part of you that learned what people were.

"You know things about me I don't," Kestrel said quietly. "You and Marisol both. You look at me like I'm a letter you've already read."

"You're not a letter."

"Then what am I?"

Dev looked at the bed. Mrs. Adeyemi breathed. The house creaked its honest creaks. And Dev, who had written notes to a thing in a wall for three years, who had traded his best friend's safety for everyone else's, who had burned those notes and offered his own throat in their place and been refused—Dev found that the only honest answer was the one he'd been carrying since October.

"You're the guy who wouldn't say yes," he said. "When it would have been so easy. When it was offering you everything back. You're the guy who paid instead." His voice cracked and he let it. "I don't care what you forgot. That part's still true. That part's the truest thing in this house."

Kestrel held his gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and stood, and at the door he paused.

"Dev. When she—" he nodded at Mrs. Adeyemi— "when it's time. I want to sit with her. Even at night. Even if I don't know why I want to."

"Okay," Dev said.

"She hummed that song, and I came. I don't know what that means. But I want to be there." He rubbed his eyes like a tired man, just a tired man, and said, "Wake me if she wakes," and went back down the hall, and the stairs creaked under him all the way, honest as anything.

* * *

Mrs. Adeyemi died nine days later, on a Sunday morning with snow starting outside the east windows. Kestrel sat with her through the last three hours, holding her hand, and Dev stood in the doorway and watched his friend do the work his bones remembered: the low voice, the unhurried silence, the permission to go. At the very end she opened her eyes and looked at Kestrel with total clarity and said, "You're the one who wouldn't sing," and Kestrel said, "Yes, ma'am," and she smiled and was gone between one breath and the next, and the room filled up with the particular silence that isn't empty.

Afterward Kestrel washed his hands for a long time at the utility sink, and Dev stood beside him, and neither of them talked, and it was fine. It was almost fine. It was the finest thing Dev had felt in a year.

That afternoon they buried the ledger's replacement—Dev had started a new one, a plain notebook, because somebody had to keep the record—and Kestrel wrote Mrs. Adeyemi's name on the first page in his careful nurse's handwriting, and under it, after a pause, he wrote: *She hummed. I came. That was enough.*

Marisol made dinner. She set three places without being asked, which she always did, and when Dev asked her why three, she frowned at the table and said, "Because someone's coming," and then couldn't say who, and laughed at herself, and served the beans.

* * *

It was in the last week of December, clearing out Mrs. Adeyemi's room, that Dev found the note.

It was tucked into the spine of the Bible on the bedside table, a folded square of paper gone soft with handling, in a handwriting Dev did not know—old-fashioned, looped, the letters leaning like they were walking into wind. He should have left it. He told himself later that any reasonable person would have left it. But he'd spent three years learning what this house did with paper and secrets, and his hands opened it before his conscience finished voting.

*To the one who comes after,* it read. *If the song starts again, and you hear it, and something in you stands up and walks toward it before you decide to—that is not the end of you. That is the part of you the house could not take. Tomas left this in 1979. I am writing it in whatever I am now. The last verse is not a door. It is a key. Keys turn both ways. Remember: it needs a voice. It has never once been able to sing alone.*

*It will try again. They always come back patient.*

Dev read it three times. Outside the window, the snow came down without wind, straight and silent, filling the garden Marisol no longer remembered planting. Down the hall, he could hear Kestrel moving through the east room, stripping the bed, folding the sheets into tight hospital corners, humming something under his breath—something tuneless, Dev was almost sure, just a habit, just a man making noise in an empty room.

Almost sure.

He put the note in his pocket, and went to find him.
