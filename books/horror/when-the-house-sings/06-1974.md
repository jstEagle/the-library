## 6. 1974

*From the parish records of St. Aldhelm's, Hollowmere, transcribed 1974:*

*Item: The Marlowe girl. Winnifred Marlowe, aged nine, last seen the evening of the 14th of June on the lane below Hollowmere House. Wearing a yellow dress, white sandals. Constable's report notes no signs of struggle, no witnesses beyond a Mrs. Tilly Hargrove, who states she saw the child "walking toward the house, not away from it," and who declined to elaborate further.*

*Item: Search suspended 3rd July. Inspector's note in margin, hand unknown: "The family asks us to stop coming."*

* * *

Edith Marlowe was twenty-six years old that summer, and she believed—she would believe for fifty years, right up until believing became the only thing she had left—that she had been a good sister.

She thought about it now the way she thought about everything: in fragments, in pieces worn smooth by handling, like river stones kept in a coat pocket. Winnie's hair, pale as wheat gone over. The way she hummed when she was happy, a tune with no name, four notes rising and one falling, over and over, patient as water finding its way downhill. Edith had asked her once where she'd learned it. Winnie had shrugged with the whole of her small body and said, *It was already here.*

Already here. In the house, she'd meant. Children said things like that. Children said things like that all the time, and you laughed, and you wrote it down in the baby book if you were sentimental, and you did not lie awake at night thirty years later turning the phrase over like a coin you suspected was counterfeit.

That June evening, Edith had been in the kitchen. She could still see the kitchen—the linoleum curling at the threshold, the clock her father had wound every Sunday, the window over the sink that looked down the lane. She had been washing dishes. There had been rhubarb stewing on the stove, and the smell of it, sweet and sharp, had filled the room, and she had let Winnie go out to play because it was June and the light lasted until nearly ten and what harm could come to a child on her own lane, within shouting distance of her own front door?

She heard the humming stop.

That was the thing she never told anyone, because there was no way to tell it that didn't make her sound mad or guilty or both. Most people, losing a sister, lost her to silence—a gap, an absence, nothing to mark the moment. But Edith had heard the exact moment. The humming had come up through the open window, thin and bright off the lane, and then it had stopped. Not faded. Stopped. Cut clean, the way a radio cuts out, the way a held breath is released.

And then—she was almost sure, she had been almost sure for fifty years—the house had hummed it back.

Four notes rising, one falling. From inside the walls. Deeper than pipes, deeper than wind, from somewhere in the bones of the place, as though the house had caught the tune the way a throat catches a swallowed stone, and was trying it out. Learning it.

She had stood at the sink with her hands in the water until the water went cold, telling herself she'd imagined it, and by the time she'd dried her hands and gone out to call Winnie in for supper, the lane was empty and the yellow dress was nowhere and the world had already begun rearranging itself around the hole her sister had left in it.

*Letter, Hollowmere House, 21st June 1974 — from the papers of Rev. A. Cudlipp:*

*Dear Mr. Cudlipp,—I write against my better judgment and at my mother's insistence. You will have heard talk. I ask you to hear me instead. What is said of us—that we did something to the girl, that we hid her, that we sent her away—is filth. My daughter Edith has not slept since the 14th. She sits in the hall at night. When I ask her why, she says she is listening. I do not know how to write down what I am afraid she is listening to.—Yrs., G. Marlowe*

*P.S. The men who came with the dogs will not come back. They left their equipment in the yard and will not answer letters. If you can learn why, I would count it a kindness.*

* * *

The searches lasted nineteen days, and Edith remembered them as weather—as pressure, as heat, as a storm system moving through the house and taking things with it when it went.

They came with dogs on the second day, and the dogs would not enter. That was the first wrongness, though at the time she'd filed it under grief, under the general madness grief made of everything. The dogs stood at the gate and leaned backward, all six of them leaning together like grass in wind, and their handlers swore and coaxed and finally dragged them away, and one young constable had looked up at the house with an expression Edith recognized later, much later, in her own mirror: the look of a man revising his understanding of the world and hating the revision.

They drained the pond. They walked the fields in lines, beating the barley with sticks. They brought a man with a device that beeped, and he walked the cellar floor for two days and found nothing but old iron and the foundations going down further than any foundation should, down into chalk and older chalk, and he'd emerged gray-faced and said the readings were "no good" and packed up without meeting anyone's eye.

Her father aged ten years in those three weeks. Her mother stopped speaking above a whisper, as though loud voices might reach wherever Winnie was and hurt her. And Edith listened.

She listened because she had heard the house hum, and because she could not unknow it, and because some stubborn animal part of her understood before her mind allowed it: the house had her sister. Not her body—the house was not a grave, she knew this with a certainty that arrived whole, unbidden, the way you know your own name. Something else. Something the searches couldn't find because they were looking for an ending, and Winnie hadn't ended.

At night she sat in the hall with her back against the newel post, and she waited, and on the ninth night it came again. Four notes rising, one falling. Faint, from beneath the floor, from behind the plaster, from everywhere and nowhere, and underneath it—so faint she doubted it even as her heart broke open around it—a smaller sound. Breathing. Or the memory of breathing. Or a child humming along with herself.

"Winnie?" she'd whispered into the dark of the hall, and the house had gone silent, and the silence had felt less like absence than like attention.

*Statement taken 11th July 1974, E. Marlowe, aged 26, in presence of Insp. R. Callow:*

*"I want it written down that my sister went into the house. Not onto the moor, not to a relative, not run off to the road. Into the house. Write it exactly like that. I don't care what you think of me. Someone should have it in writing."*

*(Witness declined to elaborate. Witness's parents present; father requested statement be sealed. Statement sealed at family request, 12th July 1974.)*

* * *

The town stopped speaking of them the way a mouth stops speaking around a rotten tooth—not all at once, but carefully, tenderly, testing.

It began with the shop. Mrs. Pym, who had sold Gertie Marlowe her bacon for thirty years and gossiped across the counter for twenty-nine of them, began wrapping parcels in silence. Then the church: the vicar called once, kindly, and after that the Marlowes' pew filled with other families, gently, the way water fills a footprint. Then the schoolteacher stopped sending home the reading lists Winnie would have needed in September, and Edith understood that the town had done its arithmetic and decided the Marlowes were a sum best left unworked.

She couldn't blame them. That was the terrible part. People needed a story with edges, and the Marlowes offered none. A runaway girl was a story. A drowned girl was a story. But a girl who walked toward her own house and was not seen again, whose family asked the police to stop searching, whose dogs refused the gate—there was no shape for that, so the town simply closed around it like water closing over a stone, and by autumn, when Edith walked to the shop, people found urgent business with their shelves as she passed.

Only Tilly Hargrove ever spoke of it, once, years afterward, catching Edith outside the post office, gripping her arm with fingers like bird claws. Tilly had been the last to see Winnie walking. *I told the truth and they looked at me like I'd lied,* she said. *I saw her walk toward the house, Edith. Toward it. Like it was calling her by name.* And then Tilly had looked past Edith's shoulder at the house on its hill, and whatever she saw there made her let go and cross the road without waiting for the traffic to clear.

* * *

By August, Edith had begun sealing doors.

It started sensibly, or what passed for sensible in that summer of grief. The cellar door first—Winnie had loved the cellar, had played there in the cool on hot days, had claimed (children said things) that the cellar was "the quietest listening place in the world." Edith bolted it from the inside, which required crawling through the low passage and working the bolts blind in the dark, and her father had shouted at her through the wood until his voice gave out, and she had not answered, and in the morning he had not made her undo it. He understood, in the wordless way of the broken, that his elder daughter was building a dam against something only she could hear.

Then the pantry. Then the little room off the landing that had been Winnie's, which Edith did not seal but plastered over, walling the doorway shut with her own hands over three days, trowel and lime and horsehair, weeping steadily throughout, so that the room stayed exactly as it was—bed made, drawings on the wallpaper, a shoe by the door—preserved like a fly in amber, like a letter never sent. Her mother watched from the landing and said nothing, and that night Edith heard her downstairs, humming to herself without knowing it, four notes rising and one falling, and Edith had gone cold from scalp to sole, because her mother had never known the tune. No one had taught it. It was already here.

The house sang, and the family began to die around it in the slow ordinary way families die, and each death Edith marked with another bolt, another lock, another seam of mortar, until Hollowmere was less a house than a reliquary, a box built around a sound.

Because here is what Edith learned in the autumn of 1974, alone in the hall at night with her ear to the wainscot, and here is what she carried forward through fifty years like a coal cupped in bare hands:

The house was not cruel. That was the horror of it, the thing no ghost story had prepared her for. The house had found a child alone on the lane, humming, and it had wanted the humming to continue. It was lonely the way wells are deep—it could not help it—and it had gathered Winnie in the way it gathered everything forgotten, everything unmourned, everything the town turned its face from. It kept her. It kept her the way amber keeps.

But keeping costs. A thing cannot hold a soul together without material, and the house fed on memory, on names, on the small warm facts of being remembered—and so Edith had struck her bargain in the winter of '74, alone, kneeling in the hall, speaking aloud to the walls because there was no one left to speak to:

*Keep her. Keep her singing. Take mine instead.*

And the house, gentle as gravity, had begun to take.

*Final entry, diary of E. Marlowe, December 1974:*

*I forget things now. Small ones. The name of Mrs. Pym's dog. Which hand Father wound the clock with. I give them gladly. Let the ledger balance however it must, only keep her. This morning I could not recall the color of the kitchen linoleum, and I sat on the stairs and laughed until I cried, because it is such a small thing, such a cheap thing, and she is worth every year of it. I heard her just now, under the floor. She sounds happy. She sounds like June.*

*Note appended in different ink, decades later, hand shakier:* *I no longer remember what color it was. I no longer remember Mother's face. Worth it. Worth it. Worth it.*

* * *

Fifty years later, in a room that smelled of lavender and disinfectant, an old woman lay with her hands folded on the coverlet and hummed four notes rising and one falling, and the young nurse paused in the doorway and said, "That's pretty. What is it?"

Edith opened her eyes. For a moment something moved behind them—something vast and patient surfacing from very deep water—and then it sank again, and she smiled the pleasant vacant smile they all knew, and said:

"I've no idea, dear. It was already here."

Outside the window, far off across the fields, the excavators had begun to gather at the foot of Hollowmere Hill like black beetles on a grave, and in the walls of the house itself—though no one remained inside to hear it—the song rose, four notes and a falling fifth, faster now, urgent now, a tune that had been holding its breath for half a century and had just realized, with the first bite of machinery into the hillside, that it was running out of time.
