## 9. The Tenant Below

Vesper had told herself she wouldn't go down there.

She'd said it out loud, in fact, standing at the top of the cellar stairs with her torch beam cutting a pale wedge into the dark below, the words leaving her mouth with the particular firmness of a woman negotiating with herself: *You are not going down there tonight.* She'd said it the way she said *I don't drink on weekdays* and *I'm fine on my own*, statements that were less descriptions than load-bearing walls, holding up the architecture of who she needed to be.

It was eleven minutes past midnight when she started down the stairs.

The house had been quiet for three days since the camera incident — since she'd watched footage of her own sleeping face filmed from the corner of the room she slept in, filmed from an angle no camera she owned could occupy, filmed with a patience that felt almost tender. Three days of silence. And somehow the silence was worse than the humming had been. Silence meant it was thinking. Silence meant it had decided something about her.

The cellar door waited at the bottom of the stairs, and Vesper's torch found it the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

It was oak, older than everything else in the house, older than the house itself if the grain was anything to judge by, and the bolts were wrong. That was the detail the foreman hadn't been able to stop talking about before he quit, the detail that had lodged in Vesper's mind like a splinter: the bolts were thrown from the inside. Four of them, iron, hand-forged, each one slid home into its bracket by someone standing on *this* side of the door — the sealed side, the airless side, the side that should have held nothing but fifty years of dark and had instead, when the excavator peeled back the earth, exhaled air that smelled of cut grass and rain.

Someone had locked themselves in. Or someone had locked something in and the something had locked it after them, which was a sentence Vesper refused to finish thinking.

She stood in front of it now, close enough to touch, and did not touch it.

"Right," she said, because talking to empty rooms was still more dignified than not talking at all. "I know you're in there."

Her voice fell flat against the stone. No echo. That was another thing — sound didn't behave down here. It went out and simply stopped, as though the cellar swallowed acoustics the way the house swallowed pest controllers, and Vesper thought of the parish records spread across her kitchen table forty miles away, eight names in two hundred years, every one of them dead within twelve months of their last visit, every one of them unmourned. Unmarried men. Widowers. A woman struck off the nursing register. People the world had already begun forgetting before the house finished the job.

*I would be missed,* she'd told herself, reading those names. *My sister would—*

The thought had stopped there, snagged on nothing, and she'd sat for a long moment trying to remember why it had stopped, what she'd been reaching for. She had no sister. She knew she had no sister. But for half a second the sentence *my sister would miss me* had felt like a key fitting a lock in a door she'd never seen before, and the feeling had frightened her more than the fingerprints, more than the wildflowers in the traps, more than her own sleeping face blinking up from the corner of a screen.

That was three days ago. Tonight the silence had finally gotten to her, and here she was, doing the single least rational thing available to a woman whose entire identity was built on rationality: talking to a bolted door at midnight.

"I've read the records," she said. "Eight of you. Eight names." She pulled her phone from her pocket, checked the recorder app was running — evidence, she told herself, for a report that existed nowhere except her own stubborn need to document — and set it on an upturned bucket. "Thomas Greave, 1889. Albert Fenwick, 1921. I have all of you. So whoever you are, whatever you are, you should know somebody's keeping track now. Somebody's writing it down."

Nothing. The door sat in its frame like a closed eye.

Vesper exhaled slowly through her nose. Of course nothing. What had she expected — a knock? A voice? She was a grown woman with a mortgage arrears notice in her glovebox and a professional reputation built on the phrase *there's always a mundane explanation*, and she was standing in a freezing cellar performing for an audience of oak and iron.

She turned to go.

"Mummy?"

The word came from behind the door.

Not loudly. Not urgently. A child's voice, small and sleep-roughened, the way a child sounds at two in the morning padding down a hallway, and every hair on Vesper's arms rose beneath her jacket because she knew — she *knew* — that the sealed room beyond the door was barely six feet square, that the survey drawings showed solid rock behind its far wall, that no child had lived in Hollowmere House since 1974 and none had died in it either, according to every record the county kept.

She turned back. Her torch beam trembled, and she hated it for trembling.

"That's not funny," she said quietly.

"Mummy, I can't find my socks."

And then the voice changed. It happened between one word and the next, seamless as water finding its level — the child's pitch deepening, roughening, becoming a woman's voice, a middle-aged woman's voice with a faint West Country burr at the edges of it, and Vesper's whole body went cold from the inside out because she recognized it. She would have recognized it anywhere. She'd spent thirty years trying to forget it and failing, hearing it in dreams, in supermarket aisles, in the last voicemail saved on a phone she'd kept through four handset upgrades.

"*Vessie.* Come away from that door, love. You'll catch your death."

"No," Vesper said. Her voice came out thin. "No, you don't get to do that."

"It's cold down here, sweetheart. Why don't you come and sit with me? I'll make us tea. Proper tea, not that rubbish you drink—" and the voice laughed, and the laugh was right, it was *exactly* right, the little hitch in the middle of it, the way her mother's laugh had always sounded slightly surprised at itself, and Vesper pressed her palm flat against the cold oak because if she didn't anchor herself to something she was going to sit down on the floor and weep.

"You're dead," she said. "You died in 2016. St. Aldhelm's ward. I signed the forms. I chose the hymns. You don't get to be down there."

Silence. Then, gently, in her mother's voice: "Then how am I remembering your seventh birthday, love? The cake with the wonky icing? You cried because the sugar mouse lost his ear, and Dad ate him anyway and said he'd gone to live on a better shelf?"

Vesper's breath left her in a long, unsteady stream.

Nobody knew that. Nobody living. Her father had died before her mother; there was no one left on earth who'd been in that kitchen, and the detail wasn't in any record, wasn't in any letter, existed only in the memory of a dead woman and the memory of the daughter who'd carried it alone for decades without ever once speaking it aloud.

"How," she whispered.

"We keep things here," the voice said, warm as a hand on her hair. "That's all this is, Vessie. That's all it's ever been. A place where things that are forgotten get kept safe. Your mum's been down here having a lovely rest, and she's ever so sorry she never said goodbye properly, and if you'd just open the door—"

"No." Vesper snatched her hand back from the wood as if it had burned her. "No. My mother is buried in Greenbank Cemetery under a slate headstone I pay to have cleaned twice a year. Whatever you are, you're not her, and you don't get her voice."

The pause that followed had texture to it. Vesper imagined — absurdly, horribly — something on the other side of the door tilting its head, reconsidering, choosing a different tool from the box.

Then the voice came again, and this time it was her own.

Not her present voice. Her childhood voice — seven years old, gap-toothed, the slight lisp she'd had before the speech therapist fixed it, a voice that had not existed in the world for over thirty years and that no recording of her family had ever captured because her mother hadn't owned a video camera and the tape recorder had broken the summer Vesper was six.

"*When I grow up I'm going to live somewhere with lots of people,*" her seven-year-old self said through the door, conversational, dreamy, reciting. "*A big noisy house where you can always hear someone in the next room. I hate our house. It's too quiet. It's like nobody lives in it even when everybody does.*"

"Stop." Vesper's back hit the cellar wall opposite. She hadn't remembered moving. "Stop it. Stop it now."

"*I asked Mum if we could get a lodger just so there'd be footsteps,*" the small voice continued, tender and merciless. "*She said don't be silly, Vessie, we've got each other. But I used to lie awake listening to the pipes and pretend the house was breathing. I pretended it was singing to me so I wouldn't feel alone.*"

Vesper slid down the wall until she was sitting on the cold flagstones, the torch rolling from her slackening grip, and she put her face in her hands.

Because it was true. All of it, true, every humiliating syllable — the lodger request, the pipes, the pretending. Things she had never told anyone. Things she had barely admitted to herself, folded flat and shoved into the deepest drawer of her memory, and this thing behind the door had opened that drawer and was reading her aloud in her own child's voice, and the worst part — the part that made her stomach turn over even now — was that it didn't feel like an attack.

It felt like being *known*.

"Do you understand yet?" the voice asked, and now it was neither her mother nor her child-self but something in between, something patient and ageless and very tired, the voice of the house itself speaking through borrowed mouths. "Everyone comes down here eventually. The lonely ones. The ones nobody writes to. We listen, Vesper. That's all we do, at first. We listen to what people have lost, and then we give it back to them, and they stay, and they're not lonely anymore, and nobody misses them because there was nobody to miss them. Isn't that kinder? Than the other thing? The other thing is dying in a bedsit and being found three weeks later by a bailiff. We've seen the other thing. We're offering you the alternative."

"The alternative," Vesper said into her hands, "is a bolted door."

"The door keeps the noise out," the voice said simply. "Yours, mostly. The screaming phase passes quickly. After that it's just — company. Forever. Winnie's down here. Edith's sister. Fifty years and she's still nine years old and she still sings every evening, and she has never once been alone, and when the machines come to knock the house down—" the voice caught, or performed catching, and Vesper couldn't tell anymore and hated that she couldn't tell — "when the machines come, she goes out like a candle. Everything down here goes out. Every kept thing, scattered. Is that justice? Is that what your rationality wants? For the sake of your principles, a little girl gets erased twice?"

Vesper lifted her head. Her eyes were wet and furious and she let them be.

"Winnie Marlowe," she said. "That's the shoe. That's the humming. That's what Edith's been paying for."

"Fifty years," the voice agreed softly. "One memory at a time. She gave us her mother's face first. Then her wedding day. Then her own name, nearly. She'd have given everything, in the end. They always would, the ones who stay. Because staying means someone remembers, and being remembered—" the voice dropped to almost nothing, and it sounded, for one unguarded moment, genuinely hungry — "being remembered is the only heaven there is."

Vesper got to her feet. Her knees complained. She picked up the torch, picked up the phone with its recorder still running, and looked at the door for a long moment.

"You want me to open it," she said.

"We want you to *stay*. Opening the door is just how staying starts."

"And everyone who stayed — Greave, Fenwick, the rest of them — they're still in there? Fifty years, a hundred years, still in there?"

A pause. Slightly too long.

"They're happy," the voice said.

"That's not what I asked."

Another pause, longer, and in it Vesper heard — or imagined she heard, or perhaps the house let her hear deliberately, a taste of the merchandise — a great many voices underneath the floor of the silence, murmuring, humming, overlapping like pages turning, like a congregation whispering along with a familiar prayer, and among them, faint and clear and endless, a child singing a tune that Vesper now realized she'd been hearing in her sleep for three nights without knowing it.

"Come inside," her mother's voice said, gentle as snowfall. "We saved you a room."

Vesper backed up the stairs without turning around, the way you back away from a dog you don't trust, and at the top she paused with her hand on the light switch and heard, following her up through the dark, patient and unhurried, her own seven-year-old voice:

"*Don't go, Mummy. It's cold out there, and nobody knows you're here.*"

She did not sleep that night. She sat at the kitchen table in the damp flat she rented in town, the recorder playing back the cellar session, and it was only on the fourth listen, near dawn, that she caught it — beneath her mother's voice, beneath her own, beneath the congregation-murmur, a sound she'd missed in the moment because terror flattens everything.

Scratching. Rhythmic, deliberate, from the far side of the door.

She slowed the playback, leaned close, and counted.

Knocks. Groups of five, then groups of one, repeating. And Vesper Ravensworth, who did not believe in messages from behind bolted doors, who believed in rats and rot and coincidence, sat very still in the grey light and made herself translate it anyway, because some old Girl Guide reflex had woken in her blood and refused to lie back down.

Five and one. Five and one.

*H-E-L-P.*

Someone in that room — someone who was not the house, or was a piece of the house the house itself had forgotten it was holding — was asking to be let out.

And the tune the child had been singing, the one Vesper had carried out of the cellar stuck in her head like a burr, matched exactly, note for note, the song Edith Marlowe hummed in her care home bed forty miles away.

Vesper reached for her car keys.
