## 17. What Vester Can't Remember

The doors had stopped being doors sometime after midnight.

Vesper knew this in the way she knew the pressure before a storm—not through any single sense but through the whole body's quiet arithmetic. She had tried the front door at one a.m. and found it locked from a side that had no lock. She had tried the kitchen door at two and found it had become a wall, plaster smooth and warm as skin, as though the doorway had never been anything but a mistake the house had corrected. At three she stopped trying, because the trying felt like exactly what the house wanted: proof of her presence, proof of her struggle, proof that she was still a living thing worth keeping.

Now she sat in the front parlour with her back against the cold chimney breast, her torch between her knees, and listened to Hollowmere sing.

It was not the humming anymore. The tune had come apart somewhere in the small hours, its gentle lilt pulled apart like knitting, and what remained was something vaster and stranger—a chord held in the walls, in the floorboards, in the water standing in the pipes. It was not unpleasant. That was the worst of it. It was the sound a house might make if a house could be content, and it wrapped around her like a blanket someone else had warmed.

*You could stay,* the chord said, in the wordless way that houses say things. *You could be one of ours.*

"I don't want to be one of yours," Vesper said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them outside her head, and outside her head was the only territory she still controlled.

The chord shifted, and she felt—she had no other word for it—she felt the house *consider* her. The way she herself considered a wasp's nest before deciding whether it could be left alone through winter.

Her torch flickered. She had spares in her van. Her van, she remembered with a lurch, was parked at the end of a drive that now, presumably, ended at a wall.

She got up because sitting still felt like sinking, and because movement was the last discipline she trusted. She would do what she always did when a job went wrong: work the problem. Inventory the space. Map the exits. Treat the house as a structure, not a presence, and the presence as a pest, not a god.

The pest controller's creed. She had said it to herself a thousand times over fifteen years of wasps and rats and one unforgettable squirrel in a bakery. *Everything can be managed. Everything has a protocol.*

The house hummed its contentment, and for the first time in her professional life, Vesper Ravensworth could not remember her creed's last line.

* * *

She found the pages in the morning, or what she took for morning—a grey seepage through the boarded windows that might have been dawn or might have been the house's idea of dawn.

She had slept, she realized, though she had no memory of lying down. She had slept in the parlour chair like a tired woman in her own home, and that fact sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had not slept. She had been *allowed* to sleep. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

Her satchel was open on the floor beside the chair.

She never left her satchel open. Fifteen years of contract work had made her fastidious—zipped, clipped, inventoried. But there it sat, gaping, and from its throat protruded a sheaf of paper that was not her report pad, not her invoice book, not any paper she had brought into Hollowmere House.

The pages were cream-coloured, soft-edged, old stock. There were perhaps twenty of them. And the handwriting on the first page was hers.

Not similar to hers. *Hers.* The same forward-leaning script, the same way she crossed her sevens, the same little hook she made on her lowercase *r* that a schoolteacher had once tried and failed to correct. Vesper knew her own hand the way she knew her own face, and this was it, unmistakably, covering page after page in blue-black ink.

She read the first line and the floor seemed to tilt.

*My sister used to hide in the walls when we played hide and seek. She was so small she could fit between the lath and the plaster, and she'd hold her breath and wait for me to find her, and I never could, and she'd laugh and laugh.*

Vesper did not have a sister.

She stood very still. The house hummed. Somewhere above her, in the space between floors, something shifted its weight with the patience of a thing that had done this before, to others, for fifty years.

She picked up the pages. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady; it was the one inheritance from her father that had ever been useful—and she read.

*My sister Winnie was afraid of the dark but she loved the cellar, which I never understood, because the cellar was the darkest place in the house. She said it wasn't dark down there, it was just quiet, and there's a difference. She said you could hear the house thinking.*

*My sister had a gap in her teeth and a scar on her left knee from falling off the wall at the bottom of the garden. She got the scar the summer she was eight. I was there. I was always there. That was the whole point of me.*

*I can't remember my sister's face. I can remember that I can't remember it, and the shape of the not-remembering is exactly her shape.*

Vesper sat down on the floor without deciding to. The pages trembled now, and it took her a long moment to understand that the trembling was coming from her hands after all.

Because here was the thing—the thing that turned her blood to something cold and mineral. The handwriting was hers. The memories were not. She had never had a sister. She had never hidden or sought in these rooms. She had never been to Hollowmere before the callout three weeks ago; she had checked her own records to be sure, because the house had made her doubt everything and checking was how she fought. She was an only child of a woman who had died alone and a man who had left before memory began, and her childhood had been a long grey corridor of foster homes and council estates and learning, early and thoroughly, that no one was coming.

She knew the shape of her own life the way she knew the shape of her own hand.

And yet the pages described a sister with a scar on her left knee, and when Vesper read that line, some part of her mind—the soft, stupid, starving part—went *yes, the wall by the garden, she fell, I remember the sound she made.*

She did not remember the sound. She had never heard it. But the memory was *there*, sitting in her head like furniture that had been delivered while she slept, and she could not tell, looking at it, that it had not always been in the room.

"Oh," she said, very quietly. "Oh, you clever bastard."

The house hummed, pleased, or what she chose to hear as pleased.

* * *

She spent what remained of the grey morning taking inventory, and the inventory was the most terrifying work she had ever done, and she had once spent four hours in a crawlspace with a badger.

She made a list. Writing things down was her oldest defence; her whole life had been built on the principle that what is recorded cannot be quietly revised. On the back of an invoice, in her own hand, she wrote the facts of Vesper Ravensworth:

*Born March. Only child. Mother: Alma Ravensworth, died eleven years ago, Whittington Hospital. Father: unknown, name on nothing. Foster placements: seven. Schools: four. No siblings. No siblings. No siblings.*

She wrote it three times. The third time, her pen hesitated over *no siblings* the way a tongue hesitates over a missing tooth.

Then she went through the pages again, all twenty of them, and this time she read them not as a victim but as an examiner. What was the house doing? What was its method? Because the house had a method—everything did; rats followed walls, wasps followed eaves, and this thing followed *grief*—and a method could be understood, and what could be understood could be fought.

The pages were not random. They were not the house inventing a sister out of whole cloth. That she might have dismissed—hallucination, gas, sleep deprivation; she had a whole shelf of rational explanations and she clung to them like rungs over a drop.

No. The pages were precise. The sister had a name—*Winnie*, which was a name that belonged to this house, to 1974, to the child whose shoe sat in an evidence bag in her van, if her van still existed. The sister had a scar on the correct knee. The sister hid in walls. The sister was afraid of the dark and loved the cellar.

The house was not giving Vesper false memories. The house was giving her *Edith's* memories—the real ones, the ones Edith had paid away over fifty years—and it was fitting them to Vesper like a tailor fitting a coat, cutting here, pinning there, sewing Vesper's name into a collar that had been made for someone else.

It was dressing her in a dead girl's sisterhood.

And the fit was good. That was the horror she kept circling and could not land on. The fit was *good*, because Vesper's life had so few pieces in it that almost anything could be slotted into the empty space. A woman with a full life would notice the forgery at once. A woman whose life was a grey corridor, a van, a ledger of small unpaid bills and smaller unremembered birthdays—what did she have to compare it against? The house had found the one kind of person it could edit without seams: the kind no one would miss, the kind with room in her.

*You could be one of ours,* the house had said. And it had not been a threat. It had been an offer, made in the only currency the house possessed. It had looked at the hole where Vesper's family should have been, and it had offered to fill it—with a sister, with a childhood, with the sound of someone laughing in the walls, waiting to be found.

And the most terrible thing, the thing she would not write down, was that some part of her wanted to say yes.

* * *

She tested herself in the afternoon, because testing was all she had left.

She stood in the centre of the parlour and recited her own history aloud, chronologically, like a report. Birth. Mother. First foster home—the Hendersons, who had a dog called Buster who bit her when she was six. The second home. The third. The school in Ashbourne where she'd won a prize for a project on urban foxes and no one had come to the assembly.

She got to the summer she was nine and stopped.

There was a summer she was nine. She knew there was, because the years did not have gaps; she had checked them all, in the night, in her head, like a woman counting stairs in the dark. But when she reached that summer, she found that it had a *texture* now, a warmth, a golden light lying across it, and in the golden light there was a small figure with a gap in her teeth, and they were—

They were doing what? Sitting in long grass? Playing some game with a ball and a wall?

She pressed on the memory the way you press on a bruise to learn its edges, and it gave, and it was sweet, and it was *wrong*, and it was the loveliest thing she had felt in fifteen years.

"No," Vesper said.

The house's chord paused. Listening.

"No," she said again, louder, to the walls, to the thing in the walls, to the soft starving animal in her own chest. "She's not mine. She was never mine. You don't get to give her to me. She has a sister. Her sister's name is Edith Marlowe, she's ninety-one years old, she's in a care home in Ashbourne, and she paid fifty years for that girl, and you will not spend her on *me*."

The chord came back changed. Not angry. Worse than angry—*reasonable*, the way the house always was, the way it had been reasonable with Edith, the way it had been reasonable with the town's forgotten dead for half a century. And in the reasonableness Vesper heard, plain as speech:

*But you are alone. And she is alone. And I am only keeping lonely things company.*

"That's not keeping company," Vesper said, and her voice cracked on the last word, which frightened her more than the walls did. "That's eating."

* * *

The last of the pages she did not find until dusk.

She had gathered the others, rolled them, stuffed them in her jacket against her ribs—evidence, she told herself, though evidence for what court she could not have said—and she was checking the satchel one final time when her fingers found a page sewn into the lining. Not slipped in. *Sewn*, with fine stitches that had not been there that morning, in thread the colour of old blood.

She cut the lining open with her utility knife and unfolded the last page, and this one was not about Winnie.

*My sister used to say that being forgotten was the worst thing that could happen to a person, worse than dying. She said the dead don't mind being dead, they just mind being nobody. I promised her I'd never let that happen. I promised her. I don't remember making the promise. I don't remember her. But I know I promised, because the promise is the only thing in me that's certain, and it's certain the way hunger is certain.*

Vesper read it twice. Then a third time, and on the third reading she understood—not with her rational mind, which had been losing arguments for three weeks, but with something older and less negotiable—that the page was not a forgery at all.

It was a *forecast*.

This was what she would be, when the house was finished. A woman with a sister-shaped hole and a promise-shaped scar, loving someone she could not remember, keeping a faith she could not source, walking out of Hollowmere House into the world carrying a grief that had been installed in her like a window, and calling the grief *love*, and never knowing.

The house was not going to kill her. The house had never killed anyone who mattered to it. The house was going to *keep* her—the way it kept Winnie, the way it had kept Edith, the way it kept every forgotten thing: alive, and hollow, and humming.

And the demolition was in nine days. And Edith was dying. And the only person who could sign the house back to the dead was ninety-one years old in a care home in Ashbourne, and Vesper was locked in a parlour with a wall where the door used to be.

She looked down at the forecast page, and then at her own inventory list, *no siblings, no siblings, no siblings*, and she did the only thing left that felt like hers.

She took her utility knife, and she cut the forecast page into pieces, and she cut the pieces into pieces, and she ate none of them and burned none of them because there was nothing to burn, but she folded every scrap into the tightest ball she could make and held it in her fist until her knuckles went white.

"You don't get to write me," she told the walls. "I write me. Bad handwriting and all."

The house went quiet. Truly quiet, for the first time—the chord withdrawing like a tide, the pipes standing empty and silent, the whole structure holding its breath the way Edith had said it did.

And then, from somewhere above her, from the space between the floors where a small girl had once hidden and waited to be found, Vesper heard a sound that undid her more thoroughly than any scream could have.

A child's voice, thin and clear and terribly patient, speaking through the lath and plaster directly above her head.

"You promised," the voice said. "You promised you'd never let me be nobody."

And Vesper, standing in the dark with her fist full of shredded forecast, felt the words land where they were aimed—because she *had* promised. She could feel the promise in her like a bone.

The trouble was that the promise had been made by Edith Marlowe, fifty years ago, to a girl in the walls.

And the house had just handed it to Vesper as if it were hers to keep.

She stood in the dark, holding a promise that wasn't hers, in a house that was closing like a fist, and understood at last what she had to do—not escape the house, not fight it, but reach a dying woman's bedside before the house could finish making her into the sister Edith had lost.

She turned to the wall where the kitchen door had been, put her palm flat against the warm plaster, and said, in the steadiest voice she had left:

"Edith Marlowe is dying. And when she dies, your bargain dies with her—unless I get there first. So you can keep me, or you can let me save her. But you can't have both."

The plaster under her palm grew warm. Then warmer.

Then, with a sound like a long-held breath finally released, the wall opened.
