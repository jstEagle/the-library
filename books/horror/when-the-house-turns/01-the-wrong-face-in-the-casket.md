## 1. The Wrong Face in the Casket

The flowers at Blackwood House had always been wrong for funerals—too bright, too many, peonies and marigolds crowding the parlor like guests who hadn't heard the news. Tamsin stood at the edge of the receiving line with her gloves still on, her coat still buttoned, and let the relatives come to her in a slow tide of perfume and pity.

*So sorry. So young. Twins, you said? Identical? God, that must make it—*

*Make it what?* she wanted to ask. *Make it what, exactly?*

She had not cried yet. She was aware of this the way a person is aware of a missing tooth—the tongue returning again and again to the absence, probing for grief the way it should be there. Sable was dead. The call had come on a gray Tuesday, the county sheriff's voice careful and flat: *found at the property, Ms. Blackwood. We believe it was an accident. We're very sorry for your loss.* And Tamsin had sat down on the edge of her bed in the city, four hundred miles away, and waited to feel something arrive.

What arrived instead was the smell of turned earth, though her window was closed, though it was February, though nothing had been turned anywhere.

Now she was here, and the house had that smell everywhere, under the flowers, under the candlewax, a sweet-rotten undertone like a cellar that had learned to breathe.

"Tamsin." Aunt Verna took her hands, squeezing through the gloves. Verna was seventy and built like a fence post, all vertical lines and iron gray hair pinned so tight it looked structural. Her eyes did the thing they had always done when they landed on Tamsin—a flicker, a hesitation, half a syllable of some other name swallowed whole. "You look tired, dear."

"I drove all night."

"You should have called. We'd have left the porch light on." Verna said it the way she said everything, as if quoting a rule from an invisible manual. Then, lower, with a glance toward the casket at the front of the parlor: "Are you ready? You don't have to look. Some people don't."

"I'm her twin," Tamsin said. "I've looked at that face my whole life."

Verna's hands tightened, just once, and then she was gone, folding back into the relatives like a card returned to the deck.

* * *

Tamsin made herself wait. She made herself work the line, accept the casseroles of sympathy, the hands pressed to hers, the men who had known her grandfather and the women who remembered her mother's wedding and the cousins whose names she'd never learned because the family had always sorted itself into the ones who mattered and the ones who didn't, and Tamsin and Sable had been raised carefully, deliberately, on the wrong side of that line.

*Ignored daughters live longer,* Sable used to joke, when they were teenagers, smoking stolen cigarettes in the orchard. *Nobody bothers to sacrifice the ones they forget about.*

Tamsin had laughed then. She was not laughing now.

The room emptied by degrees, the way rooms do at wakes—first the strangers, then the distant kin, then the neighbors, until only the family remained, and then not even them, just Verna directing the undertaker's assistant in a low voice and the candles burning down in their brass holders, and Tamsin walking at last down the length of the parlor toward the open casket as if walking toward a dock at the end of the world.

The flowers around it were white lilies, the only white flowers in the house, and their perfume was so thick it had a texture, a weight, pressing against her face like water.

She looked down.

The woman in the casket was wearing Sable's gray dress, the good one, the one with the pearl buttons. Her hands were folded on her breastbone, and the undertaker had done kind work with them—there was nothing wrong with the hands. It was the face that stopped Tamsin's heart and then, a long moment later, started it again at a different speed.

It was her face.

Not *like* hers. Not the family resemblance, not the twin-mirror similarity that strangers always fumbled—*which one are you?*—but hers, exact, down to the small asymmetry at the corner of her mouth that Sable had never shared. Sable's smile had pulled left. This one pulled right. Tamsin's smile pulled right.

She leaned closer. The candlelight moved on the dead woman's skin, waxy and yellow, and the features held. The arch of the brow. The shallow dent in the chin. And there, at the hairline above the left ear, half-hidden by the mortician's careful arrangement of the hair—

The scar.

A thin white crescent, four centimeters, maybe five. Tamsin knew its length because she had measured it in a bathroom mirror at nineteen, the night after she'd earned it, falling from the low branch of the apple tree in the orchard, the branch that Sable had dared her to climb and then, when Tamsin slipped, had stood beneath with her arms crossed and not caught her. *You should watch where you put your weight,* Sable had said. Tamsin still had the scar. Sable had never had it. Sable had never fallen for anything in her life.

Tamsin's hand came up before she could stop it, fingers hovering an inch above the dead woman's brow, close enough to feel the cold rising off the skin like the cold off a stone in shade.

"That's not—" she said, and her voice came out strange, flattened, a stranger's voice in her own throat.

"Tamsin." Verna was beside her. Tamsin hadn't heard her cross the room. "Dear. Step back."

"That's my face." She heard herself say it and heard how it sounded, and hated it, and said it again anyway, louder, because it was true and the truth deserved volume. "That is *my face.* That's my scar. Sable didn't have this. Sable was never—she never fell, she never—"

"Grief plays tricks." Verna's voice was calm, level, the voice of a woman reading instructions. She took Tamsin's wrist—her grip was much stronger than her age suggested—and drew her hand away from the casket. "It's very common with twins. The mind confuses. The eye confuses. You're seeing what you expect to see."

"I'm seeing what's *there.*"

"Are you?" Verna looked down at the dead woman, and for one unguarded moment her expression did something complicated—something that was not grief, or not only grief. Something closer to the look of a woman checking a lock. "It's Sable, dear. The sheriff identified her. The dental records confirmed it. There's paperwork."

"There's paperwork," Tamsin repeated.

"There's always paperwork. It's how we know what's true." Verna smoothed Tamsin's hair back from her face with one hand, a gesture from childhood, from fevers and bad dreams, and it should have been comforting and instead made Tamsin's skin crawl, because Verna's eyes were still on the casket, still checking. "You're exhausted. You've been driving all night and grieving all day. Go upstairs. Your old room is made up. Sleep, and in the morning it will look like your sister again."

*It will look like your sister again.*

The sentence sat in Tamsin's ear long after Verna had steered her away, long after the candles were pinched out and the parlor door closed and the lilies gathered up for the morning. It will *look* like your sister. Not *it is.* Not *you'll feel better.* The house had always spoken in that register—Verna, the aunts, the grandmother before them, all of them fluent in a language where nothing was ever stated, only arranged.

* * *

Her old room was at the back of the house, over the kitchen, the small room, the one given to the daughter who didn't matter. The bed was made with sheets that smelled of cedar and something underneath the cedar, faintly, like matches. Tamsin sat on the edge of it without undressing and listened to the house settle around her.

Blackwood House had a vocabulary of sounds she'd known as a child: the tick of the boiler, the sigh of pipes, the way the third stair announced you to the whole first floor. All of it was there, unchanged, and all of it was wrong, because the house was not settling the way houses settle. It was quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone has just stopped talking about you.

She thought about the scar. She thought about the smile that pulled right. She thought about the phone call—*found at the property*—and how the sheriff had never once said *your sister* without a half-second pause before it, a small mechanical hitch, like a man reading a line he'd been given.

She thought about how Sable had called her three weeks ago, at eleven at night, and how unlike her sister the voice had sounded. Not drunk. Not high. *Frightened.* Sable, who had never been frightened of anything, whispering: *Tammy. Tammy, listen. If anyone ever tells you I'm dead, don't sign anything. Don't touch anything with your name already on it. And whatever you do—*

The call had dropped. Tamsin had told herself it was nothing, that Sable was being Sable, dramatic, cruel, hungry for attention she'd never been denied so much as she'd claimed to be. She had not called back. She had told herself she'd call back tomorrow.

There was a knock at the door.

One knock. Soft. Patient.

Tamsin did not move. The house ticked. The pipes sighed. Outside, far off across the dark lawn, she could hear the excavator's engine ticking as it cooled on the trailer—the crew had come early for the foundation work, Verna had said, they'd start digging at first light—and beneath all of it, so faint she might have invented it, a sound like breathing from the other side of the bedroom door.

"Tamsin," said the voice on the other side. Her own voice. Her exact voice, the one that pulled right when it smiled. "It's only me. Let me in."

She sat very still on the bed, in the small room, in the house that had never wanted her, and did not answer.

The knock came again. Then, after a long moment, in a voice that was not hers at all—lower, older, patient as water wearing down stone:

"We know you're in here. You're on the list."

Downstairs, in the dark parlor, the lid of the casket settled shut with a sound like a book closing.
