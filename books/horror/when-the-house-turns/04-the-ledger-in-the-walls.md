## 4. The Ledger in the Walls

The study smelled of cold pipe smoke and old paper, a smell that had outlived the man who made it by thirty years and would likely outlive me too.

I had been avoiding this room since I arrived. That was the truth of it, though I dressed it up in practicality—there were boxes to unpack, arrangements to confirm with the funeral home, a casket to stop looking at every time I closed my eyes. But on the third morning after the wake, with the excavators growling somewhere below the hill like a beast turning over in its sleep, I found myself standing in the doorway of Grandmother Odile's study with no memory of deciding to come here.

The house did that. I was beginning to understand that about it. It didn't move you; it simply arranged itself so that the room you needed was the room you arrived at, the way water finds the low place without any intention at all.

"Verna?" I called, though I knew she'd gone into town to argue with the bank about probate. The house answered with silence—the particular, attentive silence of a thing listening back.

I went in.

* * *

Odile Blackwood had ruled this house from behind that desk the way a spider rules a web: never moving, everything arriving at her. The desk itself was oak gone amber with age, its surface scarred by decades of rings from cups she never offered guests. The walls were lined with books whose spines had faded to the color of bone. A taxidermied crow watched from the mantel with one glass eye, the other long since lost, so that it seemed perpetually mid-wink, sharing some joke at my expense.

I told myself I was looking for the deed. That was the reasonable story. Verna kept saying the papers were "somewhere safe," which in our family meant *hidden*, and if I was going to decide what to do with this house—and I hadn't decided, I kept telling myself I hadn't—I needed to see what exactly we owned.

But my hands knew better than my head. They went past the deed box on the shelf, past the filing cabinet with its brass handles, straight to the bookshelf nearest the window, where the light fell worst and the dust lay thickest. They walked themselves along the spines until they reached a slim volume bound in something that wasn't quite leather—too smooth, too warm, as if it had a pulse slowed nearly to stopping.

It had no title. Just a symbol pressed into the cover: a door, drawn in a single unbroken line, closed.

I carried it to the desk because my legs had gone strange and I didn't trust them to hold me standing. Outside, the excavator's engine cut off abruptly, and in the new quiet I could hear the house settling around me, joists ticking like a cooling engine, or like knuckles rapping gently against wood.

I opened the ledger.

* * *

The first page was dated 1911, in handwriting so fine it looked engraved:

*Invited in: Edmund Blackwood, of his own free will, having knocked.*
*Debt recorded: one future, unnamed.*

Below it, another hand—firmer, later—had added: *Paid in full, 1949. See Marguerite.*

I turned the page. My skin had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the drafty room.

*Invited in: Marguerite Blackwood, born under this roof, having no choice in the matter of her arrival.*
*Debt recorded: one winter's protection. Interest accruing.*

Page after page. Four generations of us, each entry in a different hand but always the same two lines, always the same terrible bookkeeping. *Invited in.* *Debt recorded.* Some debts were small—a harvest saved, a fever broken, a husband returned whole from a war that ate other men's husbands whole. Some were larger. In 1963, beside my grandmother's own name, someone had written: *Debt recorded: one child's future, to be named at signing.* And beneath it, in Odile's upright, schoolroom-perfect script: *Signed.*

Signed for what? I turned the pages faster. There were addenda, amendments, little marginal notes in ink that shifted color when I tilted the page toward the window—not brown to black, but brown to something darker, something wet-looking. A note from 1987: *Renewal declined by Harold Blackwood. Consequences logged.* And below that, in a different hand entirely, one that slanted and hurried like a person writing while walking away: *Harold gone by spring. Renewal resumed.*

My uncle Harold. The one nobody talked about. The one whose photograph had been removed from the hallway staircase, leaving a pale rectangle on the wallpaper like a scar.

I sat down in Odile's chair—it received me with a creak of recognition that made my stomach turn—and I read the ledger the way you read a diagnosis. Slowly. Twice. Hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something survivable.

They didn't.

Every fortune the family had ever enjoyed was there, itemized. The timber money in the twenties. The shipping shares that survived the Depression untouched while everyone around us drowned. The way the bank had *begged* my father to take a loan in '91 rather than the reverse. Each windfall entered on the right-hand page under *Consideration rendered*, each payment owed on the left under *Debt recorded*, and the arithmetic between them was the arithmetic of a loan shark: generous beyond reason, and compounding forever.

We had never been rich. We had been *collateral*. The house held the note on all of us.

And then I found Sable.

* * *

Her name appeared first on a page dated twenty-six years ago, in Odile's hand:

*Invited in: Sable Blackwood, twin-born, second by four minutes.*
*Debt recorded: deferred. To be assessed at majority.*

Second by four minutes. I had forgotten that anyone knew that—that anyone had bothered to record which of us came first. I stared at the line and felt the strangest vertigo, as if the ledger had reached up through the paper and taken my chin in its hand. *Second by four minutes.* Sable had spent her whole life telling people she was older. She'd said it so many times I think she believed it. She'd said it the night before she died, on the phone, laughing: *"You've always been the baby, Tam. Let me handle the house. You know how she dotes on me."*

Let me handle the house.

I turned the page. My fingers were shaking badly enough that the paper whispered like something breathing.

There it was. Dated eleven months ago, in handwriting I knew better than my own—because it was nearly identical to mine, because we were twins, because Sable had practiced my signature in the margins of her schoolbooks when we were girls just to make me scream:

*Sable Blackwood, having attained majority, offers payment in full settlement of all outstanding debt:*
*One twin, freely given.*
*Tamsin Blackwood.*
*Signed: Tamsin Blackwood.*

The letters of my name curved across the bottom of the page in my own hand. My own hand, to the last flourish—the little hook I put on the *T* that drove my third-grade teacher to distraction. Except I had never signed anything in this house. I had never seen this ledger before tonight. And I had not set foot on this property in six years, not since the funeral I didn't attend, not since Sable called and called and finally stopped calling, and I let the silence between us grow into a wall I told myself she'd built.

She hadn't built it. She had forged her way over it, using my face, my name, my hand.

*Freely given.* The words seemed to pulse on the page, patient as a trap left baited. Eleven months ago Sable had sold me to this house. And then—my eyes went back up the page, and the horror turned over like a stone showing what lives underneath—she had run. She had fled to the city, to the apartment I visited twice a year, to the life she kept at arm's length from me because proximity might have shown me what she'd done. And the house had waited. Had sent her dreams, maybe. Had knocked. And when she finally came back—when she came back to die, or to pay, or to bargain again—

The casket. The wrong face. The scar only I should have had.

The house had rejected her substitute. Or she had offered it a substitute, and the substitute had been *me*, and the house knew the difference even if the mourners didn't, even if I hadn't.

I don't remember standing up. I remember the ledger open on the desk, and the crow winking its one eye, and outside the window the afternoon going the color of tarnished silver. I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like falling: *Sable didn't trade places with me. She tried to. And it didn't take.*

Which meant the debt was still open. Which meant the house was still waiting for its payment.

Which meant the thing in the casket wearing my face had been an apology—or a down payment—or a message.

Behind me, softly, from somewhere inside the wall, something wrote a single chalk mark.

I heard it clearly: the dry squeak of chalk on plaster, one stroke, unhurried. A tally. An accounting updated.

I turned around slowly. The pantry wall shared its back with this study; I'd found the fresh marks there yesterday, a column of them behind the flour tins, and told myself a previous owner's children had played at counting. Now I understood they weren't counting doors, or days, or anything so innocent.

They were counting *us*. Every name in the ledger had a column of marks somewhere in these walls, and the marks grew whenever—

Another stroke. Squeak.

I looked down at the ledger, still open to Sable's forged signature, and saw that the ink of my name had darkened. Not faded. Darkened. Wet-looking. As if the page were drinking it in, testing it, confirming that the signature belonged to a living hand.

As if, now that I had touched the book—now that I had *read* it, now that I stood inside these walls knowing what I knew—I had, in some sense the ledger recognized and I did not, been invited in.

From the front hall, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the front door opening. Footsteps on the tile. Verna's voice calling my name—but wrong somehow, stretched a half-tone flat, the way a recording stretches when the tape drags.

"Tamsin? Are you in the study?"

I closed the ledger. The cover's pressed door-symbol was warm under my palm, warmer than it should have been, warm as skin.

"Yes," I said, and my voice sounded borrowed too. "I'm here."

The footsteps started down the hall toward me, slow and even, and behind the pantry wall, patiently, the chalk began to write again.
