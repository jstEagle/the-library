## 2. Porch Light

The walk from the bus stop to Crane House took eleven minutes when I was a child. I know this because Aldous and I timed it once, racing, and because timing things was how we made the world feel like it had rules. It took me considerably longer now, partly because of the urn in my arms—heavier than I expected, the way grief always is, denser than its contents—and partly because my legs kept trying to stop.

Not stumble. Stop. Like the town itself was thickening around me, air gone the texture of old honey.

Frost Hollow at dusk is a watercolor left out in the rain: the church, the feed store, the diner with its sign missing the *e* in DINER, which I swear was missing when I was nine too, and which nobody had fixed in twenty years because fixing it would have meant admitting someone still lived here. The mountains behind everything, going purple and then black. And above it all, on its hill, the house.

Every window was lit.

I stood in the middle of Hollow Road with an urn full of my brother and stared, because that was impossible. The power to Crane House was cut before I was born—before *we* were born, I corrected myself, and the correction hurt in a place I didn't have anatomy for. Dad used to joke that we were raised by candlelight like Victorians, and Mom would say *it builds character* in the voice she used when she meant *stop asking questions.* Twenty years since anyone lived there. The realtor's listing, the one time I'd looked it up out of morbid curiosity, had been blunt about it: *As-is. No utilities. Estate sale.*

And every window burned gold.

Not flickering, the way candles or gaslight flicker. Steady. The steady light of a house that had been waiting up.

People do strange things when the impossible presents itself politely. We negotiate. I told myself it was a squatter. I told myself it was solar panels, which is the kind of lie that only works if you've never seen solar panels. I told myself that grief does this, that the brain under grief is a bad landlord who lets all sorts of strange tenants in, and that by morning the windows would be dark and I'd feel foolish and human and merely sad.

Then I started walking, because the urn was getting cold in my arms, and that was its own small wrongness I decided not to examine. Ashes don't chill. Ashes are room temperature forever, the temperature of finished things. This one was cold as a milk bottle.

The driveway was gravel, then dirt, then the crushed shell Mom had insisted on because it crunched, she said, so you could always hear yourself coming home. I heard myself coming home for a hundred feet and it sounded like someone else.

* * *

The porch was exactly as I remembered it, which was the first thing that genuinely frightened me, though I wouldn't understand why until later. Houses age. Houses rot. Houses get the sag and gray of anything abandoned. This porch had been painted—recently, by the smell of it—in that green Dad called *Crane green* and everyone else called *hospital green.* The swing hung level. The boards didn't creak. Twenty years of weather had been politely asked to wait elsewhere.

And on the top step, in the pool of light falling out the front window, sat Wren.

I want to be careful here, because memory is a liar with excellent penmanship, and I've had twenty years to embellish. But I don't think I'm embellishing when I say my first feeling wasn't fear. It was relief. A whole, stupid, childhood-shaped relief, the kind you feel when you wake from a nightmare and the nightlight is still on. There Wren was—small, maybe eight years old in the way Wren was always maybe eight years old, in the way that Wren had never been any age at all but had settled on eight because eight is when you stop needing to be held and start needing to be believed. Pale hair like frost on a fence rail. A sweater I recognized, which is a sentence I'd like to take back, because I recognized a sweater that had never existed in any store, a sweater that had only ever existed in the shared invention of two children and one impossible friend.

Wren looked up at me with the patient, pleased expression of someone whose guest has finally arrived, and said:

"You came back to the wrong door."

My mouth did what mouths do, which is nothing useful. The urn's cold soaked through my coat. Somewhere behind the lit windows, deep in the house, something shifted its weight.

"Wren," I said finally. Because what else do you say. Because the name came out whole, unbroken, like it had been sitting in my mouth all these years waiting for its owner.

"You remembered." Wren beamed, and it was the same beam, the exact beam, and that was worse than anything. "I was worried, after. People forget so fast. It's the first thing they do." A pause, and the smile tilted, became something older. "You kept the counting, though. I can tell. You counted the windows from the road. Eleven lit, and you checked for a twelfth."

I had. I had absolutely done that, without deciding to, the way you check for your keys.

"There's no twelfth window," I said. "There never was."

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "Not from the road."

I climbed the porch steps because the alternative was standing in the dark forever, and because some part of me—the part that had raced Aldous up these steps, the part that had sat cross-legged on these boards while Wren taught us the door-counting game—was still nine years old and wanted very badly to sit down. I didn't sit. I stood at the edge of the light with my brother's ashes and looked at my imaginary friend, who was real, who had always been real, who was watching me with eyes the color of a winter sky deciding what to do about it.

"You're not possible," I said. "You were never possible. That was the whole—you were the thing Aldous and I *made up.*"

"We made each other up," Wren said, with the gentle pedantry of a child correcting an adult, or an adult correcting a child. "It's not the same thing. And you're wrong about the making. But we have all night for that, and you don't, actually, so." Wren patted the step beside them, an invitation I did not take. "You look like her. Your mother. You have her way of standing like the floor might lie to you."

"Don't talk about my mother."

"She'd have said the same thing. She was very protective of the parts of herself she'd sold." Wren said it lightly, the way children say terrible things, and then tilted their head. "You brought him home. That was kind. Most people scatter. Scattering is rude, I've always thought. Like returning a letter unopened."

The urn was so cold now it burned. I shifted it in my arms. "Aldous is dead. He died in—"

"In the city. In the winter. In his apartment, alone, which he hated, because he hated being on the wrong side of any wall." Wren's voice didn't change, didn't soften, and that was the horror of it—Wren delivered my brother's death like a weather report. "You were the one who found him."

"How do you know that?"

"Because you were the one who found him," Wren said again, patiently, as if I'd asked why water was wet. "You were always the one who found him. That was the arrangement."

I should have left then. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger this house keeps, which I now know it keeps: I *should* have left. I had a car—I didn't, I'd come by bus, but I could have gotten a car, could have walked back down the shell drive with the crunching and the cold urn and caught the last bus out of Frost Hollow and never learned why the windows were lit or what my mother had sold or which door was the wrong one.

Instead I asked, "What door should I have come back to?"

Wren's face did something I had never seen it do in all the years of the games, all the summers and snowed-in winters, all the counting. It went careful. The way an adult's face goes careful when a child asks the one question too many.

"Ah," Wren said softly. "You felt it. Good. That's better than most manage." They stood—unfolding, unfolding, a little more unfolding than a body that size should have required, though I'd swear to my grave I saw nothing wrong, only felt it, the way you feel a stair that isn't there. "The front door is the one your family has always used. It's a good door. It opens. It closes. It has done its job for a hundred years." Wren looked at the door, and the door, I could have sworn, looked back. "But it isn't the door you were invited through. You came home through the front door, and the front door was Aldous's door, and now Aldous doesn't need a door, does he."

The light in the windows flickered then—once, all of them, in perfect unison, like a house flinching.

"So whose door," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted, "was mine?"

Wren smiled at me, and it was the old smile, the game smile, the one from a hundred hide-and-seeks where Wren was always somehow both the finder and the thing found, and said:

"That's the right question. That's the *best* question. You always asked the best questions." They reached past me—close, so close I felt cold come off them like off an open freezer—and pressed one pale finger against the front door's wood. "Come inside, Orsen Crane. Keep the lights on. Count your doors. And whatever else happens tonight—" the smile widened by one tooth's worth, "—don't answer the knocking before sunrise. He does so hate to be kept waiting, and he's been waiting such a long, long time."

The door opened without anyone touching the handle.

Warm air came out. It smelled like my childhood: woodsmoke and cinnamon and, underneath, faintly, like the inside of a freezer left open too long.

I went in. Of course I went in. I had an urn to set down and a night to survive and, God help me, a house that had left the lights on for me.

Behind me, on the porch, I heard Wren settle back onto the step, staying outside, keeping watch, and say—to me, or to the door, or to the dark coming up the hill—

"Welcome home. One of you, anyway."

The door closed. The lock turned itself. And somewhere deep in the house, patient and polite, the first knock came—hours too early, or exactly on time.

I counted the doors in the hallway. Eleven.

For now.
