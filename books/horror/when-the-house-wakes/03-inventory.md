## 3. Inventory

The ritual came back to me the way a language comes back to someone who swore they'd forgotten it—suddenly, completely, and with a faint taste of shame.

I was standing in the front hall, still holding my coat like a man who might change his mind about staying, when my eyes did it without asking permission. They went to the doors. They counted.

One. The front door behind me, its paint gone the color of old teeth, the brass knob worn to a shine by four generations of Cranes who apparently never learned to knock on their own way out.

Two. The parlor door, closed, though I had left it open—hadn't I? I'd walked through it not two minutes ago. It stood shut now with the smugness of furniture that knows something you don't.

Three. The dining room. Four. The kitchen. Five. The pantry, which in my memory was a closet full of mothballs and my mother's preserves, labeled in her small furious handwriting: *PLUMS — DO NOT TOUCH, ALDOUS.*

Six. The stairs going up, which counted as a door in our system because Aldous said so, and what Aldous said about counting was law. Seven through ten, the bedrooms. Eleven, the bathroom at the end of the hall, its door swollen shut since roughly the Nixon administration.

Eleven doors. That was correct. That was *right*. Eleven inside, twelve at midnight—the twelfth being the one that appeared, or didn't, depending on how honest you were willing to be about what you saw—and I stood there in the hall of my dead parents' house feeling the first real relief I'd felt since the bus dropped me off, because eleven was the number, and the world still had rules, and one of those rules was mine.

Then I noticed the hallway was longer than the house.

This is the sort of sentence a person doesn't say out loud, so I'll say it here, where no one can commit me for it. The hallway was longer than the house. I know the footprint of this building the way you know your own mouth—I grew up in it, I hid in it, I mapped every inch of it during games of hide-and-seek that Wren always won, which should have been my first clue that the games were rigged. The house sat on its lot like a fat black beetle: forty feet deep, give or take. The hallway I was standing in ran, by my best estimate, sixty. Maybe seventy. The far end receded into a dimness that the light from the windows didn't seem to reach, as if the darkness back there were a liquid and the daylight couldn't swim.

I told myself: shock. Grief. You haven't slept; you carried your brother's ashes across three states in a duffel bag; your sense of proportion is shot.

My sense of proportion is excellent, actually. It's one of the few things about myself I've never had cause to doubt. And it was telling me, in the calm professional tone of an appraiser finding water damage, that the hallway was wrong.

* * *

I did what any reasonable person does when reality misbehaves. I ignored it and started taking inventory.

This too was ritual. Every childhood visit to a new place—or an old place, after a long absence—Aldous and I would walk it together and count everything. Doors, windows, stairs, chairs. "You have to know what's yours," he used to say, in his solemn seven-year-old voice, "or things can pretend they're yours." I thought it was a game we invented. I have since revised my opinion of how many of our games we invented.

So I walked the ground floor, cataloging, and the house let me, which somehow made it worse. A house that fights you is a house with a position. This one just... opened itself, room by room, like a hand uncurling to show you what it's holding.

The parlor was wider than it should have been. Not dramatically—no cathedral nonsense—but by a yard, maybe more, the kind of difference you feel in your knees before your brain signs off on it. The wallpaper was the same faded roses my mother hung the summer I was nine, but there was more wall than there used to be, extra stretches of roses repeating with a patience that felt manufactured. The window seat was still there. The cushion still bore the fossil impression of where Aldous used to sit curled up with his books, a shallow dent in the foam shaped exactly like him, preserved like a saint's knee-print in stone. I put my hand in it. It fit. Of course it fit. We shared a shape; that was the whole joke everyone made at every family gathering until we stopped having family gatherings.

The dent was warm.

I took my hand out of it and wrote *shock, grief, bad heating* in the ledger of excuses, which was getting fuller by the hour, and moved on.

The kitchen was the worst of the ordinary rooms, because the kitchen was fine. Table for six, set for none. My mother's cast-iron skillet hanging on its hook, seasoned black as a meteorite. The clock over the stove—the one that never worked, the one Dad kept meaning to fix for twenty years—was ticking. Not running fast or slow or backward, nothing dramatic. Just ticking. Steady, patient, keeping time for a house with no electricity and no reason to expect any.

I unplugged it. There was nothing to unplug it from. The cord ended in a frayed stump tucked neatly behind the stove, coiled like something sleeping.

I ticked anyway. No—that's not right. The clock ticked. I stood there listening to it tick, and somewhere in the back of my skull a counter started keeping time with it, and I understood that I could leave this kitchen and the counter would keep going, all night, all my life, and I would never again hear silence without a beat under it.

"Fine," I told the clock. "Great. Love that."

Talking to appliances. Day one. The town would hear about it by Thursday.

* * *

The stairs creaked in the same places. That was almost tender—the third step, the seventh, the landing where the banister had been chewed by some dog we never owned, a story Dad told with such conviction that I half-believed in the ghost dog for years. My feet found the loud steps without looking, and the house creaked its old complaints beneath me, and for three seconds I was just a kid coming upstairs for dinner, and the grief hit me sideways, clean, without any frost in it. Just missing them. Just missing Aldous, whoever Aldous had turned out to be, whatever was in the urn on the porch table downstairs.

Then I reached the top and the hallway up there was even longer than the one below, and the tenderness packed its bags.

Upstairs, the geography had opinions. My bedroom door—seven, in the count—opened onto my bedroom, and my bedroom was correct down to the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a rabbit being arrested. But Aldous's room, directly across the hall, was... further away. Not metaphorically. The distance between the two doors had grown. What should have been four strides was eight, then ten, and the wallpaper between them repeated the same small pattern of blue birds so many times that the birds began to look less like decoration and more like a crowd, gathered, watching, waiting for something to happen in the corridor between the twins' rooms.

I did not walk to Aldous's door. I want that on the record. I stood at my own threshold and looked down the lengthening hall and decided, with the full authority of a grown adult, that there was nothing over there I needed tonight.

The count disagreed. The count wanted completeness. Eleven doors, and I'd only stood at eight of them, and the ritual itched in me like a bite you're not supposed to scratch. Because that was the dirty secret of these rituals, the thing nobody tells you about compulsions: they don't protect you because they're true. They protect you because doing them feels like holding the wheel. And I was in a house where the steering had gone soft, and I would have counted fence posts to the moon if it kept my hands busy.

Nine was the spare room, unchanged, smelling of cedar and dust. Ten was the master bedroom, where the bed was made—made, corners tucked, pillows plumped, in a house empty for years—as though my parents had stepped out to buy milk and would be back any minute, and I shut that door quickly and stood breathing through my nose like a man in a burning building deciding which smoke to inhale.

Which left eleven. The bathroom. Swollen shut since forever.

It was open.

Not ajar. Open, wide, welcoming, and beyond it the little tiled room stretched back farther than the bathroom had any business stretching, the mirror above the sink receding into a perspective that suggested the room continued past the back wall of the house, past the lot line, possibly past several neighboring counties. I could see the far end of the mirror's reflection, and the reflection showed the bathroom door behind me, and the door in the reflection was closed.

I closed the actual door. It shut with a click, obediently, like it had only been open to see what I'd do.

"Eleven," I said out loud, to the hallway, to the birds, to the ticking clock below. "Eleven doors. Counted twice. All present."

The house received this information without comment. Somewhere below, the clock ticked on, and I realized with a lurch that I hadn't heard it stop when I came upstairs, which meant sound traveled through this house better than it should, or the house was carrying the sound to me deliberately, like a host making sure his guest knows the entertainment has begun.

* * *

There was one more door. That was the problem. There was always one more door.

I found it at the end of the upstairs hall, past the last stretch of blue birds, where no door had ever been—where there had been, if memory served, a wall, and on that wall a framed cross-stitch of my mother's reading BLESSED BE THIS HOUSE, which I had privately always found menacing. The wall was still there. The cross-stitch was still on it, slightly crooked, slightly accusing. But beside it now, flush and seamless and painted the exact shade of the surrounding plaster so that you'd miss it if you weren't counting—which I was, God help me, I was born counting—stood a door.

It wasn't in the count. The count had concluded. Eleven, verified, complete. And yet here was a twelfth, at nine o'clock in the evening, hours ahead of schedule, standing quietly against the wall like a guest arrived early for a party that hadn't been announced.

I knew what it was before I touched the knob. Some things you know in your teeth. Behind this door was a room that hadn't existed yesterday, or twenty years ago, or ever, in any survey of this house I'd ever taken—and I had surveyed this house the way other children learned prayers.

The knob was cold. Colder than metal in an unheated house had any right to be, cold like a coin left out overnight in January, cold enough to stick briefly to my fingertips, and when I pulled my hand away the doorknob held the print of my fingers in frost, five small white ghosts slowly fading.

I should have gone downstairs. I should have sat in the parlor with the urn and waited for morning like a sensible mourner. Instead I stood there with my heart going like the clock, and I heard myself say, in the reasonable tone I use on editors:

"You're not due until midnight."

The door said nothing, which I appreciated. Silence, from a door, is at least traditional.

But as I watched, the frost print of my fingers spread. Thin white feathers crept outward from each fingertip across the painted wood, branching, ferning, until the whole face of the door wore a pattern like frost on glass—and then the pattern shifted, settled, resolved, and I was looking at lines. Deliberate lines. Straight edges. Corners.

The frost on the door was drawing a picture of another door.

Behind me, down the long hall, in the room I'd refused to enter, something shifted—a soft, heavy settling, like a crib being leaned into—and I turned toward the sound before I could stop myself, and the blue birds watched me turn, and at the far end of the corridor, where Aldous's door should have been, there was now a different door entirely. Smaller. Painted white. With a wooden cutout of a duck nailed to it at the height of a child's shoulder.

I have never seen that door before in my life. Neither, I am quite certain, has this house.

We stood there together, the house and I, each pretending we didn't notice the other growing.

"Okay," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "New rule. Nobody builds anything else until I've had coffee."

And because the ritual was the only armor I had, and armor doesn't care whether it fits, I raised one finger and pointed it down the hall—at the nursery door, at the frost-door, at the whole impossible inventory of the place—and I began the count again from the top.

"One," I said.

Downstairs, the clock ticked. And from behind the white door with the duck on it, very softly, in a voice I knew better than my own, something counted along.
