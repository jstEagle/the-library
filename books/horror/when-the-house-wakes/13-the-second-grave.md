## 13. The Second Grave

Wren told me not to go to the plot. That was the first clue it mattered.

Everything else Wren had said that night came wrapped in ceremony—rules recited like scripture, warnings delivered with the gravity of a doctor handing over test results. But the instruction about the graveyard had been almost casual, tossed off while I was winding my scarf: *Stay off the family ground before dawn. The soil there is still deciding.* And then it had looked at me. Not at the window, not at the frost writing its slow alphabet across the glass, but at me, with an attention so total it felt like being read aloud.

You don't survive a childhood in Frost Hollow without learning that when someone tells you *don't* in exactly that tone, they've already told you where the truth is buried.

Literally, as it turned out.

* * *

I left through the kitchen door because the front door had started to feel like a mouth. The cold hit me like a slap from something that loved me and wanted my attention. Frost Hollow in November doesn't have weather; it has temperament.

The family plot sat on the rise behind the house, past the dead apple tree my father used to threaten to prune and never did. Four stones. My great-grandparents, side by side, worn smooth as river rocks. My parents, newer, granite still sharp-edged, my mother's dates ending eleven years ago and my father's three years after, as if he'd simply decided he'd finished.

And then the two open graves.

I'd seen them that afternoon at Halloway & Sons, in the undertaker's ledger—the neat column of figures, two plots prepared, two vaults ordered, paid for in cash by an account that hadn't been touched since 1998. I'd assumed it was a clerical haunting, the house's paperwork outliving the house's purpose. Houses do that here. So do towns.

But standing on the rise now, breath smoking, I could see them plainly in the moonlight: two rectangles of turned earth behind the family stones, each lined with pale wooden framing, each exactly grave-shaped, each waiting with the patience of furniture.

Two. For one funeral. For ashes, which don't even need a plot, strictly speaking. You can scatter ashes anywhere. You can keep them on a shelf between the good china and the photo albums.

Somebody had dug two graves for my brother, and nobody in town could tell me why, and Wren didn't want me looking.

I'd brought the shovel from the shed. Of course I had. There's a version of this story where I stand at the edge of the plot, feel the appropriate dread, and walk back inside to drink tea with an imaginary friend until sunrise. I'd like to meet that version of me. He sounds well-rested.

The first bite of the shovel into frozen ground felt transgressive in a way I couldn't name—like reading someone's diary, like answering a knock. The earth gave grudgingly, frost-crusted on top, soft and wrong underneath. Wrong how? Soft the way bread goes when it's been sitting out too long. Soft the way flesh—

Don't. Dig.

I worked in the rhythm I remembered from burying our dog Biscuit when I was nine, Aldous beside me with the smaller trowel, both of us crying, both of us secretly thrilled to be allowed to be sad about something ordinary. Two boys in a hole's worth of grief. One boy. I kept getting the count wrong. That was becoming the theme of my life.

Which grave? I'd chosen the nearer one arbitrarily, but halfway down I understood there had been no arbitrariness about it. The nearer one was mine. I knew it the way you know your own bed in the dark—not by sight but by the shape of the hollow waiting for you.

That thought should have stopped me. Instead it made me dig faster, which tells you something about grief, or denial, or whatever the third thing is that lives in the space between them.

* * *

Three feet down, the soil changed.

It wasn't just softer. It was *warmer*. Not blood-warm, not yet—but warm the way a stone is warm hours after the sun leaves it, holding something. My breath stopped smoking above the hole. The air down there was room-temperature, and the wrongness of that in a Frost Hollow November was worse than any cold would have been. Cold, I could understand. Cold was the house's native language. Warmth, down here, meant something had been keeping this place heated. Meant something had been living in it.

Four feet. The shovel rang against wood.

I climbed out and knelt at the lip of the grave, heart going like a fist on a door, and looked down at what I'd uncovered.

Not a coffin. A lid—a simple plank cover over a shallow box, unvarnished pine, no nameplate, no handles. The kind of box you build in an afternoon if what you're putting in it isn't a body.

I lifted the plank. The hinges were new. Everything down here was new, or rather, everything down here was *unaged*, preserved in that pocket of warmth like fruit in a jar.

Inside the box, on a bed of white cloth, lay a mirror.

It was an old mirror, oval, silvered glass gone cloudy at the edges, in a frame of dark wood carved with— I leaned closer—carved with small repeated figures. Children, I thought at first. Then, with the slow turn of a key in a lock, I recognized the game. The carvings were of the counting game. Little stick-figure children standing in little doorways, eleven doors, twelve doors, and around the rim of the frame, in letters too fine to read without touching, words I chose not to decipher.

The mirror was fogged. Not dusty—fogged, from the inside, condensation blooming across the glass like breath on a winter window. Like the breath of whoever was on the other side, breathing against the glass, trying to see out.

And it was warm. Warm as a held hand. I lifted it out—it weighed nothing, or less than nothing; my arms reported weight but some deeper ledger recorded none—and the warmth soaked through my gloves into my palms, and I swear to God the mirror leaned into me. The way a cat leans. The way a child leans into the parent they're pretending is a horse.

"Okay," I said aloud, to the plot, to the house behind me, to whatever was listening. "Okay."

I looked into the glass.

My reflection looked back. Fine. Normal. Except—

Except the reflection was breathing harder than I was. Its chest moved faster. Its eyes were wider, darting, scanning the sky above the grave as if checking for something I hadn't thought to check for. And when I raised my hand—slowly, the way you do, testing—the reflection did not raise its hand. It pressed both palms flat against the inside of the glass instead, fingers spread, and mouthed something.

No sound came through. But I've spent enough nights in that house to read lips through frost.

It said: *which one are you.*

"I'm Orsen," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like ice underfoot.

The reflection shook its head. Slowly. With pity. It pointed—at me, at the grave, at itself—and then it did something I will carry out of this life if I carry anything. It smiled. My smile. The one Aldous and I shared, the asymmetric one, the left corner higher. And then it stepped backward, away from the glass, into the fogged dark behind the silvering, and it was gone, and the mirror went cold in my hands all at once, cold as a stone in a stream, cold as an apology.

I sat down in the dirt beside an open grave and held a mirror that had just shown me someone wearing my face who was afraid of *me*, and I laughed. I'll admit it. I laughed until it turned into the other thing, the thing laughter keeps in its basement, and I knelt there in the turned earth with my forehead nearly touching the box, saying, "Aldous. Aldous. Aldous." His name like a rosary. His name like a handhold. Because Wren's rules said never say both names in the same room, and this was not a room, and I needed one name that was safely, verifiably his.

Wasn't it?

* * *

Here is what I know, and let me lay it out flat, because laying things out flat is how I keep from screaming:

Aldous died in March. I identified the body. I remember the fluorescent hum of the morgue in Carver Falls, the attendant's bored kindness, the sheet pulled back to show a face I'd known longer than I'd known myself. I signed papers. I drove home with the urn seatbelted into the passenger seat because it seemed disrespectful to let it ride loose, and I talked to it the whole way, the way we'd talked on long drives, me doing voices, him laughing at the bad ones.

That's what I know. That's the load-bearing wall of my week.

But.

When I was six, I fell through the ice on Miller's Pond. This is a true memory. Mother pulled me out, wrapped me in her coat, and carried me home, and I remember the specific quality of that rescue—the panic, the prayer, the way she counted my breaths aloud all night. And I also remember, with equal texture, equal sensory weight, watching from the bank while *Aldous* was pulled out of the water, watching Mother wrap him in her coat, sitting dry and untouched in the snow and feeling—this is the part I've never told anyone—feeling *relieved*. Not glad he'd drowned. Glad it was him and not me. A six-year-old's relief, hot and shameful, and I have spent thirty years telling myself I invented that memory, that guilt dressed itself up as memory, that children confabulate.

Children confabulate. Sure. But children don't confabulate the smell of pond water in their brother's hair, or the exact pitch of their mother's counting—*breathe, breathe, breathe*—or the way the ice looked from below, which is the detail that undoes me. In one version, I see the ice crack overhead, white webbing against gray sky, hands reaching down. In the other version, I am the hands reaching down, and the face under the ice is my own face, and it is calm, and it mouths something at me through the water, and every time I reach this point in either memory, I wake up.

The mirror was warm because something had been breathing on the other side of it.

The second grave was dug because somebody always digs two.

And Wren—who has been unchanged for twenty years, who greeted me by name on a porch lit by electricity that doesn't exist, who told me *you came back to the wrong door*—Wren didn't want me opening this grave. Which means the grave contains something Wren needs unopened. Which means either it holds the answer, or it holds me.

* * *

I put the mirror back.

I want that on the record, whatever record survives. I did not run screaming through the town with my brother's death-mask under my arm. I laid the mirror in its box, folded the white cloth over it the way you'd tuck in a blanket, replaced the plank, and shoveled the earth back over it with more care than I've shown anything in years. If it was a person in there—if it was *me* in there—it deserved a made bed.

The last shovelful I patted flat with my gloved hand, and I stood there in the wind, and I said, "Whoever you are, I'm coming back for you. Before sunrise. I promise."

Behind me, at the property line, where the dead apple tree threw its shadow long across the frost, Mrs. Halloway stood at her fence in a nightgown and coat, watching. She'd been there a while; the snow showed no footprints leading to her, only leading away, as if she'd grown up out of the ground like one more stone.

"You opened it," she said. Not a question.

"It was already dug."

"They're always already dug." She came as close to the line as her fence allowed, and I noticed her hands were gripping the wire hard enough to whiten even in the dark. "Sixty years ago I stood where you're standing, Mr. Crane. My sister's plot. Second grave. Same warm dirt." Her breath plumed and tore away. "Do you know what I found?"

"A mirror."

"A mirror," she agreed, and something in her voice folded. "And I did what you're thinking of doing. I took it inside. I kept it. I talked to it. And every year it got clearer, and every year *I* got foggier, until the day my own daughter looked at me and asked who I was, and I realized the glass had been trading places with me the whole time. Polishing itself bright while it dulled me down." She looked at the house, and the house—I'd swear this—looked back. "The house doesn't collect debts, child. Debts get paid and done. The house collects *identities*. It keeps them the way you keep preserves. Labeled. Shelved. Warm."

"What do I do?"

"Sunrise is in four hours." She released the fence, one wire at a time, like letting go of a railing over a drop. "Whatever you're going to be, decide it fast. And Orsen—" She paused, and the pause had a terrible gentleness in it. "When you look in that glass again, and you will, ask it the question it asked you. Ask it which one of you is breathing."

She turned and walked back toward her house, and her footprints appeared behind her only after she'd passed, stitching themselves into the snow one beat late, like a sound dubbed out of sync.

I stood alone on the rise with two closed graves and one open question, and behind me, in the upstairs window of the waking house, a light I did not leave on flickered twice—patient, polite, like a knock.

Then, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, from the ground itself, from the warm dirt under my boots, I heard counting.

*Eleven. Twelve.*

*Thirteen.*
