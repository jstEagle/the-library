## 17. What Aldous Wants

The frost door in the nursery had grown a handle overnight.

I want to be clear about that, because it matters: doors don't grow handles. Doors are made with handles by people who intend for them to be opened. This one had simply *decided*, sometime between the knocking and the ledger and my hour of staring at the ceiling counting the eleven real doors like a rosary, that it was finished being a suggestion. The frost had thickened into something with depth to it now—glass under glass under glass—and set into its surface, at the height of a child's reaching hand, was a latch of ice so clear it looked almost apologetic.

I stood in front of it for a long time before I touched anything.

This was, I want it noted, personal growth. Twenty-four hours ago I would have opened it immediately, because that is what Orsen Crane does, or did, or was supposed to do—I was still fuzzy on which twin had the market cornered on recklessness. But I'd read Mother's ledger. I'd seen what Wren's face did when it forgot to hold its shape. I'd dug up an empty grave and found a warm mirror wearing my reflection like a coat. I was learning caution the way you learn a language: badly, under duress, from a textbook written in frost.

So instead of opening it, I breathed on it.

It's an old trick. Aldous and I used to do it on the school bus windows in winter—one of us would fog a pane and write a message backwards so the person on the other side could read it forward. He always wrote them backwards better than I did. He had the patience for it. He had patience for everything, which I mistook, for twenty years, for slowness.

I breathed on the frost and waited.

The fog of my breath didn't fade the way breath fades on cold glass. It *sank*. It went into the frost the way water goes into dry earth, and then, beneath where my breath had been, letters began to rise. Not forming—the way the names had formed on the windows downstairs, pushed up from some pressure behind the glass—but surfacing, like things floating up through dark water.

*YOU KEPT COUNTING.*

My heart did something complicated. "Aldous?"

*IT HATES WHEN YOU COUNT. THE DOORS. IT LOSES TRACK OF YOU.*

"Is that—" My voice came out wrong, too high, scraped thin. I crouched down to the level where the writing surfaced, knee-deep in nursery dust, talking to a wall of ice like it was a visitation window at a prison. "Is that why you told me? In the letters? Not just the door—don't open the door, but also *count the doors*—"

*THE COUNTING IS YOURS. MOTHER TAUGHT US BOTH. ONLY ONE OF US LISTENED.*

I sat down fully on the floor. There didn't seem to be any dignity left to protect, and my legs had opinions about standing.

"I don't know which one of us listened," I said. "That's the problem. That's the whole entire problem, Aldous. I don't know if I'm the one who learned the ritual or the one who needed it done for him."

The frost was quiet for a while. Frost is good at quiet. Then:

*I KNOW WHICH ONE YOU ARE.*

"Do you." It wasn't a question. It came out flat and ugly and I hated myself for it even as I said it, because underneath the flatness was the real question, the one I'd been carrying since the mirror: *are you sure it's me? Are you sure you're not the one in there, looking out, and this whole conversation is the house doing ventriloquism?*

*I HAVE TWENTY YEARS TO THINK ABOUT WHO I AM,* the frost wrote, letter by patient letter. *YOU'VE HAD TWO DAYS. I DON'T BLAME YOU FOR BEING SLOW.*

Despite everything—despite the knocking, the ledger, the thirteenth door waiting somewhere in the house's architecture like a held breath—a laugh came out of me. Small and cracked, but real. "That's a very Aldous thing to say."

*THAT'S A VERY ME THING TO SAY. WHICHEVER OF US THAT IS.*

*

Wren found me there an hour later. I'd moved from the floor to the old rocking chair, which rocked without being rocked, keeping time with nothing, and I'd been talking to the frost in a low continuous murmur—the way you talk to someone through a hospital door, when the words matter less than the fact of your voice arriving.

Wren stood in the doorway of the nursery with its hands folded, wearing its small boy shape the way a bad actor wears a costume it has finally stopped believing in. It watched me talk to the wall for a moment.

"You should eat," Wren said.

"I'm not hungry."

"You should sleep."

"I'm not tired."

"You should stop talking to the glass," Wren said, and there was something new in its voice—not the patient warmth of my childhood, not the careful neutrality of last night, but a thin wire of something that might have been worry, if worry were a thing that landlords felt about their property. "It isn't a conversation. It's a *drain*. It will take your warmth and give you back words."

"It gave me back words already," I said. "Good ones."

"Orsen." Wren stepped into the room, and the temperature dropped the way it does when it enters anywhere, frost blooming outward from its feet across the floorboards like ink through paper. "Whatever is speaking to you, it is not your brother. It is the house using his shape. The house has been using shapes all week. Mine. Your mother's. It will use yours next, if you keep feeding it attention."

"That's funny," I said, "because I was about to say the same thing to you."

Wren went still. Not human-still, not thinking-still. *Furniture*-still. And in that stillness I saw—or thought I saw, or let myself see—the tall jointed thing underneath, the one it had shown me in the attic's bad light, standing inside the outline of a child like a hermit crab standing inside somebody's abandoned shell.

"The letters," I said. "In the attic. Dated after he died. His handwriting. You told me those were the house baiting me."

"They are."

"And the frost writing now. Also bait."

"Yes."

"And the knocking last night, using his voice. Bait."

"Orsen—"

"So here's what I can't figure out, Wren." I stood up from the rocking chair, and it kept rocking behind me, faithful to nothing. "If everything that comes through the glass is the house lying to me, how do I know you're not? You've been the house's *employee* a lot longer than Aldous has been its prisoner. Maybe the exhausted brother asking to be remembered is the fiction, and the friendly childhood ghost telling me to stay away from the door is the trap. Maybe the trap is shaped exactly like kindness because that's what works on me."

The frost between us had gone silent. Watching. Everything in this house watches; I've stopped finding it rude.

Wren looked at me for a long moment with eyes that were the exact blue of my mother's good china, and then it said, quietly, "You've gotten smarter since yesterday. That's unfortunate. It makes the next part harder for both of us."

And it turned and walked out of the nursery, and the frost on the floor closed over its footprints like water.

*

I didn't follow it. That felt important. Instead I sat back down in front of the door and put my palm flat against the ice, and this time I didn't flinch from the cold, which had stopped being temperature and become more like *attention*—the feeling of being known thoroughly by something vast and indifferent, except that this particular patch of vast indifference knew me *specifically*, and its attention had a tremble in it.

"Aldous," I said. "If it's you. If any part of it's you. I need to know what you want. Not what the house wants through you. What *you* want. Because I've spent two days assuming you're angry. Assuming you came back for revenge, or a swap, or to take the life back that got dealt to you. And I've been bracing for it. And I'm so tired of bracing."

The cold under my hand shifted. Breathed, almost.

Then the letters came, slower than before, each one rising through the frost like something being carried up from a great depth, and they were smaller too, cramped, the handwriting of someone conserving strength:

*NOT ANGRY.*

"No?"

*TIRED. SO TIRED, ORSEN. YOU CAN'T IMAGINE. THE HOUSE DOESN'T LET YOU SLEEP BEHIND THE GLASS. IT JUST LETS YOU WATCH. TWENTY YEARS OF WATCHING YOU LIVE MY—LIVE A LIFE. AND I USED TO KEEP SCORE. WHO GOT THE GOOD CHRISTMAS. WHO GOT MOM'S LAUGH. I DID, AT FIRST. ANYONE WOULD.*

"What changed?"

*THE HOUSE CHANGED ME. OR WORE ME DOWN UNTIL WHAT WAS LEFT WASN'T THE KIND OF THING THAT KEEPS SCORE. YOU CAN'T HATE PROPERLY IN THE COLD. HATE NEEDS HEAT. ALL THAT'S LEFT AFTER A WHILE IS—*

The writing stalled. My hand pressed harder against the glass, stupidly, as if I could push the word up through the frost by force.

"Is what?" I whispered. "What's left?"

*WATCHING. AND NOTICING. AND REMEMBERING. I REMEMBER EVERYTHING NOW. THAT'S THE HOUSE'S MISTAKE. IT MADE ME A PERFECT WITNESS AND THEN IT NEEDED ME TO BE A MONSTER, AND I WON'T BE ONE. I'M NOT GOING TO HELP IT COLLECT YOU.*

"Why not?" My throat was tight. "You'd be free. If we swapped. If I took your place—"

*BECAUSE FREEING ME BY PUTTING YOU HERE ISN'T FREEDOM, IT'S A TRANSFER OF OWNERSHIP. THE HOUSE STAYS FED EITHER WAY. DON'T YOU SEE? IT DOESN'T WANT ALDOUS. IT DOESN'T WANT ORSEN. IT WANTS A CRANE BEHIND THE GLASS, FOREVER, GENERATION AFTER GENERATION. WE'RE NOT PEOPLE TO IT. WE'RE A LINE ITEM.*

I thought of Mrs. Halloway on her porch, refusing to cross the property line, sixty years of knowing. *The house doesn't collect debts,* she'd said. *It collects identities.* She'd buried a twin herself. Somewhere behind some glass, some Halloway was watching some Halloway live a borrowed life right now, and neither of them had ever figured out the loophole.

"The contract," I said slowly. "Mother's contract. The renunciation clause. If I give up belonging before sunrise—"

*IT WILL KEEP WHOEVER'S UNCLAIMED. YES. READ IT AGAIN. READ THE PART ABOUT WHO DOES THE CLAIMING. THE HOUSE WROTE IT. THE HOUSE GETS TO INTERPRET IT. IF YOU RENOUNCE, IT DECIDES WHO WAS NEVER CLAIMED, AND GUESS WHO IT'LL SAY.*

"Me."

*OR ME. WHICHEVER OF US IS MORE USEFUL BEHIND THE GLASS. IT'S BEEN MANAGING THIS SWAP FOR SIXTY YEARS ACROSS FOUR FAMILIES. IT'S VERY GOOD AT PAPERWORK.*

I laughed again, that broken little laugh, and wiped my face with the sleeve that wasn't frozen stiff. "God. Aldous. All these years I thought—if I thought about you at all, after—I thought you'd hate me. Whoever 'me' is. I thought if there was a you behind a window somewhere, you'd be clawing at it."

*I CLAWED FOR THE FIRST YEAR,* the frost admitted. *THEN I LEARNED THE WINDOWS ARE ALSO ITS EYES. YOU'RE NOT CLAWING AT A WALL, TWIN. YOU'RE CLAWING AT ITS FACE. IT ENJOYS THAT.*

"So what do you want?" I asked again, and this time I meant it as the only question in the world. "Not the swap. Not revenge. Tell me what you actually want, and I'll spend whatever I have left of tonight getting it."

The frost was quiet long enough that I started to fear the connection had drained away, that Wren had been right about the drain, that I'd talked my brother's remnant into exhaustion. Then, one letter at a time, with the care of someone signing their name to something binding:

*TWO THINGS. EITHER REMEMBER ME—REALLY REMEMBER, BOTH OF US, THE WAY ONLY A TWIN CAN, HOLDING US BOTH IN ONE HEAD WITHOUT PICKING A FAVORITE—OR LET ME GO. OPEN NOTHING. SIGN NOTHING. RENOUNCE NOTHING. JUST... STOP LETTING THE HOUSE BE THE ONLY ONE WHO DECIDES WHAT I AM.*

"Remembered or released," I said. "That's it? No demands? You're owed a whole life, Aldous. You're owed forty years and a funeral with your own name spelled right."

*I'M OWED A BROTHER WHO ISN'T AFRAID OF ME,* the frost wrote. *THAT'S THE DEBT I CARE ABOUT. THE HOUSE CAN KEEP ITS LEDGER.*

And that—that was the moment the fear broke. Not all at once; fear doesn't shatter, it melts, unevenly, in patches. But sitting on the nursery floor with my hand against the coldest thing in the world and hearing, through it, not vengeance but *exhaustion*, not hunger but homesickness—I understood, finally, with total clarity, who the enemy was.

It was never Aldous. It was never even really Wren; Wren was a mask, and masks aren't guilty of the face underneath. The enemy was the house itself, the landlord of the frost, the great patient bookkeeper that had been trading children across four families for sixty years and calling it a bargain. The house wanted us divided—twin against twin, heir against heir, remembered against released—because division was how it fed. Every contract it wrote had a line down the middle. Every game it taught us had sides.

And my brother, twenty years behind the glass, had spent his perfect witness-memory figuring out the one thing the house couldn't survive.

Us. Together. Undivided.

"Aldous," I said. "When I was little—you remember the bus windows. Backwards writing. Teach me one more thing through the glass. Something the house wouldn't think to guard, because it's too small and too stupid and too much ours."

*ASK.*

"When sunrise comes, and every door opens at once—it's going to try to make me run alone. It always makes them run alone. Halloway said so. So teach me the backwards writing. Because if I'm going to cross that hallway, I want to write something on the frost as I go, and I want it to be in *our* language, and I want the house to have to read it backwards, letter by letter, the way it makes everyone else read its contracts."

The frost warmed under my hand. Just a degree. Just enough to feel like fingers pressing back.

*START WITH THE WORD FOR HOME,* Aldous wrote. *WE INVENTED IT ON THE BUS IN THIRD GRADE. YOU'VE ALREADY FORGOTTEN IT. BUT YOU'LL REMEMBER. YOU ALWAYS REMEMBERED BY MORNING.*

And far below us, deep in the bones of the house, something old and patient heard a Crane laughing softly on a nursery floor at three in the morning, teaching itself a dead language with its dead brother, and understood—for the first time in sixty years—that the ledger might not balance.

Upstairs, a clock I had never been able to find began to strike four.
