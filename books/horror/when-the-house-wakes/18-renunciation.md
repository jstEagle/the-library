## 18. Renunciation

The thirteenth door had stopped pretending to be anything but what it was.

It stood at the end of the hallway that hadn't existed an hour ago, taller than the others, wider, its wood pale as birch bark and cold enough that the air around it frosted my breath into little ghosts. Behind it, I knew without opening it, was the nursery. And in the nursery was the thing shaped like me, wearing my patience like a borrowed coat, waiting with the terrible courtesy of the house itself.

I had the quilt spread across the hallway floor. Mother's wedding quilt, the one she'd sewn her bargain into stitch by stitch, because paper burns and ink fades but a quilt gets handed down, gets slept under, gets *kept*. The contract was written in thread so fine I'd needed the porch light's impossible glow to read it, and even then the words seemed to rearrange themselves when I blinked, patient sentences waiting for me to be ready for them.

*The swap may be undone before first light,* the stitches said, *if the named heir does willingly renounce: name, inheritance, and place.*

Willingly. That word had been worked into the fabric twice, as if the seamstress hadn't trusted it the first time.

I knelt on the quilt with my knees aching and my hands shaking, and I thought about how strange it is that the most important thing you'll ever say might be said kneeling on your mother's needlework in a hallway that grew overnight, speaking to a house that eats identities, while something wearing your best friend's face watches from the shadows.

"Okay," I said to no one, to everyone, to the frost creeping down the walls like ivy. "Let's do this."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about renouncing your name: you have to mean it. Not perform it, not recite it like a phone number you're reading off a receipt. The house could tell the difference. The house had been listening to Cranes lie to it for generations, and it had developed, I imagine, a very refined palate.

So I started small.

"My name is Orsen Crane," I said, and the words came out steady, which surprised me. "And I give it up."

The frost on the walls shivered. Down the hall, the thirteenth door did not move, but the darkness behind its seams deepened, the way water darkens when something rises beneath it.

"I give up the name they gave me at birth. I give up the name on the funeral notice. I give up whichever name is true and whichever one is the lie, because I don't know anymore, and maybe I never did." My voice cracked on the last part, and I let it. Let the house hear that too. "Maybe I'm Aldous. Maybe Aldous was me. It doesn't matter. Take the name. Both of them. They were never mine—they were assigned, like seats at a table, like beds in a nursery."

The temperature dropped. My breath hung in front of me like a small white flag.

"I renounce the inheritance." I pressed my palm flat against the quilt, against the stitched contract, against my mother's handwriting in thread. "This house, this land, this debt—I want none of it. I didn't sign anything. I wasn't born yet when they made the bargain. Whatever they promised on my behalf, whatever they traded before I could speak—" I swallowed. "Unmake it. I release the estate from me, and me from the estate."

The lights along the hallway flickered—not the dying flicker of bad wiring but the deliberate flicker of something considering. Somewhere below us, in the walls, in the floors, in the foundation, the house groaned like a ship changing course.

"And the place," I said. "I renounce this place. Frost Hollow. This ground, this plot, this plot of graves dug in advance. I don't belong here. Maybe I never belonged here. Maybe belonging was the thing they sold before I had a say in it."

I stood up. My legs felt like someone else's, which, given the circumstances, was not a comforting sensation.

"So take it," I told the door. "Take all of it. I walk out unclaimed, and whoever's behind that glass—Aldous, my brother, my other self, whatever he is—gets released. That's the deal. That's what your own contract says."

The thirteenth door began to open.

Not fast. Not slow. With the unhurried grace of something that has never once been surprised, never once been hurried, never once lost. Cold poured through the widening gap, and beyond it I saw the nursery—two cribs, one scorched, one pristine—and standing between them, the figure.

It looked like me. It looked like me the way a photograph looks like you after the negative has started to decay—right in every particular, wrong in every proportion. Its face was my face smoothed of everything that had ever happened to it. No grief. No fear. No twenty years of missing a brother who might have been me.

It smiled with my mouth, and behind the smile was nothing but patience, and behind the patience was hunger, and behind the hunger—

"—don't finish the sentence."

The voice came from behind me, and I knew it before I turned, the way you know a song from the first two notes. Wren stood at the mouth of the hallway, and for the first time since I'd come back to Frost Hollow, Wren looked *worried*.

"You don't get to stop me," I said. "You gave me the rules. You walked me through the night. You don't get to—"

"I'm not stopping you." Wren came closer, and the shadows bent around it, reluctant to touch it. "I'm asking you to think. Which is more than I've ever asked anyone in this house. So consider it historic."

"The contract says—"

"The contract says what it says." Wren's face flickered—that horrible half-second where the child-shaped mask slipped and something tall and jointed and old looked out from behind it—but it held, barely, and when the boy's face returned there was something almost like sorrow in it. Almost. "But contracts have readers, Orsen. And readers have interests. Did you really think the landlord of the frost wrote a loophole into its own bargain out of generosity?"

My stomach dropped. "Then why—"

"Because a loophole isn't an escape." Wren gestured at the opening door, at the thing inside wearing my face. "It's a *funnel*. Look at what happens if you succeed. You renounce. You become unclaimed. Unnamed. Unhoused. A person with no name, no inheritance, no place—which means no claim on you, yes, but also no claim *by* you. Nothing anchoring you to the world. And the house keeps whoever remains unclaimed."

"That's Aldous. That's who remains unclaimed—the swap undoes, he comes back, he's claimed again—"

"Is he?" Wren tilted its head, and the gesture was so perfectly, painfully the one my childhood friend used to make when I'd gotten a sum wrong that I nearly wept. "Read it again. *The house will keep whoever remains unclaimed.* Whoever. Not whichever twin. Whoever. If you renounce, Orsen—if you stand there with no name and no place and no inheritance—then for one moment, one beautiful legal moment, *you* are the unclaimed one. And the house has been hungry for a long time."

Behind the thirteenth door, the thing with my face took a step forward. The floorboards didn't creak. They *thanked* it.

"No," I said. "No. He's been behind the glass for thirty years. He's the reserve. He's the—"

"He's *tired*, Orsen. He told you himself. He doesn't want to be swapped back. He wants to be remembered or released. But the house doesn't care what he wants, any more than it cares what you want. The house wants a Crane. It has always wanted a Crane. And if you empty yourself out to save him, you'll both be standing in the doorway at sunrise—one of you named, one of you not—and the house will simply reach past the named one and collect the other."

The cold coming through the door wrapped around my ankles like hands. Gentle hands. Patient hands. The hands of something that had done this many times and had never once been in a hurry.

"Then what do I do?" I hated how small my voice sounded. "If the loophole is a trap and the contract is a funnel, then what's left?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When it spoke again, its voice had changed—lost some of the child's music, gained something older and wearier underneath, like hearing the actor under the character.

"Do you know what I am? Truly?"

"You're the thing they bargained with. You said. You're the landlord wearing a mask."

"I said I was *a* thing they bargained with." Wren's eyes—my childhood friend's eyes, warm brown, familiar as my own hands—held mine. "But ask yourself, Orsen. Why would the landlord of the frost need a mask? Why would something that old and that hungry bother learning to play games with a lonely child on a porch? Landlords collect rent. They don't babysit."

The floor was very cold. The hallway was very long. I suddenly understood that I was standing in the middle of a sentence that had been being written for sixty years, and I had assumed I was the punctuation.

"Because it's not the landlord's mask," I said slowly. "It's yours. You were real. You were my imaginary friend, and imaginary things are real in exactly one way—we invent them, and then they exist, and then they *belong* to us."

Something moved across Wren's face. Not the flicker of the tall jointed thing. Something smaller. Sadder.

"I was invented by a lonely child in this house," Wren said. "And then the house learned the trick. Every generation, a Crane child gets lonely, and the house offers them a friend, and the friend keeps them company, and keeps them *here*, and keeps the line going, and when the child grows up and has children of their own—well. Lonely children are renewable." It smiled, and this time the smile was entirely its own, and it was terrible. "That's the harvest, Orsen. Not the twins. The twins are just the plow. The crop is the *friend*. Someone has to sit on the next porch. Someone has to teach the next child to count doors. Someone has to keep the lights on."

"And if I renounce—"

"If you renounce, the house collects you, and I—" Wren's voice caught, or performed catching, and I realized I would never again be able to tell the difference, and that this was the true horror, not the tall jointed shape, but the fact that twenty years of love had been installed in me like plumbing. "I go free. I've been bound to this property since before your mother was born. Renounce, and my lease ends. The house gets its Crane. I get the door."

The thing in the nursery had reached the threshold now. It stood in the gap of the thirteenth door, wearing my body like a well-fitted glove, and it looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors on bad mornings: polite, exhausted, waiting to be allowed to exist.

And I understood, finally, the shape of the trap. Not the contract. Not the frost. The trap was that every way out had been priced, and the currency was always the same—someone stays, someone goes, someone is claimed, someone is kept—and the house did not care which, because the house had never once in its long cold life run out of Cranes willing to pay for each other.

"Orsen," Wren said softly. "Finish the renunciation. Free yourself the honest way. You'll save him or you won't, but either way, you'll have done the brave thing, and I will remember you kindly, for as long as I last, wherever I end up."

And that was when I noticed it. The one flaw. The one hairline crack in all that patient ice.

Wren wanted me to finish.

In a night full of rules—keep the lights on, answer no knock, never say both names—every rule had been designed to protect me. Every single one. Wren had drilled them into me like a mother teaching a child to cross a street. And now, at the final hour, the only instruction Wren had ever given me that served Wren instead of me was: *stop.*

I looked at the thing with my face. I looked at the friend with its borrowed warmth. I looked down at the quilt, at my mother's stitches, at the words she'd sewn twice because she hadn't trusted them once.

*Willingly.*

The house fed on division. Twin split from twin, heir from home, friend from child. Every clause, every generation, every grave dug in advance—it all ran on the same engine: *you or them.*

And I had spent the whole night playing that game, choosing between doors, counting my way through a house built to make me choose.

My name was the one thing I still owned. Not the name they gave me. Not the name on the notice. The one I was about to make, right here, in the hallway, out loud, with no one's permission.

"Wren," I said, "how does the contract define 'the named heir'?"

Wren went very still. Stillness, on it, looked wrong—a child's body holding a statue's discipline. "Why?"

"Because my mother tore a page out of her ledger. Because the frost wrote three names and scraped one away. Because nobody in this house has ever actually known which twin was named." I picked up the quilt, and the stitches caught the porch light, and I saw what I should have seen hours ago: the thread spelling *the named heir* ran not to one crib in the pattern but *between* them—to the space where both had lain, to the room where both had been counted. "And I think the house has been guessing for thirty years too."

The frost on the walls began, very slowly, to write.

And for the first time all night, Wren looked afraid.
