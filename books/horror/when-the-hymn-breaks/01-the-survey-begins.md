## 1. The Survey Begins

The bus to Hollowmere runs twice a day, and I caught the second one, which is the kind of decision that tells you everything about how a person feels about their work. The first bus is for optimists. The second is for people who have learned that arriving early to an empty property just means standing in an empty property longer.

I'm Ines Thornbury, and for eleven years I've appraised buildings for estates, banks, and the occasional eccentric trust. My reports are famous among exactly four people for being exhaustive to the point of pathology. I measure doorways. I photograph light switches. I once logged a hairline crack in a Cornish conservatory so precisely that a structural engineer later used my notes in a paper on subsidence, and sent me a thank-you card with a cartoon of a sinking house on it. That's the level of glamour we're operating at.

Hollowmere was supposed to be simple. A manor house, Wrenfield, left vacant by a death in the family, no living heirs interested in keeping it. Standard rural decay, standard valuation, standard recommendation of either restoration or demolition depending on what the numbers said. Three days' work. Four if the weather turned.

The bus driver didn't say anything when I told him where I was going, but he looked at me in his mirror a beat too long, the way people look at someone who has just admitted to something mildly embarrassing, like enjoying marmite or voting for a losing party.

"Surveyor," I said, holding up my case as evidence.

"Right," he said, and drove on.

* * *

The village announced itself the way shy animals do: not at all, until suddenly it was there. One moment hedgerow, the next a lane of grey stone cottages leaning toward each other like old men sharing a secret. A shop with a faded green fascia. A church tower rising above the roofs, square and blunt, its belfry windows dark. A war memorial shaped like an obelisk, polished oddly bright against all that dullness.

I got off at the stop—which was a bench—and stood a moment with my case while the bus shrank down the lane and the quiet closed behind it. Not silence. Villages are never silent; there's always a dog, a tractor, a television through a window. This was quieter than that. Quieter than a place has any right to be on a Tuesday afternoon in October.

The shopkeeper was arranging tins when I went in for water and a sandwich. Middle-aged, cardigan the colour of weak tea, hair pinned with the efficiency of someone who had decided decades ago that appearance was a solved problem.

"Morning," she said. "Visiting?"

"Working. Wrenfield House. I'm surveying it."

Her hands paused over the tins. Just a half-second. Then they resumed, and she smiled, and the smile was fine, absolutely fine, and I noted—because noting things is what I do—that her eyes had gone somewhere else while her face stayed put.

"Wrenfield," she repeated. "That's been empty a long while."

"So I understand."

"That'll be three-forty." She bagged my sandwich and water and added, unprompted, "Mind the floors on the east side. They creak."

"I'll mind them," I said, and she laughed, a small polite laugh, and I left, and only halfway up the lane did it occur to me that I hadn't told her which side of the house I'd be surveying first. Or at all. Or that east meant anything to me yet.

Probably she'd seen the surveyors before. Probably everyone knew the east floors creaked. Probably.

I filed it under *probably*, which is where I file most things, and walked on.

* * *

Wrenfield House sat at the end of a lime avenue whose trees had given up on symmetry long ago. Georgian bones, Victorian additions, slate roof patched in at least three different eras of slate. From the outside: handsome, tired, worth saving if anyone cared and not worth saving if nobody did. I've stood in front of two hundred houses like it. You develop a feel. Most houses feel like coats left on hooks—shaped by whoever wore them, holding nothing now.

Wrenfield felt occupied.

Not haunted—I want to be clear that I don't use that word, professionally or otherwise. Occupied. The way a room feels different when someone is in it even if you can't see them, even if you know perfectly well you're alone. The windows had that quality of being watched from rather than looked at. It's a stupid sensation. I catalogued it as fatigue from the journey and let myself in with the key the solicitors had posted me.

The hall was oak-panelled and cold in the specific way of houses that have been shut up rather than abandoned. Shut-up houses hold their breath. Abandoned ones exhale. This one held its breath, and I had the distinct impression that it would go on holding it for exactly as long as I was inside, and resume breathing when I left.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to empty houses is a habit that keeps you sane, or at least keeps you company. "Ground floor first. Then up. Then outbuildings. Then we're done with each other."

I set up at the hall table: laptop, clipboard, tape, laser measure, camera charged to full. I photograph everything. Every room from every corner, every ceiling, every skirting board where damp might hide. Eleven years of this. My archive contains forty thousand photographs of buildings, and I could tell you the story of any one of them from the images alone. Buildings confess to cameras. People edit; plaster doesn't.

The drawing room came first. South-facing, tall windows, shutters half-closed so the light fell in bars across a floor of wide boards gone silver with age. Dust lay evenly everywhere except along one path from the door to the window, where the boards were merely dull rather than furred. Something had walked there recently enough to keep the dust thin. Foxes get into empty houses all the time. So do badgers, cats, teenagers, and the occasional vagrant. I photographed the path, measured its width—forty centimetres, give or take, bipedal—and wrote *possible animal ingress, recommend check* in the anomalies column, and did not write *bipedal*, because *bipedal* invites questions, and questions invite site visits, and site visits cost my clients money.

The fireplace was marble, cracked. The mirror above it was foxed to blindness. In the blind grey of the glass my reflection was a smudge, which is normal, mirrors do that, and I photographed it anyway because I photograph everything, and then stood looking at the photograph afterward for slightly too long, because in it the smudge seemed to be standing very straight, head tilted, the way a person stands when they're listening.

Fatigue, I wrote. Journey fatigue.

* * *

By late afternoon I'd done the ground floor: drawing room, dining room, study, kitchen, scullery, pantry, back stairs. Two hundred and six photographs. The kitchen had a range you could park a car in and a table scarred by a century of knives. On the pantry shelf, three jars of preserves with handwritten labels in copperplate: *damson 1971*, *damson 1972*, *greengage, uncertain*. Someone had left in a hurry or stayed longer than the paperwork suggested. I logged both possibilities.

The house made sounds. Old houses all make sounds—timber contracting, pipes ticking, wind finding gaps—but Wrenfield's sounds had a rhythm to them that I kept almost catching. A settling groan upstairs, directly overhead, whenever I finished a room below. A soft tick from the direction of the corridor each time I checked my watch. Coincidence stacks up fast in an empty building; your brain is a pattern machine starved for input, and it will serve you patterns made of nothing. I know this. I have read the studies. I have recommended, more than once, that clients be aware of the psychological effects of prolonged solitude in large properties.

Knowing a thing has never once stopped me feeling it.

At five I climbed the main staircase, which was oak and magnificently solid, and started on the principal bedroom. More dust, more silvered boards, a wardrobe big enough to hang a body in—not that I wrote that—a bedframe stripped of linen, a washstand with its jug intact, which pleased me obscurely. I worked the room corner to corner, calling measurements under my breath, and the house received them the way a congregation receives a lesson: silently, attentively, without comment.

It was while I was photographing the ceiling rose that I noticed the sound had stopped.

All of it. The settling, the ticking, the wind at the shutters. The house had gone absolutely still around me, the way a classroom goes still when the teacher stops mid-sentence, and I stood there with my camera raised and felt the silence press against my ears like water.

Then, from somewhere below—the hall, maybe, or the cellar hatch—a single soft sound. Not a footstep. Closer to the noise a book makes when it closes.

"Hello?" I called, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved credit for. "Solicitors sent me. Surveyor. Ines Thornbury."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. But the silence after my voice had a quality I can only describe as satisfied, the way silence does after a name has been said aloud in an empty room, and I stood on the landing of Wrenfield House with my heart going like a trapped bird and told myself, firmly, in the tone I use for junior colleagues who cry at site meetings, that I was tired, that old houses breathe, that I had two hundred and six photographs and a job to finish.

I went back to work. That's the whole secret of my profession, if it has one: you go back to work. Measure the next wall. Photograph the next sill. The numbers don't care what you felt; the numbers are true, or near enough, and everything else is weather.

I finished the principal bedroom. I did the second bedroom, and the box room, and the linen cupboard with its shelves still smelling faintly of lavender and mothballs. At the end of the east passage there was a door I hadn't expected—it wasn't on the floor plan the solicitors sent, a small omission, plans drift from reality the way maps do—and beyond it a narrow room with a single window facing the church tower.

I stood in that little room longer than I needed to. Through the window, across the roofs and gardens, the tower of St. Aldric's stood black against the last of the light, and its belfry openings were empty and dark, and I thought, idly, unprofessionally, that it was strange a village should leave its tower hollow. Even decommissioned bells usually leave something. A frame. A beam. Some scar of the thing that used to live there.

This tower looked swept. Emptied on purpose. Like a mouth after the tooth's gone.

I photographed the view, because I photograph everything, and in the photograph the tower's emptiness had a weight to it that the naked eye had missed—four black rectangles where the bell-openings were, and something about their arrangement, their spacing, nagged at the part of my brain that measures things for a living. Proportion. The openings were spaced like—what? Notes? Windows of a particular hymn?

Ridiculous. Openings are openings. I marked the mystery room on my plan as *Room E, unrecorded on title plan, verify with solicitors*, packed up for the day, and went downstairs to make tea on a camping stove in a kitchen built for a household of twelve.

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it does in October, sliding under the doors before you've noticed the afternoon is gone. I ate my sandwich at the scarred kitchen table and entered the day's data, and outside the window the light drained from the sky in stages, gold to pewter to a blue so deep it was nearly black, and the village below the hill went from dim to dotted to dark.

At some point—I couldn't tell you when, which bothers me more than I let on—the house changed again. Not a sound this time. An attention. The difference between an empty coat and a coat with someone watching you put it on. I sat with my laptop's glow on my hands and felt Wrenfield House notice me the way you feel a stranger's gaze between your shoulder blades on an empty platform: no evidence, total certainty.

"Three days," I told it quietly. "Then I write my report and we never see each other again."

The house said nothing, in a way that felt less like agreement than like patience.

I am a meticulous woman. Meticulous people survive strange places by writing everything down, so before bed I opened my notebook—the real one, paper, the one that goes everywhere with me—and dated the page, and wrote:

*Day 1. Wrenfield House. Structure sound overall; east floors creak (per local). Room E absent from title plan. Pantry stores suggest departure later than records state. Dust path, drawing room, bipedal width. House acoustics anomalous—sounds patterned, responsive. Atmosphere: attentive. Recommend verification of plan discrepancies with solicitor.*

I looked at the word *attentive* for a while. It's not a surveying term. There's no column for it. But there was no other word, and I have promised myself since my first year in this trade that the notebook tells the truth even when the report can't afford to.

Underneath, without deciding to, my pen added one more line:

*It knows I'm here.*

I crossed it out. Firmly. Twice.

Then I went to bed in the principal bedroom with the door wedged open and the camping lamp burning, and lay listening to the house hold its breath around me, and somewhere in the drowse between waking and sleep I heard—clearly, distinctly, from the direction of the village—the sound of a bell.

One toll. Deep, bronze, patient.

Which was impossible, because I had seen the tower myself, and the tower was empty, and tomorrow I would walk down to St. Aldric's in daylight and confirm it, because I am a meticulous woman, and meticulous women check.

The bell rang once more, softer, almost fond. Like a teacher marking a register.

Like a name being taken.
