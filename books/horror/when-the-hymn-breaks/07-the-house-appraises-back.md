## 7. The House Appraises Back

I want to be precise about what happened on the fourth day, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept, and I could feel it being tested.

The morning began normally, which by then already meant: abnormally quiet. Wrenfield House has a particular quality of silence—not the absence of sound but the presence of attention. A held breath. Most empty buildings echo; this one absorbed. My footsteps landed in the hallway like questions no one intended to answer.

I made coffee on a camp stove because the plumbing had been condemned decades ago, and I sat at the kitchen table with my floor plan of the ground floor spread out before me, and I did what I do every morning of every survey: I reviewed yesterday's work.

That's when I found the first room.

It was drawn in my own hand—my drafting pen, my lettering style, even my habit of putting a small tick mark at wall junctions when I've verified them. Room 14, off the east corridor. Dimensions noted. Door swing indicated. And in the margin, in my handwriting, a note I had absolutely never written:

*Occupied.*

I stared at it for a long time. Long enough for the coffee to go cold, which is my usual metric for existential crises. Then I got up and walked to the east corridor and counted doors.

There were eleven rooms off that corridor when I surveyed it on day one. I have the photographs. I have the measurements. Eleven doors, three of them painted shut, one bricked over behind lath and plaster.

Room 14 was the twelfth door. It stood slightly ajar, and from the gap came the smell of woodsmoke and lavender, which are not smells an unheated derelict manor produces in October.

I should tell you what I did not do. I did not run. I want that on the record, even if the record is just me, talking to myself in a haunted house, which increasingly felt like the arrangement. I stood in that corridor with my clipboard held against my chest like a shield with very poor stopping power, and I said, out loud, in my most professional voice:

"If you're going to do something, I'd appreciate it if you'd let me document it."

The door swung wider.

Not slammed, not creaked—it *swung*, smoothly, the way a door moves when someone inside has heard you coming and opened it in welcome. The hinges didn't make a sound. Hinges always make a sound. That silence frightened me more than any noise could have.

Inside was a bedroom. Furnished. Lived-in. A brass bed with a quilted coverlet faded to the color of weak tea. A washstand with a jug and basin. A window with clean glass—the rest of the house's windows were furred with grime—and through it, sunlight fell across the floorboards in a warm rectangle, though outside the sky was the color of dishwater.

On the nightstand sat a book, face-down, holding its place. I didn't touch it. Some instinct older than my professionalism told me that touching things in that room would constitute an answer to a question I hadn't finished asking.

But the coat—I couldn't avoid the coat. It hung on a peg by the door, a child's coat, wool, dark blue, sized for someone maybe seven or eight years old. It had been worn. The elbows were shiny, the cuffs frayed soft. One pocket sagged with the weight of whatever children carry: stones, string, a marble, a secret.

And it was warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. The warmth of a coat recently taken off by a small person who would be back for it.

I photographed it. Of course I photographed it. My hands were steady, which surprised me; apparently terror and competence can share a body without introduction. In the viewfinder the coat hung exactly as it hung in life. When I checked the image later, the timestamp read 11:47 a.m.—and then, when I checked again twenty minutes later, 11:47 a.m. on a date nine years earlier.

I stopped checking timestamps after that.

* * *

Here is the thing about surveying: it is supposed to be a monologue. I walk into a building, I ask it questions with my tape measure and my laser level, and it answers in the only language buildings have—settlement cracks, damp courses, joist spans. The building doesn't get to talk back. That's the whole covenant. That's why I chose this profession: buildings don't remember you, don't judge you, don't leave. They just stand there being measurable.

Wrenfield House had broken the covenant. Wrenfield House was answering questions I hadn't asked, in a voice I hadn't agreed to hear, and worse—it was answering *in my own handwriting*.

By afternoon there were four new rooms on my floor plan. Four. Each drawn in my lettering, each annotated in my shorthand, each verifiably real the moment I walked to find it. A linen closet that smelled of cedar where the plan showed solid wall. A narrow stair down to a cellar I hadn't known existed, its steps worn concave by generations of feet. A study with a cold fireplace and a chair pulled out at an angle, as if someone had risen from it moments before I entered, every single time I entered.

And a nursery.

I stood in the doorway of the nursery for a long while before I could make myself cross the threshold. Rocking horse. Cot with the rail down. Wooden blocks arranged on the floor in a pattern that was almost a word—or was a word, if you knew how to read it, and I suspected the house knew that I might, eventually, learn.

The blocks spelled nothing I recognized. But they were arranged with intent. Someone had been mid-game when the game stopped, and the house had preserved the moment the way amber preserves an insect: perfectly, and forever, and with something hungry in the clarity.

I logged all of it. Room dimensions, contents, condition. My pen moved across the page and I noticed my notes had shifted register somewhere along the way—from survey language (*"Nursery, NE corner, 3.2m × 2.8m, plaster sound"*) to something else entirely (*"She liked the blocks best. She always put the red one last."*)

I don't know who she is. I wrote it anyway. The sentence arrived in my head fully formed, the way sentences do when you're tired, except I wasn't tired, I was wide awake and cold, and the sentence was not mine.

I crossed it out. Then, because I am who I am, I drew a box around the crossing-out and annotated it: *"Contaminated entry. Do not rely."*

You have to laugh, or you have to leave, and I couldn't leave—the roads out of Hollowmere were still, as far as I knew, ordinary roads, and my car was still, as far as I knew, where I'd parked it. So I laughed. It came out wrong. The house took the sound and gave back a faint settling groan from somewhere above me, which might have been timber and might have been amusement.

* * *

Toward dusk I did the thing I probably shouldn't have done. I sat down at the kitchen table, laid out the floor plan with its impossible additions, and spoke to the house directly.

"Let's establish terms," I said. "I'm here to appraise you. That's all. I write reports. People read them, or they don't, and then buildings get sold or demolished or left alone, and none of it is personal. If you're trying to tell me something, I'm listening. But I need you to stop writing in my notebook, because that's not communication, that's forgery."

Silence. The specific, loaded silence of a room where someone is deciding whether you're worth responding to.

Then, from the direction of the east corridor, the twelfth door closed. Gently. Deliberately. And when I went to look—because of course I went to look, curiosity is the flaw that will be engraved on my headstone—the room behind it was empty. Stripped. Floorboards bare, wallpaper hanging in ribbons, the pegs on the wall naked. Just another dead room in a dying house.

Except my floor plan now showed five new rooms.

The fifth was one I hadn't seen. Drawn in my hand, dimensioned in my hand, annotated in my hand: *"Small room under the west stair. Door concealed. Ask Ellery."*

Ask Ellery. The vicar who watched me too long at St. Aldric's, whose gaze had the weight of a hand on my shoulder. The house knew his name. The house was giving me instructions.

I stood in the kitchen holding the plan, and I understood—with the calm, terrible clarity that comes when a situation finally admits what it is—that the appraisal had changed direction. I wasn't assessing the property anymore. The property was assessing me. Every room it revealed was a question: *What will you do with this? Will you run? Will you write it down? Will you come closer?*

And I realized, folding the plan with hands that only shook a little, that I had answered already. Three days running, I had answered. I documented everything. I stayed. I came closer.

Whatever Wrenfield House had been waiting a century to find, I was beginning to suspect it hadn't summoned a surveyor at all.

It had requested a reader.

Outside, far off across the fields, the church tower stood black against the last of the light—empty, I reminded myself, empty, the bell melted down in 1911, there's nothing up there—and as I watched, the first thin note of dusk reached me across the grass, and beneath it, so faint I could have imagined it, the house exhaled around me like something settling in to listen.
