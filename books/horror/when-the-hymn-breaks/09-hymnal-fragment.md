## 9. Hymnal Fragment

The morning after the corridor grew a foot and a half, I did what any reasonable professional would do: I made coffee, checked my measurements a third time, got the same impossible numbers, and then deliberately did not think about them while I ate toast standing at the kitchen counter like a person with nothing wrong in their life.

Denial is an underrated surveying tool. You can't measure anything if you've already run screaming into the lane.

Wrenfield House had been quiet overnight—or rather, it had been *attentive* overnight, which is worse. Quiet is a house that doesn't care about you. Attentive is a house that's waiting to see what you'll do next. The floorboards under my boots had a listening quality to them, the way a dog goes still when you say its name. I'd started narrating my work aloud, partly for the dictaphone, partly because I'd noticed the house seemed calmer when I talked to it. Like it just wanted to be included.

"Ground floor, morning of day four," I said, into the recorder. "Proceeding to the service wing. Chimney stack and flue inspection, per clause fourteen of the appraisal brief."

Clause fourteen. Even the boring parts of my job were starting to feel like stage directions written by someone with an agenda.

The service wing was the part of Wrenfield House nobody loved. The grand rooms had been photographed for estate agents and county histories; the scullery, the stillroom, the boot room had been left to accumulate their own weather. Wallpaper hung in long tongues. A cast-iron range sat in the kitchen like a dead beetle, its enamel crazed with age. And against the kitchen's north wall rose the chimney breast—brick, soot-blackened, with a hearth opening wide enough that I could have stood inside it if I'd wanted to, which I did not, professionally speaking, want to do.

But the flue was on the checklist. And there was something about the hearth. I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last thing I had left that week: there was a *draft* coming out of the chimney. Downward. Chimneys pull air up; that is their entire personality. This one exhaled. Cool air, moving down the flue and out into the kitchen, carrying a smell I couldn't place at first—old smoke, yes, but under it something sweeter. Paper. Burnt paper that had given up halfway through burning.

I knelt on the hearthstone with my torch and looked up.

The flue narrowed above me into darkness, and wedged in the throat of it, maybe eight feet up, was a shape. Angular. Pale-edged. I got the extension pole from my kit—the one I used for poking loft hatches and, once memorably, a wasps' nest, never again—and reached up and hooked it.

It came down in a soft avalanche of soot and settled on the hearthstone in front of me.

A book. Or what had been a book. It was the size of a hymnal, because—and I'm getting ahead of myself—it *was* a hymnal. Charred black along one entire side, swollen with damp, its cover gone to a crust like burnt toast. Someone had tried to destroy it in a fire and either lost their nerve or lost their fire. Half-burned books are more unsettling than whole ones. A whole book has an author. A half-burned book has a *reason*.

I put on nitrile gloves—I document everything, including my own fingerprints' absence from evidence—and opened it on the kitchen table, which I had wiped down first, because even in extremis I am not a barbarian.

The inside was worse than the outside. Pages fused into leathery slabs. Where I could make out text at all, it was the standard fare: numbers, first lines, the names of tunes in small italics. *St. Aldric.* Of course. *Martyrdom.* *Eventide.* A parish hymnal, late Victorian by the typeface, the kind that would have lived in a pew rack or on the vicarage shelf. Someone had carried it to a fire, held it in the flames long enough to char it through, and then—what? Thrown it in the river? Buried it? No. Wedged it up a chimney, as far as it would go, where no one would look and where, apparently, no one had looked for a very long time.

Except the house had wanted me to find it. I know how that sounds. I'd been in Hollowmere four days; I was still telling myself things like *coincidence* and *settling foundations*. But the downward draft had started the moment I'd entered the service wing, and I had not imagined the way the flue seemed to lean its contents toward my torch beam, patient as a shopkeeper arranging stock.

I worked through the fused pages with a palette knife and a hairdryer on low heat, which is exactly as glamorous as it sounds. Most of them surrendered nothing but a smell like a bonfire's ghost. Page after page, carbon and glue. Then, near the back—a single leaf that came free almost willingly, as if it had been waiting to be asked.

One page survived. Not intact—its edges were lacework, browned and brittle—but legible, top to bottom, in a way that felt less like luck and more like policy.

It was a hymn. Numbered 114 in a hand that wasn't printed. The title line had been burned away entirely, which struck me even then as tidy. Below it, verses. Four of them.

I read them silently first. That matters. Remember that I read them silently first.

The first verse was ordinary enough—pastoral imagery, some language about bells and water that I skimmed with the professional detachment of someone who had spent three days being gaslit by architecture. The second verse turned on a hinge. It stopped describing and started addressing. *You who kept the count, you who hold the roll*—that sort of thing. Hymns do address their singers sometimes; it's a genre convention. But this one addressed them the way a creditor does.

The third verse I read twice without meaning to. It was about names. About giving names back, or taking them—it was genuinely ambiguous, the syntax doing something slippery, the pronouns refusing to settle. *What we lent we lend forever; what we keep we never knew.* I wrote that line down in my notebook before I'd decided to, my hand moving with a fluency that made me look at the pen as though it might be someone else's.

And then the fourth verse.

The fourth verse ended mid-line.

Not faded. Not torn. Burned—no, wait. I looked closer. Torn? No. It simply *stopped*, the ink ending cleanly at the edge of a burn scar that ran diagonally across the lower third of the page, as though the fire had reached for the final lines and been called off. Or as though the final lines had never been written at all, and the burn was decoration. A period shaped like damage.

The last full words were: *and the bell shall call us home to—*

Home to what. Home to *where*. Home to *whom*.

I sat in that dead kitchen with a dead range and a live draft and a hymn with a hole in it, and I felt the hole. That's the only way I can describe it. A hymn that ends mid-line is a held breath. My eyes went to the end of that sentence over and over, the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth, and each time I arrived at the break I felt a small muscular urge—not quite mine—to supply the rest. To finish it. *Home to rest,* my brain offered. *Home to stay. Home to the water. Home to the—*

I closed the page.

"Right," I said aloud, to the kitchen, to the house, to whoever was keeping the minutes. "That's enough of that."

I photographed the page—front, back, raking light, macro on the burn scar, forty-one frames, because documentation is my religion and I was about to need all the faith I could get. I logged it: *Item 9-1, bound volume, hymnal, fire-damaged, recovered from flue, service wing kitchen. One leaf extant. Text incomplete.* I noted the downward draft. I noted the time. I did not note that my hands were unsteady, because the report goes to a client, and clients ask questions.

Then I made my mistake. I'd like to tell you the mistake was dramatic—that I heard voices, that the page whispered, that something made me. None of that. The mistake was mine alone, and it was the oldest mistake in the reader's repertoire: I read it aloud.

Here's why. I'd been transcribing the legible portions into my notebook, and transcription by eye is slow and error-prone, and every surveyor knows you catch errors by reading back aloud. It's procedure. It's *my* procedure. So I stood in that cold kitchen with my notebook flat on the table and I read the surviving verse in a clear, level, professional voice, the voice I use for boundary disputes and dilapidation schedules:

"*You who kept the count, you who hold the roll,*
*You who gave the bronze a tongue and took the toll—*"

The room grew colder.

I don't mean draughty. I don't mean the door was open. I mean the temperature fell between one line and the next, a drop I felt on my cheeks and in my fillings, the way you feel a cellar before you see it. My breath, which had not been visible in October, bloomed white in front of my mouth. On the second line, frost feathered across the kitchen window in a slow fan, growing outward from a point at its center like something pressing its palm against the glass from outside.

I should have stopped. Any sensible person stops. But here is the thing I understood only later, standing there with frost on the window and my own voice hanging in air gone the temperature of a locked church: the hymn didn't want to be sung. Singing is a commitment; singing joins you to the melody, gives the song your breath to work with. Reading is different. Reading is just *witnessing*. And the hymn, I realized, with the particular clarity of a person making a terrible discovery in real time, was perfectly happy to be witnessed. Witnessing was enough to let it know I'd seen the shape of it. That I now knew it was unfinished.

Every unfinished thing wants completing. That's not supernatural. That's grammar.

"*What we lent we lend forever; what we keep we never knew,*" I read, and my voice came out wrong on *forever*—stretched, doubled, as if a second voice a half-second behind mine were learning the words off me. The frost on the window had formed, if you squinted, into patterns. Feathers. Or fingers. Or the veins of a leaf. I chose feathers and kept reading, because stopping mid-verse suddenly felt more dangerous than finishing, the way you don't stop telling a story halfway down a dark staircase.

"*And the bell shall call us home to—*"

I stopped. There was nothing to read. The line ended, the burn began, and the silence where the rest should have been was the loudest thing in the house.

The cold held for three seconds. Five. Long enough for me to notice that the soot in the hearth had stirred, fine grains shivering toward the flue's mouth as though the chimney were inhaling. Long enough for me to understand, with total certainty and zero evidence, that somewhere above me—in the flue, in the tower two miles away at St. Aldric's, in whatever register a melted bell keeps—something had heard the fragment and recognized it. Not the words. The *gap*. The place where its last line lived.

Then the temperature climbed back, gradually, grudgingly, like a landlord returning a deposit. The frost on the window thinned to steam and ran. The draft reversed—upward now, normal, obedient physics—and the kitchen smelled only of soot and old paper again.

I put the leaf in an archival sleeve. I put the sleeve in my document case. I put the case across the room from my bed, and then, an hour later, moved it into the car, and then, at midnight, brought it back inside, because leaving it in the car felt like abandoning a witness, and I had already promised too many things to documents.

Here is what I wrote in my notebook that night, sitting up with all the lights on, in handwriting I checked twice against my own habits:

*The song has a shape. The shape is incomplete. Incomplete shapes exert pressure. Do not complete it. Do not hum it. Do not read it aloud again. If you catch yourself humming it, write down the time.*

I underlined the last instruction twice. It seemed paranoid. It seemed like the kind of thing a woman writes in a notebook when she's been alone in a big house too long.

I woke at dawn with the taste of cold metal in my mouth and a tune in my head I did not recognize and could not stop, four notes rising, two falling, patient as water finding a level. I did not remember hearing it anywhere in the house.

I wrote down the time.

* * *

The next evening I drove into the village to buy bread I didn't need, mostly to prove to myself that roads still worked and other people still existed. Mrs. Halloway's shop smelled of wax and strong tea, and she chatted at me about her hip while she wrapped my loaf, and none of it was remarkable, and I clung to the ordinariness of it like a rail in rough water.

It was on the walk back, past the churchyard wall, that I saw Mr. Ellery standing at the lychgate in the last of the light, watching the empty tower with an expression I recognized from funerals: the face of a man counting something only he can see.

He saw me looking. He crossed the lane faster than a man his age should move, and he said, without preamble, "You found the book."

Not a question. The wind moved in the yews. Somewhere behind his eyes a decision was visibly being made, and losing.

"There's a page," he said. "One page always survives. It survived the first fire, in 1911. It survived the second." He looked at my coat pocket, where the document case wasn't, and then very carefully at the middle distance instead. "Whatever you do, Miss Thornbury—don't learn the tune."

"I already—" I started, and stopped, because the truth was ridiculous: *I woke up humming it.*

Ellery's face did something complicated. Pity, mostly. He patted my arm once, a dry papery touch, and said, "Then you'd best come to the vestry tomorrow. Before it teaches you the rest."

Behind him, in the towerless churchyard, at an hour still legally afternoon, I heard—for the first time with my ears and not just my sleeping head—one clear note of a bell that had been melted down a hundred years ago.

Four notes rising. Two falling.

Patient as water.
