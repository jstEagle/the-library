## 17. Tamsin Sings

I want to be clear about something before I tell you what happened that night: I did not teach Tamsin Lowe the second verse.

I want to be clear about it because for a long time afterward I wasn't sure. That's the thing about Hollowmere — it doesn't just take your memories when you sing. It takes the ones you need to trust yourself. By Thursday morning I had a notebook full of my own handwriting telling me one story and a hollow feeling in my chest telling me another, and I had to decide which one was the forgery.

But we'll get there. Let me start with the tea.

* * *

Tamsin came to Wrenfield House at half past six in the evening, carrying a thermos and a look I recognized from mirrors. It's the look of someone who has decided to do something unwise and is hoping you'll talk them out of it so they don't have to take responsibility for talking themselves out of it.

"I brought tea," she said, holding up the thermos like evidence at a trial. "The good kind. Not the stuff from Mrs. Halloway's shop."

"Mrs. Halloway's stuff is fine."

"Mrs. Halloway's stuff tastes like it was brewed in 1987 and has been sitting on that shelf ever since, waiting." She let herself in past me without being invited, which by then was our arrangement. "Also I need to ask you something and I need you not to do the thing where you answer like a surveyor."

"I have no other mode."

"You do. I've seen it. It comes out when you're scared." She set the thermos on the kitchen counter — the one room in Wrenfield House that still behaved like a kitchen and not like an audience — and poured two cups without asking if I wanted one. "The verse. The second one. You've got it written down."

It wasn't a question, which was its own kind of warning sign. I'd shown her the hymnal fragment two days earlier, the burned page from the chimney flue with its mid-line amputation, and I'd watched her read it the way you'd watch someone lean over a well. I should have taken it back. I'm writing this down now as fact, not excuse: I should have taken it back.

"It's transcribed," I said. "Not learned. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. One is data. The other is—" I stopped, because the honest word was *contagion*, and I wasn't ready to say it out loud yet. "One is safe."

She looked at me over her cup. Twenty-three years old, chapped hands, a grandmother-shaped hole in her that she'd been carrying since she was old enough to notice the shape of it. Edith Lowe, vanished 1974, last seen walking toward St. Aldric's at dusk with her coat buttoned wrong. The village remembered her the way it remembered everything: briefly, conveniently, and only when useful.

"My gran used to hum," Tamsin said. "My mum told me. Before she — before she stopped talking about her altogether, which was weird, everyone said it was weird, how fast Mum just... closed that door." She turned the cup in her hands. "I used to think Mum was cold about it. Now I think maybe Mum heard something once and paid for it without knowing what the bill was."

"Tamsin—"

"I'm not going to sing it." She said it fast, the way people say things they've already decided against. "I just want to hear it. Read it out loud, maybe. Just the words. If Gran's voice is anywhere in this village, it's in that song, isn't it? That's what the whole thing is. It's made of voices. People who sang along and got... kept."

I want to record, for the archive of my own self-deception, that I believed her. That's the part that still makes me put the pen down sometimes. She said *I'm not going to sing it* and I believed her because I wanted to believe her, because she was the only person in Hollowmere who could stand next to me at dusk and hear the bell too, and loneliness will make you credulous faster than any bell can.

"Reading aloud is not neutral," I said. "When I read the fragment, the room dropped four degrees. I measured it."

"You measure everything."

"Yes, and it's saved my life twice this week, so let me finish. The song doesn't care about intent. It cares about participation. Reading is participating. Humming is more. Singing is—" I searched for the word Ellery had used, standing in his vestry with his collar slightly crooked and his eyes doing that thing where he looked at me like a man watching weather come in. "*Singing is signing.*"

"Signing what?"

"A contract you can't read until after."

She was quiet for a moment. Outside, the light was going the color of old brass, which in Hollowmere meant roughly forty minutes until dusk and the bell. Forty minutes of honest daylight left, and I remember thinking I should use them to check the east corridor again, because the numbers had moved again that afternoon and I hadn't logged it yet, and logging was — I know how this sounds — logging was my rosary. My proof that the world could be pinned down if you were patient enough with it.

"Show me," she said.

"No."

"Ines."

"No. And I'd like the record to show I said no clearly and with conviction."

"The record can also show that you showed me anyway." She wasn't angry. That was the worst part. She was calm, the way water is calm right before it finds the crack in the foundation. "Because here's what I think. I think you're going to keep saying no, and I'm going to keep asking, and eventually one night you'll be tired, or I'll be tired, or the bell will ring and I'll already know the tune because I've been hearing it since I was six and thought it was the wind in the chimney. And I'd rather it happened with you in the room than without."

I didn't show her.

I want that on the record too, even though the record and I both know how the night actually went.

I told her no. I walked her to the door. I gave her the surviving hymnal page to hold — not to keep, just to hold, because I thought, God help me, that letting her touch it would satisfy whatever was pulling at her, the way you let a child hold the matches so they stop wanting to strike them. She held it in both hands like something warm. Her eyes went soft and far away. And she said, very quietly, almost to herself:

"Oh. There you are, Gran."

And then she thanked me, and left, and took the page with her.

I noticed it missing at seven-forty. I noticed the door of Wrenfield House standing open at seven-fifty-two, which it had not been when I checked the locks at seven-fifteen, because the house and I had an understanding about locks. And I stood in that doorway looking down the lane toward the churchyard with the dusk coming down like a lid, and I understood that I had not lost the page.

I had handed it over.

* * *

I found her in the churchyard, of course. Where else. The empty tower stood up against the last grey light like a tooth pulled from the sky, and Tamsin sat on the low wall beneath it with the burned page open on her knees, and she was humming.

Not singing. Humming. As if the distinction would save her. As if the bell graded on a curve.

The sound was already ringing — early, ahead of dusk, which should have frightened me more than it did; I filed it under *anomaly, investigate later*, which is what I did with everything back then that I couldn't afford to feel — and her humming slid into it the way a hand slides into a glove. The tune found her pitch and adjusted around it. Welcomed it. And I stood ten feet away doing the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do all week: three choirboys in 1911. Edith Lowe in 1974. A ledger in the vestry with names and dates and the annotation *verse owed* running back a century like a pulse.

And now Tamsin Lowe, humming the second verse into the dark, giving it her voice the way you'd give blood.

"Tamsin." My voice came out flat and professional, which was the only voice I had left. "Stop."

She didn't stop. But she turned her head, and her face in the dying light was — I've tried to describe this to myself a dozen times and I keep landing on the same inadequate word — *happy*. Not ecstatic. Not trance-struck. Just happy, the plain domestic happiness of someone listening to a familiar voice in the next room. The happiness of a person who has spent twenty years missing someone and has suddenly, impossibly, heard them clearing their throat down the hall.

"She's in it," Tamsin said between phrases. Her humming didn't falter. That was the detail that made my skin try to leave my body: she could talk and hum at once, two tracks, as if the verse had moved into her mouth and was paying rent. "Ines, she's *in it*. Second verse, third line — that's her. That little turn she does at the end, Mum used to do it too, she never knew where she got it—"

"That's how it works." I crossed the grass and put my hand over hers, over the page. The paper was cold. Colder than the evening justified. "That's the mechanism. It doesn't invent anything. It recycles. Every note in that song belonged to somebody first. You're not hearing your grandmother, you're hearing what's *left* of her, worn smooth from a century of mouths—"

"She sounds happy too."

"Tamsin. Stop humming."

And she did. That's the thing I couldn't understand for days afterward, and I've finally made peace with not understanding it: she stopped. Immediately. Obediently. Like a radio switched off. She looked up at me with the page crumpling slightly in her grip and said, "Oh. Right. Sorry," as if she'd been caught picking at a scab, and the bell above us rang on alone for another minute and then, with what I swear was reluctance, faded into the ordinary sounds of a village putting itself to bed.

We walked back to Wrenfield House together. She talked the whole way — about her gran, about the turn at the end of the third line, about how her mother used to hum it while washing up and go quiet halfway through and never explain why. I listened. I catalogued. I wrote nothing down, because some part of me had begun to suspect that my notebook was no longer exclusively mine, and I didn't want to give the evening anything else to copy.

At her gate she hugged me. Quick, fierce, grateful. "Thank you for coming," she said. "I know you wanted me to stop. But I needed to hear her once. Just once. Whatever it costs."

"Whatever it costs," I repeated. "Do you hear yourself? Do you hear what you just—"

"Goodnight, Ines."

The gate clicked shut behind her. In the lamplight through her curtains I watched her silhouette cross the kitchen, put the kettle on, move through the small choreography of an ordinary evening. Normal. Completely normal.

I stood there long enough to see the light go off, and I told myself the price, if there was one, would announce itself loudly. Bells. Cold rooms. Moving measurements. Everything in Hollowmere announced itself. That was practically the village's charm.

I was wrong about that. I was wrong about almost everything that week, but I was most wrong about that.

* * *

She knocked on my door at nine the next morning, and I knew before I opened it.

You'll ask me how, and the honest answer is that grief has a knock. Everyone's does. Hers was usually quick and doubled, an impatient little tattoo, the knock of a person raised by a mother who hated waiting. This knock was slow. Single. Almost polite. The knock of someone who has forgotten why they came and is hoping the door will remind them.

She was holding two cups of tea from the café in Ashford, forty minutes away, which meant she'd been driving since eight, which meant she'd woken up wrong and gone looking for something to fix it with.

"Do you want to tell me," she said, "or do you want to pretend it's fine?"

"Tell you what?"

She came in without waiting — our arrangement, always our arrangement — and sat at the kitchen table, and set the cups down, and looked at her hands.

"Mum rang me last night. Late. She was upset, and she wouldn't say why, and I thought — I don't know what I thought. Then this morning I was making coffee and I reached for my phone to send her a photo of something, I don't even remember what, and I realized—" Her breath caught, restarted. "I was trying to picture her face. To caption it, I suppose. And it wasn't there."

"Tamsin."

"Not blurry. Not vague. *Not there.* I know her birthday. I know she takes sugar, one sugar, and hates coriander, and votes Labour, and cries at insurance adverts. I know everything about her except—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Her face. I stood at the mirror this morning trying to build it out of my own face, because people say we look alike, and I couldn't. I couldn't find her in me. It's like — you know when a word's on the tip of your tongue? It's like that, except the word is my mum, and the tip of my tongue is my whole head."

I sat down across from her. I did the thing I do. I got the notebook.

"What's the last thing you remember seeing?" I asked. "Her face. When."

She thought. I watched her think, watched her reach, watched her come back with nothing twice before she came back with something.

"Sunday," she said slowly. "Roast. She was laughing at something on telly. She had gravy on her chin and she didn't know." A wet, startled laugh escaped her. "Gravy on her chin. That's — that's the last one. Sunday. Four days ago." She looked up. "Ines, faces don't expire. What expires?"

What expires. I wrote the date down. I wrote *last retained image: Sunday, gravy, laughter* in my neat surveyor's hand, and I felt the notebook drinking it in, and I hated myself for feeding it, and I did it anyway, because if I didn't write it down, who would? That was becoming the whole thesis of my existence in Hollowmere: if I don't write it down, who will?

"Here's what I believe," I said. "And I need you to listen without humming."

That got half a smile out of her. Small victory. I banked it.

"The song is built out of memories. Stolen ones. Every verse is stitched from what singers surrendered — that's why it grows, why it wants completion, why it keeps finding new mouths. Your grandmother went into it in 1974. Last night you went looking for her inside it, and the bell — the bell doesn't give refunds. It trades. You reached in to touch her voice, and it took payment in the nearest currency." I turned the notebook around so she could see her own entry. "It took your mother's face."

"But I didn't sing."

"You hummed."

"Humming isn't—"

"It counted." I said it gently, and I meant it gently, and it landed like a verdict anyway. "Tamsin, I don't think there's a minimum transaction. I think you can buy a single note. I think you bought one line of one verse, and the line cost exactly what it weighed."

She was quiet for a long time. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and gave up, and the village went on performing normalcy with its usual flawless commitment.

"Can I get it back?" she asked finally. Very small.

And here is where I have to confess the thing I've been circling this entire chapter, the thing that made me unsure, at the top of all this, whether I'd taught her the verse. Because I looked at her — red-eyed, hands shaking around a paper cup, twenty-three years old and already amputated — and every instinct I had said *lie*. Say yes. Say we'll get it back, say you'll see her face again, say the bell owes you and I'll collect. She needed it. I needed to say it. And the words were right there, fully formed, in my mouth, in my handwriting, in a rhythm that was almost a melody—

"No," I said.

It cost me something physical to say it. I felt the false sentence dissolve on my tongue like a tablet, sweet and then bitter, and I understood in that moment exactly how the bell works on people. It doesn't force. It *offers*. It stands at the edge of your grief and holds out the one sentence that fits the hole, and the sentence is always a hook.

"No," I said again, steadier. "Not by going back in. Every trip in costs more than the last. But—" and I flipped to a fresh page, because this part was true and mine, "—there are other ways to keep a face. Descriptions. Photographs. Other people's memories. Your mother remembers her own face, presumably. We rebuild it from testimony. It won't be the same. It will be *documented*, which is not nothing. Documentation is how I pray. Let me pray for you."

She stared at me. "Did you just call note-taking prayer?"

"I contain multitudes. Mostly spreadsheets."

And she laughed — cracked, wet, real — and something in the kitchen loosened half a degree, and for about ninety seconds we were just two people at a table with cold tea and a plan.

Then she went pale all at once, the way a tide goes out.

"Ines," she whispered. "The third line. The little turn at the end. Where Gran's voice does the thing." Her hand rose to her throat. "I can't hear it anymore. Yesterday I could hear it perfectly. It's not — it's not that I forgot the tune, the tune's still there, but *her* — the part that was *her* in it—" 

She started humming, hunting for it, and I had my hand on her wrist before the second note landed.

"Don't," I said. "Don't hunt in there. Please."

She stopped. Her eyes were enormous. And I understood — with the clean, terrible clarity that Hollowmere dispensed like a vending machine — what we'd just learned, what the bell had demonstrated with the patience of a good teacher: it doesn't take everything at once. It takes the specific thing you love most, and then it leaves the rest intact so you can feel the shape of the absence. It keeps you functional. It keeps you *coming back*.

Grief, weaponized. Not the wound itself — the *appetite* for the wound. The bell had turned Tamsin's love for her grandmother into a recurring subscription, and last night's payment had only upgraded the plan.

"There's something else," she said, as I walked her to the door. She wouldn't look at me. "Last night. After. When I got home. I wrote something down and I don't remember writing it."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the foundations, into whatever Wrenfield House kept underneath itself.

"Wrote what?"

She handed me a folded piece of paper. On it, in her handwriting, in her hand but not her rhythm — the letters leaned wrong, leaned *hungry* — was a single line:

*The third verse knows your name, Ines Thornbury.*

I looked at it for a long time. Then I looked up at her.

"Tamsin. Have you shown this to anyone else?"

"No. Why?"

"Because," I said, folding it into my notebook with hands that were almost steady, "as of last night, the song isn't just taking payments anymore."

"It's placing orders."

* * *

That night the bell rang at dusk, on schedule, obedient, innocent. I logged it. Time, duration, approximate pitch. I logged the temperature drop in the kitchen: three degrees, consistent with prior readings. I logged Tamsin's loss in my neatest hand, dated and witnessed, because if the bell was going to keep books then so was I, and my ledger was going to be honest.

And at the bottom of the page, below all of it, my pen paused — hovered — and I watched my own hand write, in handwriting that was mine and not mine:

*Verse owed: one face.*

I did not write that.

I am writing this down so that somewhere, in some record, it is stated plainly: I did not write that.

And the house, around me, settled contentedly — like a ledger closing for the night.
