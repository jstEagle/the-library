## 24. The Sexton's Room

The false wall announced itself the way all liars do: by being slightly too eager.

I'd spent three days measuring Wrenfield House's ground floor, and by day two the east wall of the back parlour had developed a habit of being 4.2 centimetres deeper than every other wall in the row. Not dramatically. Not enough that a casual eye would catch it. But I am not a casual eye. I am a woman who once delayed a property sale for six weeks over a 9-millimetre discrepancy in a boundary wall, and I would do it again.

So when the east wall of the back parlour insisted on its extra 4.2 centimetres, I knocked on it. The wall answered with the hollow, apologetic thud of a wall that knows it's been caught.

"Of course," I said to the house, because by this point I talked to it the way you talk to a dog that keeps rearranging the furniture. "Of course there's a room in you. Why would there not be."

The house said nothing, which from Wrenfield was practically a signed confession.

Finding the mechanism took most of the morning. The seam was disguised under a century of paper — a florid Victorian pattern of pheasants that had faded to the colour of old tea — and the catch, when I finally located it, was a small brass knob recessed into the skirting board, the kind of thing a housekeeper would have known and a surveyor would have to lie on the floor to find. I lay on the floor. I have fewer dignities left than I once did.

The panel swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently. Recently. Within the year, I'd have sworn to it. The smell that came out was dust and candle wax and something underneath both, something older and sweeter, like flowers kept too long in a closed drawer.

I stood in the doorway for a long moment, torch in hand, and I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the only currency I have left that the bell hasn't devalued.

I felt welcome. That was the horror of it. Not fear. Welcome. The room had been waiting, and it was pleased I'd come, and some traitorous part of me — the part that had grown up invisible in a house full of people who remembered my brother's birthday and not mine — was *pleased to be pleased*.

I went in anyway. I am a professional. Professionals document.

* * *

The room was small, perhaps three metres by four, and it was the first space in Wrenfield House that had not changed its dimensions for me. I measured it twice, out of superstition rather than doubt. Three metres by four. Both times. It felt almost like courtesy.

A desk stood against the far wall under a window that looked out onto — I checked — the churchyard, or rather the place where the churchyard would be if the window had been glazed in this century. The glass was so old it had sagged, and through its ripples St. Aldric's tower looked like something seen underwater, which, given the tower's history, felt less like an accident of manufacture and more like a joke the house had been sitting on for a hundred years.

The desk was covered in papers. Real paper, foxed and brittle, covered in a small, cramped, beautiful hand — the handwriting of a man who had been taught penmanship as a moral discipline and never quite forgiven the world for it. An oil lamp sat at the corner, its reservoir still holding a finger's width of oil that had gone the consistency of honey. A candle stub, burned to a nub, stood in a saucer. A pair of spectacles lay folded on top of a ledger, one lens starred with a crack.

Everything was dusty. Nothing was decayed. The room had been closed like a wound, sutured behind its false wall, and whatever air was in here had been in here since 1911, give or take.

I photographed everything first. That's the discipline. Photograph before you touch, log before you interpret, because interpretation is where surveyors go mad and where, lately, I had been going mad at an accelerating rate.

Then I sat down in the sexton's chair — it was his chair; I knew it the way you know your own name, the room told me without telling me — and I began to read.

* * *

His name was Ambrose Pell. I knew that already, in the way I now knew things about Hollowmere: sideways, without permission. The sexton of St. Aldric's, father of two of the three boys who drowned in the mere on the last night the bell rang. The clipping in the parish archive had scratched his name out. The village had scratched him out of itself, and then it had walled his study shut like a tumour, and then it had gone on prospering.

The papers were research. That was the word for it, though *obsession* would also have served, or *confession*, or *the only penance available to a man who traded his sons for a harvest*.

The earliest pages were dated three weeks after the drownings. His hand was steady then, almost clinical. He wrote about the casting of the bell — 1849, a foundry in Ashbourne, and here Ambrose Pell had uncovered something the parish records had very carefully not recorded. The bronze had not come from the foundry's own stock.

*The bell was cast from metal bought off a travelling man,* he wrote, *who had in his cart the remains of a pyre. A funeral pyre, burnt the week previous, of a woman the villages round here still will not name. The foundry master paid for the bronze and asked no questions, because the price was good and the man was leaving anyway. But bronze remembers. Everyone who works metal knows it. You pour a bell from a thing that carried a voice, and the bell carries voices. I have stood under it for thirty years. I have heard it holding its breath between the strikes.*

I turned the page with a pencil, not my fingers. I don't know that it would have mattered. I no longer knew what mattered and what didn't, and that, I think, was the bell's real victory so far — not the moving measurements, not the ledger, but the slow erosion of my confidence that cause still preceded effect.

The middle pages were the bad ones. I'll say that plainly: the middle pages were the bad ones.

* * *

Ambrose Pell had spent years, it seemed, working out the arithmetic of the hymn. He wrote like a man doing accounts for a business he despised. The bell, he had concluded, was not a bell. It was a ledger in bronze. It had been cast from a pyre — from a death — and it could not make its own sound. Every note it rang had to be *given* to it, and the only currency it accepted was remembering.

*A hymn is a structure,* he wrote, in a hand that had begun to shake. *Four verses, the old pattern. But the bell's hymn is built wrong, or built backwards. Each verse is not composed. Each verse is harvested. When a soul sings along — truly sings, gives itself to the melody — the bell takes what the soul remembers, and it builds the next verse out of the taking. That is why the hymn grows. That is why it has never ended. It cannot end, because it is always hungry for its own conclusion, and its conclusion must be made of everything it has eaten.*

*My boys. God forgive me. I rang it knowing, or knowing enough, and it took them, and what it took it kept, and their voices are in the third verse. I have heard them. I have heard Thomas on the high line and Edmund underneath him, and I have stood in this room with my hands over my ears and known that they are not suffering, which is worse, because they are not anything. They are verse.*

I had to stop there. I put the pages down and looked at the cracked spectacles on the ledger and I thought about Tamsin Lowe, who could no longer reliably summon her grandmother's face, and about Mrs. Halloway, whose shop I had not been able to bring myself to walk past in two days, and I understood — not intellectually, not as a finding, but in the stomach, where understanding actually lives — that the third verse of the hymn was two drowned boys who had been eleven and nine.

I am a surveyor. I deal in facts. But there is a kind of fact that gets into you like a splinter, and you can log it, photograph it, file it, and it still works its way toward the heart.

* * *

The last pages were the hope. That's what I keep returning to, days later. A man who had sold his children to a bell spent the last years of his life trying to build it a lie it would believe.

*The bell cannot be silenced,* he wrote. *I have tried. Silence is not the opposite of its song; silence is its food. A bell that is never answered rings louder in the places where no one is listening. No — the hymn must be *finished*, but finished falsely. Completed without payment.*

*Every verse must be returned, not sung. That is the flaw in it, the seam. The bell holds what it has taken, but it does not own it. Ownership requires consent, and the dead did not consent — the dead never consent, they are only *used*. If the verses could be given back — spoken, not sung; named, not merged; each voice returned to itself, outside the melody, outside the harmony, outside the bell's grammar entirely — then the hymn would be complete and empty. A finished song with nothing in it. A purse turned out.*

*It must be spoken in the order it was taken. First theft, first return. And it must not be sung. Not one note. Not one hummed syllable. The moment the speaker joins the melody, the speaker becomes a verse.*

*I have begun the counter-verse. I have begun with my boys. Thomas first. Edmund second. I will speak them back to themselves, every memory I have of them, every memory they had, as much as I can reconstruct, and I will not sing. God keep me from singing. I rang the bell once. I will not give it my voice twice.*

The next page was the attempt itself. The counter-verse. Ambrose Pell's reconstruction of everything Thomas Pell had been — his birthday, his crooked tooth, the way he whistled through the gap, his fear of eels, his love of his brother, the joke about the vicar's hat, the memory of learning to swim in the mere in the summer of 1908, the last summer, the good summer — written out line by line, each line a returned thing, each line addressed to the boy by name.

It stopped mid-line. Mid-word, in fact.

*Thomas, who once said that when he grew up he would*

And nothing. The ink trailed off into a small blot, the kind a pen makes when the hand holding it stops being a hand.

I sat in that room for a long time with the unfinished sentence in front of me, and I confess I finished it myself, silently, in my head, the way you finish a stranger's sentence at a funeral. *He would come back. He would be a bell-ringer like his father. He would never leave Hollowmere.* Any of them. All of them. It doesn't matter what the boy would have said. What matters is that his father never got to hear it, and neither, in any way that counts, did the boy.

He got close. That was the thing I couldn't stop looking at. He had worked out the mechanism, the flaw, the seam — *returned, not sung* — and he had begun, and he had failed. Not because the theory was wrong. I don't think the theory was wrong. I think he failed because he tried to do it alone, at night, in a room behind a false wall, with a bell in his blood and two verses of his sons in his ears, and a man alone with a hymn will always, eventually, hum.

There was no note about what happened next. There was no next. There was a blot, and a wall, and a village that prospered.

But under the desk, when I finally made myself kneel and look — because a surveyor checks under things; it's in the blood — there was a tin box, rusted shut, and inside the tin box, wrapped in oilcloth, were two things.

The first was the vestry ledger. Not the one Ellery kept — this was older, the original, the master, its columns in Ambrose Pell's disciplined hand, names and dates and the annotation *verse owed* in every margin, and the last entry in Ambrose's writing was his own name, dated 1911, annotated not *verse owed* but *verse refused*.

The second was a single sheet of paper, folded once, and on it, in that same beautiful cramped hand, one sentence, underlined twice:

*The last verse isn't sung. It's returned. Tell whoever comes after.*

*Whoever comes after.*

I sat on the floor of a dead man's hidden room, in a house that had requested me by name, holding a message that had been waiting sixty years — no. More. Waiting over a century, waiting for a property surveyor who measured walls too carefully, and I felt the room's welcome close around me like a hand, and I understood at last why the house had been so attentive, why the floor plan had grown rooms, why the 4.2 centimetres had been left like a door ajar.

Wrenfield House hadn't been appraising me.

It had been *introducing* us.

* * *

I climbed out of the sexton's room as the light was going, and I stood in the back parlour with the false wall shut behind me, and that was when I heard it.

The pipes. The old lead and iron pipes that ran up through the house like veins, the ones that had groaned and muttered on my second night — they were carrying sound again. Not water. Not the bell, not yet, not the tolling.

Humming.

A chorus of voices, faint, rising through the walls from somewhere below the cellar, below the foundations, below anything a surveyor could put a number to. Dozens of voices. Maybe hundreds. And threaded through them, high and clear, a boy's voice, whistling through a gap where a tooth used to be.

Thomas Pell, singing the verse that was made of him.

I put my hand flat against the wall, over the hidden seam, and I said — out loud, because I am apparently the kind of person who talks to houses now — "I know how to finish it. And I'm not going to sing."

The humming stopped.

All of it. Every voice. At once.

And in the silence that followed, from the direction of the churchyard, the bell began to ring — not dusk, not its pattern, not its patient century-keeping schedule, but *now*, urgent, angry, a sound like a door being locked from the other side.

It knew what I'd found.

And somewhere below my feet, I heard the house begin, very quietly, to move its walls.
