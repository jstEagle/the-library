## 30. Refusal

The fireplace in Wrenfield House's study had not been swept in decades, but when I knelt before it at three in the morning with my notebook open on my knees, the flue drew perfectly. A clean, eager pull of air, like lungs inhaling.

Of course it did. The house wanted a fire. The house had opinions about what I was about to do, and I had learned—slowly, expensively, in the currency of other people's faces—that the house was never neutral.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the notebook for a long time.

It was a good notebook. Archival paper, sewn binding, the kind I'd ordered in bulk after my third year of surveying because cheap spiral-bound books fell apart in damp basements and I had developed, over the years, something adjacent to a relationship with these objects. They were the only witnesses I trusted. They didn't hum along. They didn't remember me into their childhoods. They just held ink where I put it.

Except this one had started holding ink where I *hadn't* put it.

I turned to page forty-one. My handwriting—or something wearing my handwriting like a well-fitted coat—had filled the margins overnight:

*The second verse rises on the B-flat. It wants the low voice first. It wants me.*

Below that, in slightly smaller script, as if the hand had been hurrying to get it all down before dawn:

*I have decided to sing. It is the reasonable choice. Everyone reasonable would sing.*

"I haven't decided anything," I told the empty room. My voice came out flat and unconvincing, the way voices do when they're arguing with paper.

The house creaked somewhere above me. Not settling. Listening.

Here is what nobody tells you about being haunted by a song: it doesn't scream at you. It doesn't need to. It just makes its case, patiently, in your own handwriting, while you sleep. And the horrifying thing—the thing I kept circling like a tongue finding a chipped tooth—was how *reasonable* the case sounded. Sing, and be remembered. Sing, and have parents who knew my birthday without checking a calendar. Sing, and walk into rooms where people were glad to see me instead of vaguely recalibrating around me.

I had spent thirty-four years being the person colleagues described as "thorough" in performance reviews, which is the word they use when they mean *forgettable*. The bell had found the softest part of me in one night. That's not evil, exactly. That's craftsmanship.

But Mrs. Halloway had poured tea for a customer two days ago and then stopped existing, and her shop was still open, and her tea was still warm, and every soul in Hollowmere would tell you she'd moved away years ago, moved away years ago, moved away years ago, in the flat chorus of people reciting an edit. And Tamsin Lowe had hummed eight bars of the second verse because grief is a hunger and the bell knows how to plate grief beautifully—and by morning she'd looked at a photograph of her own mother and asked me, politely, kindly, who the woman was.

That was the price list. That was what "reasonable" cost.

So: fire.

* * *

I want to be honest about how hard it was, because honesty is the whole point of documentation, and if I'm going to burn most of my documentation I should at least document why.

Page by page, I sorted.

This was slow work. I sat cross-legged on the cold hearthstone with a torch clamped under my chin like a surgeon and went through the notebook the way I'd go through a property's deeds—looking for the original instruments, discarding the amendments, especially the amendments nobody signed.

Original observations: safe. The measurements from my first day, before the numbers learned to move. The photograph log. The note from the east corridor—*measured 14'2" at 10:40; 14'9" at 11:15; corridor declined comment*—that was mine, written in my actual hand, with my actual dry little joke that I only make when I'm frightened and covering it. Keep.

The entry describing St. Aldric's empty tower, the plaque, the vicar watching me too long: mine. Keep.

The clipping transcription, the ledger names, the harvest records, the sexton's notes copied out in careful block capitals so I wouldn't lose them: mine, all mine, earned the hard way through archives and dust and Ellery's terrible tired confession. Keep, keep, keep.

Then there was the rest.

Page fifty-three onward, the contamination bloomed like mold behind wallpaper. Entries dated days I could account for down to the quarter hour—days I'd spent in the parish archive or walking the lane—and yet there they were, in my handwriting, describing conversations I'd never had. *Ellery says the fourth verse needs a vessel.* *Tamsin came to the house tonight; she sang; it was beautiful.* I had never heard Tamsin sing. I had made sure of it. I'd have sooner let her handle wet electrical wiring than let her near a melody.

And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain—which, I suppose, is exactly what it was:

*Sing.*
*You'll be remembered.*
*They're waiting for you to finish it.*
*It's only fair. You started listening.*

I tore those pages out carefully, because I'd learned by then that tearing carelessly felt like losing something, and the feeling was the trap. The bell didn't live in the ink. It lived in the wanting. But the pages were its paperwork, its filings, its claim staked in my name, and I am a surveyor—I know what happens to land when someone files a claim and nobody contests it.

The first page caught immediately. The flue pulled the flame upright, greedy, and for half a second the burning words glowed legible—*you'll be rememb*—and then they were ash.

"Wrong," I said to them. "Ask me properly next time."

The house groaned. A long, structural sound, deep in the joists, the kind of noise a building makes when a load shifts somewhere it shouldn't. I'd catalogued forty-one distinct house-sounds over the past weeks in my anomaly log. This was new. This was the sound of something not getting its way.

Good, I thought. Get used to it.

* * *

By page sixty I had a rhythm going. Tear, sort, burn. Original to the left pile, contamination to the fire. The study filled with the smell of hot paper and the orange light made the room's shadows lean in, and I narrated what I was doing out loud—not because the house needed to hear it, but because I needed to hear myself say it. Documentation as devotion, Tamsin had called it once, or maybe I'd called it that and she'd laughed. Either way, saying things aloud keeps them real. That's half of why I keep notebooks. The other half is that paper can't be edited by bells.

"Entry," I said, feeding another page to the flames. "Ines Thornbury, three-forty a.m., refusing a supernatural bribe. Witnessed by no one. Filed under: character."

The fire ate the word *sing* four separate times.

Somewhere around page seventy I hit the worst one.

It wasn't verse-logic. It wasn't the bell's handwriting at all—it was mine, genuinely mine, dated the night after the bell had shown me the life where I was loved. My parents' kitchen. My mother laughing at something I'd said. My father asking my opinion about the garden like my opinion was a thing worth having. The dream had been so detailed I'd woken up with the smell of cardamom in my nose, and I'd written it all down because writing things down is what I do with pain, same as anyone, and the entry ended with a line I remembered writing through tears:

*If it's a lie, it's the kindest one ever built. Maybe kindness counts even when it's bait.*

I held that page over the fire for a long time.

Because here's the thing. The offer hadn't been fake, exactly. That's the genius of the thing, if you want to call it genius. The bell had shown me a real door. Somewhere in the architecture of what it was—a chorus of stolen memories, a hymn assembled entirely out of other people's remembering—there existed enough material to build a convincing mother, a convincing kitchen, a convincing laugh. It had the inventory. It had stolen enough lives to furnish any dream it liked.

But a furnished room isn't a home. I'd spent weeks learning that difference in the most literal way possible: Wrenfield House kept adding rooms to my floor plans, fully furnished, lived-in, a child's coat on a hook, and not one of them was real, and the coat was always the same coat, and eventually you notice that the house only has the one child, the one memory, recycled forever, because the house doesn't *have* more. It just rearranges what it takes.

My mother—if she was still alive, if she still thought of me at all—was not a room the bell could furnish. She was a specific, flawed, absent person, and the dream-mother was a composite, and loving a composite is just a slower way of disappearing.

Also—and this is the part I said out loud to the fire, because it deserved to be said where the house could hear it—"she never checked whether I ate. Not once. Your version did. You got her wrong."

The house groaned again. Louder. Somewhere upstairs a door swung shut on its own, unhurried, like a parent leaving a room after a conversation gone badly.

I burned the page. All of it. Even the true sentence at the end, because a true sentence stapled to a lie is how lies travel.

* * *

Near dawn I reached the last contaminated section, and this one fought me.

Not physically. Nothing so honest. It fought me the way the hymn fought me—by making itself easy. Because the final pages weren't the bell's arguments anymore. They were the transcriptions. The verses themselves, lifted whole from the air: the first verse I'd dreamed, the second I'd pried out of Tamsin's description, the third I'd taken down at noon with shaking hands while the bell broke its own ancient pattern just to rattle me.

Three verses, complete, in my notebook. The only complete record in existence, probably, since the sexton's copy had burned with his hymnal and his counter-verse had died unfinished behind a false wall.

And I needed them.

That was the problem. That was the whole knot of it. Ellery's last note to me—*the last verse isn't sung, it's returned*—and the sexton's notes, and everything I'd pieced together about funeral-pyre bronze and metal that carries voices, all of it pointed the same direction: to end this, I had to give the stolen material back. Every name. Every memory. Every verse, returned to its owners without joining the melody. Which meant I couldn't destroy the verses. They were evidence. They were cargo. They were, God help me, the inventory I'd have to read aloud in that tower while a bell assembled from door knockers screamed itself back together above my head.

So I sat on the hearthstone with the three verse-pages in one hand and the fire in front of me, and I understood that resolve isn't a wall. It's a series of small decisions, each one renewed, each one available for reversal, forever.

I didn't burn the verses. I tore them free of the contaminated notebook—all three, plus the ledger transcription, plus the sexton's notes—and folded them into the oilskin pouch I'd bought in Mrs. Halloway's shop a lifetime ago, back when she still existed to sell it to me. Then I took a fresh notebook from my bag, wrote across the first page in block capitals—*CONTAMINATED SOURCE. VERIFY EVERYTHING AGAINST ORIGINALS. DO NOT TRUST ENTRIES DATED AFTER THE 12TH*—and transferred, by hand, line by line, every observation I trusted. Copying is slower than keeping. That's the point. Slowness is scrutiny. If the bell wanted to slip something into my records, it would have to survive being rewritten by a very tired, very stubborn woman who checked dates against train tickets.

The old notebook, stripped to its poisoned pages, went into the fire all at once.

It burned fast. Too fast—paper doesn't usually go up like that, with a sound almost like an intake of breath, almost like a chord cut off mid-note. The flue roared. The light in the room went white, then orange, then ordinary.

And then the house answered.

I've heard buildings grieve. I've stood in condemned mills and empty hospitals and one decommissioned lighthouse where the silence had texture, weight, opinion. But I had never heard a building *refused*. The groan started low, below hearing, felt in the floorboards and the roots of my teeth, and rose through the structure like water finding its level—walls flexing, plaster dust sifting down through the torchlight, the chimney breathing out one long disappointed exhalation that smelled of cold bronze.

A pipe somewhere inside the walls knocked. Once. Twice. Then, faintly, in a voice made of many voices—Halloway's warm burr underneath, Ellery's tired tenor layered over it, others I didn't know, dozens, a congregation—

*"Thornbury."*

Just the name. Just to let me know it was still there, still filing, still patient. Patience is the bell's longest verse.

"No," I said.

The pipes went quiet. The house settled into a sulking silence, and outside the window the sky was doing the grey thing it does before dawn, and the lane below was empty except for frost.

I looked down at my hands. Ink-stained, ash-dusted, steady now that it was over, the way hands only steady after the decision is made and can't be unmade.

"You don't get my memories," I told the room, quietly, in the tone I use for clients who try to negotiate the fee. "You don't get my mother. You don't get Tamsin. You've had a century of this village, and look what it bought them."

The house said nothing. Houses are cowards when you stop being afraid of them.

I packed the oilskin pouch, the fresh notebook, the sexton's notes. Tomorrow—today, technically—I had work to do. There was a tower to climb, a ledger full of names to carry up it, and a hymn that had been eating this village since 1911 and had never once, in all that time, been made to give anything back.

I was going to change its diet.

I blew out the torch, and in the dark, from the direction of the church, very faintly, the bell began to ring.

Not at dusk. Not at noon.

Continuously.

And beneath my window, in the frost-grey lane, I could already see figures moving toward the churchyard—serene, unhurried, humming—while upstairs in the spare room, Tamsin slept on, and I went to wake her, and I did not yet know that when she opened her eyes, she would look at me the way you look at a stranger on a train.
