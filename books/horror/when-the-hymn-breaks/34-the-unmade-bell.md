## 34. The Unmade Bell

The tower stairs were wrong, and I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last thing I owned.

A bell tower has a fixed number of steps. You can count them. That's the entire point of stairs—they are the most honest architecture there is, a staircase never lies to you the way a corridor does, the way a floor plan does. Stairs go up or down and they commit to it.

These stairs went up for eleven minutes.

I counted. Of course I counted; counting is my native language, my mother tongue, the only prayer I've ever known by heart. Two hundred and six steps. Then two hundred and six again. The stone under my palm was warm—not sun-warm, not hearth-warm, but warm the way a throat is warm, blood-warm, and it pulsed very faintly against my fingers in a rhythm I refused to let myself recognize as a rhythm at all.

I carried three things: the sexton's notes, wrapped in oilcloth from his hidden study behind Wrenfield's false wall; the surviving hymnal page, its lyrics ending mid-line like a sentence struck dumb; and the ledger, heavy as a sin, every name from 1911 to Tuesday, including mine, including Ellery's fresh entry, including Mrs. Halloway's, which had appeared sometime during the night in ink so new it still shone.

The air thickened as I climbed. Not fog—thickness. It resisted me the way water resists a hand pushed through it slowly, and it tasted of bronze and pond water and, underneath, faintly, of church incense and cut grass and something sweet I couldn't place until I could: funeral flowers. Lilies gone over. The smell of a room after the mourners have left.

*Voices,* the sexton's notes had said, in handwriting that matched my own contaminated notebook stroke for stroke. *It is not metal. It never was metal. Metal is only what remembers sound best.*

At the top, the stair ended at a hatch, and beyond the hatch was the belfry, and the belfry was empty.

That was the official version. That was what the plaque downstairs said, what the parish records said, what the village had voted into being in 1911 with the solemnity of people who believe a vote can unmake a thing: *the bell was melted down.* Three choirboys drowned the night it last rang, and Hollowmere had done what villages do with wounds—they'd closed them with heat and fire and a plaque, and called it healing.

I stood in the empty belfry and I listened to the emptiness ring.

* * *

Here is what I saw, and I have written it down four times since, and each time the description comes out the same, which frightens me more than any moving measurement ever did, because my documentation has finally stabilized around the one thing I wish would keep shifting:

There was no bell in the tower. And the tower was full of bell.

It hung where a bell hangs, in the space where the beam would be, except there was no beam either—just the suggestion of one, an absence shaped like oak. And in that absence hung the bell: vast, bigger than any bell cast in England, bigger than the tower could logically hold, and made of pieces.

I could see the pieces. That was the horror of it, or one of the horrors, the one my surveyor's eye kept snagging on like a nail in a floorboard. The bell was assembled. Riveted together out of fragments, each fragment catching the grey noon light differently—the dull gleam of a door knocker, lion-mouthed, from the Hollis farm; the polished face of the war memorial's bronze plaque, names still legible on its curve; a section of church railings; something that might once have been a candlestick; a hundred smaller shards, each one ordinary, each one the kind of object you'd pass in a village without a second glance, all of them now fused into a single swelling shape the size of a chapel dome.

Every piece of bronze in Hollowmere. Every knocker and ingot and memorial scrap. The village hadn't melted the bell in 1911. It had distributed it—hidden it in plain sight, hammered into the fabric of daily life—and the bell had spent a century quietly collecting itself back.

And it was molten-edged. That's the phrase my hand keeps writing even though I don't fully understand it. The seams between the fragments glowed, a low orange like banked coals, like the moment in a foundry when the pour is almost ready and everything still holds its shape but only barely, only by agreement. The bell existed in the state just before being finished. It hummed.

Not rang. Hummed. A held note, sustained, patient, the way a singer holds a breath before the final line. I felt it in my teeth. In my sternum. In the scar tissue of every memory I had left.

"You're not real," I said. My voice came out flat and professional, God help me, the voice I use for telling clients their drainage is non-compliant. "You were melted down in 1911. There's a plaque."

The humming deepened, and I understood—with the certainty you get in dreams, unearned and total—that it was amused.

* * *

I did what I always do. I took out my phone and I documented.

The camera app opened onto the belfry and showed me nothing: empty beams, pigeon dirt, grey light through louvres. I turned the phone toward myself, toward the space I knew the bell occupied, and the screen showed me standing alone in an empty room, mouth open, photographing vacancy.

"Right," I said. "Playback fails. Photographs fail. Noted." My hands were shaking and I noted that too, because if I stopped noting things I was going to start screaming, and screaming in a bell tower seemed like exactly the wrong instinct. "You exist outside the record. Everything I've got says you don't. So why am I here? Why show yourself to me at all?"

The hum shifted key.

I've thought about how to describe this for a long time, and I keep coming back to something Tamsin said, weeks ago now, or days ago, time having gone soft in Hollowmere like fruit going over. She said: *it doesn't speak to you, Isandro—it speaks as everyone who ever spoke to you.* And standing under the unfinished bell, I finally understood what she meant, because the hum resolved, briefly, into a voice, and the voice was my own.

Not an imitation. Mine. The exact timbre of my voice reading measurements aloud in an empty house, the voice I use when no one else is there, which in my life had been most of the time.

*"Wrenfield House, east corridor, first survey,"* my own voice said from the bell's molten seams. *"Fourteen feet, six inches. Second survey: fourteen feet, nine inches. Anomaly logged. Foundation settlement suspected."* A pause, and then, warmer, with something horribly like fondness: *"You measured us before we asked you to. Do you know how long we waited for someone who would measure?"*

"I'm not yours," I said.

*"No,"* it agreed, in my voice, gently. *"Not yet. But you wrote us down. Every room. Every anomaly. Do you know what that is, little surveyor? That is attention. We have been fed names for a hundred years, but names are cheap. Villagers give us names the way they give coins to a collection plate—grudgingly, in small denominations, hoping to buy another good harvest."* The seams brightened. Somewhere below, distantly, I heard the churchyard filling—footsteps on gravel, dozens of them, unhurried, serene. *"But you gave us observation. You looked at us the way no one has looked since the sexton. He tried to write us down too, at the end. He got close. He failed. He is in here."*

The hum changed again, and beneath my own voice I heard others—layered, braided, a chord made of people. An old man's laugh. A woman singing scales. A child counting steps—counting steps, two hundred and six, two hundred and six—and I gripped the ledger tighter and told myself the child was not relevant, was not the sexton's son, was not one of the three choirboys whose drowning had bought Hollowmere its prosperous decade.

"He traded them," I said aloud. "The sexton. His own sons. For the village."

*"For the village,"* the bell confirmed, in the sexton's voice now—I'd read enough of his notes to know it, that dry careful clerk's voice, and hearing it come out of the thing that ate him was like watching a signature forged well. *"And the village thanked him by scratching his name off the newspaper. Out of the ledger's first page. They erased the bookkeeper and kept the books. But he is here. They are all here. Everyone the verses were built from. Would you like to hear your friend Mrs. Halloway? She has been practicing."*

"No."

*"She remembers you, Isandro Thornbury. She remembers the childhood visit you never made. She remembers it beautifully. We gave her that memory ourselves, and she loved it, and she loved you for it, and now she is ours, and she sings."*

My chest did something complicated and painful. I put it away. Later. Grieve later. Document now.

"That's why you're unfinished," I said. "The hymn. Four verses. You've sung three. The last verse was never completed—the sexton interrupted the casting, or the bargain was never closed, whatever the truth is. You've been humming toward completion for a hundred years. Every stolen memory is a note. Every name in the ledger is a bar of music. And when the fourth verse ends—" I looked up at the vast molten-edged assembly of knockers and memorials and dead men's voices, and I finished it the way Ellery's single sentence had taught me to finish it: "—the song completes, and the village goes with it. Paid in full."

The bell was quiet for a moment. When it answered, it used my mother's voice.

I won't write down what it said in that voice. Some entries stay private even in a document like this one. I'll say only that it knew things about my childhood that no one living knew, and that it offered them to me like a hand extended across a table, and that for three full seconds I wanted to take it more than I have ever wanted anything, including survival.

Then I opened the ledger instead.

* * *

The pages moved under my thumb like something breathing. Names and dates, 1911 to now, each annotated in successive vicars' hands—Ellery's was the latest, small and tired—*verse owed, verse owed, verse paid.* And near the end, in ink still wet enough to smudge: *Ines Thornbury. Verse owed.*

"You need me to sing," I said. "That's the whole architecture of it, isn't it? A hundred years of engineering—houses that request surveyors by name, roads that loop, memories rewritten into welcome—and it all funnels down to one act. Someone has to complete the hymn willingly. The sexton rang the bell, but he wouldn't sing the last verse. That's why he's still in there, unfinished, along with everything else. You can't finish yourselves. You need a voice from outside the song."

The bell's hum rose, and rose, and became—for the first time since I'd entered the tower—an actual melody. The first verse. The one I'd dreamed, the one I'd woken humming, the one I'd burned out of my notebook page by page. It poured through the belfry like floodwater, and every fragment of bronze flared along its seams, and somewhere below, the villagers in the churchyard began, softly, in unison, to hum along.

I clamped my teeth shut. I pressed my tongue to the roof of my mouth. I thought about Tamsin's face going politely blank, and Mrs. Halloway's warm tea going cold beside an empty chair, and Ellery writing his own name into the ledger with the steadiness of a man paying a bill he'd been dreading all his life.

The melody crested. Held. Waited for me.

And I understood, finally, completely, the shape of the trap Ellery had died—or worse—to warn me about. The bell didn't want my death. Death was nothing to it; it had eaten plenty. It wanted my *voice*, freely given, joining the melody—because a verse returned in anger was still a verse returned, but a verse surrendered in song was a verse it could keep forever, and re-spend, and build the next century on. My whole remembered life, offered up as music. The ultimate entry in the ledger. The note that finishes the chord.

The hymn reached the end of the third verse and paused there, hanging, the way the burned page hung mid-line. Waiting for the fourth. Waiting for me.

I unwrapped the sexton's notes with numb fingers and found the passage I'd read a dozen times in the false-walled study, the passage I'd half-understood: *The bell was cast with bronze stolen from a funeral pyre—metal that carried voices. What was taken must be given back. Not silenced. Returned.*

Returned. Not sung. Not destroyed. Given back.

I looked up at the unmade bell—at the war memorial's names curved along its flank, at the knocker from the Hollis farm, at the hundred ordinary objects that had carried it patiently through a century of village life—and I raised the ledger above my head like a surveyor raising a plumb line, and I said, in the flattest, most professional voice I possess:

"Ines Thornbury, property surveyor. Final inventory. Beginning with the names."

The hum stopped.

The whole tower went silent—a silence so total and so sudden that I heard my own heart stumble—and in that silence, from the molten seams of the unfinished bell, from the assembled voices of everyone Hollowmere had ever fed it, something spoke. Not in my voice. Not in my mother's. Not in the sexton's dry clerk's tones.

In Mrs. Halloway's.

*"Mind how you go, dear,"* it said. *"And do put that book down. You'll strain yourself."*

Below me, in the churchyard, the villagers turned their faces upward toward the tower—all of them at once, serene as painted saints—and began to climb.
