## 38. The Final Appraisal

The morning after the bell died, Hollowmere smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet.

I stood in the churchyard with the others while they wept, and I did what I always do when a room becomes too much: I took out my notebook and I checked my equipment. Laser measure. Camera. The clipboard with the survey forms, warped from damp but legible. Tamsin stood three feet away from me, polite as a stranger at a bus stop, and I read her our days aloud anyway—quietly, under the sound of everyone else's grief, because I had promised, and because promises are the only documents I've ever believed were binding.

She listened the way you listen to a story about someone else's holiday. Nodding. Occasionally frowning at a detail, as if trying to place it in a catalogue that no longer existed.

But she stayed. That was something.

By mid-morning the villagers had dispersed to their houses, carrying their returned dead like armfuls of awkward furniture—Mrs. Halloway's keys, Ellery's last entry, a century of names suddenly heavy again. Nobody thanked me. I hadn't expected them to. You don't thank the surveyor; you sign off on the report and hope the foundations hold.

Wrenfield House was waiting for me at the end of the lane, and I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only prayer I know: it was waiting. Not looming. Not brooding. Waiting, the way a patient sits in a consulting room after the diagnosis has been delivered, wanting to hear the treatment plan.

I let myself in through the front door, which opened before my hand reached it—not dramatically, not with any theatre. It simply swung inward an inch, the way a door does when someone inside has just lifted the latch.

"Right," I said, to the hallway, to the dust, to whatever remained of the thing that had eaten a village's memory for a hundred years. "Let's finish the job."

* * *

A property survey is a strange kind of intimacy. You learn a house the way you learn a lover's body: where it settles, where it groans, which rooms run cold, where the water pools. For two weeks Wrenfield House had been learning me back—furnishing rooms I never drew, hanging a child's coat on a hook, requesting me by name through channels I still don't fully understand. The appraisal had never been a job. I'd known that since the roads started looping. But a contract is a contract, even one written by something hungry, and I am constitutionally incapable of leaving paperwork unfinished.

So I began at the beginning. Front elevation.

The house held still for it. That was the first mercy. Every measurement I took came back the same twice, then three times—the east corridor a clean fourteen metres, six centimetres, no argument. I ran the laser along walls that had spent weeks refusing to agree with themselves, and they lay down flat and honest, like dogs called to heel. When I photographed the stairwell, the image showed a stairwell. No extra landings. No doors that weren't there.

"You could have done this from the start," I told it, marking dimensions in my book. "Fourteen-oh-six. Was that so difficult?"

The house said nothing, but the silence had a different texture now. Before, its silences had been attentive—the silence of something listening through the plaster, taking notes of its own. This was just a house being quiet. Dust motes. Settling timbers. A draft finding the gap under the scullery door the way drafts have done in English houses since the Normans.

I worked through the ground floor methodically. Kitchen: damp course failed at the north wall, rising moisture to a metre, recommend injection or full re-plaster. Scullery: the pipes—the same pipes through which a chorus of stolen voices had offered me my parents' faces, my colleagues' love, a whole warm life built on the lie that I had ever been remembered—I tested them by running the tap. They knocked once, hollowly, and went quiet. Old air in the system. That's all. That's all it ever had to be.

I wrote *airlock in pipework, bleed radiators* in the defects column, and my hand didn't shake, and I counted that as a victory, though I'll admit I counted it twice.

* * *

The east corridor took me until noon.

Not because the measurements misbehaved. Because the child's coat was gone.

I had documented it in week one—a small grey coat, wool, moth-eaten, hanging on a hook in a room that appeared on my floor plan without my permission. Room 7, I'd called it, in the end, because refusing to name things felt like a different kind of surrender. Now the hook was bare. The room beyond was empty, stripped to plaster and lath, exactly the size my original drawings said it should be, smelling of nothing but cold brick and bird droppings from a broken chimney vent.

On the floor, where the coat had hung above, lay a single button. Horn, small, the kind you'd find on a child's coat.

I photographed it. I logged it. Then I picked it up, and I put it in my breast pocket, next to the pen, and I don't intend to explain that decision in the report.

Some things are anomalies. Some things are offerings. I've learned the difference, mostly, though the taxonomy is still developing.

The upper floors went easier. Bedrooms with bowed floorboards—defect, note, photograph. A window in the west gable whose putty had crumbled to powder—defect, note, photograph. In the principal bedroom I found the burn scar in the chimney flue where the hymnal had lived, and I knelt there a long time with my torch, looking up into the dark throat of it, half expecting a page to flutter down like a black moth.

Nothing did. The flue was just a flue. Whatever had survived in there, I carried now—in my notebook, in my head, in the promise I'd made to a girl who looked at me kindly and blankly and asked if I was the new vicar.

"No," I'd told her. "I'm the surveyor."

"Oh," she'd said. "That's useful. Houses need looking after."

They do, Tamsin. God help me, they do.

* * *

In the sexton's room behind the false wall, I sat down on the floor with his notes spread around me and did the arithmetic of his failure.

He had gotten so close. His handwriting marched across page after page—casting records, pyre inventories, the names of the dead whose bronze had gone into the bell unasked, their voices stolen at the moment of melting. He'd understood the mechanism a century before I did. He'd even drafted fragments of a counter-working, lines meant to give the voices back. But he'd tried to write it as a verse. He'd kept reaching for melody, for rhythm, for the shape of the thing that ate him, and every line he composed slid a little further toward song, and one night he walked out of this room and out of the village and into the ledger, and his sons' names waited for him there.

I gathered his papers into an archive box I'd brought for site documents. They belonged somewhere safe. The county record office, maybe. Or with Tamsin, when she was ready, if she ever asked the right questions. I wasn't going to decide her inheritance for her. I'd seen what happened when other people decided what a village should remember.

Before I sealed the box, I copied out one line in my own hand, on a fresh page:

*The last verse isn't sung. It's returned.*

Ellery's sentence. The sexton had almost written the same thing, forty years before Ellery was born—his draft reads *not sung but given back*, crossed out twice, abandoned. Two men, a century apart, arriving at the same truth and dying—or worse—before they could act on it. I'm not a religious person. But there is a word for knowledge that arrives too late in generation after generation, and the word is *haunting*, and I have professional opinions about hauntings now that I didn't have a month ago.

I closed the false wall. It fit flush, the way it was built to, hiding nothing anymore except an empty room and a swept floor.

* * *

By late afternoon I was back at the front of the house with my summary sheets, and here is where I have to be honest, because the whole point of the exercise—the whole point of me—is honesty in documentation, even when the document is damning.

Wrenfield House was dying.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean it the way I'd mean it about any building: the roof timbers over the east wing had rotted past salvage; the lintel above the cellar stairs had sheared and been propped, badly, sometime in the 1950s; the foundations, cut into clay that swells and shrinks with the water table, had moved enough to crack every load-bearing wall from footings to eaves. Any surveyor in England would have written the same report. Any surveyor in England would have recommended demolition, and slept fine afterward.

The difference is that most buildings don't hear the recommendation.

I stood in the entrance hall with my clipboard and my camera and I said it aloud, because it seemed owed. Professional courtesy. One professional to another.

"The structure is unsound," I told the house. "Beyond economic repair. My recommendation will be demolition."

The hall received this the way churches receive weather. And then—and I know how this sounds, and I have decided I don't care how this sounds—the house exhaled.

There's no better word. Every door in the building eased on its hinges at once, a soft collective sigh of timber, and the tension I hadn't realized I'd been carrying in my shoulders for two weeks drained out through the soles of my boots into the flagstones. The light through the fanlight over the door turned gold, ordinary, late-afternoon gold, the kind of light that falls on houses that are only houses.

It let go. I felt it let go. Whatever had worn Wrenfield House like a coat—whatever had learned my name, furnished my nightmares, sung to me through copper pipe—it released the building the way you release a breath you've been holding since 1911.

What remained was a very old, very tired house with bad foundations, standing in a village that had finally finished grieving.

I photographed every elevation in that light. The images came out clean. No anomalies. No rooms that shouldn't exist. Just architecture, failing gracefully.

* * *

I finished the paperwork at the kitchen table, because the kitchen table had good light and because I wanted the last pages written in the room where Mrs. Halloway's tea had once steamed, where the pipes had once sung, where I had burned my contaminated notebook page by page and answered a god with silence.

Summary of condition: poor. Roof: replace or demolish. Walls: cracked throughout, movement ongoing. Services: obsolete. Recommendation: demolition, site clearance, no retention of fabric.

I signed it. Dated it. My registration number beneath, small and official and absurdly mundane, like a period at the end of a very long sentence.

Then I turned to the final section of the survey form. Section 12: Anomalies Observed During Inspection. There were thirty-one numbered lines. I had filled twenty-nine of them over the past weeks in my tightest hand—*corridor remeasures at 14.11m, 14.06m, 14.19m within one hour; room present on plan not drawn by surveyor; child's coat, grey wool, hook, Room 7; bell audible at dusk, source absent; playback contains wind only*—a complete clinical record of a haunting, itemized and cross-referenced, evidence that would have ended my career and possibly my sanity if anyone had ever read it.

I struck through all twenty-nine entries. Single line each, dated, initialled. *Superseded.*

And on line thirty, in the space where the last anomaly should go, I wrote four words:

*It let go.*

I looked at them for a while. Four words against a month of terror. Somewhere in a filing system, some future surveyor might pull this report and find those four words sitting in a defects schedule like a stone in a shoe, and wonder, and shrug, and move on. That seemed right. The best documents hide their prayers in plain sight.

Line thirty-one I left blank. Not everything needs recording. Some things need keeping.

* * *

I locked the front door at dusk—for habit's sake, though the key turned reluctantly in a lock that no longer cared—and walked down the lane with my archive box under one arm and my clipboard under the other. Behind me, Wrenfield House stood in the last light with its windows going dark one by one, top floor first, like eyes closing.

At the gate I stopped. I don't know why. Instinct, maybe. Or the professional habit of checking your work.

The house sighed again—just timber settling, I told myself, just an old building easing into its final winter—and one window on the second floor, Room 7's window, caught the sunset and held it a moment longer than the glass should have.

Then it was only a window.

I walked on. At the bend in the lane I passed Tamsin coming up from the churchyard with an armful of flowers, chrysanthemums by the look of them, for a grave she couldn't name. She smiled at me the way you smile at a stranger who seems familiar, that small apologetic curve of the mouth.

"Lovely evening," she said.

"It is," I said. "Take care of yourself."

"You too." And then, as I passed—without seeming to know she was doing it, the way you hum a tune you've never been taught—she pressed something into my free hand, quick as a pickpocket in reverse, and walked on toward the house I'd just condemned.

I didn't look at what she'd given me until I reached the crossroads. A scrap of paper, folded twice, soft at the creases from handling. Her handwriting, round and careful:

*Thank you.*

Dated last night. And the night before. And the night before that—a little stack of them, folded together, written nightly on an instruction she could not recall, by a girl who remembered nothing of me except, somehow, the debt.

I stood at the crossroads until the light went, holding the paper, and then I put it in my breast pocket, next to the horn button, where I keep the things that aren't data.

Behind me, Hollowmere settled into its first honest evening in a hundred years. Somewhere a dog barked. Somewhere a door closed, and stayed closed. The church tower stood empty against the last grey light, and from it came no sound at all, and the no-sound was the most beautiful thing I had ever surveyed.

I turned toward the road out, and the road, for the first time, went where it said it went.
