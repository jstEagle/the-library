## 40. Coda: The Notebook

The report ran to forty-one pages.

I know because I counted them twice, the way I count everything now—twice, then once more for the record. Forty-one pages of load-bearing assessments and moisture readings and a structural recommendation I wrote in language so dry it could be used as kindling: *Wrenfield House is not fit for habitation and should be demolished at the earliest opportunity.* Underneath, in the section where the firm's template asked for "notable features," I typed one line: *The house cooperated fully with this survey.*

My supervisor flagged it. Sent it back with a sticky note in the margin—*Ines, what does this mean?*—and I sat there for a long time with my pen hovering over the printout, trying to decide what it meant, or what I was willing to say it meant.

In the end I changed it to *The property showed no resistance to assessment,* which is true in the way that most true things are true: technically, and not at all.

He approved it. The demolition order went through in eleven days. I have thought about attending. I have decided against it. Some farewells are better conducted by proxy, and besides—I don't think there will be much to see. A house that has learned how to let go does not go out like a building. It goes out like a breath.

* * *

The private notebook is a Moleskine, black, the kind you can buy in any stationery shop for the price of a sandwich. I chose it deliberately. After everything that happened in Hollowmere, I wanted my records to look like nothing. No leather, no ribbon, no embossing. A book so ordinary that if the world ever came looking for it, it would look straight past.

Old habits. Or new ones. With me it's hard to tell the difference anymore.

The first page is dated the third of November, three weeks after I drove out of Hollowmere with the windows down and the radio on, listening to a pop station I'd normally skip, because every song I heard that day sounded like a song someone had chosen to sing on purpose, and that felt like a miracle worth savoring.

The entries are simple. That was the discipline of it—the whole point, really. Every name from Ellery's ledger. Every memory I had documented being returned during those last hours in the churchyard, when I stood in the tower with my voice going hoarse and read aloud, flat and toneless, refusing melody the way you refuse a drink offered by someone who has already ruined your life once. Each entry has three parts: the name, the memory restored, and the date I confirmed its return.

*Edith Halloway — remembers her sister Margaret, drowned 1953, whom she was made to forget in 1961 — confirmed 14 October.*

*Tamsin Lowe — remembers her grandmother's face, her grandmother's voice, the last thing her grandmother said to her — confirmed 19 October.*

*Harold Vane — remembers his brother, the one the village voted out of its history in 1938 — confirmed 22 October.*

On and on. Sixty-one names. Some of them belonged to people long dead, whose memories I returned to descendants who didn't know they were missing anything until suddenly they were weeping into their breakfast porridge over an uncle they'd never heard of. Mrs. Halloway told me about three of those herself, over tea in her reopened shop, in the tone of a woman reporting weather. "Young Petch's boy cried all Tuesday," she said. "Good cry, though. The sort that clears a room."

That's how she talked about it. The sort that clears a room. As though grief were housework. And maybe she was right. Maybe that's all mourning ever is—finally letting yourself notice the space where something used to be, and sweeping it, and keeping the window open so the light gets in.

* * *

Tamsin doesn't remember me.

I want to write that plainly, because I spent a long time writing around it, in earlier drafts of this notebook, in letters I never sent, in the small hours of hotel rooms between Hollowmere and home. Tamsin Lowe looked at me on my last morning in the village with the polite blankness of a stranger at a bus stop, and thanked me for directions I hadn't given, and walked away humming something tuneless and harmless, and that was the price, and I paid it, and she never knew there was a price at all.

Except.

She pressed a note into my hand as I passed her on the lane. Her handwriting. Four words: *Thank you.* And beneath them, smaller, as if added later: *I don't know why I'm giving you this.*

She'd been writing them nightly. She couldn't tell me why—couldn't remember any instruction, any promise, any reason. But some part of her, the part the bell couldn't reach because the bell was dead bronze by then, had kept the appointment anyway. Every night, a thank-you note, addressed to no one, waiting for someone.

I keep it in the back pocket of the notebook now. It's gone soft at the folds from handling. Sometimes, when the work is bad—when a client screams at me about a boundary dispute, when the city feels like a machine designed specifically to make people invisible to each other—I take it out and read it, and I remember that documentation is a kind of prayer, and that this once, something answered.

Not a god. Not exactly. Something more like an accounting. The universe, or whatever runs underneath it, apparently respects a well-kept ledger. Who knew. All those years my colleagues teased me about my obsessive field notes—"Thornbury photographs the *dust*, Thornbury timestamps the *shadows*"—and it turns out I was training for a war none of us could see.

* * *

Ellery's grave is in the churchyard at St. Aldric's, under a yew tree, with a stone that says only *A faithful bookkeeper.* His own phrasing; he left instructions. The villagers thought it was a joke. I know it was a confession, and an apology, and the closest a man like him could come to peace.

I visit twice a year. Spring and autumn. I bring the notebook and read him the new entries, because I think he'd want to know the account stayed balanced after he stopped keeping it. In April I told him about Harold Vane's brother. In October I told him that the church tower has been re-hung—not with a bell, but with a windvane, a plain iron arrow that points wherever the weather wants. The parish council debated it for months. Someone suggested a commemorative bell, naturally. Someone always will. The motion failed nine votes to two, and the two who voted yes were old Mr. Petch and a newcomer who didn't know the history, which tells you everything about how memory works when it's healthy: unevenly, imperfectly, held by some and dropped by others, instead of curated by something hungry.

The empty tower rings at dusk sometimes. Just wind through the louvres. Everyone hears it. Nobody hums along.

That's the whole victory, right there, in that last sentence. Nobody hums along. You could build a cathedral on that sentence. I've built a notebook on it, which is smaller but sturdier.

* * *

Here is the thing I don't say at parties, when people ask about my work, when they ask why I got into surveying, why I'm so precise, why I keep everything:

I grew up feeling unrecorded. My parents loved me, I believe—they must have, there's evidence—but their attention slid off me like rain off glass. Teachers forgot my name mid-roll-call. Photographs of family gatherings somehow cropped me out. By thirty I had built an entire profession out of the wound: I would measure things, I would log things, I would timestamp and photograph and file, because somewhere deep down I believed that anything documented could not be erased. That existence was a paperwork problem.

Then I met a bell that agreed with me—and weaponized it. It turned out I was half right all along, just aimed at the wrong enemy. Memory isn't kept alive by ink. Ink is only the vessel. What keeps a person real is being *carried*—by someone who chooses, daily, freely, without compulsion, to carry them.

So I carry them. Sixty-one names. Plus Edith Halloway's sister Margaret, plus Harold Vane's brother, plus the sexton's two boys, whose names I found scratched into a beam in the sexton's hidden study and entered into the ledger myself, in my own hand, under the heading *Returned posthumously, with interest.* Plus Tamsin's grandmother, whose full name was Agnes Lowe and whose favorite hymn was the one that ate her, and who I think would find that funny if she were here to appreciate it.

Plus one more name, at the very back, on the last page, where I promised myself I wouldn't put it and then did.

Mine.

*Ines Thornbury — remembers themselves — confirmed daily.*

It sounds vain written down. It isn't. It's maintenance. Every morning I sit with coffee and I read my own entry aloud—not sung, never sung, spoken in the flattest voice I own—and I confirm it. I remember myself. I am the surveyor of my own existence now, and the survey is ongoing, and the findings are favorable.

Some mornings it takes longer than others. Some mornings the world still tries to slide off me like rain off glass, and I feel the old erasure gathering at the edges like frost. On those mornings I read the entry twice. Then once more for the record.

* * *

Last week I took on a job in a village called Marlow Dene. Ordinary place. Church, pub, a war memorial with three door knockers' worth of bronze in it—no, I checked, I always check now, it's just ordinary metal, cast in Sheffield in 1921, no voices in it at all. The house I surveyed was damp and crooked and completely indifferent to me. I logged forty-seven anomalies, all of them boring. Foundations settling. Roof failing. The usual honest decay of honest buildings.

At the end of the second day, the homeowner's daughter—a girl of about ten, all elbows and questions—followed me around asking what I was doing, and why I measured everything twice, and whether I was afraid of ghosts.

"No," I told her. "Ghosts and I have an understanding. They stay undocumented, I stay unhaunted."

She laughed like that was the funniest thing anyone had ever said, and then she asked me the question children ask, the one adults have forgotten how to ask: "But what happens to the house when everyone forgets it?"

I crouched down to her level, because some questions deserve eye contact.

"Someone remembers it," I said. "There's always someone. That's the whole trick. That's the entire secret of the world."

She considered this with the seriousness it deserved, then asked if she could hold the tape measure, and we spent twenty minutes measuring the garden gate together, and I wrote the number in my log with her name next to it as witness: *Poppy Ellery-adjacent, aged 10, confirms 4.2 metres.* (Her surname wasn't actually Ellery-adjacent. But she had the same way of watching a door too long, and I liked the company.)

When I drove away that evening, the church bell rang six o'clock, a real bell, rung by a real volunteer on a real rope, slightly out of time and slightly out of tune, and I pulled over and listened to the whole peal with the engine ticking and my eyes stinging, because it was the most beautiful sound I had ever heard: a bell that wanted nothing. A song with no appetite. Just people, making noise at the sky, for no reason except that the hour had come and their arms were willing.

* * *

Tonight I finished the sixty-second entry.

It arrived by post, of all things—an envelope with no return address, containing a single sheet of lined paper torn from a spiral notebook. The handwriting was shaky but legible. An elderly hand. The letter said:

*Dear whoever keeps the accounts. I don't know how I know you exist, but I do, the way you know a stair is missing in the dark. My name is Ruth Callow. I lived in Hollowmere until 1969. There is a hole in my life shaped like a summer, and a boy in it shaped like nobody, and I have carried the shape for fifty years without knowing what it held. If you are who I think you are, please write the boy's name down somewhere safe. I think it was Daniel. I think he deserved better than a hole.*

I checked the ledger copy I keep photographed on my phone. Daniel Callow, entered 1919, verse owed. One of the early ones. He'd been returned with all the rest, back in October—his memory went back into the village like water into a dry bed—but Ruth had already moved away by then, out beyond the reach of the restoration, carrying her hole across five decades and three counties.

So I wrote him down again. New entry, dated today, signed by me, witnessed by no one but the yew tree I'll read it under in spring. *Daniel Callow — remembered by his sister Ruth, who carried the shape of him for fifty years and named him in the dark — confirmed this night, by hand.*

And then I sat for a while with the lamp on, thinking about holes, and shapes, and the strange arithmetic of a universe that lets a sister's fifty-year ache reach a stranger's desk by post, addressed to *whoever keeps the accounts.*

Because here is what I have decided, in the quiet weeks since the bronze cracked and the birds came back: the bell was never the opposite of what I do. It was what I do, corrupted. It also kept names. It also refused to let the forgotten stay forgotten. The difference—the whole difference, the difference worth dying for, the difference Ellery died for—is consent. The bell took. I ask. The bell sang people into its melody. I read them back in prose, flat and toneless, each in their own words, each free to disagree.

Documentation was always a kind of prayer. I understand that now, all the way down. But a prayer, properly understood, is not a demand. It's a request, sent up in humility, to something that might decline.

This once, something answered.

Tomorrow morning I'll open the notebook, and read my own name aloud, once more for the record, and close it, and go to work. There's a house in Marlow Dene that needs measuring twice. There's a girl named Poppy who'll want to hold the tape measure again. And somewhere, in a drawer or an attic or a shoebox under a bed, there is always another envelope, another hole, another name waiting for a hand willing to write it down and let it go.

The notebook has two hundred pages left.

I intend to need every one of them.
