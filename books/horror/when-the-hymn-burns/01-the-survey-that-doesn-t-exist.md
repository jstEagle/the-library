## 1. The Survey That Doesn't Exist

The thing about a tunnel that isn't supposed to exist is that it doesn't care what you believe about it.

I've been telling people—editors, cavers, the occasional skeptical municipal engineer—that line for five years now, and it still gets a laugh, which is good, because laughter keeps the conversation in the territory of *quirky local eccentric* and out of the territory of *please seek help*. Dominic Blackwood, forty-one, freelance cartographer and documentarian, spends weekends crawling through passages that appear on no survey, no plat map, no utility record, no geological study going back to 1830. The town of Hollowmere sits on top of them like a lid on a pot nobody remembers buying.

I keep meticulous records. That's the ritual. Ritual is just rationality with better posture.

Saturday morning, October 14th. I was at the access point behind the old tannery by 6:40 a.m., which is late for me, and I stood there doing the checklist out loud because saying things aloud makes them real, and making things real is my whole personality:

"Helmet. Lamp, primary. Lamp, backup. Batteries, spare, two sets. Air meter. Chalk." I patted the chalk tin in my jacket pocket. "Notebook. Pencil, not pen—ink runs when it's damp, and everything down there is damp. Water bottle. Granola bar, which I will forget to eat and then eat angrily in the truck at two p.m."

Nobody answered, which was correct. I said it all anyway. Five years of this. You'd think I'd have gotten tired of hearing my own voice bounce off limestone, but honestly it's the only conversation where I'm guaranteed to be the smartest person present.

The new section had opened eleven days ago, when the ground behind the tannery gave way during a rainstorm and swallowed a quarter acre of scrubland into a sinkhole maybe nine meters across. The city put up orange fencing and a sign reading HAZARD — DO NOT ENTER, which in Hollowmere functions less as a warning than as an invitation with extra steps. I waited four days—not out of caution, I want to be clear, but out of *procedure*. You don't survey a fresh collapse while it's still settling. You let the earth finish its sentence before you transcribe it.

By day four the edges had stopped sloughing. By day six I'd done a preliminary descent and confirmed the sinkhole opened onto a passage running roughly north-northeast, dry-floored, ceiling between one and two meters, air moving faintly from somewhere deeper. Moving air means another opening. Another opening means the tunnel goes somewhere. And every time I'd found a new branch of the Hollowmere system, it had gone somewhere that made less sense than the last.

* * *

The descent itself was unremarkable, which I noted in the log with a small sense of professional disappointment. Six meters down a rubble slope, feet finding the same holds they'd found on the preliminary trip. At the bottom, the passage narrowed, then opened into a corridor with walls of that particular Hollowmere limestone—pale, almost buttery under lamplight, shot through with seams of something darker that I'd never had identified because sending samples to a lab requires explaining where you got them.

I started the survey proper at Station A1, which I marked with a dab of blue chalk and a number. This is the part people don't understand about tunnel mapping: it's slow. Gloriously, meditatively slow. You measure your baseline. You take your compass bearing. You measure the ceiling height, the floor width, you note the rock type, the water, the temperature. You sketch. You move five meters and do it again. A good day underground is three hundred meters of passage and a kind of silence in your head that I have never once achieved above ground, where my brain insists on narrating, worrying, rehearsing arguments with people who aren't there.

Down here, the narration stops. Down here there's only the numbers.

A1 to A2, bearing 22 degrees, 4.7 meters. Ceiling 1.8. Floor dry, fine silt. Temperature—I checked the thermometer—11 degrees Celsius. Consistent with the rest of the system. Consistent is good. Consistent is the whole religion.

A2 to A3, bearing 19 degrees, 5.1 meters. Here the corridor bent slightly east and the walls changed character. Someone—or something—had cut them. Not crudely, either. The faces were smooth in a way water never manages, with long horizontal striations like planed wood, except the grain ran in directions wood doesn't have. I ran my glove over it. Cool. Slightly tacky, the way stone gets when it wants to hold onto your hand a half-second longer than physics strictly requires.

I wrote: *Walls worked? Tooling marks? Unusual.* Then, because I am a professional: *Possibly natural slickensides.* Possibly. The word "possibly" is load-bearing in my entire body of work.

It was around Station A5 that I first heard it.

* * *

Here's what I need you to understand about sound in the tunnels. There are exactly four sounds in the Hollowmere system, and I can name them like old friends. One: water. Somewhere below the lowest known level there's a substantial flow—you hear it through the floor in certain sections, a deep arterial hush. Two: drips. Condensation cycling off the ceiling, irregular, arrhythmic, maddening if you let it be. Three: me. My boots, my breathing, my chalk, my occasional muttered commentary. Four: settling. The groans and ticks of a mine-adjacent landscape redistributing its weight, especially after rain.

What I heard at A5 was none of these.

It was tonal. That's the word I keep reaching for, and I hate it, because "tonal" sounds like something a wine person says. But it was—a held note. Several held notes. Underneath the drip and the distant water there was a chord, low, sustained, so steady it took me nearly a minute to be sure it wasn't my own blood doing what blood does in quiet places.

I did what I always do. I got out the equipment.

The hydrophone-style contact mic went against the wall first. Nothing. Wall reads silent. I moved it along the striations, meter by meter, watching the recorder's input meter sit flat as a dead man's EKG while the sound continued, unbothered, in the air around my head. Then the ultrasonic detector, because sometimes hums are harmonics of machinery—pumps, transformers, ventilation fans—and machines leave fingerprints up in frequencies we don't normally attend to. Nothing. Then the plain old microphone, the one I use for ambient documentation, pointed straight up into the dark of the corridor.

When I played it back, I got drips. I got my own breathing. I got, distantly, the water.

No chord.

I played it again. I put on the headphones, closed my eyes, turned the gain until my own pulse came through like a kick drum. Drips. Breathing. Water.

And underneath it—faintly, so faintly I could have been manufacturing it, and I want to be honest with you, reader, whoever you eventually are, because honesty is the only thing keeping this project from being a case study—underneath it, something that might have been a note. Might have been several. Might have been the recorder's own noise floor arranging itself into coincidence.

Might have been.

I stood there in the dark with my headphones on like a man praying into a seashell, and I thought, very calmly and very reasonably: *water pressure.*

That's the official explanation, by the way. It's in my notes. It's been in my notes since year one. The Hollowmere system sits above an aquifer channel; constriction points create flow-induced vibration; certain passage geometries act as resonators; the result is a low sustained tone that varies with groundwater conditions. This is a real phenomenon. It happens in caves. It has a name—humming sands, singing dunes, aeolian tones. Geology is full of rocks that sing. Nobody writes horror novels about the ringing rocks of Pennsylvania, or if they do, they're bad novels.

Water pressure. Constriction resonance. I wrote it in the log at Station A5: *Tonal hum, approx. 60–110 Hz, source unresolved. Probable hydrological resonance. Monitor.*

Probable. Monitor. Two more load-bearing words. My notes are basically a suspension bridge built entirely out of qualifiers.

But here's the thing I didn't write, because the notebook is a scientific instrument and I try not to perjure myself even to myself: the hum wasn't steady. Steady is what water does. What this did was *phrase*. It rose slightly, held, rested, rose again. There were gaps between the phrases, and in the gaps the drips sounded wrong—like applause in an empty theater, like they were filling space that expected to be filled.

It sounded, and I am choosing this word with great care and considerable reluctance, like a hymn. Like the humming of a congregation that knows the verses so well it doesn't need the words anymore.

I know how that sounds. I grew up in a church town; I know exactly what a congregation sounds like from the parking lot, where you're not allowed in yet, where you stand with your hands in your pockets listening to something you're not part of.

I turned off the recorder. I told myself the batteries were getting cold, which they were, technically, in the sense that all facts can be technically true while missing the point entirely.

* * *

I pushed on to Station A9 before turning around, which was further than procedure recommended for a fresh section, and I'll cop to that. The corridor kept its worked-stone character, smooth-walled, gently descending, and twice it passed small alcoves—niches, really, waist-high, their floors worn concave as if objects had rested in them for a very long time. Dust sat in the niches in neat rings, like the ghost outline of whatever used to live there.

I photographed both. In the log: *Two niches, purpose unknown. Possibly mineral concretions removed. Possibly structural.* Possibly possibly possibly. Someday someone is going to read my complete field notes and conclude that the Hollowmere tunnels were surveyed by a woman made entirely of hedging.

At A9 the passage forked. Left branch descended steeply and exhaled cold air that smelled of wet iron. Right branch leveled out and, after ten meters, ended at a collapse—not fresh, this one. Old. The rubble was furred with pale mineral growth, calcite probably, and had clearly been undisturbed for decades.

I should have mapped the fork and turned back. Procedure. Instead I stood at the junction for a while, listening to the hymn-hum-water-pressure, and felt something I couldn't file anywhere: the specific loneliness of standing in a place that has been waiting. Not for me specifically. Just waiting, the way an empty chair waits.

Then I saw the mark.

It was on the right-hand wall, two meters past the fork, at about shoulder height. Chalk. White chalk, drawn directly on the stone, and old enough that the dust of decades had softened its edges without erasing it.

A vertical stroke. A loop at the top, drawn clockwise. A second smaller loop crossing the stem near the bottom, open to the left.

I knew it the way you know your own signature. Not because I've studied it. Because I drew it.

Or—I need to be precise here, precision is the last tool I have left—because something exactly like it hung on the inside of the closet door in my childhood bedroom, on Larkspur Street, drawn at my eye level when my eye level was considerably lower, in chalk my mother swore she never bought me, in a house where we didn't keep chalk. I used to look at it every night before bed. I had a whole cosmology around it by age six. It was a door, I decided. It was a door that a friend of mine could come through, a friend named Wren, who was tall and frost-pale and patient and who I told everyone about and whom everyone, kindly, correctly, attributed to an only child's imagination.

The mark on the tunnel wall was the same size as the one on my closet door. Same size. Same hand, if hands were involved. As if the person who drew it had been exactly my current height, standing exactly where I was standing, drawing a door.

My lamp flickered once—the battery, I told myself, the cold, the contacts—and in that flicker the hum swelled, just slightly, the way a congregation swells when the minister finally walks in.

I did not draw anything. I want that on the record. I stood there for—I checked later, the timestamps don't lie—four minutes and eleven seconds, and then I backed away from the mark without turning around, which is a thing I have never once done in five years of tunnel work, because turning your back on a passage is Surveying 101 and I have never been superstitious, I have been *thorough*, those are different, everyone says so.

In the log, at Station A9, I wrote: *Junction, two branches. Right branch collapsed, terminal. Anomalous marking on wall, chalk, matches no survey convention. Photographed. Note: resembles folk boundary sigils; possible 19th-century miner's mark. Research.*

Folk boundary sigils. Miners' marks. See, this is the craft. You take the thing sitting in your chest like a swallowed stone and you find it a little taxonomic drawer to sleep in, and you close the drawer, and you climb six meters of rubble into October sunlight, and you drive home past the church where the parking lot is empty on a Saturday, and you tell yourself the granola bar story, and you laugh at your own joke, alone, in the truck, at 2:07 p.m., because that's what rational people do.

The photographs came out fine. The niches, the fork, the collapse. All of them.

Except the one of the mark. That one came out blurred—not motion-blurred, I braced properly, I know how to brace—but soft, as if the camera had focused on a point slightly in front of the wall. As if the mark were standing a little ways off its own surface, waiting to see what I'd do.

I deleted it. Bad data. You delete bad data.

Tomorrow I'll go back with the good tripod and reshoot it. Tomorrow I'll bring the spectrogram rig and get a clean recording of the resonance and run the numbers and find the aquifer constriction on a map and write a tidy paragraph about karst hydrology, and the drawer will close, and everything will stay filed.

Tonight, though, driving home with the windows down and the first real cold of the season coming through, I caught myself humming.

Three notes. Rising, holding, resting. Rising again.

I don't know any hymns. I never learned the words to anything.

I'm sure it's nothing. It's probably just the radio.
