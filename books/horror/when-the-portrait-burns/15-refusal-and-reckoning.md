## 15. Refusal and Reckoning

The portrait asked me a question, and I said no.

I want it on the record that I said no quickly. No hemming, no hawing, no dramatic pause for the benefit of whatever passed for an audience in that warm, breath-held room. The assembled voices had offered me everything — belonging, remembrance, a home with my name carved into its lintel — and I looked at the scorched canvas where a face should have been and gave it the same answer I give to clients who ask me to fudge a square footage.

"No."

The word came out smaller than I intended. Words do that in Hollowmere. The house ate sound the way the fen ate roads.

"You would be *valued*," the voices said, all of them at once, Delia's dead mother and the surveyor from 1974 and children who'd wandered off before my parents were born, a choir of the comfortably erased. "You would never again have to prove you exist. You would simply *be*, the way we are."

"The way you are," I repeated. "Preserved. Mid-life. Hollow-eyed. Standing in a parlor waiting for someone to notice you've been gone since 1931." I shook my head. "That's not being valued. That's being filed."

The warmth in the room changed temperature. Not colder — *tighter*. The held-breath quality of the air became a held-grudge.

"Think carefully," said one voice alone now, and it was almost my mother's, which was a low trick even for a haunted house. "You spent your whole life measuring rooms so people could decide what they were worth. Who measured *you*?"

And there it was. The question underneath every question, the one I'd driven three hundred miles to avoid by hiding inside a laser measure and a clipboard. I thought about the eviction when I was nine, the landlord's men carrying our couch out into the rain while my mother cried in the kitchen and I sat on the stairs counting doors to keep from counting what we were losing. I thought about the name I'd buried — the one on my birth certificate, the one that fit like a shoe two sizes wrong — and how I'd traded it for Marguerite, for something I chose, and how choosing still felt like trespass some days.

"I did," I said. "I measure myself every morning. It's called getting up."

The portrait made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a scream. A *settlement* — the groan a house makes when it shifts on its foundations, except it came out of a painting, out of a hundred mouths' worth of scorched canvas, and it meant: *very well.*

Then the lights went out.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about darkness in a house that lies: it isn't the absence of light. It's the presence of *options*. Every direction becomes equally plausible. Every wall becomes negotiable.

I had a flashlight in my survey bag because I am, above all things, a professional, and professionals carry backup systems for their backup systems. I clicked it on and the beam found a corridor where the parlor door should have been.

"That's new," I said, because talking to myself kept the fear at a manageable altitude.

I turned in a slow circle. Doors. Doors everywhere, standing open like mouths mid-sentence, and behind each one a different version of Hollowmere — the parlor, the nursery, the east wing, a staircase I'd never seen, a kitchen that smelled of bread no one had baked since the Coolidge administration. The house had stopped pretending to have a floor plan. It was showing me its true nature: not a building but a *choice*, endlessly branching, and every branch ended somewhere warm.

Somewhere I could stay.

"Wren," I said. My voice bounced down corridors that shouldn't connect. "Wren!"

Nothing. And then — worse than nothing — a hum. Three notes, drifting from behind the fourth door on the left, the tune I'd been hearing for days, the one I almost knew. Except this time it cut off halfway through, like a recording snatched away, and I understood with a certainty that bypassed my brain entirely and landed in my sternum:

They had taken Wren.

Collateral. That's what the ledger-keepers call it. When a debtor refuses terms, you seize assets. I had refused the house's offer, and the house had reached into the one account I couldn't bear to close and withdrawn everything.

My hands were shaking. I noted this the way I note everything — *tremor, bilateral, onset acute* — because naming a thing is the first step to measuring it, and measuring a thing is the first step to controlling it. My hands were shaking. My breathing was fast but regular. My flashlight worked. The exit — the actual front door, the one I'd walked through two nights ago — was somewhere behind eleven or twelve or forty doors.

I could leave.

Let me be honest, because honesty is the only tool I have left that hasn't been compromised: I thought about leaving. I stood in that impossible corridor with my flashlight beam trembling against the wallpaper and I thought, *walk out, drive back to the motel, file the report, let Ashen Fen keep its secrets, grief is not your department.* I thought about how Wren wasn't real anyway — how I'd invented them at seven years old out of loneliness and a child's stubborn imagination, how adults don't get to have imaginary friends, how losing them again would just be losing them twice and the second time wouldn't even count.

The thought lasted four seconds. I know because I counted, the way Wren taught me to count, one door at a time, steady as a heartbeat.

Because here's the thing about imaginary friends: you don't invent them. You *recognize* them. Something is already there, waiting in the shape of your loneliness, and you give it a name so it can sit beside you at dinner. And if Wren had been real enough to wait twenty-five years on a porch, holding a lantern, unchanged, patient as masonry —

— then they were real enough to steal.

And I do not permit theft. Not of property. Not of persons. Not of the small, strange, beloved things a child builds to survive a childhood.

I clicked off the flashlight, because the house wanted me fumbling, and I refused to fumble. In the dark I pressed my palm flat against the nearest wall and did what I have done in every building I've ever surveyed.

I listened.

* * *

A house tells you what it is through its bones. Load paths, joists, the way sound travels through plaster. Most people think surveying is arithmetic. It's actually eavesdropping.

Hollowmere's bones were wrong — I'd known that since day one, since the laser measure returned numbers that violated geometry — but wrong has a pattern too. Wrong is consistent, if you pay attention. The corridors extended past the exterior walls always toward the north. The nursery existed where blueprints showed brick, and brick showed where the nursery should be, as if the house and its plans had swapped places sometime around 1931 and neither had fully recovered. The eleventh door appeared only after midnight, and only on the second floor, and only when someone was counting.

Someone had built rules into this place. Someone had played a game here, long ago, and the game had become load-bearing.

Wren's rules. *Our* rules. Keep the lights on. Count the doors. Never sing along.

I'd thought we invented them. Sitting on the porch steps of a rented house in a town I barely remembered, seven years old and friendless, making up a game with someone only I could see. But chapter thirteen of my own personal horror story had shown me otherwise — the house had shown me rooms built from my own suppressed memories, and among them, half-buried like a foundation stone, was this truth: the child knew something real. Children always know. That's why we spend our adulthoods teaching them not to.

So: the rules were a map. The map led to the nursery. The nursery held Wren.

And between me and the nursery stood a house with infinite doors, failing lights, and a grudge a century old.

"Fine," I told the dark. "Let's appraise."

* * *

I moved by touch and count. Left hand on the wall — the left wall, always the left wall, because the house branched north and I needed to go south and east, against its grain. I counted my steps aloud in a flat surveyor's monotone, because the eleventh door only mattered if you lost track, and I did not intend to lose track of anything ever again.

Forty-one steps. A corner. The wall under my palm changed from plaster to wood paneling — original, 1890s, oak, worth nothing now because the whole house was worth nothing now, because value requires a willing buyer and I was the last person alive who wanted anything from Hollowmere except its surrender.

Fifty-three steps. Another corner. Under my feet, the floorboards began to hum.

Not mechanically. *Musically.* Three notes, faint, rising through the soles of my boots like heat through a stove top. The song. The Merrick lullaby, the one everyone in Ashen Fen half-remembers, the one whose last verse died in a little girl's memory and waited a century for someone to finish it.

The house was singing to me. Or — no. I stopped. Pressed my hand harder against the paneling. Listened past my own panic.

The house wasn't singing. The house was *vibrating with* singing, the way a wineglass vibrates near a voice about to break it. Somewhere ahead, Wren was singing — or trying to, or being made to — and the whole structure rang with it like a struck bell.

They were using Wren's voice to bait me. Of course they were. The house had read my file; it knew exactly what I'd walk into fire for.

It had miscalculated one thing. It thought knowing it was a trap would make me refuse the bait.

I started running.

* * *

Running through a lying house is a special kind of nightmare. The corridor lengthened under me — I could feel it, boards stretching like taffy, distance being manufactured faster than I could consume it. Doors opened as I passed, offering glimpses: my mother's kitchen from the apartment on Delancey Street, steam on the windows; the county archive room with its dead fluorescent light; the porch at dusk with a lantern burning. The house threw my own memories at my feet like bread crumbs, trying to lure me off the wall, off the count, off the path.

"Keep the lights on," I gasped, and it wasn't a rule anymore, it was a prayer, it was a *plan* — because the lights were mine to keep now, the flashlight in my fist, and I thumbed it on and swept the beam forward and the corridor flinched.

It actually flinched. Light is a measurement, and measurements are a kind of truth, and Hollowmere has been dodging the truth since 1931. The stretching stopped. The boards settled. Ahead of me, maybe sixty feet, a stairwell descended, and at the bottom, a door with a brass knob worn bright by a century of small hands.

Behind me, the dark gathered itself up and came.

I heard it coming the way you hear weather — a pressure change, a hush, a sense of enormous mass deciding to move. All those doors I'd passed swung shut in sequence, *bang, bang, bang*, like a drumline, and the temperature dropped so fast my breath went white, and from everywhere and nowhere the voices came back, the assembled kept, and they weren't offering anymore.

*"Stay,"* they said, and it sounded like a thousand people sighing at once. *"Stay. Stay. Stay."*

"No," I said, and ran.

The stairs took me three at a time. My survey bag banged against my hip — laser measure, ledger, the county records I'd photocopied, Delia's confession scrawled on a napkin like the world's bleakest recipe. Ammunition, all of it. Paper is power; ask any bureaucrat, any ghost, any town that expunged a family from existence and got haunted for a hundred years.

At the bottom of the stairs, the door with the bright knob. Behind it, the humming was louder, and underneath the humming — a voice. Small. Familiar. Cracked with something I refused to identify as fear because if Wren was afraid then the world had failed at a level I couldn't fix with a refusal and a flashlight.

"Wren!" I hit the door with my shoulder. It didn't budge. Locked, or worse than locked — *decided*. "Wren, I'm here! I'm coming! Don't sing along with anything, do you hear me? Don't you dare sing along!"

The humming stopped.

And then Wren's voice came through the door, clear and close, closer than the door should have allowed, right against the wood, right against my ear, and it said:

"Margo. Count the doors."

I froze. Because that was the rule I'd never understood — count the doors, count them every night, ten doors on the second floor, always ten, and if you find eleven, run. I'd thought it was a game. A kid's arbitrary ritual, like not stepping on cracks.

But Wren hadn't taught me to count doors so I'd know when to run.

Wren had taught me to count doors so that someday, when it mattered, I'd know when one was *missing.*

I turned around slowly. The stairwell behind me. I counted the doors along it, the ones that had slammed shut, my flashlight beam touching each knob in turn.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Ten.

There should have been eleven. There had been eleven doors on that landing when I came down. The house had eaten one — swallowed it whole, hinges and all — and where it had been there was now smooth plaster, seamless, innocent, a wall with no memory of ever being anything else.

Except the wallpaper didn't match. A strip of it, doorframe-wide, slightly newer, slightly paler, and behind it — I pressed my ear flat — behind it, faint as a pulse, I could hear breathing. A lot of breathing. Dozens of lungs, keeping time together, the way people breathe when they're standing very still in a room, waiting.

The house hadn't taken a door away.

It had *added* a room.

And somewhere inside it, behind fresh paper and old lies, Wren was waiting — along with everyone else the house had ever kept — and the only way in was the way the house feared most: the truth, sung correctly, all the way to the end.

I put my hand flat against the mismatched wallpaper. It was warm. Warm like held breath. Warm like a face leaning close.

"All right," I whispered, and I don't know if I was talking to the house, or to Wren, or to the youngest Merrick girl whose last verse waited somewhere in the dark like a debt a century overdue. "You want to negotiate? Fine. But I'm bringing witnesses."

I stepped back, pulled the appraisal ledger from my bag — the town's own record of theft, every fraudulent lien, every forged signature, every acre stolen from the Merricks and laundered through a century of polite paperwork — and held it up to the wall like a warrant.

"And I'm bringing fire."

The breathing behind the wall stopped.

Somewhere far above me, in the dark heart of Hollowmere House, something very old began, very quietly, to sing the first verse — and for the first time, it sang it scared.
