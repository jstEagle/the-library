## 21. Aftermath, Measured

Dawn came to Ashen Fen the way it comes everywhere—indifferently, in gray increments, without any regard for what had burned in the night.

I stood on the front lawn of Hollowmere House with a laser measure in one hand and soot on everything else, and I did what I have always done when the world stops making sense: I measured it.

The house was a ruin now. Not the dramatic kind—not a shell with teeth, not a blackened jaw against the sky. Just an ordinary ruin, sagging and sad, the way farmhouses go when nobody loves them. The east wing had collapsed inward like a lung giving up. The roof over the nursery was gone entirely, open to a sky that looked embarrassed about the whole business. The walls that had extended past their own foundations were simply walls again, brick and rot and bird droppings, ending where they ended.

I ran the measure along the south face three times.

Forty-one feet, six inches. Every time. The same number my device had refused to give me on day one, back when it had stuttered and spun and offered me figures that would have required the house to be larger inside than out, larger than its own footprint, larger than geometry allowed. Now it gave me forty-one six like a tired clerk reading off a form.

The house had been straightened by fire. Or by release. Or by the simple fact that nothing that remains of a thing can lie about its size.

I wrote the number down anyway. That's the job. You write down what's true even when the truth is anticlimactic, especially then. Truth is mostly anticlimax with occasional interruptions of horror, and the horror never shows up on the final report.

* * *

Wren died at 6:14 in the morning, or stopped, or finished—I've spent weeks deciding which word to use and I still don't know. "Died" feels wrong for something that was never alive in the way I'm alive. "Stopped" sounds like a watch. "Finished" is the word Wren used, so maybe that's the honest one, but honesty has been complicated between us since roughly 1998.

They faded in my arms on the porch steps—the same porch steps where they'd waited twenty-five years with a lantern, unchanged, patient as furniture. Except now they were changing. Now they were coming apart into light the way sugar comes apart into water, slowly and then all at once, and I held as much of them as arms can hold, which turned out to be almost none.

"Thank you," Wren said. Their voice was already half elsewhere, thin as paper held to a window. "For finishing the game."

"It wasn't a game."

"It was *always* a game." A smile, or the memory of one, or the blueprint of one. "You made up rules because rules keep the dark in its room. I just kept playing after you left. Somebody had to count the doors."

"I should have come back."

"You were seven. Seven-year-olds go home." The light in them flickered, guttered, steadied. "Besides. You came back eventually. Twenty-five years isn't so long if you're made of waiting."

I want to tell you I said something magnificent. I didn't. I said, "Don't go," which is the least original sentence in any language, and Wren laughed—a real laugh, brief and bright, the laugh from the summers we'd invented out of boredom and chalk—and said:

"Marguerite. Say the last verse."

So I did. Right there on the porch, voice cracking, no melody to speak of, just the words Edith Merrick had carried in her head for a hundred years and Wren had carried for her, and me carrying them now, out loud, into ordinary morning air where they did absolutely nothing except exist. That was the whole miracle. They existed, and nothing happened, and nobody vanished, and the song was just a song again—a lullaby some family sang before another family burned them out of it.

When I finished, Wren was gone. All of them. Even the lantern, which rolled once down the steps and went out, and did not roll back up, and I have decided to believe that means they weren't afraid anymore.

* * *

Theo found me an hour later sitting on the porch holding a dead lantern, looking, by his later account, "like a guy who lost a fight with a building." He had soot to his elbows and a bandage on his forearm from a door—one of the false ones, the multiplying ones—that had tried to close on him during the climb to the hearth. He sat down next to me without asking, which is most of what friendship is.

"Is it over?" he asked.

I gestured at the ruin with the laser measure. "It's forty-one feet, six inches."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know." I turned the device over in my hands. "Yeah. It's over. They're free. All of them. The kept rooms are just rooms now. I checked."

He nodded slowly. Then, because he was nineteen and honest in a way the older generation of Ashen Fen had never learned to afford: "Was Wren real?"

"They were as real as anything else in this town," I said. "Which is to say: believed in, badly maintained, and burned by people who should have known better."

Theo looked at his boots for a while. Then he said, "Delia wants to see you. She says—" he hesitated, "—she says the town owes you a debt, and she knows you're going to collect it in the worst possible way."

"She knows me well."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving a haunting: afterward, there is paperwork.

I filed the appraisal report on Tuesday. I am professionally obligated to tell you it was the most accurate document of my career. Every measurement verified twice. Every anomaly explained. Square footage consistent with exterior dimensions. Structural integrity: failed. Fire damage: extensive, origin undetermined. Prior occupancy records: irregular.

That last line took me four drafts. How do you write *the client file listed me as a prior occupant of a house I first entered at thirty-two* in language that satisfies a county review board? How do you footnote *a corridor extended eleven feet past the north wall until it didn't*? I settled on: *Discrepancies in historical documentation appear to reflect deliberate falsification by parties unknown, circa 1931.* Technically true. The parties unknown burned a family alive and then erased them, and the falsification was so thorough it became architecture. But the board doesn't need the poetry.

The report was rejected, incidentally. Not for inaccuracy—for irrelevance. The county ruled that a structure with no salvageable value and no claimant required no formal appraisal. My measurements, the only true things anyone had ever said about Hollowmere House, were deemed unnecessary.

I framed a copy anyway. Some jobs you do for the client. Some you do for the record. And some you do because somewhere under the floorboards of reality there's a family who deserves to have their dimensions written down correctly, once, by somebody.

* * *

But the report was never the point. Delia knew that before I did.

She was behind the bar at the Fen & Anchor when I came in, wiping glasses that didn't need wiping, which is barkeep for *I've been standing here working up my nerve.* Theo sat in the corner with a soda he wasn't drinking. Two other people had come at my request—Marion Voss, seventy-something, heir to the Voss land holdings that had swallowed the Merrick acreage in '31; and old Peter Halloran, whose grandfather had signed the petition that declared the Merricks "a nuisance to orderly settlement," which is bureaucratic for *we are going to burn your house with you in it.*

Nobody spoke for a long time. The pub smelled like beer and old wood, and outside the window Ashen Fen went about its morning, polite in the way people are polite around an open grave—which, as of last night, it technically was.

"The song," Delia finally said. "You know all of it now."

"All five verses."

"And you're going to teach it."

"I'm going to *require* it," I said. "That's different."

Marion Voss bristled. "You can't require—"

"Your great-grandmother signed a petition to erase a family," I said, "and the erasure worked so well it started eating people. Your uncle Raymond walked into the east wing in 1974 and he's still in there—or he was until last night, standing in a kept room, smiling, hollow as a gourd. Did you ever wonder why nobody talks about Raymond? Nobody talks about Raymond because *that's how it feeds.* Forgetting is the food. This town has been a feeding trough for ninety years and every single one of you kept the trough full by being polite."

Silence. Peter Halloran's hand shook around his glass.

"So here's what happens," I went on, and I was surprised at how steady my voice was, how much it sounded like someone who trusts measurements more than memories, which is what I'd always claimed to be. "On the first of next month, at the ruin, the whole town gathers. Every family that holds Merrick land, Merrick money, or Merrick silence. And we read the names. All of them. Thomas Merrick. Edith Merrick. The sons. The daughter who died in the fire, whose name I recovered from a parish fragment in the county archive basement, filed under the wrong year because even God's paperwork got falsified. We read them aloud. And then we sing the song—all five verses, including the one this town spent a century pretending didn't exist. Not to summon anything. There's nothing left to summon. We sing it because testimony is the only memorial that can't be expunged, and because the opposite of forgetting isn't remembering—it's *saying it out loud where children can hear.*"

"And if we refuse?" Marion asked.

I set the framed copy of my rejected appraisal on the bar.

"Then you refuse," I said. "It's a free country. But I'll file the full history with the county, the state archive, and three newspapers, with documentation, and Ashen Fen can spend the next hundred years being famous instead of haunted. Your call. I'm told I'm very thorough."

Delia looked at the frame for a long moment. Then she reached under the bar and brought out a bottle of whiskey older than both of us, poured five glasses, and pushed one toward each of us.

"To the Merricks," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it. "And to being done being polite."

We drank. Even Marion. Especially Marion, I think, though she'd deny it.

* * *

The gathering happened on the first of the month, under a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain. Four hundred people showed up, which in Ashen Fen is nearly everyone, drawn less by conscience than by the irresistible gravity of a spectacle—but I'll take witnesses however they arrive. Theo read the names. His voice broke on Edith's daughter, and mine broke on Wren, whom I listed last, as *companion, keeper of doors, friend,* because the record should be generous where the world wasn't.

Then we sang. Four hundred voices, ragged and uncertain, stumbling through verses everyone half-remembered and halting together at the fifth—the one nobody knew, the one that existed only in a child's memory and a ghost's keeping and now, in mine. I taught it to them that afternoon. I sang it alone first, all the way through, and then they followed, badly, beautifully, wrong in forty different keys and right in the only way that mattered.

Nothing happened. No lights, no doors, no cold. The ruin just stood there being a ruin, forty-one feet six inches of honest masonry, while a town sang a stolen lullaby back to its owners.

Afterward, an old woman I didn't know took my hand and said, "My mother used to hum that. She never knew where it came from." And that, I think, is the whole story of Ashen Fen in one sentence: the song survived the theft, passed mouth to mouth like contraband, waiting a century for someone to ask it where it lived.

* * *

I stayed one more night in town. I told myself it was to finalize paperwork.

At dusk I walked out to Hollowmere one last time and sat on the porch steps, in the spot, and watched the light go long and gold across the fen. Nothing waited for me. No lantern, no small figure, no humming in the reeds. The absence sat beside me on the step, quiet and complete, shaped exactly like a friend.

I counted the doors of the ruin out of habit. Ten. Exactly ten. Correctly measured, correctly counted, a house that had finally agreed to be only what it was.

"Good game," I said to nobody, and meant it, and got up, and left.

In my car, the laser measure sat on the passenger seat, and for one absurd moment I thought about pointing it at the empty air beside me, just to check. I didn't. Some numbers you don't need. Some absences you trust.

I drove out of the valley as the first star came up, carrying five verses in my chest like documents that could never be expunged, and behind me Ashen Fen shrank in the mirror until it was just a smudge of dark against darker—smaller than its grief, smaller than its guilt, and from tonight on, at last, exactly the size of itself.
