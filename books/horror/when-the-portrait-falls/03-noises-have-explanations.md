## 3. Noises Have Explanations

The cottage came furnished with a kettle, two mismatched armchairs, and a smell of old lavender that no amount of open windows could evict. It also came, I discovered on my first night, with a soundtrack.

I was unpacking books at eleven forty when the humming started.

It came through the wall behind the kitchen — low, patient, more felt than heard, like a wasp trapped in the plaster. Not tuneless, either. That was the thing that stopped my hand halfway into the box. It had *shape*. A melody, rising and falling in a way that suggested someone, somewhere, knew exactly what they were doing.

I stood very still in my new living room, holding a paperback of *Wuthering Heights* like a weapon, and listened.

Then I did what I always do. What I've done for three years, ever since Dr. Ferro taught me the trick in a beige office overlooking a car park in Leeds. I said it out loud, because thoughts can lie to you, but your own voice is harder to fool.

"Explanation one," I said. "Pipes."

Old houses have pipes. Old pipes sing. Water pressure drops at night when the mains quiet down, and air pockets in the system produce vibrations that the human ear, being an eager and stupid instrument, translates into patterns. People hear voices in white noise. It's called pareidolia. The brain is a pattern-matching machine that would rather find a face in the wallpaper than admit the wallpaper is just wallpaper.

The humming continued, unbothered by my lecture. If anything, it seemed to settle in, the way a cat settles when you start talking to it.

"Explanation two," I said, setting the book down. "Wind."

Hollowmere sat in a fold of hills that funneled weather the way a bottle necks sound. Corvin Blake had mentioned it during my interview — actually, he'd mentioned almost nothing during my interview, but he had said the word "draughts" twice, which for a headmaster is practically a sonnet about local climate. Wind through a chimney flue, through a gap in the eaves, across the top of an empty chimney pot — all of it produces tones. Aeolian tones. There's a whole phenomenon named after the god of wind, which means enough people have heard their houses sing that we gave it a classical name rather than calling it what it sounds like, which is *someone humming in your walls*.

I walked to the kitchen wall and pressed my ear against the cold plaster.

The humming was louder there. And underneath it — this took me a moment, standing there with my heart doing an unprofessional little quickstep — there was something else. A second voice. Higher. Thinner. Like a child humming along with an adult, slightly behind the melody, the way children do when they're learning.

"Explanation three," I said, and my voice cracked on the number, so I cleared my throat and tried again. "Explanation three: mice."

Mice don't hum. I knew that even as I said it. But the list isn't about accuracy; the list is about *motion*. Dr. Ferro's rule: anxiety feeds on stillness. Give it a task. Count the causes. Name the fears out loud and they shrink to inventory. *You are not hearing a ghost, you are experiencing a sensory event with a probable physical origin, and you will now enumerate those origins in descending order of likelihood.*

It had gotten me through three years since the incident I do not talk about, through nights in three different flats where the floorboards spoke and the radiators knocked out Morse code and once — in Manchester — the smoke alarm chirped in a rhythm so precisely like a heartbeat that I'd slept with a coat over it for a week.

The list works. That's the thing people don't understand about denial, when they bother to think about it at all. Denial isn't weakness. Done properly, it's a discipline. A craft. You build it brick by brick, explanation by explanation, and after a while you can live inside it the way other people live inside houses.

I finished the tea I'd forgotten I'd made — cold, but rules are rules — and went upstairs, and the humming followed me up through the floorboards like a polite escort, and I told myself it was resonance traveling through the joists, and I slept, eventually, with the lamp on, which was fine, because lamps are for reading, and I am a reader.

* * *

The second night, the humming had learned a new trick.

It waited until I was nearly asleep. Then it stopped — completely, all at once, the way music stops when the radio is switched off rather than faded — and in the silence that followed, from the landing outside my bedroom door, came a single clear note. Held. Sweet. High.

A child's note.

I lay in bed and stared at the ceiling and my whole body performed its full repertoire: scalp tightening, breath going shallow, the animal certainty that something stood six feet away beyond a hollow-core door. Every horror film I'd ever half-watched over a friend's shoulder queued up to offer suggestions.

"Explanation one," I whispered, because the list works better quietly at night, like a prayer. "Foxes."

Foxes scream. Everyone who lives anywhere near countryside knows foxes scream, and everyone who hears one at 2 a.m. briefly believes a woman is being murdered in the hedge, and every morning the hedge is empty and the fox has moved on. Foxes make sounds that mimic human distress because evolution doesn't care about our comfort. A fox could absolutely make a sustained high note.

Could a fox hold a single pitch, perfectly steady, for eleven seconds? I counted. Eleven seconds. Could a fox then stop, pause for exactly as long as a breath might last, and repeat the note a semitone lower?

Because it did that. Twice more. Three notes, descending, deliberate, spaced like the opening of something. Like the first three notes of a song deciding whether to continue.

"Explanation two," I said, sitting up now, lamp on, voice steadier than I felt, which I counted as a professional achievement. "Owls. Tawny owls have a wide repertoire. Explanation three: the cottage settling. Thermal contraction. Timber shrinks at night when the temperature drops, and wood under stress emits—"

Wood does not emit pitched notes in semitones. Wood creaks. Creaks are arrhythmic, graceless, random. This was not random. This was *composed*.

I got up — because motion, motion is the other half of the Ferro method — and opened the bedroom door.

The landing was empty. Moonlight through the small window laid a gray parallelogram across the floorboards, and dust hung in it, undisturbed. No fox. No owl. No child. Just the cottage breathing its lavender breath, and below me, faintly, the humming resumed in the kitchen wall, picking up exactly where it had left off, as if it had only paused to see whether I'd come out.

I stood on the landing for a long moment, hugging my cardigan around myself, and I addressed the house directly, because at 2 a.m. dignity is a luxury.

"I'm a teacher," I told it. "I have a PGCE and a safeguarding certificate and a reference from a headmaster who cried at my leaving do, and I am not going to be driven out of a perfectly adequate cottage by plumbing."

The humming, I could have sworn, warmed by a degree. Almost approving. Almost *pleased*.

I went back to bed. I did not sleep. In the morning I made a list — an actual written list, on the back of a lesson-planning sheet — titled CAUSES, and I filled it in with the neat handwriting I use for things I need to believe:

1. Pipes/airlocks
2. Wind (aeolian)
3. Rodents (unlikely — pitch too controlled)
4. Neighboring property noise carrying
5. Settling timber
6. [left blank]

I looked at the blank sixth line for a while. Then I drew a line under the list, wrote *investigate sources systematically*, and felt better, because a problem with a plan attached is just a project, and I am extremely good at projects.

* * *

On Thursday I asked Wren Okafor, casually, over the marking I pretend to do in the staff room, whether the cottages near the school had any... issues. Damp. Pests. Acoustic oddities.

Wren looked up from a dog-eared paperback with the particular stillness I'd already learned to read as significant. Wren had a way of going quiet that wasn't absence — it was compression, like a spring loading.

"Acoustic oddities," Wren repeated.

"Humming," I said. "In the walls. My cottage. Probably pipes, but I like to rule things out."

Something crossed Wren's face — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone checking whether the water is safe before telling you what's swimming in it.

"The singing class used to practice," Wren said. "Before." And then, when I waited: "You'll want to check your loft insulation. Old places carry sound weird."

That was all. Wren went back to the book. But I noticed Wren didn't say *there's nothing there*, and I noticed that the phrase *the singing class* had been delivered in the same flat, careful tone the rest of the staff used whenever anything older than last term came up — the tone of people reciting lines they'd agreed upon.

I decided not to think about it. I was getting quite good at deciding not to think about things. It's a transferable skill.

* * *

Friday night, the third night, I brought reinforcements: a torch, a screwdriver, and a notebook, because if the house wanted a dialogue, I would document it. Rational people investigate. Rational people get into lofts.

The humming began at eleven fifteen, punctual as a metronome, and tonight it was different again. Tonight there were words.

Not clear ones. Not yet. But syllables, riding the melody, muffled by plaster and decades the way voices are muffled through a party wall — and the rhythm of them was unmistakably language. Lullaby-shaped. The kind of thing you'd sing to a child to make the dark feel furnished.

I stood in the kitchen with my notebook and my torch and I did the thing. The whole ritual. Out loud, steady, itemized.

"Explanation one: radio. Some neighbor's radio through a shared wall or a party structure." I checked. The cottage was detached. I wrote that down anyway. "Explanation two: television. Same objection. Explanation three: a mobile device left playing somewhere in the house by a previous tenant." I searched. Under the armchairs, behind the wardrobe, in the kitchen cupboards, which contained, notably, a single jar of marmalade and a tin of buttons and nothing else. No devices. "Explanation four: tinnitus. Musical ear syndrome — it's real, it's documented, the auditory cortex generates melodies in silence, especially in people who—" especially in people who spend years teaching themselves not to listen for footsteps behind them. I wrote *possible* next to it, because it was possible, because everything was possible except the thing it actually was.

The humming swelled gently, the way a singer leans into a chorus, and one syllable surfaced clean above the rest, and it sounded like *down*, or possibly *dawn*, or possibly a name.

I put the notebook down. I put my palm flat against the wall. The plaster was warm. Not heated-by-pipes warm. *Throat* warm. The warmth of something mid-song.

And here is the part I have not told anyone, the part I filed that night under a sixth line I never wrote: I did not pull my hand away. For three or four seconds — I counted later, obsessively, the way you count the seconds of a car crash — I kept my hand against the warm plaster and I *listened*, and some small traitorous chamber of my heart recognized the melody. Not remembered. Recognized. The way you recognize a face in a crowd before you know whose it is. As though I'd heard it before somewhere safe, somewhere soft, somewhere far back before lists and lamps and locked doors.

Then I pulled away, and I laughed — one sharp bark of a laugh, alone in a kitchen at midnight, the laugh of a person renegotiating terms with themselves — and I said, clearly, to the wall, to the house, to whatever staffed its night shift:

"You're pipes. You're wind. You are a draught with delusions, and I have a full timetable on Monday."

The humming stopped.

And in the ringing silence, from directly overhead — from the empty loft above my bedroom — came a single child's voice, clear as a bell in clean air, finishing the phrase the wall had left hanging. Four notes. Descending. Patient.

Waiting, I understood with a clarity that had nothing rational in it whatsoever, for me to sing the next one.

I did not sleep that night either. But I wrote one more entry in the notebook before dawn, in handwriting less neat than the first page: *Tomorrow: school records. If this house has a history, it'll be in filing cabinets, and filing cabinets don't hum.*

Below it, without letting myself think, I added: *Ask about the class photo. Ask about 1971.*

The ink dried on that last line while the light came up gray over the hills, and somewhere beneath the floorboards, faint and content, the humming carried on — as if it had all the time in the world, and knew, better than I did, how much of mine was left.
