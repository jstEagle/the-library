## 5. One Second Late

The humming stopped on a Tuesday, which I took as a good sign. Things that stop are things that end. That's the kind of arithmetic I'd been doing for three years — the careful, desperate bookkeeping of a person who has decided that the world is explainable and intends to audit it into submission.

*The pipes settle when the temperature drops,* I told myself in October. *Old buildings talk. It's called thermal expansion.* I said it out loud, actually, standing in my kitchen at two in the morning with a mug of cold tea, addressing the wall like a colleague. "Thermal expansion," I informed it. The wall did not reply, which I counted as agreement.

That was the habit Corvin Blake would have called denial if he'd known about it, and which I preferred to call *methodology*. Every unexplained thing got catalogued, cross-referenced, and filed under a rational heading before it could grow teeth. Drafts were drafts. Scratches on window glass were branches. The empty desk in Year Nine was administrative error. Wren Okafor's notebook of eleven names was grief looking for a shape.

I was very good at the filing. I want that on the record. Three years of practice since — well, since the thing I don't file, the drawer I keep locked even from myself — and I could explain away almost anything.

Almost.

It started small, the way these things do. Small enough that I nearly filed it and moved on, and I have spent every night since grateful to whatever stubborn animal part of me refused.

I was brushing my teeth. Ordinary Tuesday evening, ordinary bathroom with its water-stained ceiling and its mirror above the sink, a rectangle of glass in a wooden frame that had come with the cottage, along with the damp and the door that swelled shut in rain. I was brushing my teeth and thinking about lesson plans — *Macbeth*, Year Nine, the bit where Lady Macbeth can't wash her hands clean, which felt thematically on-the-nose even for Hollowmere — and I spat, rinsed, and reached up to wipe fog from the glass with the heel of my hand.

And my reflection wiped the fog a moment after I did.

Not with me. After.

I stood there with my hand still raised, watching my own hand hang in the glass like something pinned. Then the reflection lowered its hand. A beat late. Half a second, maybe less. The kind of delay you'd blame on a video call, on lag, on tired eyes.

"Right," I said aloud, because saying things aloud is step one of the methodology. Naming the phenomenon strips it of atmosphere. "You're exhausted. You've been sleeping badly because of the humming, which is pipes, and you've been reading eleven names in a child's notebook before bed, which is nobody's idea of restful."

My reflection mouthed the words back at me. Late. Always late, now that I looked. My lips finished moving and then its lips finished moving, a shadow chasing its owner across a gap too thin to measure and impossible to ignore once seen.

I did what any reasonable person does. I performed an experiment.

I raised my right hand. The reflection raised its right hand — after. I put it down. It followed, tardy. I waved. It waved back like someone answering across a wide street, a full breath behind.

"That's not how mirrors work," I told it. My voice sounded fine. Steady, even. Inside, something was doing the cold arithmetic: *mirrors reflect at the speed of light; light does not lag; there is no mechanism by which—*

I stopped the thought there, because the thought had no destination except the one I refused to visit.

*Condensation,* I filed instead. *Your brain is pattern-matching on a fogged surface. Sleep deprivation. Visual processing delay. There are documented cases of—*

There are not, as it happens, documented cases of this. I checked. Later, at three a.m., wrapped in a blanket with my laptop balanced on my knees, I searched until the search results circled back on themselves like something eating its own tail. Nothing. No forum posts, no optometry articles, no "why does my reflection lag." The internet, that great landfill of human experience where people document their dreams and their digestive complaints and their theories about pigeons, had nothing.

Which meant either I was uniquely mad — possible, the locked drawer in my head suggested, *more than possible* — or whatever this was didn't happen to other people, because other people weren't here.

At Hollowmere. Where a class had vanished. Where a photo's faces had been scratched out by hands unknown. Where an entire staff went quiet as held breath whenever the year 1971 came within a hundred miles of conversation.

I closed the laptop. I made tea I didn't drink. And I told myself, in the firm tone I used on Year Nine when they were testing me, that tomorrow I would feel silly about all of this.

* * *

Tomorrow I did not feel silly about all of this.

Because in the morning, the hallway mirror did it too.

The hallway mirror is a tall oval thing at the end of the corridor, positioned so that you catch yourself on your way to the front door — a design choice from some earlier decade when people apparently liked being reminded of themselves before facing the world. I passed it every day without a glance. On Wednesday morning, bag over shoulder, coat half-buttoned, I glanced.

The woman in the mirror was buttoning her coat.

I had finished buttoning mine two steps ago.

I stopped walking. She kept going — finished the last button, smoothed the lapels, and only then looked up and met my eyes with my own eyes, and I swear to you, I swear on every rational bone in my body, she looked *startled*. Like she hadn't expected me to catch up. Like we were two people briefly occupying the same life at slightly different times, and she'd been enjoying the privacy.

"No," I said.

She said it a heartbeat later. I watched her mouth form the word — watched my own mouth do it without my permission, delayed, doubled, wrong — and the sight of it went through me like cold water through cloth. Not fear exactly. Fear has heat in it, adrenaline, the body's old fire alarm. This was colder than fear. This was the specific chill of a fact rearranging itself in front of you, of furniture moving in a room you thought you knew.

I walked to school with my collar up and my mind running the methodology at full sprint. *Trick of the light. Peripheral vision. You saw yourself in peripheral vision, which processes slower — that's real, that's documented, motion in the periphery lags—*

But I'd looked directly. Dead center. Both eyes. And she had buttoned her coat after I buttoned mine, and she had looked *up*.

By first period I had constructed a beautiful theory involving double-glazing and refraction angles, and I delivered it internally with such conviction that I almost believed it, and then Wren Okafor raised their hand and asked, in that flat, careful way of theirs, "Miss — sorry, sir — Emrys. Do you ever feel like you're being *watched*? By, like. Flat things?"

The class laughed. Wren didn't.

"Flat things," I repeated.

"Mirrors," Wren said. "Windows. Screens. Anything that shows you back." They were looking at the empty desk — third row, by the window, the one nobody sat in and nobody mentioned, the one I'd officially logged as *unassigned pending enrollment review* — and their voice dropped to almost nothing. "My gran says you shouldn't look at yourself too long. She says the looking goes both ways."

"Wonderful folk wisdom," I said briskly. "Page twelve, everyone. Macbeth's having a very bad night."

But my skin had gone tight all over, and for the rest of the lesson I kept catching myself checking the dark rectangle of the classroom window, where thirty faces looked back at me and none of them were late.

* * *

That evening I did the thing I should have done immediately and had been avoiding, because avoidance is also a methodology, just a dishonest one.

I counted the mirrors.

Cottage inventory, conducted room by room with a torch and a notebook, because if I was losing my mind I intended to lose it in an organized fashion:

Bathroom, above the sink. One.
Hallway, the oval. Two.
Bedroom wardrobe doors, mirrored panels. Three.
A hand mirror in the dresser drawer, silver-backed, older than everything else in the house, engraved with initials I couldn't make out. Four.
The dark kitchen window at night. Five, if windows counted.
The television, off. Six, if screens counted.

Six surfaces that showed me back. I stood in the middle of my sitting room and tested each one in turn, and this is the part I have never managed to file, the entry that broke the ledger:

They were all late. But not equally.

The bathroom mirror lagged by half a second. The hallway oval by a little more. The wardrobe doors by a full second — I timed it against my watch, raised my hand, counted the beat. And the hand mirror, the old silver one with the worn initials, was worst of all. In that one, my reflection was nearly two seconds behind, moving through its own private version of my evening like a woman watching her life through thick glass and deep water.

And here is the detail that turned my blood to well-water: the further the lag, the more *wrong* the reflection looked. Not distorted. Not warped. Just... slightly elsewhere. Slightly *other*. The bathroom-me was recognizably, boringly me. But the hand-mirror-me tilted her head when I hadn't yet decided to tilt mine. Paused when I hadn't paused. Once — once — she smiled, briefly, at nothing, while I was still frowning at her.

I set the hand mirror face-down on the dresser. My hands were shaking, and I noted that too, clinically, the way you'd note weather. *Subject experiencing tachycardia and tremor. Subject's rational framework under structural stress.*

Then I sat on the edge of my bed, in a room flanked by mirrored wardrobe doors showing me a half-second in the past on both sides, and I finally opened the locked drawer.

Not the real one. The one in my head. The one with the thing in it I never look at, the reason I came to a village at the end of nowhere to teach children about a play where everyone dies of pretending. The reason the methodology exists at all.

Three years ago I lost someone, and the losing was sudden and senseless and left me with a superstition I've never said aloud: that the world is thinner in places, that some rooms hold more than air, that grief had shown me a glimpse behind the wallpaper and I'd spent every day since nailing the wallpaper down. The humming in the walls. The scratched-out faces. Eleven names in a notebook. The desk by the window that no one will claim.

And now my own face, arriving late to its own life.

"You're doing it again," I said to the wardrobe reflections, both of them, my twin sentinels half a second in the past. "You're connecting things because connection feels like control. Coincidence isn't conspiracy. A lagging reflection is an optical—"

The reflection on the left raised its hand.

I had not raised mine.

It held the hand up — my hand, my chipped nail polish, the scar on my thumb from a tin of soup — and it beckoned. Slowly. Twice. The way you'd beckon a child across a road. The way you'd beckon someone toward you, toward the glass, toward—

I was off the bed and out of the bedroom before I'd decided to move, heart slamming, and I stood in the hallway with the lights all on, breathing like I'd run miles, staring back through the doorway at the wardrobe doors where my reflections stood now, both of them, hands down, innocent, synchronized, *late*.

The house hummed. Just once. Low, under the floorboards, a single sustained note like the beginning of a song.

And it was not pipes. I knew it was not pipes the way you know your own name. It was a note. Notes belong to melodies, and melodies belong to songs, and somewhere in this cottage or under it or behind its six lying surfaces, something knew a song and wanted company.

I did not sleep. I sat in the kitchen with every light burning and the hand mirror face-down under a stack of books, and I drank tea gone cold and bitter, and I wrote in my notebook until the page filled:

*Mirrors lag. Lag increases with age of mirror (?) or proximity (?). Reflections act independently at least once (beckoning). Humming = musical note, not mechanical. 1971. Eleven names. Empty desk. Staff silence. Song.*

Underneath, my hand moving on its own, smaller:

*If the looking goes both ways, what have I been letting look at me?*

And underneath that, in letters pressed so hard they tore the paper:

*I need to know what happened to those children.*

Dawn came gray and thin. In the bathroom, toothbrush in hand, I met my own eyes in the mirror — half a second behind me, always half a second, my tardy twin, my shadow on a delay — and I did not look away, and neither did it, and between us hung the understanding I could no longer file, folder, or explain:

Something was coming through. Or I was being pulled toward. Either way, the glass between us was getting thinner, and the song under the floorboards was patient, and I had exactly one lead and it was a music syllabus from 1971 locked in a school archive that Corvin Blake would sooner burn than open.

I spat, rinsed, and raised my hand to wipe the fog from the glass.

My reflection raised its hand first.

Just once. Just early. Just enough.

And smiled with all my teeth.
