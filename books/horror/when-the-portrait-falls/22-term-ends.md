## 22. Term Ends

The thing nobody tells you about miracles is how boring the paperwork is afterward.

I'd imagined — in the weeks after the mirrors came down, in the weeks after Emrys came back — that life would feel different. That I'd walk through Hollowmere's corridors and feel the walls listening. That every pane of glass would hum a warning. Instead it was June, and the radiators clanked even though they were off, and Mr. Adeyemi was still assigning homework like nothing had ever crawled out of a reflection, and the only genuinely strange thing left in the world was sitting across from me at a café table in the village, stirring tea with a spoon that cast no shadow of itself in the cup.

"You're staring," Emrys said, without looking up.

"I'm not."

"You've been watching my teaspoon for four minutes. If you're waiting for it to do something, it won't. It's a spoon. It has no ambitions."

"It doesn't reflect," I said. "Nothing of yours reflects. You can't just — narrate past it."

Emrys set the spoon down and looked at me then, and their eyes were the same as always: tired, dry-humored, a little too old for their face. Warm. Real warm, the kind you could put your hand near and feel.

"Wren," they said gently, "I've had six weeks to make peace with it. You're allowed more time than that. But you have to stop checking whether I'm real every time we meet. It's exhausting for both of us, and frankly it makes the waiters nervous."

I looked at the window instead. The morning sun lay on the table, on Emrys's hands, on the sugar bowl — and where a person should have been doubled in the glass, there was just the street behind them. A bus stop. A dog tied to a railing. The gap where my teacher should have been, shaped exactly like my teacher, filled with nothing but the world continuing on without them.

It didn't matter how many times I saw it. My brain kept trying to fill the hole in, kept insisting that people *reflect*, that this was a rule, and rules don't bend for one stubborn English teacher who refused to sing a song when singing it would have saved them.

Refused to sing it, and gave it away instead.

That was the part I kept circling back to, six weeks on. Not the shattering — though I'd heard it, felt it, all eleven panes of the great mirror going at once like a held breath finally released. Not the children walking out of the broken glass into the grey light of that morning, eleven of them, blinking, suddenly *aging* as they crossed the grass — hair silvering, faces softening, decades arriving all at once like mail that had been held at the depot. Not even Idris, who had pressed his hand briefly to my shoulder before he went, and whose touch felt like being remembered by someone who had almost forgotten himself.

It was the choice. Emrys had known — had worked out, somewhere in those backward days inside the glass, with their name carved into windowsills over and over to keep from dissolving — that the last verse wasn't a key you used. It was a gift you gave. And gifts cost. And they paid without haggling.

"Term ends Friday," Emrys said now, turning their teacup. "Last staff meeting was today. Corvin resigned, incidentally."

I nearly choked. "He what?"

"Handed in his notice. Cited 'personal reasons.' Which is technically true — his personal reason being that he couldn't stand to look at the 1971 photograph anymore." Emrys's voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, tectonic and slow. "He came to find me before he left. Stood in my doorway for a full minute. Then he said, 'You could have taken the deal,' and I said, 'Yes,' and he said, 'Why didn't you?'"

"What did you tell him?"

"Same thing I'll tell you if you ever ask properly." They smiled, small and crooked. "I said: because fifty years of quiet isn't survival, Corvin. It's just a longer way of disappearing. He didn't argue. He shook my hand. His hand was shaking worse than mine." A pause. "Then he asked me to water his office plant over the summer, which I think was his version of an apology. I said yes. The plant deserves better than him anyway."

I laughed — really laughed, the first time in weeks it hadn't felt like borrowing someone else's laugh — and outside the window, in the glass, the dog at the bus stop barked at nothing, and the street went on being ordinary, and the empty space where Emrys sat stayed empty and stayed *them* all the same.

* * *

We walked up to see Agnes Vane on the last Thursday of term.

Emrys had been three times already. I knew because I'd gone once alone, early on, and been turned away at the door of the narrow stone cottage by a woman with ninety-four years of iron in her spine, who had looked at me and said, "If you've come about the song, the answer is no, and if you've come about anything else, the answer is also no, but slower." So I'd retreated, and let Emrys do whatever slow, patient work they were doing, and waited.

This time she opened the door before we knocked.

"I saw you coming," she said, which was impossible, because her front windows faced a hedge. She looked at Emrys a long moment — at the place where a reflection should have stood beside them on her doorstep and didn't — and something in her face did what old stone does in rain: not crumbling, exactly. Softening.

"Well," she said. "Come in, then. Both of you. Before the whole village sees me being hospitable and starts rumors."

Her cottage smelled of lavender and coal smoke and, underneath it, very faintly, of dust on piano strings. Photographs covered every wall — school photos, mostly, decades of them, classes arranged in rows on the Hollowmere steps. Her eye had been good; even in the oldest ones you could pick out the children who would grow up to be somebody. On the mantel, in pride of place, stood a framed photo of the 1971 Year Nine class, and I felt my stomach drop before I understood why it looked wrong.

The faces weren't scratched out.

They were whole. Eleven of them, squinting into a sun that had set half a century ago, grinning the way kids grin when the photographer has just told a joke and they haven't yet learned to be embarrassed about joy. Idris was in the middle row, second from the left, mid-laugh, caught forever at fourteen and finally allowed to stay there honestly, as a picture, instead of dishonestly, as a prisoner.

Agnes watched me looking at it.

"The school's copy repaired itself," she said. "Three days after the mirrors broke. Corvin rang me about it — first time he'd rung me in nine years. He thought I should know." Her mouth twisted. "As if I could have missed it. As if I hadn't been waiting fifty years for those faces to come back."

She made tea we didn't want and made us drink it anyway, and then she sat down across from us, folded her hands, and was silent for so long that the clock on the mantel got embarrassed and ticked louder to cover for her.

"I tore the page out," she said at last, to no one, to the room, maybe to the photo. "May of 1971. I'd taught that lullaby for years — my mother taught it to me, and hers to her, God help us all, none of us knowing what we were passing down like a recipe. And that spring the music mistress came to me white as milk and said the Year Nines were singing it in the dormitories after lights-out, harmonizing, teaching it to the little ones, and that when she asked them where they'd learned the ending, they said there wasn't one yet, but it was coming, they could *feel* it coming, like a tooth coming loose." Agnes's hands tightened around each other. "So I took the manuscript out of the archive and I tore out the final page and I burned it in the boiler, and I forbade the song, and I told myself silence would starve it."

"And it didn't," Emrys said quietly. Not a question.

"No. It didn't. Because a song cut off mid-verse doesn't die. It *waits*. It hung there over that classroom like a held note for thirty years, and then the children—" Her voice did something I'd never heard a voice do: it aged backward. For a moment she sounded fourteen herself, caught mid-laugh, mid-mistake. "The children finished it themselves. In the dark. Without knowing. And the glass took them, and I knew — I *knew* what I'd done, leaving them stranded halfway through a door — and I told no one. Fifty years I told no one. I told myself it was mercy. Forgetting is mercy, isn't that what we all said?" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and free, all three at once. "It wasn't mercy. It was cowardice wearing mercy's coat."

The clock ticked. Outside, a blackbird sang something with no verses at all, thank God.

"I'm not asking for forgiveness," Agnes said. "I'm telling you what happened so it exists outside my head. That's all forgiveness ever needed from me, I think. Witnesses."

And then Emrys did the thing I will remember until I die, which given recent events is a meaningful commitment: they reached across the table and took her hands, and said, "Mrs. Vane. The verse you kept. The one you never wrote down, never spoke, never sang — you carried it alone for fifty years so it couldn't hurt anyone else. It nearly killed you. I know. I carried it for three days and it nearly killed me too." Their thumb moved over her knuckles, gentle. "But here's what you couldn't know from inside your guilt: the verse was never meant to be kept or sung. It was meant to be *given*. Freely. To someone who chooses to let go. You couldn't give it, because giving requires trusting someone to receive it — and you trusted no one, least of all yourself. That's not cowardice. That's just loneliness with a heavy load."

Agnes Vane, who had turned away a grieving teenager at her door with iron courtesy, who had outlasted headmasters and inspectors and half the village's funerals, looked down at their joined hands and began, very quietly, to weep.

Not elegantly. Not the way people cry in films, one tasteful tear. She wept the way a wall comes down — in sections, groaning, taking years of scaffolding with it. And I sat there holding my cooling tea and understood that some griefs are fifty years long not because the feeling is that big, but because no one ever opened the door wide enough for it to leave through.

When she finished — a long while later, the tea stone cold, the blackbird gone hoarse — she wiped her face with a handkerchief pressed flat as a hymnal, and said, "Right. Well." And then, to Emrys, with the ghost of her old headmistress authority reassembling itself around her like armor she no longer needed but liked the weight of:

"You'll come back next week. And the week after. I've fifty years of stories about that wretched school and apparently I'm done keeping them, and you two are going to sit there and listen whether you like it or not."

"We wouldn't miss it," Emrys said.

And in the dark window behind her armchair, where the lamplight should have shown two visitors and an old woman, there showed only one visitor's shape and an old woman's, and the empty space between them glowed softly with reflected firelight, and for the first time since I'd learned what reflections could hold, the emptiness didn't frighten me at all.

* * *

Term ended on a Friday of unreasonable sunshine, the kind of day Hollowmere usually rations out one per decade.

The last bell rang at three-fifteen, and the corridors emptied in the usual stampede of bags and shrieking, and I stood by the doors with Emrys watching the kids pour out into summer. Somewhere among them, a group of Year Sevens were singing something tuneless and triumphant — a pop song, mercifully, nothing with cradles in it — and Emrys flinched at the first bar and then made themselves stop flinching, and I pretended not to notice, because that's what family is sometimes: agreeing not to notice the flinch.

"They'll be all right," Emrys said. "The school, I mean. Without him. Without it."

"How do you know?"

"Because the photo in the corridor shows eleven smiling faces now, and not one person remembers scratching them out, and not one person will ever need to." They shrugged. "Truth doesn't need everyone to carry it, Wren. It just needs enough of us that it can't be dropped."

We locked up our classrooms — mine still had the empty desk, though it wasn't empty anymore in any way that mattered; I'd put a small potted plant on it, the kind that thrives on neglect, and told my form class it belonged to a student who'd moved away, which was true, if you stretched the word *moved* generously across the surface of a shattered mirror — and we walked out through the gates together, past the flowerbeds, past the spot on the lawn where eleven ghosts had aged fifty years in eleven steps and faded into the light like breath off glass.

At the gate, Emrys stopped.

"One more errand," they said. "Before summer. There's a mirror in the village barber's shop I've been avoiding. Full-length, gilt frame, hideous thing. I've walked past it forty times and looked at the pavement every time." They exhaled. "I think it's time I looked."

"You don't have to."

"No," they agreed. "But I gave everything else away freely. Seems daft to keep holding onto the fear."

So we walked down to the barber's, and the barber was closed, Saturday hours chalked on the door, and the shop window was dark, and there in the gloom stood the gilt-framed mirror like a held breath. Emrys stepped in front of it. Straightened their shoulders. Looked.

And I watched them look at the space where they should have been — at the shop interior rendered perfectly behind and around a person-shaped absence, shelves and scissors and fading posters all present except the one thing standing right there. Emrys studied it for a long moment. Then they raised their hand, slowly, and waved — the same wave, I realized, that their reflection had given from inside the glass on the morning the mirrors broke, the goodbye that had cost them everything and bought eleven children their deaths back, their real ones, the kind you earn by growing old.

"Hello," Emrys said softly, to no one, to themselves, to the absence. "Still me."

They turned away from the mirror, and their eyes were bright, and their hand found my shoulder and squeezed once, warm and solid and utterly unreflected, and together we walked out into the last afternoon of term, two people and no reflections, into a summer that had finally, finally stopped humming.

Behind us, in the dark shop, the gilt-framed mirror stood empty and quiet.

Mostly.

Because if you had looked — and neither of us did, and neither of us ever would again — you might have seen, deep in the glass where the light doesn't quite reach, a faint shape like a person made of held breath, pressing one palm flat against the inside of the world, patient as a song cut off mid-verse.

Waiting for someone, someday, to give it away.
