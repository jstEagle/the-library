## 19. The Last Verse

The dawn came up gray and thin over Hollowmere, the color of old linen wrung dry, and Isandro stood at the edge of the excavation pit with the detonator's wires coiled at his feet like sleeping snakes.

He had not slept. None of them had. The night before had been given to cards and candlelight and Mara reading Elowen's diary in a voice soft enough to be a lullaby, and now that warmth sat in his chest like a coal he was afraid to breathe on too hard, because he knew what he had to do with it.

The report lay open on the folding table behind him. Nineteen lines of his own handwriting — or the house's handwriting wearing his hand like a glove — and beneath them, a single blank space where the twentieth line waited to be written. He had watched it all night, that blank line, the way you watch a mouth you expect to speak. It did not move. It didn't need to. They both knew what it would say.

*Surveyor's certification: complete.*

And when it said it, so would he.

"Isandro." Mara's voice, low, from the tent flap. She came out barefoot across the cold grass, her jacket over her shoulders like armor she'd forgotten how to buckle. "Bram says the charges are set. Teo's wired the perimeter. We've got maybe twenty minutes before the city crew arrives."

"I know."

"You don't have to go in there." She said it the way she'd been saying it for three days, each time with less conviction, because they both knew the truth now: the song would not stay buried under rubble. Blasting the door would only release everything unfinished at once — every verse, every hunger, two hundred years of a child's loneliness let loose across the valley like floodwater. Bram had explained it in his gravel voice, staring into the fire: *You don't drown a song, girl. You finish it or you feed it.*

"There's no other way," Isandro said.

"There's always another way. That's what I keep telling myself." Mara looked at the house. In the gray light it seemed almost gentle, its symmetry softened by mist, its windows dark as closed eyes. "Elowen tried to finish it and the house took her. My whole family has bled for this mistake. If you go in there and sing it right—"

"Then it keeps me. I know."

"Then don't sing it right."

He turned to look at her then, really look, and saw what three weeks of shared stew and shared dread had built between them — something he had no professional vocabulary for, no measurement precise enough. A family, assembled out of strangers and circumstance, held together with card tricks and bad coffee and the particular tenderness of people who have decided your survival matters to them.

"I have a plan," he said. "It's not much of one."

"It never is, with you." Her mouth twitched. Then it didn't. "Come back wrong, Isandro. Come back off-key. That's all I'm asking."

He nodded, because his throat had closed around anything larger.

* * *

The twelfth door stood inside the house, in the room that wasn't there, and it was warm.

That was the thing he could never explain to anyone who hadn't touched it — the warmth. Not the warmth of sun through glass or a stove left burning, but body-warmth, the temperature of a palm pressed flat against a fevered forehead. The iron bands were blood-heat. The wood breathed. When he laid his hand against it, he felt a pulse that was not quite his own, slightly slower, slightly deeper, patient as geology.

Behind him, the front door of Hollowmere swung shut with a sound like an exhale.

He didn't flinch. He'd stopped flinching somewhere around the time his reflection started lagging, somewhere around the time his mother's voice slipped out of his memory like water through cupped hands and he'd realized the house was taking payment in the only currency he had left. What remained of him felt strangely light. Stripped. A man reduced to his final measurements.

He thought about the ritual of counting, the armor he'd worn since childhood — counting stairs, counting breaths, counting the seconds between lightning and thunder because numbers were the only things in his boyhood that stayed where he put them. He counted now, one last time, out of habit and farewell.

One door ahead of him. One heart in his chest. One verse left to learn.

Three people on the road behind him who would rather lose him than the world.

The door opened before he pushed it. Of course it did. It had been waiting longer than he could imagine.

* * *

The room beyond was small and round and utterly without windows, and it was full of dawn even though dawn could not possibly reach it — a pale gold light rising from nowhere, from everywhere, from the walls themselves, which were not plaster but something older, packed earth shot through with threads of silver like frost in July. And in the center of the room, in a chair that might once have been a cradle grown enormous, sat the daughter.

She was a child. She was an old woman. She was both at once, the way a note held long enough becomes a chord, and when she turned her face toward him Isandro felt his knees soften — not with fear, though there was fear, but with the terrible recognition of someone who has been waiting so long that waiting has become the shape of their soul.

"You came," she said. Her voice was the lullaby. He understood that now, finally, completely — every night of humming through the floorboards, every phrase caught in static, had been this voice, worn down to melody by two centuries of having no one to talk to. "Mama said someone would come. She said it right before—" The child-face flickered; the old face surfaced; the child returned. "She said a lot of things. Most of them weren't true. But that one was. You came."

"My name is Isandro Ashgrove."

"I know." She smiled, and the smile was Elowen's smile, he realized, the same one reproduced in the diary's margins in faded ink. "I've known your name since you parked your car. I've known it since before that, maybe. The house counts everything, surveyor. You'd like that part, if it weren't happening to you."

"It is happening to me."

"Yes." No apology in it. Only honesty, which was somehow worse and better. "Are you afraid?"

He considered lying, and found he couldn't — not here, not to her, not with the last of his memories still warm in his chest. "Yes."

"Good." She rose from the chair, and she was tall suddenly, taller than him, and then small again, and the light moved with her like something alive. "Mama wasn't afraid. That was her mistake. She sang the way she did everything — perfectly, dutifully, because it was her responsibility and Vanes do their responsibility. The house heard perfection and thought: *this is what love sounds like. This is what we are owed.*" The old woman's eyes were wet. "But Mama didn't love the house. She loved me. She just couldn't tell the difference anymore, by the end. Neither could I."

"The third verse," Isandro said. "You offered to teach it freely."

"And I will. Freely means freely — no debt, no trade, no memory taken. That's my gift, and it's the only thing I own after two hundred years, so don't insult it by refusing." She stepped closer, and the warmth of her was the warmth of the door, of the walls, of the whole patient hunger of Hollowmere concentrated into one small enormous person. "But hear me, Isandro Ashgrove, because this is the part Mama never learned: the fourth verse doesn't exist. It never existed. The song was written to be finished by whoever loved the singer enough to finish it. Mama tried to force it. The house took her mid-note and has been holding that note ever since, waiting for someone brave enough or foolish enough to write the ending."

"And if I write it?"

"Then it's yours forever." The child's face again, guileless, devastating. "Or it's the house's forever. The house can't tell the difference. Can you?"

He thought of Mara saying *come back off-key*. He thought of Teo's laugh, Bram's scarred hands dealing cards, the folding table with its nineteen lines and one blank space. He thought of his mother's voice, gone now, and how he could not remember whether she had sung to him or only hummed while she worked, and how the not-knowing hurt more than the loss.

"I think," he said slowly, "that I'm about to find out."

"Then stand where Mama stood." The daughter gestured to the center of the room, to a circle of silver thread inlaid in the earthen floor, worn smooth by two hundred years of absence. "And begin."

* * *

He began with the first verse, the one the house had taught him through the joists at dusk, wordless except for that recurring phrase that had lodged in his skull like a splinter. His voice came out rough. He hadn't sung — truly sung — since childhood, since before the counting started, and his throat remembered nothing and his chest remembered everything.

The house rose through him.

There was no other way to describe it. The moment the first note left his lips, the walls drank it in and gave it back amplified, harmonized, multiplied — the packed earth thrumming, the silver threads brightening, the door behind him pulsing in time. The melody entered his bloodstream. He felt it in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the soles of his feet where they pressed the worn circle. The house was singing *with* him, through him, using his ribs as a soundboard, and the hunger in it was bottomless and old and so lonely it made his eyes burn.

*This is what it feels like,* he thought, dizzy. *To be wanted this much.*

And underneath the terror, shamefully, thrillingly, some starved part of him that had spent forty years trusting measurements because feelings had failed him — that part leaned into the hunger like a man leaning into warmth after years in the cold. The house wanted him. The house needed him. The house would hold him forever and call it love, and God help him, some cellar-deep chamber of his heart whispered: *isn't that more than anyone else ever offered?*

"No," he said aloud, mid-verse, and kept singing.

The second verse. The daughter's voice joined his, high and clear and cracked with age and youth at once, teaching him the turns of the melody, and he wrote the words silently in his mind the way he'd trained himself — listening without echoing, learning without surrendering, marking each phrase in his surveyor's precision. *Here the melody descends a third. Here it hesitates, as if asking permission. Here it repeats the first phrase but slower, sadder, the way grief revisits joy.*

His reflection shimmered in the silver-threaded wall, half a second behind, mouthing along, and he refused to look at it. Looking was agreeing. Looking was signing.

The third verse she gave him freely, weeping as she taught it, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard — a melody shaped exactly like forgiveness, rising and falling like breathing, like a mother's hand on a child's back, like the specific mercy of being told *you are not broken, you were only alone*. He felt it rearrange something in his chest. He felt his mother's absence ache inside it, and then, impossibly, feel *held* by it.

And the report behind him, on the table in the gray morning, wrote its twentieth line.

He didn't see it happen. He felt it — a soft click somewhere behind his sternum, a door unlatching, a countdown reaching its final number. Somewhere out on the road, Mara would be watching the pages settle, watching the ink dry, watching the last line of Isandro Ashgrove's life certify itself in his own stolen handwriting.

*Surveyor's certification: complete.*

The house knew it too. The whole structure shuddered with anticipation, the walls swelling inward like lungs filling, the silver threads blazing, the daughter's face turning toward him with two hundred years of hope breaking across it like a wave. The hunger rose through the floor, through his feet, through his spine, gathering itself for the final verse, the unwritten one, the ending that would make everything whole —

"Sing," the daughter whispered. "*Please.*"

Duty pulled him forward: finish the job, complete the appraisal, do the work properly, the way Vanes had done their duty and died of it. Love pulled him forward too — real love, the strange new kind, the kind made of card tricks and bad stew and a woman asking him to come back off-key; because finishing the song would save them, would bury the hunger forever, would give the daughter rest.

And selfhood — the last thin wire of himself, the man who counted because counting was the only prayer he knew — pulled him backward, screaming without sound: *if you sing it perfectly, you belong to it. If you belong to it, you were never here at all.*

Three directions. One voice. Dawn pressing gold against the windowless walls.

Isandro Ashgrove opened his mouth to sing the last verse of a two-hundred-year-old song, and the house rose through him like a tide going out, hungry for completion, certain of its prize —

— and he smiled.

Because Mara had told him what to bring back, and he had promised her, and Isandro Ashgrove, meticulous property surveyor, was very good at keeping specifications.

He drew one final breath, tasted the house's longing on it, and began the last line — already bending it, already cracking it, already reaching for the beautiful ruin of a note sung wrong on purpose.
