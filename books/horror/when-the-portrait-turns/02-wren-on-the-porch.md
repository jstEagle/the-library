## 2. Wren on the Porch

The drive out to Hollowmere took forty minutes and cost Isandro Underhill most of her composure somewhere around the twenty-minute mark, right about where the paved road gave up and became a gravel apology.

She talked to herself the whole way, which was normal. Pest controllers talked to themselves. It was a occupational hazard of working alone in other people's dark corners — you narrated, or you started hearing things that weren't narration.

"Routine call," she said, downshifting around a pothole deep enough to have its own weather system. "Structural smudging. Probable raccoons. Raccoons get into walls all the time. Raccoons are basically tiny men in masks with no respect for property lines."

The salt bag rustled in the passenger seat when she hit a bump, and she told it to shut up.

Fifteen years she'd been doing this work. Fifteen years of crawlspaces and attics and basements where the air had opinions, and in all that time she'd built herself a very sturdy toolbox of explanations. Drafts. Settling foundations. Old houses breathing through their seams the way old people breathe through their mouths. She'd gotten so good at explaining things away that she could do it in her sleep, which was fortunate, because for a long stretch of her childhood she'd needed to do it there too.

Hollowmere House was not in any of her explanations. Hollowmere House was the reason she *had* explanations.

The trees closed over the road like a hand closing over a coin. Dusk came early under them, pooling violet in the low places, and by the time the house appeared at the end of the drive — tall, gray-gabled, its windows catching the last light like eyes deciding whether to open — the sky behind her was still technically blue. Houses shouldn't have their own weather, she thought. But then, plenty of things shouldn't happen that happened anyway.

She parked at the bottom of the drive. Professional habit: never box yourself in. Also personal habit, though she'd have denied it under oath: never park so close to a house that you can't change your mind.

"Mrs. Aldous," she said, checking the work order on her phone. *Smudges at impossible heights. Please come before dark.* The client had written *before dark* twice, and underlined it once, and Isandro had decided not to think too hard about what kind of person knew to underline that.

She gathered her kit — flashlight, ladder hooks, moisture meter, the salt she'd tell Mrs. Aldous was for slugs if asked — and started up the walk. Gravel crunched. Somewhere off in the treeline, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, then thought better of it and went quiet.

That was the thing about Hollowmere, she remembered as she got closer. It wasn't loud. Loud places you could work with. Hollowmere was *attentive*. It had always felt, even as a kid, like a room full of people pretending to read.

The porch came into view. Wide, sagging gently in the middle like an old mattress, wrapped around two sides of the house. A swing hung motionless at one end despite a wind she could feel tugging her collar. And on the steps —

On the steps sat a boy.

Small. Maybe seven years old, seven the way seven is drawn by children: knobby knees, a cowlick standing up at the crown of his head like a question mark, a sweater with one elbow worn thin and gray. He was sitting with his chin in his hands, elbows on his knees, watching the driveway with the patience of someone who has been watching it for a while. Possibly a long while. Possibly, some traitorous part of her brain suggested, a very long while.

Isandro stopped walking.

Her body stopped first — boots planted, kit suddenly heavy as ballast — and her mind caught up a half-second later and immediately began filing objections. *Kid waiting for a ride. Kid from the village. Kid who belongs to somebody.* All reasonable. All fine. Except that the light was doing something wrong around him, or maybe he was doing something wrong with the light; the dusk lay across the porch like spilled ink, and he sat in it crisp-edged and unshadowed, lit from no direction she could name.

And she knew him.

That was the objection her mind couldn't file. Fifteen years of explanations, an entire filing cabinet of rational, sensible, adult reasons why children see things that aren't there, and every folder in the cabinet flew open at once, because she knew this boy the way you know your own handwriting. The cowlick. The chipped front tooth he'd gotten — supposedly — from falling out of a tree that wasn't there. The way he sat perfectly still except for his eyes, which moved constantly, cataloguing everything, missing nothing.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like a recording of herself played back too soon after recording.

The boy looked up.

He didn't smile. That was the first wrongness, the one that cracked everything else open — because the Wren of her childhood had smiled the way other kids breathed, constantly, reflexively, at moths and mud puddles and her terrible jokes. This Wren looked at her the way adults look at adults, with something old and careful and sorry underneath it, and then he said, in a voice exactly as she remembered it, high and clear and unhurried:

"You came back late."

* * *

For a moment neither of them moved. A leaf detached itself from the gutter above the porch, spiraled down between them, and landed without a sound, which leaves should not be able to do. Isandro noted this the way she noted everything now: automatically, professionally, filing it under *later, deal with later.*

"I'm not—" she started, and stopped, because the sentence she'd been about to say was *I'm not back,* and that was a lie so flimsy it would have embarrassed both of them. She was back. She was standing on the gravel with a bag of ritual salt disguised as slug deterrent and fifteen years of carefully maintained denial, and she was back.

"I'm here for work," she said instead. "Pest control. There's a client inside. Mrs. Aldous."

"I know." Wren unfolded himself from the steps and stood, and up close he was exactly knee-height-to-her-elbow, exactly as he'd been when she was nine, which meant he hadn't grown, which meant — she slammed that door shut. He tilted his head at her toolkit. "You brought salt."

"For slugs."

"Mm," said Wren, in the tone of a person humoring a child, which was a genuinely upsetting reversal of their historical dynamic.

She crouched down to his level, mostly because standing over him felt obscene, partly because her knees were considering resigning. Up close his eyes were the same gray-brown as wet river stones, and they did not blink as often as eyes should. She counted, without meaning to. Once every forty seconds. Maybe less.

"Okay," she said, in the voice she used for spooked clients and cornered animals. Gentle, level, professional. "Let's say — hypothetically — I know you. Let's say I knew you a long time ago. Kids make up friends. It's documented. It's developmentally normal. Imaginary friends are a sign of a healthy, creative—"

"You're doing the thing," Wren said.

"What thing?"

"The list thing." He held up small fingers, counting. "Drafts. Raccoons. Carbon monoxide. That one year you blamed the neighbor's radio tower for the voices in the wall, which was very creative, I liked that one." His mouth twitched — almost a smile, almost the old smile, but weighted at the corners like something sunk in water. "You've been doing it since you were little. Explaining me away. You got very good at it."

Heat crawled up Isandro's neck. Not embarrassment, exactly. Something closer to being caught mid-sentence in a conversation she'd been having alone for fifteen years.

"That's different," she said. "That was — look, a nine-year-old hears humming in the walls, the responsible thing is to find a *reason.* My mother—"

She stopped.

Wren watched her stop. He didn't push. He just stood there in his threadbare sweater with his cowlick sticking up, patient as furniture, letting the silence do what silence does, and Isandro found herself looking at the house instead of him because the house was easier. Tall windows. Peeling paint the color of old bone. And along the porch rail, faint in the dying light —

Smudges. Small ones. Five-fingered, climbing the white post in a slow spiral, the same marks from the work order photos, except these were fresh, and they were at the height of a child's hand, and they were *warm-looking*, somehow, the way breath fogs glass.

"They're friendly," Wren said, following her gaze. "Mostly. They want to be noticed. That's all fingerprints ever are, really. A request."

"A request for what?"

"To be touched back." He said it simply, the way he'd once explained where rain goes. "Nobody ever does. It makes them persistent."

Isandro straightened up. Her hands had gone cold inside her gloves, and she became aware that she was gripping the salt bag hard enough to feel individual grains through the paper. Somewhere behind the house's dark windows, deep inside, something hummed three notes and stopped, as if testing whether anyone was listening.

She had heard those three notes before. She had spent fifteen years not remembering where.

"Here's what's going to happen," she said, and was proud of how steady it came out. "I'm going to go inside. I'm going to talk to my client. I'm going to inspect the gallery — there's apparently a smudge situation in the gallery — and I'm going to invoice Mrs. Aldous for my time, and then I'm going to drive home, and none of this—" she gestured at Wren, at the porch, at the whole violet-dusk weight of the place, "—is going to require further discussion."

Wren considered this. Then he walked to the front door — she hadn't even registered it opening; it stood ajar now, black gap in the gray facade — and put his small hand flat against the wood, fingers spread, exactly matching one of the smudges.

"She'll show you the portraits," he said, not turning around. "She'll apologize for the mess. She'll offer you tea in a cup with a chip in it, and you'll take it to be polite, and you'll regret it, because the tea will taste like the cellar smells." Now he turned, and the last of the daylight caught his face, and for one disorienting second Isandro saw past the boy-shape to something underneath it — something vast and tired and *housed*, the way a bell is housed in a tower — and then it was gone, and he was seven again, and sad again, in the ordinary human way.

"But you'll want to see them anyway," he said. "You'll tell yourself it's just a job."

"Don't tell me what I'll do."

"You came back late," Wren said again, softly, and this time it landed differently — not an accusation, not a greeting. An accounting. As if lateness were a debt the house had been keeping, accruing interest for fifteen years, and he was only the clerk sent to mention it. "I kept them off you while you were gone. It's harder now. They know your name again."

The humming behind the windows swelled — three notes, four, five — and stopped.

Isandro stood on the gravel with her bag of salt and her flashlight and her fifteen years of excellent explanations, and understood, with the cold clarity of a woman reading her own autopsy report, that every single one of those explanations had been load-bearing. Pull one out and the whole structure came down. Drafts. Raccoons. Carbon monoxide. A radio tower. A boy on a porch who hadn't aged a day because he'd never been aging toward anything.

"Inside, then," she heard herself say, because the alternative was getting back in the truck, and some part of her — the part that had been nine, the part that had missed him with a specific and unshareable ache — had already crossed the porch.

Wren stepped aside to let her pass, and as she reached the threshold, he added, cheerfully, terribly:

"Mind the third floorboard. It remembers being stepped on."

It was going to be a long night.
