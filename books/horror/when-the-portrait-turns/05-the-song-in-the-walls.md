## 5. The Song in the Walls

Isandro had a system for sleeping in strange houses, and it had nothing to do with comfort.

Comfort was for people whose jobs ended when they clocked out. Her job followed her into the dark like a dog that wouldn't stop licking its wound, so she'd built rituals instead: boots by the door, salt line across the threshold of whatever room she'd claimed, flashlight on the nightstand with the batteries checked twice, and — this was the important one, the one she'd never admit to anyone — her back to the wall. Never facing the door. Facing the door meant watching the door all night. Back to the wall meant the door could do whatever it liked behind her, and she could pretend, in the generous dark, that it didn't.

She'd been doing this since she was nine years old. She'd told herself, for fifteen of those years, that it was just a quirk. A habit. Something raccoons had taught her.

At Hollowmere House, at 2:47 in the morning, the system failed her, because the house didn't come through the door.

It came through the walls.

* * *

The first sound was so small she mistook it for the pipes. Hollowmere was an old house, and old houses talked — she'd spent half her career explaining that to frightened clients. Pipes knocked. Joists settled. Squirrels held what sounded suspiciously like union meetings in the attic. There was always an explanation, and Isandro Underhill had made a very good living handing explanations to people like wrapped packages, neat and reassuring, while keeping the unexplained ones for herself.

But pipes don't hum.

This did. Low, threadbare, seeping up through the plaster behind her headboard the way damp seeps through stone. Not a tune she could place at first — more the *shape* of a tune, the skeleton of one, notes spaced too far apart, like someone singing across a very wide room to someone who might not answer.

She lay still. She counted her own heartbeats, which is what she did instead of panicking, and got to forty-one before the humming stopped.

Then, from somewhere deeper in the house — down the hall, maybe two rooms over — it started again. Different verse. Fuller. As if the first room had only known part of the song and this one knew more.

Isandro sat up.

"Okay," she said quietly, to the room, to herself, to the profession she'd chosen. "Okay. It's a draft. Drafts hum. That's — no. No, drafts don't hum."

She said it anyway, because saying things out loud was another ritual, and rituals were load-bearing. You didn't question the beams; you just kept putting weight on them and hoped the house stood.

The salt line at her door was intact. She checked it with the flashlight beam, crouching in her t-shirt and jeans she'd never actually changed out of, and found every grain exactly where she'd poured it. Whatever moved in this house didn't cross lines. It went *around* them. Or through them. Or it had never needed to move at all, because it was already everywhere the way water is already everywhere in a sponge.

The second verse faded. The third began above her — ceiling, then attic, then somewhere beyond the attic where houses keep their oldest secrets — and this one had words.

She couldn't make them out. But the melody slid into a shape she recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd from twenty years away: not with your mind, but with your stomach dropping first.

Her mother used to sing that.

* * *

Here is what Isandro remembered, lying on a stranger's guest bed at three in the morning with her knees pulled to her chest:

She remembered being small, and feverish, and her mother sitting on the edge of the bed in a house that was not this house — a flat above a dry cleaner's two towns over, smelling of steam and starch — singing something low and wandering, a lullaby that never seemed to end so much as pause. Her mother had a voice like a hinge that needed oiling, warm and slightly rough, and she'd sing with her eyes closed, and Isandro would close hers too and pretend the fever was a fire she was warming her hands at.

She remembered asking, once, *where did you learn that?*

And she remembered — this was the part she'd spent fifteen years not remembering, the part that surfaced now whole and cold as a coin from a drain — her mother going quiet. Not sad-quiet. Careful-quiet. The quiet of someone stepping around a hole in the floor they know is there.

"A long time ago," her mother had said. "In a big old house."

And then she'd kissed Isandro's forehead, and turned off the light, and left the hall light on, and Isandro had fallen asleep believing that was the end of it.

Three months later, her mother was gone. Not dead — gone. Coat, keys, car, a note on the kitchen table that said *I'm sorry, little bird. Don't follow me.* Isandro had been nine. Social services had asked questions. Her mother's sister had been mentioned once, vaguely, as someone "lost years ago," and then never again. Isandro had grown up in a series of foster kitchens, learning to fix things because fixing things was the only kind of love that never left without a note.

And through all of it, the lullaby had lived in her head like a splinter too deep to dig out. She'd hummed it sometimes without noticing. She'd caught herself doing it on job sites, in her truck, in the shower, and each time she'd stopped mid-note with a small cold feeling she'd filed under *grief, unresolved, see therapist eventually.*

She had never once let herself finish it.

Now the house was finishing it for her.

* * *

By 3:15, Isandro was dressed, booted, and standing in the hallway with her flashlight and a fistful of salt she was holding the way other people hold rosaries.

The corridor stretched toward the front of the house, past closed doors, and the song was traveling. That was the thing she couldn't explain away, and God knows she tried — she stood there in the dark running through the catalog, the whole well-worn inventory of fifteen years: *old heating ducts carry sound weirdly, it's a music box stuck in a wall cavity, it's a neighbor's radio, it's wind through a chimney flue, it's carbon monoxide making me auditory hallucinate, that's a real thing, that happens.* Each explanation went up like a match and burned down just as fast. Ducts don't change key between rooms. Music boxes don't know four verses. And carbon monoxide didn't explain why her chest hurt, why her eyes were stinging, why she was gripping a bag of slug deterrent like it was her mother's hand.

Because that was the truth under everything, wasn't it. The song wasn't frightening her. The song was *grieving* her. Every note was a note her mother had sung, pitched a half-step lower, sung by walls instead of a warm rough voice, and hearing it was like hearing her mother call her name from another room in a house her mother had died out of years ago.

Except her mother hadn't died. Her mother had *left*. And left a note that said don't follow me.

And now Isandro was following her. Through a house her mother had apparently once known. Toward a song her mother had clearly learned here.

"Don't follow me," Isandro muttered, walking anyway. "Yeah. Great parenting. Ten out of ten."

Humor was another ritual. Load-bearing. She leaned on it hard.

* * *

The fourth verse came from the room at the end of the west wing, and it was nearly complete — melody, harmony, words she could almost taste. It rolled through the plaster like a tide coming in, and Isandro noticed something that raised the hair on her arms despite everything: the rooms weren't competing. They were *taking turns*. Politely. One verse per room, passed along like a bucket brigade, each voice — and they were voices, plural, some thin and high, some low and cracked, none of them quite alive-sounding — picking up exactly where the last had set it down.

The house wasn't haunted. Haunted was chaotic. This was *organized*. This was a household.

A household singing itself to sleep. Or singing itself awake. She genuinely didn't know which was worse.

She passed Mrs. Aldous's door. Closed, dark, silent — the old woman either slept like the dead or lay awake listening to her family sing, and Isandro made a mental note to ask her in the morning which, and immediately dreaded both answers.

The gallery doors stood at the end of the hall, tall and paneled, and Isandro stopped ten feet short of them because the fifth verse was coming from inside, and it was the fullest yet — layered, almost choral, dozens of voices braided together, and underneath them all, faint as a watermark, a child's voice holding the melody steady. Steady and patient and terribly familiar.

"Wren," she said, before she could stop herself.

The singing didn't stop. But it *dipped*, the way a conversation dips when someone says your name in the next room, and Isandro felt that dip land directly on her sternum.

He'd been on the porch yesterday. Small, unchanged, seven years old forever, saying *you came back late* like she'd merely kept him waiting through lunch. She had spent the entire day — the inspection, the wallpaper spirals, the portraits with their politely turned faces — refusing to think about him, which had required a level of mental discipline she usually reserved for crawling under porches after possums.

Now, at three in the morning, with her mother's lullaby pouring through the walls, the refusal collapsed.

She had invented Wren when she was six. That was the story. Lonely kid, new neighborhood, imaginary friend, totally normal, outgrown by nine, end of file. Except imaginary friends didn't teach you which floorboards to avoid. Imaginary friends didn't stand between you and the closet when the humming started in your foster home's walls — different house, different town, same humming — and say, in his small serious voice, *don't sing along, Issy. Whatever you hear, don't ever sing along.*

She hadn't sung along. Not once in twenty-six years. She'd bitten the melody back so many times it had become instinct, a reflex deeper than flinching.

And now she stood in a corridor full of plaster that sang her mother's song verse by verse, and understood, with the slow terrible clarity of 3 a.m., that the reflex had never been hers. Someone had installed it. Someone small and patient had spent her entire childhood standing guard against a song, and she had thanked him by growing up and forgetting him.

"Nope," Isandro said aloud, because if she didn't say something she was going to cry, and crying in a haunted gallery corridor was a rookie mistake. "Nope. We're not doing feelings at three a.m. Feelings have business hours."

The gallery doors hummed on, gently, as if amused.

* * *

She should have gone back to bed. Every professional instinct she owned said go back to bed, document nothing, bill for the visit, and drive until the mirrors showed nothing strange. Fifteen years of survival were built on exactly that instinct.

Instead she walked to the gallery doors and put her palm flat against the wood.

The vibration came up through her hand — the song, physical, patient, enormous, like touching the flank of a sleeping animal. And beneath the many voices, close to the doors, right on the other side, the child's voice was singing the fifth verse, and Isandro realized with a jolt that he was singing it *slightly wrong*. Deliberately wrong. Dropping a note here, flattening one there, the way you'd sing if you wanted to teach someone a song while making sure they could never quite learn it.

Keeping her safe. Even now. Even through a door.

"Issy," she whispered, testing it. "You called me Issy."

The voices in the gallery went quiet all at once — every room in the house went quiet, a silence so total she could hear the salt grains settling on the floorboards behind her — and in that silence, from just beyond the doors, came a sound that undid her completely.

A small boy, humming the sixth verse. The one her mother had never sung. The one Isandro had never heard.

It sounded like a hand reaching through twenty years to take hers.

Isandro stepped back from the doors. Then she turned, walked briskly back down the corridor, retrieved her flashlight, her salt, and her notebook, and sat down on the top stair with all three arranged on her knees like instruments.

Because here was the thing about grief arriving dressed as terror: terror made you run, but grief made you *stay*. And her mother had learned this song in this house. Her mother had fled it. Her mother had left a note that said *don't follow me* — which meant her mother had known, absolutely known, what following would cost.

Which meant the song had a last verse. Songs that needed fleeing always did.

And somewhere in this house — in the walls, in the frames, in the mouth of some relative who'd never left — the rest of it was waiting to be sung.

The gallery, behind its doors, began again from the first verse. Patient. Circular. Willing to wait all night, all year, all century.

Isandro clicked her pen, wrote *3:40 a.m. — subject identified: NOT pests,* and underlined it twice.

Tomorrow, she was going to get answers out of Mrs. Aldous if she had to check the woman's baseboards herself.
