## 6. What the Portraits Remember

Edith Aldous had spent seventy-one years learning which floorboards announced her, and she avoided every one of them now as she made her way down to the kitchen at half past six in the morning. Not because she was sneaking. Because there was a young woman asleep in her guest room — a pest controller, technically, though Edith had paid her deposit in cash and crossed her fingers behind her back, a habit she'd picked up from her grandmother, who had crossed her fingers for far worse reasons — and Edith wanted tea and toast and ten minutes alone with her thoughts before the day demanded honesty of her.

The kettle was copper, older than she was, and it whistled in E-flat. Everything in Hollowmere House had a note. The stairs creaked in B minor. The wind through the chimney flue sang something that was almost, but not quite, "All Through the Night." Edith had stopped mentioning this to visitors decades ago, after her nephew Gerald's wife had laughed herself into hiccups and then quietly stopped visiting.

She buttered her toast and looked out at the garden, where the roses were doing their best despite everything, and admitted to the morning what she'd been refusing to admit to herself since the telephone call.

She hadn't hired Isandro Underhill for pests.

Well. That wasn't entirely true. There *were* pests. Something had been getting into the attic again — she could hear it moving up there at night, light and quick, and she chose to believe it was squirrels because the alternative was believing it was Gerald, and Gerald had been in the frames for eleven years and had never once come down to visit, which she considered rude even by his standards.

No. She had hired Isandro Underhill because of the name. Underhill. And because of what her mother had told her on her deathbed, gripping Edith's wrist with the last of her strength: *"If you ever need it done, find an outsider. A stranger's ears can hear the whole song without being claimed. Ours can't. Ours never could."*

Edith stirred her tea and watched the spoon make its small weather system and thought about how strange it was, the things families pass down. Recipes. Nose shapes. Guilt.

* * *

The girl — woman, Edith corrected herself; Isandro was thirty-something if she was a day, with forearms like someone who hauled ladders for a living, which she did — came down at seven-forty looking rumpled and suspicious, which Edith felt was the correct way to look in Hollowmere House. Most people looked either terrified or enchanted, and both were exhausting to be around.

"Mrs. Aldous," Isandro said, accepting coffee with the wariness of someone who had been poisoned before, or at least had read about it. "I want to walk the gallery this morning. You mentioned the portraits."

"Did I mention them?" Edith asked innocently.

"You said 'the paintings have been misbehaving.' Your exact words."

"Well, they have." Edith set down her cup. Here it was, then. The morning demanding honesty, right on schedule. She'd hoped for another cup of tea first, but mornings were notoriously unsentimental. "Miss Underhill, before we go up there, I think I owe you a confession. Or several. I'll try to keep them in order."

Isandro pulled out a chair and sat, slowly, the way people sit when they've decided to stay for bad news. "In order would be nice."

"The first one is that I lied about the fingerprints."

"I figured. Pest control doesn't get calls about fingerprints."

"No," Edith agreed. "Though I'll say this for the fingerprints — they're very tidy. Five fingers each. Better manners than my late husband, who used to leave prints on every window in the house and never wiped a single one." She heard herself and winced. "That's not — I'm not being glib, Miss Underhill. It's a defense mechanism. My family has been defending itself against this house for six generations, and we've developed certain... calluses."

"Six generations," Isandro repeated. Her eyes had gone still and careful, the way a deer's do when it can't decide whether the thing in the bushes is a cat or something else. "What does that mean, exactly?"

Edith took a breath. It was time. She'd rehearsed this in the bath, in the garden, into her pillow. None of the rehearsals had gone well, because there was no version of this that went well. There was only the version where she said it out loud and the other person decided whether she was mad or whether she was telling the truth, and both options required courage.

"It means," she said, "that nobody in my family has died outside these walls since 1841."

* * *

To her credit, Isandro didn't laugh. She also didn't run, which Edith privately ranked higher.

"Go on," Isandro said.

"My great-great-grandmother, Constance Aldous, bought this house from a family called the Merrows, who sold it at a loss so steep the solicitor assumed there must be dry rot or a murder. There wasn't rot. There wasn't a murder, exactly. There was a *song*." Edith wrapped her hands around her cooling tea. "Constance was a practical woman. She heard the humming in the walls the first week and decided it was the pipes. She heard it the second week and decided it was birds. By the third week she'd stopped deciding anything, because her father-in-law had begun sitting in the gallery for hours, listening to the portrait of his own dead mother hum to him, and one morning he simply wasn't in his bed anymore, and he *was* in his portrait. New frame. Fresh varnish. Same scowl."

Isandro's hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the salt packet in her shirt pocket. Edith pretended not to see. One did not survive in Hollowmere by commenting on other people's rituals.

"That's impossible," Isandro said, but her voice said *please don't stop*.

"Yes," Edith said comfortably. "It is. Entirely. I agree completely, and I said the same thing for the first forty years of my life. But here's what I learned, Miss Underhill, living with the impossible: it doesn't care whether you believe in it. Belief is our problem, not its. The house goes on being what it is whether you call it madness or miracle. The only thing belief changes is how much it hurts."

"And people — your people — they go into the frames." Isandro said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Voluntarily?"

"That's the cruel part." Edith looked out the window at the roses. "It never feels voluntary. It feels like coming home. That's what my uncle Ambrose said, the week before he went. He was ninety-three, sharp as a tack, and he sat me down and said, 'Edie, the gallery is warm.' Just that. 'The gallery is warm, and I'm tired of cold rooms.' And three days later he was hanging between his sister and his mother, and when I stood in front of him and asked him why, he couldn't answer. He just hummed. They all just hum, after. The first verse, always. Over and over, sweet as anything, like they're happy." Her voice caught and she smoothed it flat with practice. "Maybe they are happy. God knows. Maybe that's the trap — that it isn't a trap at all, from the inside."

"How many?" Isandro asked quietly. "How many of your family?"

"Twenty-two portraits in the gallery. Nineteen are Aldouses. Two are Merrows, who came with the house, like the copper kettle. And one—" Edith hesitated. This was the part she'd been circling all morning, the part that had made her dial a stranger's number instead of a niece's. "One is a young woman no one in the family has ever identified. Dark hair. She looks like no one we married. But she was there when Constance arrived, already in her frame, already humming. Constance wrote in her diary that the girl's portrait was 'the reason the house sings at all,' and then, typical of Constance, she never explained what she meant."

Something moved across Isandro's face — fast, quickly buried, like a fish under ice. "And the other two Aldouses? You said nineteen."

"Nineteen are in frames," Edith corrected gently. "Two more went missing. My aunt Ruth, in 1968. And—" she met Isandro's eyes, and saw the girl already knowing, already bracing, "—a woman named Maren Underhill. 1994. She was Ruth's daughter. She grew up here, left at nineteen, and came back once, twenty years later, for a single night. In the morning she was gone, and so was half a page of her journal, torn clean out. We found the rest of the journal in the attic."

The silence that followed had texture. Edith let it sit there between them like a third person at the table.

"Maren Underhill," Isandro said at last, "was my mother."

"I know," Edith said. "I've known since you gave your name over the phone. I nearly dropped the receiver. I'd wondered for thirty years what happened to Maren's child — whether there *was* a child. And then a pest controller calls me back within the hour, says she'll take the job, says the address sounds 'familiar' in a voice like she's swallowing glass, and asks me — asks *me*, Miss Underhill — whether the house still has its gallery." Edith smiled, and it cost her. "You knew. Some part of you knew before you ever drove up that hill."

* * *

They walked up to the gallery together after that, because Edith judged — correctly, she thought — that the truth would land better standing among the evidence than sitting in a kitchen with cold toast. Isandro carried her toolkit out of habit and then seemed embarrassed by it, like a man bringing an umbrella into a drought.

The long corridor ran along the east wing, tall-windowed, carpeted in a red gone the color of dried blood, and hung — floor to ceiling, wall to wall — with gilt frames. Morning light came through the east windows and touched each face in turn, and Edith watched Isandro watch them, and waited.

"They're facing forward," Isandro said slowly.

"This morning, yes."

"But yesterday—"

"Yesterday evening, when I showed you in, every single one of them had turned toward the door. Toward *you*." Edith clasped her hands behind her back, an old habit for keeping them steady. "They do that, sometimes. When someone new comes. Or when someone old comes back. The faces turn when no one's watching — I've tested it, dear, don't look at me like that, I rigged a mirror on a broom handle in 1987 and watched them swing round the moment my back was turned, smooth as owls. And then they turn back, innocent as Sunday, when you look."

"And the humming."

"You've heard it, then."

"Last night. Around three." Isandro's jaw worked. "It was a lullaby. I know it. I *know* it, Mrs. Aldous, and I don't know from where, and that's the part making me crazy."

Edith nodded slowly. "Each portrait keeps one verse. Or a piece of one. Nineteen singers, and the song gets fuller the deeper you walk — Uncle Ambrose starts it, near the door, just three notes, and by the end of the corridor old Great-Aunt Verity is carrying the melody with harmonies that would make a cathedral weep. Except—" and here Edith lowered her voice, though heaven knew why; the portraits had heard everything she'd ever said, "—except the song is incomplete. Has been since before Constance. There's a final verse. Nobody alive knows it. The house knows it. The walls know it — you can feel the plaster holding its breath, some nights, waiting. But the house can't sing it *itself*. It needs a living voice. It's needed one for a hundred and eighty years."

"What happens if someone sings it?"

"That," said Edith, "is the question that brought you here, whether you know it yet or not." She walked a few paces down the corridor, her slippers silent on the blood-red carpet, and stopped before the portrait of her husband, Harold, who had gone into his frame at sixty-eight with his tie crooked because he'd refused to let anyone fix it. He looked comfortable. He looked happy. He looked like a man at peace, and Edith had hated him a little for it every day since.

"My mother told me the terms before she passed. The house feeds on sung verses — that's its meal, its warmth, its life. Every verse sung by a living voice feeds it, and binds the singer a little closer. Sing enough, and you don't die elsewhere. Ever. You hang here, humming your share, fed and kept and *warm*, forever." She turned back to Isandro. "But the last verse is different. The last verse completes the song, and a completed song is a finished contract. Sung by an Aldous — by any of the blood — it claims the singer outright. Instantly. No negotiation. But sung by an outsider..." Edith spread her hands. "An outsider hears the whole thing without being claimed. Can carry it without being bound. My mother believed — and her mother before her — that a stranger singing the final verse wouldn't join the song. Would end it."

"End it how?"

"I don't know. Free everyone, perhaps. Break the house, perhaps. Mother said the song is a lock, and the last verse is the key, and locks don't care whose hand turns them — only that the hand isn't already inside the mechanism." Edith's mouth twisted. "Poetic woman, my mother. Useless with practical details, like what happens to the stranger afterward."

Isandro was quiet a long moment. Down the corridor, faint as breath, the first portrait — Uncle Ambrose, three notes, patient as tide — began to hum.

"Why me?" Isandro asked finally. "There must be other outsiders. You could have hired anyone. A singer. A priest. Anyone."

"Because outsiders forget," Edith said simply. "I've tried, dear. Four times over the years I've brought strangers in — a music teacher, two clergymen, a very game graduate student — and every one of them heard the verses, learned the shape of the song, and then woke the next morning having lost it entirely. Slipped out of their heads like water. The house won't let the song be carried away in a stranger's memory. It lets them hear it *here*, under this roof, and nowhere else." She looked at Isandro steadily. "But you're not quite a stranger, are you? Half your blood was raised in these walls. The song might hold in a mind like yours. Might. It's the best chance this family has had in a hundred and eighty years, and I took it, and I'm not proud of how I took it — lying about fingerprints, hiring you under false pretenses, letting you sleep under this roof without telling you what sleeps beside you." She drew herself up as straight as her spine allowed. "So there's my confession, Miss Underhill, complete and in order. I used you. I'm using you still. And I'm asking you now, honestly, with no lies left: will you help us anyway?"

The humming had spread while they talked. Three portraits now, four, the verses braiding together down the corridor, and Isandro stood in the middle of it with her face doing something complicated — grief and fury and recognition all fighting for the wheel.

"One question first," Isandro said.

"Anything."

"The girl. The dark-haired one no one identified. The one who was here before your family." Isandro's voice was very steady, which Edith suspected was costing her enormously. "Show me her portrait."

Edith inclined her head, and led her down the corridor, past nineteen humming Aldouses, to the far end — where the oldest frame hung, gilt gone green with age, and the dark-haired girl smiled out of the canvas with an expression Edith had never trusted, because it was the expression of someone who knows a joke the rest of the room hasn't heard yet.

Behind them, unnoticed by either woman, every portrait in the gallery had begun, very softly, to turn.
