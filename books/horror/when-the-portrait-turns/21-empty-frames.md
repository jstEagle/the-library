## 21. Empty Frames

The morning after the song broke, Hollowmere House was so quiet that Isandro could hear her own pulse in her ears, steady and unremarkable, the way a pulse ought to be.

She stood in the gallery with a mug of tea she'd made herself — Mrs. Aldous had offered, but her hands still trembled too badly to be trusted near a kettle — and looked at twenty-two empty frames.

Not blank canvases. Not painted-over faces. Empty. The portraits were simply *gone*, the way a bird is gone from a branch after it flies. The paint remained: landscapes, still lifes, a stern man in a ruff, a woman with a lace collar and no face at all beneath it now, just canvas-colored absence where features used to live. But whatever had animated them, whatever had hummed verses into the plaster for six generations, had packed up and left without leaving a note.

Except, Isandro thought, taking a sip of tea, that was exactly what they'd done. They'd left a note. She'd written it herself, at midnight, in a voice she hadn't known she owned.

*Renamed,* she thought. *Gone wherever renamed things go.*

She walked the length of the gallery slowly, boots quiet on the floorboards. The frames hung straight now. That was the detail that kept catching her — not the emptiness, but the *straightness*. For three days every frame had hung slightly askew, tilted like heads cocked in curiosity or hunger, and now they hung plumb and level as if a carpenter had come through overnight with a spirit level and a debt to settle.

At the corridor's end, the blank canvas — the one sized for her, freshly primed, waiting — had split down the middle. The two halves leaned against each other like tired dancers. Someone had primed that canvas for her. Someone had measured her shoulders while she slept. And now it was ruined goods, a dress cut for a woman who'd refused the wedding.

"Good," she said aloud, just to hear a voice in the room that wasn't humming.

Nothing answered. The silence had texture to it, though — a softness, almost a politeness. The house wasn't dead. Houses this old were never dead. But it was, for the first time in its long greedy life, *empty*, and it seemed to be holding its breath, wondering what empty felt like.

Isandro finished her tea and went to pack up her tools.

* * *

Packing took longer than it should have, because packing meant walking through the house, and walking through the house meant noticing things.

The fingerprints were fading. Three days ago they'd climbed the wallpaper in ascending spirals, five-fingered smudges at impossible heights, evidence of something climbing walls on hands that shouldn't have existed. Now they were going gray at the edges, blurring, sinking back into the plaster like footprints in tide-wet sand. By evening, she suspected, they'd be gone entirely. Whatever had left them had been released along with everything else.

Her toolkit sat where she'd abandoned it in the front hall, midnight being a poor time for tidiness. She knelt beside it and began putting things away, and her hands did what her hands always did — sorted, stowed, cataloged — while the rest of her tried to process the fact that she was alive, whole, and about to drive away from Hollowmere House for the second time in her life.

The salt came last.

She'd brought four boxes of it, packed in the truck bed alongside the sprayers and the foggers and the bait stations, and she'd told herself — the way she'd told herself for fifteen years — that it was for slugs. Slugs loved damp foundations, and old houses had damp foundations, and pest controllers carried salt, and none of that was a lie exactly. It was just a lie wearing a true coat.

Now she held one of the open boxes, half-empty, grains spilled across the hall floor in a scatter pattern she didn't remember making. Around the spill, pressed into the salt itself, were small handprints. Five-fingered. Child-sized.

Wren's size.

He'd played in it, she realized. Sometime in the last three days — maybe the night he'd admitted what he was, maybe the night before the end — he had knelt down in this hallway and pushed his hands into the salt, the way any seven-year-old would push their hands into anything soft and forbidden. Making prints. Leaving proof he existed.

Her throat went tight. She crouched there a long moment, thumb resting on the edge of one small print, and let herself feel it: grief with gratitude threaded through it, which was the worst kind, the kind you couldn't put down.

Then she closed the box, set it gently on top of her toolbox, and left it there.

She wasn't taking it home. She also wasn't throwing it out. It could sit in the truck bed until it decided what it was — slug deterrent or grave goods — because some things deserved more than one night to make up their minds.

* * *

Mrs. Aldous found her in the kitchen at noon.

The old woman moved differently now. Three days ago she'd been all flutter and apology, wringing her hands over "the pests," steering Isandro past certain doors, watching the corners of rooms with the practiced non-attention of someone who'd spent eighty years pretending not to see. Now she walked through Hollowmere like a woman touring a museum of her own life — interested, unhurried, touching nothing.

"I've decided to stay," Mrs. Aldous said, without preamble, in the way of people who'd rehearsed a speech and then thrown it away.

Isandro looked up from the kettle. "Stay here?"

"The house is quiet now." Mrs. Aldous lowered herself into a chair at the scrubbed pine table, folding her translucent-edged hands — they were still fading, Isandro noticed; the trade had nearly taken her, and some part of her would always be a little bit elsewhere — on the wood. "Six generations of my family lived in these walls, Miss Underhill. Most of them unwillingly, toward the end. I find I'd like to know what it's like to live here willingly. Just once."

"It's a big house for one person."

"It's a big house for twenty-two people, if those people are in the walls." Mrs. Aldous smiled, and for the first time since Isandro had met her, the smile reached all the way up. "Besides, someone should hear it settle. A house holds its breath for two hundred years, it'll want to sigh eventually. I'd rather be here for that."

Isandro poured hot water over the tea leaves and thought about arguing. She was good at arguing; she'd argued with houses, with raccoons, with the entire concept of the supernatural for fifteen years. But there was nothing left to argue against. No portrait would turn its head tonight. No lullaby would seep through the plaster at 3 a.m. The only thing left in Hollowmere House was an old woman who had chosen it, freely, for the first time.

"You'll want new locks," Isandro said instead. "These are rubbish. Half of them don't latch."

"They never needed to latch before." Mrs. Aldous accepted the tea. "Things got in whether we wanted them or not. Perhaps now things can simply... visit. If they wish. And leave when they're ready." She blew on the steam. "That's what freedom is, isn't it? Doors that work both ways."

Isandro sat down across from her. Through the window behind Mrs. Aldous's head, the garden went gold and green in the afternoon light, ordinary as anything, and somewhere out there a real bird sang a real song with no verses in it.

"Your husband," Isandro said quietly. "In the frames. He's gone now. Wherever renamed things go."

"I know." Mrs. Aldous's voice didn't waver. "I offered myself in exchange, you know. To save your family the trouble. And the house nearly took me — look at my hands — but your friend stopped it. Or your singing did. Or both." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "I spent sixty years wanting to join him. Now he's gone somewhere I can't follow, and do you know what I feel?"

"What?"

"Relieved." She laughed, a small dry sound. "Isn't that awful? Sixty years of longing, and it turns out what I wanted wasn't him. It was an ending. And endings, it turns out, can happen right here in the kitchen, over tea, with the doors unlocked." She sipped. "I think I'll plant roses where the east wall used to sweat. Roses like a house that's breathing again."

Isandro nodded, because there wasn't anything useful to say, and because some griefs are better honored with company than with words.

They drank their tea together in the quiet house, two women who'd survived the same song by different doors, and it was — Isandro was surprised to find — pleasant. Almost warm. The kind of afternoon she'd have called boring a week ago, and now understood to be priceless.

* * *

She loaded the truck at golden hour.

Sprayers, foggers, bait stations, the ladder, the tarps. Each item went into the truck bed with the small satisfying clank of work that was finished. The salt stayed where she'd left it, tucked in the corner, box closed, handprints hidden. She'd figure out what it was later. Maybe much later. Some cargo keeps.

Last of all she walked the house. Not checking — she caught herself doing it, hand drifting toward doorframes to test latches, eyes flicking to ceiling corners — and made herself stop. Instead she just walked, room to room, saying goodbye the way you say goodbye to a place that hurt you and housed you both.

In the gallery, the empty frames caught the last light and threw it around in pale rectangles. She paused at the one that had held her aunt — the woman with Isandro's own jawline, who'd spoken a single word through the canvas: *Finish.* The frame was empty now, the canvas showing only faint landscape, hills and a river, painted by some long-dead hand for some long-dead reason. Ordinary art. Dead art. Art that asked nothing of anyone.

"Bye," Isandro said to it. Then, feeling foolish, and doing it anyway: "Rest well. Both of you. However many of you there were."

The hills and river said nothing, being hills and a river, and Isandro found she preferred them that way.

At the front door she stopped, keys in hand, and looked at the lock.

Every instinct she had — fifteen years of instincts, fifteen years of locking things out and locking things in and locking herself away from addresses like this one — told her to turn the key. Seal it. Make it safe. Make it *done*.

But Mrs. Aldous was staying. And Mrs. Aldous had talked about doors that worked both ways. And somewhere in the last three days Isandro had learned — coldly, clearly, in the space between one verse and the next — that staying invisible was never actually on the menu. You couldn't lock a house like this out. You could only refuse to sing its song. And she had. She'd renamed the dead and broken the melody and walked out alive, and the least she owed the place was an unlocked door on her way through it.

She put the keys back in her pocket.

"Locks are rubbish anyway," she told the empty hall. "Half of them don't latch."

And she stepped off the porch into the evening, leaving Hollowmere House standing open behind her — not vulnerable, just available — and got in the truck, and started the engine, and drove.

In the rearview mirror the house shrank against the darkening sky, windows catching the last light like eyes finally closed. It didn't watch her go. It didn't need to. For the first time in two hundred years, nobody inside it was counting.

Isandro drove with the window down and the radio off, and the silence in the cab was the good kind — earned, hollowed-out, roomy enough to breathe in. Freedom, she was learning, leaves its own silence. You don't fill it right away. You let it sit.

The salt rode in the corner of the truck bed, closed box, small handprints sleeping inside it, going wherever she went.

She didn't mind.

Some things you keep until you know what they are.
