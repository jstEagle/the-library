## 22. The Song's Last Verse, Unwritten

The call came on a Tuesday in October, which felt right. Hollowmere had taught me that hauntings keep business hours too, and Tuesdays were when the world remembered it had loose ends.

"Underhill Pest Solutions," I said, balancing the phone against my shoulder while I restocked the van with bait stations nobody would ever check.

"Hi — yes — hello." The voice was a woman's, young, pitched in the particular register of someone who has rehearsed not sounding crazy and is failing at it. "I don't know if this is... I mean, you do *pests*, right? Like, actual pests?"

"Rats, mice, wasps, raccoons, the occasional bat. What've you got?"

A pause. The sound of someone deciding.

"There's humming," she said. "In the walls."

I set down the bait station. Outside the shop window, October was doing its best impression of a watercolor — all gray wash and bleeding light — and I watched a woman cross the parking lot with her collar up and thought: *she doesn't know how lucky she is, walking through weather that can't hum.*

"Could be bees," I said. "Colonies in wall cavities make a low drone. Very common this time of year."

"It's not bees." She laughed, but there wasn't anything funny in it. "It's a song. My daughter knows the words."

The shop radio behind me was playing something forgettable, and for one stupid second I wished it were louder, wished it could fill up the whole conversation so I wouldn't have to say what I said next.

"What's your address?"

She gave it. Maple Street, two towns over from Hollowmere, which is as close to Hollowmere as anything gets without being inside it. Forty minutes' drive. I wrote it down even though I'd already memorized it, because writing things down is a thing people who are fine do.

"We'll come out Thursday," I said. "Morning."

"Thank you." Relief, then, quickly, like she was embarrassed by it: "Is it — do you get calls like this often? About the humming?"

I looked at the salt canister on my shelf. I hadn't refilled it since June. I hadn't needed to.

"You'd be surprised," I said, "how many houses sing."

* * *

Thursday morning I drove out alone. I'd let Danny go months ago — told him business was slow, which was true, though the slowness had less to do with the economy than with the fact that I'd started turning down jobs within a nine-mile radius of a certain derelict estate. Word gets around in pest control. *Underhill won't touch the old Aldous place.* People assumed superstition. People assume a lot of things about a woman who keeps salt in her truck bed next to the snap traps.

Maple Street turned out to be the kind of street where every house has a name — not on a plaque, just in the way neighbors say them. *The Hutchins place. The one with the copper beech.* My client's house was called nothing yet; it was too new, all clean siding and a porch swing still wrapped in plastic. A starter house. A house that hadn't learned any songs.

The woman — Ms. Ferro, per the invoice — met me on the porch with coffee I didn't drink and a story she told in pieces, the way people do when they're afraid the whole shape of it will scare them.

"It started in September. Just a little, at night. I thought it was the furnace." She hugged her elbows. "But then my daughter started humming back. She's four. She doesn't know any lullabies — I never sang to her, I can't carry a tune, my mother used to say I could clear a room — but she hums along with the walls like she's known it forever. And last week—" Her voice did something complicated. "Last week she asked me who Wrennie was."

The porch swing creaked under its plastic wrap. Or something creaked. On Maple Street, everything got the benefit of the doubt.

"What did you tell her?" I asked.

"That she made it up. That's what you say, isn't it? When kids have imaginary friends?"

It is. It's exactly what you say. My mother had said it to me across a kitchen table in 1998, and I had agreed, because agreeing was cheaper than the alternative, and we'd both carried that agreement around like a stone in a shared pocket for years.

"Show me where the humming is loudest," I said.

* * *

It was the nursery, of course. It's always the room where somebody sleeps.

Ms. Ferro stood in the doorway while I walked the perimeter with my stethoscope — an actual medical stethoscope, bought off a retiring nurse, the single most useful tool I own and the one no certification course ever mentioned. I pressed the bell against the plaster and listened.

Nothing. Then, faintly, far down inside the wall like a voice at the bottom of a well:

*Hush now, hush now, the day is done—*

Three verses. I knew the tune the way you know a scar. My hand came off the wall faster than professional composure allows, and Ms. Ferro saw it, because people always see the thing you're trying hardest to hide.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's it exactly. Is it — what *is* it?"

I thought about Mrs. Aldous, six generations deep in complicity, hiring strangers because outsiders can hear the last verse without being claimed. I thought about twenty-two empty frames in a gallery at dawn, faces gone slack as deflated balloons. I thought about a blank canvas split down the middle, hanging in a corridor that smelled of turpentine and ended things.

I thought about a boy on a porch saying *you came back late*, and a man in a mirror saying nothing at all.

"Houses settle," I said. "Old lumber, new paint. Sometimes the materials remember other buildings — reclaimed timber, salvaged beams. Your house might have bones from somewhere else."

"Somewhere else where?"

"Doesn't matter," I said, and it was mostly true. "What matters is it's not feeding. A song needs a singer to grow. As long as nobody finishes the verses, it stays a draft. A hum. Annoying, not dangerous."

"But my daughter—"

"Your daughter hums along. She doesn't harmonize." I put the stethoscope away. "There's a difference, and the house knows it. Here's what you're going to do. Play music in this room. Loud, cheerful, terrible music — the kind with words that don't mean anything. Fill the frequencies. And when she asks about Wrennie again, don't say he isn't real."

Ms. Ferro blinked. "What do I say?"

"Say he's a friend from far away who can't visit anymore. Say it kindly. Kids can tell the difference between a lie and a kindness, even when adults can't." I shrugged. "And call me if the humming learns new verses. That's the only part that matters."

She paid me in cash and walked me out, and on the porch she stopped and said, quietly, "You've heard this before. The song."

The wind moved through the copper beech two doors down, and I let a few seconds pass the way you let a held note fade.

"I grew up with it," I said. "It ends. That's the thing nobody tells you about songs like this one. They end, if you're stubborn enough."

* * *

I sat in the truck at the curb for a while after, engine idling, watching the house. The nursery curtain twitched — small hand, probably, pressing the fabric aside to watch the strange truck go. Through the glass I caught a flash of movement, a child-shape waving.

I didn't wave back. Some doors you leave politely shut.

Then I checked my mirrors, because fifteen years of habit plus six months of aftermath equals a woman who checks her mirrors, and in the rearview —

He was standing on the sidewalk behind the truck.

Not the boy. Not the seven-year-old in the too-big coat with the grass-stained knees. A young man, mid-twenties, tall, with dark hair going silver early at the temples — aged right, aged *owed*, all those decades arriving at once and settling into a face like sediment into a riverbed. He wore a plain jacket. His hands were in his pockets. And he was smiling — not the patient, sad, warden's smile I'd known on the porch of Hollowmere, but something easier. Something that belonged entirely to him.

He raised one hand. A wave. Small. Unhurried. The wave of someone who has all the time in the world and finally, finally means it.

My heart did something unprofessional.

By the time I turned fully around — because some part of me will always turn fully around — the sidewalk was empty. Just leaves, and the long October light, and a stretch of concrete where a man had been or hadn't been or had been in the way that matters more than physics.

I sat there until the idle timer killed the engine.

Here's what I know, and I know it the way I know the weight of a snap trap in my hand: the dead went wherever renamed things go. Every frame in that gallery emptied. But Wren wasn't dead, not exactly — he'd been mortal once, and I'd given mortality back to him mid-note, and mortal things don't evaporate. Mortal things *go*. They walk out of galleries and down corridors and out into a world they haven't seen since it was lit by lamps instead of memories.

Maybe he'd been following the song. Maybe he'd heard a hum start up in a nursery two towns over and come to stand guard one last time, out of habit, out of love, out of whatever a warden is once the ward is empty. Maybe he'd seen my truck pull up and stayed just long enough to see me handle it — to see that the girl he'd kept from singing had become a woman who taught other mothers how to keep their daughters quiet.

Or maybe he was just passing through. Freed things get to do that.

Either way, he'd waved. Not *thank you*. Not *goodbye*. Just: *here. Still here. Somewhere.*

I started the truck.

* * *

The drive back took forty minutes and I spent all of them not humming, which is harder than it sounds. The lullaby lives in my head the way my mother lives in my head — permanently, imperfectly, with the last line torn away. Some mornings I wake up with three verses running and have to drown them in coffee and traffic reports. Some nights I catch myself at the edge of the fourth verse and stop so hard I bite my tongue.

But I don't finish it. And I don't unravel it anymore either. I just let it sit there, unfinished, unwritten, a song with its ending torn out — which is, when you think about it, the most honest state a song can be in. Open. Unclaimed. Nobody's.

Halfway home I passed a sign for Hollowmere Road and, on impulse I'll defend to my grave, took the turn.

The house stood the way I'd left it in June: shutters closed, gallery dark, grass gone shaggy and proud of it. Mrs. Aldous had moved to the east wing full-time, she'd told me, and taken up painting — *landscapes only, dear, nothing with eyes* — and the house had gone quiet around her the way a dog goes quiet around an old owner. Not tamed. Just... settled. Two old creatures keeping each other company.

I didn't go in. I pulled onto the shoulder and looked at it for a minute, the way you look at a grave you've made peace with, and I noticed — because I notice, it's the whole job — that the porch steps had been swept. Recently. Broom lines in the dust, neat as handwriting.

Mrs. Aldous didn't sweep. Her hands hadn't been up to it since before the renaming.

I looked at the swept steps for a long time. Then at the windows, dark and polite. Then at the road behind me, empty in both directions, holding its leaves and its light and whatever — whoever — chose to walk it.

"Well," I said, to nobody, to the house, to him, whichever was listening. "Don't be a stranger."

The house said nothing. Houses are bad at goodbyes. But the wind came down off the ridge and moved through the shaggy grass in a long soft rush, almost like breath, almost like a laugh, and if it carried the ghost of a melody it carried only the first three bars — the safe ones, the unfinished ones — before letting them go.

I drove on.

The radio found a station playing something with words that didn't mean anything, and I left it loud, filling the frequencies, and I did not hum along. Behind me Hollowmere shrank in the mirror and vanished, and ahead the road ran open into October, and somewhere in the world a freed man was learning what weather feels like, and a four-year-old on Maple Street was asking kind questions and getting kind answers, and a song with no ending sat quiet in the walls of every house I'd ever failed to explain.

Some songs end.

Some just change singers.

And me — I keep driving, and I keep listening, and I keep a broom in the back of the truck now, though I couldn't tell you why.
