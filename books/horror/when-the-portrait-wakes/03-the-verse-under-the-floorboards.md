## 3. The Verse Under the Floorboards

Wren had always been terrible at hiding things, which was strange for a woman who'd spent her whole life hiding how much she felt. As children, her secret stashes of contraband sweets were discoverable in under a minute — behind the loose brick, inside the hollow bedpost, once, memorably, in the flour bin, where our mother found them by the simple method of making dinner. So when I found the first clue tucked into Wren's journal, I almost laughed. Almost. Grief has a way of sanding the humor off everything, but it doesn't remove the grain entirely.

The clue was a pressed flower — a marsh marigold, butter-yellow even in death — and beneath it, written in Wren's slanted hand: *Where the water learned to pray.*

I knew the place immediately. Everyone in Hollowmere knew it, though nobody spoke of it above a mutter: the flooded chapel, half-drowned in the old quarry pond since the river was dammed in the fifties. The villagers called it the Drowning Church, which struck me as the sort of name a town gives a thing when it wants to stop asking questions about it. The roofline still broke the surface on calm days, a black ribcage of rafters, and on foggy mornings you could see the tip of the bell tower standing out of the water like a man wading out to meet something he'd rather not.

I went at dawn, because I'd learned in three days in Hollowmere that dawn was the only hour the village didn't watch me. Mrs. Tansy had taken to leaving a covered dish on my doorstep — shepherd's pie, then a rice pudding, then a pie again, the pastry pinched into little waves along the crust — and each dish came with a smile so warm and so fixed it could have been bolted on. "You must eat, love," she'd say. "Grief takes it out of you." What she meant, I understood now, was: *Stay indoors. Stay small. Don't go looking.*

I ate the pie. It was excellent. I went looking anyway.

* * *

The path down to the pond was overgrown but not abandoned — someone had cut the brambles back recently, and the cuts were clean, recent enough that sap still shone at the stems. Wren's work. My sister, who complained for twenty years about the blackberry cane in our mother's garden and then, in the last months of her life, came to a drowned church with pruning shears and a flashlight and the particular stubbornness of a Zhou twin told *no*.

The water was glass-still and the color of old tea. I stood on the stone lip of the churchyard — the wall still rose out of the pond like the edge of a sunken world — and I felt, for the first time since the coffin, something other than dread. I felt my sister. Not her ghost. Her *attention*. The way you feel a person watching you from across a room, the prickle at the neck, the certainty of being found.

The church door was below the waterline, but the vestry window on the east side had half-emerged with the summer drought, its frame crusted white with mineral lines like the tide marks of a bath. I took off my boots, said a small prayer to whatever god looks after idiot sisters, and went in through the window.

Inside, the air was wet stone and rot and, underneath it, something sweeter. Hymnal. Old paper and old incense, drowned a hundred years and still holding its breath.

Wren had left a trail only I would have followed: a chalk arrow on a pew end, half-dissolved; a ribbon from my birthday present two years ago tied to a rotted beam. Under the vestry floorboards — the ones she'd pried up with a flathead screwdriver I found still wedged in the gap, its handle wrapped in her red hair ribbon — there was a tin. A biscuit tin, rusted at the corners, and inside it, dry as the day it was hidden, a bundle of paper.

The top sheet was a hymnal page, torn from a book I'd find later in the village archive, its final verse inked over in a hand that shook — not with age, I realized, but with haste, with fear, with the terrible urgency of a person writing something down before someone made them stop.

And beneath the verse, in that same shaking hand, a name.

*Maren Oduya.*

Not the name the village used. The village, when it spoke of 1911 at all, spoke of "the woman at the mill," nameless, the way you'd speak of a stain. But here it was, in ink a century old: Maren Oduya, who came to Hollowmere in 1909 with a voice the choir mistress called "a gift from God" and the village called, when the crops failed and the children sickened, something else entirely. Who was tried by no one, sentenced by everyone, and drowned in this pond on the fourth of November with a hymn in her mouth that the congregation cut off at the third verse.

The journal page tucked beneath it was Wren's, and her handwriting had gone strange by the end — looser, as if the pen were moving faster than she was.

*Juniper, if you're reading this, then I was wrong about how much time I had. The verse is real. The bell is her throat. They drowned her mid-hymn and the song never ended, it's been waiting a hundred years for someone to finish it, and finishing it is a door — but a door swings both ways. Learn it. Do not sing it. If you feel it rising in you, if you feel how badly you want to — and you will want to, it's like wanting air — then remember: she doesn't need a voice. She needs a witness.*

I read it twice, kneeling on the wet floorboards with the pond lapping at the sill behind me, and I will not pretend my eyes stayed dry. But it was the last line that undid me, the one written smaller, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

*They put a stranger in my coffin, Junie. Don't let them put one in yours.*

* * *

I climbed out of the church at eleven in the morning, and the sun was wrong.

Not dim — Hollowmere's sun is never dim; it's the flat, polite, catalog-photo sun of a town that keeps its weather like it keeps its secrets. But it was wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has moved a chair two inches. The light lay on the pond without glittering. The birds had stopped. And from the village, a mile off across the fields, I heard the bell.

It had only ever rung at dusk. Every account, every journal entry, every muttered warning — dusk, and only dusk, the hour of the unfinished verse. But it was noon, and the bell rang, and it did not ring the way bells ring. It rang the way a throat clears before speaking.

I ran. I'm not proud of how I ran — boots in my hand, journal clutched to my chest, splashing up the quarry path like a woman late for a train — but terror is honest, and terror told me to get behind a door before whatever was calling found me in the open.

I made it to Mrs. Tansy's gate before I saw them.

They came up the lane in a loose procession, unhurried, barefoot in the noon frost, and they were people I knew. Mrs. Tansy herself, still in her apron, flour on her wrists. The postman, Mr. Callow, his bag still slung across his chest, letters sticking out of it like a paper wound. The vicar's widow. Two of the Hollis brothers. Old Mr. Petch, who had once, my first morning back, pressed a pound coin into my hand "for the bus, love," as though grief were a fare you could pay your way out of.

Their eyes were open. Their eyes were the worst part — open, and empty as the pond, and fixed on my door.

And they were singing.

It was the hymn — of course it was, it had always been the hymn, the one the village mouthed at dusk with their lips moving like fish in shallow water. But now they sang it aloud, all of it, verses one and two and three, Maren Oduya's voice poured into a dozen borrowed mouths, sweet and low and rising, and they sang every word except the last line of the last verse. There they stopped. All of them, together, mid-breath, mouths open on the shape of a word that would not come.

Then they began again.

I got inside and threw the bolt and stood with my back against the door, and I want to be truthful about what happened next, because it matters. The singing came through the walls. Not muffled — *through*, the way warmth comes through, the way a name called in a dream comes through. And the verse rose in me unbidden, every note of it, perfect, because I had read it off a dead woman's page an hour before and the song had been waiting in the ink for a throat.

I felt my mouth open. I felt the first note gather, low in my chest, warm as a hand on a cold night, and God forgive me, it felt like *relief*. Like setting down a weight I'd carried since the coffin. Like Wren's hand on my shoulder, saying *it's all right, Junie, let it out, let me hear you.*

That's how it gets you. Not with horror. With homecoming.

I bit the note back. I bit it so hard I tasted blood, and I stood in my sister's kitchen with my hand clamped over my own mouth like a child afraid of saying the wrong thing at a funeral, while outside, a dozen of my neighbors sang everything but the end, over and over, patient as tide.

The bell stopped at sundown. The sleepers woke, blinked at my door, smiled their bolted-on smiles, and walked home barefoot through the frost, and not one of them remembered a thing.

But under the door, sometime in the dark, a slip of paper had been pushed through. I knelt and picked it up with fingers that would not quite stop shaking.

It was a photograph, old and silvered, of the drowned church on a summer day in 1911. A congregation stood on the bank, posed and proud, every face accounted for.

And on the back, in Wren's handwriting, one line:

*Count the faces, Junie. Then count the graves.*
