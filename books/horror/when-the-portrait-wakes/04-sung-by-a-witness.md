## 4. Sung by a Witness

The bell began at sundown and did not stop.

Juniper had heard it ring at dusk every night since her arrival — a slow, drowned tolling, like something being lifted and dropped in deep water. But tonight was different. Tonight it rang the way a heart rings when the body knows it is dying: faster, then slower, then faster again, never settling, never pausing for breath. The sound came through the floorboards of Wren's cottage, through the soles of Juniper's shoes, into the bones of her feet, and settled there like a second pulse that did not belong to her.

She had spent the day preparing, though "preparing" felt too tidy a word for what she'd actually done. She had copied the verse out three times in her own handwriting until she no longer needed the paper. She had written nineteen names on a separate sheet — the four men who had held Edith Marlowe under the millpond water in October 1911, and the fifteen descendants who still lived in Hollowmere, who still said *good morning* to her with those practiced smiles, who still left pies on her step like offerings to something they hoped would stay fed.

Wren's journal lay open on the table. The last entry was dated nine days before the funeral.

*Found her name carved inside the bell's shadow on the chapel stone. E.M. They didn't even bury her properly. I'm going to sing it, June. Someone has to finish what they started. If you're reading this, don't follow me. Sing it your own way or not at all.*

Too late, little sister. Juniper closed the journal gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead.

Outside, the first of them appeared at the gate. Then the next. Then, like ink spreading through water, the whole village — every soul in Hollowmere, moving up the lane toward Wren's cottage with the unhurried certainty of sleepwalkers, their lips already shaping the hymn's familiar verses. Mrs. Calloway, who had brought the pies. Old Tam Hedley, who had wept at the funeral. The Reverend's widow, whose grandfather's grandfather had held Edith Marlowe's left arm.

They did not knock. They simply stood in the yard, dozens of them, faces lit by the moon and by nothing else, and sang everything but the end. The silence where the last verse should have been was louder than the bell.

Juniper stepped out onto the porch.

"You know why we've come," Mrs. Calloway said, and her voice was kind, which was the worst part. It was always kind. "It wants a voice, dear. It's waited a hundred years. Your sister tried to take it all on her own — poor thing, she wasn't strong enough. But you could finish it. One verse. You'd be free, and so would we."

"Free," Juniper repeated.

"To stop hearing it." Tam Hedley's eyes were wet and pleading. "Do you know what it's like, hearing it every dusk for sixty years? Knowing if anyone ever finishes it, one of us becomes her voice forever, and the rest go quiet? We drew lots once, when I was a boy. My mother burned the hat before the drawing."

"So you let it wait," Juniper said. "You let it wait a hundred years. And when my sister found the truth, you let *her* pay for your quiet."

No one answered. The bell tolled. Their lips moved together, a congregation of the guilty, singing around the hole in the song the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be.

"She's here," whispered the Reverend's widow suddenly, staring past Juniper at the dark garden. "Can't you feel her? She's been waiting for someone to open the door."

And Juniper did feel it — a cold rising from the ground, from the direction of the flooded chapel ruins beyond the village, a cold with a shape and a patience and a hundred years of unfinished breath behind it. Part of her wanted, desperately, to sing. To give the ghost what it asked for and be done. To trade her voice for Wren's body, for an ending, for sleep. The melody sat in her chest like a key sitting in a lock, begging to be turned.

That was the trap. She understood it now. The song didn't want to be finished. It wanted to be *answered*.

"No," Juniper said.

The word fell into the yard and the villagers flinched as one.

"I'm not going to sing it. Not tonight, not ever, not the way you want. Because a song can be sung by anyone. A mouth is just a mouth. But testimony—" she took the folded paper from her pocket, though she didn't need it— "testimony needs a witness. And I am the only witness Hollowmere has left who isn't afraid of her own name."

She began to speak.

Not sing. Never sing. She planted her feet on the porch boards and spoke the last verse flat and clear, each word a nail driven into the night:

*"On the thirteenth of October, in the year nineteen eleven, Edith Marlowe was drowned in the millpond by four men of this parish, while the bell was melted down to drown her song with her. She was innocent of what they accused her. She begged, and they held her under. Her name was Edith Marlowe, and she died calling for mercy that none of them gave."*

The bell stopped ringing.

In the terrible silence, Juniper kept speaking. She named them. Aldous Crane, who held her right arm. Silas Hedley, the left. Matthew Pike, who had accused her. Thomas Vane, who had ordered the bell recast silent. And then the descendants, one by one, standing in the moonlit yard: *"Calloway, of Crane's line. Hedley, of Silas's. Vane, whose family still owns the millpond—"*

"Stop," Mrs. Calloway breathed. "Stop, you'll wake her, you'll—"

"She was never asleep," Juniper said. "You were."

And the bell cracked.

It was not a loud sound. It sounded, Juniper thought afterward, like ice giving way on a pond in early spring — a single clean fracture running through a hundred years of bronze. The crack widened into a peal, the peal into a shattering, and somewhere in the ruins of the flooded chapel, the melted bell broke apart into pieces that had never truly been a bell at all, only a cage shaped like one.

The cold rushed past her, down through the yard, between the frozen villagers, toward the water. Juniper followed it at a run, down the lane, over the stile, to the black edge of the millpond — and there, in the moonlight, the water gave up what it had been keeping.

Wren rose slowly, gently, the way autumn leaves rise when you lift them off a grave. Not alive. Never alive again. But *herself*, at last — her own face, her own hands, the small scar on her chin from the bicycle fall when they were nine. The stranger in the coffin had been a shell the bell had built, a placeholder, a lie told in Wren's shape. Now the lie was empty, and the truth floated to the surface like something finally allowed to exhale.

Behind Juniper, the villagers stood at the pond's edge, and one by one they began to weep — not the practiced grief of funerals but the raw, ugly weeping of people who have heard their own names spoken as a verdict. Mrs. Calloway sank to her knees in the mud. Tam Hedley covered his face. Nobody sang. For the first time in a hundred years, nobody in Hollowmere sang anything at all.

Edith Marlowe passed through the yard as dawn came — not as a shape, not as a voice, but as a warmth, brief and enormous, like sunlight breaking through after a long storm. Juniper felt her go the way you feel a door close softly in another room. *Thank you*, the warmth seemed to say, though it had no words. *Witness.*

They buried Wren properly this time, in her true body, on high ground where the bell could never reach. Juniper spoke at the graveside, and her voice held, mostly.

She stayed three more days. Long enough to see the pieces of the bell carted away, long enough to watch the villagers begin — awkwardly, haltingly — to say true things to each other instead of kind ones. Then she packed Wren's journal and her own grief into the same bag and walked out of Hollowmere without looking back, because looking back was a luxury the living buy with forgetting, and she had promised herself she would not forget.

But some nights, in whatever town she woke in next, Juniper would hear a bell at dusk — a real one, an ordinary one, calling an ordinary congregation — and her heart would lurch against her ribs, and she would have to sit very still until it passed.

Justice, she had learned, does not set you free. It only tells you the price.

And the price, for Juniper Zhou, was this: she would carry Hollowmere with her for the rest of her life — its pond, its names, its broken song — and every year on the thirteenth of October, she would stand wherever she was, face the east, and speak Edith Marlowe's name aloud into the ordinary air.

Not because any ghost demanded it.

Because someone had to remember, and now, she was the only witness left.
