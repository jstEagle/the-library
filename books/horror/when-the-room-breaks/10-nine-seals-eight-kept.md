## 10. Nine Seals, Eight Kept

The ledger did not want to be read.

Ada understood this the way he understood weather, in his bones before his mind caught up. He had carried Wren's copy down from the attic at dawn and set it on the kitchen table beneath the one window that still let in honest light, and for three hours it had resisted him—not physically, not dramatically, but with a kind of patient opacity. The founders' script was dense, cramped, written by hands that had learned penmanship in an age when ink was mixed with things Ada preferred not to name. Whole passages dissolved into marginalia. Diagrams bled through the paper from pages he hadn't turned yet, so that reading forward felt like walking into a room where someone had already been, leaving footprints in the dust ahead of him.

He drank coffee gone cold twice without noticing. Outside, Hollowmere lay white and silent under a sky the color of old pewter, and the frost on the window had grown inward overnight—feathering across the glass toward the room instead of climbing from the sill, as if the cold outside were leaning in to watch him work.

*It's just condensation,* said the voice in his head, the old voice, the survival voice. *Old houses breathe. Old windows weep.*

"Shut up," Ada told it, and was surprised to find he meant it.

Because here was what seven years of explanations had never accounted for: Wren's annotations. Her handwriting looped through the margins in blue ballpoint—the same blue she'd used for grocery lists, for margin notes in her nursing textbooks, for the birthday card she'd given him two months before everything went wrong. The ordinariness of it was unbearable. She had sat somewhere, probably this very table, probably under this very gray light, working through the ledger the way she worked through everything: methodically, patiently, circling what mattered.

And what mattered, she had circled nine times.

---

The first circle was on the third page of the founding entries. Ada had almost missed it because the annotation wasn't words—it was a small map, sketched freehand, of the town square as it had looked a century ago. At its center, where the war memorial now stood, Wren had drawn a small symbol: a circle bisected by a vertical line, like an eye viewed edge-on.

Beneath it, in her handwriting: *the first seal. the stone remembers being a door.*

Ada set down the ledger and pressed both palms flat against the table until the wood bit back.

He knew the memorial. Everyone in Hollowmere knew the memorial—a squat granite obelisk listing the town's dead from three wars, its base worn smooth by a hundred years of schoolchildren's hands. He had laid flowers there after the funeral. He had stood in the cold while Pastor Ellery spoke about sacrifice and permanence, and the whole town had nodded in unison, gently, like wheat.

*Sacrifice and permanence.* He heard the phrase again now and felt it turn over, showing him its underside.

He went to the hall closet and found the town map he'd bought at the gas station his first night back—the kind tourists bought, though no tourists came anymore—and spread it beside the ledger. And then, page by page, entry by entry, he began to match Wren's circles to the living town.

---

The second seal was the well behind the Vane house on Corben Street. The Vanes had drawn their water from it since before the town had plumbing; old Mrs. Vane still refused city water, and everyone thought it was stubbornness. The ledger called it *the throat*, and noted that it must be *kept drinking*. Wren had written: *they lower something down every equinox. i saw them do it when i was small. they told me it was apples.*

The third seal was the church itself—or rather, the ground beneath it. The ledger's entry ran four pages and described, in language that circled its own meaning like a dog afraid to bite, a foundation stone that was *not a foundation but a lid*. Pastor Ellery's family had held the parish for six generations. Six generations of sermons about watching, about vigilance, about keeping faith with what sleeps.

Ada laughed, once, and the sound frightened him. It came out wrong—too high, too thin, the laugh of a man reading his own diagnosis.

The fourth seal was the millrace. The fifth, the orchard on Hollis Hill, where every tree grew in rows too straight for any orchardist's hand and the fruit came in heavy years that followed no agricultural logic anyone could explain. The sixth was the schoolhouse bell, which had rung by itself twice in living memory, and both times the town had spoken of it afterward in the careful, lowered voices people used for funerals.

The seventh seal stopped him cold.

It was the Ashgrove house.

Not the cellar—he'd expected that, had braced for it—but the house entire. The ledger described the family seat as *the hinge*, the dwelling placed precisely where the ninth door's threshold met the world above, and it named the duty of the Ashgroves in terms that made Ada's mouth go dry: *to keep the household. to keep the hearing. to answer when answered.*

*To answer when answered.*

Seven years ago, something had knocked. And he had stepped back, and Wren had answered, and the ledger said the answering was the whole of the duty, passed down like the house itself, like the name.

He sat with that for a long time. The frost on the window had reached the center of the glass now, spreading in patterns that were almost—almost—legible, and he kept his eyes on the ledger because looking at the window felt like making eye contact.

---

By late afternoon he had found eight seals, and Wren had circled eight symbols, and the correspondence was perfect, and that perfection was the most frightening thing he had ever held in his hands. Because the eighth entry ended differently from the others. The others closed with maintenance instructions, schedules, duties. The eighth closed with a line that had been struck through by the original hand and then rewritten, and rewritten again, the ink layered like scar tissue:

*nine seals, eight kept. the ninth is held open. the ninth is held open. the ninth is held open.*

Three times. As if the writer had needed to convince himself.

And beside it, Wren's final annotation—the last thing she had written in the ledger, in handwriting gone hurried, the blue ink dragged thin:

*it's not a lock with a hole in it. it's a lock MADE of the hole. the town isn't protecting us FROM it. the town IS the lock. we're the pins inside it. ada, look at who lives where. look at WHO lives where.*

*Look at who lives where.*

Ada looked.

He took the map and wrote each founder-family name beside its landmark, and then, because Wren had told him to, he wrote the families who lived there now. The Vanes at the well. The Ellerys at the church. The Marshes at the millrace—Delia Marsh, foreman of the crew digging at the frost-line, her grandfather before her, hers now. The Hollises above the orchard. The Bells at the schoolhouse. The Corbens, the Fenns, the Ashgroves. Eight names, eight places, eight bloodlines that had never left, never scattered, never married out beyond a generation—the way small towns simply *didn't*, everyone had always said, the way Hollowmere folk were rooted, were loyal, were *of the place*.

Rooted. Pinned.

Every kindness at the wake, every casserole, every gentle unison voice—they hadn't been hiding anything. They were components performing their function, the way a tumbler performs its function inside a lock, faithfully, blindly, beautifully. The town didn't keep the secret. The town *was* the secret, built outward from the door like a pearl built around a wound, and every person born inside it was another layer of nacre, smooth and white and load-bearing.

And the ninth seal—the held-open one, the unfinished one—was anchored here. Under this house. Under the cellar where he had knelt seven years ago and stepped back and let his sister answer.

The debt hadn't been deferred. It had been *structural*. The whole town had been holding its breath around the gap where an Ashgrove should have been sealed, and now Wren was dead—dead smiling at a door that shouldn't exist—and the gap was open wider than it had ever been, and the frost was coming inward off the glass, and somewhere below the floorboards the house was breathing with the slow patience of a mechanism that has waited ninety years for its missing pin.

Ada stood up so fast the chair went over backward. He caught himself on the table, on the ledger, on Wren's looping blue annotations, and he understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only at the far end of denial—that she hadn't died investigating this. She had died *continuing* it. Mid-ritual. Mid-answer. Finishing the duty he had abandoned, alone, because he had left her to carry it.

The frost on the window had formed letters. He didn't look at them. He was afraid that if he read his own name written in ice on his own window, some part of him would agree to be claimed, and he wasn't ready—not yet, not tonight—to be read.

Instead he picked up the map, folded it into his coat pocket, and took the flashlight down the hall toward the cellar stairs, where the cold rose to meet him like a held note, and where, faint and unmistakable beneath the concrete floor, something knocked twice—

—the way you knock on a door when you already know someone is home.
