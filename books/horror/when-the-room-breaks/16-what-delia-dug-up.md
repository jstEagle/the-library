## 16. What Delia Dug Up

The frost had stopped pretending to be weather.

Delia Marsh stood at the lip of the excavation pit at six in the morning, breath fogging inside her hood, and understood that the cold down there was not coming from the sky. It came up. It rose out of the broken ground like heat off summer asphalt, only inverted—a cold that climbed her boots, her shins, settled into the hollows of her knees like water finding its level. The site lights burned all night now, four sodium lamps on rented generators, because the crew refused to work in the dark and she couldn't blame them. Two of her people had walked into the drifts eleven days ago, smiling, and the search had been called off when the drifts closed over their tracks like a mouth finishing a sentence.

She hadn't reported it. That was the thing she kept circling, the way her tongue kept finding the chipped tooth she'd never fixed. She hadn't reported it, and the company had rewarded her for it—*Senior Site Director, effective immediately*, with a salary that made her mother's voice on the phone go strange and careful—and every morning since, she woke up knowing exactly what she was. Not a victim of the town. Not a bystander. A component. The ledger page with her signature already on it sat in her truck's glove box, and some nights she took it out just to look at her own handwriting agreeing to things she didn't remember agreeing to, and she thought: *this is how they get you. They don't push. They open doors and let you walk through on your own legs.*

So this morning, when the ground-penetrating radar showed a chamber beneath the door—not a void, not bedrock, but a *room*, furnished with shapes—she did not call the regional office first.

She called no one. She put on the harness herself.

"You're not going down alone," said Okafor, the only crew member left who still met her eyes. He'd been with the company nineteen years and had the flat calm of a man who had decided fear was a luxury billed later. "Protocol says—"

"Protocol says I'm the director," Delia said. "And protocol also says we wait forty-eight hours for corporate approval, and I don't think this place gives us forty-eight hours anymore." She checked the carabiner twice, then a third time, because her hands wanted something mechanical to do. "You hold the line. If I stop answering on the radio, you pull me up and then you burn the rope."

"Burn the rope?"

"I'm serious, Theo—" No. Not that name. She'd been reading too much of the Ashgrove boy's file; it leaked into her head like dye through water. "Okafor. Burn the rope."

He held the line. She went down.

* * *

The shaft below the door was older than the excavation above it. That was the first wrongness, and it registered in her body before her mind: the walls were fitted stone, dry-laid, seamless, the kind of work that predated mortar in the region by centuries, and yet the company's own survey records listed Hollowmere as purchased land with no prior structures. Someone had built this room before anyone was supposed to have been here. Or the room had built itself. Down here, past thirty feet of frozen earth, those two possibilities stopped feeling different.

Her headlamp carved a cone out of the dark. The air was still—not dead, nothing down here was dead, but *held*, the way a breath is held in a concert hall between movements. Her boots crunched on frost that coated the floor in a skin thick as candle wax, and each crunch echoed wrong, arriving a half-beat late, as if the room were considering whether to return the sound.

The chamber was small. Maybe twelve feet square. And it was furnished.

That was the word her mind kept offering and she kept rejecting, but it would not be rejected, because it was true. There were chairs. Nine of them, rough-hewn, arranged in a shallow arc facing the far wall—the wall beneath which, somewhere above and behind, the scratched door waited in its frame of black ice. The chairs were occupied.

Nine skeletons sat in nine chairs, facing the door, hands folded in their laps.

Delia stood very still. The frost on the floor crept up over her boot soles with a soft, patient crackle, like something settling in to stay.

They were old bones—old the way the stones were old, older than the town's founding charter, older than the company's acquisition documents, older than anything with a right to be under a parking lot in Vermont. Their clothing had rotted to lace and shadow, but traces remained: buttons of antler, a clasp worked in the shape of a key. And they were not slumped. That was what her mind snagged on, again and again, turning it over like a stone looking for the worm underneath. Dead things slump. Dead things collapse into the posture of surrender. These nine sat *upright*, spines straight, chins level, hands folded one atop the other with a neatness that spoke of long practice—of people who had sat this way so often, for so long, that the posture had outlived them.

Patient as furniture. The phrase arrived in her head unbidden, complete, and she hated it, because it was exactly right. They were not corpses. They were *installations*. They had been placed here the way a foundation is placed, the way a keystone is set, and they were doing a job.

"Oh," she said aloud, and her voice fell into the held air and did not echo at all this time. "Oh, you're the lock."

She should have left then. Every animal instinct she owned voted for leaving. But she was Senior Site Director now, and senior directors documented, and documentation meant her light, her camera, her feet carrying her along the arc of chairs while her radio stayed silent in her pocket because she did not want whatever was listening to hear her describe this out loud.

The first eight chairs were identical in their arrangement. It was the ninth that stopped her.

It stood slightly apart, at the arc's end, closest to the wall—and it was empty. Not empty the way an unused chair is empty. Empty the way a pulled tooth is empty. The seat was worn smooth in the center, polished by weight, and the frost around its base had grown in a spiral, tight and deliberate, like script written in a language of cold. On the armrest, someone had carved a single letter, deep and old: **A**.

Ada. Ashgrove. The name was seven years early or ninety years late, and either way it had been waiting here, worn into wood by hands that no longer existed, for someone to come down and read it.

"They were going to replace themselves," she whispered. "That's what renewal means."

It assembled itself in her with the ugly click of a lock finally turning. The company archives—the ones she'd pulled after the promotion, the ones with Ada's grandfather listed under *MAINTENANCE* in a column where a human being should have had a name—they'd used the same vocabulary. Renewal cycle. Component refresh. She'd read those words as bureaucratic rot, corporate euphemism, the usual way institutions talked about people. But they'd been literal. Every ninety years, the seal thinned. Every ninety years, it needed hands. New hands. Living hands. The founders hadn't built a town on top of a door—they'd built a town *out of* themselves, nine families seated in a circle of patience, holding the lock shut with their folded bones, and when the holding wore out, they were replaced. Generation by generation. Signature by signature. A page signed in your handwriting before you ever saw it.

And Wren Ashgrove—

Wren Ashgrove, found smiling at a door that shouldn't exist, seven years ago, at seventeen. Found kneeling, the police report said, though Delia had read the coroner's addendum and the position had been wrong for kneeling. Kneeling faced away from the door. This chair faced it. Wren hadn't been praying at the door. Wren had been *waiting to sit down.*

The ninth chair wasn't vacant because the ritual failed. It was vacant because the ritual succeeded halfway—it always succeeded halfway, that was the design, that was the trap. You filled eight seats across ninety years, family by family, and the ninth seat stayed open, held, *reserved*, until the debt came due and someone sat in it. The founders had engineered a lock that required a sacrifice on a schedule, and then they'd had the indecency to die before explaining it to anyone, leaving their descendants to rediscover the terms one horrified generation at a time.

Delia's camera hung forgotten at her side. She looked at the eight seated dead—at the nearest skull, its jaw set in what might have been serenity or might have been the rictus of a scream held for three hundred years—and she felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing beam of complicity cracking clean through.

She had signed the page. She had taken the promotion. She had told Okafor to log the frost patterns as *paraelectric ice phenomena* and the missing crew members as *voluntary departure*, and she had done it all in the comfortable belief that she was managing a liability, containing a problem, doing the grim necessary arithmetic that adults do. She had thought she was the company's hand in Hollowmere.

She was the company's *next chair.* That was what the promotion was. That was what the signature was. They weren't buying her silence. They were fitting her for a seat.

The frost at her feet had crept to mid-shin without her noticing. It wasn't painful. That was the worst part—it was warm, almost, a counterfeit warmth, the kind of warmth that a smile is warm, and she thought suddenly of the two crew members walking into the drifts with their faces lit up like children at a fair, and she understood that the cold didn't take you. It *invited* you, and you went, because everyone wants to be welcomed somewhere.

"No," she said—to the room, to the chairs, to the whole patient architecture of the place. "No. I know what this is now. I see it."

The held air changed. Not moved—*changed*, the way a room changes when someone enters it behind you. Her headlamp flickered once. In the stutter of darkness, the nine chairs seemed to lean, a degree, no more, the way furniture seems to move at the edge of sleep. The spiral of frost around the empty ninth chair had begun, very slowly, to turn.

Delia Marsh had spent her whole career being the person who looked at the numbers and signed anyway. She knew the exact weight of a decision made against your better self; she could have taught a seminar on it. And standing in a room full of seated dead who had made the same kind of decisions, three centuries ago, and were still sitting in them—still keeping the bargain their signatures had bought—she felt the last of her pragmatism go out like a match in wind.

Not because she'd become brave. Because she'd become *specific*. Fear is general; refusal is specific. She could not save the two who'd walked into the snow. She could not un-sign her page. But she could refuse the chair. Whatever else happened in Hollowmere, whatever the frost wrote and whatever the company ordered, she would not sit down in that smooth-worn seat and fold her hands and call it duty.

She keyed her radio. "Okafor. Get the winch ready. And get me everything we have on the Ashgrove family—all of it, back to the charter. I need to talk to Ada Ashgrove tonight."

Static, then his voice, thin with distance: "The novelist? Why him?"

Delia looked at the ninth chair, at the letter worn into its arm, at the spiral of frost turning beneath it like a slow white eye.

"Because he's the one they're saving the seat for," she said. "And I think somebody finally has to tell him."

Above her, faint through thirty feet of frozen earth, something knocked—once, politely, the way a guest knocks—from the underside of the world.
