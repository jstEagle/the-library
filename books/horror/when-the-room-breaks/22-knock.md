## 22. Knock

There is no time behind the door, but there is weather.

Ada learned this in the first hour, or the first year—the distinction stopped mattering the way a word stops mattering when you say it too many times. The cold here is not the cold of Hollowmere winters, which had teeth and appetite. This is an older cold, patient as sediment, the temperature of stone that has never once been warm. It does not bite. It simply *is*, the way he now simply is, and after a while his body—or the memory of his body, or the shape the door agreed to let him keep—stopped complaining about it.

He sits with his back against the inside of the door.

That is the first thing they tell you, though no one tells you anything here; you simply remember it, the way you remember your own name, except that you don't remember your own name, not exactly. You remember that you had one. Ada. Two syllables, like a small door opening. He holds onto it the way Wren used to hold onto the railing on the ice-slicked stairs outside their house, both hands, knuckles white, laughing at herself for needing to.

Wren.

She is here too. Or the shape of her is. She keeps mostly to the far side of the room—which is not a room, which is the space behind the ninth seal, which is a throat of stone and frost that goes further inward than any architecture should allow—and she practices words. That's what she does, most of the time. Practices. Her voice comes out close to right and then drifts, like a radio finding a station and losing it again, and Ada has learned not to flinch when it drifts, because flinching feeds something, and he made a promise about that.

*You will be seen,* he had said, kneeling at the threshold with her coat around his shoulders, the founders' rite half-remembered and half-invented in his mouth. *That was always the price. Not death. Being seen.*

And so he is seen. Constantly. By whatever lives in the deep cold past the last wall, by the thing that was never given a name because names are doors and this place ate all its doors long ago. It looks at him the way winter looks at a window: without malice, without interest, with the simple total attention of a force that has nothing else to do. At first it had been unbearable. He had screamed for—days? He still doesn't know. And then, somewhere in the screaming, he had understood what Wren must have understood seven years ago at the age of nineteen, kneeling where he knelt: that being seen is only torture if you are still trying to hide.

He stopped hiding. The cold became almost companionable after that. Almost.

Almost is the operative word behind the ninth seal, and Ada clings to it like a rosary.

* * *

The knocking started three days ago. Or three years. Time here moves like water under ice—you can see it going somewhere, but you can't say where.

It comes from the other side.

This should not be possible. He knows the architecture of his own imprisonment better than anyone alive, because he wrote himself into it: the seal closes from the inside, held by a willing pair, one who offers and one who holds. Nothing gets in. That was the whole point. He ended the bargain; he did not renew it; the town above sleeps soundly now beneath eight kept seals and one closed, and Delia Marsh is up there somewhere keeping the ledger hidden and telling no one, and the frost writes nothing on anyone's windows anymore except the ordinary fern-patterns that mean only that it is cold.

So the knocking cannot be real. And yet.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Three strikes, spaced evenly, polite as a neighbor asking to borrow sugar. They come through the stone behind him—not through the door itself, which is strange, since the door is the thinnest part of everything here—and they arrive not as sound exactly but as memory of sound, the way you can hear a song in your head more clearly than you ever heard it played.

Ada does not answer. Not at first.

Because he knows this knock. Of course he knows it. He has known it since he was nine years old, crouched at the top of the cellar stairs with Wren's hand crushing his, listening to the old men downstairs explain the rules of the initiation in voices gone soft with reverence: *When it knocks, you do not open. When it knocks, you let it think you might.* Seven years ago, in the dark beneath this very house, something had knocked three times on a door that opened inward, and Wren had answered it, because Ada had stepped back—one step, half a second, a cowardice measured in inches—and let her.

Now something knocks from the other side of his sacrifice, and every instinct he owns stands up screaming.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

"Ada," says the shape of Wren, from across the frozen room. Her voice is close today. Close is dangerous. "Someone's at the door."

"I know."

"You should answer it." She tilts her head, and in the grey light that comes from nowhere, her face is almost perfect. Almost is the operative word. There is a softness where her cheekbone should cast shadow, a smoothness like unfired clay. "You always answer it. That's what you told me once. That's why they chose us."

"I never said that."

"You will," she says gently. "You haven't said it yet. It's hard to keep the order straight in here."

He looks at her—at the shape wearing his sister's patience, his sister's crooked smile, saying things in their childhood private language that are *almost* right, one word wrong each time, always one word wrong, and God help him, he loves it anyway. Loves it the way you love a photograph of someone dead: knowing full well it cannot see you back, loving it precisely because it cannot.

"What's the word?" he asks. It's their game. Their old test.

The shape considers. Behind it, the deep cold stirs, attentive.

"Home," it says at last, and the word lands perfectly, and that is how he knows it's lying, because Wren could never say *home* without flinching—she'd called the house *the hold*, their secret word, *the hold, the hold,* dragging him by the sleeve away from the cellar door. The thing across the room smiles with her mouth and waits to see if he noticed.

"Not quite," Ada says softly.

"Not quite," it agrees, and there is something almost like respect in the borrowed voice. "But closer than yesterday."

* * *

He walks the perimeter while the knocking continues. It helps to move. The space behind the seal is larger than the house above ever was—he has measured it in footsteps, in memories, in the slow erosion of certainty—and it contains rooms he recognizes and rooms he doesn't and rooms that rearrange themselves when he isn't looking, the way the house above once rearranged itself around him, insisting he never left. Here the insistence is gentler. Here the walls don't lie to him so much as dream near him.

There is a corridor lined with doors that lead nowhere. He checked, early on. Every handle turns; every door opens onto the same grey cold, the same absence, the same hum like a held breath. He thinks these were the other seals, once. Eight doors for eight families, and behind each one, down the centuries, some willing pair holding the lock from the inside, hearing the town live and die above them like weather. The Ashgrove door is the ninth. The unfinished one. The one the ledger circled in Wren's handwriting: *the ninth seal, held open.*

Held open. Held. As if it were a hand waiting to be taken.

He passes the corridor and comes, as always, to the window.

There is no window. He knows there is no window. But there is a place where the cold thins and lightens, and if he stands there and lets his mind go soft, he can see Hollowmere. Not the real town—something truer than the real town, the way dreams are truer. Main Street under snow. The church with its eight founder-names cut into the lintel. The excavation site, paved over now, spring-green grass coming up through the seams despite everything, stubborn as gossip. And people. Small and moving and gloriously oblivious. A woman he thinks might be Delia Marsh, standing at the edge of the site longer than she needs to, looking at nothing, keeping something hidden the way you keep a coal alive under ash.

They don't remember him. He watched them forget. It happened the night the door sealed from the outside—Delia's chapter of the story, though she'll never write it—and it happened gently, the way snow covers a footprint: no violence, just gradual erasure, until even the funeral records showed a plot purchased for a Wren Ashgrove and no one named Ada at all. His headstone still bears both names. He saw it, once, through the not-window, before the grass came. *ASHGROVE.* Two of them. The town read it as an old family marker, a duplication error, a curiosity. They smiled at it, the way everyone in Hollowmere smiles, and walked on.

Being forgotten, it turns out, is just being seen from very far away.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Louder now. Or closer. In here those may be the same thing.

* * *

On the fourth day—or the fourth decade—the shape of Wren comes to sit beside him against the door.

This is new. She has kept her distance since the beginning, circling, practicing, testing him with almost-right words, and he understands why: she is not his sister, and she knows he knows, and there is a courtesy between them built on that mutual knowledge, fragile as ice over a well. But today she folds down next to him, knees drawn up, chin on her arms, in exactly the posture Wren used to take during thunderstorms, when the twins would sit in the hall closet with a flashlight and count the seconds between flash and boom.

"Seven seconds meant it was far away," the shape says. "Four meant it was close."

"One meant run," Ada says.

"One meant run." A pause. The hum of the held breath deepens. "I'm going to tell you something true, Ada Ashgrove. One true thing, so you can weigh it against all my false ones."

He doesn't answer. He has learned that silence draws truth out of things the way salt draws water.

"The knocking is real," she says. "And it is not coming for you."

The cold seems to lean in.

"It's coming *from* you. From what you left on the other side. You didn't just seal the door, Ada. You sealed yourself into the lock, and locks remember the hands that made them, and part of you—" the borrowed voice catches, artfully, terribly, "—part of you is still up there. Walking around in the gaps. In the spaces shaped like you. In Delia Marsh's careful silences. In the way the town flinches from the ninth grave without knowing why. And that part of you is lonely, Ada. That part of you is knocking."

"That's not true."

"No," she agrees pleasantly. "Probably not. But it's *almost* true, and you've had such a long time to practice believing almost-true things. You invented explanations for the noises for seven years. Scratching under the floors—old pipes. Breathing in the walls—the wind. You were the best self-deceiver I ever met. I admired it. We all did." The shape turns its clay-smooth face toward him. "We taught you every one of those excuses, you know. Back when you were a boy. The house teaches, and the children learn, and the learning feels like growing up. That's all initiation ever was. Graduation."

Ada closes his eyes.

Here is the thing he has never said aloud, not to Delia, not to the ledger, not to the watching cold: in the worst hours—in the screaming hours, in the first century or the first week—he had wanted the excuses back. Wanted the comfortable numbness of explanation, the warm blanket of *it's only the wind*. Being seen was the price, yes, but seeing was worse. Seeing meant knowing that every rationalization of his adult life had been the house speaking through him, teaching him to ignore his own sister's fear, his own terror, the truth scratching under the floorboards of his whole life. He had not survived Hollowmere. He had been *digested slowly*, and called it survival.

And now something knocks, claiming to be him.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Polite. Patient. Waiting to be let in, the way the thing beneath the house waited for Wren, the way the bargain waited seven years for Ada, the way Hollowmere waits, always, for whoever is due.

"If I answer it," Ada says slowly, "what happens?"

"Nothing happens," the shape says. "That's the point. That's always been the point. Someone answers, and the world stays the same, and the town sleeps, and the cold keeps its appointment. Answering is maintenance." A pause, and then, softer, in a voice that is almost—almost—hers: "Your grandfather was listed as maintenance. Did Delia ever tell you that? It runs in families. Tending the lock. Keeping the seal from the inside." The shape smiles Wren's crooked smile. "You're already doing the job, Ada. All you have to do is admit it."

* * *

He does not sleep here—sleep is one of the things the door traded away—but there is something like rest, and he takes it, and when he rises the knocking has changed.

It is no longer three strikes. It is a rhythm now. A pattern. And Ada stands with his palm flat against the cold stone of the inner door, listening, and his blood—or his memory of blood—goes thin and ringing, because he knows this pattern too.

It is the rhyme. The counting rhyme Wren used to chant on the cellar stairs, the one the old men pretended not to hear, the one that wasn't a children's rhyme at all but the rite broken into syllables small enough for a child to carry:

*Nine little seals in a ring of stone,*
*Eight kept faith and one alone—*

The knocking finishes the verse. Then begins again. Then again. Whoever is on the other side has all the patience of the thing he sealed away, and all the loneliness of the man he used to be, and the combination is unbearable in a way that pure horror never managed to be.

"Ada," the shape of Wren says, from across the room. "Don't."

"He's in pain."

"It's not a he. It's a gap. Gaps don't hurt. Only edges hurt, and you're the edge now, and it wants to wear you smooth."

"He's *me*."

"Yes," she says simply. "That's what I've been trying to tell you. That's the one true thing. The knocking is you, Ada. It has always been you. Every knock you ever heard under the floors, in the walls, at the door seven years ago—it was someone on the other side, answering themselves, forever, and calling it a bargain." The shape rises, crosses the frozen room, stands close enough that he can see the flaw in her, the single wrongness at the center of her face, the place where Wren ends and the cold begins. "The question was never whether to answer. The question is whether you can answer kindly."

Ada looks at the door. At his hands, which are still hands, mostly, still the hands that held a pen and wrote novels and once, unforgivably, stepped back.

His grandfather's job. His father's inheritance. His.

He thinks of Delia Marsh, standing at the edge of the paved-over site, keeping the ledger hidden, inheriting the maintenance role without ever having been asked—because no one is ever asked, that's the trick of it, the horror of it, the quiet engine under everything: the work continues because someone must continue it, and someone always does, and calling it a choice is the oldest excuse the house ever taught.

But it *is* a choice. That's what he learned here, in the cold, being seen. The founders made it a cage. Wren made it a deferral. He made it a closing. And whoever comes next—Delia, or Delia's successor, or some child not yet born on the cellar stairs—will make it whatever they have the courage to make it.

The knocking starts the rhyme again.

Ada Ashgrove—who was a coward for half a second seven years ago, and a liar for seven years before that, and a seal for all the time since—turns to face the inner door, and puts his ear against the stone, the way you'd put your ear against a wall to hear a heartbeat, and listens to himself on the other side, frightened and patient and alone.

Then he raises his hand, and answers.

Not by opening. Never by opening. He answers the way the kind answer: three knocks in return, evenly spaced, gentle as a neighbor offering sugar, and then he speaks, low and clear, in the voice the door agreed to let him keep, and the words come from somewhere deeper than the rite, older than the ledger, truer than any explanation he ever invented:

"I hear you. I'm here. You're not alone in there."

The knocking stops.

The cold holds its breath. Across the room, the shape of Wren watches him with something that might be pity and might be hunger and might, impossibly, be pride.

And then—from the other side of the door, faint and clear and unmistakable—a child's voice begins to count.

"One," it says.

Somewhere above, in a town that has forgotten him completely, the first frost of autumn writes itself across a window on Main Street, in letters no one will read until spring.

"Two."
