## 6. The Boy in the Cistern

The archive in Saint-Aubin occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, a room that smelled of cold stone and binding glue, lit by fluorescent tubes that buzzed at a frequency just below headache. Marguerite had spent two mornings there now, and the archivist — a narrow man named Goubert with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair — had stopped asking her questions after the first hour. He had simply begun fetching things. Boxes, ledgers, a rolled map of the commune from 1968 that he unrolled across two tables and weighted with brass paperweights shaped like snails.

She had told him she was doing a property survey. It was close enough to the truth that it came out of her mouth without friction.

The Delaunay farm sat on two parcels north of the village, forty-one hectares of mixed ground that had passed through three hands since 1974 and had been empty, in one form or another, for most of that time. The last sale was a paper transaction, a company registered in Lyon that had dissolved before the ink dried. Nobody had lived there properly since the family left.

*Left.* That was the word the records kept choosing, and the word kept snagging on something in her, the way a nail head snags a sleeve.

She worked backwards through the tax rolls. Armand Delaunay, head of household, farmer, listed through 1973. His wife, Hélène, née Vasseur. Four children. Camille, born 1954. The twins, Luc and Marc, born 1957. And at the bottom of the 1973 household register, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath:

*Étienne. Born 1966.*

She sat with that name for a long time before she noticed she was sitting with it. Étienne. Eight years old in 1974. The same age, give or take a season, as the hands in the flour.

In the 1974 register, the household listed three children. No annotation, no destination, no forwarding parish. She checked the confirmation records at the diocesan archive in the afternoon — Goubert had made a phone call and gotten her a reader's card on the strength of her survey fiction and, she suspected, the simple hunger of a man who spent his days alone with paper. The parish register for Saint-Aubin listed Étienne Delaunay's baptism, 1966. His first communion, 1974, spring. And then, in the column where a later record should have followed — marriage, ordination, burial — nothing. A blank that ran down the page like a held breath.

She asked Goubert about school records. He found them: the école communale's enrollment book, the same careful teacher's hand year after year. *Delaunay, Étienne* — present, present, present, the small marks marching through the winters of 1970, '71, '72, '73. Then, in January 1974, a single line in different ink, thinner, apologetic:

*Delaunay, E. — retiré. Envoyé chez des parents.*

Withdrawn. Sent to relatives.

She pulled every thread that line offered and none of it held. No relatives appeared in any subsequent register under the name Delaunay. No school in the surrounding cantons had received a transfer. No parish recorded a confirmation. The boy had been *envoyé* the way a letter is sent — and like a letter, he had simply stopped being anyone's business.

She copied the line out by hand in her notebook, and her handwriting came out smaller than usual, pressed hard, as if the page needed to be convinced.

* * *

The café in Saint-Aubin closed its kitchen at two, but the bar stayed open, and Madame Perrin held her corner table from three o'clock until the light went amber, every day, a ritual as fixed as tide. Marguerite had bought her two coffees already this week and knew better than to rush her. The old woman talked the way frost forms — slowly, in patterns, covering whatever was underneath.

Today she was talking about the Bresson girl's divorce, and Marguerite let her, turning her coffee cup in its saucer, waiting.

"Four children, the Delaunays had," Madame Perrin said, apropos of nothing, in the way that told Marguerite the woman had been circling toward it all along. "Camille, the twins. And the little one." She stirred her coffee without drinking it. "Étienne. A strange child. Not strange *bad* — people said that, but people say that about any child who watches instead of talks. He watched." She looked up, and her eyes, milky at the rims, were suddenly very direct. "You've been in the house."

"I'm doing a survey."

"Mm." The spoon clinked. "My mother cleaned for them, you know. Before her hips went. She said Hélène Delaunay scrubbed that kitchen floor every day of her life and it never came clean, and my mother said that wasn't the floor's fault." A pause. "Armand went into the cellar more than a farmer needs to. Everyone said he kept his wine down there. He didn't drink."

Marguerite kept her voice level. "The records say the youngest boy was sent to relatives. In January of '74."

"Sent away." Madame Perrin repeated it the way you'd repeat a word in a foreign language, testing its weight. "That's what was said."

"And was it true?"

The old woman set the spoon down. Outside, a tractor ground past on the road, and she waited until its noise had gone the way you'd wait for a wave to pass before stepping into the sea.

"In February of '74," she said, "the week after the boy was *envoyé*, my husband — God keep him — was hired to seal the cistern. The old well behind the house, the stone one, with the iron cover. Armand paid him cash and told him to pour the cap thick and not to come back for the tools. My husband left his good trowel down there and never asked for it." Her hands were flat on the table now, as if steadying it. "Fifty years, and I never thought about the timing until you started asking your survey questions. The boy goes away, and the water goes under stone, in the same week. In the same *week*, madame."

The café noise went on around them — the espresso machine, the radio, a man arguing cheerfully about rugby — but Marguerite heard it all from a distance, from the far side of a glass.

"Why seal a cistern in February?" she said. "The ground's frozen. It's the worst month for masonry work."

"It is," Madame Perrin agreed. "My husband said so. Armand said it couldn't wait." She finally drank her coffee, grimaced at the cold of it. "You want to know what I think? I think you should stop asking me and go look at the parish burial records. Because I was at church my whole life in that village, madame, and I never once saw that family at a funeral. Not for the boy. Not for anyone. People who lose a child — even a child that's only *sent away* — people light a candle. Hélène Delaunay never lit so much as a match."

* * *

That night the farmhouse did its small, patient work on her nerves. She ran through her routines: every light on, every door counted twice. Eleven doors. Eleven again. She wrote the number in the notebook she now kept for this purpose alone, dated it, and set it on the kitchen table where she could see it from the bed she'd made up in the front room, the one farthest from the mirrors.

She had covered the hallway mirror with a bedsheet on her second night. It hadn't helped. The sheet moved when there was no draft, a slow billow, in and out, like something beneath it breathing — or like a sail catching a wind that came from inside the wall. She had taken the sheet down on the fourth night, reasoning that a covered mirror was worse than an honest one, that at least she could see what the lag was doing. The lag was now a full second and a half. She had timed it against her watch. Her reflection lifted its coffee cup into the air and held it there, waiting for her hand to catch up, and the sight of it did something to her spine she chose not to examine.

Tonight she didn't look at it at all. Tonight she sat at the kitchen table with her notebook and the photocopied pages from the archive, and she made lists, because lists were the shape her courage took.

*Étienne Delaunay. b. 1966. Withdrawn from school Jan. 1974 — 'sent to relatives.' No relatives found. No confirmation. No burial. Cistern sealed same week, February 1974, cash, off-season, tools abandoned.*

She looked at the list for a long time.

The flour bin was six feet away. She had scrubbed it out on her first day — professional habit, you cleared the site before you assessed it — but she had kept the photographs she'd taken of the handprints before she cleaned them, and she took her phone out now and looked at them again. Five-fingered. Small. Pressed into the flour with the flat, deliberate pressure of a child who has been told to keep his hands where they can be seen. And warm. She had put her own hand over one before she'd thought better of it, and the flour beneath had held the heat of a living palm, a heat that should have faded in a cold kitchen in minutes and had not faded at all.

Eight years old in 1974. Eight years old in the flour, fifty years later. The arithmetic didn't work and didn't need to. She had taken enough strange jobs to know that the things that persisted in houses didn't age, because they hadn't been allowed to finish. A child sent away and a well sealed in the same week. A boy who watched, and parents who went into the cellar more than a farmer needs to. The story assembled itself in her with the grim click of a mechanism, and she hated how easily it assembled, hated that she had seen enough in her line of work to know that the simplest terrible explanation was usually the true one.

*Sent to relatives.*

She thought about the word *envoyé*, and about letters, and about the difference between being sent and being kept, and she put her pen down because her hand had begun to shake, and she was not going to dignify that with attention.

The grief arrived anyway. That was the thing nobody warned you about, in this work — that somewhere between the skepticism and the dread there was a door that opened onto plain, ordinary sorrow, and once it opened you couldn't shove it shut. She sat in the cold kitchen of the Delaunay house and grieved for a boy she had never met, a boy who had been erased so thoroughly that even his own parish register had let him go, and the grief was worse than the fear because the fear was about her and the grief was entirely about him. Fifty years in the dark under a stone cap. Fifty years of pressing his hands into flour and ash, into anything soft enough to take an impression, because a handprint was the only proof of existence a child knew how to make.

*I'm here. I'm here. I'm here.*

She got up, poured herself the last of the coffee, cold, and drank it standing at the window. Beyond the glass, the yard lay under a sky scoured down to its bones, and the old well was a low dark shape by the fence line, its iron cover furred with frost. She had walked past it a dozen times without a thought. Now she couldn't look away from it, and the frost on the iron caught the moon and threw it back in a pattern that looked, from here, from this distance, from the safe side of the glass — like a small hand, pressed flat, waiting.

* * *

She was nearly asleep when the knocking started.

Not at the front door. At the well. She knew it the way you know a sound in a dream — with certainty and without sense — three soft knocks, stone on stone, spaced like a child counting. She lay in the dark with her heart going hard and told herself it was ice shifting on the cover, contraction, physics, the honest world doing honest things.

Three more knocks. Then a pause the exact length of a held breath.

She did not answer. Madame Perrin's voice was in the room with her, *don't answer anything that knocks at 3 a.m.*, and she lay with her fists at her sides and did not answer, and after a long while the silence came back and settled over the house like snow.

But sleep, when it finally took her, took her down into the 1974 kitchen — the one from the mirror, the woman scrubbing the floor that was never dirty — and in the dream the woman straightened up, wringing her rag, and looked at Marguerite with Hélène Delaunay's too-wide smile, and said, in a voice like flour sifting:

*He saw what was under the house. You'll see it too. Everyone who stays long enough sees it.*

Marguerite woke at dawn with her notebook open on the table to a page she had no memory of writing. In her own handwriting, pressed hard enough to emboss three pages down, in letters that leaned as though written in a hurry or a fear she hadn't been present for, was a single line:

*The well was sealed the week he vanished. Open it.*

She sat there as the light came up grey through the frosted windows, and she did not know which frightened her more — that the house wanted it opened, or that, deep down, in the place where her courage lived, so did she.
