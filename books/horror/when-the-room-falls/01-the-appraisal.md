## 1. The Appraisal

The commission came through on a Tuesday, which Tamsin Ashgrove would later think of as the last ordinary day of her life.

She remembered it precisely because it was so unremarkable: rain against the office window, the radiator ticking, Priya leaning over the partition with two coffees and a printout she was already frowning at. "Hollowmere House," Priya said. "Out past Marlowe's Cross. Client wants a full structural survey and valuation before the estate settles it. Big property. Been empty a long time."

"Empty how long?"

Priya squinted at the file. "Doesn't say. That's odd." She said it lightly, the way you say something odd when it isn't odd enough to matter. "Access key's in the lockbox. You take it or I do?"

"I'll take it," Tamsin said, because she always did. She liked old houses. She liked the way they held their silence, the way dust hung in slanted light like something suspended mid-thought. Thirty-one years old, eleven years surveying properties nobody wanted, and she had never once been afraid of a building. Buildings were just money wearing wood and brick. Her father — no. Her mother used to say that. Her mother used to say a house is only ever worth what someone will pay to stop remembering it there.

Tamsin didn't remember why that phrase felt like a stone dropped down a well. She filed it away with all the other phrases that echoed without source, and drove out past Marlowe's Cross with the laser measure warm in its case beside her and the wipers keeping their patient time.

* * *

The house announced itself before she saw it, the way large animals do — a change in the pressure of the air, a hush along the tree line. The road narrowed, then gave up being a road altogether and became two ruts stitched with weeds, and then the trees opened and there it stood.

Hollowmere House was three stories of grey stone gone black at the joints, with a porch that wrapped around it like a hand considering whether to close. The windows were dark but not dead; they had the quality of windows that are watching from behind glass rather than simply letting light through. Ivy had taken the east wall entirely. A weathervane turned slowly at the roof's peak, though the morning was windless, and it turned, Tamsin noticed as she got out of the car, against the direction it should have turned, creaking like a door deciding.

She stood in the gravel for a moment longer than professionalism required. There was a smell off the house — not rot exactly, though there was rot in it somewhere, deep. It was the smell of a room that has been closed for years and then, very recently, opened. Dust and cold plaster and underneath it something almost sweet, almost like the inside of a piano.

"Routine," she said aloud, to hear a voice in the wide quiet. "Structural survey, condition report, valuation. In and out by four."

The porch steps were sound under her boots. The front door — heavy oak, paint peeling in long grey curls — was unlocked. Of course it was. The lockbox code had worked, but the door wasn't even latched, as if the house had been holding it ajar for someone expected.

Inside, the hall smelled of cold ash and old paper. Tamsin clicked on her headlamp, then, after a beat, every light switch she could find along the walls. Most of them worked. That was the first wrong thing, though she didn't log it yet: an unoccupied derelict with a live current and working switches, forty-one bulbs burning in an empty house. She told herself the estate kept it powered. Estates did that sometimes. She wrote it down anyway, because writing things down was how she kept the world in order:

*Utilities live. Unexplained. Note for client.*

Her handwriting looked strange to her on the page. Slightly too neat, slightly too upright, like a forgery of itself done well.

* * *

She began where she always began: the ground floor, room by room, measuring.

The laser measure was a Leica, three years old, calibrated in June. She'd trust it over tape, over her own eyes, over anything. She set it on its little tripod bracket in the corner of the first reception room and shot the long wall.

The display read 14.2 metres.

She walked the wall herself, counting paces, then paced it again. Fourteen metres was possible. Old houses did strange things. But when she shot the same wall from the opposite corner, the display read 11.6.

Tamsin stood in the middle of the room with the device humming in her hand and made herself be a professional about it. Reflected beam error. Irregular surface. She checked the wall — flat plaster, no alcove, no projection. She measured the width: 5.1 metres one way, 5.1 the other. Consistent. Only the length disagreed with itself, and only depending on which end of the room she stood at, as if the room were longer when approached from the hall and shorter when approached from the window, which was not how rooms worked, which was not how *anything* worked.

She remeasured six times. She got six different numbers, ranging from 9.8 to 16.4, and the numbers were not random — that was the part that sat wrong in her stomach later, when she let herself think about it. They descended. Each measurement was shorter than the last, as if the room were contracting politely around her, making itself smaller, more manageable. Accommodating.

*Possible subsidence affecting internal geometry,* she wrote, and heard how ridiculous it sounded even inside her own skull, and crossed it out, and wrote nothing.

By noon she had done the ground floor. The kitchen had a pantry whose interior dimensions exceeded its exterior ones by a full metre — she checked the external wall twice from outside through the ivy, pacing it, pacing it again. The staircase rose fourteen steps to a landing that had clearly been built for twelve; the extra two steps were narrower, older wood, darker, as if the house had grown them. On the second floor, a corridor ended in a wall that her plans — she'd found a 1970s survey folded in the estate file — said should hold a door.

She stood in front of that wall for a long time. The wallpaper there was different. Not newer — older, a pattern of small blue birds on cream, faded almost to ghosts — and it bulged very slightly, the way paper bulges over a filled-in doorway. She put her palm flat against it. Cold. Colder than the rest of the wall, cold like the other side of a river.

Behind the paper, faintly, she thought she heard something settle. Not a mouse-scratch. More like a person shifting their weight from one foot to the other after standing still a long while.

She took her hand back and went downstairs to eat her sandwich in the car, with the doors locked, telling herself the drive out here had been longer than she'd thought and she was tired.

* * *

She came back in. Of course she came back in — the job wasn't done, and besides, some part of her that she didn't examine had already decided this house was going to get finished today or not at all, because she was not coming back tomorrow. She knew that the way you know things without knowing them.

Afternoon light came through the west windows in long amber bars, and in that light the house was almost kind. Dust drifted. A grandfather clock in the study — stopped, she noted, at 7:14 — threw a shadow across the desk that moved when she moved, correctly, obediently, and she felt foolish for the morning's unease. This was what old houses did. They got into you. Every surveyor knew it: spend enough hours alone in a dead building and it starts speaking your language.

She worked the third floor last. Small rooms, servants' rooms, a nursery at the end of the hall with a window seat and a view of the overgrown garden and, half-under the peeling wallpaper, a crayon drawing of a house with too many windows, drawn by a child who had given every window eyes.

In the nursery she found the diary.

It was wedged where the skirting board had pulled from the wall, a gap like a slot cut for exactly this purpose. A child's notebook, cover soft with handling, spine cracked white. *Property of the surveyor,* was written on the first page in careful capitals.

Tamsin laughed — one short breath through the nose, alone in a dead nursery — because children wrote that sort of thing. *Property of the surveyor.* Some kid playing at her future job. She flipped the page to see the date, to place the child, to add a small human note to her report.

The handwriting was hers.

Not similar. Hers. The particular forward lean of her lowercase t's, the way she never quite closed her o's, the little hook she put on the numeral 7 that a college professor had once circled and called *affected*. Page after page, in her own hand, dated the summer of 1994, when Tamsin Ashgrove was seven years old and living — she frowned, reached, found the memory soft as wet paper — living somewhere. Near here. Hadn't she? There had been a summer. There had been a porch. There had been—

The entry read:

*We counted the doors again today. Eleven going in. Milo says the twelfth only comes out when you're leaving, and Wren says that's because the house likes to give you one more chance than you gave it.*

Milo.

The name went through her like a nail finding a stud. She sat down hard on the window seat, notebook open on her knees, and the afternoon light lay across the pages, and she read her seven-year-old self describing a brother she did not have. She had no brother. She was certain of it the way you're certain of your own name — and then she tested the certainty, pressed on it, and found that beneath it there was nothing. No memory of an only childhood. No memory of much of anything before the age of nine, actually, a flat grey stretch where other people had birthday parties and holidays and first days of school. Her mother had called it stress. Therapists had called it —

She couldn't recall what they'd called it.

Outside, the light was going. She hadn't noticed how late it was. The clock in the study said 7:14 now, she was almost sure of it, though it had been stopped at 7:14 since before she arrived, and she made herself stop thinking about that too.

*Finish the count,* said the child's handwriting, in her own hand, on the last written page. *Always finish the count. Never leave a door uncounted. The song starts at dusk.*

There was no song. There was no Milo. She was a grown woman with a laser measure and a client deadline, sitting in a nursery at dusk, and the sensible thing — the professional thing — was to pack up, photograph the notebook as a find for the estate file, and go home.

She photographed it. Then she put it in her bag instead of leaving it, and hated herself a little for how fast her hand moved.

* * *

Dusk came down over Hollowmere like a lid.

Tamsin did the last of the exterior shots from the gravel with the tripod, working quickly, breath fogging. The house behind the lens was just a house: sagging porch, blind windows, ivy. She checked the display of each frame twice. In none of them did the attic window appear, though she could see it plainly above her whenever she lowered the camera — a small square of deeper dark, and once, in one frame, briefly, a shape at the glass that might have been a curtain moving and might have been a face deciding not to be one.

Enough. She packed the gear, got in the car, and then — because the notebook's childish capitals were still ringing in her, *always finish the count* — she got back out and did the one thing she had been avoiding since morning.

She counted the doors.

Going in, that first time, she had registered the hallway without registering it: doors off the main hall, left and right, the tall front door behind her, service doors, the under-stairs cupboard. Eleven. She was sure it was eleven because eleven was the number she'd have sworn to in court; she'd counted them without counting, the way surveyors do, the way you know a room is twelve feet without measuring.

Now, leaving, lamp beam swinging, she walked the hall slowly and touched each doorframe as she passed, saying the numbers under her breath like a rosary.

"One. Two. Three."

The front door, tall and patient. Four.

"Five. Six. Seven."

The under-stairs cupboard breathed cold against her ankles as she passed it. Eight.

"Nine. Ten. Eleven."

And there, at the end of the hall where the corridor to the kitchen should have bent away into darkness, stood a door.

It was a good door. Solid, panelled, painted the same exhausted grey as all the others, fitted to its frame with the confidence of centuries. It did not shimmer. It did not glow. It simply existed, with the calm of something that had always been there and would be mildly surprised to learn otherwise — and it was open perhaps two inches, and from the gap came warmth, and lamplight, and a smell she knew from somewhere below memory: bread baking, and woodsmoke, and underneath it that sweetness again, the inside-of-a-piano sweetness, closer now, almost like breath.

From beyond the door, very softly, someone was humming.

It was a tune she didn't know. It was a tune her body knew. Her lips had already begun to shape the first note before she clamped her jaw shut, heart slamming, and stepped backward, and backward again, until her shoulders found the front door and the night air hit her face.

She locked the front door behind her with hands that would not quite obey. Stood on the porch in the dark. Made herself turn around and look back through the fanlight at the hall she had just left.

Eleven doors. She counted them through the glass, twice, her breath fogging the pane. Eleven, and the corridor bending away where the twelfth had stood, empty, ordinary, innocent.

The lights she'd switched on that morning were going out one by one, unhurried, room by room, like someone walking through the house behind her turning them off — except she was outside, and there was no one inside, and the last light to die was the one in the nursery, at the end, on the third floor, where a child's notebook had waited thirty years in a wall for her specifically.

Tamsin got in the car. Her notes were on the passenger seat, and in the dome light she saw that the page she'd written that morning — *Utilities live. Unexplained. Note for client* — had changed. The words were still hers, still in her hand, but beneath them, added in the same neat upright script, fresh ink, was a single line:

*Welcome back, Tam-Tam. Your room is ready.*

She did not remember any nickname. No one alive had ever called her that.

Down the weed-stitched road, in the rearview mirror, Hollowmere House stood black against the last grey of the sky — and on its porch, small and still, lit by nothing, something was waiting for her to come back.
