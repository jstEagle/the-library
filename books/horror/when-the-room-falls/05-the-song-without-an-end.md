## 5. The Song Without an End

Wren wouldn't come inside until the sun was fully up.

Tamsin learned this on her second morning at Hollowmere, watching from the kitchen window as he sat on the porch steps with his knees drawn up, small hands wrapped around his shins, waiting for the light to reach him like a cat waiting for a patch of warmth. He didn't shiver. He didn't fidget. He simply sat, patient as furniture, and when the first true gold of morning crossed the threshold and touched his shoes, he stood, brushed off his shorts, and walked in as if a door had opened that only he could see.

"You're up early," she said.

"I'm always up early." He climbed onto one of the kitchen chairs — the only intact one, its seat mended with wire she didn't remember mending — and folded his hands on the table like a businessman. "You slept badly."

"I slept fine."

"You counted the doors again. I heard you."

She had. Eleven going down, twelve coming back. She'd lain awake until four doing the arithmetic of it, trying to make the numbers reconcile, and somewhere around five she'd realized they never would, because the house wasn't adding. It was inviting.

"Wren." She sat across from him, keeping the table between them, though she couldn't have said why. A child. He was a child. Thirty years ago he'd been a child she invented, and now he was a child who drank tea she made and left wet rings on wood and knew things he shouldn't. "Yesterday you said you'd teach me the song."

His face did something complicated. For a moment he looked older than her — not in features but in posture, in the way his shoulders settled, in the weight that came into his eyes like sediment settling in still water. Then it passed, and he was seven again, gap-toothed and freckled, and she almost believed she'd imagined the other thing.

"The porch song," he said. "Yeah. You should learn it."

"Why?"

"Because you live here now."

"I don't *live* here. I'm surveying the property. There's a difference."

He looked at her the way children look at adults who are lying to themselves about something obvious — not cruelly, just with a kind of tired clarity. "You've been here three nights. You've stopped eating lunch. You talk to yourself in the hallways and the hallways answer. That's living here, Tamsin. The house already thinks so."

The kettle ticked on the stove behind her. She turned to take it off the heat, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and when she turned back Wren was looking at the window with an expression she couldn't read.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing. Sit down. I'll teach you the first verse."

* * *

They went to the porch, because Wren insisted the song belonged to the porch the way a signature belongs to a name. The morning was cold and clean, the lawn silvered with frost, the treeline beyond the yard standing dark and dense as a held breath. Tamsin sat on the top step with her coat pulled tight while Wren stood in front of her, feet planted, hands at his sides, taking the posture of someone about to perform something serious.

"It's four verses," he said. "You sing them at dusk. All together, all the way through, no stopping. Stopping is bad."

"Stopping is bad," she repeated. "Why?"

"Because the song isn't for you. It's a conversation. You start it, and something answers, and if you stop in the middle—" He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "It's like hanging up on someone mid-sentence. They come over to finish talking in person."

She wrote that down. She was aware, even as she did it, of how absurd it looked — a forty-one-year-old woman in a wool coat taking notes in a leather-bound appraisal journal while an eight-year-old boy lectured her on the etiquette of haunting. But her hand moved anyway, because writing things down had become, in the last three days, the only proof she had that the world was still operating on rules.

"First verse," Wren said. And sang:

*"Low door, low light,*
*keep your candle burning white,*
*count the corners, count the seams,*
*tell the house your honest dreams—*
*honest dreams, honest dreams,*
*the house forgets by morning's gleam."*

His voice was thin and clear, a child's voice, unbroken and unselfconscious, and the melody was simple — a rising fourth, a falling third, the kind of tune that seems like it has always existed, that no one wrote, that children simply find the way they find skipping rhythms and counting-out rhymes. Tamsin felt it settle into her chest before she'd decided to let it in.

"That's it?" she said. "That's the whole verse?"

"That's the whole verse. Say it back."

"I don't—"

"Say it back, Tamsin."

She closed her eyes. And the words were there, whole, waiting, as though they'd been sitting in her throat for thirty years behind a door she'd never thought to open. She sang them — quietly, badly, her adult voice clumsy around the simple intervals — and the strange thing wasn't that she remembered them. The strange thing was how *good* it felt. Like scratching an itch she hadn't known she had. Like setting down a bag she'd forgotten she was carrying.

When she finished, the frost on the lawn had begun, very slightly, to melt. Just near the porch. Just in front of the steps. A rough circle of dark grass where the silver had retreated, as though the ground itself had leaned toward the sound.

Wren nodded, satisfied. "Second verse."

* * *

By noon she had three verses.

The second was about doors — *count them going in, count them coming out, and if the numbers disagree, sing louder till they don't* — and when she sang it, every door in the house audibly shifted on its hinges, a soft wooden sigh running through the frame like a shudder through a body.

The third was about names. *Say your name into the floorboards, say it twice and say it slow, give the house a name to hold so it doesn't take your own.* Singing it, Tamsin tasted copper, and her own name felt strange in her mouth afterward, borrowed, like a coat from lost-and-found.

The fourth was the longest, and the saddest. It was about summer ending. About going home before dark. About mothers calling from other yards, other streets, other lives, and the children answering *yes, yes, we're coming, we remember the way.* When Wren sang it his voice caught on the last line, and Tamsin saw — clearly, unmistakably — that he was not catching it for effect. Something in him was grieving the verse as he gave it to her.

"That's four," she said, when the silence came back. "You said four. But there were five, weren't there? At dusk. Milo always said there were five."

The change in him was instant and total. It was like watching weather arrive — the light going flat, the air pressure dropping, everything about him closing at once.

"We don't sing the fifth," he said.

"You told me nobody remembers it."

"Nobody remembers it." He said it too fast, too smooth, the way you repeat a line you've been given. "Or—" and here he hesitated, and the hesitation was worse than anything he'd said so far— "or nobody who remembers can say."

"What does that mean? Who remembers?"

"Tamsin."

"*Who remembers, Wren?*"

He looked past her then, out at the treeline, and for a long moment he didn't answer, and the wind moved through the dead grass making a sound that was almost — almost — rhythmic. Almost melodic. Almost like a fifth verse being hummed by something with no mouth.

"People sang it," he said finally. "A long time ago. Before the porch songs, before the game, before any of this. Someone wrote it down once, maybe. Someone taught it to someone else. And everyone who ever sang it—" His jaw worked. "Everyone who ever sang it stayed."

"Stayed where?"

He pointed at the house. Not at a room. Not at a window. At the whole of it, the gray bulk of it rising behind them both, its empty windows reflecting the empty sky.

"In there. As part of it. The first four verses keep it fed and quiet. The fifth verse is different. The fifth verse is the house singing *itself*, and if you learn it — really learn it, all the way into your body the way you've learned these four—" He shook his head. "You don't get to stop being someone who knows it. The knowing stays. The knowing becomes walls."

"So don't tell me the melody. Just tell me the words."

"No."

"Just the—"

"*No.*" It came out cracked, doubled somehow, a child's voice with something vast underneath it, and the windows above the porch rattled in their frames, and Tamsin felt the sound of it in her teeth. Wren pressed his fists against his eyes. When he took them away he was crying without noise, the way children cry when they've learned crying gets them nothing. "Please. Don't ask me again. I'm asking you not to ask me again."

She reached for him and he stepped back, out of her reach, and the distance between them on that small porch suddenly felt enormous, measured in more than feet.

* * *

That evening she cooked dinner she didn't eat and took notes she didn't believe and told herself, in the flat professional voice she used for difficult clients, that tomorrow she would drive into town, call Priya, request a reassignment, walk away from Hollowmere House and its impossible measurements and its impossible boy and let it be someone else's file.

Then dusk came.

She didn't decide to go out to the porch. Her feet decided. She found herself standing at the screen door with the last of the light going amber across the yard, and the strangest part — the part she would turn over and over in the months after, examining it like a coin from a country that no longer existed — was that it felt *pleasant*. Standing there. Waiting. Like a child waiting to be called to supper.

The treeline darkened. The wind picked up its almost-rhythm. And without meaning to, without choosing to, Tamsin Ashgrove began to sing.

First verse. Low door, low light. Her voice came out steady and sure, far surer than it had any right to be, and the words fit her mouth like they'd been fitted there, and the frost-circle on the lawn from that morning was still there, bare earth in the shape of a listening.

Second verse. Count them going in. Behind her, inside the house, she heard the doors shift on their hinges, all eleven of them, or twelve, answering their cue like a choir coming in on time.

Third verse. Say your name into the floorboards. Copper on her tongue. Her name tasting borrowed. She kept singing.

Fourth verse. Going home before dark. Mothers calling. *We remember the way.* Her voice caught exactly where Wren's had caught, and she understood, in a flash of cold clarity, that the catch was built into the song, a designed flaw, a hinge —

And then she was humming.

That was the horror of it. That was the thing she would never fully be able to explain. Between the fourth verse and whatever came after, her voice hadn't stopped. It had gone on without her — a low, wordless melody rising out of her open mouth, climbing through intervals she didn't know, following a line she had never been taught, and it was beautiful, God help her, it was the most beautiful thing she had ever produced, and some deep animal part of her brain lit up screaming *this is not yours, this is not yours, you do not know this song*—

She stopped.

Mid-note. Clamped her jaw shut so hard her molars ached, and the unfinished note died in her throat like something swallowed alive.

The silence afterward was not silence. The house breathed. She could feel it — the whole structure inhaling through its walls, holding what she'd given it, savoring it. And the walls leaned. There was no other word. Standing on the porch she watched the vertical lines of the house — the corner boards, the window frames, the gutter line — go very slightly, very gently, out of plumb. Toward her. By degrees. The way a listener leans toward a whisper.

The note she hadn't finished hung in the air between her and the siding, unresolved, expectant, a question the house very much wanted answered.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, to it, to herself, to whatever was leaning. "No. That's not — I don't know that part. I *don't know that part.*"

The walls straightened. Slowly. Reluctantly. The way a dog backs off a plate, not convinced, only delayed.

She got inside and threw both bolts and stood with her back against the door, heart slamming, and became aware that she was humming again — quietly, under her breath, the same climbing line — and she made herself stop, and started again before she noticed, and stopped, and started, a war conducted entirely inside her own chest.

"Wren," she said to the dark kitchen. "Wren, what happens if you sing the fifth verse?"

From the stairs, in the dark, his voice came back small and flat and final:

"Then the house doesn't need a keeper anymore. It'll have a singer."

A pause. The floorboards overhead creaked, once, in a rhythm she refused to recognize.

"And Tamsin — whoever hums it first isn't learning it." The darkness seemed to lean with the walls. "It's remembering. Which means somebody already taught it to you."
