## 13. Small Surrenders

The morning she lost her mother's face began like any other in Hollowmere House: with the smell of bread that no one had baked.

Tamsin stood at the kitchen window with a mug of tea going cold in her hands and tried to picture her mother. It should have been easy. Thirty years of grief had worn the shape of that face into her the way water wears stone — the crooked part in her hair, the reading glasses pushed up on her forehead where they lived more often than on her nose, the small scar above her left eyebrow from a fall off a bicycle in 1981, a story Tamsin had heard so many times she could feel the gravel under her own palms.

She reached for it and found a smooth white wall where the face should have been.

Not blankness exactly. Blankness would have been honest. This was worse — a *suggestion* of a face, the way a mannequin suggests a person, all the geometry right and everything living gone. She could remember facts about her mother the way you remember facts about a stranger in a newspaper: born 1953, died 2011, liked her tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. But when Tamsin closed her eyes and said *Mum* into the dark behind them, nothing answered.

"Stop," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the house, to whatever was listening. She was always saying *stop* now. The word had become a kind of prayer, and like most prayers it went unanswered, or worse — it was answered by something else entirely.

Because here was the thing. When she reached for her mother's face and found the wall, the wall offered her something back. A warmth bloomed behind her eyes, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, and a face arrived. Soft. Smiling. Standing in a garden full of light that never quite resolved into any season. The woman's mouth moved and said, in a voice like honey poured over static: *You're doing so well, darling.*

"That's not her," Tamsin whispered.

The face smiled wider. Somewhere down the hall, a door closed itself, softly, the way a nurse closes a door at night.

* * *

She started writing on her arms that afternoon.

It was Wren's idea, though he'd delivered it sideways, the way he delivered everything — not as advice but as an anecdote, a story about a girl who'd lived in the house before either of them, who'd "tied knots in herself" so the house couldn't unpick her. He'd told it while sitting cross-legged on the nursery floor, small hands folded, his unaged face wearing an expression Tamsin couldn't read anymore. She used to be able to read him. That frightened her more than the twelfth door.

"Knots," she repeated.

"Things that are true are hard to untie," Wren said. "The house likes loose thread."

So she sat on the bathroom floor with a marker pilfered from the survey kit she hadn't touched in weeks — the survey, God, there had been a survey once, numbers and load-bearing walls and a deadline — and she wrote on her left forearm in careful block capitals:

MY MOTHER'S NAME WAS MARION ASHGROVE.
SHE HAD A SCAR ABOVE HER LEFT EYEBROW.
MILO IS REAL. MILO IS IN THE WALLS.
I AM TAM SIN ASH GROVE. I AM FORTY-ONE.
THE HOUSE LIES.

Her handwriting came out strange, the letters leaning like they were tired. She stared at the last line for a long time before adding:

I WANT TO LEAVE.

Then, because some part of her noticed the hesitation, the half-second gap where the pen hovered over skin and the house leaned in close enough that she could feel its attention like sunburn, she crossed out *want* and wrote *WILL*. I WILL LEAVE. Firmer. A knot instead of loose thread.

The marker stank of chemicals and the smell made her dizzy in a way that felt almost pleasant, which was its own warning sign, and she catalogued it as such, adding to her arm: THE DIZZINESS IS NOT YOUR FRIEND.

"You look mad," Wren observed from the doorway. Not unkindly. Nothing Wren did was ever unkind, and that was the whole horror of him.

"I look like someone holding onto a rope," she said.

"And if the rope is what's pulling you under?"

"Then I'll climb it." She capped the marker. "Whose side are you on, Wren?"

He looked at her for a long moment with those grey eyes that hadn't changed since 1994, since she'd invented him or he'd been given to her or whatever the truth of it was, and he said, softly, "The side that keeps you," and left before she could ask what that meant.

* * *

The birthday went next.

She discovered it on a Tuesday, or what the house insisted was a Tuesday — the days had begun arriving without papers, without provenance, just handed to her one at a time like numbered tickets. She'd been keeping a tally scratched into the pantry doorframe, four strokes and a slash, and she counted back through them trying to work out how long she'd been in the house and found the arithmetic wouldn't hold. Too many days for the amount of food in the kitchen. Too few for the length of her hair, which had grown past her shoulders when she was fairly sure it had been chin-length at the start.

And then, standing there with her finger against the doorframe, she thought: *my birthday is in—*

Nothing.

A month should have been there. March? June? She knew she was forty-one because it was written on her arm in her own hand, but forty-one *since when*? Her birthday wasn't just a date; it was a whole constellation attached to it — the year she'd gotten the red bicycle, the year Milo had hidden her presents so well that two of them surfaced in July, the cake her mother made every single year, lemon, with the icing pulled up into peaks like a crown—

Lemon. She could taste lemon. Sharp and sweet and wrong, because she couldn't remember the cake but the taste was there anyway, vivid, *provided*, sitting on her tongue like a coin left in her mouth.

"No," she said. "No, I'll do it myself."

She marched to the kitchen and ate an actual lemon — rind and all, eyes streaming, throat burning — to overwrite the counterfeit. It worked, mostly. The fake sweetness retreated. But as she stood over the sink gagging on pith, she caught herself thinking, with a calm that belonged to someone else: *the house's version tasted better, though, didn't it.*

That was the trick of it. That was the thing she was learning to fear most, more than the doors, more than the humming behind the twelfth panel. The house didn't steal roughly. It stole politely, and then it *replaced*, and its replacements were better than the originals. Its version of her mother never tired, never wept in the bathroom with the taps running. Its version of childhood summers was all golden light and cut grass, no boredom, no fights, no fear. It edited. And the edits were gorgeous, and every day it got a little harder to remember why gorgeous was suspicious.

Part of her — she made herself write this on her arm too, pressing hard enough to leave a mark — PART OF HER PREFERS THEM.

She looked at that sentence for a long time. Then she added: THAT PART IS NOT ALL OF YOU.

* * *

Rain was the third thing to go, and losing it nearly broke her.

It happened on a morning thick with weather — she could see it through the study window, grey sheets of it hammering the dead garden, puddles leaping in the yard. Proper rain, the kind that made the world smell new. Except the world didn't smell new. The world smelled of nothing at all, and when she pressed her palm flat against the cold glass and breathed in, waiting for that green mineral rush of petrichor, it simply wasn't there. She knew rain had a smell the way she knew France had a language. The knowledge sat in her head like furniture in a dark room — present, useless, unfelt.

She stood at that window for over an hour. Watching water fall and smelling dust and old wood and, underneath it, faintly, the bread again. Always the bread. The house's signature scent, warm and domestic and utterly false, laid over everything like a sheet over a body.

"It's taking the small things first," she told Wren that evening. They sat in the front room, which the house kept lit with a fire that gave heat but no smoke and burned logs that never shortened. "Face, birthday, rain. Small things. Why small things?"

"Because you won't miss them until they're gone," Wren said. He was winding a piece of string around his fingers, making a cat's cradle, undoing it, making it again. "A big theft wakes you up. You'd fight. But a face? A smell?" He shrugged, a child's shrug, boneless and easy. "You only notice the hole when you reach in."

"And then?"

"Then the house fills it." He looked up. His expression was gentle, and terrible. "It's very good at filling, Tam-tam. It's had such a long time to practice."

"Don't call me that." The nickname landed like a needle. No one alive called her that. No one alive *had ever* called her that except—

Except. Except whom? There had been someone. A small voice, a summer voice, singing on a porch. The name *Milo* rose in her like a bubble through deep water, and she grabbed for it, said it out loud to anchor it: "Milo. My brother is Milo. He went into this house in 1994 and he never came out and everyone pretended he'd never existed and I am going to get him back."

Wren's string went still between his fingers.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Hold onto that one. That's the strongest knot you've got."

"Why does it feel like you're coaching me?"

"Because I am." For a moment something moved behind his grey eyes — something old and drowned and sorry. "Tamsin. Listen to me. The house is patient with everyone else, but with you it's hurrying. Do you understand? It doesn't usually bother replacing things. With you it's *furnishing*. It wants you comfortable. It wants you settled before—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening around whatever came next.

"Before what?"

"Before the key finishes waking up." He set the string down. "How much do you remember about yesterday? Honestly."

She opened her mouth to say *all of it* and discovered, with a lurch like a stair missing in the dark, that she didn't. Yesterday was there in outline — meals, walking the halls, the tally marks — but the texture was gone, smoothed over, like a photograph left in the sun. She remembered *that* she had done things. She could not remember *doing* them.

"Oh," she said. Very small.

"Write faster," Wren said.

* * *

By the end of the week her arms were a palimpsest.

She'd filled both forearms, then her hands, the backs of her fingers, the soft skin inside her elbows. Facts stacked over facts, older lines fading where the marker wore away, newer ones crowding in. MY NAME. HIS NAME. THE YEAR. THE SONG HAS FOUR VERSES AND THERE IS A FIFTH. DO NOT HUM. DO NOT HUM. DO NOT HUM — written three times because twice she'd caught herself with the melody already halfway up her throat, sweet and rising, before she'd bitten down on it hard enough to taste blood.

The house retaliated in its own gentle way. The fabrications grew more generous. She woke one morning remembering a holiday that had never happened — her mother alive, Milo at nine years old building sandcastles, the whole family golden on a beach that glowed like the inside of a lamp — and it took her twenty minutes of reading her own arms to claw back to the truth: mother dead, brother in the walls, no beach, no lamp. Twenty minutes. And during those twenty minutes, the false memory had felt like *home* in a way nothing real had felt in years.

She wrote on her wrist that night, in letters large enough to read at a glance: IF IT'S BEAUTIFUL, CHECK IT TWICE.

But her arms were running out of skin. That was the arithmetic she lay awake with in the dark, listening to the walls hum their endless patient tune: she was a finite surface, and the forgetting was not finite. Already she'd had to decide what earned ink. Her mother's face — gone, unwritable, no words could rebuild it. Her therapist's name — let it go, what use was it. The smell of rain — she'd written RAIN SMELLS LIKE THE WORLD AFTER and stared at it, knowing it was true and feeling nothing, a sentence describing a country she'd been deported from.

Some surrenders were small. Some were small only at first.

On the last night of the week — or what the house called night, the windows going black on schedule — she sat on the nursery floor surrounded by candle stubs and added the final line to her left forearm, beneath the fading knot of MILO IS REAL:

IF I FORGET HIM, THE ARMS REMEMBER. IF THE ARMS FADE, THE WALLS KNOW HIS VOICE. GO TO THE TWELFTH DOOR. HE'LL SING IT BACK TO ME.

She blew out the candles. In the sudden dark, the house exhaled — that long, satisfied breath through the floorboards — and somewhere below, behind wood that was not wood, faint and clear and thirty years unchanged, the humming changed key.

It was calling her name.
