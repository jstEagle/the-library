## 18. Singing Along

The sheet music sat on the nursery floor where I'd left it, and all night the house had been trying to take it back.

I knew because I'd slept — if you could call it sleeping — with my hand flat on the paper, and three times I woke to feel it moving beneath my palm like a live thing, a slow patient tug, as though the floor itself were swallowing. Each time I pressed down harder. Each time the tug stopped, waited, and began again. The house had infinite patience. It was made of nothing else.

Dawn came gray through the boarded window, the light falling in slats across the five staves of the original score. Four verses, whole. The fifth torn away to its stub, Milo's initials penciled at the edge in a child's careful hand: *M.A., age 9.* He'd been proud of knowing something the grown-ups didn't. He'd guarded that verse for thirty years inside the walls, holding it the way you hold your breath.

And now I had to learn it before the house learned me.

I sat cross-legged on the nursery floorboards with the music in my lap and my arms bare, the truths I'd written there already fading — *my name is Tamsin Ashgrove* gone soft at the edges, the ink blooming like a bruise. My birthday was a smudge. My mother's face was a warm blur where a face should be, and when I tried to summon it, the house helpfully offered me a substitute: a kind woman in a yellow kitchen, humming, her features smoothed like a thumbprint in wax. A mother-shaped absence filled with pleasant light. Part of me wanted to keep her. That was the part I didn't trust anymore.

"Read it," I said aloud, to myself, to Milo, to the room. "Don't sing it. Read."

The notes were old notation, quill-inked, some of the clefs wrong in ways that suggested they'd been written by someone who'd learned music by ear and fear. I traced the melody with one finger, silent, feeling the intervals without giving them voice. Up a fourth. Down, down, then a strange leap — a minor sixth, the interval that makes the skin prickle, the sound of a door opening onto dark. The fourth verse ended on an unresolved chord, hanging, waiting for the fifth to catch it.

I could almost hear how it would resolve. That was the trap, I understood now. The song wanted finishing the way a sentence wants its last word. Any mind that held four verses would reach, involuntarily, for the fifth — and the reaching was the hook.

I read the words beneath the staves instead. They were in no language I knew, or in several at once, letters shifting when I looked away and back. But under the shifting there was a grammar I recognized from Wren's teaching, from the porch dusks of 1994: *keep it out, keep it out, count the doors, hold the line.* And then, in the margin beside the torn stub, in different ink, smaller, hurried:

*the last verse is not sung to keep it out. it is sung to let it go.*

My finger hovered over the first note of the fifth verse — the fragment of it, the few surviving measures before the tear.

And I hummed.

It wasn't a decision. It was what fingers do when they hover over piano keys; it was what a throat does when a melody has lived in it since childhood, coiled around the shape of a brother's name. One note, low, barely breath — the resolving tone the fourth verse had been reaching toward for two hundred years.

The house inhaled.

I felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm — a sweetness in the air, a settling, every room leaning toward me at once. Down the hall, doors I hadn't opened swung wide in welcome. The nursery wallpaper seemed to flush warmer, the faded roses deepening to living red. And my edges — God, my edges. They went soft. Not painful. That was the horror of it. It felt like sinking into a bath drawn to exactly my temperature, like being known so completely that the boundary between me and the knowing stopped mattering. The second note rose in my throat without my permission, harmonizing now, finding a part beneath the melody, a low root note, and the walls drank it, and somewhere far below the floorboards something vast turned over in its sleep and smiled.

*Tamsin.*

Wren's voice, but wrong — cracked, urgent, a child's voice pushed through thirty years of use.

I couldn't stop. The third note was already coming. I watched my own mouth open from somewhere slightly outside myself, watched the breath gather, and I thought with strange clarity: *this is how it happens, this is how it got everyone, not by force, by invitation* — and the note came out sweet and true and the hallway beyond the nursery door lengthened with pleasure, boards flexing like muscle—

Hands closed on the sheet music and tore.

Wren ripped it from my lap — tore it clean down the middle, then again, again, five pieces, six, stuffing them into his shirt, his small chest heaving, his eyes wet and furious and terrified all at once.

"NO." He screamed it into my face, into the room, into the walls. "No, no, NO — do you know what you were DOING?"

"Wren—"

"That's HOW." His voice broke apart. "That's how it got me. Do you understand? Nobody dragged me. Nobody chained me to the wall. I sang along. I was seven years old and I loved the song and I sang ALONG—" He was shaking, the torn pages crinkling against his ribs. "One verse. I harmonized for ONE VERSE and the house said *yes, more, yes, stay,* and I stayed, Tamsin, I stayed for a hundred years before you were born, and I am STILL HERE—"

The nursery shuddered. The pleasure draining out of it was worse than the pleasure had been — the walls contracting, the warmth curdling, the roses in the wallpaper bleeding their color back to gray with an almost audible sulk. Somewhere below us, the vast thing rolled over again, and this time it did not smile. A door slammed at the end of the hall. Then another. The house counting itself, counting us, deciding.

Wren grabbed my wrist. His grip was stronger than a child's hands should be, and cold as the iron key had been.

"Say your name," he demanded.

"Tamsin Ashgrove."

"Say it like you mean it. Say it like it's the only thing you own."

"Tamsin Ashgrove." My voice came out thin. In the silence after it, I could still feel the melody sitting in my throat like a swallowed coal, warm, patient, waiting to be fed again. "Wren, I couldn't stop. I read the warning in the margin and I opened my mouth anyway."

"I know." All the fury went out of him at once, and what was left underneath was older and sadder than any child should carry. He sank down onto the floorboards beside me, small and rumpled, the torn music clutched to his chest like a wounded bird. "That's the thing nobody tells you about it. It doesn't take anything you're protecting. It takes the thing you love too much to protect carefully."

We sat in the ruined nursery while the house sulked around us. Through the boarded window the dawn was strengthening, ordinary and gold, a world going on without us. I looked at my hands. They were still mine. Mostly. When I spread my fingers I thought I could see, at the very edges of them, a faint translucence — or maybe it was just the light, maybe it was just morning, maybe I was learning to lie to myself in the house's own dialect.

"How much did it get?" Wren asked quietly. "When you sang. How much did it take?"

"I don't know." I searched. Mother's face: the substitute woman in the yellow kitchen, smoother now, more confident, less real. Rain: I reached for the smell of rain and found a description of rain instead, the word *petrichor* floating up like a dictionary entry, clean and dead. "Something. I can't tell what's missing because the missing parts have been... furnished."

"That's how it works." He nodded slowly. "It doesn't leave holes. It leaves rooms."

"And the verse?" I looked at the torn pieces against his chest. "Did I learn any of it? Before you—"

"You learned the first phrase." His jaw tightened. "Which means it knows you have the first phrase. Which means tonight it will start offering you reasons to use it."

"What kind of reasons?"

Wren didn't answer right away. He looked at the doorway, where the hallway had shortened itself again, polite, harmless, twelve feet of rotten corridor pretending to be only a hallway. When he spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"It'll give you back something you lost. Something you'll want badly enough to open your mouth for. That's the trade it always makes. You sing a phrase, it returns a memory — real, whole, better than the original, polished until it gleams." He finally turned to look at me, and his eyes were ancient in his unlined face. "Last night it took your mother's face. Tonight it will offer it back. And Tamsin — it will look exactly like her. It will smell like her. It will say the thing you most needed her to say. And you will not be able to tell, because the part of you that could tell is the part you already gave it."

The coal in my throat pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat that wasn't mine.

I stood up. My legs were steady, which surprised me. Maybe terror was a kind of ballast. I held out my hand to Wren.

"Give me the pieces."

He flinched. "Absolutely not—"

"I'm not going to sing it." I kept my hand out, level. "But Milo memorized it alone in the walls for thirty years because he had no choice. I'm going to learn it the same way — silently, by heart, with my mouth shut. And you're going to watch me the whole time, and the moment I open my lips to make a sound, you tear it again. Burn it. Swallow it. Whatever it takes."

"You don't understand what you're asking. Watching you learn it is like watching someone juggle knives over a sleeping—"

"Wren." I crouched to his height, the way you kneel for a child, except we both knew which of us was the child here. "You told me once that you've been keeping my room ready. Thirty years, you kept it ready. Why? If you're just the house's lure, why does it matter to you whether I walk out?"

His face did something complicated. Grief and hope fighting over the same small ground.

"Because you're the first one who ever came back for somebody else," he whispered. "They all come for themselves. Or they come because the house sent for them. You came to find your brother."

"Then help me find him. Help me finish this."

For a long moment he didn't move. The house held its breath around us — I could feel it listening through every board, every nail, every layer of sedimented wallpaper, patient as rot. Then Wren unfolded the torn pieces of the score with great care, smoothing them on the floor between us like a map of a country we were about to invade, and put his small cold hand over mine.

"Not by voice," he said. "By feel. Like Milo taught himself. You press the notes into your body where your throat can't reach them — here." He guided my hand to my sternum. "And here." To the pulse point at my wrist. "The song lives in the body before it lives in the breath. If you keep it below the breath, the house can't drink it. That's the trick. That's the only trick there is."

"And if I slip?"

"Then I'll do what I should have done a hundred years ago." He smiled, and it was the saddest expression I had ever seen on a face that never aged. "I'll scream louder than you."

We worked through the morning. Phrase by phrase, silently, my lips sealed, my finger tracing the torn notation while Wren mouthed the counts and tapped the rhythm against the floorboards — sound enough to guide, too broken to feed on. The house hated it. I felt its attention like weather, pressing at the windows, pooling under the door, looking for the gap where a note might leak. Twice the melody rose in me unbidden, warm and willing, and twice I bit it back behind my teeth, and twice Wren's hand shot out and gripped my knee hard enough to bruise, anchor and alarm in one.

By noon I carried the first half of the fifth verse in my chest, wordless, weightless, a secret kept below the waterline.

That was when the house made its first offer.

It came as a smell — coffee, burnt the way she always burnt it — drifting up through the nursery floorboards. Then a sound: a kettle, a radio murmuring low, a woman humming off-key in a kitchen that existed thirty years ago and six miles away. Then her voice, clear as a bell across water, calling up the stairs in the exact cadence I had spent three decades failing to forget:

*"Tamsin! Come down, love. Your brother's home."*

Every hair on my arms stood up. The missing memory came rushing back into the shaped space the house had built for it, perfect, gleaming, polished until it hurt — and my lips parted, and the first note of the verse climbed my throat like a bird returning to a roost—

Wren's hand closed over my mouth.

Behind us, softly, in the empty hallway, a door began to count.
