## 4. What the Twins Keep Under the Bed

The rain came in off the fields that afternoon like something with an errand, and Odile Thackeray stood at the kitchen window of the Harrow farmhouse watching it flatten the yard into pewter. He had been on the property four days now. Four days of methodical inspection: joists tapped, sills examined, droppings catalogued by species and diameter. Four days of finding nothing that any pest had made.

And one night of hearing a bell that did not exist ring eleven times over the millpond.

He had not written about the bell. That was the strange discipline he'd discovered in himself at three in the morning, sitting up in the narrow guest room with his logbook open and his pen dry against the page. His creed was total documentation—*what is observed is recorded; what is recorded is real*—and yet when it came to the bell, his hand simply refused. Not fear, exactly. Something more like courtesy. A sense that writing it down would be a kind of introduction.

*You rang. I heard. Allow me to present myself.*

He clicked the pen closed and went downstairs to find the twins waiting for him in the hall.

They were always somewhere, Wren and Sorrel. Twelve years old and identical only in the way weather is identical to itself twice—the same front arriving from two directions. Wren watched him the way a lighthouse watches a ship: steadily, without affection, as a matter of duty. Sorrel watched him the way you watch a wasp that has landed on your sleeve. This morning they were both standing at the foot of the stairs in their wool socks, side by side, hands behind their backs, like children summoned before a headmaster who happened also to be their idea.

"Mr. Thackeray," Wren said.

"Wren."

"You want to see what's under our bed."

"Sorrel," Odile began, "if this is about the scratching—"

"It's not about the scratching." Sorrel's voice was flat and quick. "There's no scratching. There's never been scratching. Gran told the council there was scratching because the council sends people, and sometimes the people are useful, and mostly they leave after two days."

"And you've stayed five," Wren said.

Odile looked between them. The rain ticked against the window glass. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"You said *our* bed," he said carefully. "You share a room?"

"We share everything," Sorrel said. "That's rather the point of us."

Wren turned and started up the stairs without another word, and after a moment's hesitation that he would later examine for the exact texture of its foolishness, Odile followed.

* * *

The twins' room sat under the north gable, the coldest corner of the house. Two beds, made with military corners that could not have been the work of twelve-year-olds and therefore were. A washstand. A window looking out toward the millpond, which lay grey and patient in the rain like a coin at the bottom of a pocket.

Wren knelt at the far bed and dragged out a shoebox.

It was an old box, the cardboard gone soft and furred at the edges, the lid darkened where decades of thumbs had pressed it open. She set it on the bed between them with both hands, the way you'd set down something either holy or explosive, and lifted the lid.

Slate. Dozens of pieces of slate, small ones, roof-slate sized, each scratched over with rows of tally marks. Four strokes and a diagonal strike-through, four strokes and a strike-through, group after group, some slates filled edge to edge until the marks crowded together like grass seen from above. The oldest pieces had gone brown at the corners. The newest still showed the silver gleam of fresh scoring.

"One for every night," Wren said. "One slate lasts about a year now. It used to be less."

Odile did not touch the box. He found that his hands had retreated into his pockets without consulting him.

"Nights of what, Wren?"

She looked up at him with those level grey eyes, and for a moment he saw Vesper in her face—not a resemblance so much as a preview. "You know what nights. You counted along on Tuesday. Eleven. We heard you stop counting after six because you thought you were being silly."

The cold in the room seemed to organize itself around him.

"How long," he said, "has your family been keeping these?"

"Gran says the first tally was cut into the cellar door with a paring knife," Wren said. "But doors rot. So we keep slate. Slate keeps." She lifted a piece out and turned it over. On the back, in a child's careful hand, was a date: *3rd of October.* "This is mine. Sorrel does odd-numbered months, I do even. When we were little we couldn't agree, so Gran said split it, and—" she shrugged, a small economical motion, "—that's how it's been."

"That's how *what's* been? What happens if you miss a night?"

Wren considered the question with genuine seriousness, the way she might consider a sum. "We don't know. Nobody's ever missed one. That's sort of the whole thing, Mr. Thackeray. You don't find out what happens if you miss, because finding out is missing."

"That's circular," he said.

"Yes," said Wren, pleasantly. "Most true things are."

Odile crouched slowly beside the bed so that his eyes were level with hers. Up close he could see the tally marks weren't uniform. The older slates carried a variety of hands—a broad confident gouge, a thin nervous scratch, a round schoolroom script—and the newest, the last year or two, were all in the twins' two hands, alternating by month like sentries changing watch.

"Your grandmother. Aldous. They do this too?"

"Everyone does it who lives here. Gran did it when she was our age. Great-Gran did it. There's a slate in the cellar older than the house." Wren set the piece back with exaggerated gentleness. "It's not a chore. It's more like... feeding something. You know how you have a fire, and if you don't put wood on it, it doesn't get angry, it just goes out? It's not like that. It's the opposite of that."

"The opposite," Odile repeated.

"If you stop feeding a fire it dies. If you stop doing this—" she tapped the box lid once, lightly, "—it doesn't die. It just starts paying attention to why."

From the doorway, Sorrel spoke, and Odile realized she had positioned herself there deliberately, filling the frame, arms crossed, a small sentinel against the corridor.

"You should go home, Mr. Thackeray."

"Sorrel—"

"You write everything down. We've seen you. Little book, little pen, everything goes in the little book." Her voice wasn't hostile. It was worse than hostile—it was pitying, the way you'd speak to a man cheerfully walking toward a frozen pond. "Gran likes you. Aldous thinks you're funny. But you need to pack your case and catch the morning bus and never write one single word about this house again."

"I'm here to do a job—"

"There's no job!" It burst out of her, high and cracked, and then she mastered herself with an effort that visibly cost her, dragging her voice back down to that flat adult register the twins used like a shared coat. "There's no rats. There's no mice. There's nothing in the walls except the walls. You're not here because of scratching. You're here because the room wanted someone new to look at, and Gran knew better than to say no, and now you've been here five days and you've been *looking*, and looking is—"

"Sorrel." Wren's voice was quiet but it landed like a hand on the shoulder. "He hasn't done anything yet."

"He writes things down," Sorrel said. "That's already something."

* * *

*Here is what I know,* Wren Harrow thought, later, lying in the dark while the rain worked the window and her sister's breathing went slow and even across the gap between the beds.

*I know the bell rings eleven times and we make one mark. I know Gran's hands shake now, so she does her marks in pencil, and Aldous pretends not to notice, and every month Gran's pencil-marks get lighter and lighter like she's fading along with them.*

*I know the room is not the house. Gran explained it once, when I was eight, kneeling by my bed the way mothers kneel, and she said: the house is just where the room keeps its things. The room is bigger than the house. The room is wherever the ledger is, and the ledger is wherever the count is, and the count is wherever somebody is listening.*

*I know that Mr. Thackeray listens. He listens with his eyes too, which most people can't do. He looked at our slates today and he didn't laugh and he didn't pray and he didn't do the face grown-ups do where they decide you're making it up and smile kindly at your nonsense. He got very still, and his hand went into his pocket where his book is, and I saw his fingers move inside the fabric like a spider testing its web, and I thought: oh no. Oh no, he's going to be devout about it.*

*Because that's the thing nobody tells you about people who write everything down. Gran says the drowned don't read. They don't read words, they don't read names, they don't read cleverness. What they hear is care. And a man who writes everything down is a man who cares about everything, and a man who cares about everything is singing all the time, whether he knows the tune or not.*

*Sorrel wants him gone. Sorrel is right, probably. Sorrel is usually right, which is the exhausting thing about having a twin.*

*But I showed him the box anyway.*

*I've been turning that over all evening, why I did it. Partly because he asked nothing and waited, and waiting is rare in grown men. Partly because the slates were getting heavy—not in the hand, in the chest—and it seemed suddenly unbearable that we should be the only ones who knew what they weighed.*

*And partly—I think partly because of the twelfth mark.*

*Nobody talks about the twelfth mark. Every slate in that box has eleven tallies struck through and then, below them, a space. A blank. Gran says the blank isn't empty, it's reserved. Like a chair kept at a table. And tonight when Mr. Thackeray crouched down by the bed and looked at the newest slate, his eyes went to the blank space the way everyone's do eventually, and then—they did something different. They went back up to my face. To me. As if the blank might be a person, and the person might be standing right there.*

*I didn't like it. I liked it enormously. Both of those are true, which Sorrel says is impossible, and Sorrel is usually right, but not about this.*

*Tomorrow I'm going to ask him what he writes in his book. Tomorrow I'm going to ask him if he's ever heard of a count balancing. And if he looks at me the way he looked at the blank space—*

*Well. Then maybe the room didn't send him to be counted.*

*Maybe the room sent him to do the counting.*

*Across the room, Sorrel sighed in her sleep and rolled over, and outside, far off, though it was nowhere near the hour and nowhere near the night for it, something that had been bronze once gave a single soft note, like a throat cleared in an empty church.*

*Wren lay very still and did not reach for the slate.*

*Not yet. One mark per night. Those were the rules, and the rules were the wall between the family and the water, and you did not lean on the wall.*

*But she lay awake a long time, listening, wondering if anyone else in the house had heard it too—and whether, come morning, the pest controller's neat little book would have a new entry in it, dated tonight, and what word he would choose for a sound that hadn't technically happened.*

*Please don't write it down, she thought at him through two floors of old farmhouse timber, with all the force a twelve-year-old can put behind a wish.*

*Please, please don't write it down.*

* * *

Downstairs, in the kitchen he had been quietly forbidden to enter after dusk, Odile Thackeray sat at the scrubbed table with a single candle because he'd run out of reasons to trust the electric light, and opened his logbook to a fresh page.

The house creaked its way through its settling noises. Rain on slate. Wind in the elms. All of it ordinary, all of it documentable, none of it the point.

He wrote: *Day 5. Interviewed the twins. Subject: tally records, slate, approx. 90+ units, continuous series, one mark per night, commencing (per subject) 1911.*

His pen hovered.

Below that, his hand—his disciplined, professional, incorruptible hand—wrote: *On the night of the 4th, at approximately 2 a.m., I observed—*

And stopped.

Because the candle flame had leaned sideways, though the window was shut. Because the ink of the words *I observed* had gone slightly glossy, as if the page were breathing damp. Because somewhere beneath the floor, beneath the cellar, beneath the packed clay and the coffin-dark roots, something vast and patient had turned its attention toward the sound of a nib moving across paper—the way a man asleep turns toward a voice saying his name.

Odile looked at the sentence. Three words. *I observed—*

Observed what? A bell melted down in 1911. A sound with no source. If he finished the sentence, he finished the introduction. If he finished the introduction, he became, formally and forever, a correspondent.

In the hallway above, faintly, he heard a floorboard shift. One of the twins, awake, listening to whether he would write it.

Odile Thackeray drew a single line through *I observed*, blotted the page, and closed the book.

The candle straightened. The house exhaled.

But as he climbed the stairs to bed, past the twins' door, past the small cold room where a shoebox full of years sat under a made bed, he could not shake the arithmetic that had followed him up from the kitchen, quiet as a second shadow:

Ninety years of slates. Eleven rings a night. And a blank space on every one of them, reserved—

—for a name that had never yet been written down.

Until, perhaps, tonight. Until perhaps, in a farmhouse kitchen, by candlelight, a meticulous man had come within three words of finishing a century-old invitation—and something below the floor had felt the pen pause, and waited, with the whole of its drowned patience, to see whether he would take it up again.
