## 12. Eleven Names

The parish archive smelled of cold stone and older paper, and Odile Thackeray loved it the way other men loved churches.

It occupied the ground floor of the old tithe barn behind St. Brannoc's-in-the-Field, a building that had not held a congregation since before the war. The vicar's records had been boxed and shelved by some diligent hand in the 1950s and left to sleep in the dark ever since. A single bulb hung from a rafter on a cloth-wrapped cord. When Odile pulled it, moths scattered from the shade like startled thoughts.

He set up at the long table by the north window, where the light came down grey and even through glass thick with age. He laid out his tools with the small ceremony he permitted himself: logbook open to a fresh page, pencil sharpened twice, ruler, magnifier, and the photograph he'd made of the cellar ledger's first pages, developed overnight in the farmhouse scullery while the household slept around him like people pretending very hard to be asleep.

*Eleven names,* Vesper had said once, or almost said, her mouth closing around the number like a door pulled shut. *The count must balance.*

The ledger's opening column ran:

1. Merrick Doyne
2. Tamsin Doyne
3. Colm Petherick
4. Elowen Petherick
5. Jago Trevanion
6. Sennen Trevanion
7. Bruer Coad
8. Kerensa Coad
9. Pascoe Lanyon
10. Morwenna Lanyon
11. Ottillie Harrow

And beneath it, half a line lower, as if it had been added by someone leaning over the shoulder of the original writer: a twelfth entry, begun and abandoned. Two letters only — *Ma-* — followed by a long ink stroke that trailed off the edge of the ruled line and pooled there, a small black lake no bigger than a thumbnail.

Odile had stared at that pool for a long time the night before, the way you stare at a hole in ice.

Now he opened the parish registers.

* * *

The drowning was easy to find because everyone had written about it. That was the thing about 1911 — the village had been so horrified by its own silence that it had filled three separate books trying not to talk.

The burial register came first. Eleven entries in a single week, September 1911, all recorded in the same careful hand, all with the same cause noted in the margin: *drowned, millpond.* The vicar of the day — one Reverend Anselm Quiller, whose name Odile had already met scratched into the belfry ruins — had run out of the standard printed form for burial certificates and taken to writing them out by hand, each one longer than the last, as though verbosity were a rope he could throw after them.

Merrick Doyne, miller, aged forty-one. Tamsin Doyne, his wife, thirty-nine. Colm Petherick, wheelwright, forty-four. Elowen Petherick, his wife, forty. The Trevanions, brothers, farm labourers, twenty-six and twenty-two. The Coads, a mother and son, fifty-eight and nineteen. The Lanyons, sisters, thirty-four and twenty-nine.

And Ottillie Harrow, aged seven.

Odile sat back from the page.

Seven years old. He read the entry again to be sure. *Ottillie Harrow, daughter of Josiah Harrow, drowned, millpond, interred 14th September.* There was a note in Quiller's hand beneath it, pressed so hard into the paper that it showed through to the next leaf: *God forgive us all.*

He copied every date, every age, every marginal note into his logbook, and his pencil moved steadily, and his hands did not shake, because he would not permit them to. But something in his chest had gone quiet and wide, the way a room goes when a clock stops.

Then he found the coroner's file.

* * *

It was misfiled under "W" — *Watercourse, obstruction of* — which told Odile that someone, sometime in the past century, had wanted it hard to find. Inside a brittle folder tied with green ribbon were the depositions taken two days after the drowning, and the story assembled itself out of the flat declarative voices of witnesses like bones knitting wrong.

There had been a fair. The last day of summer, the whole village down at the millpond, lanterns strung between the mill and the willows, a fiddler, ginger cake, a barrel of cider tapped at sundown. Eleven adults and children had gone onto the water in the old ferry punt — the big one, the one that could hold twelve — for the traditional last crossing of the season, lanterns hung at bow and stern.

And there had been a boy on the bank. A child of nine or ten, name given variously in the depositions — nobody seemed able to settle on it, which struck Odile as the single strangest fact in the entire file. A boy who had been seen earlier that evening playing at the water's edge, skipping stones, laughing too loudly. A boy who, when the punt reached mid-pond, had done something to the sluice gate.

Bruer Coad's widow gave the clearest account, her voice flattened by shock into something almost administrative: *"I saw him turn the iron handle. I have known that child since he was born. He turned it and the water went down and the punt went with it, and then he stood on the bank and watched, and he was not laughing anymore."*

Another witness, the fiddler: *"He was a stranger's child, come with the fair folk perhaps. No one here knew his name."*

A third, the miller's apprentice, who had survived because he'd been sent up the ladder to light the mill lamp: *"When we got the living out — there were none living — the boy was gone. The constable searched three days. Nothing. Not so much as a shoe."*

Odile turned the pages slowly. Deposition after deposition, and always the same gap where the boy should be. No name. No parent coming forward. No description that agreed with any other description — dark hair, fair hair, small for his age, tall for his age. As if the village's memory had been asked to hold him and had refused, or as if he had never quite finished arriving in the world and so could not properly be remembered out of it either.

The inquest returned a verdict of accidental drowning by misadventure against all eleven. The sluice mechanism was found to have been tampered with; the tamperer was recorded, in the coroner's own handwriting, as *a male child, identity unknown, presumed departed the district.*

Presumed departed the district. Odile read those four words three times. Presumed. Departed. The district. It was the language of a man filing away a thing he could not afford to look at, and Odile recognized the language intimately, because it was the language of every pest controller who had ever closed a job with *no further evidence of activity* when what he meant was *I am leaving now and I am not coming back.*

But the village had not filed it away. The village had melted its bell.

The petition was in the same box, folded into quarters, signed by forty-three households. Odile unfolded it with both hands, carefully, the way you'd unfold something sleeping. The text was brief. The bell of St. Brannoc's had rung the night of the drowning — rung unstruck, rung by itself, eleven strokes at the hour the punt went down — and had rung again every night since, and the signatories begged the diocese's permission to have it taken down and broken up, *that our village may sleep.*

Forty-three signatures. And near the top, in a strong slanted hand: **Josiah Harrow** — father of the drowned Ottillie, eleventh name in the ledger, and great-great-grandfather of Vesper.

Odile looked at that signature for a long time.

A man loses his daughter to the pond and his answer is to melt the bell that mourns her. Unless — Odile turned the thought over slowly, testing its weight — unless the bell was not mourning. Unless the bell was counting. Unless Josiah Harrow had stood in his kitchen night after night hearing eleven strokes ring out over the fields and understood, before anyone else, that the sound was not grief but arithmetic, and that arithmetic does not stop at eleven unless someone makes it stop.

The permission had been granted. The bell had come down in November 1911, carted to a foundry in Truro, and the record ended there — except that Odile had spent enough nights in the Harrow yard by now to know that whatever had been carted to Truro, it had not been the whole of the bell. The room still sang. The ledger still counted. Somewhere under the black water of the millpond, bronze was waiting.

He wrote none of this down.

His pencil hovered over the page and did not move. For the first time since he'd taken up this trade — since he was an apprentice copying roach droppings into a ledger with a steadiness his master had called monkish — Odile Thackeray chose not to document. Because he understood, now, with the certainty of a man who has watched smoke spell shapes above a hearth, that writing it down was not recording it. Writing it down was *feeding* it. Every line in his logbook was a verse of the song. Every careful observation was a candle lit at an altar he had sworn he didn't believe in.

He closed the logbook and sat in the grey light with his hands flat on the table, feeling the shape of his own creed crack down the middle like river ice.

* * *

It was the twelfth entry that kept him at the table until the bulb burned dim with the failing afternoon.

He went back to the photograph of the ledger. *Ma-* and the pool of ink. And now, holding the coroner's file beside it, he saw what he should have seen weeks ago: the twelfth entry was not a later addition by a different hand. The ink matched. The pressure of the nib matched. It was the same writer, the same sitting, the same terrible evening in September 1911 when someone — Josiah? Quiller? whoever kept the book first — had sat down to enter the names of the dead and arrived at the end of the list and found it incomplete.

Because there had been twelve in the pond's accounting that night. Eleven bodies recovered. One child missing.

Not fled. Not departed the district. Odile looked at the trailing stroke of ink, the way it ran off the ruled line as if the pen had been snatched away mid-word, and he understood with a pity so sudden and total it felt like vertigo: the boy had gone into the water after all. Or the water had gone into the boy. Either way, he had never been found, never named, never buried, never entered fully into any book — and so the count had stood at eleven-and-a-half for over a hundred years, a sum that could not close, a door held open by two letters and a blot.

*Ma-.*

Mary. Malachi. Mabon. Matthew. Mark. Odile said the syllable aloud in the empty archive just to hear how small it was. *Ma.* A beginning. Children's names are mostly beginnings.

He thought of the fingerprints in the flour — five fingers, too many knuckles, the prints of a hand that had grown wrong the way a tree grows wrong around wire. He thought of the thing in the walls scratching, not hunting but *counting*, keeping its own tally because no one else would keep it. He thought of a nine-year-old standing on a bank at a fair, turning an iron handle — why? mischief, anger, a game gone past recall — and watching eleven people go down into the dark, and then going down himself, unpunished by any court because no court knew his name, and punished instead by a century of nights, ringing.

The monster is a missing person.

The words arrived in Odile's mind with the plainness of a measurement, and they undid him more thoroughly than terror ever had. Terror he knew how to file. But this — this was a child who had done a monstrous thing and been swallowed by it, who had waited a hundred years not for vengeance but for *entry*, for someone to finish the sentence, to write the rest of his name, to say aloud what the ledger had left hanging: *you existed, you are counted, the sum is complete, you may stop now.*

Every haunting Odile had ever read about reduced, in the end, to an unpaid debt of attention. He had thought it a professional truism. Now it was a wound.

He thought of Wren's slate shoebox, hundreds of tally marks scratched by a twelve-year-old hand, one for every night. He thought of Sorrel saying *stop writing things down* with the gravity of a doctor delivering a prognosis. He thought of Vesper's bread, warm from the oven, offered like a treaty. Four generations of one family standing watch over a drowned stranger's unfinished name, feeding the count small offerings so it would never reach for theirs — and never once, in a hundred years, thinking to do the one thing the count actually asked.

To finish it. To find the name. To say it.

Odile opened his logbook to the clean page he had refused all afternoon. His hand hovered. Then, deliberately, he turned past it — past ten blank pages, past everything he'd learned today, leaving it unwritten, unsung — and went back through the register instead, hunting the fair records, the itinerant workers' lists, the school admissions, anything from the summer of 1911 that might hold a boy's name beginning with *Ma-*.

Outside, the light failed. The moths came back to the bulb. And far off across the fields, faint as a memory of a memory, the millpond wind moved through the willows with a sound almost — but not quite — like bronze.

He found nothing that night. But walking home along the dark lane with his collar up, he heard the bell begin — eleven strokes, patient, unhurried, the rhythm of a thing that has waited a century and can wait a century more — and Odile stopped in the middle of the road and spoke to the darkness, quietly, the way you speak to a child crying in another room.

"I'm looking for your name," he said. "Hold on."

The eleventh stroke faded. Then, unmistakably, impossibly, a twelfth began to rise out of the pond — thin, cracked, eager —

—and cut off short, as if a hand had clapped over a mouth.

At the farmhouse, when Odile let himself in, Wren was waiting at the bottom of the stairs in her nightdress, the slate shoebox hugged to her chest, her face pale and set with a resolve far too old for her.

"I've worked it out," she said. "How to balance it. And you're not going to like it."
