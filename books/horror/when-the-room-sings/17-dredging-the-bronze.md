## 17. Dredging the Bronze

We went out at the dark of the moon, because Aldous said the pond kept better records than I did and he preferred to work when it couldn't read over his shoulder.

He had the boat. It was a flat-bottomed thing, older than either of us, its planks gone silver with age and patched in places with tin that had been hammered flat by hands long dead. He carried it on his shoulders the last quarter mile from the track, and I carried the grapnel, the rope, the lantern, and the crowbar, and I noticed that he walked the same way Vesper walked through the kitchen after dusk: quickly, without looking at anything directly, the way a man crosses a room he knows is listening.

The millpond lay flat as poured lead. No wind. No frogs. The reeds along the eastern margin stood perfectly still, which was wrong — there had been a breeze all evening, enough to move the wheat on the walk up, enough to touch the back of my neck. But it stopped at the waterline. It stopped the way a conversation stops when someone unwelcome enters the room.

I set the lantern down on the bank and did what I always did, because a creed does not stop being a creed just because it has started to cost you. I opened my logbook. I wrote the date, the time, the conditions, the names of the two men present. My pen hesitated over the last line — *purpose of operation* — and then I wrote it anyway: *Recovery of bell fragments, per ledger, per twelfth entry.*

Aldous watched me write. He didn't tell me to stop. That was the change between us, the thing that had grown in the days since Sorrel ran for the water and Vesper's countersong pulled her back by the hair of the dark. He had stopped telling me to stop. He had decided, I think, that if the room was going to sing my name, it might as well sing it doing something useful.

"You'll want to take your boots off," he said.

I looked at him.

"Last time anyone dredged here — my grandfather's time, and before that his mother's — they took their boots off. Bare feet in the boat. I don't know why. It's just one of the rules nobody wrote down." He was already pulling his own boots off, peeling his socks, folding them with a housewifely neatness that would have made his mother proud. "The pond doesn't like to be stood on. It likes to be *touched*."

I took my boots off. The grass was cold and wet and I felt every blade of it, and I understood, in the way I had come to understand most things in this parish — too late, and by the skin — that bare feet were not superstition. They were humility. You cannot stand *on* something you are asking a favor of.

We pushed the boat out.

The water took us without a ripple. That was the second wrong thing. A flat-bottomed boat, two grown men, a grapnel and a crowbar and a lantern, and the pond accepted the whole weight of us the way a ledger accepts a figure — without complaint, without displacement, as if we had always been entered there and were only now being read aloud.

Aldous rowed to the middle. He didn't need to find it. He shipped the oars at a spot that looked like every other spot and said, "Here. My grandfather said here. And his mother said here before him, and she was a girl when they melted it down, so she'd have known."

"You've never done this yourself?"

"Nobody's done it since 1911." He took the grapnel from me and held it the way you hold something you're afraid of and can't put down. "They only ever dragged it once, to make sure the melting had taken. Grandfather said his mother stood on the bank and told them to stop after the first pass. Said the water had gone *warm*."

"Warm."

"Warm in October." He let the grapnel over the side. "Ready?"

"No," I said, honestly.

"Good," he said. "Honesty keeps. Bravery doesn't."

He dropped it.

* * *

The rope paid out through my hands and I counted the fathoms without meaning to — it is an occupational habit, counting, measuring, the pest controller's rosary — and at four fathoms the rope stopped, and Aldous began to drag.

The sound came up through the hull and into the soles of my bare feet and into my teeth. Not the sound of iron tines raking mud. Mud is soft, apologetic. This was the sound of iron finding iron, over and over, a slow ringing conducted through thirty feet of water and a hull of old planks, and with each pass the note came clearer, and with each pass I recognized it, because I had been hearing it in my sleep for a week.

The bell. Or what the bell had become. A century of silt had not buried it. A century of silt had *kept* it, the way the cellar ledger had kept the Harrows' column, unbroken, tended, waiting.

"Pull," Aldous said, and we pulled.

The first fragment broke the surface at half past one in the morning, and I want to be precise about what it was, because precision is the only prayer I have left. It was a piece of the bell's shoulder, maybe the size of a dinner plate, curved, crusted green and black, with a broken edge where the foundry's saw had bitten through in 1911. Water sheeted off it into the boat. And it was *humming*.

Not ringing. Humming. A note below hearing that I heard anyway, that I felt in the bones of my hand before I felt it in my ear, the way you feel a wasp on your skin before you see it. Aldous set it down on the sacking in the bottom of the boat as gently as a man laying down a sleeping child, and then he sat back on his heels and looked at me with an expression I had not seen on him before.

It was relief. That was the terrible thing. He looked *relieved*.

"Keep going," he said.

We dredged until the sky went the color of dishwater. Eleven passes. I counted them, of course. Eleven fragments large enough to lift, and a scatter of smaller pieces we gathered by hand, reaching into the black water, and I will record here what my logbook records, in my own hand, in ink that stayed put for once: *the water was warm.*

The last piece we raised was the smallest. A shard no bigger than my palm, from the lip of the bell, where the inscription would have been. The parish record said the bell bore a motto, the old standard, *Vivos voco — I call the living.* The letters were gone, melted or worn or eaten, but when I took that shard out of the water and the pond finally let it go — and it did let it go, there was a moment of resistance, a tug, the whole pond tightening around my wrist like a hand reluctant to release a held thing — when I had it in my palm, the humming stopped.

And something spoke.

* * *

It did not speak in a voice, not at first. It spoke the way the flour spoke, the way the prints in the lard spoke: by arrangement. The hum moved from fragment to fragment in the bottom of the boat, from the shoulder-piece to the crown-piece to the shard in my hand, a sequence, a rhythm, and my mind — my meticulous, cataloguing, doomed mind — resolved it into words the way a mind resolves a knock into a name.

*Count me.*

I looked at Aldous. He was looking at the fragments, and his lips were moving slightly, the way Vesper's lips moved when the bell rang at night, following along.

"You hear it," I said.

"I've always heard it." He said it flatly, a fact among facts. "All my life. I thought every family heard their pond. Wren was six before I understood she couldn't." He picked up one of the smaller pieces and held it in his cupped hands, and I watched the muscles of his face fight each other — the Harrow stoicism against something younger and rawer underneath. "It's a child's voice, Odile. It's been a child's voice the whole time. All the scratching in the walls. The prints in the flour. Little hands. We fed it names for a hundred years and it never once asked for blood. It asked to be *counted*, and we —" His voice went out from under him. "We kept it on a ledger instead."

"The twelfth entry," I said. "The child who caused the drowning. Never recovered."

"The millrace took him. My great-great-grandmother's own —" He stopped. That was Vesper's confession to make, and he knew it, and I respected him for holding it behind his teeth like a hot coal. "They melted the bell to stop it calling. Eleven names, eleven rings, every night, and the village couldn't sleep, couldn't bear it, so they signed the petition and they melted it and they threw the pieces in the pond where the child had gone. And it didn't stop anything. It just moved the ringing underwater. And my family stayed, and fed it, and called it keeping the count balanced." He looked up at me, and in the grey pre-dawn light his eyes were wet and furious. "You know what we did? We heard a child calling from the water for a hundred years, and we wrote down the names of everyone else it might take instead."

I did not answer, because there was no answer, because I had done the same thing. I had come to this parish with a logbook and a creed of neutral observation, and I had documented a drowning child the way I'd document a wasp nest in a soffit — *site of activity, recommend monitoring* — and my documentation had been devotion, and my devotion had been heard.

The fragments hummed in the bottom of the boat. Patient. The way a child waits at a school gate long after everyone else has been collected.

"Say something to it," Aldous said.

"What?"

"You're the one who writes things down. You're the one it's been reading. Say something."

I am not a man who prays. I want that on the record, in my own hand, because everything else in this account is in my own hand and it should be true throughout. But I took the lip-shard in my bare palm and I held it the way you hold a bird, and I said, out loud, on the black water, in the grey hour when the world's ledgers are open:

"You were never counted. Eleven went into the pond and eleven were mourned and eleven were rung for, and you went in twelfth, and no one rang, and no one wrote your name, and the bell was melted so it would stop asking. I know. I've read the ledger. I've read the parish record. I've read everything, because reading is the only thing I know how to do." My voice cracked on the last of it and I let it. "You are not vermin. You were never vermin. You are a missing person, and I am going to find your name."

The hum changed.

I cannot describe it more honestly than that. It did not get louder. It got — *fuller*, the way a room gets fuller when someone who has been standing in a doorway for a long time finally, hesitantly, steps inside. All eleven fragments and the scatter of shards hummed together, one note, and the note was not a demand and not a threat. It was the note a bell makes when it is rung *well*, by someone who knows it, for the purpose it was cast for.

Across the pond, the reeds moved. There was still no wind.

Aldous put his hand on my shoulder, and his grip was shaking. "It likes you," he said, and laughed, and the laugh was half a sob. "A hundred years of offerings burned on the hearth and it never — Vesper says the offerings only ever made it *quiet*. You've been here three weeks and it's —" He stopped. He was looking past me, at the eastern bank.

I turned.

On the bank, beside my lantern, stood a shape. Small. Child-sized. It was not solid; it was the way the bell's hum was not a sound — present by arrangement, by consent of the eye. It stood at the waterline with its bare feet just touching the edge of it, and it had its hands raised, and I saw — I will write this plainly or not at all — I saw that it was counting on its fingers. One, two, three, four, five. Then starting again. One, two, three, four, five. Over and over. The way the twins counted the nights. The way the ledger counted the names. The way I counted fathoms and passes and pages.

It had been counting for a hundred and eleven years, and it was still only at the beginning, because no one had ever told it that it was allowed to finish.

"It's not asking to be fed," I said. My voice came out very small over that enormous flat water. "It's never been asking to be fed. It's been asking someone to count *it*."

The shape lowered its hands. And though it had no face that I could bear to look at directly — the lantern light went through it and bent, like light through warm air — I had the absolute, unshakable certainty, the kind of certainty I have only ever had about a well-laid trap, that it was looking at me.

Waiting for me to pick up the pen.

* * *

We carried the fragments back up the track as the sky went pink, wrapped in sacking, cradled against our chests like infants, because setting them down on the ground felt like setting down something that had only just been found. The pond behind us lay flat and still and, for the first time since I had come to this parish, *empty* — the way a room is empty when the person who waited in it all night has finally been acknowledged and has gone to sit somewhere warm.

At the gate, Aldous stopped. He was holding his bundle against his chest and he looked older than his father must have looked, older than the ledger, and he said:

"The belfry. You'll do it in the belfry. Where it used to hang."

"I'll do it where it used to hang," I said. "With the ledger. With its name. Properly. In ink."

"And then the count closes."

"And then the count closes."

He nodded slowly. But he didn't go through the gate, and after a moment he said, very quietly, in the voice of a man reading the last line of a page and dreading the turn:

"Odile. The ledger closes both ways. You know that. A closed count needs a last name on it. Vesper's been keeping the column unbroken for a hundred years by feeding it strangers' names, and there are no strangers left, and there's no more burning, and the twelfth name closes the eleven — but something has to *ring* it. Something has to stand in the belfry and finish the arithmetic out loud." He looked at me, and I looked back, and neither of us said the rest, because the rest was already written, and I suspected — with the cold, clean, professional certainty I once reserved for confirming an infestation — that I had known it since the night I first heard the bell ring in a church that no longer had a bell.

The fragments hummed against my chest, all the way up the path, in a child's patient rhythm.

Counting down.
