## 22. When the Room Sings

The call comes in on a Tuesday, the way the bad ones always do.

*Scratching in the walls,* says the voice on the phone, a woman's, older, with the flat patience of someone who has made this call before and expects nothing from it. *And something in the flour. You'll want to see the flour.*

I write it down. That is the first thing I do with everything—write it down. My name is not important; what matters is that I have been a pest controller for six years, three months, and eleven days, and in that time I have kept a logbook of every job, every site, every finding, in a hand so even my old instructor called it typesetting. Documentation is the whole of the craft. Anyone can lay a trap. The record is what makes it true.

The address is Harrow Farm, out past the millpond road, where the county goes soft at the edges and the hedges grow like they're eavesdropping. I look it up before I go. There isn't much. A farm, held by the same family for over a century, which by itself means nothing and everything—a place that old has either been lucky or has learned something about luck.

There's one other thing. An old incident report, digitized badly, from years back. A pest controller named Thackeray, contracted to the same address, whose file simply ends mid-entry. No closing report. No invoice settled. His last logged sentence reads: *The count must balance.*

I read that sentence four times in the cab, and each time my pen hovered over the page to copy it out, and each time I didn't. Some instinct older than my training told me not to put those words into my own hand.

I went anyway. Of course I went. That is also part of the craft.

* * *

The farmhouse sits low against the sky like something crouching rather than standing. The land around it is tended—good fences, a clean yard—but there is a stillness to the place that I can only describe as *attended*. As though the house were being watched, or watching.

A woman meets me at the door. She is old but not frail, with white hair braided close to her skull and eyes the color of pond water in November. She looks at me for a long moment before she speaks, and I understand, without knowing how, that she is deciding something.

"You're new," she says.

"Thackeray couldn't be reached," I say. "His firm forwarded the contract."

Something moves behind her face—not surprise, exactly. More like weather changing. "Did he leave anything? Papers, notes?"

"He left an unfinished file."

She nods slowly, the way people nod at funerals. "Come in, then. Mind you don't write anything down in the kitchen."

I laugh. It's a professional reflex, a small social lubricant. She does not laugh with me. She watches me until the laugh dies of natural causes, and then she leads me inside.

The house smells of bread and lamp oil and, underneath both, faintly, of pond water. A boy of maybe ten passes us on the stairs and stops dead when he sees my bag. He stares at my hands—specifically, at my right hand, the one that holds the pen—and then he runs, hard, up the stairs and out of sight. A door closes. From behind it, muffled, a girl's voice says something soothing.

"The children are shy," the woman says, in the tone of a woman who has said that exact sentence many times and knows precisely how unconvincing it is.

"I'm here about scratching in the walls," I say. "And the flour."

"Yes." She turns toward the kitchen and then doesn't. "It starts after dark. The bell hour. You'll hear it if you stay, and you'll want to stay, because you're the kind who stays. I can tell." She looks at my logbook, tucked under my arm, with an expression I will spend a long time trying to name. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. "You write everything down."

"It's the job."

"It was his job too."

* * *

The kitchen is clean in the way of rooms that are cleaned obsessively and still feel dirty. Scrubbed floorboards, a blackened range, a table worn pale at its center. And on the shelf beside the flour bin, a sack of flour, open at the top, dusted across its folded lip with fingerprints.

I put on gloves before I touch anything. That is procedure. But I will admit here, in the only place I will ever admit it, that my hands were shaking, and the gloves were less protection than theater.

The prints are beautiful. That is the word that came to me first and the word I have never managed to displace. They are pressed into the flour in a single neat row, five of them, evenly spaced, each one a full hand—palm and five fingers—with the whorls crisp as engraving. Human prints. Adult-sized. Except that each finger carries one knuckle-crease too many, as though the hand had been drawn by someone working from memory, from longing, from a description given secondhand by someone who missed the person very much.

Beside the prints, laid flat on the shelf where no one could have missed it, is a ledger.

It is an old book, swollen with damp, its spine repaired more than once with thread and care. I did not open it. I want that recorded: I looked at it, and I did not open it. On its cover, in faded ink, someone had written a title, and beneath the title, smaller, a final line:

*When the Room Sings.*
*Count balanced. All accounted for.*

The handwriting was even. Typeset-neat. I knew whose it was before I let myself know.

"Vesper," I called, because the woman had told me her name at the door and I hold onto names the way other people hold onto railings. "Who closed this ledger?"

She appeared in the doorway with a plate of bread and butter, as though feeding me were the answer to a question I hadn't asked yet. Which, I would learn, it always had been.

"A friend," she said. "The best one we ever had. He stayed so we could go." She set the plate down next to the flour sack, next to the prints, as casually as a woman setting bread beside a grave. "He finished the count. Wrote the last name in himself. Rang the bell with his own two hands." Her voice did not waver. It had clearly done its wavering decades ago and come out the other side smooth as river stone. "We buried an empty box. We don't talk about him much. The children do, though. In their own way."

"In what way?"

Vesper looked at the flour, at the five neat prints, and smiled the smallest smile I have ever seen on a human face.

"That way," she said.

* * *

I am a professional. Let the record show what happened next happened in strict accordance with procedure.

I set traps. Flour-and-lard boards along the skirting, talc sheets under the sink, a lamp rigged to a tripwire of thread across the kitchen doorway. I photographed the prints in situ. I measured them—nineteen centimeters, palm to fingertip, consistent across all five impressions—and noted the anomalous phalangeal creases in my logbook, in my even hand, because the record is what makes it true.

And all day, while I worked, the family orbited me at a distance. The old woman baking bread she set down near me and never quite handed to me. A grown man—her son, Aldous—mending a fence within eyeshot of every window I passed, his hammer keeping a slow, deliberate rhythm, like a man counting something. The twins upstairs, behind their door, and once, passing the landing, I heard one of them whispering—not words, but numbers. Counting. Down from some number I couldn't catch, slow and steady, the way children count to make a game less frightening.

At dusk, Vesper found me packing my kit in the parlor.

"You'll stay for the bell hour," she said. Not a question.

"Should I?"

"No," she said. "But you will."

She was right. I want that in the record too: she told me plainly, and I chose it, and whatever follows is mine.

* * *

The bell rang at 3:17 in the morning.

I know the time to the minute because I wrote it down, because writing things down is the only prayer I know, and God help me, I prayed all night.

It did not come from any direction. That is the first thing to understand, and I understood it in my bones before my mind caught up: the sound had no bearing. It came from everywhere the way cold comes from everywhere, from the walls themselves, from the floorboards, from the water in the pipes, from the space just behind my ears. A bell note, deep and bronze and patient, rung once, and then silence long enough to make you doubt it, and then again.

One ring. Then another. Then a third, spaced like footsteps approaching a door.

I got up. I want to say I crept, but the truth is I walked, evenly, calmly, the way I do everything, pen already in hand, because if I was going to die in a strange kitchen at three in the morning I was going to die with an accurate timestamp.

In the kitchen, the flour sack stood open. No one had opened it. I would swear that on anything you asked me to swear on.

As I watched, the flour at its lip dimpled. Pressed. Rose again in the shape of a palm and five fingers, one knuckle-crease too many on every finger, and beside it, a moment later, another print, and another, laid out in that same patient row, like a man setting a table. Like a man keeping records.

And underneath the bell-note, so faint I had to stop breathing to hold it, there was humming. A tune with no melody I recognized, but with a rhythm I knew in my spine—the rhythm of a pen moving across a page. Steady strokes. Even lines. Someone, somewhere inside the walls of that house, inside the room itself, taking careful notes.

I wrote: *3:19 a.m. Audible humming, source indeterminate. Prints appearing in flour, no agent present. Sound has no directional origin. Recommend—*

My pen stopped. Because I realized what I was about to write. *Recommend further observation.* Recommend that I come back. Recommend that I keep listening, keep logging, keep giving this room my evenings, my attention, my devout and meticulous care.

The humming changed. Just slightly. It quickened, the way a heartbeat quickens when it hears its name.

Oh, I thought. Oh, you can hear me thinking about you.

I closed the logbook. The humming slowed again, disappointed, patient. Somewhere upstairs a child began counting aloud—downward, fast now—and an older voice joined in, steadying the number, and between them they got through the bell hour the way families get through storms, together and counting.

* * *

I left in the morning. That was the plan, and plans are sacred to me.

Vesper walked me to my cab with the bread wrapped in cloth for the road, and at the gate she stopped me with one hand, light on my sleeve, and said: "You heard it sing."

"Yes."

"And you wrote it down."

I said nothing, which was an answer, and she nodded as though I'd confirmed a diagnosis.

"My grandmother fed that thing bread for sixty years," she said. "Her mother fed it silence. Odile—he fed it attention. Real attention, the kind nobody gives anything anymore. And it loved him for it, and it let us go, and it keeps his prints in our flour like a widow keeps a ring." She looked back at the house, at its low crouch against the sky. "It doesn't want blood. It never wanted blood. It wants to be counted. It wants someone to write it down so it's true." Her eyes came back to mine, pond-water gray, and kind, and terrible. "You're the kind who writes things down. So I'm asking you, as one keeper of records to another: don't come back. Send your invoice. Mark it resolved. Let the poor devil hum."

I took the bread. I thanked her. I drove away with my mirrors full of that house getting smaller, and I meant to mean it.

* * *

Here is what I have learned in six years, three months, and twenty-two days of pest control, and what I should have learned sooner: you cannot un-know a thing. Knowledge is not a trap you can spring and reset. Once you have documented a phenomenon, it exists in two places—in the world, and in you—and only one of those places can be closed.

For eleven days I did not write in my logbook. Eleven days. I filed the Harrow job as *unresolved — client declined follow-up.* I worked other calls. Mice in a bakery, wasps in a soffit, a perfectly ordinary rat in a perfectly ordinary wall. And every night at 3:17 I woke without an alarm, lay in the dark of my flat, and listened to my own silence, and found it wanting.

Because here is the thing I could not stop thinking, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: that thing in the walls had waited a hundred years to be counted. It had drowned unheard. It had been melted down and scattered and forgotten, and one man—one meticulous, stubborn, devout man—had finally written its name in a ledger and spoken it aloud and given it, at the cost of everything, the only thing it ever asked for.

*All accounted for.*

No one had accounted for Odile. He'd written the closing entry himself, alone in a belfry, and the family had buried an empty box, and the file at his firm ends mid-sentence, and somewhere in a farmhouse kitchen a sack of flour fills up, night after night, with the prints of a man keeping careful records in the only office left to him.

On the twelfth day I took out my logbook. I turned past the mice and the wasps and the rat to a fresh page, and I wrote, in my even, typeset hand:

*Harrow Farm. Follow-up visit warranted. Phenomenon unresolved. Prints recur nightly. Subject appears to be documenting in return.*

I looked at that last line for a long time.

Then, because the record is what makes it true, I left it in.

* * *

I told myself it was a welfare check. I told myself it was thoroughness. I told myself a great many accurate-sounding things on the drive out past the millpond road, and the hedges leaned in to listen, and none of them believed me either.

Vesper met me at the gate, and she did not look surprised. She looked tired, in the specific way of a woman who has lost this argument before, twice, across generations, and has finally begun to find the losing almost companionable.

"You came back," she said.

"The file's unresolved."

"The file was unresolved for a hundred years before you were born, and it will be unresolved a hundred years after, and that is not why you're here." She stepped aside to let me pass, because she had already decided, sometime before I arrived, that she was going to let me in. "You heard the humming."

"I heard the humming."

"And you've been hearing it since. At night. At the bell hour. In the quiet of your own rooms." Not questions. Confirmations. "It sings to whoever writes it down. That's the whole of it. That's the terrible simplicity of it. The room finds its singer, and the singer finds the room, and there's bread on the table either way." She led me toward the kitchen, and paused at the threshold, and said, gently, "Wash your hands first. House rule. It matters to him."

I washed my hands at the scullery sink, in water that smelled faintly of pond, and dried them on the cloth she held out, and followed her in.

The flour sack stood open on the shelf. Beside it, the ledger, closed, its cover reading its two lines in that typeset hand. And on the lip of the sack, fresh, this morning-fresh, a single set of fingerprints—just one, neat and complete, palm and five fingers, one knuckle-crease too many on every finger—positioned, I could not help noticing, exactly where a man might rest his hand while he waited for someone to arrive.

While he waited, patient as bronze, for the next entry.

Vesper set a plate of bread and butter on the table, and poured tea for two, and sat down across from me, and folded her hands, and waited, with the flat patience of a woman who has made this offer before and expects nothing from it and everything from it.

"So," she said. "You'll want to see the cellar."

I took out my logbook. I uncapped my pen. And I wrote it all down.
