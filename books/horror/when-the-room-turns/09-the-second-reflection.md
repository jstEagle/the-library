## 9. The Second Reflection

I want to be clear about something before I describe what happened in the dining room, because the order of events matters to me. Order matters to a surveyor. Sequence is how we tell a real building from a hallucination with load-bearing walls.

So: first I ate half a granola bar standing in the kitchen, because fainting surveyors appraise nothing. Then I checked my watch — 9:47 p.m., which meant roughly eight hours until sunrise, assuming the sun still intended to rise over this particular patch of county. Then I decided that if I was going to be locked inside a house that rearranged itself like a Rubik's cube with abandonment issues, I would do it professionally. I would document.

The notebook had become something more than a notebook by then. It was my tether. Every page of timestamps and measurements and small dry observations — *hallway, 41 feet, smells of pennies* — was a handhold on the cliff face of the evening. As long as I kept writing, I kept being a person who wrote things, and not whatever the house wanted me to become instead.

I started on the ground floor. Methodical, room by room, north to south. The parlor was where I'd left it, which felt almost rude of it, like a dog that had finally stopped following you from room to room. The study was where I'd left it too, Ezekiel's ledger still open on the desk to the page of struck-out names, though I noticed — and noted — that the ledger had shifted two inches toward the door. Toward me. I chose not to interpret this. Interpretation was a luxury for people whose ancestors hadn't signed things in blood-adjacent ink.

Then I came to the dining room.

Here's what I knew about the dining room as of 6 p.m. that day: twenty-two by eighteen feet, plaster ceiling with a water stain shaped vaguely like Ohio, a table under a dust sheet, three windows boarded from the outside, and one mirror — sheeted, like all the others — on the east wall between the windows. I had photographed all of it. The photos were timestamped. I am telling you this so you understand that when I say the dining room had changed, I am not speaking loosely or poetically. I am speaking as a professional.

The dust sheets were gone. All of them, throughout the whole house — I would confirm this later, but I confirmed it in the dining room first, because the mirror there was bare, and the mirror was showing me dinner.

Not my reflection. Dinner.

There were seven people seated around the long table, and every single one of them was facing the east wall. Facing the mirror. Their chairs were turned away from the places set before them — and the places were set, oh, they were set beautifully, china with gilt edges, crystal glasses catching candlelight, a roast or something roast-shaped steaming in the center of the table — and not one of them was eating. Not one of them was talking. They sat with their bodies angled toward the glass, seven faces lifted toward it, and they were smiling.

They were smiling the way people smile in photographs their mothers make them retake. Held. Committed. Smiles with tenure.

I stood in the doorway with my flashlight and did the thing I do, which is narrate my own panic in a calm internal voice until the panic gets bored and leaves. *All right,* I told myself. *Projection. Residual image. Old mirrors can hold impressions — silvered glass degrades, light behaves strangely, there are documented cases of—*

The woman at the head of the table blinked.

Mirrors don't blink. Reflections blink when you blink; that's the whole arrangement, the fundamental contract. This woman blinked on her own schedule, slow, like a cat deciding whether you're worth the effort, and her smile didn't move at all while she did it. The smile stayed exactly where it was, fixed above her blinking eyes, and I understood in one cold drop down the back of my neck that I was not looking at an impression. Impressions don't have schedules.

I raised my camera and took a photograph. The flash lit the room white for a quarter second, and in that quarter second the mirror showed me only what should have been there: my own face, sheet-white, mouth open, holding a flashlight like a weapon, which — fair.

When the flash died, the family was still there. Still facing the glass. Still smiling.

But now — and I want to be precise, because precision is all I had left — now they were facing slightly more toward me. Or rather, their reflections were arranged such that if any of them had actually been sitting in that room, in those chairs, they would have been looking directly through the glass at the doorway where I stood. At me.

Their smiles widened. Not much. A centimeter each, maybe, distributed across seven faces, like the house couldn't afford to spend more than that at once.

"Okay," I said out loud, because saying things out loud makes them smaller. "Okay. Timestamp: 9:53 p.m. Dining room mirror displaying... historical imagery. Seven subjects. Non-responsive to flash."

My voice sounded very good and very steady and completely unconvincing, the way a witness's voice sounds when they're describing something they know will get them committed.

I stepped into the room.

This was, objectively, stupid. I knew it was stupid while I was doing it, the way you know the stove is hot while you touch it anyway, because some part of your brain insists on verifying the universe's claims personally. But here's the thing — here's the thing I've thought about since, in whatever version of since exists for me now — the room pulled. Not physically. Nothing touched me. But the air between the doorway and the mirror had a grain to it, a current, like walking upstream, except the current ran toward the glass, and walking against it took actual effort, and walking with it took none at all.

I walked with it. I'm not proud. I walked with it about four steps before my surveyor brain grabbed the wheel and made me stop and mark the spot, literally, a pencil tick on the floorboard, so I'd know how far the undertow went.

Up close, the mirror resolved. That's the wrong word — up close it *clarified*, the way a dream clarifies in the moment before it turns. The family came into focus: a man at the far end with a mustache like a shoe brush; a woman beside him wearing a high lace collar; two girls, maybe ten and thirteen, seated side by side; an older couple; a young man with his collar open, looking like he'd been dressed by someone who hated him. Sunday best, circa — I'd guess from the collars and the cut of the dresses — somewhere between 1910 and 1930. Candlelight on all of them, though the candelabra on the table held no candles. Just five black wicks, burned down to nothing decades ago, still going through the motions.

And every one of them looking at the glass. Looking out of it.

At some point — I couldn't tell you when, which frightens me more than anything else in this account — the young man's smile changed. The others held theirs, static, painted-on. His moved. It softened into something almost human, almost apologetic, and his lips shaped words.

No sound came through. Of course no sound came through. But I read them, because I've spent years reading lips through bad video calls with clients who refuse to turn on their microphones, and what he said, twice, slowly, was:

*Don't look back.*

Which is the kind of thing that means nothing until it means everything, and I stood there in a dead man's dining room trying to work out which direction "back" was when every direction contained a mirror.

Because that was the other change. The sheets weren't just gone from the dining room mirror. In the glass behind the family — the mirror reflecting the mirror, the infinite regress you get when two mirrors face each other — I could see the rest of the house. Reflected hallways. Reflected doorways. And in the reflected hallway behind the reflected young man, another figure stood. Small. A child, in a nightgown, at the end of a corridor that I had measured that morning and could swear had no corridor at its end.

The child was counting. I couldn't hear it, but I could see the numbers on its lips, one after another after another, patient as tide.

I looked down at my notebook. I wrote: *9:58. Family at table, facing glass. Approx. 1910s dress. One subject attempting communication. Message: "don't look back." Child visible in reflected corridor, counting.* My handwriting had gone from architect-neat to doctor-drunk somewhere around the word *corridor*, and I stared at it and hated it and kept writing anyway, because the notebook was the rope and I was not letting go of the rope.

When I looked up, the young man was gone from his chair.

The others remained, six smiles aimed at the glass, but his chair stood empty, pushed back at an angle, as if he'd risen in a hurry. And the temperature in the room dropped — not dramatically, not a horror-movie plunge, just a few degrees, the way a room cools when a door opens somewhere in a house. Somewhere behind me.

I did not look back. I want that on the record. Whatever part of me the house had already gotten its hooks into, whatever curiosity it was counting on, it lost that round, because I kept my eyes on the mirror and I walked backward out of the dining room, heel to toe, heel to toe, counting my own steps like a rosary, until I was in the hallway with the doorframe between me and the glass.

In the last sliver of mirror I could see, the six remaining diners had turned their heads. All together. Smoothly, simultaneously, like a weathervane finding the wind. They watched me go. They smiled me out of the room.

* * *

I gave myself ten minutes in the kitchen with the flashlight off, sitting on the floor with my back against cabinets that at least pretended to be ordinary, breathing in fours. In, two, three, four. Out, two, three, four. It's a trick a therapist taught me for client meetings, which tells you something about my life before Hollowmere, and it worked about as well as it ever does, which is to say it kept me functional without even pretending to keep me calm.

Here's what I kept circling back to, sitting on that floor: the family hadn't been performing for me. That was the detail my brain kept snagging on. When I first saw them, they hadn't been looking at the doorway. They'd been looking at the glass the way people look at a fire, or a lake, or anything you can stare into for hours without needing a reason. They'd been watching the mirror the way you'd watch television. For a long time. Possibly for a hundred years.

They weren't haunting the dining room. They were *watching* it. All of them, facing one wall, smiling too long, generation after generation of whoever got set at that table — and the smile wasn't joy. I understood that finally, sitting in the dark kitchen. The smile was compliance. It was the expression of people who had learned that the house preferred them smiling, and who had been smiling so long their faces had given up on anything else.

Were they Montroses? The name on the ledger, the name on the deed, my own surname worn by strangers in lace collars — I didn't know. I'd grown up with a thin file of family history: a grandmother in a care facility who called everyone *darling* including the nurses, a father who left, a mother who worked doubles and pronounced our name differently depending on how tired she was. Nobody had mentioned estates. Nobody had mentioned dinners with candlesticks. If these were my people, they were people who had been edited out of my inheritance so thoroughly that all I'd inherited was the debt.

Or — and this thought arrived with its shoes off, quietly, so I wouldn't hear it coming — maybe they weren't Montroses at all. Maybe they were surveyors. Appraisers. People with clipboards and cameras and professional detachment, who had come through that door one by one across a century, and sat down to dinner because the house asked politely, and smiled because the house preferred it, and stayed because staying was the one thing the house never asked permission for.

Odile Vasseur's sister had come to survey this house. Odile had said it like a warning and I'd written it down like data. *Last surveyor, 1974. Never found.* I looked at the empty chair in my memory, the one the young man had risen from in a hurry, and I wondered what year his collar was from, and whether anyone had ever found him, and whether his family had gotten a bill for the appraisal.

Stop, I told myself. Stop. Data first. Meaning later. Meaning is how it gets you.

* * *

I finished the ground floor inventory at 11:20 p.m., and I want to report, for the sake of whoever reads this notebook in whatever state I leave it, that the mirrors behaved consistently from that point onward. Consistently wrong, but consistent. Every bare glass in the house showed the same trick: the room as it was, furnished, occupied, lit — the past layered over the present like double-exposed film. The parlor mirror showed a Christmas tree I could measure against the modern wallpaper. The foyer mirror showed a coat rack holding coats, including a child's galoshes, positioned exactly where I'd set my equipment bag that morning. The bathroom mirror — small, cracked, mercifully fogged — showed only water running in a basin, and hands, and I left before the hands finished whatever they were doing.

The house wasn't showing me ghosts. Ghosts would have been simpler. Ghosts are people with nowhere to be; you can pity them, avoid them, burn sage at them. What the mirrors showed was *occupancy*. Continuous, contented, monitored occupancy. The house had always been full. It was full right now. I was the only one who hadn't been told.

And underneath the occupancy, in every glass, in every era of it, the same constant: the faces angled toward the surface. Everyone in every decade, at dinner, at breakfast, in the nursery, in the bath — everyone watching the mirror from the other side. Watching the room. Watching, when the eras aligned, watching *me*.

A house full of witnesses. That's what Hollowmere was. Not haunted — *staffed*. Every vanished owner and surveyor and stray Montrose, promoted to audience. The house didn't eat its people, I realized, climbing the stairs with my heart going like a metronome set by someone unhinged. The house *employed* them. It kept them smiling at the glass forever, and it fed on something else entirely, something I'd seen itemized in Ezekiel's ledger in a column headed with a word I'd read three times before it landed:

Belonging.

I reached the upstairs landing and stopped, because the hallway mirror at the end — the one that had lagged my reflection by a second that afternoon, the first crack in the world's finish — was doing something new.

It showed the landing. It showed the stairs. It showed me, standing where I stood, notebook in hand, flashlight in the other. And it showed, standing one step below my reflection, close enough to touch the back of my reflection's shoulder, a figure in a high lace collar with a held, tenured smile, looking up at the glass with the patience of furniture.

She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the mirror. They all looked at the mirror. But she had taken up her position behind me, in the reflected world, the way an usher takes up position behind your seat, and I understood that I had been assigned a place at the table.

Downstairs, faint through the floors, through the walls, through whatever the house used instead of physics, I heard the dining room clock begin to strike midnight — although I had checked that clock at noon and it had no hands.

Somewhere beyond the boarded windows, out in the dark and the rain, a car door closed.
