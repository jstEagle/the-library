## 19. The House Speaks

The pipes started at 3:14 a.m., according to my notebook, which was the only witness I trusted anymore.

I want to be precise about that, because precision had become the last thing standing between me and the soft, warm idea of just lying down on the hallway floor and letting the house do whatever houses do to people who stop taking notes. 3:14 a.m. The radiator in the east corridor — a radiator I had measured, photographed, and tagged as *cast iron, non-functional, est. 1910s* — began to tick.

Not the ticking of cooling metal. Metal doesn't cool in syllables.

It took me four full minutes to admit what I was hearing, because admitting it meant writing it down, and writing it down made it real, and real things in Hollowmere House had a way of being used against you.

*Wren.*

My mother's voice. Coming out of the plumbing.

I sat down on the stairs — the good stairs, the ones near the front door that still obeyed Euclid most of the time — and I pressed my pen so hard into the notebook that the next three pages would carry its ghost. *3:14. Auditory phenomenon. Source: piping, east wall. Resembles: M. Montrose (mother), b. 1958, d. 2019.* I wrote *resembles* the way a lawyer writes *allegedly.* I wrote it because if I wrote *is*, then the house had her, and if the house had her, then everything I'd been telling myself since dusk — that this was architecture misbehaving, that hunger was a metaphor — collapsed like wet plaster.

*Wren, sweetheart. It's cold in here. Come sit by me.*

She used to say that when I came home from university visits, when I'd spent four days pretending I belonged somewhere with ivy on it and came back hollowed out by the effort. *Come sit by me.* And I would sit, and she would not ask how it went, because she knew, and I knew she knew, and we were kind to each other about it in the way that only people who share a bloodline and a disappointment can be.

"You're not her," I said to the radiator. My voice sounded small and professional, which was the best I could manage. "You're a pipe."

*You always did talk to the plumbing,* she said, and laughed — her actual laugh, the one with the catch in it, the one from before the illness flattened everything into a polite whisper. *Your father said you'd end up talking to walls. Look at you.*

"Look at me," I agreed, because arguing with grief is a losing hand and I've never been a gambler. "Talking to walls. In a house that eats surveyors. Very funny, Mom."

*I'm not making jokes, Wren. I'm worried about you. You sound tired.*

And God help me, I was. That's the thing I need to confess, here in the margins where nobody will read it until it's too late to matter: it worked. For almost a minute, sitting on those stairs with my flashlight dying by degrees, I wanted nothing more than to put my head against the cold iron and let my mother tell me it was all right. The house knew that. The house had been reading me all day like I read floor plans — patiently, room by room, noting load-bearing weaknesses.

That's what an appraisal is, really. Finding where a thing can be broken.

*

I stood up. Standing helps. There's a reason exorcisms are performed upright.

"I know what you're doing," I told the pipes, walking the corridor with my light raised like a badge. "You did it with the mirrors. One second late, then fifty years late. Now you're doing it with my dead mother. You escalate. It's your whole personality."

The pipes were quiet for eleven steps. Then, further along the wall, closer to the kitchen:

*You think I'm doing something TO you.*

Her voice moved through the house the way water moves through old plumbing — appearing where it shouldn't, following logic of its own. I followed it, because the alternative was standing still, and standing still in Hollowmere after midnight is how you become a portrait in the attic.

*Sweetheart. I'm not haunting you. I'm checking on you. There's a difference.*

"There isn't."

*There is,* she said gently, the way she used to say it when I insisted the closet monster and the basement monster were different entities requiring different protocols. I was eight. I had charts. Some things about me have been true for a very long time. *A haunting wants to hurt you. A mother wants to know if you've eaten.*

"Have I eaten," I repeated. "Yes. Granola bar. 11 p.m. It's in the log."

*A granola bar.* The pause that followed had texture to it — the specific disappointed silence of my mother confronted with evidence of my self-neglect. *You get like this. You disappear into work. You forget you're a person. You've been forgetting since you were small.*

"That's not—"

*You were always looking for a room of your own.*

The sentence landed differently than the others. The others had been comfort, or its counterfeit. This one had edges.

I stopped walking. The corridor ahead of me ended at the kitchen door, which had been there at noon, and was still there now, and I noted both facts with the grim gratitude of someone whose standards have dropped catastrophically.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

*You know what it means.* Not unkind. Worse than unkind — accurate. *Every apartment you ever rented, you measured before you unpacked. Every hotel room. Your first act anywhere is to count the doors and find the exits. You don't rest until you know a space belongs to you more than you belong to it.*

"That's called being thorough. It's called competence."

*It's called being a little girl whose family moved six times in nine years,* said my mother's voice from inside the wall, *and who learned that nothing stays unless you claim it first. And now look. Look where you are. A great big house, Wren. All these rooms. Nobody's claimed them in years.*

The kitchen door opened for me. Politely. Invitingly.

I did not go through it. I turned around and walked back toward the study, talking the whole way, because silence was a resource I couldn't afford to spend.

"You're offering me the house."

*I'm asking if you're hungry,* said the pipes behind me, in every pipe now, the whole circulatory system of the building speaking with one borrowed throat. *You've been hungry your whole life. Don't make that face — mothers notice. You'd walk into rooms and wait to see if anyone moved over. They never did. Not at school. Not at your firms. Not even at home, really, after Dad left. You'd stand in doorways like you were waiting for permission.*

"Stop."

*And here's a house with forty rooms, sweetheart. Forty rooms and no one in them. It's been waiting for you longer than you've been waiting for it. Doesn't that feel like — *

"*STOP.*"

My voice cracked off the walls and came back wrong — came back a half-second late, actually, which told me the acoustics had joined the mirrors, and I filed that away in the trembling drawer of my mind marked LATER.

The pipes hissed. Steam, maybe. Or breath.

Then, softer, wounded, in exactly the tone she'd used the last time I saw her alive, when I told her I couldn't come home for Christmas because of a closing deadline:

*You always did take things personally.*

*

Here is what I did instead of crying, because crying in this house would be like bleeding in shark water:

I inventoried.

Study, 3:31 a.m. Desk intact. Ledger open to page — I didn't check which page, I couldn't, not after the names. Window sealed. Candle stubs in drawer, three. Matches, nine. I lined them up on the desk like a little artillery battery and felt marginally more human.

The pipes kept murmuring while I worked. It had learned not to shout. Shouting made me write things down; murmuring made me want to listen. So it murmured — fragments, half-heard, the acoustic equivalent of a hand brushing the back of your neck.

*—remember when you got lost at the fair—*

*—you cried in the bathroom stall at Henderson & Pike for forty minutes, no one ever knew—*

*—you could stay here, no one would ever know—*

"No one would ever know," I repeated. "That's the sales pitch? That's what you lead with?"

*It's what everyone wants,* said the house, reasonable as a mortgage broker. *To be known and to be safe from being known. You can't have both, Wren. Except here. Here you can be invisible and held at the same time.*

"That's not holding. That's swallowing. Those are different verbs."

*Are they?* And there was something in the voice then — a slip, a flicker, the mask shifting half an inch — something older and hungrier underneath my mother's cadence, something that pronounced *are* with a vowel from a century that didn't have radiators. *Ask the last girl who stayed. Ask Odile's sister. She also thought she was too smart for it.*

The matches rattled in their row. All nine of them, simultaneously, without wind.

I picked one up and struck it against the box just to prove I still could. The flame stood up straight and indifferent to the house, and I held it close to the ledger, not to burn it — I wasn't ready to burn anything, I understood now that burning was a language this place spoke fluently and I'd need to save my vocabulary — but to see what the firelight showed.

What it showed was my own reflection in the dark study window, and beside it, faintly, in the glass, my mother. Sitting in a chair that wasn't there. In a room that wasn't there. Smiling at me with an expression I remembered from hospital hallways and school recitals and every doorway I'd ever waited in: proud, and sorry, and already saying goodbye.

"She's dead," I told the reflection. "You dug her up. That's desecration. That's the worst thing you've done, and today you did a lot of things."

*She gave me the idea,* the house said, almost shyly. *Years ago. When you were small. She said you needed a room of your own, and I've been setting one aside ever since.*

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

*Come see it. I kept it exactly how you'd want it.*

And here is the hook the house baited, the thing I have not written down until now, the thing I am writing now only because the notebook has become the only place my thoughts are safe from myself:

For one full second — one measurable, documented second, logged at 3:52 a.m. — I wanted to see it.

I wanted it the way you want your childhood home back, the way you want the apartment you can't afford, the way you want the office with your name on the door. I wanted a room that fit. And the house felt me wanting, and the whole building exhaled around me like something enormous settling deeper into its own appetite, and every mirror on the ground floor fogged at once, from the inside.

I blew out the match.

"Not tonight," I said, to the house, to my mother, to whatever wore her voice like a coat. "I don't sign anything after midnight. Professional rule."

*It's always after midnight here,* said the pipes, and the warmth drained out of the voice entirely, leaving something patient and vast and terribly offended, and the temperature in the study fell ten degrees in a breath. *But you'll come around. Everyone comes around. Your mother came around, in the end. She asked me to keep the room ready.*

"She would never—"

*Wouldn't she?* A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. *Ask her yourself. She's closer than you think. She's been trying to reach you all night.*

And then, from the top of the stairs — from the nursery wing, from the direction of the counting child, from everywhere and nowhere — the pipes went silent all at once, and in the silence I heard, very faintly, unmistakably, my own name being called in my mother's voice from a room I had not yet found.

I picked up the notebook. I checked the matches. Nine minus one.

Eight left, and a whole house between me and whatever was waiting in the room my mother had supposedly kept ready — and the terrible, dawning suspicion, cold as cellar stone, that the voice upstairs hadn't been the house at all.
