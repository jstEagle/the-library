## 7. The Song Without a Last Verse

The bell came back on the fourth night, and this time it didn't toll. It sang.

I was in the east bedroom on the second floor, running my thermal camera along the wainscoting where the dust had begun to move in patterns I could no longer call drafts, when the first note arrived. It came up through the floorboards the way cold comes up through a cellar — not as sound exactly, but as a pressure that found the bones of my feet and climbed.

I stood very still.

The note held. Then it stepped down, and down again, and rose — four notes, five, six — and stopped. Not faded. Stopped. The way a sentence stops when someone is standing behind you with their hand raised, waiting for permission to finish.

Then it began again.

Same phrase. Same six notes, same little turn at the end where the melody leaned forward like a person leaning toward a door. And then nothing. Silence with a shape to it, silence shaped exactly like the next phrase would have been if there had been one.

I got my recorder out. I always get my recorder out; that's the whole of my religion, such as it is. You write down what happened. You photograph what you can't write down. You keep the record clean and honest and complete, because a record is the only prayer I've ever trusted — *I saw this, I was here, it was real, somebody after me will know.*

I set the recorder on the windowsill and let it run through three full cycles of the phrase. Six notes, lean, stop. Six notes, lean, stop. On the fourth cycle I timed it: eleven seconds of music, nine seconds of waiting. Over and over, patient as tide.

At 2:47 in the morning it ended, mid-phrase — cut off at the fourth note instead of the sixth, which felt somehow worse, like watching someone trip on a stair — and did not come back.

I played the recording back before I left the room.

Silence. Nine minutes of the house's ordinary night noises: settling joists, wind at the shutters, the tick of cooling pipes. No bell. Not one note.

I checked the file twice. I checked the microphone. I recorded myself speaking into it, and my voice came back clear and close and slightly too loud, the way voices do on cheap equipment. The machine worked fine. The machine had worked fine all night, sitting eight inches from a bell that rang hard enough to vibrate the water glass on the sill — I know because I watched the rings spread across the surface of the water, concentric, perfect, while the recorder sat mute beside it.

I wrote it down anyway. That's the discipline. You record the failure of the record. *Bell audible to naked ear, 02:31–02:47. Recording device present, functioning, captured nothing.* Underneath, in smaller letters, because my hand wanted to write it and I've learned to let my hand have its way on the small things: *It does not want to be heard by machines.*

* * *

By the third night I could transcribe it.

I don't read music. I want to be honest about that, because everything else in this account I can stand behind professionally — the photographs are dated and time-stamped, the measurements are to scale, the chain of evidence is clean. But the transcription that follows I made the only way available to me: I drew five lines on a page, called the bottom one low and the top one high, and put dots where the notes went, night after night, correcting as my ear got better at its terrible new job.

Four phrases. That's what the bell was singing. Not one phrase repeated, as I'd first thought — the repetition had been a disguise, or my failure to listen properly. On the third night it gave me the whole structure: Phrase One, rising, confident, the kind of opening that expects an answer. Phrase Two, answering, dipping lower, a little darker. Phrase Three, climbing again, higher than the first, almost joyful. And Phrase Four —

Phrase Four was the problem.

Phrase Four went up and up and then hung there, on a note that wanted desperately to come home, and did not come home. Any child who has ever sung a round in school knows the feeling: the tune leans toward its last note the way you lean toward sleep, and when the note doesn't arrive, something in your chest keeps reaching for it. The bell reached. The bell waited. The bell started over.

Up, answer, climb, hang. Up, answer, climb, hang. For two hours and forty minutes, until 3:11 exactly, when every sound in the house stopped at once — not just the bell but the pipes, the wind, the mouse I'd been tracking in the wall — one full minute of absolute stillness, and then the ordinary night resumed as if a page had been turned.

I sat on the floor of the chapel landing with my notebook and my wrong-shaped dots and I understood, with the plain certainty of a woman who has spent twenty years reading other people's infestations, that I was looking at a question.

Not a haunting. A question. Asked over and over, in the only language the asker had left.

*What comes next?*

* * *

Mrs. Fennimore teaches piano in the front room of her cottage on Chapel Lane, the one with the metronomes in the window. She's eighty-three, mostly deaf in one ear, and has forgotten more about hymnody than the county library ever knew. I brought her my transcription on a Tuesday afternoon, my five hand-drawn lines with their careful dots, and a plate of ginger nuts I'd bought as rent for her time.

She put on her good ear's side of her glasses and looked at the page for a long moment.

"Where did you hear this?" she said.

"Around," I said.

"Mm." She tapped the third phrase. "This is a hymn tune."

"You're sure?"

"Girl, I have accompanied 'Hymns Ancient and Modern' at approximately four hundred funerals. This is a hymn tune." She hummed Phrase One — and the hair on my arms stood up, because it was exact, note for note, the same lean and lift the bell gave it, coming out of a small dry human mouth in a sunlit cottage. "It's in the old books. *The English Hymnal*, early numbers. Common meter, four phrases, the last resolving to the tonic." She hummed Phrase Four the way it should go — up, up, and then down, down, home, settled, finished — and the resolution was so satisfying, so inevitable, that I actually exhaled.

"That's how it ends?" I said.

"That's how it ends." She looked at me over the glasses. "That is how every version in print ends. It's a perfectly ordinary Victorian tune. Solid, unremarkable, the sort of thing they taught village choirs so the congregation wouldn't wander off-key." She set the page down. "Except yours doesn't end."

"No."

"Your fourth phrase stops here—" she tapped the hanging note, "—and never resolves. Which isn't a variant. Variants change notes. This *omits* the ending. Do you understand the difference? Someone took this tune and amputated it."

I said nothing. My hands were very steady on my knees, which is how I knew how far from steady I actually was.

Mrs. Fennimore studied me for a moment with the flat blue appraisal of a woman who has taught children for sixty years and can smell a lie at forty paces.

"There's a story attached to this," she said. "There always is, with Hollowmere things."

"What story?"

"I'm too old for stories I'd have to apologize for later." She pushed the transcription back across the table. "But I'll tell you what a music teacher knows. A tune is a promise. First phrase makes it, second confirms it, third raises the stakes, and the fourth pays it off. Every listener on earth is built to expect that payoff. When you deny it to them—" she tapped her own sternum, "—here. Right here. It's not frustration. It's grief. Unfinished music hurts the way unfinished sentences hurt at gravesides. Whoever is ringing your bell—"

"It's not my bell."

"Whoever is ringing *the* bell," she said, without a flicker, "knows exactly what they're doing. They're not playing a broken song. They're holding a finished song open at the wound and asking someone to sew it shut." She gathered my page, squared its edges, and handed it back with both hands, formally, like returning something borrowed from a church. "Find out who cut it. Find out why. And Miss Hartwell — whatever you do, whatever anyone asks of you — do not sing it yourself. Not the ending. Especially not the ending."

"Why not?"

"Because a resolution wants a voice," said Mrs. Fennimore, "and voices get kept."

* * *

I walked back to Hollowmere in the long gold light of late afternoon, and the house stood at the end of its lane with its windows catching the sun, looking for all the world like a place where nothing had ever happened.

In the pantry, the flour had been disturbed again. Five fingers, pressed from inside the sealed cupboard, neat as a signature on a letter.

And beneath the print, written in the flour by something with no hand I could see, in strokes shallow enough that another hour would erase them:

Four small marks. Rising. Like the beginning of a fifth phrase.

Like a question mark, drawn by someone who already knew the answer, and was waiting — patiently, terribly, the way the bell waits between phrases — for me to finish it.

I photographed it. The camera caught it perfectly.

Some things, apparently, it is willing to let me keep.
