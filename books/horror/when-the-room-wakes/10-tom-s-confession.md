## 10. Tom's Confession

The bell cracked on a Tuesday, and Tom Ellery didn't sleep that night, and by Thursday he'd drunk through the good bottle his wife kept for Christmas and was standing in the taproom of the Lamb & Flag after closing with the chairs upturned on the tables and Mara Hartwell sitting across from him looking at him the way she looked at everything — like it might be evidence.

"I know why you're here," she said. "You don't have to—"

"Shut up a minute." He said it kindly, or as kindly as he could manage with his hands doing what they were doing. "If I stop now I'll never start."

He poured himself one more, though his stomach had been arguing against cider for two days, and set the glass down without drinking it, because this wasn't a drinking story, it turned out. It was a counting story. His family had always been counters.

* * *

"My grandfather was called Josiah Ellery," he said. "Apprentice to the foundry at Wexcombe, the one that did church work all down the valley. He was seventeen in 1911 when Hollowmere's bell came down off its tower to be melted for debt." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on the wood. "Everyone tells it like it was poverty. The Vanes were broke, the creditors were circling, the bell was the last thing of value. That's the version in the parish magazine."

"But?"

"But my grandfather kept a book. Not a ledger — a book. Notes. He couldn't write well, so it's mostly drawings, but there's writing too, in that old copperplate they beat into apprentices. And I've got that book in a biscuit tin under my bed, and I've read it maybe six times in fifty years, because every time I read it I don't sleep for a week, and I'm telling you this because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because your photographs are all over the village now. Because Iris Vane is humming in her sickbed. Because the replacement bell cracked down its seam the night I rang it like a fool trying to drown out something that can't be drowned, and the crack runs straight through the casting mark, and my grandfather's mark is on that casting, and I think — I *know*, Hartwell, I've known since I was nine years old — that what's in that bronze isn't finished with us."

Mara didn't touch her notebook. He'd expected her to reach for it immediately; that was her reputation, the woman who wrote everything down like it was a rosary. Instead she just sat with her hands flat on the table and waited, which was somehow worse and better both.

"The deeds," she said quietly. "Eleven families' deeds, melted into the pour."

"You've read the county records."

"I've read everything I can find."

"Well, the records are the polite version." Tom's voice came out lower than he meant it to. "Here's what my grandfather wrote. They didn't just melt the bell. Vane's solicitor brought the deeds themselves to the foundry — paper, wax seals, the lot — and the order was that they go into the crucible *with* the bronze scrap, so the land would be held in metal instead of ink. Permanent. Unchallengeable. You can't burn a deed twice, see, and you can't forge what no longer exists except as a ring in a bell. It was a ritual, whatever the solicitor told himself it was. Old country logic. Bind the claim into something that sings."

"And Josiah poured it."

"Josiah poured it." Tom finally drank, needing it. "Seventeen years old, holding the shank with his master shouting the count, and the deeds went in one family at a time — eleven bundles, eleven names — and my grandfather wrote that each bundle screamed. Not the men feeding the fire. The fire itself. A sound like a word being said very fast backwards, high, and it went *through* the metal rather than the air, so you felt it in your teeth and your wrist bones before you heard it in your ears. The master heard it too. Everyone in the shed heard it. And everyone agreed, that same evening, over ale, that they had heard nothing. That it was the wind in the chimney. That old men imagine things."

Mara's hands had gone still on the table. Completely still. He recognized it now — he'd seen it in her face at the bar a hundred times without understanding — the stillness of a woman listening for something under the words.

"He poured anyway," she said.

"He poured anyway. Seventeen, a wage, a master's approval, and a shed full of grown men all pretending together, which is the strongest magic there is. Pretending in company." Tom laughed, and hated the sound of it. "That's the thing nobody understands about what happened at Hollowmere. It wasn't one wicked man. It was a whole village agreeing not to hear. My grandfather spent sixty years not hearing it, professionally, and then on his deathbed he gripped my father's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, 'Count the tolls, boy. When it rings, count them, and mind the eleventh,' and died before he could say what the eleventh was."

* * *

Tom got the biscuit tin down from under the bed when they got back to his house, because once started, he wanted it done, wanted the weight of it off him and onto someone whose whole trade was carrying weight. The tin was rusted at the corners. Inside, wrapped in a 1950s *Western Gazette*, was a small exercise book with a marbled cover gone soft as cloth.

Mara put on white cotton gloves — of course she did, she had them in her coat pocket, the woman traveled armed against her own fingerprints — and turned the pages under the kitchen lamp while Tom sat with his back to the window so he wouldn't have to look at the dark.

Most of it was drawings: the furnace, the mould pit, the bell hanging in the frame with careful hatching for shadow. But the writing was there between the drawings, and Mara read it aloud in a low voice, slowly, like a woman handling something hot.

*"June the 14th. Deeds burned. Eleven bundles. Each gave a cry. Mr. Prowse says wind. It was not wind."*

*"June the 15th. Bell cast. Strike note true. Master pleased. I dreamed of hands."*

*"June the 16th. First tolling for practice. At the ninth stroke I counted a tenth that no one struck. Master says miscounting. I do not miscount. Counting is my gift."*

"There," Tom said. "There it is. Counting is my gift. That's us, that's the Ellerys, that's why I keep the books at the pub and why my father kept the tithe and why I'm telling you this at half past midnight instead of taking it in my coffin."

"What tithe?" Mara asked, and her voice had changed — flatter, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice and testing with their weight first.

Tom told her. All of it, the thing the village had never said aloud in a hundred years but had all somehow known, the way you know which fields flood. Every year, on the anniversary week of the melting, an Ellery man went up the tower of St. Brannoc's — the replacement bell's tower — and paid out. Not money. Attention. He climbed up and he counted strokes. If the bell hung silent, he tolled it himself, a certain number of times, and the number was written in the back of Josiah's book and passed down father to son without explanation, because explanations were how you ended up like old Josiah, sleeping badly for sixty years.

"How many?" Mara asked.

"Ten." He watched her take that in. "We ring ten. Never eleven. My grandfather's dying words were about the eleventh, and we were always told — my father was told by his father, who was told by Josiah — *never the eleventh*. Ten strokes honors the ten who were paid off. The eleventh is not ours to ring. We don't know who the eleventh is. We were never told. And every year I climb those steps thinking, this year it'll want the eleventh, this year the count will come up short, and every year I get down the ladder and drink until my hands stop."

Mara was writing now. He'd known she would eventually; watching her write was like watching someone pray in a language he didn't speak but recognized the shape of.

"The night you rang the replacement bell wild," she said, not looking up, "trying to drown out the phantom. What number did you reach?"

Tom's mouth went dry. "I wasn't counting."

"I know. That's why it cracked." She turned the exercise book toward him, to the last written page, dated June 1911, and read it out. *"The bell remembers what went into it. Metal holds a note the way paper holds ink. God forgive us, we have made a document that cannot be closed."*

"A document that cannot be closed," Mara repeated. She took off the gloves, folded them, and looked at him across the table with an expression he'd seen on the faces of men coming out of courtrooms. "Tom. Your family has spent a century paying interest on a theft you didn't commit, in secret, alone, and the principal has never once been touched. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The tithe was never going to work. Ringing ten strokes a year isn't payment. It's a *confession* you make privately so you never have to make it publicly."

"That's easy for you to say."

"It's not easy. I've got handprints in flour that belong to people dead a hundred years, and one of them is starting to look familiar." She closed the exercise book gently, the way you close a hymnal. "But here's what I actually came to say, and then I'll let you sleep, if you can. The dead aren't haunting Hollowmere. I've been saying it wrong even to myself. They're *filing*. Fingerprints beside objects, prints matched to names, a melody that stops mid-cadence because the cadence was stolen along with everything else. This house isn't angry, Tom. It's *unresolved*. And unresolved things don't want tithes. They want signatures."

Tom sat with that, and with the silence of his own kitchen, and thought about his grandfather's drawings of the bell, the careful hatching, the way the boy had drawn the clapper resting against the sound-bow as if it were leaning in to listen.

"If I help you," he said slowly, "openly, in front of the village — if I stand up and say my grandfather poured the screams and my family kept it quiet — what happens to me?"

Mara considered it honestly, which he appreciated. "I don't know," she said. "Probably nothing. Possibly something. But I'll tell you what happens if you don't. The house keeps waking. Three minutes later every night, Tom. Doors, stairs, footfalls, rehearsing. It's on a schedule, and schedules end, and when this one ends, the bell finishes its song one way or another — either the verse gets given back, or the bell takes a new name in the bronze, and I promise you it won't be a name anyone wants."

She stood, put the exercise book back in the tin, and pushed the tin across the table to him. "Keep it. It's yours. But I photographed every page, and tomorrow I'm going to cross-reference Josiah's eleven bundles against the ledger names, and Tom — when I get to the eleventh name, whatever it turns out to be, I'd like you standing next to me when I read it. Not because you owe me. Because counting is your gift, and somebody in this village has to finally count out loud."

At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at him with something almost gentle in her tired face.

"One more question. The tithe. Ten strokes, every year, on the anniversary week. Did your grandfather ever say *why ten works*? Why the dead accept it?"

Tom had carried the answer for thirty years without once saying it aloud, and it came out of him now like a splinter leaving the skin.

"Because ten is a payment," he said. "And the eleventh isn't owed anything. The eleventh is the one who *did* the owing. Grandfather wrote it plain, last page, under the drawing of the bell." He swallowed. "*The tenth stroke is for the tenants. The eleventh is for the Vanes. Never ring for the Vanes. Let them ring for themselves.*"

Mara stood very still in the doorway for a long moment, and outside, far off across the dark fields, faint as memory, something began — softly, patiently, three minutes later than the night before — to test the hinges of Hollowmere House.

"Then it's time," she said, "somebody taught the Vanes to count."
