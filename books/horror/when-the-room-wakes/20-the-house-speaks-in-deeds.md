## 20. The House Speaks in Deeds

The first deed arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, except that I wrote it down, because I write everything down now. Tuesday the ninth of October. Fog on the lane thick enough to drink. First deed.

I had left my work table by the kitchen window of my rented cottage — not at Hollowmere; I'd stopped sleeping there after the third night of doors testing their hinges — covered with the ordinary clutter of my trade: trap inventory, a coil of wire, three rolls of film I hadn't developed yet, Iris Vane's handwritten contract with its strange, formal language. *Record all findings.* When I came down at six with the kettle, the table was clean.

Not empty. Clean. The way an altar is clean before something is laid upon it.

The deed sat in the exact center, squared to the table's edge with a precision no hand could have managed without a set square and patience. Heavy paper, cream-colored, gone soft and tea-brown at the corners. The watermark was a bell. Of course it was. Everything in this business was a bell now.

I did not touch it for eleven minutes. I know because I timed myself, standing in my own kitchen with the kettle going cold behind me, watching a document that had not existed when I went upstairs exist now, and asking myself the question I have learned to ask instead of the other questions: *What is this evidence of?*

Not of a break-in. The door was bolted from inside. Not of a trick — I checked the window latches, the chimney, the gap under the back door, all of it, because I am thorough even when thoroughness has stopped being comfort and become liturgy. Nothing. No route in. Which left only the route I didn't want to name: things in this county had begun arriving out of the past the way weather arrives, through no door at all.

So I photographed it. Front, back, raking light from the left, raking light from the right. Then I put on cotton gloves — the kind archivists use, bought for exactly this eventuality, though I'd told myself at the time I was being fanciful — and I read it.

It was a conveyance. Dated the fourth of March, 1908. A parcel of land: fourteen acres, pasture and woodland, in the parish of Hollowmere. The grantor's name was printed in faded iron-gall ink: **Edmund Vane**. The grantee's space was blank.

Not damaged. Not faded. Blank the way a form is blank. Waiting.

And below the blank line, where a signature would go, the paper bore a mark I recognized with a lurch that started in my teeth and dropped straight through me into the floorboards. Five fingerprints in the dust-film that old paper grows, pressed into the surface as if someone had laid their hand flat against it from underneath — from inside the document itself, the way the prints had been pressed into the flour from inside the sealed cupboard, weeks ago, on my first day at Hollowmere House.

One finger was too long.

My own finger. My altered fingertip, the one that had printed itself in dust the night I caught myself humming the fourth phrase of the bell-song. It had left its mark here too, on a deed dated 1908, on a signature line that had been waiting, I understood suddenly and completely, for a hundred and thirteen years.

I sat down. I made tea I didn't drink. And then, because the contract requires it and because the contract has become the only solid thing in my life, I opened my ledger and wrote:

*Item one. One conveyance, 1908, grantor Edmund Vane, grantee unnamed, signature line bearing dust-prints matching subject M.H. (right index, anomalous). Method of appearance unknown. Time of discovery 06:12.*

Then, because the ledger is also a prayer and prayers are allowed to be honest, I added:

*I think it wants me to watch somebody sign this.*

* * *

The second deed came the next morning. The third the morning after that.

By Friday there were five, each one centered on the cleared space of my table like specimens pinned in a case, each one older or younger than the last, each one a parcel of Hollowmere land passing out of some family's hands and into the Vane estate. The Acklands. The Ferris family. A widow named Prudence Sowerby whose holding was four acres and a beehive yard, the bees itemized separately, valued at two pounds ten.

I read them all. That's what I do. I read everything, I photograph everything, I log everything, and somewhere in the third week of this work the reading stopped being investigation and became attendance — the way you attend a bedside, the way you attend a service. Each deed told the same story in different handwriting: land sold under pressure, land sold at famine prices, land sold, in two cases, with no price entered at all, just the word *consideration* followed by a dash, as if the amount had been considered and found to be nothing.

The county records had told me about the bell. Eleven families dispossessed in 1911, their deeds burned and melted into the bronze so the theft would ring forever. But the records were summaries. These documents on my table were the bodies themselves. Here was the Ackland farm, described by metes and bounds, every hedge named, every drain, every oak — a family's whole world reduced to a legal description, and then signed away, and then burned, and then rung out over the village every hour of every day for a century while everyone agreed to call it silence.

On Saturday morning there were eight. On Sunday, ten.

I stopped sleeping properly. I would lie in bed listening for the house three miles away, though sound doesn't carry that far and whatever was happening at my table wasn't sound anyway. At 3:11 the house woke — I knew its schedule better than my own now — but the deeds came earlier, quietly, in the grey before dawn, appearing the way frost appears: everywhere at once, and no process visible.

Ten deeds. Ten families. I laid them out in order of date across my table, weighted at the corners with teacups, and photographed the whole arrangement, and stood over it for a long time with my hands in my pockets because I did not trust them near the paper.

Here is what I understood, standing there. It was not a haunting. I had been wrong about that since the flour. Hauntings are complaints. Hauntings say *look what was done to us.* This was different. This was procedure. Every document was correctly formatted, correctly dated, complete in every particular except one. Whoever — whatever — was producing these papers had studied the forms. Had kept the forms. Had waited a century with the forms in its hands, patient as paper, and now it was filling them out.

The dead were not asking me to fight. They were asking me to witness.

*Record all findings,* Iris's contract said. I had thought it was an odd phrase for a pest controller's job. Now I understood it was a job description for something else entirely. Somewhere in that decaying house, a ninety-one-year-old woman had written me a contract not to catch anything, but to *notarize*. To stand in the room with my camera and my ledger and my unbroken rule — never sing along — and record the moment the debt came due.

There was only one problem, and it lay on my table Sunday night when the tenth deed appeared, and it was this: every deed needed two parties. Ten grantors, dispossessed, their lines waiting for signatures they could never give in ink. And across from them, in every single document, the same receiving party, the same estate, the same name.

Vane.

Ten blanks for the wronged. One signature outstanding for the wronger. And the last Vane was ninety-one years old, alone in a waking house, burning restoration papers in her grate because she was more afraid of erasure than of the dead.

I went to Hollowmere on Monday morning carrying the ten deeds in an archive box on the passenger seat, strapped in with the seatbelt, because it seemed important — it seemed *respectful* — and because I have learned that in this county you obey the etiquette of the dead or you learn why they had rules.

* * *

Iris was in the morning room, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet ash, sitting very straight in a chair that faced the window. She watched me come up the drive. She had been watching the drive, I think, for days.

"You've received them," she said. Not a question.

"Ten of them." I set the box on the table between us. "They come at dawn. They're blank where the signatures go."

"I know where they're blank." Her hands were folded in her lap, knuckles like a bag of marbles. "I've known since Thursday. Mine came too."

She reached into the sewing basket beside her chair and drew out a single sheet, cream and brown-cornered, watermarked with a bell. She held it the way you hold a bird — loosely enough not to crush it, firmly enough that it couldn't escape.

"The eleventh," she said. "Dated 1911. The last one. The one that isn't like the others."

I took it with gloved hands. The grantor was listed as *Ellen Vane, of Hollowmere Tenancy* — Ellen Vane, Iris's grandmother, the tenant who became the betrayer, the victim who became the house. The consideration was entered, unlike the others. One line: *In consideration of the retention of the freehold, and of silence.*

"And the grantee?" I asked, though I could see it.

"Blank," Iris said. "All these years I thought we owned Hollowmere. We never did. Look at the form. We were supposed to sign it back. Grandfather burned it instead, and his father before him, and the house has been holding our signature in escrow ever since — holding *everything* in escrow — waiting for a Vane with the nerve to finish the transaction."

Her voice didn't shake. That was the terrible thing. Ninety-one years old, confessing to fraud across three generations, and her voice was level as a spirit gauge.

"They'll take the name," she said. "That's what the bell does at the end, girl. You've seen the bronze. When it finishes the song, it takes a new name into the metal, and the name it takes is the name of whoever holds the debt unpaid. Three generations of Vanes, and the bronze is *hungry* for ours. My father heard it trying. He burned the papers and nailed the chapel shut and told himself he'd won."

"Your grandmother tried to teach the verse," I said. "The fifth verse. Your grandfather stopped her."

Something moved behind Iris's eyes — grief, or its fossil. "He stopped her mid-word. I was six. I remember her mouth moving under his hand like a fish. He said *never teach them the ending*, and none of them ever asked him *them who? Them what?*" She looked down at the eleventh deed in her lap. "Because if she'd finished teaching it, someone might have sung it *to* us. Given it to us, the way it's meant to be given. And then we'd have known how to end it. And ending it means signing. And signing means—"

"Giving up the name," I said.

"Giving up the *lie*," Iris said. "The name was always part of the lie. Vane. We hid behind it for a hundred years like a coat too big for us." She looked up at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, the gentility cracked all the way through, and what was underneath was just a tired old woman in a cold house. "You're the recorder. That's what you are, isn't it? That's what I hired. God forgive me, I didn't hire a pest controller. I hired a witness, because I haven't had the courage to be one."

Outside, far off toward the village, a sound rolled across the fields — low, bronze, impossible, one toll that I felt in my fillings and she felt, I could see it, in her sternum. Neither of us moved. Neither of us reached for a camera or a ledger. Some sounds you just attend.

"It rang once," I said. "Unprompted. Daytime."

"It's counting," Iris said. "It knows the papers are out. It's counting down to the signing, and it will keep ringing until either I sign—" her hand flattened on the eleventh deed, "—or until the song finishes without me, and the bronze takes what it's owed the other way."

We sat in the ringing aftermath, two women and a box of blank futures, and I understood at last the true shape of the thing I had walked into with a canister of humane traps and a habit of photographing everything. The house was not my enemy. The house was a creditor. And I was not its opponent.

I was its clerk.

"There's a pen," Iris said quietly, as I packed the deeds away. "In the chapel. On the rope. Bronze, they say. My father saw it once and never spoke of it again. The house drafts its own instruments, Mara. It always has." She smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all year. "You'd better bring your camera. Whatever happens next, someone will need to prove it happened."

I drove home through the fog with eleven deeds behind me and my altered fingertip throbbing like a struck bell, and on my windowsill that night, in the dust I had stopped wiping away, the five fingers appeared again — waving, patient, one too long.

Waiting for me to write it all down.
