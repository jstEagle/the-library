## 1. Ten

The thing about dying people, Oscar Winterbourne had learned in twenty-two years at Marlowe Hospice, was that they were terrible liars and excellent accountants.

They lied about being fine — *I'm fine, dear, don't fuss* — with the serene confidence of women who had survived two wars and one husband. But they kept immaculate books. They knew exactly how many grandchildren they had, exactly how many morphine doses since midnight, exactly how many days since their daughter last called. And somewhere in the final week, most of them started counting down.

Not up. Never up. Down.

"Ten," Edith Garrick said, from Room 4, in a voice like dry leaves settling.

Oscar stood at the nurses' station with his clipboard and did not look up. It was 11:47 on a Tuesday night in late September, and the fluorescent light above him buzzed at the exact pitch that made his left eye twitch, and he had four charts to finish before Priya's shift ended and his began properly.

"Nine," said Edith.

He looked up anyway. He always looked up. That was the trouble with Oscar — everyone said so, back when there had been people to say things to him over coffee — he was a man who noticed everything and then filed it all under *coincidence*, which is where noticing goes to die.

Edith Garrick was ninety-one years old, a retired schoolteacher with hands like crumpled tissue paper and a mind that had been wandering off its leash for the better part of three years. She'd called him "Ronald" that morning — Ronald being her late husband, dead since 1988 — and asked him whether he'd remembered to bring the good biscuits. He had not brought the good biscuits. He had brought the hospital ones, which tasted like sweetened chalk, and she had told him he was a disappointment, and he had agreed cheerfully, because that was the arrangement between them and they both found it comfortable.

Now she lay with her hands folded on the coverlet like a woman waiting for a bus, eyes open, fixed on the ceiling, counting.

"Eight."

Oscar came down the corridor and leaned in her doorway. The room smelled of lavender hand cream and the particular mineral coldness of a body winding down. Her monitor traced its slow hills and valleys, steady as a metronome.

"Edith," he said gently. "What are we counting?"

She didn't answer. Her lips moved. "Seven."

"You know," he said, pulling the chair closer and sitting, because this was the part of the job nobody trained you for but everybody learned — the sitting, the witnessing, the being-there-ness of it — "my mother used to count backwards when she couldn't sleep. Said it emptied her head out like a drawer."

Nothing. Not even a flicker. Just the ceiling, and the slow breath, and:

"Six."

Oscar checked her chart. Morphine at nine. No distress indicators. Pulse sixty-two and ambling downhill, but nothing dramatic. By every clinical measure, Edith Garrick was simply an old woman doing what old women do at the end, which is leave.

Except.

Except that this was the fourth time this month.

He didn't write that down. What he wrote, in his small precise physician's hand, was: *Patient exhibiting terminal vocalization; rhythmic verbal pattern consistent with terminal lucidity. Remains non-responsive to stimuli.* Terminal lucidity — the mysterious clarity some patients find right at the end, the fog lifting for one golden hour before the dark. There were papers on it. There were always papers. Papers were how you made peace with things you didn't want to think about: you gave them citations.

"Five."

"Priya's on in ten minutes," he told Edith conversationally. "She'll bring you the good tea. Don't tell her I said so, but hers is better than mine. She puts actual honey in it instead of pretending the sugar packet counts."

Edith's mouth curved. Not quite a smile. More like the memory of one, surfacing briefly through deep water.

"Four."

Her breathing changed. Oscar had heard ten thousand people breathe and could read breath the way sailors read weather. This was the long exhale, the tide going out. He rose, checked the monitor, adjusted nothing, because there was nothing to adjust. Some machinery you don't touch.

"Three," said Edith Garrick, and now her voice had changed too — clearer, younger somehow, a schoolteacher's voice taking attendance in a classroom fifty years gone. "Two."

And here was the thing Oscar would later lie about, first to himself and then, eventually, to everyone: in the pause between *two* and whatever came next, the corridor lights flickered. Once. A soft brownout, the kind old buildings have, the kind you report to maintenance and forget by morning.

But the lights hadn't flickered. He knew that later, standing in the dark of his own kitchen. Because the hallway clock — the big analog one donated by the Hollowmere Women's Institute in 1974, which had never once been wrong — had stopped. Both hands straight up at twelve, quivering like a compass needle finding north.

"One," whispered Edith.

The monitor flatlined with a tone so soft it was almost polite.

"Zero," said Edith Garrick, and died, precisely, exactly, on the number, the way a train arrives on a schedule, and Oscar Winterbourne stood in the humming room with his pen still uncapped and felt something in his chest shift half an inch, the way furniture shifts in a house when a lorry passes — barely perceptible, easily denied, impossible to unfeel.

* * *

Priya Chandrasekaran found him forty minutes later filling out the paperwork, because death, whatever else it was, was also a form.

"You've got the face," she said, setting two teas down on the desk. Hers with honey. His with the sugar packet that pretended.

"I don't have a face."

"You have the face. The 'I have noticed something and will be burying it in the garden' face." She sat on the edge of his desk, which was against regulations, which was why she did it. "Edith?"

"Peaceful. Terminal lucidity. She counted down from ten." He signed the form. "Which is common—"

"It is not common."

"—it is documented—"

"So is the plague, Oscar. Documented doesn't mean fine." She pulled the census of the month's deaths toward her without asking, because Priya had never once in six years asked permission for anything. "Mrs. Hollis. Mr. Fenwick. Dorothy Aimes. Now Edith. Four patients, four weeks. You want to guess what they had in common besides dying?"

"Being ninety-something in Hollowmere."

"They counted." She tapped the page. "Hollis got to eleven. Fenwick stopped at eight — we thought he'd lost the thread, but I went back through the audio notes, Oscar, he didn't lose the thread, he *paused*. Like he was waiting for something to catch up. Dorothy counted twice. Started over. Got it right the second time." Priya's voice dropped, and that was the frightening part, because Priya's voice never dropped; Priya's voice could carry across a car park in a gale. "They all landed on zero. To the second. And tonight—" she nodded toward the corridor, toward the stopped clock, which maintenance had already been summoned for and had already shrugged at, "—tonight the clock died with her."

"The clock is older than both of us put together."

"The clock has been wrong exactly never." She leaned in. "Oscar. Please. Look at what you're looking at."

He looked at her. Priya was thirty-four, sharp as a tack and twice as hard to get rid of, with a laugh that could restart hearts and a faith in patterns that Oscar privately considered her one weakness. She saw meaning the way he saw symptoms — everywhere, urgently, needing triage.

"Priya," he said kindly, "dying people count down. They count down rosaries and stairs and sheep and the days till their son visits. Numbers are how humans hold on. It's not a pattern. It's a pulse."

"And when does a pulse become a pattern?"

"When it kills somebody."

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then she took her tea and left, pausing at the door. "My gran used to say," she offered, not turning around, "that denial is just grief wearing a work uniform."

"That's very wise."

"She also said men who file things under coincidence die stupid, so." She shrugged. "Watch yourself."

* * *

He drove home through Hollowmere at two in the morning, past the shuttered bakery and the church with its leaning spire and the excavation fencing on the far edge of town, where orange construction lights glowed against the September dark like embers refusing to die. The Okafor crew had broken ground that week on the old Winterbourne land — his family's land, three generations sold off and forgotten, and now dug up by strangers with machines.

His house was cold. His kettle was loud. He stood in the kitchen with tea he wouldn't drink and thought about Edith's voice — clear, young, certain — saying *zero* like a woman marking an exam. Correct. Complete.

He opened his laptop. He told himself it was to double-check the literature on terminal vocalization. He typed *counting backwards death hospice* into the search bar, watched the cursor blink, and closed the laptop again before typing anything else.

Denial, he reminded himself, was not cowardice. Denial was load-bearing. Twenty-two years of it had held up the whole structure: the deaths, the numbers, the names. You didn't go pulling out walls just because they hummed.

In the bedroom, he set his alarm for seven. On the bedside table, his own small clock ticked steadily onward, faithful, forward, up.

It was the last night, though he didn't know it yet, that any clock in Hollowmere would keep time honestly for him.

Because downstairs, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, in a house built on Winterbourne land, something had begun to count.
