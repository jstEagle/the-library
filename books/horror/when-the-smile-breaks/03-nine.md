## 3. Nine

Oscar Winterbourne had a system for grief, and the system was paperwork.

He'd learned it from his mother, who had kept the books for the family's old hardware store until the day she died — neat columns, balanced sums, nothing left to interpretation. When a patient died at Marlowe Hospice, Oscar filled out the forms in his small, square handwriting, and somewhere between *time of death* and *attending physician*, the loss became a fact instead of a wound. Facts didn't keep you up at night. Facts didn't count.

He was filling out Edith Garrick's fourth form of the month — the fourth form that ended in the same impossible column entry — when Priya knocked on the chart room doorframe with both knuckles, twice, like she always did.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"I'm doing my job."

"You're doing the thing where you alphabetize your pens so you don't have to think." She came in anyway, because Priya had never once respected a closed door or an open one, and set a manila folder on top of his clipboard. "I need you to look at something, and I need you to not be a brick wall about it."

"Priya—"

"Ten minutes. If you still think I'm crazy after ten minutes, I'll buy you that terrible gas station coffee you pretend to like."

Oscar looked at the folder. It was thick. Nobody brought thick folders to conversations they expected to lose.

"Gas station coffee is objectively good," he said, "and this conversation never happened," and he opened the folder.

* * *

The first page was a printout of the hospice census log, and Oscar knew it by heart, which was the problem. Four patients in October. Edith Garrick. Harold Boone. Sister Agnes Whitlow. And little Tommy Ferro, who had been ninety-one years old and called everyone "champ," including the morphine drip. All four had died within minutes of finishing a backwards count from ten. Not during. Not partway through. On zero, every time, as though something were keeping score.

The second page was Priya's handwriting, and it was a list of times.

"Edith started counting at 2:14 a.m.," Priya said, tapping the page. "Harold at 2:41. Agnes at 3:03. Tommy at 3:30."

"So they died at night. People die at night, Priya. There's a whole body of literature about it."

"They died seventeen to thirty minutes apart. Consistently. Every single one." She pulled out the third page. "And then there's Greenhollow Assisted Living, across town. Mrs. Delacroix, counted down, dead on zero, 4:12 a.m. Mr. Pruitt, same, 4:39. Two facilities. Six miles apart. No shared staff, no shared visitors, no shared anything except—" she flipped to the last sheet, "—this."

It was a photocopy of a page from the Hollowmere town archive, yellowed and soft-edged, headed **CENSUS OF INHABITANTS, 1847**. Down the left margin ran a column of names in faded iron-gall ink, and someone — Priya, judging by the fresh red ink — had drawn careful lines connecting six of them to six names on her death log.

Garrick. Boone. Whitlow. Ferro. Delacroix. Pruitt.

All present in Hollowmere in 1847. All gone from the record by the spring of 1848.

"The disappearances," Oscar said slowly. "The Vane family thing. The founding families."

"The Vanes and everyone tied to them. Eleven people walked into the Vane house in the winter of '47 and eleven people stopped existing in every document afterward. No death certificates. No burial plots. No estate settlements. The historian I called at the county library got very quiet and then got very busy and then hung up on me." Priya sat down across from him, and for the first time since he'd known her — four years, two failed office birthday parties, one memorable incident involving a fire drill and a bedpan — she looked tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. "Oscar. Our patients are dying in the order their names appear in a ledger from a hundred and seventy-seven years ago. And they're all counting down from ten while they do it."

Oscar did what he always did with facts that misbehaved. He reached for his pens.

"Coincidence of surnames. Garrick, Boone, Whitlow — these are common valley names. Families stay in valleys for generations. You could connect anyone to that census if you tried hard enough."

"I know," Priya said. "That's exactly why I checked yours."

The chart room went very quiet. Somewhere down the hall, a call light chimed its gentle, patient chime.

"My name isn't in any ledger," Oscar said. "I'm not dying. I'm not counting. I'm the guy who fills out the forms."

"No," Priya agreed. "You're not in the death log." She turned the census page around and slid it across the table, and her finger came down on a line near the bottom, beside a notation in cramped period script: *Winterbourne, E. — laborer, hired, cellar works, Nov.*

"But you're in the hire book. November 1847. Right before everyone vanished." She looked up at him. "A Winterbourne helped build whatever the Vanes built. And your family never left Hollowmere either, did they? Same house on Corben Lane since whenever. My grandmother used to say the Winterbournes were the only people the old families still tipped their hats to."

"That's called being polite, Priya. It's a regional custom."

"Oscar."

"It's a *coincidence*," he said, and heard his own voice come out too flat, too quick, the voice of a man stacking sandbags against a river that had already changed course. He softened it with a smile, because that was the other thing he did. "Look. I'll grant you it's strange. Genuinely. But strange things happen when you've got enough data points. Terminal lucidity presents in patterns. Families share names, share genes, share — I don't know, shared hypnagogic phenomena, there are papers—"

"There aren't papers about this."

"There are papers about everything. Someone wrote nine hundred pages about whether Napoleon was poisoned by his wallpaper." He gathered the folder and held it out to her, and his hand was steady, and he was proud of how steady it was. "I'll tell you what I'll do. I'll pull the archived charts tonight. All four. Full medication timelines, lucidity windows, the lot. If there's a clinical pattern, we'll find it together, clinically, like healthcare professionals, and then I will buy *you* the gas station coffee."

Priya took the folder. She didn't smile back.

"That's not what I asked you to look at," she said quietly, "and you know it," and she left him alone with his alphabetized pens.

* * *

He did pull the charts. That was the thing about Oscar — even his denials had a work ethic. He stayed past shift change with cold coffee and warmer excuses, and he built a spreadsheet, because spreadsheets were how he prayed.

The pattern held. Of course it held. Seventeen-to-thirty-minute intervals, descending counts, deaths on zero. He added the Greenhollow data and the intervals didn't waver. He told himself it was circadian clustering. He told himself it was the autumn moon. He told himself a great many things in the firm, reasonable tone of a man reading aloud in an empty house.

At 11:52 p.m., his phone buzzed with the night nurse's text: *Mr. Aldous in 4 started counting. Thought you'd want to know.*

Aldous wasn't terminal. Aldous was a hip fracture with a discharge date of Thursday.

Oscar walked the corridor fast, badge bouncing, and pushed open the door to room 4 in time to hear an eighty-year-old retired schoolteacher — a man who three days ago had complained exclusively and at length about hospital pudding — say, in a clear, unhurried, conversational voice:

"—four. Three. Two."

Oscar stood in the doorway and felt the hairline cracks in his fortress widen into something structural.

"One," said Mr. Aldous, and looked directly at Oscar, and smiled — a warm, ordinary, grandfatherly smile, the kind you'd give a favorite student — and said, "You're late, dear. It's been waiting such a long time to meet the rest of you."

Then he closed his eyes and went to sleep, steady as a metronome, heart monitor ticking along contentedly at sixty-two beats per minute.

Oscar stood there for a long moment. Then he took out his phone and typed to Priya, hands no longer steady at all:

*You were right. Come in early. Bring the whole folder.*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

*I already did,* she wrote. *Room 7. She started twenty minutes ago.*

He ran.

In room 7, under the thin hospital blanket, lay Mabel Vane — ninety-one years old, admitted for heart failure, sharp-tongued and card-sharp and the closest thing Oscar had to a friend among his patients. Her eyes were open. Her lips were moving.

"Seven," whispered Mabel Vane, and her hand found his wrist with surprising strength, and gripped.

"Six," she said. "You have to listen faster than I can talk, Oscar Winterbourne. Five. The key is in my house. Blue teapot, top shelf, behind the good china nobody uses because it's cursed, long story. Four. Iron. Old iron. You'll know it when you hold it because it'll feel like holding a verdict."

"Mabel, slow down, I'm calling Dr. Rao—"

"Three." Her grip tightened. "No doctors. No priests. They only make it curious. Two." She pulled him close, close enough that he could see her eyes were clear — clearer than they'd been in weeks, bright and ancient and terribly awake. "Come to Corben Lane before the moon fills out. Come to the house your people helped mine build. One."

She smiled at him then, and it was Edith's smile, and Harold's, and Mr. Aldous's, warm and wrong and patient as the earth.

"And when you get there," Mabel Vane said, "ask me what we buried. Because I'm the last one who remembers, love. And I don't intend to reach zero before I tell you."

Her hand let go. Her eyes drifted shut. Her breathing went slow and even, and the monitor ticked on, sixty-two beats, sixty-two beats, like something counting much larger numbers, far below.

Oscar stood in the dark of room 7, holding a dead woman's warmth in his fingers, and understood — finally, completely, with the sick clarity of a man reading his own name in a ledger — that denial had never been his fortress.

It had been his alibi.

Down the hall, faint and sweet, from a dozen rooms at once, the night shift began to hum a counting rhyme none of them had learned in childhood.

Oscar picked up the phone and dialed the number for Corben Lane.
