## 5. Eight

Danny Okafor had been a foreman long enough to know that a crew was like a marriage: it didn't fall apart all at once, it fell apart in small complaints. Somebody's boots leaked. Somebody's wife was mad about the hours. Somebody thought the coffee situation was, and he was quoting here, "a war crime."

He had not been a foreman long enough to know what to do when the complaints turned into this.

"Nobody's clock works," said Marcus, the youngest of them, holding up his phone at the end of the second day after the door was found. "Not my phone, not my watch, not the truck clock. They all say the same thing."

"What do they say?"

"Zero. They all say zero. They've been saying it since yesterday."

Danny looked at his own phone. It said zero. The date had gone too — no Tuesday, no month, just a blank white field where the numbers should have been, like the phone had forgotten the concept of time and was too embarrassed to admit it.

"Buy new batteries," he said, because that was the kind of thing a rational man said.

"We did. All of us. Different stores." Marcus's voice had a wobble in it that Danny didn't like. "They go to zero the second we put them in."

* * *

The two who vanished were Callum Reyes and Pete Wozniak, and the thing Danny couldn't get past — the thing that kept him awake in his trailer with the lights on like a scared kid — was how ordinary it was.

No blood. No struggle. No torn clothing, no dropped tools, no muddy footprints leading off into the treeline. Callum's hard hat was on its hook by his cot, exactly where it always was. Pete's boots were side by side under his cot, laces tucked in, the way his mother had apparently taught him, because Pete had told that story at lunch a hundred times, how his mum said a man who left his boots in a heap would die in a heap.

Their boots were tucked in. Which meant either they'd walked out barefoot into an October night, or something had taken them so gently that even their boots hadn't been disturbed.

The tents themselves were worse. Danny went in with the state trooper, a broad, tired woman named Deputy Hollis who kept touching her radio like it was a rosary, and the first thing he noticed was the cold. Not autumn cold. Cellar cold. The kind of cold that has a smell to it, wet stone and old earth, though both tents were a hundred yards from the excavation.

The second thing he noticed was the clocks.

Callum had an alarm clock, a cheap plastic thing with glowing red digits. Pete had a wind-up travel clock, the kind with hands. Both sat on their respective nightstands, both working, both ticking — Danny could hear them, a soft double-tick, slightly out of sync, like two hearts in one small room.

Both read zero.

"Wind-up clock," Hollis said, turning it over. "No battery. No cord." She wound the knob, and the hands spun forward, past one, past five, past eleven, and then, with a small apologetic click, slid back to zero. She wound it again. Zero. A third time, harder, and the little clock made a sound Danny would remember the rest of his life — a sound like a sigh, like something tired of being asked.

"They didn't run," Hollis said quietly. "People who run leave their boots on."

"I know."

"Where do you figure they went?"

Danny looked at the tents, at the frost on the inside of the canvas — the *inside* — in delicate fern patterns, like breath on a window. Like the tents had been breathing out in their sleep.

"I figure," he said, "they went where the counting goes."

He didn't know what that meant. He said it anyway, and it felt true, and being a foreman meant knowing the difference between what you could prove and what you knew.

* * *

That evening the door breathed.

Danny was the last one at the site — he'd sent everyone home, all nine of them, and half hadn't argued, which told him everything. The floodlights made a white island in the dark, and at the edge of it, sunk in the trench like a tooth in a gum of earth, was the door.

It was granite, or something like granite, grey and dense, and it had no handle. No hinges visible on the outside, no seam wide enough for a knife blade. When the crew had first uncovered it, Danny had run his palm along its face and felt — he'd told himself it was machinery vibration, generators, groundwater — ten soft knocks, evenly spaced, like knuckles on the other side of a table. Patient knuckles. The knock of someone who knows you're home.

Now, standing at the trench edge in the blue hour, he watched frost form on the door.

It didn't creep. It *exhaled*. A thin white mist curled out of the seams — the hairline joints between the door and its frame that hadn't been there that morning — and where the mist touched the stone, frost bloomed in feathers and ferns, then faded, then bloomed again. In. Out. In. Out. Slow as a sleeping man's chest.

Danny stood there for a long time, a big man in a big coat, doing the math he'd been avoiding all week. Doors don't breathe. Doors don't knock. Doors don't make two men vanish out of zipped tents and leave their boots tucked in like good boys.

But something behind a door could do all of those things. Something patient. Something counting.

And here was the thought that finally, fully, took the legs out from under his rationalism, the thought he would have laughed at a month ago: *it isn't counting for itself.* It was counting *them*. The crew. The town. Maybe everyone. And it was in no hurry at all, because it had already waited — Danny had read the plaque, the historical society marker at the road's edge — a hundred and seventy-some years.

He turned off the floodlights and went home to his daughter, and he drove the speed limit the whole way, because breaking a small rule suddenly felt like an invitation.

* * *

Nia was four going on forty, with her mother's stubborn chin and a crayon habit that had colonized every flat surface in the house. Danny found her at the kitchen table after dinner, tongue out in concentration, coloring with the ferocious seriousness she brought to everything, while his wife Amara washed dishes and shot him the look that meant *you're late and you smell like a worksite.*

"Hey, bean. What are we drawing?"

"A door," Nia said.

Danny's hands went cold in his warm kitchen. "A door, huh?"

"Uh-huh. The man under my bed showed me."

Amara's hands stopped in the sink.

Danny pulled out a chair very slowly, the way you'd sit down near a bird you didn't want to startle. "There's a man under your bed?"

"Not a *real* man." Nia said it the way you'd correct someone who'd confused a dog with a wolf. "A counting man. He's very polite. He counts and counts and he never gets mad." She pressed so hard on the crayon the tip snapped. She didn't notice. "He's almost done, though. He's on the little numbers now."

"Nia." Danny kept his voice level, warm, a father's voice, and inside he was screaming down a long dark hallway. "Sweetheart, I need you to tell me about the drawing."

She turned the paper around.

Danny had seen the door every day for a week. He knew it better than his own front door now. And his four-year-old's crayon rendering was — there was no other word — *accurate*. The proportions. The slight lean of it, the way it sat a half-degree off plumb in its frame. The grain of the stone, which she'd done in careful grey spirals.

And the scratches.

Three weeks ago, on the second day, before anyone understood anything, Danny had crouched at the trench edge and found four thin scratches low on the door's face, knee height, old and shallow, almost worn away. He'd never mentioned them. Not to the crew, not to Hollis, not to Amara. He'd photographed them and told no one, because they'd looked — and this was the detail he'd kept in the locked drawer of his mind — they'd looked like fingernail marks. Like something on the inside, once, long ago, small enough that its hands reached only knee height, had tried to get out.

Nia had drawn them. Four thin lines, knee height. She'd even gotten the second one wrong in the same way the real one was wrong — doubled, as if the scratcher's hand had slipped.

Danny looked up. His wife was standing at the sink with a dripping plate in her hands, staring at the paper, and he could see her doing the same math, arriving at the same zero.

"Nia, bean," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he hated that, hated the fear in it, because she could hear fear the way dogs hear whistles. "Has the counting man ever told you his numbers?"

She frowned, considering, a scholar of the question. "He only says one number now." She held up one small hand, fingers spread. "Eight." Then she went back to her drawing, adding a small stick figure beside the door — a daddy figure, she explained, because daddies always came to look at doors. "He said to tell you eight, and that you'd know what it means."

Danny did not know what it meant. He knew only two things with absolute certainty, and both of them lived in his chest like stones: his daughter was talking to something through the floor of her bedroom, and the something had started with ten, and it was not counting up.

He swept her up into his arms — she protested, crayon still in hand, leaving a grey streak across his shoulder — and held her tighter than a four-year-old strictly required, his face in her hair, breathing her in, that warm cereal-and-shampoo smell that was the single best thing in any of his days.

"Daddy's squeezing too tight," she said, muffled, patient, the way you'd remind someone of the rules.

"I know, bean. Just for a second."

Over her head, through the kitchen window, past the dark garden, Danny could see the far ridge where the site was, where the floodlights weren't on because he'd turned them off, and yet — he squinted — yet there was a faint glow at the trench, a pale seeping light, like moonlight that had decided to come up out of the ground instead of down from the sky.

The frost ferns on the window were forming as he watched, blooming inward from the glass, feather by feather, and the kitchen was getting cold, and somewhere — under the floor, under the town, under everything — something polite and patient said, in a voice like a knock:

*Eight.*

Danny reached for his phone to call the one man in Hollowmere who might understand what was happening to his patients — the hospice nurse, Winterbourne, the one whose family name was on the land — and found the screen already lit, already dialing, a call he hadn't made, showing a number he didn't recognize.

He answered it. Of course he answered it. That was the kind of week it was.

The voice on the other end was an old woman's, thin and dry and very tired, and it said, "Mr. Okafor. You need to come to Mabel Vane's house. Bring the drawing. And Mr. Okafor — don't let the child finish the door."
