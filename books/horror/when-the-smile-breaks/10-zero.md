## 10. Zero

The first thing you notice about Hollowmere, six months after the harvest moon, is the silence where counting used to be.

Priya noticed it on her first shift back at Marlowe Hospice. She'd braced herself for it — had rehearsed it in the car park, actually, hands gripping the steering wheel, telling herself that she could do this, that she could walk those corridors again. What she hadn't prepared for was how quiet the dying had become.

Mrs. Hollis in Room 4 went in February, ninety-three years old and sharp as a lemon zester to the end. She didn't count. She talked about her first husband's terrible dancing, asked twice whether her roses had been cut back, and then, somewhere between one breath and the next, simply wasn't there anymore. No numbers. No ledger. Just a woman finishing a sentence about dahlias and stepping off the edge of the world as if it were the most natural thing a person could do.

Priya stood by the bed afterward with her clipboard against her chest and cried the way you cry when something has been holding you by the throat for months finally lets go.

Oscar would have documented it clinically, she thought. *Patient expired peacefully, 4:17 a.m., no unusual vocalizations.* And then he would have made some dry joke about Mrs. Hollis's husband's dancing, because Oscar Winterbourne's humor was the load-bearing wall of his entire personality, and God, she missed him. She missed him the way you miss a tooth — not constantly, but at every meal, at every stupid moment when your tongue goes looking for what isn't there.

She wrote the note herself: *No unusual vocalizations.* Then, after a moment, she added: *He kept his word.*

Nobody at Marlowe knew what that meant. That was fine. It wasn't for them.

* * *

The meadow, of course, was the town's favorite miracle.

The excavation site off the old Winterbourne land should have been a scar — torn earth, idle machinery, the kind of wound a town stares at and pretends not to stare at. Instead, by the following spring, it was grass. Not scrubby, reluctant, we-are-tolerating-this-grass, but deep green velvet that came up weeks before anything else in the county bloomed, dotted with crocuses in October and snowdrops in November, flowers that had no business existing out of season and yet insisted on doing so anyway, cheerfully, like guests who arrive early and start making drinks.

The Okafor family came out to see it most Sundays. Danny would stand at the edge with his daughter on his shoulders and just look at it, the way other men looked at fishing holes or old ballparks. The girl — Ada, seven now, gap-toothed and ferocious — had stopped drawing the door. Her sketchbooks filled instead with the meadow, over and over, though she always drew one small gray shape in the middle of all that green, a rectangle no bigger than a breadbox, half-swallowed by flowers.

"That's not real," Danny told her once, gently. "There's nothing there."

"I know," Ada said, in the tone of a child humoring an adult, which is the worst tone in the world. "I just like to remember where things are."

Priya understood that better than anyone in Hollowmere except maybe Mabel Vane, and Mabel had been gone since December — gone gently, gone without counting, gone with her whole scattered family around her bed in a vigil that lasted three days and felt, everyone agreed afterward, less like dying than like a homecoming that had been postponed for a hundred and seventy years.

Before the end, Mabel had held Priya's hand with a grip that belied her ninety-one years and said, "You'll go out there, won't you? On the equinoxes. Someone should tell him."

"Tell him what?"

Mabel had smiled — the first unguarded smile Priya had ever seen on her, and it transformed her face into something young. "Whatever there is. He was always such a stickler for being informed."

* * *

So Priya went.

The first time was the spring equinox, and she almost didn't find it. The door didn't want finding; she was fairly sure of that. She walked the meadow in widening circles while crocuses nodded around her ankles like a crowd craning to watch, until finally — there. A seam in the green. A rectangle of weathered stone, low, barely knee-high now, as if the earth itself were patiently tucking it in for sleep. No handle. No hinges visible. Sealed from the inside.

She sat down in the grass beside it, cross-legged, the way you sit beside a hospital bed, and cleared her throat.

"Hi," she said. "It's me. Obviously. Who else would come stand in a field talking to a rock."

Silence. Wind. A bee investigating her sleeve with great professional interest.

"Right. Well." She pulled a folded paper from her jacket. "News. You'll want the news, you were always terrible at sitting still without news. Mrs. Hollis passed in February — peaceful, no counting, I put it in the chart. The Hendersons' baby arrived three weeks early and both mother and daughter are fine, seven pounds two ounces, which I mention only because you once bet me a coffee the baby would be late, so technically you owe me. I'm not saying I've forgiven you. I'm saying the debt stands."

The wind moved through the grass. It sounded almost like breathing.

"Marlowe's quiet," she went on, softer. "Everyone's quiet. Nobody counts anymore, Oscar. Not the patients, not the town, nobody. Little kids don't do it playing hide-and-seek. Old men don't do it waiting for the kettle. There's this—" she laughed, wet-eyed, "—there's this whole generation growing up who will never know what it was like, checking their watches at midnight, listening to their neighbors through the walls. They'll think we're strange for flinching at round numbers. Good. Let them think we're strange."

She reached out and laid her palm flat on the stone. It was cool, but not cold. Not breathing-frost cold. Just stone, keeping its own counsel.

"Danny says thank you," she said. "He can't say it to your face because your face is currently a door, but he says it. His daughter leaves flowers here. So does half the town, honestly — you're becoming a local legend, which would horrify you, which is why I'm telling you. There's talk of a plaque. I'm campaigning hard against it. You'd hate a plaque. Plaques are for people who are finished, and you're not finished, you're just... elsewhere. Doing the job."

The sun moved. The crocuses opened and closed around her like slow applause.

On zero — she'd learned to wait for it, learned the rhythm of her own telling, the way her voice would run down like a clock winding to its resting place — on zero, sometimes, she heard it.

Not words. Never words. But a sound beneath the wind, beneath the bees, a sound like a man exhaling after a long shift, or like someone on the far side of a heavy door leaning their forehead against it. Once — only once, in autumn, with the first impossible snowdrops nodding around her knees — she could have sworn she heard her name. Two syllables, warm and dry and faintly amused, exactly the way Oscar Winterbourne had said *Priya* a thousand times across a nurses' station, usually right before telling her she was being sentimental and usually while being secretly pleased about it.

She never mentioned it to anyone. Some things you keep the way Ada kept her drawings: not because they're proof, but because you like to remember where things are.

* * *

The second year, she brought coffee.

"One sugar, no cream, because you were a monster about it," she announced, setting the paper cup on the stone. "And before you say anything, yes, I know you can't drink it. Consider it an offering. Or consider it me being sentimental, which you also accused me of, frequently, and look how that turned out — I was right about everything, and you're a door."

The grass rustled. Somewhere behind her, Ada Okafor was teaching her father the names of the out-of-season flowers, and Danny was getting most of them wrong, and their laughter carried across the meadow like something being mended.

Priya stayed until the light went long and gold. She told him about the new hire at Marlowe who reminded her painfully of a younger Oscar, all rationalism and raised eyebrows, and how Priya had taken her aside and said, *When the patients tell you something strange, write it down anyway,* and the girl had nodded the way you nod at superstition, and that was fine. Seeds took time. Everything here took time — a hundred and seventy years, sometimes, from planting to bloom.

"Equinox after next," she said, standing, brushing off her knees. "Same time. Don't go anywhere."

And because it was zero, because the last number had settled into the earth like a coin into a well, she paused with her hand on the stone one more time and listened.

The meadow breathed. The flowers leaned in.

And from beneath — soft, patient, unmistakably content — came the sound of a man who had finally, after a lifetime of disbelief, been believed in: a single knock, gentle as a knuckle on wood, like someone on the other side saying *noted*, like a nurse's chart signed at the end of a very long shift.

*I'll keep writing it down,* Priya promised the door. *Every equinox. Every piece of news.*

*No unusual vocalizations,* she imagined him replying, dry as ever. *Except one.*

She smiled all the way back to the car, and behind her the meadow bloomed on, out of season, out of turn, out of debt — a whole town's belonging paid for by one man who had chosen, at the last, to be counted.
