## 3. The Bell That Isn't There

The archive had been very clear about the bell.

Sable Eastwick had spent two evenings in Hollowmere's parish records room, a low-ceilinged space above the vestry that smelled of dust and lamp oil, reading through ledgers and minutes with the methodical patience of a man who believed that paper did not lie. The entry was dated October 1911, in a curate's careful hand: *The bell having cracked beyond repair, it is resolved that same be sold to Messrs. Whitcombe & Sons, founders, of Ashleigh Cross, there to be melted down. Proceeds toward the repair of the schoolhouse roof.*

There was even a receipt. Eleven pounds, four shillings. The bell of St. Aldhelm's had gone into the fire sixty years ago and come out as something else entirely—candlesticks, perhaps, or a farm gate hinge. It was not in the tower. It was not anywhere. It had ceased to exist before any living villager had been born.

Which made it difficult to account for what he heard at three o'clock in the morning on his ninth night in Hollowmere.

He woke the way one wakes from falling—not with a start but with a lurch, already upright in bed, heart going hard against his ribs. The cottage he rented from Mrs. Tallow was small and clean and smelled faintly of beeswax, and its silence was normally total, the deep rural silence he'd told himself he wanted when he left the city. But this silence had a hole in it. And through the hole came sound.

*Bronn.*

Low. Bronze-dark. A single stroke that seemed to arrive not through his ears but up through the floorboards, through the soles of his feet, through the marrow of him.

*Bronn.*

Sable sat perfectly still. He told himself: cart on the lane, a gate swinging, some piece of metal struck by wind. He told himself: you are new here; your ears are still learning this place's vocabulary. He told himself these things in the flat, reasonable voice he used on pupils who claimed their homework had been eaten by ghosts, and all the while he counted the seconds between strokes, because counting was what he did instead of being afraid.

Fourteen seconds. Then another stroke. Fourteen seconds again. The interval never varied, and that was the worst part—the *patience* of it. Wind does not keep time. Carts do not keep time. Whatever this was, it kept time like a clock keeps time, like a heart keeps time, like something that intended to go on for as long as it pleased.

*Bronn.*

He got up. He told himself it was to close the window against the cold, which was true, incidentally. He crossed the narrow room and looked out over the lane, where frost had climbed the glass in ferns and feathers, thick enough to blur the world into watercolor. Through the gaps in the pattern he could see the village: dark cottages, no lights anywhere, and beyond them the square gray tower of St. Aldhelm's standing against a sky the color of dirty wool.

No light moved in the lanes. No figure stood in the churchyard. Nothing explained the sound, and yet the sound went on—*bronn*, fourteen seconds, *bronn*—and now that he listened properly, he could hear that it wasn't coming from the tower at all. It was coming from everywhere. From under the ground. From inside the walls. As if the whole village were a bell and something unseen were walking around it, tapping it, listening for the note it made.

His hand found the latch. He hesitated, then closed the window anyway, because his fingers were numb, because the room needed warming, because closing a window is a thing a sane man does at three in the morning. The glass sealed. The cold stopped. The bell did not.

It rang until a little past four. Sable knew because he sat up watching the clock, wrapped in a blanket he refused to admit he needed, counting strokes. Forty-one, by his tally. Then nothing. The silence that followed was worse than the ringing—it had texture, weight, the quality of held breath—and he lay down without undressing and did not sleep so much as wait for morning.

* * *

In daylight, Hollowmere was aggressively ordinary. Frost on the hedges, smoke from the chimneys, Mrs. Tallow's hens complaining in their run. Sable walked to the school with his collar up and his jaw set, rehearsing how he would raise the subject, and raised it before he'd meant to—with Mrs. Tallow herself, over the fence, casual as commenting on weather.

"Odd noise last night," he said. "Carry quite far, don't they, church bells."

Mrs. Tallow smiled her broad, warm smile—the smile everyone in Hollowmere wore like a uniform—and said, "Oh, I slept like the dead, Mr. Eastwick. Not a peep out of me." She gathered eggs into her apron, unhurried. "You hear something?"

"A bell," he said. "Ringing most of the night."

Her hands did not stop moving, but they slowed—a fraction, a half-beat, eggs settling one by one into cloth. "Well," she said pleasantly. "Frost can play tricks on the ears. Sound carries strange in cold air. Bounces about." She handed him two brown eggs over the fence, unasked, unrefusable. "For your breakfast. You want keeping warm, this weather."

He took the eggs because refusing felt somehow dangerous, and walked on, and asked the butcher's boy, and the postman, and old Mr. Quill sweeping his step. All of them had heard nothing. All of them smiled. All of them offered him something—a sausage, a stamp, a boiled sweet from a tin—and all of them, when he mentioned the bell, performed the same small slowing of the hands, the same pleasant deflection, the same gentle implication that the fault lay in his ears, his sleep, his nerves.

By the time he reached the school gate he had stopped asking. Not because he believed them—he did not believe them, not for a moment—but because he had understood something that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone: they weren't surprised. Surprise looks different. Surprise has edges. What he'd seen instead, six times over, was recognition wearing politeness as a mask, and behind the mask, something that might have been pity.

They knew what he'd heard. They had known before he opened his mouth.

* * *

Eleven children looked up when he entered. Twelve desks waited. He had stopped trying not to count them.

The morning proceeded—arithmetic, recitation, inkwells and cold fingers—and Sable taught well, because teaching well was the one solid thing beneath his feet. It was only at the midday break, grading sums alone in the empty room while the children shouted in the yard, that Mrs. Alder appeared in his doorway.

She was headmistress in name and something else in fact; he had never quite determined what she actually did all day, only that she was always near, always kind, always watching with those pale, patient eyes. Today she carried a covered dish. Of course she did. There was always a dish.

"Lamb and barley," she said, setting it on his desk. "For your supper. You're looking thin, Mr. Eastwick."

"Thank you. Mrs. Alder—" He laid down his pen. "May I ask you something plainly? Last night, the bell. The church bell rang for over an hour. And this morning I'm told the church has no bell. I've read the records myself. Melted down in 1911. So either I imagined an hour of sound, or—" He spread his hands. "I'd rather know than be managed, if it's all the same."

Mrs. Alder regarded him for a long moment. Outside, the children's voices rose and fell. When she spoke, her tone was gentle as a poultice laid on a wound.

"You heard it," she said. Not a question.

"So it does ring."

"It rang," she agreed softly. "And now it's stopped, and that's what matters. It stops, Mr. Eastwick. It always stops." She smoothed her skirt, and for just a moment her eyes went to the twelfth desk in the back row, then away again, quick as a tongue touching a broken tooth. "Hollowmere is a good village. We look after our own, and we look after guests. You've been welcomed here properly—I hope you feel that."

"I feel," Sable said carefully, "that everyone is very kind."

"We are." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, and that was somehow the most frightening thing yet—that it genuinely did. "Then take a bit of advice, from someone who's been here longer than you've been alive. Stay in after dark. Keep your windows shut. And if you hear anything in the night—a bell, a knock, a voice calling friendly as you please—you let it ring, and you stay abed, and come morning it'll all be as if nothing happened. Because it will have been nothing. Do you see? Nothing at all."

"And if I don't stay in?"

The kindness in her face didn't falter, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond—no crack, no sound, just a change in the depth of things.

"Why would you go out?" she asked, wondering, almost tender. "Whatever could be out there that isn't in here?" She touched the covered dish once, lightly, a benediction. "Eat while it's hot."

She left. Sable sat looking at the lamb and barley, steam curling from under the lid, and understood that he had just been given a rule without being told the game, a warning without being named the danger, and that every warm thing in this village—the dishes, the smiles, the eggs over fences—was arranged around a hole shaped exactly like whatever happened after dark.

At the back of the room, the twelfth desk stood empty in the gray light. On its surface, though he had wiped it clean himself two days ago, a fine dust had settled overnight, and in the dust, drawn by no finger he could imagine, was a small round shape.

A circle. Like the mouth of a bell.

That evening he locked his door, shuttered his windows, and set his watch beside the lamp. At 3:04 a.m., he was still awake to hear it begin again—*bronn*—and this time, he noticed, the strokes were closer together.

Twelve seconds now. And counting down.
