## 8. The Choosing

The bell began at midnight.

Sable knew the time precisely because he had not slept—he had spent three nights now with the ledger open on his desk like a wound he kept returning to, reading and rereading the entries in their fading brown ink until the words had worn grooves in him. *We welcome the stranger into our homes and hearts.* The same sentence, every winter since 1911, in a different hand each year. Mrs. Alder's hand for the last forty of them.

The bell did not ring so much as it *arrived*—a single deep note that seemed to come up through the floorboards, through the bones of the house, through the marrow of him, and then again, and again, unhurried, patient, as though it had all the cold in the world to wait through.

He had heard it before, at 3 a.m., and told himself it was wind in a chimney, ice shifting on a branch, his own blood in his own ears. But this was different. This was the bell rung *on purpose*, by something with intent, and it did not stop. Ten minutes. Twenty. A peal with no arm tiring behind it, no rope, no tower—because there was no tower. There was no bell. It had been melted down in 1911 and poured into ploughshares, according to the archive, and Sable had read that entry twice and laughed at it once, alone, in a way that sounded strange even to himself.

Now he stood at his window with his coat already on, because some part of him had dressed before his mind agreed to anything, and watched the frost climb the glass in patterns too deliberate to be weather. Feathers. Then fingers. Then, unmistakably, letters—the frost writing in a slanted child's hand, forming a word, losing it, forming it again: *COME.*

"No," Sable said aloud, to the window, to the village, to whatever listened. And then, because Wren was missing and had been missing for two days and the whole village wore its grief like a borrowed coat: "Yes."

He took the lamp, though he suspected he would not need it.

* * *

Hollowmere at midnight was a village holding its breath. Every window he passed was dark, but he felt the watching anyway—that pressure on the back of the neck he'd been living with for weeks, the sensation of being a fish in a bowl of kindness. No curtains twitched. No doors opened. They all knew what tonight was. They had known since the first frost, since the casseroles began, since Mrs. Alder pressed the pen into his hand over that ledger dated 1911 and smiled her warm, terrible smile and said, *Welcome home, Mr. Eastwick.*

Home. He had wanted it so badly. That was the hook of the thing, he understood now—they hadn't caught him with tricks. They had caught him with soup, with firelight, with eleven children chanting his name on his first morning, with the simple animal relief of being *wanted*. He had been lonely in a way he'd never admitted even to himself, and Hollowmere had smelled the loneliness on him like blood in water.

The churchyard gate stood open. It was never open.

Inside, the frost had taken the ground entirely—not a dusting but a covering, an inch-thick rime that gleamed under a moon he couldn't see through the cloud, glowing faintly from within, the way snow does at dusk, the way things glow when they are not reflecting light but making it. And across that white field, written in lines of black frost that cut down to the earth itself, were names.

Row upon row of them. Children's names.

*Edith Marlowe. Thomas Vane. Alice Prewitt. Samuel Ockham. Beatrice Fen. Henry Alder.*

Sable's breath stopped. He knew these names. He had found them in the school register, in the census pages, in the margins of Mrs. Alder's own ledgers—the class of 1911, twelve pupils who had walked into Hollowmere school one December morning and had never, in any record anywhere, walked out. The village said fever. The village said they'd been sent to relatives. The village said nothing at all if you asked twice.

And beneath them, fresh, the black lines still smoking faintly where they burned through the rime:

*Wren Calloway.*

"No," he said again, and this time it came out broken. He stepped through the gate and the frost accepted his weight without a sound, and the bell rang on overhead—from everywhere, from inside the air itself—and with each peal another letter seared itself into the churchyard, spelling Wren's name deeper, finishing it, the way you finish writing a letter you intend to send.

"You shouldn't have come out."

Mrs. Alder stood by the lych-gate, wrapped in a grey coat, her silver hair loose and uncombed, and she looked older than Sable had ever seen her—older than old, older than the village, her face in the frost-light like something carved and left out in weather for a century. In her hands she held a lantern she had not lit.

"You heard it," she said. Not a question. "They never hear it. That's how we know, you see. When the stranger hears the bell, the choosing is nearly done." She smiled, and it was the smile from the first day, the welcome-home smile, and it cracked down the middle like river ice. "I hoped—I prayed—you'd be deaf to it. I prayed wrong, I expect. I usually do."

"Where is she?" Sable's voice came out low and shaking. "Wren. Where is Wren?"

"In the frost." Mrs. Alder said it the way you'd say *in bed*, or *in God's hands*, gently, terribly. "Not gone. Never gone. Taken. There's a difference, Mr. Eastwick, and I've had sixty years to learn it." She lifted her chin toward the names on the ground. "Henry Alder is my brother. I was the thirteenth pupil. I stayed home that morning with a cough, and the frost came to the schoolhouse at noon, and when the mistress went in at one o'clock the room was empty and the windows were open and there was snow *inside*, drifted against the desks, though the sky was clear." Her voice didn't waver. It had been worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for sixty years. "It left the twelfth desk empty for me. It has left a twelfth desk empty every year since. It is very polite, the frost. It always leaves a place setting."

"The ledger," Sable said. "Your grandfather invited it. For the harvest."

"For the *children*," she corrected softly. "1910 was the starving winter. The crops failed, the well froze solid to the bottom, and the little ones were dying of hunger by February—we buried four before the thaw. So the men of Hollowmere went up to the hollow oak with the old book, the one from before the church, and they wrote the invitation, and they rang the melted-down bell one last time—it was still in pieces then, in the smithy, and it rang anyway—and the harvest came in January. Green shoots through a foot of snow. Wheat in six weeks. We ate. We lived." She looked at him with those pale eyes. "And every winter since, it has come back to collect what was promised. A child. Unless—" and here her voice changed, quickened, went almost tender, "—unless we give it a stranger instead. Someone welcomed. Someone who signed the book."

"I never signed it," Sable said. "I read it. I never—"

"You drank our tea," Mrs. Alder said. "You ate our bread. You let Mrs. Prewitt kiss your cheek at the harvest supper. You said *thank you*, Mr. Eastwick. You said *I'm happy here*." Each sentence landed like a nail. "That's all an invitation ever is. That's all it's ever been. Welcome given and welcome received. The words in the book are just the receipt."

The bell rang again, closer now, though it had no location—or every location—and in the ringing Sable heard, for the first time, what it was made of. Not bronze. Voices. Hundreds of small voices, layered and looped, singing one sustained note, and underneath the note, if you listened the way you should never listen, children laughing.

He thought of Wren's face on her last morning—she had come to his desk before lessons and put a paper crane beside his register, folded badly, and said, "Don't answer the bell, sir. Whatever happens. Don't say yes to anybody after dark." He had patted her head like a fond uncle and gone on marking sums.

Two days. She had been in the frost for two days, and the frost wrote her name patiently, completely, and the last letter was half-formed, a curve waiting to close.

"What do I do?" Sable asked, and hated how small his voice was. "Tell me what ends it."

Mrs. Alder crossed the frozen ground to him—her feet made no prints—and took his hands in hers, and her hands were warm, impossibly, horribly warm, the only warmth in the whole white churchyard. "You accept," she whispered. "You walk to the center and you say yes. You say *I am glad to be here, I am glad to stay, I will never leave.* You mean it—I think you could mean it, I've watched you, you love them, you love *her*, you love this place that isn't yours—and it will take you and it will let Wren go, because the bargain only wants one. One welcome, freely given, and the debt is paid and the village sleeps and next winter—" her grip tightened, "—next winter we find another teacher, and it begins again, but not your problem, not your grave. You'll be at peace. They're all at peace, the ones it takes. I hear them sometimes, in the walls, in the bell. They sound *happy*, Mr. Eastwick. Happier than any of us who stayed."

"And that's your answer?" Sable pulled his hands free. "Sixty years, and your answer is to feed it forever?"

"Our answer is to keep our children!" For the first time her voice broke, and under the break was everything—the buried graves of 1910, the empty desks, the decades of casseroles and smiles and rehearsed grief, all of it terror wearing a welcoming face. "You think we don't know what we are? You think we sleep? We are a village that sells strangers to the cold, and we do it *gratefully*, and we hate ourselves, and we do it again every winter, because the alternative is carrying small coffins to the churchyard and we have done that, we did it once, and it unmade us." Tears froze on her cheeks without falling. "Take the offer. Be happy. Let the girl go. Let us go. Please. You're the kindest man we've ever chosen—be kind all the way to the end."

The bell swelled. The frost-light rose. And in the center of the churchyard, where Wren's name lay finished and smoking, the rime began to move—gathering, rising, shaping itself into something tall and thin and patient, something with the outline of a doorway where its face should be, an open door with warmth spilling out of it, golden light, firelight, the smell of bread, the sound of eleven children laughing, everything he had ever wanted to belong to standing in the snow with its arms open.

*Sable,* said the doorway, in a voice like the first day, like Mrs. Alder's welcome, like home. *You came. We saved you a seat.*

Twelve desks. Eleven children. One empty chair, small and wooden, waiting.

Mrs. Alder was weeping. The bell was singing. The door was open.

And Sable Eastwick, who had wanted so badly to belong, understood at last that belonging was the bait, and that the thing offering it had never once, in a hundred years, been refused—

—until he opened his mouth, and spoke.
