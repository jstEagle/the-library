## 2. Counting Doors

The studio at KRVL has four doors, and I check them twice.

Not because I'm afraid someone will come in. Because I'm afraid something already has.

The first pass happens at 1:47 a.m., thirteen minutes before my shift begins, when the building belongs to the janitorial crew and the vending machine hums its one long note down the hallway like a monk who has forgotten the rest of the chant. I walk the perimeter of Studio B with my coffee cooling in one hand and my flashlight off, because the lights are never supposed to be off in here and I want to see them all burning before I do anything else. Overhead fluorescents. The board glow, green and amber, patient as a hearth. The little lamp on the production desk that Dale from engineering swears is a fire hazard and that I have sworn, in writing, to keep. The ON AIR sign out in the corridor, red as a struck match.

All burning. All accounted for.

Then the doors. Front door of the studio—deadbolt, thumb-turn, chain I installed myself and which management pretends not to notice. Back door by the record shelves—the old vinyl library nobody uses anymore, thousands of albums sleeping in their sleeves like a congregation mid-prayer—that one has a slide bolt, brass, older than me. The bathroom door doesn't count; bathrooms are liminal, and Grandmother taught me early that you don't count what doesn't want to be counted. And the double doors to the lobby, glass and steel, which I can't lock because the public has a right to the lobby, but which I can watch, and I do, the way a lighthouse keeper watches a horizon he cannot close.

Front, back, lobby. Count them. Then again: front, back, lobby. Twice, always twice, because once is checking and twice is *knowing*, and there is a difference, and the difference is the whole architecture of my life.

Ivo Ravensworth, 2 a.m. to 6 a.m., KRVL 1340 AM, Hollow Creek's voice after dark. That's what the license says. What it doesn't say is that the graveyard shift got its name honestly, or that I took this shift on purpose, eleven years ago, fresh out of broadcasting school with a voice too low for morning drive and a set of rules too old for anyone to remember teaching me.

* * *

My grandmother's house had nine doors, and she made me count them every night before bed.

This was not a game, though she dressed it as one, the way she dressed everything. *Count the doors, little wren,* she'd say, her hands warm around mine, walking me down the hall of the house on Corben Street where I went to live the year the creek took my parents. *Count them out loud. Numbers are honest things. They don't lie even when everything else does.*

One: the front door, painted the blue of a bruise healing. Two: the kitchen. Three: the pantry, which was more a closet than a room. Four through six: the bedrooms, hers and mine and the one we kept shut, which had been my parents', which stayed shut for reasons she never explained and I never asked about, because asking felt like opening it. Seven: the cellar. Eight: the attic. Nine: the back porch door, screen and wood, breathing with the weather.

Nine doors, counted twice, every night, in a whisper, while she stood behind me with her hand on my shoulder like a paperweight keeping me flat against the world.

And other rules, delivered the same way—not as warnings, never as warnings, but as manners. As etiquette. As though the world were simply a very large dinner party with very particular customs, and my grandmother its most gracious guest.

*Keep a light burning in every room you mean to live in.* Not superstition—housekeeping. *A dark room is an empty room,* she said, *and an empty room gets ideas.*

*Don't say your full name aloud. Not to strangers, not to friends, not into telephones.* When people asked who I was—and children are asked constantly, at school, at church, at the pharmacy counter—I said Ivo. Just Ivo. Teachers assumed I was shy. I was obedient.

*Avoid mirrors after sundown.* This one she kept herself so strictly that the house on Corben Street had almost none. The bathroom mirror hung high enough that I had to stand on my toes, and even then I saw only my chin and mouth, a stranger's lower face saying words I couldn't quite hear. She covered the hall mirror with a crocheted cloth shaped like a pineapple. When I asked why, once, age eight or so, she looked at me for a long time with those pale eyes that seemed lit from somewhere behind, and said:

*Because a mirror shows you what's standing in front of it. And sometimes, little wren, you don't want to know what's standing behind.*

I laughed, because I was eight, and she smiled, because she loved me, and neither of us laughed at the same thing.

* * *

Here is what I understood at seven and understand now, at thirty-four: the rules were not fear. Fear is loud. Fear runs in the halls and slams doors and makes you call your ex at three a.m. The rules were quieter than fear. The rules were *furniture*. You don't think about furniture. You don't lie awake wondering whether the chair will hold. You sit, and it holds, and your life arranges itself around the sitting.

So: I check the doors twice. I keep every light burning. I don't say my full name on air—which makes for interesting radio, let me tell you, a host who introduces himself as only half himself—and I haven't owned a mirror in eleven years. I shave by feel. I check my hair in the reflection of the studio glass, angled, partial, a suggestion of a face rather than a fact. Nadia thinks it's vanity inverted, some hipster affectation, and I let her think it, because the truth sounds like either madness or confession, and I've spent thirty-four years being neither.

Mostly.

There are nights—there was last night—when the rules feel less like furniture and more like sandbags, stacked against a river I can hear but cannot see.

* * *

Last night. Let me tell you about last night, because I've been circling it since I clocked in, the way my tongue keeps finding the chipped tooth I got falling off Denny Okafor's garage roof when we were twelve.

Marla Voss called the station at 2:14 a.m.

I knew Marla's voice before I knew it was Marla, which is the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a stone looking for what's underneath. Warm. A little hoarse at the edges, the way smokers get. She said, "Is this the night man? The one who talks till sunup?" and I said yes ma'am, this is Ivo, you're on KRVL, what's on your mind tonight?—and there was a pause on the line, a soft staticky pause, and then she laughed, low and pleased, like I'd passed some kind of test.

"I used to listen to you," she said. "Back when you did Sundays. My daughter thought you lived inside the radio. Little Ruthie—" no wait, that's not right, her girl was Angie, Angie Voss, who bagged groceries at Hendricks' and called everyone *hon*, including me, especially me. "Little Angie thought you lived in there. In the little box."

"Sounds about right," I said, doing the bit, the warm-voiced bit, the thing I do at two in the morning for truckers and insomniacs and the lonely, which is to say: for everyone. "Some nights I'm not sure she's wrong."

Another laugh. Then, quieter, closer to the microphone than any caller ever gets, as if she'd leaned in until her lips touched the mesh: "I need you to do something for me, honey. Tell my girl goodbye. Tell her it wasn't her fault about the pills, and tell her I saw the garden she planted, and it's beautiful, and tell her—" and here her voice did something I have heard exactly once before in my life and hoped never to hear again, it went *flat*, level as a table, all the warmth still in it but nothing underneath, "—tell her I'm sorry I missed the frost."

"Ma'am," I said carefully, "who is this?"

"You know who I am, Ivo." And she said my name the way my grandmother used to say it—just the one name, just *Ivo*, but with a weight on the end of it, like setting down something heavy. "You buried me Tuesday. It was a lovely service. The pastor talked too long, but they always do. You wore the gray suit. You cried a little, right at the end, when they played 'In the Garden.' Don't be embarrassed. It's a good hymn."

I want to tell you I handled it well. I want to tell you my voice didn't change, that my hands didn't go cold on the board, that I didn't look at the lobby doors—at the glass, at the dark beyond the glass—with the sudden animal attention of prey. But this is my account, and I promised myself I'd keep honest books if I ever wrote any of this down.

I said, "This is a prank, and it's a cruel one, and I'm going to let you go now," and my voice held, professional, smooth as the board readouts, and I faded her mic down and punched her off and sat in the green-and-amber glow with my heart going like a drumline.

Because here's the thing. Here's the thing I logged in the station log as *prank call, 2:14 a.m., disconnected* and then stared at for four minutes without blinking:

I attended Marla Voss's funeral on Tuesday. I wore the gray suit. I cried a little, right at the end, when they played "In the Garden." I have told no one about the crying. Not Nadia, not the pastor, not the notebook I keep under false bottoms.

Angie Voss planted a garden last spring. I helped her carry the tomato cages from Hendricks'. I have not been to her house since June. I did not know about the frost.

The frost came Thursday.

* * *

After I killed the call I did what I always do, which is the routine, which is the furniture, which is the sandbags. I walked the doors. Front—locked, chained, holding. Back—bolted, brass cold under my thumb, holding. Lobby—glass, watching, the parking lot beyond it empty except for my car and the sodium light making its orange island, and nothing standing at the edge of the light, nothing waiting, nothing wearing the posture of patience.

Nothing. I checked twice anyway.

Then I turned up every light in the building, including the ones in the hallways and the break room and the transmitter shed out back, which I could see glowing through the window like a lantern in a story, and I put the Pineapple Boys' "Midnight Train" on the turntable even though it's 1978 and scratchy and nobody has requested it since 1997, because I needed something human in the room, something with breath in it, and scratch and hiss is breath, is proof somebody sang this once, alive, with lungs.

Grandmother would have approved of the record. She'd have covered her eyes at the rest.

* * *

I keep coming back to her hands. Isn't that strange, of all the things—the rules, the counting, the mirror with its crocheted shroud—it's her hands I keep seeing tonight. How they felt on my shoulders during the counting. How they smelled of lavender and camphor and underneath it something mineral, something like wet slate, like the stones down past the tree line that the historical society calls the Hollow Creek Standing Stones and that she called, once, when she thought I wasn't listening, *the fence*.

She died when I was nineteen. Peacefully, in her sleep, in a house with nine doors, every light burning. The coroner wrote cardiac arrest. What I remember is that the night before, she made me count the doors with her—one last time, she said, just to be sure—and afterward she held my face in those slate-and-lavender hands and looked at me so long and so sadly that I asked what was wrong, and she said:

"Nothing, little wren. Nothing at all. You count so beautifully."

And then she kissed my forehead and sent me to bed, and in the morning the house was full of neighbors and casseroles and the particular silence of a place where the person who made the rules has stopped making them.

I was nineteen and orphaned twice over, and I did the only sensible thing. I moved into a studio apartment with exactly four doors, and I bought every lamp at the thrift store, and I applied for the graveyard shift.

People ask—Nadia asks, gently, over coffee, in the way of someone who has decided not to push—why nights? Why does a man with a voice like mine, a voice built for mornings, for waking people up, bury himself in the dead hours?

And I always give the true answer, which sounds like a joke: because the dead hours are when I'm needed.

I just never explain what I mean.

* * *

It's 3:40 a.m. now. The record has ended; the needle rides the runout groove, that soft rhythmic heartbeat of nothing, and I should lift it, and I will, in a second. First I'm going to write this down, because writing it down makes it a log entry instead of a haunting, makes it furniture instead of floodwater.

Caller identified herself as Marla Voss. Marla Voss deceased, interred Tuesday. Call logged as prank. Disconnected.

Except.

Except that as I sat there, needle ticking, lights burning, doors counted and counted again, the request line lit up. One soft amber bulb on the board, the way it does when someone dials in, patient, polite, waiting its turn among the trucks and the weather warnings and the lonely.

And caller ID, which is a modern miracle and mostly lies, showed a number I recognized, because I'd seen it on the funeral program, on the card that says *IN LIEU OF FLOWERS*.

The Voss residence.

The phone is ringing again. Second time tonight. Third ring now, fourth, and the studio is bright as noon and every door is locked and I am a grown man with a rule for everything, and none of my rules say anything at all about what to do when the dead won't stop dialing.

On the fifth ring, I reach for the fader.
