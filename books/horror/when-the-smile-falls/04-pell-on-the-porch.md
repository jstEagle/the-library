## 4. Pell on the Porch

The drive home from the station took eleven minutes in daylight and fourteen at night, because Ivo took the long way around the reservoir, where the road bent away from the houses and the dark had nothing in it but dark. It was a rule they'd never written down. Take the long way. Give the dark room to be only itself.

Tonight the dark had something else in it. Not on the road—nothing so simple. It was more that the dark felt *occupied*, the way a house feels when someone is home but hasn't turned on a light. Ivo kept the radio on low, some syndicated ghost of a station playing soft rock from two states away, and let the music fill the cab of the truck the way you'd fill a silence with your own humming so you wouldn't hear the other thing humming back.

The reservoir was flat and black and perfectly still. Not a breath of wind anywhere in Hollow Creek, and yet as Ivo passed the boat ramp, the water shivered—once, all at once, like a held breath released—and went still again.

Ivo did not stop. Ivo had learned, at seven years old, the architecture of not stopping.

* * *

The house sat at the end of Corvid Lane, the last one before the tree line took over, a narrow two-story with a wraparound porch that Ivo's grandfather had built and Ivo's grandmother had painted the color of dried blood and never let anyone repaint. The porch light was on. The porch light was *always* on; Ivo had not turned it off since 1998, when they were nine and had tried, once, just to see, and had spent the whole next day sick with a dread that had no name and no shape and no reason, and had never tried again.

Ivo parked in the gravel and sat for a moment with the engine ticking, doing the thing they did every morning at the end of every shift: the inventory. Doors locked at the station—checked twice, once at midnight, once at five. Lights burning in the studio, the hall, the lobby, the bathroom, all of them, even the one over the transmitter rack that buzzed like a trapped wasp. No mirrors looked at. No full names said aloud. The drive home. The long way. The reservoir, and its one shiver.

All the rules kept. All the small, stupid, load-bearing rules that had held up the roof of Ivo's whole life without anyone ever seeing the beams.

Ivo looked at the porch light. It was on. That was right.

The figure sitting on the porch steps was not right at all.

* * *

Later—Ivo would think about this later, in the way you probe a tooth that aches, unable to leave it alone—the strangest part was not that someone was there. Strangers sat on that porch sometimes. Hollow Creek was the kind of town where a lost fisherman might wander up the drive at dawn, or a kid who'd missed the last bus, and Ivo had developed a whole gentle protocol for it: come up slow, hands visible, offer the phone, keep your own name out of your mouth.

No. The strangest part was that Ivo knew him.

Knew him the way you know the smell of your childhood kitchen, the way your hand knows a banister in the dark. The figure sat on the third step from the bottom, knees drawn up, chin on his knees, in the exact posture Ivo had seen ten thousand times in a childhood that everyone else insisted had been solitary. He was small—not child-small, but small the way some grown people stay small, compact and folded, as though he'd been made slightly under scale and never corrected. He wore a canvas coat too big for him, sleeves rolled in thick cuffs, and the coat was buttoned wrong. The top button sat in the second hole, the second in the fourth, so that the whole front of him hung crooked, one flap longer than the other, mismatched, mended in places with thread that didn't match.

Ivo had memorized those buttons. Ivo had, at seven, at eight, at nine, traced them with a finger while Pell sat patiently still, because touching them proved he was real, and Ivo had needed him to be real so badly that the needing had made its own proof.

"Pell," Ivo said. The name came out of them the way a splinter comes out—sudden, relieving, leaving a small dark hole behind.

"Morning, Ivo." Pell didn't lift his head. His voice was exactly as remembered too: soft and slightly rough, like a recording of a voice played through a wall. "You look tired."

"You're not real." Ivo's hands had gone cold on the wheel. "You were never real. Dr. Ammerman said—I did the drawings, I did the interviews. You were a coping construct. Grief response. I was seven and my parents had just—"

"And now you're thirty-two," Pell said, "and your parents are still dead, and the grief never left, did it. It just learned to sit quietly." He lifted his head then, and the porch light fell across his face, and Ivo's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the October air. He was exactly as remembered. *Exactly.* Children's imaginary friends were supposed to be foggy, half-invented, assembled from cartoons and wishful thinking. But Ivo had never invented Pell's face. Ivo had only ever *recognized* it, the way you recognize a face in a crowd that you've never seen before and will never see again. Round-cheeked, solemn, with eyes the color of creek water over slate—gray going green going gray, never settling on one. Hair that looked cut by someone in a hurry with dull scissors. A small scar through one eyebrow that Ivo had never asked about because asking felt dangerous, even at seven.

Especially at seven.

"You kept the rules," Pell said. "Good."

* * *

Ivo got out of the truck because the alternative was sitting in it forever, and because some deep animal part of them understood that the porch light was on and the figure was sitting *inside* the light, and that had to mean something. It had to. Twenty years of rules had to mean something.

The gravel crunched under their boots. The cold smelled like woodsmoke and wet leaves and, underneath it, faintly, like the inside of the station—hot electronics and old carpet—the smell of the hours when the phones rang. Ivo stopped at the bottom of the steps, one step below Pell, close enough now to see that his coat was damp at the shoulders, beaded with dew, as if he'd been sitting there a long time. Since before dawn. Since before Ivo left the station, maybe. Since Marla's voice came down the line at 2:07 a.m. asking them to tell her daughter goodbye.

"How long have you been sitting here?" Ivo asked.

"Awhile." Pell smiled. It was a warm smile. It was the warmest thing Ivo had seen in days, and that was the most frightening thing about it. "You always did ask how long. At the flood house. At the school. You'd say, *how long have you been standing there, Pell*, and I'd say, *awhile*, and you'd never ask the next question."

"Because the next question was how you got there."

"And you didn't want to know." Pell unfolded, slowly, joint by joint, and stood on the step so that he and Ivo were nearly eye to eye, though Ivo had to look down, slightly, at a grown man who was somehow still the size of the friend who'd fit under a child's blanket fort. Up close he smelled like nothing. That was wrong too. Everything smelled like something—woodsmoke, coffee, the copper tang of the truck's old heater. Pell smelled like the moment before you remember a word. Like the white of a page.

"Ivo." He said their name gently, and it landed strangely, because no one said it anymore, not whole, not in the open air. At the station it was *Ravensworth* or *hey, Ivo* clipped short by people in a hurry. Nadia said it softly, but Nadia said everything softly. Pell said it the way Grandmother Ruth-Ellen had said it—full, unhurried, with weight behind it, a name set down on a table like a key. "You had a hard shift."

"You were listening." It wasn't a question. The cold in Ivo's chest spread outward, into the arms, the hands. "You were listening to the show."

"I listen to everything on this street." Pell said it without apology, without menace, the way you'd say *I water the plants*. "You logged the call. Marla Trenholm. You wrote *possible prank* in the log, and then you sat in the dark studio for forty minutes with your hand on the fader, waiting for her to call back."

Ivo's throat closed. "I don't want to talk about the show."

"You kept the rules," Pell said again, and this time he said it like an accounting, like a man reading a ledger aloud. "Doors counted twice. Every light burning. No mirrors. No full names spoken. The long way home. You've done it every day for twenty-five years, Ivo. You think Grandmother made those up to keep a lonely child busy? You think she taught you to count doors because counting was calming?"

The porch light buzzed overhead. Somewhere down the lane, a dog began to bark—one sharp, rhythmic complaint—and then stopped all at once, mid-bark, as if a hand had closed over its mouth, and the silence afterward was worse than the barking.

"Come inside," Ivo said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them; inside, everything was shaking like the reservoir had shaken. "It's cold."

"It's not the cold you need to worry about." But Pell came up the steps anyway, and at the door he stopped, and Ivo watched—watched with the particular horror of an adult watching a childhood belief walk through a door it shouldn't fit through—watched Pell pause at the threshold and look back over his shoulder at the tree line, at the dark standing between the trees like a crowd of people facing away, and say, quietly, to the dark, or to Ivo, or to both: "Not yet. He's not done learning."

Then he stepped inside, and the house did not resist him, and that was somehow the worst thing of all.

* * *

Ivo made coffee because hands needed something to do. The kitchen was small and yellow-walled and full of Grandmother Ruth-Ellen's stubborn afterlife—the copper pots she'd never let anyone polish, the calendar from three years ago still turned to March because the month she died in seemed, to Ivo, like a room you didn't redecorate. Pell sat at the table in the canvas coat, still buttoned wrong, hands wrapped around a mug he didn't drink from, and the mug steamed, and the steam rose, and he didn't drink, and Ivo watched the steam and understood that some questions were better left in the ground and was going to leave every one of them there anyway, and then heard themself ask:

"What are you?"

Pell looked up. Creek-water eyes. Gray going green going gray.

"I told you what I am when you were seven. You just didn't believe me, because what I told you was impossible, and you were a sensible child. Sensible children survive longer, Grandmother said. So you decided I was imaginary, and I let you, because it was safer for you to have an imaginary friend than a real one."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm giving tonight." He said it kindly. That was the trap of him, Ivo thought, watching the steam curl; the kindness was load-bearing too. "Ask me the other question. The one you've been carrying since the truck."

Ivo set their own mug down. The ceramic clicked against the table too loudly in the quiet house.

"Why now?" Their voice cracked on *now*, and they hated it, and kept going. "Twenty years, Pell. I stopped seeing you when I was twelve. I grieved you. I did the *grieving*, I did the therapy, I put you away with the flood photographs and my mother's handwriting and everything else that hurt to look at. And now Marla Trenholm calls my station from her own grave, and nine other lines light up behind her, and the reservoir shivers with no wind, and a dog stops barking like it was switched off, and I come home and you're sitting on my porch with your buttons wrong, exactly, *exactly* as I remember you, not one day older. Why now? What changed?"

For a long moment Pell just looked at them, and the steam rose between them, and the refrigerator hummed, and the house held its breath.

"You did," Pell said. "Something changed in you. Tuesday night, when you logged Marla's call as a prank—something in you stopped pretending. You've been pretending since you were twelve, Ivo. Pretending the rules were habits. Pretending the dark on Corvid Lane was only dark. Pretending you couldn't hear the phones before they rang." He leaned forward, and the crooked coat hung, and the porch light through the kitchen window caught the scar through his eyebrow. "The dead found the frequency because you finally started listening. And the thing that's been waiting since before the town had a name—it noticed you listening. It's been noticing for a while. But now it's *looking*."

Ivo's mouth was dry. "The rules. Grandmother's rules. You said I kept them. You said it like—like you were checking."

"I was checking." Pell sat back, and folded his small hands around the warm mug he would never drink, and looked at Ivo with an expression Ivo had last seen on their grandmother's face the winter before she died, when she'd stood at this same window looking out at the tree line and said, *it's almost time to teach you the rest, child, but not yet, not yet, pray it's never.*

"You kept them," Pell said, "and it bought you twenty-five years. But the rules were never the whole of it, Ivo. They were the fence, not the house. And the fence—" his eyes went to the window, to the dark beyond it, to the tree line where the dog had stopped barking, "—the fence is coming down."

Outside, far down Corvid Lane, every porch light on the street flickered once, all together, like a held breath.

Pell smiled the warm smile again, and said: "Now. Sit down. It's time I told you what the rules were *for*."
