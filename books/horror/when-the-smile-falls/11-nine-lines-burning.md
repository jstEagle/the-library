## 11. Nine Lines Burning

The board had been quiet for three nights, and I had learned to distrust quiet the way sailors learn to distrust a flat sea.

I sat in the studio at KRVL with the fader on the console glowing amber, the ON AIR sign burning its patient red eye into the glass, and I let a Billie Holiday record spin because the dead, whatever else they wanted, seemed to like her. Marla hadn't called since Tuesday. Denny Okafor hadn't called at all, though I thought of him every time line three blinked and didn't. The town of Hollow Creek lay under a sky the color of wet slate, and the station's fluorescent hum was the only thing between me and the sound of my own heartbeat, which had gotten louder lately, like something knocking to be let out.

I had rules for nights like this. I always had rules.

Keep the lights on. Check the doors twice. Never say your full name on air, not even once, not even as a joke, not even when the sponsor copy had it printed in bold at the top of the page—*brought to you by the good folks at Hollow Creek Hardware, Ivo*—because a name spoken is a name given, and a name given is a hand extended into the dark, and something in this town had been waiting a long time for a hand like mine.

Grandmother's rules. I'd thought they were superstition, the way you think that at seven and at seventeen and, if you're lucky, at thirty-seven too. But Tuesday a dead woman had asked me to tell her daughter goodbye, and Wednesday I'd hung up on that same dead woman while her widower sat forty feet away in the lobby with his hat in his hands, and the rules had started to feel less like superstition and more like a fence I'd been leaning against my whole life without ever wondering what was on the other side.

The record ended. The needle hissed in the runout groove like a small animal trapped under ice. I faded it, leaned to the mic, and did the thing I do at 2:47 a.m. when the town is asleep and the dial is mine.

"You're listening to the graveyard shift on KRVL, Hollow Creek's voice in the dark. It's a cold one out there tonight. If you're up, I'm up. Line's open."

I never said which line. I never said my name. The dead, I was beginning to understand, don't need directions.

* * *

For a while, nothing. The kind of nothing I'd once loved—rain starting against the studio windows, the coffee going bitter in the cup, the red light steady. I pulled the log sheet toward me and wrote the time, the track, the weather, the small honest facts of a small honest shift. *2:51 a.m. Rain. Wind from the north. No calls.* My handwriting had changed in the past week. It had gotten smaller, the way handwriting does when the person writing it is trying to take up less room in the world.

Nadia came in at three, the way she always did, with two cups from the diner and that walk she had, half swagger, half apology, like she was perpetually arriving somewhere she'd been invited to but wasn't sure she should stay. Nadia Reyes, night dispatcher, county emergency services, who had taken to routing herself past KRVL on her break for reasons neither of us had ever said out loud, because saying things out loud was dangerous lately, and because some warmths are better left banked than stoked.

"Black, no sugar, because you're a purist and a masochist," she said, setting the cup on the console. "Also, you've got a taillight out on the Corolla, and if a deputy stops you, I cannot save you from the paperwork."

"I never drive at night."

"You never drive at all. That's the sad part." She dropped into the guest chair, the one with the torn vinyl, and looked at me the way she'd been looking at me all week—sideways, cataloguing, a woman who spent her nights listening to other people's worst moments and had developed an ear for the sound of someone coming apart. "You look like hell, Ravensworth."

Not the full name. Just the last one. Even she had learned to give me only pieces of myself, though she didn't know why. I loved her a little for it, in the way you love a person for instincts they don't know they have.

"Rough week," I said.

"Uh-huh." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "You know what dispatch got last night? Four calls, all between two and three a.m., all from the payphone outside the Gas-N-Go. You know that payphone's been dead for six years? Line was physically cut in 2019. But the calls came through, and the dispatcher who took them swears the voice was her mother, who's been in the ground since March." She said it lightly, testingly, the way you toss a stone onto ice to hear how far the crack goes. "Anyway. Probably a prank. Kids with a voice modulator, cutting into the trunk line. That's a thing kids do, right?"

"Sure," I said.

"Sure," she agreed, and neither of us believed it, and the rain got heavier, and the ON AIR light burned on between us like a small red congregation.

She left at ten past. I watched her taillights cross the parking lot—both of them working, on her truck, because Nadia took care of things—and then the lot was empty, and the dark came right up to the glass the way it always did, patient as a dog at a door.

* * *

At 3:33 a.m.—and I want it noted that I noticed the time, that some part of me has always kept a ledger of ugly numbers—the board lit up.

Not one line. All of them.

Nine lines, blinking in perfect unison, no stagger, no randomness, the way phones ring when people call and the way they do not ring when something else does. Line one. Line two. Line three, where Denny Okafor's silence had lived. Line four through nine, every circuit the station had, every copper vein running out into the sleeping dark of Hollow Creek, all of them pulsing the same red at the same rhythm, and I swear to you the rhythm was not the rhythm of a telephone. It was slower. It was patient. It was, if I'm honest, the rhythm of a heart at rest.

I did what any professional does. I told myself it was a fault in the trunk line. I told myself it was the storm. I reached for line one with a hand I pretended was steady, because the microphone was live and the town was listening and a host does not scream on the air, a host *smooths*, and smoothing is just screaming with better posture.

"KRVL, you're on the air."

The line connected, and the sound that came through was rain—but not our rain, not the rain on the studio glass. This was rain falling on something else. Tin, maybe. A roof. A coffin lid. And under the rain, a voice, and I knew the voice, God help me, I knew it from Tuesday, warm and wrong, Marla's voice with its particular lilt, the one that turned *honey* into two syllables.

But it wasn't Marla alone.

It was nine voices. I could hear them layered, one under another under another, men and women, young and old, all speaking the same words at the same moment with the same breath, the way a choir speaks when the choir has only ever learned one hymn. And the words were:

"*Ivo Aloysius Ravensworth.*"

I pulled my hand off the fader like it had bitten me.

Nobody knew that name. Nobody. My mother had died when I was seven; my father before the flood took her, and the name Aloysius had gone into the ground with the both of them, sealed in the family Bible, spoken at no baptism, printed on no document. Grandmother had made sure of it. *Never the whole of it, child,* she'd said, in the kitchen, with the lights all on at noon. *A name is a leash, and the whole name is a collar.* I had gone thirty years as Ivo, as Ivo R., as *hey, you, graveyard guy*, and the middle name had slept in the dark with my parents like a stone at the bottom of a well.

And now nine dead voices were saying it into my ear in unison, gently, the way you'd say the name of someone sleeping, the way you'd try a key in a lock you'd been waiting decades to open.

"*Ivo Aloysius Ravensworth,*" they said again, and the rain under them got louder, or closer, and I understood with a certainty that bypassed my brain and landed somewhere in my spine: they weren't calling me. They were *finding* me. Each repetition was a step. Each repetition closed a distance I couldn't see.

I hit the dump button on line one. The call died. Lines two through nine kept ringing, in unison, in that slow patient rhythm.

I dumped them all. Every line went dead at once—not sequentially, not like circuits failing, but all together, like a held breath released. The board sat dark and clean and innocent, and the studio was so quiet I could hear the rain, the real rain, and under it, faintly, from somewhere out past the parking lot, a sound I had no name for yet. Not a voice. Not a footstep. Something between them. Something *waiting*.

I did the show. That's the part I keep coming back to, the part that shames me and steadies me in equal measure: I put Billie back on the turntable, I read the 3:45 station break in a voice that only shook on the last word, and I did not say my name, not the short one, not any of it. I talked about the hardware store's leaf-blower sale. I told the town the roads were wet and to take it slow. I was warm. I was calm. I was, for fifty-one minutes, the voice in the dark for a few hundred sleeping people, and if any of them had been awake and listening closely, they might have heard what was underneath the calm—the sound of a man counting doors in his head, over and over, because counting is a prayer when you've lost all the others.

*Front door, back door, fire exit, loading door, the little door to the transmitter shed. Four doors. Four doors. Four.*

* * *

At 4:40 a.m., line three lit up alone.

One line. One ring. The ordinary kind. I stared at it for a long time. Every instinct I had—and my instincts had teeth now, they'd been fed all week—said don't. Every professional reflex said answer, because answering is the job, and the job was the last thing I had left that looked like a life.

I answered.

For three seconds, nothing but the rain-on-tin sound, closer now. Then a voice, small and dry, an old woman's voice, and it was not any of the nine. It was new. It was tired in a way that made my chest hurt.

"You counted," the voice said. "All week, you counted the doors. That's good. That's right. My mama taught me the doors, and her mama taught her." A pause, wet at the edges. "But honey, counting's not enough anymore. They said your whole name tonight. Do you understand what that means? When they get the whole of a name, the counting stops working. The doors stop being doors. You've been *seen*, Ivo Aloysius Ravensworth, and seen things don't get to be invisible just because they want to be."

"Who is this?" My voice came out in pieces.

"You know who I am. I'm the one who called Tuesday, and you were kind to me, and I've been sitting here since trying to find the words to tell you what's coming, because I liked you, honey. I liked your voice on the radio. You were the last living person to speak to me, and that makes you mine a little, the way a midwife's mine, the way a gravedigger's mine." The rain sound swelled behind her, and I realized it wasn't rain at all. It was breathing. A great many throats, breathing together. "There's something in your parking lot. There has been for an hour. It doesn't come in, because it's not allowed to come in while the lights are on and the doors are counted. But the lights won't matter after tonight. The name broke the seal. And it's *patient*, honey. It's the most patient thing there is. It's been waiting since before this town had a name, and now it has *yours*."

The line clicked dead. Not hung up. *Consumed*—the way a candle consumes a wick.

I sat very still. The ON AIR light glowed. The turntable turned. Out past the glass, at the far edge of the parking lot, where the sodium lamp gave up and the dark took over, there was a shape.

I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is all I have left, and I can't. It stood the way a person stands when they've missed the last bus and have decided, without anger, to wait for a morning one that will never come. Shoulders easy. Hands at its sides. Head slightly tilted, in the posture of listening. It was tall—taller than the lamp post beside it, or it seemed to be, the way things seem in the corner of the eye. It did not move. It did not need to. Everything about it said: *I have been here longer than you. I will be here after. There is no hurry in me, because hurry is for things that can lose.*

It had no face that I could see. But I felt, with absolute and sickening clarity, that it was smiling.

The red light burned. The record hissed toward its end. And I, Ivo Aloysius Ravensworth, whose whole name had been spoken by the dead and heard by the dark, sat in the last lit room in Hollow Creek and did the only thing left that the rules allowed.

I kept talking.
