## 14. Pell's Confession

I have worn this body for twenty years, and it has never once felt like mine. That is the first thing you should know. The hands are borrowed from a memory of a boy who never existed, the voice is stitched from the sound a screen door makes in August, and the face—well. The face is the kindest thing I could build from what Ivo's grief gave me to work with. Children in the flood year saw all sorts of things standing at the edges of their yards. I made sure that when Ivo looked out the window at seven years old, what they saw was a friend with mismatched buttons and a smile like a paper cut, and not the thing that had been circling the house since the night the water took their parents.

I am not imaginary. I have never been imaginary. Imaginary things are soft, and I was never soft. I was unfinished.

Let me start further back, because Ivo deserves the whole of it, and because I have carried it alone long enough that the carrying has begun to deform me—this body, this borrowed boy-shape, this patient smile. Everything unfinished deforms eventually. That is the law I was made under.

In 1849, eleven people stood in a circle of stones above Hollow Creek and began a rite to close a door. I will not pretend to understand all of what they were closing; I was not there, not as I am now. But I know what they intended, because I am made of the intention. They meant to bind a boundary—to name a keeper, to seal the covenant, to make of their town a locked room with the hungry thing kept outside the walls. Eleven of them spoke their parts. Eleven names, eleven lines of the surrender, eleven hands laid on the cold stone.

And the twelfth—the one who was to be the keeper, the one whose name and face and self would become the lock itself—stopped.

Halfway. Mid-sentence. Mid-word, if the old accounts are to be believed. The first Ravensworth looked at the stones, looked at what the rite asked of them, and ran. And the rite, which is not a thing that forgives incompleteness, did what unfinished things do. It broke into pieces. Most of it sank into the ground and became the debt, the hunger, the tall quiet patience that walks the edges of Hollow Creek to this day. The ledger went into a Ravensworth attic. The covenant went half-sealed, like a door with a boot jammed under it, holding—but leaking.

And a splinter of it, the smallest piece, the part that was meant to be the keeper's companion and witness and guide through the surrender—well. That piece had nowhere to go. So it waited. A hundred and fifty years, it waited, folded up small in the stones, in the soil, in the water table of a town that did not know it was drinking diluted ritual.

Then, in the flood year, a seven-year-old child lost both parents in one night, and sat alone in a house full of rules their grandmother had taught them—count the doors, keep the lights on, don't be seen, don't say your full name aloud—and that child was so precisely, so perfectly the last of the line, the heir to the unfinished thing, that I felt myself pulled toward them like water toward a drain.

I did not choose to be born. But I chose everything after.

* * *

Here is what I did not tell Ivo, all those years ago, sitting cross-legged on the bedroom floor while the collectors passed the windows: I was not sent to be their friend. I was sent to be their warden.

The covenant, even broken, even leaking, had instructions embedded in it like shrapnel. The Ravensworth line was marked to stay unseen. And the splinter of the rite—the piece of it that had no name and no body—was bound to the line until the rite completed. Bound to keep them hidden. Bound to keep them small. Bound to keep them alive until the night the debt came due and the last of the Ravensworths walked up the hill to the stones and finished what their ancestor fled.

A warden. That was the shape of my assignment. Wardens watch. Wardens count. Wardens report to the older, larger piece of the broken rite—the one that walks, the tall quiet thing, the patient collector—when a marked child strays too far into being seen. Wardens do not play games on bedroom floors. Wardens do not teach songs to make the counting easier. Wardens do not, under any circumstances, love.

I want to be very clear about the order of events, because I have gone over it ten thousand times in the dark of my own unfinishedness, and I know exactly where the hinge was.

The first night, I stood at the foot of Ivo's bed and did not exist yet. I was deciding what shape to take. The assignment said: appear as whatever keeps them compliant. Fear keeps children compliant. I had a whole catalogue of shapes to choose from—the thing in the well, the tall quiet posture, the wet dark. I could have been a nightmare, and Ivo would have hidden under the blankets and never said a full name aloud again, and the warden's work would have been done by breakfast.

Instead I looked at that small face, blotchy from crying, and I thought: *this child has lost everything that ever said their name.* And something in me—not the assignment, something older and more stubborn, something that had waited a hundred and fifty years in the cold and knew exactly what loneliness weighed—something in me said, *be the thing that stays.*

So I chose the mismatched buttons. I chose the grin. I chose the name Pell, which I took from half of the word *pellucid*, because I wanted to be the clear thing in a house full of murk. I chose to be a friend rather than a warden, and I told myself it was strategy. A friend can teach the rules better than a terror. A friend can keep a child indoors more thoroughly than any monster at the glass.

That was the lie I told myself for twenty years, and I am only now, in this borrowed body, in this kitchen at dawn, ready to say the truth underneath it:

I chose to be a friend because I wanted to be loved. And an unfinished thing, a fragment of a broken rite with no name of its own, wanting to be loved—that is either the most human thing in Hollow Creek or the most dangerous. I have never decided which.

* * *

The morning I came back—two days after the nine lines lit up, after Marla's voice came through the board warm and wrong—I found Ivo on the porch with their coffee going cold, looking at me like a person looks at a door they thought was painted on the wall.

I said, "You kept the rules. Good." Because that was the warden in me speaking, the assignment, the old shrapnel doing its work. But I stood on the bottom step, below them, the way I always used to, and I waited, and when they finally let me inside, I knew I could not keep the confession behind my teeth one more night.

So we sat in the kitchen. The one with the humming refrigerator and the window over the sink that Ivo has never once covered, because their grandmother said a kitchen window should always be able to see the road. The light came up gray and slow, the way it does in Hollow Creek, like the sun is checking whether it's safe.

"You're not imaginary," Ivo said. Not a question. They had known for days, maybe for years, in the way people know things they've arranged their whole life around not knowing.

"No."

"You were never imaginary."

"No."

"Then what are you?"

I looked at my hands. The borrowed boy-hands with the ink stain on the left thumb I'd copied from a boy who died in 1911, whose photograph I'd found in the historical society's drawer of unclaimed faces. I build myself out of the town's dead. I have never told anyone that either. There is a lot I have never told anyone; being a secret for a hundred and fifty years makes a habit of you.

"I'm the part of the rite that never got said," I told them. "Your ancestor stood at the stones with ten others and was supposed to give themselves to the boundary—name, face, all of it, to become the lock. They got halfway through the surrender and ran. The rite broke. Most of it became the debt—the thing that collects. But a splinter of it, the part meant to stand beside the keeper and hold their hand while they gave themselves away—that splinter had to go somewhere. It waited. And when the last Ravensworth was seven years old and grieving, it took a shape."

Ivo's coffee sat untouched. Their eyes had the flat shine they get on air when a caller says something that would break an untrained person in half. "You were assigned to me."

"I was bound to your line. Assigned to you, yes."

"To watch me."

"To keep you unseen. To keep you alive until—" I stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and we both knew what was at the bottom.

"Until the rite completes," Ivo said quietly. "Until I finish what the first Ravensworth couldn't."

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere down the street a dog started up and thought better of it.

"Yes," I said. "That was the assignment."

Ivo looked at me for a long time, and I let them, because I have spent a hundred and fifty years being looked at by exactly one person and I would rather be seen and condemned than unseen and forgiven. That is the whole of my theology, if an unfinished thing can be said to have one.

"And the games," Ivo said. "The songs. The night you stayed under my bed when the collectors came up the porch steps and you—you sang until I fell asleep. Was that the assignment?"

And here it was. The hinge. The moment I had rehearsed in a hundred borrowed mirrors, and every rehearsal had failed, because the truth does not care how well you've practiced.

"No," I said. "The assignment was to watch you and report. A warden watches. A warden counts. A warden tells the tall thing when the marked child is straying. That's what I was made for. That's the shape the broken rite poured me into." I set my hands flat on the table so they would not shake; borrowed hands shake so easily, they haven't learned steadiness yet. "But I chose to be your friend instead. I chose it the first night and I have chosen it every night since, and I am telling you now because you deserve to hear it from me and not from the ledger, not from the stones, not from that patient thing at the edge of the parking lot: I was never supposed to love you. I was supposed to herd you. And I did not. I stayed. Twenty years I was gone from your sight and I never once left. I was in the walls of the station. I was in the static between songs. I was the reason your dead callers sound warm instead of hungry—I have been standing between you and the larger piece of the broken rite since the night you were seven years old, and I will stand there until one of us is finished."

The kitchen was very quiet. The gray light had turned the color of old pewter.

"You lied to me," Ivo said. "For twenty years."

"I lied to myself for twenty years," I said. "To you I only ever said less than everything. There's a difference, and I know it's a thin one, and I know it's the kind of difference people are buried over. But Ivo—I need you to understand what I am. I am the unfinished portion of a ritual that has been waiting since 1849 for a Ravensworth to walk up that hill. I am bound to your blood until the rite completes. I cannot lie to you about what you are to me, because you are the only reason I have a shape at all. Your grief made me. Your grandmother's rules kept you alive long enough to make me. And your friendship—" my borrowed voice did something I did not authorize; it cracked, "—your friendship is the only thing I have ever been given that I didn't take."

Ivo sat with that. I watched them sit with it, the way they sit with the dead on the radio, giving every hard thing its full weight, never rushing a caller through their grief no matter how the board lights up. It is the finest thing about them and it is going to cost them everything, and I would undo it if I could, and I can't, and that is my punishment and my privilege both.

"The warden," Ivo said at last. "The thing you were supposed to be. Is it still—do you still report? To the tall thing? Does it know where I am, what I've found?"

And there it was, the question I had been dreading, the splinter under the splinter. Because the answer is yes. The answer has always been yes. The binding runs both directions; I am a piece of the broken rite, and the broken rite knows its own pieces, and every night when I stand at the edge of Ivo's awareness like a dog at a fence line, the tall quiet thing stands at the edge of mine, and we regard each other, and it knows.

"It knows I'm back," I said. "It has always known where you are. The covenant marked you, Ivo. Invisibility was never a cloak—it was a leash. And the thing at the end of it has been shortening the leash since the night Marla called."

"Then everything I do—"

"Is watched. Yes. But hear me." I leaned across the table, into the gray light, into the first honest moment of my long dishonest life. "It knows where you are. It does not know what you'll choose. That is the one thing the broken rite cannot see—the completion has to be freely given, and a free choice is the one territory it can't patrol. It can herd you to the stones. It can starve the town to hurry you. It can put Nadia's name at the top of its list. But it cannot make you surrender. Only you can do that. Which means the only weapon it has left is you."

Ivo's hand had gone to the counter, to the phone, the way it does—reaching for the thing they know how to hold, the board, the callers, the ministry of the dead. I saw the thought cross their face before they said it, and I loved them so much in that moment that it felt like tearing.

"The ledger," they said. "Page forty-one. You know what's on it."

"I have always known what's on it," I said. "I was there when it was written. I was the splinter that watched the ink dry." I stood, because some truths are easier standing, the way some prayers are easier kneeling. "And I need you to hear the rest before you read it, because the page will tell you what the rite costs, and it will not tell you what the rite is *for*. It won't tell you that the keeper who stands in the boundary doesn't die. It won't tell you that they persist—nameless, faceless, keeping the lights on forever. It won't tell you that I have watched one do it before, a long time ago, and that what's left afterward is not suffering, exactly. It's something worse. It's *continuing*."

Ivo looked up at me, and in their face was the thing I had been dreading more than the tall quiet thing, more than the debt, more than the dark moon coming: not fear. Recognition. The look of someone who has spent their whole life on the graveyard shift, keeping company with the dead, and has just been told the job never ends.

"Pell," they said. "When you said you'd watched one do it before. Watched from where?"

I had hoped they wouldn't ask. I had hoped, the way unfinished things hope, which is to say without any mechanism for it to come true.

"From the stones," I said. "I stood at the circle's edge in 1849, Ivo. I was the twelfth shadow in the photograph, the blur no camera could hold. I have been at every rite this town has ever almost finished. And the keeper who stepped into the boundary that night—the first one, the only one, before your ancestor ran—" I heard my borrowed voice go thin as static. "I knew them. I held their hand while they gave their name away. And I have spent a hundred and fifty years listening to what's left of them humming under the ground, and I am telling you now, as your friend and not your warden, as the only confession I have left to make:

"I will not let it be you. Whatever it costs, whatever I have to spend of the little I am—I will find another way, or I will stand in your place at the circle, and this time, when the surrender is spoken, it will be *complete*."

Outside the kitchen window, at the far edge of the yard where the light hadn't reached yet, something tall and quiet stood in the posture of a person waiting for a bus that never comes. It had been there since before dawn. It had heard every word.

And it smiled.
