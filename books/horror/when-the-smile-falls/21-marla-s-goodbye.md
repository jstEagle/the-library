## 21. Marla's Goodbye

The call comes at 2:14 a.m., which is, by now, a kind of appointment.

I have learned the shape of the dead the way a nurse learns the shape of a fever. They come in waves. They come in the small hours, when the town's collective breathing thins and the boundary between the living line and the dead one wears translucent as wax paper. They come when the coffee has gone cold in the pot and Nadia's headlights have swept the parking lot and gone, and it is only me and the board and the red ON AIR lamp and the nine lines that no longer frighten me so much as they *instruct* me.

Marla's number is the same one she used the first time. Of course it is. The dead keep their habits longer than the living keep their memories.

I look at it for a long moment. The light blinks its patient red blink. Down the hall, the vending machine hums its one dead note. Outside, October presses its face against the studio glass, and somewhere past the edge of the parking lot—farther than last week, closer than I will admit in the light of day—something tall and quiet is waiting with the posture of a man at a bus stop, a bus that died before either of us was born.

I have not taken Marla's call in eleven days. I have logged it, twice, as a prank. I have said her name into the log book in my careful dispatcher's hand and then closed the book on it like a coffin lid. I have told myself that a woman who attended her own funeral on a Tuesday morning in a gray dress her daughter picked out because it was the only one in the closet without moth holes would understand. The dead understand. That is the terrible economy of them. They have nothing left to spend but patience.

The light blinks.

I think of Denny Okafor, begging me to warn his wife before the tall quiet thing came for him, and how I hung up, and how he came anyway, the next night, on a different line, quieter, already knowing. I think of Ruth Okafor's porch light burning every night since, burning like a question no one will answer. I think of the ledger in the historical society basement, page forty ending mid-sentence, and the nine days the archivist's calendar says I have, and the way my grandmother's voice used to say *count the doors, Ivo, count them and don't stop*—as if counting were a rope, and as long as you held on, you could not be pulled under.

I have been holding the rope for thirty years. It has not saved anyone. It has only kept me from being useful.

The light blinks.

I key the line.

* * *

"KRVL, you're on the air." My voice comes out level. I am almost proud of it. Thirty years of practice at sounding like a man who is not afraid, and it finally, finally buys something.

"Ivo." Marla's voice is warm the way a bath is warm. Warm the way a thing is warm when the warmth has nowhere left to go and just sits in it, soaking. "Ivo Ravensworth. You took my call."

"I took your call," I say. "I hung up on you, too. Twice."

"I know." A small sound that might be a laugh. It has the texture of wind through a screen door. "I was so mad. I raised you at the church bake sale, you know. You ate three of my lemon bars when you were nine and threw up in the fellowship hall and your grandmother made you apologize to me with your hands folded."

"You told me that," I say. "The first time you called."

"Did I?" She sounds pleased, genuinely pleased, and the pleasure in her voice is the worst part, because it is not performance. Whatever Marla is now, whatever the town's covenant has left of her, she is not lying to me. The dead don't lie. They only wait. "Well. I tell it twice, then. It was a good story. You were a good boy. You threw up with real commitment."

The board is quiet. Not one other line is lit. In eleven days, the switchboard has not once been quiet, and now it is quiet, and I understand that it is quiet the way a room goes quiet when the family has gathered. They are all listening. The dead of Hollow Creek, two hundred and six of them and counting, are all leaning in.

"I've been afraid of you," I say. It comes out of me like a splinter coming out, slow and graining. "I've been afraid of every call. I've been pretending you're pranksters. I've been logging you like you're teenagers with a phone book and a grudge. And I'm sorry, Marla. I'm sorry I made you beg."

The silence on her end is not offended. It is the silence of someone setting down a heavy bag.

"You were taught to be afraid," she says. "Your grandmother taught you well. It kept you alive, most of that fear. Some of it kept you *small*, but we can't pick our inheritances, can we. I got my mother's hands and her temper. You got a set of rules and a thing on your porch that loves you." A pause. "Is Pell still there, Ivo?"

"Pell's around."

"Good. That one waited a long time. Longer than you'd believe. Longer than I'd believe, and I've got nothing but time now." Her voice shifts, and I hear it coming the way you hear weather coming, the pressure dropping, the birds going quiet. "Ivo. I didn't call for me."

"I know," I say. "You called for Danny."

* * *

Marla's daughter's name is Daniela, but nobody has called her that since she was six. She is Danny, twenty-six years old, works the early shift at the pharmacy on Corben Street, drives a car with a bumper sticker from a college she never finished, and has not slept a whole night through since the Tuesday they put her mother in the ground.

"I call her every night," Marla says. "I've been calling her every night since the funeral. She doesn't pick up. Of course she doesn't pick up. The number comes up as mine and she can't— she told her aunt she can't listen to the voicemails. She saved them, though. All eleven of them. She saved them and she can't play them and she can't delete them, and she lies awake holding the phone like it's a coal."

"Marla—"

"I know what I am, Ivo. Don't you dare be gentle with me. I sat through my own funeral, I watched that Reverend Pruett say I was a woman of quiet faith, which is a lie, I was a woman of loud opinions and everyone in that church knew it and I could hear them thinking it, all of them, every pew." The laugh again, wind through the screen door. "I know what's calling my daughter at midnight. I know what I sound like. I know that what I am now is a wound that hasn't decided whether it's a ghost or a grief. But I have one thing left to say to her and I cannot say it to her, because every time she hears my voice it just opens the wound wider, it just makes the coal hotter. So I need a *man in the middle*, Ivo. I need the radio man. I need the one person in this town the dead have been talking to for a month, the one with the microphone and the mercy, to do for me what I can't do for myself."

The ON AIR lamp burns red. I look at it the way I used to look at my grandmother's kitchen door—through which you did not pass without wiping your feet, without being invited, without knowing what you were walking into.

"What do you need me to say?"

* * *

She talks for a long time.

I let her. That is the whole of the craft, it turns out, the thing they never put in the broadcasting manual: you let them. You open the channel and you let the dead talk, and what they say, when you finally stop flinching, is never what the horror movies promised. It is not hunger. It is not malice. It is the inventory of a life, taken by the only person qualified to take it, spoken into the dark to whoever will hold still long enough to receive it.

Marla tells me about the lemon bars. She tells me about Danny's father, who left when Danny was four, and how Marla spent nine years being angry about it and then one day in the Kroger parking lot she realized she'd spent so long being angry she'd forgotten to be relieved, and she laughed right there by the cart return and a woman asked if she was alright and she said *ma'am, I have never been better*. She tells me about the year Danny was fourteen and they didn't have money for Christmas and Marla wrapped every single thing in the pantry separately—cans of soup, a jar of pickles, a bag of flour with a ribbon on it—and Danny opened them all morning and by noon they were both laughing so hard they couldn't breathe, and it was, Marla says, the best Christmas of her life, and she has been dead for eleven days and she is still not over it.

She tells me about the night she died. Not the manner of it—I know the manner of it, everyone in Hollow Creek knows the manner of it, the aneurysm, the kitchen floor, the phone in her hand halfway through dialing Danny's number. She tells me about the *feeling* of it. She tells me that in the last minute there was no pain, that there was only the kitchen light, which she had been meaning to replace for two years, the one that buzzed, and how she thought, with her last coherent thought, *I should have fixed that light, Danny shouldn't have to fix that light*, and then she was on the other side of something, and the buzzing stopped, and everything was very quiet and very warm.

"I'm not in pain, Ivo. I need Danny to know that. Whatever she imagines—whatever she sees when she closes her eyes, me on that floor, me alone—I was not alone and I was not afraid. The light was on. That's what I keep trying to tell her and every time I call, all she hears is *dead, dead, dead*. So you have to say it in a living voice. You have to say it so it goes in past the fear."

"Marla," I say, and my voice cracks on her name, and I let it. "I'll say it. But I want you to hear something first."

"Go on."

"I attended your funeral. You know that."

"I saw you in the back row. You left before the benediction."

"I left before the benediction," I agree, "because I was afraid the dead would notice me, and I have spent my whole life making sure nothing notices me, and I am so *tired*, Marla. I am thirty-seven years old and I have been holding my breath since I was seven years old and my parents went in the flood, and I have counted every door and dodged every mirror and kept every light burning, and it has kept me alive and it has kept me *alone*, and last week a dead woman called my radio station and asked me to tell her daughter goodbye, and I logged her as a prank. I want you to know that the man who did that is sorry. And I want you to know that the man sitting here now is going to do what you asked. Not because I'm brave. Because you waited eleven days for me to stop being a coward, and you shouldn't have had to wait one more minute."

The line is quiet. When she speaks again, her voice has changed. It is not smaller. It is *settled*, the way a house settles at night, the way a body settles into rest.

"There he is," Marla says. "There's the boy who ate my lemon bars. Okay. I'm going to hang up now, Ivo. You won't hear from me again after tonight. This is the last call."

"Marla—"

"Because it's not a goodbye, sweetheart. That's the thing nobody tells you. It's not a goodbye. It's a *handoff*. I've been carrying her love for twenty-six years and I can't carry it where I'm going, and you—" and I swear on my grandmother's grave, on the ledger, on the stones, I swear the warmth in her voice at that moment was the warmest thing in Hollow Creek, warmer than the studio lights, warmer than Nadia's coffee at 3 a.m., warmer than anything this cold covenant of a town has let me feel since I was seven— "you're going to carry it for her. That's all the show is, Ivo. That's what it's *for*. Somebody finally gave you the job you were born for. Now do it."

The line clicks. The light goes dark.

And the switchboard—every line, all nine—stays dark. Silent. Not empty. I know the difference now. Empty is a held breath. This is an exhale.

* * *

I put on the record I always put on at 2:40, the one with the long instrumental intro, because I need ninety seconds and I need them to sound like a format decision and not like a man trying not to shake apart.

I think about what to say. I think about my grandmother, who taught me the rules and never once told me they were a covenant, who counted doors on her deathbed with her lips moving when her voice was gone, who died on a dark moon in 1994 and whose name, I now understand, is somewhere on a list the sheriff keeps in a drawer. I think about whether she is listening now. I decide that she is. I decide that she has been listening the whole time, from the first night, from the night Marla first called and I logged her as a prank, and that my grandmother has been waiting eleven days too, waiting for her grandson to stop flinching, and that if there is any mercy in the architecture of the dead, she is proud.

The instrumental ends.

I key the microphone.

"This is Ivo Ravensworth, KRVL, 2:41 in the morning. I have a message. It's from Marla Voss, of Corben Street, who some of you knew—bake sales, church, the loudest opinions in three pews. And it's for her daughter, Danny, who works the early shift at the pharmacy, and who I hope is awake right now, because I think she is. I think she's been awake every night at this hour. Danny, if you're listening—and you're listening—your mother asked me to tell you some things, and I'm going to tell you all of them, and then I'm going to keep this frequency open as long as it takes."

I take a breath. The red light burns. Somewhere out past the parking lot, the tall quiet thing has gone very still, and I find I don't care, and that not-caring is the freest I have felt in thirty years.

"She said: the light in the kitchen was buzzing and she was sorry she never fixed it, and she's sorry you'll have to fix it, and you should fix it this week, because she knows you, and you'll put it off for a year, and she cannot stand that from where she is." My voice holds. It holds because it is not my voice anymore, not entirely; it is a channel, and I have finally stopped fighting it. "She said the Christmas with the pantry presents was the best day of her life, and she wants you to know that you laughing at a bag of flour with a ribbon on it is the sound she took with her. She said she was not afraid. Danny, she said—I'm quoting her exactly—*I was not alone and I was not afraid, and the last thing I thought was about you, and the thought was not a goodbye. The thought was: she knows how much I love her. She knows.*"

The studio is very quiet. The town is very quiet. I have the sense—unprovable, unshakeable—of a whole town lying in the dark with radios on, the way they did in the flood year, when the stations were the only thing still transmitting and everyone in Hollow Creek learned to sleep with a voice in the room.

"And she said one more thing. She said: *Danny, the voicemails. You can let them go now. You don't have to keep the coal. It was always warm and it was never a wound. Let it be what it was.*"

I let the silence sit. Ten seconds. Twenty. A good broadcaster fears dead air. I am no longer a broadcaster. I am something older than the job description, something the founders started and never finished, and dead air, I understand now, is just a room where the dead are being gentle.

"Marla Voss," I say. "Corben Street. Lemon bars. Loud opinions. Not alone. Not afraid. Goodnight, Marla. Goodbye, Danny. This frequency is open. If anyone out there needs to be heard—living or otherwise—the light is on, and I'm not going anywhere."

* * *

The switchboard does not light up.

I wait for it. I sit with my hand near the board through the top of the hour, through the weather, through the record I play because Marla asked me to play it—something with a piano in it that was playing in the fellowship hall the day she caught nine-year-old me with three lemon bars and, instead of scolding me, sat down and told me the secret to the crust. The lines stay dark. The silence coming off them has a texture I can only call *satisfied*, the way a field looks after the harvest, the way a child breathes after crying itself out.

At 3:12, Nadia's headlights sweep the lot. She comes in with two coffees and the tired, crooked smile that has been the one warm constant of my nights for two years, and she sets the cups down and looks at me for a long moment.

"You did it," she says. Not a question. She's been listening in the truck, of course she's been listening; she's been listening every night, waiting for me to stop flinching too.

"I did it."

"How do you feel?"

I consider the question honestly, which is more than I have ever given it. I feel lighter, and I feel lighter the way a fuse is lighter—burned down, closer to the thing it's aimed at. Nine days. Page forty-one. The stones. The name I am supposed to surrender. But underneath all of that, underneath the countdown, there is something new and steady, and it is this: I know what the show is for now. I know what *I* am for, for nine more days. The dead need somewhere to call. I finally picked up.

"Like a man with a job," I say.

Nadia laughs, and the sound fills the studio the way the warmth filled Marla's voice, and for one moment the horror recedes to the edges of the room where it belongs.

Then the board lights up.

One line. A number I don't know. And when I answer it, the voice on the other end is a man's, young, scared, and it says words that drop the temperature of the room ten degrees:

"Is this the radio man? My name's Denny Okafor. I think— I think I crashed on Route 9. I think I'm dead. But my phone keeps dialing my wife, and she won't pick up, and there's something standing on the road behind my car, and it's *tall*, and it's not moving, and I don't know how much time I have—please. Please, radio man. Tell me what I'm supposed to do."
