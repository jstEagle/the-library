## 25. Sheriff Dunmore

The summons came on official letterhead, which was almost funny. After weeks of dead voices and a widow with a shotgun grudge, it was the mundane machinery of the county that finally caught up with me: *You are requested to appear at the Hollow Creek Sheriff's Department, Tuesday, October 14th, at 10:00 a.m., regarding an ongoing investigation.* Requested. Not summoned. The word choice felt deliberate, like someone had thought hard about how to ask a haunted man to come in for questioning without spooking him.

I almost didn't go.

I stood in my kitchen that morning with the letter in one hand and a mug of coffee going cold in the other, and I weighed it the way I weighed everything now—against the nine days, against the dark moon, against Nadia's face when she'd told me about her grandmother and the Reyes covenant. There wasn't time for sheriffs. There wasn't time for anything except page forty-one and the stones and the surrender I'd already begun rehearsing in the privacy of my skull, the way you rehearse a speech you hope you'll never give.

But Ruth Okafor had filed her report, and reports have gravity. And there was another thing, smaller and stranger, that tipped me: the letter smelled faintly of pipe tobacco. Old-fashioned, cherry-sweet, the kind nobody smoked anymore. My grandmother had kept a tin of it in the hall closet for a neighbor who died before I was born. I don't know what I expected the smell to mean. Maybe nothing. Maybe everything. But I put on my least suspicious sweater—the gray one, the one that made me look like what I was supposed to be, a tired radio host and nothing else—and I drove downtown.

* * *

The Hollow Creek Sheriff's Department occupied a converted feed store on Larch Street, all exposed brick and fluorescent hum, with a plaque by the door commemorating something about the grain industry that no one read. Inside it smelled like burnt coffee and paper and, underneath it, faintly, like the historical society basement—that same dry, mineral patience of old things stored badly.

Deputy Alvarez took my statement first, or tried to. She was young, thorough, kind in the way people are kind when they think they're handling a fragile situation. I gave her the version of events that would keep me out of a psych hold: yes, I hosted the overnight show; yes, Mrs. Okafor had called the station upset; no, I had never told anyone their loved ones were dead; no, I couldn't explain the calls; no, sir, I didn't think it was funny.

She wrote it all down. I watched her pen move and thought about how many times in my life I'd watched someone write down my name—*Ivo Ravensworth*—and how for thirty years the name had slid off people like water off wax paper, unremarked, unremembered, exactly as designed. Now every syllable of it felt like a lit match I was carrying through a dry field.

Then Deputy Alvarez closed her notebook, glanced at the clock, and said, "Sheriff will see you now," in a tone that suggested the sheriff had been waiting since before I arrived.

His office was at the back, past the evidence room, past a corkboard of missing-cat flyers that had yellowed into archaeology. Sheriff Dunmore was older than I expected—not old, but weathered, the way fence posts get weathered, gray going silver at the temples, hands like knotted rope folded on his desk. A nameplate said *SHERIFF E. DUNMORE*. Behind him, instead of the diploma or the flag or the framed photo of a squad car I'd have bet money on, hung a single item: a large county map of Hollow Creek, annotated in pencil so densely it looked like frost.

He didn't ask me to sit. He looked at me for a long moment—I felt it the way you feel a flashlight beam in the dark, a warmth with weight behind it—and then he said:

"Close the door."

I did.

"You want to know why you're here," he said. "Ruth Okafor filed a complaint. That's the official reason. Unofficially—" he tapped the letter on his desk, my letter, the one with the tobacco smell "—unofficially, I've been waiting three years for you to walk through that door. Or someone like you." He leaned back. "Your grandmother knew me, you know. Odile. She used to bring pies to the department around the dark moons. Never explained why. We never asked. In this town, you learn not to ask about certain kindnesses."

My mouth went dry. "Sheriff, if this is about the complaints—"

"It's not about the complaints." He opened a drawer. Not the top drawer, where a man keeps his service weapon. The bottom one, deep, the kind that sticks. He lifted out a ledger.

Not *the* ledger. Not the founders' book with its sentence dying mid-page. This one was newer, spiral-bound, a school-supply-store thing with a cracked cover, but it had the same heft in the room. The same gravity. He set it down between us like a man laying cards face-up.

"I keep a list," he said.

* * *

Two hundred and six names.

He turned the pages slowly, letting me see them. Columns. Dates. Causes of death in his neat, blocky hand—heart failure, car accident, drowned, fell, *found in bed, no cause determined*, over and over, a litany of ordinary endings. But it was the margins that stopped my breath. Next to each date, in smaller script, he'd written a phase of the moon. And the moons were wrong—or rather, they were right in the way nothing natural is right. Every death since 1954. Every single one. Dark moon.

"I started this in '89, when I made deputy," Dunmore said. "My father started it before me, after your—" he paused, choosing the word carefully "—after the Ravensworth donation to the historical society in '54. He was a deputy then too. He noticed the pattern in the coroner's files and couldn't let it go. Said a man who sees a pattern has two choices: write it down or go mad pretending he didn't." He turned another page. "I inherited the list the way you inherit a bad roof. You don't want it. You maintain it anyway."

"Sheriff," I said, and my voice came out strange, flattened, "why are you showing me this?"

"Because three years ago my brother called the station."

The room went very quiet. The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere down the hall a phone rang twice and gave up.

"Walt," he said. "Walter Dunmore. Heart attack on the fourteenth of March, dark moon, age fifty-one. They found him in his truck on Corben Road, engine still running, radio still on." His jaw worked. "An hour after they pronounced him, my sister-in-law swears the phone rang and it was him. Said he was cold and the road was long and could somebody leave the porch light on. She hung up on him. Thought it was a cruel joke." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and steady all at once, the eyes of a man who had been carrying something heavy for a long time and had learned to carry it without spilling. "Then she heard your show. Two weeks later. You read a name on air—somebody's grandfather—and you told him the road home was lit. And she called me crying, saying *Emmett, that's what Walt asked for, that's exactly what he asked for,* and I drove to the station that night and sat in the parking lot until dawn trying to make myself come inside."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I wasn't ready to know." He said it plainly, without shame. "A man can live next to a haunted house his whole life as long as he doesn't knock. I knocked last week, though. After Ruth Okafor came in with her story about Denny calling from the road." He exhaled. "I listened to your broadcast. The night you read the names. All two hundred and six of them."

I hadn't known he'd been listening. I remembered that night—the studio glass, the tall quiet thing standing outside it with its terrible bus-stop patience, the town listening, the dead listening, the names going out over the AM band like lanterns passed hand to hand. I remembered thinking: *someone should witness this besides me.*

"You counted along," I said.

"I checked your names against mine." He tapped the ledger. "Every one matched. Two hundred and six. You didn't miss any. You didn't add any." He leaned forward. "Now, I've had three years to imagine what I'd say to whoever was behind that voice, and none of my scenarios involved a Ravensworth reading names off a list my family's been keeping in secret since Eisenhower. So you're going to tell me what's happening in my town, Mr. Ravensworth, and you're going to tell me straight, because I am too old and too tired and too far past disbelief to be managed."

And God help me, I believed him. That was the miracle of it. Here was a man with a badge and a pencil and sixty years of his family's quiet terror pressed flat into a spiral notebook, and he wasn't laughing, wasn't reaching for paperwork, wasn't looking at me the way Dr. Voss at the clinic had looked at me when I'd once, stupidly, at nineteen, mentioned the rules. He was looking at me the way you look at the other survivor of a shipwreck.

So I told him.

* * *

I told him about Marla, and the goodbye her daughter needed. About the nine lines burning and my full name recited in chorus. About Pell on the porch with the mismatched buttons. About the founders' photograph and the blurred twelfth figure, and the ledger that stops mid-sentence, and page forty-one, and the key, and the photograph in my grandmother's box with my own face standing among eleven settlers at the stones. I told him about the tall quiet thing at the edge of the parking lot, patient as winter. I told him about the Reyes covenant, and Nadia, and the nine days, and what the rite would cost, though I kept that part brief because my voice kept snagging on it.

Dunmore listened the way few people listen—with his whole body, elbows on the desk, pencil motionless. When I finished, he was silent for a long moment. Then he got up, went to the map behind him, and turned on the desk lamp so the pencil marks showed.

"Dark moons," he said. "Every death, marked here." The map was stippled with tiny crosses, denser near the center of town, thinning toward the edges—but not toward the north. Toward the north the crosses clustered thick as flies, out past the tree line, past the old quarry road, in a blank space where the map showed nothing but contour lines.

"The stones," I said.

"The stones." He nodded. "My father measured it once. Every death within six miles of that circle, going back to '54. Outside six miles, the pattern breaks. Random. Natural. Inside it—" he drew a slow circle in the air "—nothing dies on a dark moon by accident. Nothing dies on a dark moon at all except what it takes." He turned back to me. "And here's the part that's kept me up for twenty years, Mr. Ravensworth. Before 1954? The coroner's records for this county go back to 1911. You know how many dark-moon deaths there were before '54?"

I already knew. I felt the answer arrive in my chest like cold water finding a crack.

"One," I said. "In 1893. The year the ritual was interrupted."

Dunmore stared at me. "How do you—"

"Because the debt only started accruing when the payment stopped." I heard my own voice go distant, clinical, the voice I used on air when the material got too heavy to feel. "One death a year, sheriff. For a hundred and thirty years. Except it isn't one a year anymore, is it? Your list is accelerating. Look at your own columns. Fourteen deaths last dark moon cycle. It's compounding. Interest on an unpaid balance."

"That's what my father wrote in the margin in 1961." Dunmore's voice was rough. "*Compounding.* He underlined it three times and then he never spoke of the list again except to hand it to me." He sat back down heavily. "So the founders made a bargain. Eleven of them. And a twelfth ran out halfway."

"And the town's been paying ever since," I said. "Quietly. One life at a time. On the nights nobody's watching."

"And you're going to finish it." It wasn't a question. He'd read it off me the way you read weather off a sky. "At the stones. Before the dark moon. Nine days."

"Eight now."

He was quiet again. Then he did something I didn't expect. He opened the ledger to the last written page, tore it free along the spiral—a clean, practiced tear, like he'd done it before, maybe in rehearsal, maybe in dreams—and slid it across the desk. On it, in fresh ink, was a single line:

*October 22nd — Ravensworth completes. All accounts settled.*

"What's this?"

"A record," he said. "The first entry in whatever comes after. My family kept the list of the dying. Seems right that a Dunmore keeps the record of the settling." He met my eyes. "I'm not going to pretend I understand what you're walking into. I'm not going to pretend I can help you fight it, because I've seen the thing myself—once, in '94, standing at the end of my driveway at 3 a.m. like it was waiting on a ride. I sat in my truck with my pistol in my lap until sunrise and it never moved and I never slept right again. I can't fight that. Nobody with a badge can fight that." He tapped the torn page. "But I can do this. I can make sure that when you walk out to those stones, there's a man in this town who knows your name, knows what you paid, and writes it down so it stays written. Names are the whole war, aren't they? Then let there be one more keeper of names."

Something in my throat closed. I looked down at the page, at my own surname in a stranger's careful block letters, and I thought about my grandmother bringing pies to this department on the dark moons, and I understood, suddenly and completely, what the pies had been. Not thanks. Maintenance. She'd been feeding the ones who watched the watchers, the way you oil a hinge you might need to swing open someday.

"She knew," I said. "Grandmother knew about your family."

"Odile Ravensworth knew about everything in this town," Dunmore said. "Including, I'd wager, that someday her grandchild would sit in that chair." He almost smiled; it was a tired, cracked thing, but real. "She told me once, when I was a rookie and cocky enough to ask: *'When the last of us comes to you, Emmett, you believe him. Whatever he says. Promise me.'* I promised. Took me thirty years to understand the promise." He closed the ledger. "Consider it kept."

* * *

We talked logistics for another hour, and it steadied me more than I can say, the plainness of it—road conditions on the quarry road, the gate chain that rusted shut every fall (he had bolt cutters), the fact that cell coverage died half a mile out so we'd need radios, the fact that he would be parked at the tree line on the night of the dark moon with his lights off and his engine running, not to intervene, he said, because he understood he couldn't, but to witness. To be the second name-keeper at the edge of the circle. Someone had to hold the record while I became it.

"Bring the girl," he said, as I stood to leave. "Reyes. If her line were the keepers, then the stones may answer her easier than you. Don't go alone if you can help it."

"I won't."

"And Ravensworth." I paused at the door. He was looking at me with that wrecked, steady gaze, and for a moment he looked less like a sheriff than like a man at a graveside, deciding whether to say the true thing over the casket. "My brother. Walt. When you read his name on air—it helped. My sister-in-law sleeps now. Whatever you become out there, whatever it costs you—" his voice caught, just once, and he mastered it "—this town owes you a debt it will never know it owes. That's the cruelest part of your bargain, I think. Saving everyone and being saved by no one."

"Yes," I said. "That's about the shape of it."

I walked out through the bullpen, past the yellowed missing-cat flyers, past Deputy Alvarez, who looked up and, I swear, gave me a small nod—wordless, knowing, the nod of a department that had apparently decided, in my absence, to believe impossible things. The morning outside was bright and cold and ordinary, leaves skittering down Larch Street, a dog barking two blocks over, the world performing its innocence flawlessly.

I got halfway to the car before I saw it.

Across the street, in the doorway of the shuttered hardware store, stood a figure in a long coat, posture arranged into that awful, patient waitingness. It didn't approach. It didn't need to. It simply stood in the daylight—which it had never done before, not once, not in all these weeks of shadows and parking lots and the edge of headlights—and it watched me with the place where a face should be.

And as I stood frozen with my keys biting into my palm, every radio in every parked car on Larch Street clicked on at once, all of them tuned to KRVL, all of them playing static that resolved, slowly, horribly, into a familiar warm voice.

*"Eight days,"* said my mother, from everywhere and nowhere. *"And now the lawman knows. How quickly your little invisible life is coming apart, Ivo. Come see what's left of it."*

The radios clicked off. The street was silent. The doorway across from me was empty.

Eight days. And the thing had just shown me it was done waiting in the dark.
