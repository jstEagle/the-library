## 38. The Visitor

The dark moon comes in October now, the way it came a year ago, and Nadia has learned to read its approach the way sailors read weather—not by looking up but by feeling it in her teeth, in the low hum that lives in the ground of Hollow Creek and only rises when the sky goes starless. On those nights the town's dogs won't bark. The streetlights burn a shade too steady. And somewhere past the tree line, at the standing stones, something that used to be Ivo Ravensworth waits for her.

She packs the truck the way she always does: thermos of coffee, black, because Ivo never could stand sugar in it—*tastes like lying*, they used to say—and the portable radio, the little silver one with the antenna she replaced twice, and the blanket her grandmother wove before Nadia was born, wool dyed the color of river stones. She checks the doors twice. She keeps every light burning as she leaves the house. Some habits are inheritances too.

The road out of town is four miles of gravel and then nothing, just the old logging cut where the county gave up maintaining anything nobody used. Her headlights make a tunnel of the dark, and beyond the tunnel the trees stand close and patient, and once—only once, a year ago, on her first visit—she saw something at the edge of the beams wearing the posture of someone waiting for a bus that never comes. It did not approach. It inclined its head, almost politely, the way a doorman acknowledges a guest whose invitation he resents. She hasn't seen it since. She thinks it is still there. She thinks it will always be there, at the margin of things, diminished now to a rumor of itself, because what Ivo did at the stones took most of its appetite away. But appetite is not the same as absence, and Nadia Reyes does not fool herself about what her town was founded on.

The stones rise out of the clearing like teeth.

Eleven of them, granite-gray, taller than a man, arranged in the circle the founders raised in 1789—or so the historical society's plaque says, though Nadia knows better now; the Reyes family ledger says earlier, much earlier, and says it in a hand that shook. The twelfth stone is not stone. It stands among them at exactly their height, wearing exactly their weathering, and if you look at it directly your eyes slide off like rain off waxed canvas. Nadia has practiced looking at it. She can hold it for maybe three seconds now before her vision refuses, and in those three seconds she sees what she sees: a figure, upright, still, with the suggestion of shoulders and the suggestion of a face and nothing else the eye will agree to carry home.

"I'm here," she says, because you should announce yourself. Grandmother's rule. *Never arrive unannounced where the boundary is thin.* "It's me. It's Nadia."

The air changes.

She has no better word for it, though she has had a year to find one. The air changes the way a room changes when someone you love walks in behind you—the pressure of attention, warm and total, settling over her shoulders like the blanket she hasn't unfolded yet. The hum in the ground deepens by a half-tone, a chord resolving. And from the twelfth stone comes a sound like wind through a bottle, like breath drawn through stone, and then—

"Nadia."

Ivo's voice. Not quite Ivo's voice—it has a resonance underneath it now, a second tone like a bell rung inside a bell—but the shape of it, the dry warmth of it, the way they said her name like it was a word in a language only two people spoke. A year, and she still has to press her hand flat against her sternum for the first ten seconds, every time.

"Hey," she says. Casual. Deliberately casual, because Ivo would want it casual, because grief performed loudly is just another way of making the dying person comfort *you*. "Traffic was hell. A deer gave me a look on Route 9 like I owed it money."

"You don't owe anyone money. You paid everything." A pause, and then, drier: "That was a joke. I can still joke. Mostly."

She laughs, and the laugh cracks in the middle, and she lets it, because this is the one hour of the year when cracking is allowed.

* * *

She sets up the ritual of the hour with the care of a liturgist. Blanket spread between the eleventh stone and the twelfth, facing the center of the circle. Thermos uncapped, steam rising straight up in the windless dark. Radio set on the wool, dial turned to KRVL, 104.7, volume low. The station plays through the night now—she made sure of that, she'll get to that—and right now some automated voice is reading the overnight listings, church suppers and flu shots, the small mortal music of a small mortal town.

"One hour," she says. "Starting now."

"Starting now," Ivo agrees. "You first. Tell me everything. Start with the diner."

"The diner's fine. Marisol repainted. It's green now. Aggressively green. Like the walls are trying to sell you something."

"What?"

"Mint. Some color called 'healing jade.'"

A sound from the stone that might be a laugh or might be wind; with Ivo it has become the same thing. "Your grandmother would have hated it."

"My grandmother hated every color except gray. That's not a personality, that's a symptom." Nadia pours coffee into the cup she brought—a second cup, always a second cup, set on the blanket where steam can rise toward the stone. Ivo cannot drink. They both know it. The cup is not for drinking. The cup is for the smell, for the offering, for the ordinary geometry of two people and two cups. "Ruth Okafor came in yesterday. She looked—better. She's dating the man who manages the hardware store. Denny would have hated him. He's very tidy."

"Denny hated tidy people. He ironed his t-shirts and resented himself for it." The voice softens. "How is Ruth, really?"

"Really? She sleeps through the night now. She told me that at the counter, out of nowhere, like she was reporting the weather. She said—" Nadia stops. Swallows. "She said she doesn't hear him calling anymore. From the road. She said whatever you did, it let him finish arriving. Those were her words. *Finish arriving.*"

The silence from the stone is long. When Ivo speaks again, the second tone in their voice is louder, the way it gets when they are moved and trying not to be. "Good. That's—that's the whole point, Nadia. That's the whole entire point. Two hundred and six names, and every one of them gets to finish arriving now."

"And you?" she asks, because she asks every year, because she promised herself she'd never stop asking even if the answer never changes. "Do you sleep?"

"I don't sleep. But I rest. There's a difference, and it turns out the difference matters." A pause. "I count the doors."

"There are no doors out there."

"There are eleven stones. I count them. Eleven doors, all open, all letting the right things through and keeping the wrong things out. Your grandmother's rules were about doors, did you know that? All of them. Don't be seen—don't stand in a doorway. Count the doors—know your exits. Keep the lights burning—every threshold wants a witness. She wasn't teaching me fear. She was teaching me architecture." The voice takes on a wonder that a year in the stone has not worn down. "I understand it all now, from in here. The whole covenant is a house, Nadia. Hollow Creek is a house, and the founders built it badly, halfway, and left the door hanging open for a hundred and sixty years. I just—I closed it. I stood in the doorway and became the door."

"And what does the door get?" she asks quietly. "A year in. What does the door get?"

The wind moves through the clearing without moving the grass. When Ivo answers, the words come slow, chosen like stones for a wall.

"Witness. That's the trade, in the end. I gave up being seen, and I got to see. Everything. Every porch light coming on the night I walked here. Every car radio crackling to life with the dead saying *keep going, keep going*. I watched the whole town hold its breath for me, and I couldn't be seen holding mine back, and somehow that was—" The second tone flares and steadies. "It was the most loved I have ever been, and I couldn't wave back. So. That's what the door gets. Everything except a face."

* * *

They spend the middle of the hour on small things, because the small things are the load-bearing ones. Nadia tells Ivo about the dispatcher who replaced her on nights—a kid named Tomas who chews his pens and is terrified of the 3 a.m. quiet, and how she told him the quiet isn't empty, it's listening, and how he looked at her like she was crazy and then, three weeks later, started bringing *two* coffees to the studio, one for the empty chair. Ivo tells her about the seasons passing inside the boundary, which are not seasons exactly but moods of the light: how in winter the stones ring faintly when the wind is right, a chord the founders must have heard, and how in spring the lichen on the eleventh stone blooms chartreuse and Ivo spends weeks watching it the way other people watch television.

"I have become," Ivo says, "a person with strong opinions about lichen."

"You were always a person with strong opinions about everything."

"I was a person with strong opinions about *doors* and *coffee* and whether Marla's daughter should go to college in-state. Now my portfolio has expanded." A pause, and the humor thins, and Nadia knows what's coming because it comes every year, at the same place in the hour, like a song reaching its bridge. "Tell me about the photographs."

She knew this was coming. She brought it anyway. She always brings it.

From her jacket pocket she takes a photograph, 4x6, drugstore print. She holds it up toward the twelfth stone, angling it so the moonless sky gives what light it can—which is more than it should, because the stones seem to gather light the way wells gather echo. In the photo: the KRVL parking lot, golden hour, the whole crew crammed against the studio glass last summer, Tomas pulling a face, the station manager mid-laugh. And at the edge of the frame, half-cut by the border, a space where a person should be—a gap in the group, an absence shaped like shoulders, like the suggestion of a smile. Everyone in the photo is leaning slightly toward the gap. Nobody planned it. Bodies remember.

"That's you," Nadia says. "That's the spot where you'd have stood."

"I can see it." The voice is very quiet. "I can see myself not being there. It's strange. From in here, I thought being unseen would feel like erasure. It doesn't. It feels like being the negative space that proves everyone else is real. Look how they lean. They lean because the shape of me is still load-bearing."

"You're doing the thing where you make it beautiful so I won't cry."

"Is it working?"

"No."

"Then I'll stop." And the voice drops its cleverness entirely, and what's left is just Ivo, bare, the way they sounded at seven years old in the flashback stories they finally told her, the week before the stones. "Nadia. I miss my hands. I miss the board at 2 a.m., the way the ON AIR light made the whole room red like we were broadcasting from inside a heart. I miss bad diner coffee and your terrible driving and the way you say my name when you're annoyed, which is the best way, which is the truest version of it. I miss having a name at all. Do you know what it is to be addressed by nothing? To be *the door*? Even the tall quiet thing got to be a *thing*. I'm a function."

"I know," Nadia says. Her eyes are hot. "I know, Ivo."

"But here's the thing. Here's the thing I need you to carry back down the hill." The second tone rings clear now, and the eleven stones seem to lean in, and the radio on the blanket—she will swear to this until she dies—flickers, the needle jumping off the station and onto pure carrier wave, a held breath of static, as if the whole frequency spectrum paused to listen. "I chose it. Every day, from in here, I choose it again. Because Marla's daughter finished grieving. Because Ruth sleeps. Because two hundred and six voices went quiet in the good way, the finished way. Because you're sitting on a blanket in the cold talking to a rock and you're not afraid, and the town you grew up in is the safest town in America tonight, and nobody will ever know why, and *that's the job*. Somebody has to keep the lights on. I just found out the lights were me."

* * *

There are twenty minutes left when she opens the last part of the hour, the part they never skip, the part that costs the most.

"Your turn," she says. "Say everything. You always make me go first."

"Because you're braver going first."

"Liar. Say it."

And Ivo says it. Every year, the same liturgy, and every year it lands like the first time. How Nadia Reyes walked into the station five years ago with a thermos and a chip on her shoulder and covered for them the first night a dead woman called in, no questions asked, just slid into the chair beside them and worked the board like it was a duet. How her grandmother's name was in the founders' ledger next to Ivo's grandmother's name, keeper and marked, two lines braided together for two hundred years without either knowing it, and how Ivo believes—not hopes, *believes*—that the covenant meant for them to find each other, that some bargains write their own matchmaking. How the worst moment of the surrender was not the stones, not the unraveling, not the first founder's face wearing cowardice like a mask, but the last look backward at the parking lot, at Nadia standing in the headlights with her fists clenched, mouthing *come back*, and knowing they couldn't, and choosing anyway.

"I didn't say goodbye," Ivo says. "In the letters. You noticed."

"I noticed."

"Because goodbye is a closing word, and I refuse. This isn't closed. One hour a year, forever, as long as you keep coming—and you'll keep coming, you stubborn, wonderful woman, you'll come when you're eighty and your grandkids drive you—and as long as you come, the door stays a door and not a wall. So it's not goodbye. It's *see you next dark moon.*"

"See you next dark moon," Nadia says, and her voice breaks clean through the middle of it, and from the stone comes a sound she has only heard twice before, both times at the end of the hour: a long exhale, mineral and human at once, like the mountain itself learning to sigh.

Ten minutes left. She wipes her face with her sleeve. She reaches for the thermos to pour the last of the coffee into the offering cup, and that's when the radio does something new.

The static clears. Not to music. To a voice.

"—testing, testing, is this thing—okay, okay, hi. Hi, Nadia. It's me. It's Pell."

She freezes, cup halfway poured. Pell. The engineer. The unfinished piece of the ritual that grew a body and chose friendship over wardenship, the one who carried Ivo to the stones when no one else could, the one who stayed afterward, hired officially, impossibly, running KRVL's transmitter with hands that are only mostly real. Pell, who never calls the overnight line. Pell, who knows exactly where she is and what this hour is.

"Pell," she says carefully. "This hour is—"

"I know what this hour is. I wouldn't interrupt. Listen—listen, please, there's something wrong with the transmitter, something I've never seen, and I've been staring at it for six hours and I think—" The voice on the radio wavers, and Pell's voice never wavers; Pell was built from a contract, and contracts don't tremble. "I think the boundary is humming, Nadia. Not steady like it should be. In *patterns*. And the pattern spells something, and I've checked it nine times, and I need Ivo to hear it, because I think page forty-one had a sequel. I think somebody wrote a page forty-two."

On the blanket, the offering cup steams. Above it, the twelfth stone has gone utterly still—even its ambient presence, that faint pressure of attention, has pulled tight as a wire.

Nadia looks at the stone. The stone, somehow, looks back.

"Ivo," she says softly. "Did you hear that?"

And the voice from the stone, when it comes, has lost every trace of the bell-tone. It is just Ivo, young and human and afraid, saying:

"Put Pell on speaker."
