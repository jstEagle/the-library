## 40. When the Smile Falls

I do not sleep anymore, and I have stopped missing it.

Sleep was a small death, and I have paid my debts to death in full. What I do instead is harder to name—something like resting, something like listening, something like standing very still in a doorway for a hundred years while the house breathes around you. The boundary holds me the way a held breath holds a note. I am inside Hollow Creek and not inside it, the way a reflection is inside the glass: present, patient, pressed close to everything and touching nothing.

I count the doors.

I cannot help it. It was the first rule Grandmother ever taught me—*count the doors, little wren, count them twice*—and rules outlive the people who needed them, the way scars outlive the wound. So I count. There are four hundred and eleven doors in Hollow Creek proper, if you include the church annex and the old feed store that hasn't opened since before the flood year. There are six hundred and two if you walk the county roads, and I walk them all, every night, or whatever the nights are here, where night is less a time than a temperature.

Once, counting doors was how we hid. A door was a mouth; a lit window was an eye; and the thing that fed on witnessed lives moved down the streets reading us like a menu. My grandmother taught me to be a closed book. *Don't be seen. Don't say your whole name. Keep the lights burning so it thinks someone's home and moves along.* For thirty years I lived like that—lights on, curtains drawn, voice on the radio but face never once on camera, a man made of sound and careful habit.

Now the lights are mine to keep, and there is no one left to hide from.

* * *

The tall quiet thing is gone. I want to write that plainly, because for so long I could only write it slantwise, in the margins of logs and the spaces between words. It is gone. When I spoke the founders' interrupted sentence at the stones—when I gave my name freely, syllable by syllable, like handing over coins I'd counted all my life—the shape that had worn my mother's voice and waited at bus stops that never had buses came apart like smoke deciding it was tired. And underneath it, at the last, just before the unraveling, I saw its true face, and the true face was kin. The first Ravensworth. The original coward who fled halfway through his own covenant and left the rest of us holding the bill for a hundred and seventy years.

I understand him now, which is the strangest mercy of all this strange mercy. He wasn't wicked. He was afraid, and fear does what fear does—it stops halfway, it leaves pages blank, it lets the interest accrue. Two hundred and six dead since 1954, all on dark moons, all because one man could not finish a sentence. I finished it. I stood in the circle with Nadia's hand hovering an inch from mine, close enough to warm me without breaking the rite, and I said the words until there were no words left, and then I stepped into the boundary as the twelfth figure, and the stones took my name the way the ground takes rain.

I don't remember my full name anymore. That's the honest truth of it, and I've decided honesty is the one inheritance worth keeping. I remember that I *had* one. I remember the weight of it, the way you remember the weight of a coat you've outgrown. Sometimes, on the long walks, I try it on anyway—Ivo, just the first part, the part everyone knew, the part that fit loose enough to keep—and the boundary hums around me like a struck bell settling. It doesn't hurt. Nothing hurts here. That took some getting used to. Pain was such a faithful companion for so long that its absence felt like a trick, like the quiet before a caller says something impossible.

But the quiet, now, is only quiet.

* * *

The photographs were the hardest part, the first year.

I didn't know I'd been erased from them until Nadia brought the yearbook to the stones—one visitor a year, page forty-one's hidden mercy, the clause even Pell hadn't known was there—and showed me the class photo from Hollow Creek High, and there was a gap in the second row. Not a blur, like the twelfth founder in the old daguerreotypes. A gap. Clean edges where a person should have been, the boy beside it leaning slightly into empty air, as if he'd been talking to someone his whole life and simply couldn't say who.

"You're not blurred," Nadia said. "You're *absent*. There's a difference."

There is. The blur was a warning—a thing half-hidden, still owing. Absence is completion. The ledger is balanced. I checked, that first winter, walking the historical society's basement while the archivist slept upstairs over her tea, and every record that once held my name now holds a small, tidy blankness, like a tooth socket healed over. The donation records from 1954. The sheriff's list of two hundred and six. Even the KRVL logbooks, those thousands of hours of my voice on tape, play back now as dead air with a faint warmth under it, like a room someone just left.

Dunmore keeps the list anyway. He told Nadia he would. "Somebody should remember there was a somebody," he said, and wrote *R——* at the top of a fresh page, and underlined it twice. I heard about it secondhand and it undid me more than any horror ever did. To be remembered *as absent*—that is a strange gift. I carry it with me on the walks.

* * *

The town changed first in small ways, the way ice changes a pond.

Porch lights stayed on past midnight—not the frightened, superstitious burning my grandmother taught, every bulb a ward, every wattage a prayer, but an easy kind of light. People stopped drawing their curtains. Children said their whole names out loud on the schoolyard, loud and proud, and nothing came to collect them. Mrs. Adeyemi planted tomatoes by her front window where she'd kept it dark for forty years, and when the first ones ripened she gave them away to neighbors whose names she said aloud, warmly, in full, on her porch, at dusk.

The dead went quiet too. That was the promise kept. No more calls from the recently buried asking after daughters and widows. No more nine lines burning at once with voices reciting what should have been unspoken. The switchboard at KRVL sleeps now between two and four, and the silence is not empty. It is satisfied. You can hear the difference if you've spent enough years listening to rooms. An empty silence has teeth in it somewhere. A satisfied silence is a dog asleep by the fire.

Nadia runs the graveyard shift now. She fought management for it the same way I did—*the dead need somewhere to call*, though these days it's more that the living need somewhere to confess—and she's good at it, better than I was, maybe, because she never learned to flinch. She plays the old tapes sometimes, the ones that still hold my voice where the erasure couldn't reach, the bootleg cassettes listeners made before the change. She calls it *the archive*. She plays them at 2 a.m. for the insomniacs and the grieving and the truckers coming down off the ridge, and people call in and talk to the tape like it can hear them, and maybe it can. Maybe sound leaves a residue the way heat leaves a residue. Maybe some part of me is still in the board, in the faders and the foam padding and the coffee ring on the console that nobody has ever managed to scrub out.

Pell is the station engineer now.

That still makes me smile—this smile I have, which is new, which I'll come to. Twenty years an imaginary friend, decades more a fragment of unfinished ritual wearing a body and mismatched buttons, and now Pell has a job. A lanyard. A parking spot, which Pell insists on calling "a grave with better acoustics." Pell fixed the transmitter that had been dying for years, re-soldered the board, hung soundproofing in Studio B, and learned to drink coffee purely for the ritual of holding the cup. Pell is finally useful, and usefulness, Pell says, is the only resurrection worth having.

Pell visits me too. Not at the stones—Pell can't cross the boundary, being bound to the line rather than the land—but on the walks, at the edge, keeping pace the way Pell always did, a step behind my left shoulder where a friend stands when they want you to know they're there without making it a whole event. Pell talks. Pell has always talked. About the station, about Nadia, about the archivist's terrible tea, about the raccoons in the transmitter shed that Pell has named and refuses to evict. And I listen, and sometimes I answer, and my voice comes out of me like warmth off stone—present, real, belonging to no one and therefore, at last, belonging to me.

"Are you happy?" Pell asked me once, a year in, on the ridge road with the whole valley laid out below us, every porch light burning like a constellation that had decided to move indoors.

I thought about it honestly, because Pell deserved honesty after everything—the confession, the offer to take my place, the carrying of me to the stones when no one else could.

"I'm not unhappy," I said. "It turns out that was the war. Unhappiness was the occupation. This is just—" I gestured at the valley, at the lights, at the sleeping town I could touch with my attention if not my hands. "This is after."

Pell nodded, satisfied, and ate a sandwich, because Pell had decided that being imaginary for so long earned the right to enjoy lunch without irony.

* * *

The visits are the architecture of my year now. Everything else is weather.

One hour, on the dark moon, at the stones. That's the mercy clause. Page forty-one never mentioned it—maybe Grandmother never found it, maybe the founders wrote it in a hand too tired to copy it fair, maybe mercy is always the fine print because hope needs hiding places. One hour. Nadia arrives with coffee—always bad diner coffee, always in the dented thermos, because "you liked it bad, I refuse to insult your memory with good coffee"—and a portable radio, and we sit among the stones that hummed for centuries and now only hum the way power lines hum: with work being done, not dread.

We spend the hour saying everything.

The first year we spent most of it crying, honestly, and neither of us apologized for it. The second year we spent it telling stories—Marla's daughter graduating nursing school, Ruth Okafor remarried to a kind slow man who fixes boats, Dunmore retiring and handing his list to Nadia like a relay baton made of grief. The third year—we're in the third year now, or near it—we mostly sit in comfortable silence and let the radio play low, and Nadia leans against a stone that is warm where I am near it, and that is our version of holding hands, and it is enough. It is more than enough. I have been greedy for it all my existence, this closeness, and now I get it rationed, one hour a year, and somehow the rationing makes it infinite. Every hour contains the next one already, the way a seed contains the tree.

"How's the show?" I ask, every year, first thing, and Nadia laughs, every year, because of course that's my first question, of course the radio comes before my own heart.

"The show's fine. We had a caller last month who swears her grandmother's recipe card writes itself every Christmas. I put her on with Pell. They talked for two hours." A sip of terrible coffee. "Mostly it's just people now. Living people. Being lonely at 3 a.m. Turns out that was the job all along. The dead were just the ones honest enough to say so."

"And the town?"

"The town's fine, Ivo." Nadia uses the short name, the loose-fitting coat of a name, the part the boundary lets me keep. "The town is *fine*. Tomatoes. Weddings. Somebody's opening a bakery where the feed store was. You'd hate the bakery's signage and I need you to know that."

"I love that I need to know that."

"That's the whole point of visits," Nadia says, and puts a hand flat against the warm stone, and I put whatever-I-am-now against the other side of it, and for one hour a year the boundary is not a wall but a window, and the watching goes both ways.

* * *

Here is what I did not expect: the watching became the work, and the work became the peace.

Grandmother's rules were all about avoidance. Don't be seen. Don't speak. Count the doors so you know what's hunting you and can be elsewhere. Her whole cosmology was defensive—a life spent being a closed book in a town full of hungry readers. And I inherited it, and I lived it, and I broadcast from behind it, a voice with no face, a presence with no name spoken whole.

But the rite asked the opposite of me. It asked me to be *seen*—by the stones, by the thing, by Nadia, by myself—fully, finally, at the exact center of the oldest darkness in the county. And it turned out that being seen was not the ending of me. It was the beginning of the work. Because the boundary isn't a cage and it isn't a sacrifice, not really, not anymore. It's a *post*. A watchtower. Someone has to keep the lights on, and I keep them on in a way no one sees, walking the county roads each night, counting the doors, and finding—every single time—that nothing is hunting anyone. The collectors are gone. The debt is paid. The covenant is complete.

And still I count the doors. Four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and two with the county roads. Twice, always twice, because Grandmother taught me and because some inheritances aren't burdens, they're just love with a procedure attached.

Only now, when I finish the count, I don't whisper it. I say it out loud, to the whole dark town, in a voice that is mine and no one's and everyone's:

"All present. All accounted for. Goodnight, Hollow Creek."

And the town, in its sleep, in its tomato gardens and its bakery plans and its porch lights burning easy and unafraid, seems to hear me. Or maybe it doesn't, and that's fine too. A lighthouse doesn't need the ships to thank it. It needs only to burn.

* * *

Seasons pass differently here. I feel them as pressures and releases, like a hand tightening and loosening on my shoulder. Winter is the longest watch, the stars hard and close, the town huddled, and I walk more then, checking, counting, keeping. Spring is forgiveness. Summer is the smell of Mrs. Adeyemi's tomatoes drifting up to me even here, even through the boundary—scent, apparently, is one of the things that crosses, and I am grateful for it beyond all reason. Autumn is the anniversary. Autumn is the stones.

And every autumn, one hour, Nadia, bad coffee, the radio low, everything said.

Last visit, as the hour ran down, Nadia did something new. She set the portable radio on the flat top of the tallest stone, tuned to KRVL, and let it play into the circle—the graveyard shift, live, Pell's careful hands on the board, a caller named June talking about her late husband's laugh, Nadia's voice coming in warm and steady saying the things I used to say, the ministry continuing. And the sound pooled in the stones, and the stones hummed it back, and for a few minutes the whole circle was full of the station, full of the town's voice, full of us.

"This is yours," Nadia said. "I just keep the chair warm."

"It was never mine," I said. "It was the town's. I just answered the phone."

"Well," Nadia said, and smiled, and the smile was the one I fell for across a dispatch desk at 3 a.m. a lifetime ago, "somebody had to."

The hour ended the way it always ends—not abruptly, but like a song finding its last chord. Nadia packed the thermos, kissed her fingers and touched them to the stone, walked back down the hill without looking back, because we'd agreed years ago that looking back made it harder and we were done with harder. And I stood among the stones as the dark moon set, and I listened to the radio's signal fade, and I began the long walk, and the count.

Four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and two. All present. All accounted for.

* * *

People ask—well, Nadia asks, once a year, the only one who can—which of it I miss. The name, surely. The face. Coffee that I could actually drink. Sleep.

None of those, honestly. Here is what I missed, and here is what I have:

I missed *finishing*. My whole life was interruption. Parents taken by the flood year. A childhood cut into rules. A friendship that was a fragment of ritual wearing buttons. A career spent as a voice without a body. A covenant left half-spoken by a coward's ancestor, a ledger with a torn page, a sentence that stopped mid-word for a hundred and seventy years while the interest compounded in graves. Everything I loved was incomplete. Everything I was, was waiting.

And now the sentence is finished. The page is written. The count comes out even. Whatever I am now—faceless in photographs, nameless on the lists, a warmth in the boundary and a voice in the dark that says *goodnight* to a sleeping town—I am *finished*, in the way a bridge is finished, in the way a lullaby is finished. Not ended. Completed. Doing the one thing it was built to do, forever, gladly.

So I watch over Hollow Creek. I count the doors that no longer need counting, because the counting is not for safety anymore—it's for tenderness. Each door is a life. Each lit window is someone choosing, freely, without fear, to be seen. I count them the way you count the people you love at the end of a gathering: not to make sure none escaped, but to make sure everyone got home.

And I find that I am smiling.

I didn't used to smile. There's a reason the story of this town could have been called a hundred darker things. But the watching is done—do you understand? The *watching*, the fearful kind, the flinch behind the curtain, the held breath, the voice on the radio praying the thing at the edge of the parking lot would pass by—all of that is over. What remains is only the keeping, and the keeping is joy. The lights stay on because the town deserves light, not because the dark demands tribute. The doors are counted because they're precious, not because they're vulnerable. The smile stays up because there is finally, at long last, something to smile about.

One day, I suppose, even this will end. Boundaries fail, stones wear down, and some far generation will find the ledger and wonder. But that is not tonight. Tonight the porch lights burn. Tonight the radio plays. Tonight Nadia sleeps soundly in a town that is safe, and Pell locks up the studio and feeds the raccoons, and four hundred and eleven doors stand open to the morning that is coming, and I walk my rounds with the whole valley glowing beneath me like a hearth.

The smile falls only when it is no longer needed.

And Hollow Creek, tonight, needs it still.
