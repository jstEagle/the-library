## 6. Descendants

The town announced itself the way a body announces a fever: a wrongness in the air before the first symptom. Marcus Hale felt it through the windshield of the rented sedan, forty minutes out, when the radio—tuned to static because he could not abide the local stations—shivered and produced half a syllable of something that was not static at all. A voice, or the memory of one. Then nothing.

He turned it off with two fingers, precise, unhurried.

"You heard that too," said his sister, from the passenger seat. Vivian had their grandmother's jaw and their grandfather's appetite for other people's misfortune, and she had not slept well in three days, which she took as a professional affront.

"I heard interference."

"You heard *her*."

Marcus let that sit. The road unspooled between stripped fields, the kind of country that had been giving up on itself for decades. He understood why the founders had chosen it in 1931. Isolation was a resource. So was desperation. A town that had lost its mine and half its young people would sign anything, believe anything, if you promised the dead weren't really gone.

The notes lay open on his knee in a leather folio older than any of them: Elias Hale's hand, small and exact, ink gone the color of dried blood. *The binding requires a vessel of attention,* Elias had written, in the margin of a frequency diagram. *The dead do not need power. They need to be heard. Whoever hears them holds them.*

Whoever hears them holds them.

For ninety years the family had held the frequency like a deed to land no one else could see. They had never used it—not fully, not the way Elias intended before the others flinched and sealed the final step away. Marcus had spent eleven years as an estate attorney in Chicago learning exactly how much power lived in things nobody bothered to claim. He had spent four years since his father's death learning how much of this was real.

Enough. It was enough. And now someone else was listening.

* * *

They convened at the house on Corliss Street—the old Hale property, shuttered since Aunt Ruth died, still furnished in the style of 1974. Dust sheeted the furniture like snow. Vivian went through the rooms opening curtains, letting the light fall where it wanted, while Marcus set the folio on the dining table and waited for the others.

Cousin Daniel arrived first, from the airport, smelling of recycled air and cologne applied over fatigue. He brought the third-generation copies of the broadcast logs and a laptop he did not open. Then Priya, who had married into the name and stayed after the divorce because, as she put it, someone in this family should be able to do math. She brought the ledger of grievances—a document Elias had compiled in the ritual's aftermath, cataloguing every soul bound to the signal, every debt the founders had promised to settle and hadn't.

Four of them around a table built for twelve. The house noticed the difference. Old houses did that here; Marcus had forgotten.

"Say it plainly," Vivian said. "I've driven nine hours."

"The station has a new host." Marcus laid out the printouts he'd collected over six weeks: program schedules, a headshot, archived streams. BEATRIX AMBERDALE — THE LONG NIGHT, WEEKDAYS MIDNIGHT TO FOUR. "She runs a call-in show for insomniacs. Soft-spoken. No audience to speak of. Except—" he tapped the stream analytics, "—her listener numbers are impossible. Consistent listenership in a market that can't support it. Numbers that don't correspond to any living population center."

Priya leaned in. "You think the dead are listening to her."

"I think they've been waiting ninety years for someone who would listen back, and she's doing it nightly, on our frequency, without knowing what she's holding open." He straightened. "Elias wrote that the vessel of attention completes the circuit. The founders stopped short of the final step because they were afraid of what the dead would say. But the door's been unlocked for months. All it needs is a voice willing to speak the closing words—and whoever speaks them doesn't just release the dead. Whoever speaks them inherits the binding. Every grievance settled becomes authority. Every voice answered becomes a voice owed."

Daniel laughed, short and disbelieving, the laugh of a man testing whether the water is warm. "Inherits. You're talking about commanding the dead."

"I'm talking about owning the only thing death can't take from anyone," Marcus said. "Attention. Leverage. Think about what a man like Elias understood in 1931 that we've forgotten: the living will do anything to be remembered. The dead have nothing left but to be heard. Control the hearing and you control both."

Vivian was smiling. Their family smiled like that—at funerals, at foreclosures. "And the woman?"

"Is an instrument that doesn't know it's been tuned." He gathered the printouts into a neat stack. "We don't hurt her. We don't need to. We cut the signal, we secure the chamber under the studio, and we complete the ritual properly, with the family present, the way it was designed. She'll be a footnote. A woman who talked to the dark until the dark found better company."

* * *

That evening he drove out alone to look at the station.

It sat on the ridge above town like a headstone facing the wrong way—concrete block, tower rusted to the color of old iron, windows dark except one. Third floor. A single lit rectangle against the dusk, and behind it, if he angled himself right, the silhouette of a woman moving through a room, arranging things, preparing.

He shut off the engine and sat in the cold and watched the light.

There was a discipline to this, he told himself. Know your asset. Know your obstacle. Beatrix Amberdale had come to this town eighteen months ago with no history anyone could find deeper than five years, which meant either she was running from something or she was nobody, and both kinds were easy to move. She counted doors—he'd read that in a caller transcript someone had posted online, a fan account, the kind of parasocial scrapbook the internet made effortless. Twelve doors. She kept the lights on. She talked to strangers at three in the morning because the silence was worse.

Marcus recognized the type. He'd grown up around it: the lonely ones, the attentive ones, the people whose kindness was a structural weakness. His grandfather had called them vessels with contempt and envy in equal measure.

The light upstairs went out. Then, a moment later, came on again. Out. On. Rhythmic. Almost like signaling.

Or almost like a woman checking whether the dark was still there.

His phone buzzed. Vivian: *Chamber access?*

He typed back: *Tomorrow. Service tunnel from the generator room. Elias's key still fits—I checked the lock pattern against his diagrams.* He hesitated, then added: *She's awake up there. Broadcasting soon.*

*Then we move tonight,* Vivian wrote. *Before she gets comfortable. Before it gets ideas.*

*Ideas about what?*

The reply took a long time. Long enough that Marcus looked up at the lit window again and found it dark, and found, absurdly, that he was relieved.

*About who it belongs to,* Vivian finally sent.

* * *

They worked by flashlight in the house on Corliss Street, spreading Elias's papers across the dust-sheeted table like surgeons laying out instruments. Priya read the ledger aloud in a low, level voice, because someone had to know what they were walking into, and the names came out one after another: miners, a schoolteacher, two children, the constable who had helped the founders and been bound for his trouble. Ninety-one entries. Each with a grievance recorded in Elias's careful hand—unpaid wages, unpunished crimes, apologies never made, bodies never recovered.

"Settle these," Priya said, looking up, "and the binding closes with whoever closed it holding the authority. That's what the math says. That's what the ritual says." Her finger moved down the column. "But Marcus. These aren't debts you pay with money. Look at them. This one wants her killer named. This one wants his bones dug up off the Hale property. This one—" she stopped.

"Read it," Marcus said.

"'I want to know if my daughter ever forgave me.'" Priya closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "These aren't transactions. They're hearings. You can't seize a courtroom, Marcus. You can only sit in it."

"You can," Marcus said, "if you own the building."

Daniel had gone quiet in the corner, and when Marcus looked over, his cousin was staring at the wall as though the plaster had become transparent. "Do you hear that?" Daniel said.

The house was silent. The furnace ticked. But underneath it—faint, rising through the floors like water through foundation stone—there was a hum. Not mechanical. Tonal. A note held just below hearing, the kind of sound you felt in the fillings of your teeth.

"It's the frequency," Vivian whispered. "It's in the walls. Grandmother said the whole town was wired in '31. Every building. Every speaker."

"It's stronger than it should be," Priya said. She had gone pale in the flashlight glow. "Elias's notes describe leakage. Ambient. This isn't ambient. Something upstream is drawing current."

Marcus stood very still and listened the way he listened to opposing counsel: for the thing being said beneath the thing being said. The hum swelled, faded, swelled. And within it—if he tilted his head, if he stopped insisting on the world he preferred—there were voices. Dozens. Layered. Patient. The way a congregation sounds through a chapel door before you open it.

They were not moaning. They were not raging. They were *waiting*, with the terrible composure of creditors, and somewhere across town a woman with a microphone was about to give them a forum.

"We move tonight," Marcus said. "All of it. Power first, then the chamber, then the words. We finish what Elias started before she does."

"And if she already has?" Daniel asked. "Started, I mean. Without knowing it."

Marcus looked at the folio, at Elias's marginal line—*whoever hears them holds them*—and felt, for the first time since Chicago, the small cold vertigo of a man realizing the contract he drafted might bind its author.

"She hasn't," he said. "She doesn't even know the words exist."

He believed it. He needed to believe it. Outside, above the ridge, the station's single window burned steady against the dark, and the hum beneath the floorboards rose by a fraction of a tone—as if something in the walls had heard him, and disagreed.
