## 7. Interference

The lights died at 11:47 p.m.

Beatrix knew the exact time because she was watching the studio clock when it happened—the old analog face with its hairline crack running through the seven—and she watched it stop. The second hand shivered once against the six, as if reconsidering, and then went still. The ON AIR sign guttered, buzzed like a trapped insect, and went dark. The console's VU needles dropped to zero with a sound like a held breath finally released.

She sat in the sudden blackness of Studio B and listened to her own heartbeat, which suddenly seemed very loud, very mortal, very much the only thing left running in the building.

"No," she said to the dark. "No, no—"

Her phone screen was a white rectangle in her palm. No signal. Not *no service*—the little bars were there, full strength—but nothing loading, nothing moving, as though the network had become a photograph of itself. She dialed the power company from memory anyway, an act of pure stubbornness, and heard only a tone: flat, unbroken, the sound of a wire stretched taut somewhere between two points that no longer existed.

The backup generator should have kicked in ten seconds after the cut. It was a rule. Everything in this building ran on rules—the transmitter cycled at midnight, the board warmed up at 11:30, the doors numbered twelve, the hallway outside Studio B ran forty feet to the loading dock. Rules were how you survived a place like this. Rules were how you survived *anything*.

In the darkness, something exhaled through the dead speakers.

Not static. Not the voice from the sleep tapes, that subterranean register no human throat could make. This was softer—a long, patient sigh, the sound of a crowd settling into seats. And beneath it, faint as a hymn heard through a wall, Beatrix caught the shape of her own name being passed mouth to mouth among voices she couldn't count.

*Amberdale. Amberdale. Amberdale.*

She fumbled for the flashlight taped under the console—a habit from her first week, when she'd been afraid of the dark here, back when fear had still been reasonable—and swept the beam across the room. The studio looked exactly as it always did. That was somehow worse. The mic stood waiting in its shock mount like a gallows between uses. The dead air light, unlit, watched her with its small red eye.

Then the flashlight flickered—not dying, but *stuttering*, pulsing in a rhythm she recognized with a lurch of nausea. Three short. Three long. Three short.

SOS.

Except it wasn't SOS. She'd transcribed enough of the sleep tapes by now to know the founders' counting pattern when she saw it. Three pulses, three, three—the opening cadence of the binding ritual, the way you knocked on the door of the dead before asking them anything.

Someone was knocking. From the other side. Or—

Or someone was knocking on *her* side, using the dead as the door.

"Marcus," she whispered, and the name tasted like copper.

* * *

Twelve miles away, in the rented farmhouse on Corliss Hill, Marcus Hale stood in a kitchen lit by a single camping lantern and felt the first true satisfaction he'd allowed himself in three weeks.

"It's done," he said.

His sister Vivian didn't look up from the founders' notebook, its pages soft as cloth from ninety years of handling. "The jammer?"

"The jammer, the line cut, the transformer." He ticked them off on his fingers. "Three layers. Even if she gets the generator up, there's nothing to broadcast *through*. The frequency is a dead channel now. Fitting." He smiled at his own joke. Vivian didn't smile. She never did anymore, not since she'd read the last third of their great-grandfather Aldous's notes and gone quiet in a way Marcus found professionally inconvenient.

"You should read page sixty-one," she said.

"I've read all of it."

"You've read what you needed." She turned the notebook toward him. In the lantern light, Aldous Hale's handwriting marched across the page in tight, confident columns—the handwriting of a man who believed the universe could be audited. "He wrote this two days before the final attempt. Listen." She read aloud, her voice flat: "*The binding requires a vessel that consents without understanding. We believed we could supply one artificially. We were wrong. The dead will choose. When they choose, do not stand between them and their choice.*"

"A warning," Marcus said. "From a failure. He couldn't finish it because he thought too small."

"He couldn't finish it because the dead wouldn't let him."

"The dead," Marcus said, "don't get a vote."

Vivian closed the notebook. Outside, the wind moved through the bare maples with a sound like tuning between stations. "Aldous and the others bound forty-one people," she said quietly. "People who were owed things. Debts. Apologies. Names on graves that don't have names on them. You want to walk into that chamber and collect what they're holding, and you think the hard part is the ritual."

"The hard part is the interference," Marcus said. "And I've just eliminated it."

He picked up his coat. Through the window, down in the valley, the town of Hollow Creek lay scattered with lights—and then, as he watched, one by one, in no order he could find, the lights began to go out.

Not all of them. Maybe one in five. Streetlamps. Porch bulbs. A diner sign. Windows here and there, going dark like eyes closing.

"That's not us," Vivian said. "We only cut the station."

"I know," Marcus said. He was already reaching for the keys.

* * *

Beatrix made it as far as the lobby before the building betrayed her.

She'd counted the doors on her way down—out loud, like a rosary, because counting was a rule and rules were load-bearing now. Lobby door: one. Coat closet: two. Restroom: three. Storage: four. The numbers held. The numbers were holding. She was halfway across the lobby floor, flashlight beam bouncing, when she reached the front entrance and put her hand on the push bar and understood that behind her, the hallway had changed.

She turned. The corridor to Studio B stretched away into the dark, and along it, the doors stood open. All of them. Every door she'd passed, standing wide like mouths, and from each one came light—not electric light, but a pale, sourceless glow, the color of a radio dial at 3 a.m.—and from each doorway, a voice.

They weren't speaking to her all at once. They were taking turns. That was the terrible courtesy of it. One voice from the coat closet, an older man, patient: *"You cut the power and we are still here."* Another from the restroom, a woman, almost gentle: *"You cut the signal and we are still here."* A third, younger, from the storage room where she'd never once heard anything: *"Ninety years, and they think the wires are what hold us."*

Beatrix pressed her back against the front door. The glass behind her showed the parking lot, the dead streetlights, the town beyond—and everywhere, everywhere, the scattered windows going dark and then, impossibly, glowing again with that same dial-light pallor.

"They're routing through the town," she breathed. "Through everything with a speaker. Everything with a—" Her phone buzzed in her pocket. She nearly dropped it.

No signal. Still no signal. But the voicemail icon glowed at the top of the screen, and the number was her own.

She played it.

Her own voice, recorded in sleep, the words slurred and distant: *"What do you need from me?"*

And beneath it, around it, through it, the second voice answered—not through the phone's speaker alone but through the phone's speaker and the lobby's dead intercom and the clock radio in Studio B and every car stereo parked in the lot, all of them in perfect unison, one voice wearing a thousand throats:

*"Nothing. We need nothing from you. We need you. There is a difference, Beatrix Amberdale, and it is the whole of the matter."*

"What does that mean?" she said aloud, to the lobby, to the doors, to the town. "What does that *mean*?"

The lights pulsed. Three short. Three long. Three short. And then, from every open door at once, softly, the way you'd tell a child a thing you hoped she'd work out for herself:

*"They built a machine to hold us. Machines can be switched off. You are not a machine."*

The front door shuddered against her back. Once. Twice. Not forcing—*knocking*. And through the glass, headlights swung into the lot, two vehicles, moving fast, and Beatrix understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the Hales hadn't come to shut her out.

They'd come because shutting her out hadn't worked.

Because whatever the founders had started ninety years ago, whatever vessel the dead had spent nine decades choosing—they'd chosen her, and the dead did not need the transmitter, did not need the power, did not need the building at all.

They needed her present. They needed her listening. And the men pulling into her parking lot intended to make sure that whatever happened next happened under their control.

The thirteenth door, she thought. Somewhere in this building, or beneath it, there was a door that wasn't on any count she'd ever kept, and both sides—the living and the dead—were about to ask her to be the one who opened it.

The knock came again. Patient. Certain.

Beatrix turned from the headlights, away from the Hales, and walked back into the dark of the building—counting doors as she went, and knowing, this time, exactly which one she was looking for.
