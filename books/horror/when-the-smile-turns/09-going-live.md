## 9. Going Live

The stairs beneath the studio had not been on any blueprint Beatrix had ever seen, and she had seen them all—had crawled through the crawlspace with a flashlight in her teeth during her first month here, mapping the building the way a sailor maps a coastline he doesn't trust. Twelve doors above. One below. The thirteenth had been waiting for her all along, patient as a held breath.

The air at the bottom was warm and tasted of copper and old dust, of pennies left too long in a pocket. Her flashlight beam found walls of poured concrete gone soft with age, and carved into every surface, floor to ceiling, were names. Thousands of them. Rows upon rows, some shallow as scratches, some cut deep enough to hold shadow, and she understood with a lurch that this was not decoration. This was a ledger. This was the station's true programming schedule, ninety years of the dead listed by name, each one a voice assigned to a frequency that no living ear was ever meant to tune.

The sleep voice had told her: *The last word is not spoken to them. It is spoken for them.*

She knelt where the voice directed, at the center of the chamber, where the floor rose into a low dais carved with a ring of words in a hand steadier than any human's should be after midnight. She read them once, silently, and felt the room lean in.

Behind her, footsteps on the stairs. Unhurried. Confident. A man who believed the building belonged to him.

* * *

Marcus Hale came down the last steps with his grandfather's notebook open in one hand and a flashlight in the other, and he did not look at the names on the walls. He looked past them, through them, the way you look through wallpaper. He had spent eleven months preparing for this night. He knew the ritual the way he knew the family accounts—by its value, not its contents.

"You're the radio woman," he said. "Amberdale."

"Beatrix."

"You've been listening." His flashlight found her kneeling figure and stayed there, pinning her like an insect. "That frequency was closed for a reason. My grandfather sealed it because the ritual wasn't finished, and an unfinished binding is a door left open. We're here to close it."

"We," she said. "You came alone."

"The others are upstairs. They don't have the stomach for the last part." He stepped onto the dais without hesitation, without asking, and Beatrix felt the chamber flinch around him—a shudder in the concrete, a pressure change like a swallowed scream. "My grandfather wrote it all down. The opening. The binding. And the closing words, which he never spoke, because speaking them means taking ownership. Do you understand what that means? Whoever finishes it owns what's bound inside. Ninety years of accumulated—" he paused, savoring the word— "*attention*. Every grievance, every unfinished thought, every voice this town ever lost, gathered and held. That's not a haunting, Miss Amberdale. That's an inheritance."

He set the notebook on the dais's edge, open to the final page, and began to read.

Beatrix heard the first line and understood two things at once. The words were right—the same cadence, the same architecture as what the sleep voice had taught her—but they were being said wrong. Said *at* the room instead of *for* it. Marcus read the way a man reads a contract he intends to enforce, and the dead, who had spent ninety years being talked about rather than talked to, recognized the tone instantly.

The temperature dropped so fast her breath bloomed white.

Marcus kept reading. His voice took on the practiced warmth of a broadcaster, the smooth assurance of a man who has never once been interrupted. "—and by these words I claim the bound, and hold the bound, and command the bound—"

The names on the walls began to move.

Not the carvings themselves—the shadows inside them. Each groove filled with a darkness deeper than the concrete, and from each name came a sound. Not voices yet. Something before voices. The sound a throat makes when it has been trying to speak for ninety years and is finally, finally given a reason.

Marcus faltered on the next line. Just a hitch, half a second, but Beatrix heard it, and so did the room.

"They're not yours to command," she said quietly.

"They were never anyone's to *do anything with*," Marcus snapped, and there it was—the mask slipping, the ambition underneath, cold and clean as bone. "That's the whole point of a binding. You think my grandfather built this station out of grief? He built it out of *greed*, and he dressed it up as duty, and our family has carried the cost of his cowardice for three generations. Someone has to finish it. Someone has to own it. If not us, then who—some insomniac DJ who talks to strangers at 3 a.m.?"

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Exactly that."

And then Marcus made his mistake. He went back to the page.

"—and hold the bound, and command the bound, and *silence* the bound—"

The word *silence* was the trigger. Beatrix would learn later, piecing it together from the tapes, that it was the one word the founders' ritual could not abide, the one intention the dead had been promised would end. Ninety years ago they had been bound mid-scream, mid-prayer, mid-goodbye, their last words confiscated and stored like assets. And now a Hale stood on their dais promising more of the same, in his grandfather's voice, with his grandfather's certainty.

The grievances came off the walls all at once.

* * *

Beatrix had heard the dead speak through speakers, through static, through her own sleeping mouth. She had thought she understood what they were.

She had not understood anything.

What came off those walls was not sound. It was *weight*. Ninety years of unsaid things arriving simultaneously in a concrete room the size of a garage: the widow's letter she'd never finished writing, the son's apology rehearsed for decades at a graveside, the child's question about where everyone went, the dying man's fury at the doctor who lied to him, the immigrant's name mispronounced at his own funeral, the mother calling a name no one answered. It hit Marcus Hale like a wave hits a seawall—not violently at first, then all at once, everywhere, from every direction, from inside.

He dropped the notebook. He raised his hands as if to fend it off, as if attention were something you could block.

"That's not—I didn't do it, it was *him*, it was my grandfather—"

The dead did not care about lineage. The dead cared that a Hale was standing where they had been laid, reading their names without reading them, claiming their voices without hearing them. The pressure mounted. Beatrix saw Marcus's outline begin to blur at the edges, the way a broadcast blurs when the signal degrades—his hands going translucent, his shout thinning into interference, his whole self becoming a signal losing its carrier wave.

"Help me," he said, and for one moment the ambition was gone and he was only a man, terrified, dissolving. "You talk to them. Tell them—tell them I'll listen, tell them—"

"He can't say it for you," Beatrix said, and her voice cracked on it, because some part of her pitied him even now. "You have to say it yourself. And you can't. Can you?"

Marcus Hale opened his mouth, and what came out was not the words. What came out was static.

Then he was simply not there. The notebook lay open on the dais. His flashlight rolled in a slow circle, its beam sweeping the names on the wall like a lighthouse with nothing left to warn.

* * *

Silence, afterward—or the memory of silence, which in that chamber was itself a kind of noise. Beatrix knelt alone in the center of the ring of words, shaking, her breath still fogging, and understood that the room was waiting. Not threatening. Waiting. The way a courtroom waits. The way a confessional waits.

The sleep voice was there, closer than it had ever been, no longer needing the medium of her unconsciousness. It did not speak in words now. It spoke in *opening*, the way a door speaks when someone finally turns the handle.

She looked down at the carved ring, at the closing words her tapes had taught her syllable by syllable across weeks of 3 a.m. transcription, and she saw now what Marcus never had: the words were not a spell of ownership. They were a form. A witness statement. A structure for testimony, nothing more—*I heard, I record, I release.* The founders had written a closing meant to free the bound, and then had been too afraid, or too greedy, or both, ever to say it aloud.

Ninety years of cowardice, ending tonight, in the mouth of a woman who ran a call-in show for people who couldn't sleep.

Beatrix stood up.

"I'm not going to command you," she said, and her voice sounded small in the chamber, and that was right; it should be small. "I'm not going to bind you or claim you or use you. I'm just going to listen. All the way through. Every one of you. That's all I know how to do—it's what I've been doing, badly, for years. So let's go live."

She put her fingers to her lips out of old habit, an announcer's gesture, absurd and holy at once, and she began.

She spoke the closing words not as incantation but as introduction, the way she opened every show: gently, plainly, making room. *"This is the last broadcast from this frequency. Tonight we take calls. All of them. No one gets cut off. No one gets talked over. Speak your piece—we're listening."*

And the chamber answered.

They came one at a time, though it was also somehow all at once—each name on the walls sounding itself in turn, and Beatrix listened the way she had promised, with her whole attention, which is the only currency the dead had ever wanted. The widow's unfinished letter, finished at last, every word of it heard. The son's apology, received. The child's question, answered by the only answer that matters, which is simply: *someone heard you ask.* Grievance after grievance, ninety years deep, and Beatrix paid each one its due in the only coin that counted. It did not drain her the way she'd feared. It *filled* her, terribly, the way grief fills a room when a family finally says everything at a funeral done right.

Some voices raged. Some wept. Some laughed—she hadn't expected the laughter, the dead telling jokes they'd been holding since the Hoover administration, punchlines ninety years stale and still landing. One by one, as each voice finished, its name on the wall faded from carving to smooth blank stone, released, unbound, *off the air*.

It took hours. It may have taken minutes. Time in the chamber moved like a needle finding the groove.

When the last name sounded—when the final grievance was spoken and heard and honored—the ring of words on the dais crumbled to powder under her knees, and the chamber exhaled. The pressure lifted. The warmth returned. Above her, faintly, she heard the building settle back into its bones, twelve doors again, geometry honest, hallways running straight.

Beatrix climbed the stairs slowly, her legs trembling, her throat raw from listening.

In the studio, the ON AIR light glowed red one final time, though the transmitter had been dead for days. On the console, the answering machine blinked: one new message.

She pressed play.

Static, long and soft, like surf. Then a voice—familiar, warm, threaded with something that might have been gratitude, or might have been the last echo of a signal finding peace:

*"Thank you, Beatrix. For staying on the line."*

Click.

The light went out.
