## 10. Sign-Off

The silence came all at once, the way a held breath comes out.

Beatrix stood in the chamber beneath the studio with her palms still pressed to the carved floor, and the hum that had lived in the building's bones for ninety years simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped, like a needle lifted from a record mid-note, leaving a groove of pure quiet so complete she could hear her own blood moving.

Above her—she was sure of it, though sound had no business traveling through that much concrete—the transmitter died with a soft electrical sigh. The ON AIR light in her mind's eye went dark. She had watched it burn red for six years, every night from midnight to four, a small red heart beating over the town. Now nothing.

"Is that it?" she asked aloud. Her voice sounded enormous in the chamber, obscene, the only voice left.

The floor gave no answer. It didn't need to anymore. She could feel the difference the way you feel a fever break—the pressure gone, the heat draining out, the thing beneath the surface settling into ordinary stone. The carvings under her hands were just carvings now: old lines cut by old men who had wanted to own death and only ever managed to inconvenience it. Grief had filled them for nine decades. Last night, one grief at a time, she had helped pour them out.

She thought of Marcus Hale, or what had been Marcus Hale, and made herself not think of him. That accounting was closed. He had come down those stairs wanting to be a master, and the dead had shown him what they did with masters. She had come down wanting to listen, and they had let her leave.

That was the whole ritual, in the end. The founders' notes had called it binding, anchoring, dominion—a hundred grand words inked in a founder's careful hand. The missing step carved into the floor said none of that. It said *hear them*. Four words. Ninety years, and the entire unfinished architecture of the dead had been waiting on four words no one had bothered to say.

*Hear them.*

She had said them. One hundred and eleven voices, one by one, each given its name and its grievance and its due. Mrs. Aldous, drowned in the reservoir with her lungs full of someone else's secret. The boy from the 1941 fire whose death had been ruled an accident by men who knew better. The night engineer who'd discovered what the founders built and been folded into it like a letter into an envelope. She had heard them all, and they had heard her hearing them, and then—gently, gratefully, like guests finally excusing themselves—they had gone.

Beatrix climbed the stairs. Her legs shook. The thirteenth door stood at the top of the landing where it had stood for weeks, patient as a debt, and she stopped in front of it with her hand raised.

It looked smaller now. That was the strange part. All this time it had seemed to lean toward her, to widen at the edges when she wasn't looking, hungry in the way architecture shouldn't be. Now it was just a door—warped pine, paint gone gray, a knob worn smooth by hands that were dust before her grandmother was born. Behind it, when she pressed her ear to the wood, there was nothing. No draft. No murmur. No counting.

She opened it anyway. She owed it that much.

The room beyond was a janitor's closet. Mops. A bucket furred with dust. A shelf of solvent cans rusting into orange lace. Ninety years of the station's dead weight, and nothing else. Whatever had used the shape of this room as a mouth was gone, and the room had collapsed back into its honest self, embarrassed, ordinary, smelling of bleach and rot.

Beatrix laughed. It came out cracked and wet, half sob, but it was hers, made in her own throat with no second register underneath it. She closed the door and counted under her breath as she walked the corridor back toward the studio—one, two, three—and when she reached the end of the hall there were twelve doors. Twelve. The number sat in her chest like a key turning. The building had agreed, at last, to be a building.

* * *

Dawn found her in the control room, doing the thing she had done six thousand times: signing off.

But there was no show to sign off from. The console was dead, the VU meters flat-lined at zero, the transmitter light extinguished for the first time since 1934. She sat in the chair anyway, because rituals belong to whoever keeps them, and she spoke to the empty room in the low, warm voice she'd used for insomniacs and truckers and grieving widows for six years.

"This is Beatrix Amberdale, coming to you from the last hour of the last broadcast." She smiled at the dead microphone, at the dead air, at the morning pressing gray against the windows. "I don't know if anyone's listening. I don't know if anyone was ever listening, not really—not with ears. But somebody was always answering, and for a long time that was enough to keep me company, and I want to say thank you for that. Even the parts that terrified me."

She paused. Outside, the town was waking up. She could see Main Street from here, the bakery lights coming on, a man walking his dog past the hardware store without once glancing up at the station tower. For ninety years this place had pulled at the town like a riptide under calm water. This morning the water was just water.

"The dead are done talking," she said. "And I'm done talking for them. If you're out there—if any part of you is still tuned to this frequency—I hope you're somewhere with better reception. Goodnight, everybody. For real, this time."

She reached for the power switch out of pure habit, then stopped. Left it off. There was nothing to switch off. There hadn't been since the chamber. But she rested two fingers on the toggle anyway, a benediction, and felt the cold metal and nothing behind it, and that nothing was the kindest sound she'd ever heard.

* * *

The days after were almost embarrassingly normal.

She slept eight hours. Nine, once. She made coffee in the morning instead of drinking it cold at three a.m. over a console. She went to the diner and ordered eggs and ate them while they were hot, and when the waitress asked how she was sleeping, Beatrix said *fine*, and discovered she meant it, and had to blink hard at her plate because six years of *fine* being a lie had made the truth feel like a stranger's coat that somehow fit.

The callers stopped. That was the strangest mercy. For weeks they had come at all hours—dead voices routing through phone lines, through car radios, through the speaker of a child's walkie-talkie abandoned in a hedge on Delmore Street. After the chamber: silence. The voicemail line filled up with nothing but a dentist's appointment reminder and a wrong number asking about a yard sale. She played them twice each, hunting for the register underneath, the second voice, the question she never asked aloud. Nothing. Just the town, being the town, dull and alive and gloriously unhaunted.

On the fourth day she packed the sleep recorder away in a box with the founders' logbook and the transcription notebooks—eleven hundred pages of the dead's testimony, which she would deposit with the county historical society under seal, to be opened in fifty years, when everyone who could be hurt by the truth was safely past caring. Justice belonged to the wronged dead; memory belonged to the living. She could give them both without keeping either.

Marcus Hale's family sent a lawyer. Of course they did. A thin, expensive woman with a folder full of questions about the station's transfer of ownership and the whereabouts of Mr. Hale, who had, according to his phone records, entered the building nine days ago and never exited. Beatrix answered what she could and declined the rest, and when the lawyer asked, delicately, whether she had seen anything unusual that night, Beatrix looked at her for a long moment.

"He got exactly what he came for," she said.

The lawyer wrote that down. Beatrix decided to let her.

* * *

Two weeks after the silence, on a Tuesday, the voicemail machine blinked.

One new message. Timestamped 3:47 a.m.—her old hour, the dead hour, the hour she'd owned like a shopkeeper owns a corner of the world. Her hand hovered over the button for a long time. The building around her was quiet in the ordinary way, twelve doors deep, no loops, no leaning walls. Whatever this was, it wasn't *that*. Probably. She pressed play.

Static first. Then a breath. Then a voice—old, papery, human in every particular, and familiar in a way that reached into her chest and turned something over gently, the way you'd turn a stone to see what lived beneath it.

"Miss Amberdale. You won't know my name, and that's all right. I worked the night board here in '36, before they finished what they started, before I understood what I'd helped them do." A pause, filled with the sound of a man deciding whether to go on. "I've been listening a long time. Since the beginning. I couldn't leave—none of us could—but I could listen, and for ninety years nobody listened back. Then you did. Night after night, you talked to the dark like it deserved courtesy, and when we started answering, you didn't run. You transcribed. You testified. You said our names."

The static swelled and softened like breathing.

"You gave us the only thing anyone ever offered: attention. Paid in full. So this is the last call, Miss Amberdale, and it's just to say—we heard you too. Every night. Thank you." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "You can turn the lights off now. We don't need to see."

The message ended. The machine clicked. In the control room, the tape hiss faded into the plain, clean silence of a building that was only a building.

Beatrix sat very still for a long time. Then she saved the message, copied it to two drives and a thumb drive in her pocket, and labeled the file with a date and a single word: *witness*.

* * *

Her last night at the station, she walked every floor with a clipboard, the way she had her first week, counting doors. Twelve on the ground level. Twelve upstairs. Twelve in the basement, where the chamber door now stood padlocked with the county's seal bright yellow across the wood. She wrote the number down and initialed it, and the number stayed the number, obedient, finished.

At midnight she stood in the parking lot and looked up at the dark windows. Six years she had kept those lights burning—every bulb, every hallway, every hour, because the alternative to light had seemed, once, unspeakable. She remembered the woman she'd been, alone in a cinderblock building at the edge of town, talking to strangers at 3 a.m. because the silence was worse. She had thought she was hiding from the world. She had actually been holding a door open for it.

Beatrix reached the edge of the lot, turned back one last time, and did the thing she had never once done in six years.

She left the lights off.

The station settled into the dark like a ship into harbor—no protest, no flicker, no voice rising out of the wiring to object. Just a decommissioned building at the edge of a sleeping town, holding its secrets the way the ground holds its dead: quietly, completely, and without needing anything from anyone ever again.

She drove home with the windows down. At a stoplight on Main Street, the bakery's radio drifted out into the warm night—some pop song, trivial and bright—and Beatrix found herself waiting, without meaning to, for the second voice underneath it.

There wasn't one. There never would be again.

And yet, as she pulled into her own driveway and killed the engine, she caught herself doing one last thing: tilting her head, listening, the old habit running deeper than the fear ever had. Because the dead were gone, yes. The frequency was silent. The doors were counted and true.

But she had spent six years learning that the world is full of voices nobody bothers to hear—and now, for the first time, she was free to spend the rest of her life listening anyway.
