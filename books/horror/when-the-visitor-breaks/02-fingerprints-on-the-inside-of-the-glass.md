## 2. Fingerprints on the Inside of the Glass

The Marlow house had been empty for eleven months, which in Hollowell Street terms made it practically a fixer-upper with potential.

I knew this because the work order said so, and because I'd written the work order myself, transcribing Mrs. Delgado's phone call into the bland professional shorthand that kept my job from sounding like what it sometimes was. *Residential pest inspection, vacant property, suspected rodent activity in walls.* Fourteen words that contained no mention of the fact that the walls in question belonged to 14 Hollowell Street, and that I had spent four years of my childhood listening to those same walls with a flashlight under the covers, cataloguing every sound and giving it a boring name until it stopped being able to hurt me.

*Pipes,* I used to say. *Raccoons. The house settling.*

It worked. That was the thing nobody tells you about rational explanations — they work like a tourniquet. They don't heal anything. They just keep you from bleeding out long enough to grow up.

I sat in the van at the curb for four minutes longer than any professional would admit to, watching the house through the windshield. It looked smaller than it used to. They all do, eventually; childhood houses shrink in retrospect the way grief doesn't. Peeling paint the color of old milk. A porch with one sagging step. The upstairs window over the nursery — my old nursery, before it was the Marlows' nursery, before it was nobody's nursery — stared down at me with the flat black patience of an eye that has been open a very long time.

"Rodents," I said out loud, to the van, to myself. "In the walls. Standard infestation. Standard day."

The van did not answer, which is more than I can say for some houses.

I got out. I got my kit. I walked up the walk like a woman — no, not a woman, let's not start that fight again, like a *person*, a person with a clipboard and a job to do — and I did not look at the porch.

That's a lie. I looked at the porch. Everyone looks at porches. Porches are where things wait.

But nothing was waiting on it, so I let myself have the small mercy of pretending I hadn't checked.

* * *

The lock was a joke. Vacant houses in dying neighborhoods don't get real locks; they get suggestions of locks, gestures toward security, a deadbolt that turned with the kind of soft click that said *you and everyone else, friend.* The realtor's key hung off my belt loop next to the UV flashlight and the moisture meter, and I let myself in through the kitchen door the way I'd let myself in through it a hundred times as a kid, except then it had been called sneaking and now it was called billable hours.

The smell hit first. Empty houses have a smell — dust and mouse urine and the ghost of whatever the last family cooked — but underneath it, faint as a watermark, was something else. Something I didn't have a boring name for. Warm drywall. Sun-heated wood. The particular sweetness of a house that has been holding its breath.

I stood in the kitchen and did the thing I always do, the thing that makes me good at my job: I listened.

Nothing. The refrigerator hummed its absent refrigerator hum. Wind moved somewhere in the eaves. A branch dragged its fingers along the siding out back, slow, unhurried, like something checking whether anyone was home.

"Pipes," I told the silence. "Trees. Settling."

The silence accepted my explanation the way a customs officer accepts your passport: without comment, without belief, and with every intention of looking closer anyway.

I started in the basement, because you always start in the basement, and because starting anywhere else would mean admitting the basement scared me. It didn't scare me. It was a basement. Concrete floor, water-stained walls, a furnace that had died sometime during the previous administration. I ran my light along the sill plates looking for grease marks, droppings, the little highways of fur that rodents leave when they move in and treat a house like a hotel they intend to trash.

There were droppings. Old ones, desiccated, scattered along the east wall like punctuation. There was a gnawed hole behind the water heater, chewed edges soft with age. Classic mice. Textbook. I wrote it down. I felt the specific relief of a problem with a solution, a problem that could be solved with steel wool and poison and a follow-up invoice.

Then I went upstairs, and the day stopped being textbook.

* * *

The handprints were on the windows.

Small ones. Child-sized, five fingers each, pressed flat against the glass the way kids press their hands to windows when they're watching for someone coming home. I found the first set in the front bedroom — not my bedroom, the Marlows' kids' room, though the geography of the street rearranged itself in my head anyway and for a second I was eight years old standing in a room that used to be mine.

The prints were gray. Dust prints, the kind left when a small palm presses into a layer of grime and lifts away clean. Except the glass wasn't dusty. I checked. I wiped it with my sleeve and the pane came away clear, which meant the prints weren't sitting *on* the surface. They were in it. Etched, or grown, or remembered by the glass so thoroughly that cleaning was beside the point.

I've seen a lot of weird things in vacant houses. Squatters' altars. A wall of clocks all stopped at different times. Once, a bathtub full of bowling balls, and I want you to know I charged extra for that one and still think about it weekly. But handprints in glass — handprints that survived being wiped — that was new, and new, in my line of work, is rarely good.

"Kids," I said, out loud, because saying the boring word first is half the exorcism. "Previous tenants. Kids lived here."

Kids had lived here. The Marlows had two, a boy and a girl, gone now along with the parents, moved somewhere with working streetlights. Of course there were handprints. Kids touch everything. Kids are basically hands with people attached.

Except.

I stood back and looked at where the prints actually were, and the boring word started to choke in my throat.

They were high. Not high-high. Not vaulted-ceiling high. But higher than a child could reach — higher than a child could reach *standing on anything*. I know this because part of my job is figuring out how pests access things, and access is geometry, and geometry does not care about your feelings. The bottom edge of the lowest print sat a good seven feet off the floor. There was no furniture near the window. No bed, no dresser, nothing. Even if the Marlows' kid had been a champion furniture-climber, even if he'd stacked the dresser on the bed and done a running jump, he'd have left scuff marks, drag marks, evidence. Access leaves evidence. That's the whole religion of my profession.

There was no evidence. There was only the print, five small fingers and a palm, pressed into the glass from the inside, seven feet up, in a room that had been empty for eleven months.

And here's the detail I would give anything to unnotice: the fingers pointed *down*.

Think about it. A kid pressing hands to a window stands and pushes outward, palms flat, fingers up. These prints were made by someone hanging — someone gripping the top of the frame or clinging to the wall beside it, pressing their palms to the glass while their body dangled below, the way you'd press your face and hands to a window if you were on the wrong side of it and wanted very badly to be let in.

Or out.

I photographed it. Flash, no flash, wide shot, close-up. My hands were steady, because ten years of practice will do that — the body learns to be steady even when the mind is doing arithmetic it hates. *Dust. Resin. Some chemical reaction in old float glass.* I gave each theory its moment, polite as a hostess, and each one declined to hold weight. Float glass doesn't remember hands. Chemistry doesn't make fingerprints. And nothing natural points down.

* * *

The second set was in the hallway window at the landing. The third was in the bathroom, on the little frosted pane above the tub, and those were the worst, because frosted glass blurs everything and the prints showed through it like shapes underwater, like small hands on the other side of ice.

The fourth set was in the nursery.

I want to be honest about the fact that I stood outside that door for a while. Long enough that my phone screen dimmed twice. The door was closed, which vacant-house doors shouldn't be — I'd opened every other door in the house and they'd all swung obligingly, hollow and easy. This one was shut the way doors get shut when someone means it.

"Rodent entry point assessment," I told the door. "That's all this is. You're a wall with opinions."

The knob turned without resistance, which was somehow worse than a lock. The nursery smelled different from the rest of the house. Sweeter. Like the inside of a violin. Like dust that had been warmed by something other than sun.

The wallpaper was yellowed roses, the same paper that had been there when I was a kid, when it was my room, when I'd traced the rose vines with my eyes at three in the morning and named them one by one — *that's a pipe, that's a beam, that's a raccoon, that's nothing* — until the naming wore grooves in me so deep I could still feel them now, thirty-one years old, standing in a stranger's nursery.

The handprints were on the window above the crib alcove. Two sets, side by side. Small, smaller than the others — younger. Fingers pointing down.

And beneath them, low on the glass, at exactly the height a child's hands would reach if a child stood on tiptoe at the sill, was a third print. One hand only. Pressed hard enough that the glass around it had gone slightly milky, like breath that never faded.

I knew that hand. You don't forget your own hand. You especially don't forget it at eight years old, when it's the only tool you have for holding the world together.

I had pressed my palm to that window. Hundreds of times. Watching the street. Watching for — I never let myself finish that sentence, even then. Watching for whatever children watch for when they refuse to say they're lonely.

The print was still there. Twenty years later. In glass.

"That's not possible," I said, and my voice came out level, professional, and completely insane-sounding in the empty room, and I loved it and hated it equally, because the voice was the last wall between me and the arithmetic, and the arithmetic went like this:

Houses don't keep handprints. Glass doesn't remember. Children don't reach seven feet. And Ada Thornbury does not get to stand in this room feeling watched by their own eight-year-old hand unless every boring word they ever learned was a lie they told themselves to survive.

I took the photo. My hands were steady. Somewhere under the steadiness, something cracked — not loudly, not dramatically, just the small structural sound a house makes when it settles, when it decides, finally, to stop pretending it isn't moving.

* * *

I finished the inspection. I want credit for that. I walked every room, logged every dropping, measured every gap in the soffits, and wrote *suspected mouse activity, recommend exclusion work, see attached photos* in my neatest clipboard handwriting, and if the attached photos included four sets of impossible handprints, well, the invoice doesn't ask questions.

The light had gone long and amber while I worked, the way dusk does in autumn — not falling so much as pooling, gathering in the low places first. I packed my kit, locked the suggestion-of-a-lock behind me, and came out onto the porch with my collar up and my mind already drafting the report, already building the wall back up brick by boring brick.

*Mice,* I thought. *Old glass. Light angles. Confirmation bias.*

The steps creaked under me, the same sagging step, same note, and I was halfway down the walk before I noticed the porch swing.

It was moving.

Not swinging — moving. The small, arrhythmic sway of something recently occupied, chains ticking softly against the hooks, settling. The boards of the seat held two shallow dents in the dust, side by side, spaced the way a small person sits when they're trying to take up as little room as possible. Polite. Patient.

And on the armrest, drawn in the dust with one careful finger, in a round childish hand I would have known anywhere, anywhere at all, was a single word:

*You left mid-song.*

I stood on the walk with my clipboard against my chest like a shield and read it three times, and the wall I'd spent twenty years building did not come down all at once. Walls like mine don't. It just developed, right there in the middle, a crack shaped exactly like a name I hadn't said aloud since I was nine years old.

Behind me, at the nursery window, the last of the daylight caught the glass, and for half a second I would have sworn the small dark handprints were pressed there fresh — wet, recent, waiting —

— and on the porch, in the corner of my eye, where the shadows pool first at dusk, someone very small cleared their throat.
