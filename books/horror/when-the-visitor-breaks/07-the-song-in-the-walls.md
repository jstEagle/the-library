## 7. The Song in the Walls

I want to say I went back to the Marlow house because of the contract. That's the version I'd give a client, anyway, delivered with the flat competence that has kept me fed for twenty years: *follow-up inspection, secondary entry points, standard protocol.* The truth is uglier and simpler. I went back because Wren was still sitting on my porch when I got home, and I needed to be anywhere he wasn't.

He hadn't moved. He sat cross-legged on the top step with his chin on his fists, watching the street the way children watch streets—like something interesting is always about to happen. He was wearing the same clothes. Of course he was. The same corduroy jacket with the elbow worn to fuzz, the same sneakers with one lace knotted double because eight-year-olds don't retie laces, they just make do.

"You could've told me you were coming," I said.

"I've been here the whole time," Wren said. "You just stopped looking."

That's the thing about him. Every sentence out of his mouth sounds like nonsense until it doesn't, and by then it's already inside you, doing its work.

"I have work tomorrow," I said. "Real work. Rats in a restaurant on Delmore."

"Rats are honest," Wren said, which is not a sentence an imaginary friend should know how to construct. "You should go back before dark."

"Why?"

"Because it's easier to hear at dusk." He picked at the frayed knee of his jeans. "And you're going to want to hear it while you can still pretend you don't understand it."

I told him, in the tone I use for clients who suggest the infestation might be *karma*, that I didn't take advice from hallucinations. He smiled like I'd said something sweet. Then I locked my door, and through it, muffled, I heard him humming four notes. Just four. And my hands—the traitorous, competent hands that have pulled raccoons out of chimneys for two decades—twitched toward the melody like they were greeting an old coworker.

I slept badly. In the morning I loaded the van with traps I wasn't going to set, drove to Hollowell Street, and told myself the whole way that this was due diligence.

* * *

The Marlow house sits third from the corner, the way it always did, with the sagging porch rail and the maple out front that drops helicopters all over the gutters every fall. Other houses on the street have been renovated, flipped, painted cheerful colors. Nobody touches this one. It has the specific neglect of a property everyone agrees not to see—a gray house in a photograph where every other house is in color.

I let myself in with the key from the lockbox, because the bank that owns it pays me whether or not there's anything in the walls, and banks believe in walls the way other people believe in God.

Dusk came early that time of year. By five the light through the windows had gone the color of weak tea, and the rooms filled up with that blue-gray half-dark that makes every house look like a memory of itself. I worked the perimeter with my flashlight and my thermal camera, calling out measurements to no one, because saying numbers out loud keeps a room honest. Attic access: clear. Crawlspace: droppings, old, mouse, nothing else. Kitchen wall behind the sink: cold spot, but old houses have cold spots, old houses have drafts, old houses—

Old houses do not hum.

It started so quietly I mistook it for the furnace. A vibration in the drywall, low, felt more than heard, the way you feel a truck pass three blocks away. I put my palm flat against the kitchen wall to check for ductwork.

The wall was warm under my hand. Not furnace-warm. Skin-warm.

And beneath my palm, faint as a voice through a pillow, someone was singing.

I snatched my hand back like the wall had bitten me. The song kept going without me, patient, unhurried, a woman's tune or maybe a child's, pitched in that register that lives between a lullaby and a hymn. I knew it. That was the worst part. Ten years of refusing had not erased it; it had only buried it, and the melody came up through the dirt of me intact, note for note, the way water finds the shape of the ground it once ran over.

*Hush now, hush now, the day is done—*

No. I didn't sing it. I want that on the record, whatever record exists for moments like this. My mouth stayed shut. My throat locked down so hard it ached. But my hands—my hands pressed back against the drywall as if checking a pulse, and my fingers began to move, small circles, a rocking motion, the way you'd pat a child's back. Pat-pat, pause. Pat-pat, pause. Keeping time.

"That's not happening," I told the kitchen. "That is a vibration in a load-bearing structure and I am having a stress response."

The song looped. I noticed it then, really noticed: it never ended. It ran through the verse, reached the place where a second line should come, and folded back on itself like a wave hitting a seawall. *Hush now, hush now, the day is done,* and then instead of continuing, a silence shaped exactly like a missing word, and then the beginning again. Over and over. A music box with a broken tooth. A sentence that can't find its period.

Every child on Hollowell Street knew that gap. We used to dare each other to fill it. Danny Reyes always would—he'd bellow some idiot rhyme into the hole and we'd scream and laugh and run home before dark. Priya's older sister could do a harmony that made the gap feel almost full. And me? I stood at the edge of it my whole childhood with my arms crossed, refusing, and I told myself for twenty years that the refusal was why I was fine.

Standing in that kitchen with my hands rocking against the warm drywall, I understood for the first time that I had never actually heard the song end. None of us had. It didn't end. It waited.

I got my hands off the wall. It took actual effort, the specific effort of unclenching, and when I stepped back my palms left prints in the dust—ten small ovals, absurdly neat, like the wall had taken my fingerprints for its files.

* * *

I did what any professional does with an anomaly: I documented it. Thermal camera on the kitchen wall showed a heat signature roughly the size and shape of a seated child, holding steady at 36 degrees Celsius, in a room where the ambient temperature was eleven. I photographed it. The camera's screen showed the warm patch and, when I lowered the camera and looked with my own eyes, nothing but wallpaper gone amber with age.

Then the song moved.

Not stopped—moved. It came through the wall behind the stove next, then the hallway, then up through the floorboards under my boots, always at the same volume, always the same distance away no matter where I stood, which is acoustically impossible unless the singer is standing exactly where you are, everywhere you are. I followed it to the stairs because following things is my job and because the alternative was admitting I wanted to run.

Upstairs, the nursery door was closed.

It had been open on my first inspection. I'd noted it—*nursery, north-facing, water stain on ceiling, no activity.* Now it was shut, and from behind it the song came clear for the first time, no longer felt but heard, a thin true voice with the crackle of old vinyl in it. And underneath the voice, so faint I might have invented it, other voices. Adult voices. Grown men and women humming along in their sleep, keeping the missing place empty, keeping the circle unbroken.

Four notes. That's all it took to get my hands going again. They rose without my permission, curled toward the door, fingers spread, and I watched them from somewhere outside myself with the clinical horror of a man watching his own x-ray. My hands remembered the song the way amputees remember itching. Whatever refusal lived in my head, it had never reached below my elbows.

I stuffed both hands in my pockets. I gripped the flashlight like a rosary. I recited pipe diameters, chemical trade names, the phone number of my supplier, anything with digits in it, because digits don't rhyme.

The song paused.

Right at the gap. Right at the missing line. And the whole house leaned in—I don't know how else to describe it; the silence acquired weight, attention, the quality of a held breath—and something in the pause asked, politely, the way you ask someone to pass the salt:

*Won't you finish it?*

"No," I said aloud, and my voice cracked down the middle like a kid's.

The pause stretched. Somewhere below us—I say *us* because by then I was certain the nursery and I were being counted together—somewhere below, a car door slammed on Hollowell Street, and the song resumed its loop as if nothing had happened, patient as erosion.

I backed down the stairs with my eyes on the nursery door the entire way. Behind it, the voices hummed. Under my boots, the floorboards carried the rhythm up through my soles, into my shins, into the marrow where I keep the things I don't think about. Pat-pat, pause. Pat-pat, pause.

In the kitchen my flashlight found the dust again, and I stopped dead.

There were new prints beside mine. Small ones. Child-sized, four fingers and a thumb, pressed into the dust on the *inside* of the room, at a height no child could reach, arranged in a neat row like someone had been standing at the counter, hands flat, waiting. Waiting for me, or waiting with me, or simply keeping me company the way Wren used to keep me company during the worst summers—present, silent, and somehow always between me and the wall where the noises came from.

I got out of that house at 6:47 p.m., twelve minutes after sunset, and I sat in the van with the engine running and the doors locked and my hands shaking on the wheel. In the rearview mirror, the Marlow house watched me go with all its dark windows, and I could still feel the song in my palms, a phantom rhythm ticking away like a second pulse.

Here is what I wrote in my logbook, because the logbook is real and paper and mine:

*Follow-up complete. No active infestation. Recommend structural acoustic assessment. Note: occupants of surrounding residences report auditory disturbances consistent with shared-wall sound transmission.*

All lies, in the sense that every word was chosen to hide the truth, and all true, in the sense that the disturbance is auditory and it does transmit through shared walls and it has, apparently, been transmitting into me for thirty years, waiting for the day I'd stand close enough to hear it clearly.

My phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, sent from a device registered two blocks away, which I know because I looked it up immediately, because research is also a form of running:

*You held your breath. Good. But you can't hold it forever. — D*

Danny Reyes. Missing eleven days. Texting me from inside a house that stands empty.

I turned the heater on full blast, put both traitorous hands flat on the steering wheel, and drove toward the one person who might explain what my own body was doing without my permission. Wren owed me answers. And if he didn't give them, I decided, gripping the wheel as the four-note phrase ticked away in my palms like a bomb I'd swallowed years ago—

then I'd get them from whoever was singing.
