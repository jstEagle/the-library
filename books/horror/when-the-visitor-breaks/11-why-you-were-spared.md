## 11. Why You Were Spared

I didn't sleep. That's not a dramatic flourish; it's an inventory. I lay on the fold-out couch in my van with the windows cracked and the moth spray I'd wedged under the door doing absolutely nothing, because moths were never the problem, and counted the sounds the house made across the street.

There were more of them than there used to be. Or maybe I was just finally listening, which is its own kind of disease.

Around three in the morning I heard something that I chose to file under *settling beam*, even though settling beams don't keep time. Around four, something that I filed under *raccoon*, even though raccoons don't hum in a key. And around five, when the sky went the color of dishwater, I gave up filing altogether and sat up, and that's when I saw Wren sitting cross-legged on my passenger seat like he'd been there all night, which, knowing Wren, he might have been.

"You're supposed to knock," I said.

"You left your door unlocked."

"It's a van. The door is a door. There's no protocol for this."

"There's no protocol for any of it," Wren said. "That's sort of the point." He was watching me with those eyes that hadn't aged a day in twenty years, patient in the way that only children and certain kinds of weather are patient. "You look terrible."

"Thanks. I've been researching missing people from my childhood street. It's a real spa weekend."

"I know what you found," he said. "Danny Reyes. Marcus Bell. The Okafor twins. And before you ask—yes, there's a reason you're not on the list."

The engine block ticked as the morning warmed it. Somewhere down the street a dog started up, thought better of it, stopped.

"Because I never sang," I said. "We've been over this. Chapter and verse. I named every noise. I kept my mouth shut. Twenty years of not humming so much as a jingle, and—"

"Ada."

"—and now you're going to tell me it was all for nothing, is that it? Because I swear to God, if you tell me the pipes were actually pipes and I spent my whole childhood white-knuckling a radiator—"

"*Ada.*"

He said it the way you'd say a name into a well. Not loud. Just dropped, and waiting for the echo.

I shut up. That should have been my first warning, how easily he could still do that.

"You weren't spared by silence," Wren said. "You were spared because you were *marked*."

* * *

Let me back up, because I need to, and because if I'm being honest—and this book is nothing if not me being honest against my own will—I already knew. Some part of me had known since the handprints on the inside of the glass. Since the porch. Since the way the house across the street had gone quiet when I arrived, like a room going quiet when someone walks in who everyone recognizes.

Wren climbed onto the dashboard and sat facing me, legs dangling over the speedometer, which should have been funny and wasn't.

"When you were six," he said, "the first winter, do you remember the night of the ice storm?"

"No."

"You do. You just named it. You called it 'the night the branches sounded like fingers.' You told your mother the trees were scratching at the glass and she said it was just the wind and you said, 'No. It has fingernails.' And then you decided it was branches after all, and you never mentioned it again."

"That's not remembering. That's you repeating something back at me."

"Then here's one you can't file." He leaned forward. "The morning after the ice storm, there was a circle drawn in the frost on your bedroom window. From the outside. A perfect circle, about this big—" he made a ring with his hands, "—with a smaller circle inside it, and four marks. Like a face, if a face had been designed by something that had heard about faces but never seen one."

My mouth went dry. Not metaphorically. I reached for the water bottle in the cupholder and it was empty, because of course it was.

"I remember condensation," I said. "I remember—"

"You remember telling yourself it was condensation. You were good at that. You were the best I ever saw at it." Something moved through his face then, some expression too old for eight years old, and I understood—not fully, not yet, but the shape of it—that Wren was about to say something he'd been carrying a long time, and that it was going to cost him. "But the mark wasn't on the window, Ada. The window was just where it showed. The mark was on *you*."

* * *

Here's what I want you to understand about a life built on rational explanations: they work. That's the horrible part. They work *beautifully*. Every noise gets a name, every shadow gets a source, every cold spot gets a draft, and you get to walk through the world thinking you're brave when really you've just built a very elaborate filing system over a hole in the floor.

I built mine young. Pipes. Raccoons. Settling beams. Wind with fingernails. And underneath it all, one foundational document, the load-bearing wall of my entire personality: *I never sang, and that's why it couldn't touch me.*

Take that away and the whole structure comes down. Which is why, when Wren said the word *marked*, my first instinct—the honest, ugly, human instinct—was anger.

"Bullshit," I said. "Absolute bullshit. I did everything right. I did the *hard thing*. Every other kid on that street hummed along like it was a nursery rhyme, and I'm the one who figured out you don't answer when it pauses, you don't finish another person's line, you don't—"

"You don't sing," Wren said softly. "Right. And how did you know that? Before anyone told you? Before Mrs. Okonkwo, before me—how did six-year-old Ada Thornbury know, instinctively, that the song was a door?"

I opened my mouth.

Nothing came out.

Because I didn't know. It had never been knowledge; it had been reflex, the way you flinch from a hot stove without anyone teaching you. And reflexes come from somewhere.

"The others learned the rules," Wren said. "You *were* the rules. Don't you see the difference? Marcus had to be warned by his sister. Danny figured it out from a nightmare and still got caught eventually. But you—you woke up already knowing. Because the mark isn't a target, Ada. It's an *instruction manual*. It came installed."

The dog down the street started up again and this time didn't stop. I let the sound fill the van while I tried to find a version of the world where I still made sense.

"So what," I said finally. "It picked me. Random. Bad luck. Fine. That doesn't change anything. I still didn't sing. I still—"

"It didn't pick you *randomly*." Wren's voice did something I'd never heard it do. It gentled. And gentle, from Wren, was worse than cruel. "Ada. Why do you think you could see me?"

"You're an imaginary friend. Kids see imaginary friends. That's the whole—"

"I'm not imaginary. We both know that now. So why you? Why did the boy in the walls send *me* to you, out of every child on Hollowell Street? Why did I sit with you at lunch when none of the other kids would? Why did I know where you hid when your parents fought?" He spread his small hands. "Because I wasn't your friend, Ada. I was your *keeper*. And you weren't the child who escaped the Visitor. You were the child the Visitor was saving for last."

* * *

I got out of the van. I don't remember deciding to; my body just went, the way bodies do when the mind needs a door. I stood in the middle of Hollowell Street at dawn with my arms wrapped around myself, looking at the Marlow house, and Wren appeared beside me—he was always beside me before I noticed him arriving—and we looked at it together.

"Say it plainly," I said. "No poetry. Plain words."

"Okay." He took a breath, which was strange, because I'd never been sure he needed to breathe. "The Visitor collects children who sing its song. All the way through. Every child on this street learned it except you, because you were never supposed to learn it and refuse. You were supposed to learn it and *keep*. You were the reserve. The last verse lives in you somewhere—it always has—and the Visitor has been waiting twenty years for you to surface it, because a collection isn't finished until the final piece is placed, and you are the final piece."

"That's insane."

"Yes," Wren agreed. "It is. It's also true, and those aren't mutually exclusive, and you of all people should know that by now."

"But I *left*. I moved away. Twelve years old, we moved to my aunt's in Corvallis, and nothing followed, nothing happened, twenty years of nothing—"

"Did nothing happen?" Wren asked. "Or did you make sure nothing happened? Did you ever stay anywhere longer than two years? Did you ever let anyone learn your birthday? Do you own a single photograph of yourself past the age of twelve?"

I opened my mouth to say *of course I do* and found the shelf where that memory should have been standing empty.

Not empty. *Missing.* Deliberately, cleanly missing, like a tooth pulled so nothing behind it could ache.

"Oh," I said. Small word. Enormous falling.

"You didn't escape, Ada. You were *stored*. Kept somewhere dry and dark and unremarkable, the way you keep a thing you intend to come back for. Every rule you thought was armor was a lid. Every name you gave the noises was packing material. You weren't invisible." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet, and I realized with a lurch that he was crying for me, or possibly for himself, and that I couldn't yet tell which was worse. "You were *labeled*. This one's mine. Do not open until collection."

The street was waking up around us. Sprinklers ticking on. A paper thumping against a driveway two houses down. The ordinary machinery of a morning that had no idea what it was sitting on top of.

And I stood there doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for twenty years. The loneliness I'd called safety. The friendships I'd declined before they could decline me. The apartments I'd left before the neighbors learned my name. The flat, gray competence I offered clients instead of warmth, because warmth was a door and doors were how things got in—or how things got *out*, which, apparently, was the actual danger.

Every choice I'd been proud of. Every wall I'd built. And it turned out the walls weren't mine. They were blueprints. Someone else had drafted them, and I'd signed them believing the signature was my own idea.

"I spent my whole life thinking I was the one kid who got away," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else reporting on me. "I built a career on it. *I don't acknowledge it, it can't hold me.* That was the whole— that was my *thing*."

"I know."

"And the whole time I was—what? Layaway?"

"You were hope," Wren said. "Its hope. The Visitor doesn't feel hope the way we do. It feels it the way a jar feels fullness. And you were the last inch of space at the top."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—one short, awful bark of a laugh with nothing behind it. "So the great survivor of Hollowell Street. The girl who beat it by refusing to believe in it. Turns out refusing to believe was the box it packed me in."

"Nonbinary," Wren corrected gently, because even at the end of the world he remembered, and somehow that undid me more than everything else.

"Yeah," I said. "Thanks."

* * *

We walked back toward the Marlow house without discussing it, the way you drift toward a car crash. The porch boards knew our weight and announced it. At the bottom step I stopped, because stopping was all I had.

"If I'm marked," I said, "if I'm the last piece—then why take the others first? Danny, Marcus, the twins. Why collect them and leave me?"

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that the sprinkler two houses down completed a full arc and started over.

"Because they were practice," he said. "And because taking you requires the verse. Your mark keeps you safe the way a museum case keeps a jewel safe—from everyone but the owner. The Visitor can't crack you open itself. You have to open. You have to *sing*." He turned to look at me fully, and for the first time in twenty years, my imaginary friend looked afraid. "Which means everything it's doing now—the disappearances, the song in the walls, the loop that's missing its ending—it's not hunting the others, Ada. It's *tuning* you. Every child it takes brings the song closer to complete. Every verse it reassembles is pressure on the one line you've been carrying since you were six without knowing it."

The front door of the Marlow house stood closed. Behind it, faint as breath on glass, something began to hum.

"And here's the part I hate," Wren said. "The part I've hated since I understood it. The mark works both ways. It kept you safe from everything else—but it also means that when the song finishes, you won't get a choice. You'll sing whether you want to or not. Your hands already hum. What do you think happens when the last verse arrives and finds you?"

I thought about my hands at dusk yesterday, moving on their own like small animals dreaming.

"I'll fight it," I said.

"You'll try."

"I'll *fight it*."

"I know," Wren said, and touched my hand—cold, brief, gone. "That's why it marked you and not someone weaker. That's why it waited." He looked up at me, and beneath the fear in his face there was something else, something that looked horribly like apology. "But Ada—if you want to fight it, really fight it, you need to stop trusting me."

"What? Why?"

"Because you're going to want to ask me what to do. And I can't tell you. Not because I won't." His voice cracked, eight years old and ancient at once. "Because I don't get to choose what I am to you. I never did. Check the county records. Check who lived in that house before the Marlows. Check the names of the children who went missing in 1948, and 1961, and 1979. Then come find me, and ask me my real name, and watch what happens to my face."

He stepped backward off the porch, into the shade of the eave, and the shade took him the way water takes a stone.

Behind the closed door, the humming paused.

I did not answer the pause. Old reflex. Except now I knew the reflex had been installed, and knowing didn't make the silence any easier to stand inside.

I stood on the porch of my childhood house, holding a mark I couldn't see, in a cage I'd mistaken for a life, and made myself one promise: whatever Wren was, whoever Wren had been, I would find out before the song finished finding me.

The county records office opened at nine.

It was 8:47.
