## 17. Into the Nursery

Midnight, as it turns out, is not a metaphor. It's a temperature.

I stood on the porch of the Marlow house at 11:58 with my kit bag on my shoulder — the real kit, not the prop I carried for clients — and watched my breath come out in little gray ghosts that drifted toward the front door like they knew the way better than I did. The boards under my boots had stopped creaking weeks ago, when the house stopped pretending to be a house and started being a throat.

The door wasn't locked. It hadn't been locked since Danny Reyes went into the walls, because locked doors imply a boundary, and boundaries imply a choice, and the Visitor had gotten tired of pretending either existed.

"Last chance to develop a drinking problem," I told myself.

I've never been much of a drinker. My coping mechanism of choice has always been inventory. So I inventoried: flashlight, spare flashlight, the recording of Mrs. Okonkwo's half-verse on my phone with the volume wired to a single earbud I could rip out in half a second, Edmund Vale's diary in a ziplock bag against my chest like the world's saddest heart, and a crowbar, because some problems respond to leverage.

And Wren. Or not Wren. Whatever Wren was now that I knew — a taken child from 1971, a boy named Thomas Ellery, sent back down the decades like a letter nobody wanted delivered. He was sitting on the porch rail in the same corduroy jacket he'd worn for twenty years, swinging his legs, watching me with the patient expression of a child who has been waiting so long that waiting has become his whole personality.

"You're doing the thing where you list everything in your bag," he said. "I can see your lips moving."

"It's a coping mechanism."

"It's a stalling mechanism."

"Those are the same thing with better branding." I looked at the door. It was painted a color that had been white once, in a decade that had been hopeful once. "How far down does it go?"

Wren stopped swinging his legs. That was answer enough, but he gave me words anyway, and his voice had that careful flatness he used when the truth was heavy and he didn't want to drop it on his own foot. "The nursery isn't in the house, Ada. The house is in the way of the nursery. Like a curtain."

"Great. Love architecture that requires a philosophy degree."

"You don't need a degree. You need to not stop walking, and not sing, and not look at the flyers for longer than it takes to know what they are."

"The flyers."

His face did something complicated. Twenty years old and eight years old at the same time, and both ages flinched. "You'll see."

* * *

Inside, the air was warm. Not stuffy-warm. Body-warm. The temperature of a room where someone is sleeping, or a room where someone is being kept. My flashlight beam moved through it like a hand through water and found the hallway exactly as I remembered it from childhood — the same water-stained ceiling, the same wallpaper with the faded roses, except now the roses were gone.

In their place: paper. Sheets of it, layered, corner over corner, like scales.

I got close enough to read one and immediately wished I'd taken Wren's advice about not looking.

MISSING — MARCUS HOLLOWELL, AGE 9 — LAST SEEN JUNE 3, 1961.

Under it, half-covered: a flyer from 1974, a girl with a gap-toothed smile. Under that, 1988. Under that, a photocopy of a photocopy so degraded it was just the word MISSING repeated like a heartbeat. Decades of them. The whole history of the street, printed in bold type and stapled to lampposts by weeping parents, and every single sheet had ended up here, pasted to the walls of the Marlow house by hands I didn't want to imagine, because the Visitor collected not just the children but the evidence of their absence, the paper ghosts their families had made while hoping.

"It wallpapered itself with them," I said. My voice came out wrong. Flat. "It decorated."

"It likes them," Wren said quietly. "It doesn't understand that they're sad. It thinks they're portraits. Like the families drew pictures of everyone it caught, and put them up so it wouldn't forget anyone." He touched one flyer — 1961, Marcus Hollowell — with two fingers, gentle, the way you'd touch a photograph of someone you loved. "It never forgets anyone, Ada. That's the whole problem. That's the whole *everything*."

I put my hand on the wall. Under my palm the paper was warm and faintly pulsing, and I told myself it was a pipe, because naming things is what I do, it's the only trick I've ever had, and even now — especially now — the lie was a kind of handrail.

"Which way?"

"Down," Wren said.

"There is no down. The basement's a crawl space. I've been under there with a flashlight and a bad attitude."

"The nursery isn't in the house," he repeated. "Follow the song."

And then, as if it had been waiting for its cue — as if it had always been waiting for its cue, for me specifically, for this exact midnight — the song started.

It came up through the floor. Through my boots, through the soles of my feet, through the long bones of my legs, the way music does when it's less sound than weather. The lullaby. The one the children of Hollowell Street had hummed for seventy years without knowing why, the one Edmund Vale wrote in 1948 and died without ever hearing finished. I knew every note up to the gap. The gap was the whole point of me. I was the child who'd never learned the ending, and therefore the child who'd never been collected, and therefore the woman — the person — standing in a haunted hallway at midnight with a crowbar and a diary, about to walk into the gap on purpose.

"Ada." Wren's hand found mine. Small. Cold in the way of things that have been outside a long time. "If you feel yourself starting to hum — and you will, everyone does, it's not weakness, it's just what the song *does* — squeeze my hand. Hard. I'll be rude about it if I have to."

"You? Rude?"

"I once told Mrs. Okonkwo her pie was dry."

"That was you? She made that pie for *you*. She was eight in 1955 and she made a pie for an imaginary boy and you called it dry."

"It *was* dry." But he was smiling, and for one second the porch, the flyers, the warm pulsing walls all fell away and it was just us again, the way it had been when I was eight and he was the only one who stayed. Then the song swelled a half-tone, and his smile went out like a pinched wick. "Down," he said. "Now. It knows we're here."

* * *

The stairs were where the crawl space hatch should have been.

That's the sentence I keep coming back to, even now. I stood at the top of a staircase that could not exist, in a house I had crawled under a thousand times, and the staircase went down farther than the house went tall. My flashlight couldn't find the bottom. The beam just kept going, swallowed, and the walls on either side were papered in missing children all the way into the dark, hundreds of them, thousands, faces fading into the distance like a choir seen from the back of a cathedral.

"Physics," I said, "is a social construct," and I put my boot on the first step.

The step took my weight. The next one did too. Behind me, I heard the front door close — not slam, not lock, just *close*, soft and final, the way you close a door on a sleeping child's room. I didn't look back. Looking back is for people whose houses still obey the rules of houses.

We descended. The song grew. And here is the thing I have not told anyone, the thing I will probably never tell anyone: it wasn't frightening, at first. That's the trap nobody warns you about. The lullaby is *good*. It's the melody your body has been waiting its whole life to hear, the sound of being small and safe and sung to, and every step down I could feel my throat wanting to open, my lungs wanting to join, my whole traitor body leaning toward the music like a plant toward a window. My hands were humming before my mouth was. I could feel it in my fingertips, a vibration like a struck tuning fork, and I remembered rule one — *never hum* — and squeezed Wren's hand so hard he made a small offended sound.

"Rude," he managed.

"Preemptive."

The flyers changed as we went down. That was the other thing. The top of the staircase was the old ones, the decades-dead, faces soft with age. But the lower we went, the newer the paper got, the brighter the ink, until I was passing flyers I recognized — Danny Reyes, age 34 now but photographed at nine, gap in his smile, MISSING, LAST SEEN OCTOBER. And then one that hadn't been printed yet. I know it hadn't been printed yet because it was my face on it, my adult face, and the age listed was blank, and the last-seen date said *TONIGHT*.

I kept walking. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, in whatever ledger the universe keeps: I saw my own missing poster and I kept walking.

"You saw it," Wren said. Not a question.

"I saw a flyer."

"Ada."

"It's a *flyer*, Wren. It's paper. It's a guess. It's the house trying to scare me with a Xerox." My voice held. I was almost proud of it. "It doesn't know how this ends."

"No," Wren said, and there was something in his voice I didn't have a name for yet, something I'd understand later, much later, when understanding it would cost me more than fear ever had. "It doesn't guess. It remembers forward. It's not saying this is how you end, Ada. It's saying this is how it *ends for everyone else* if you fail." He tugged my hand. "It's a promise, not a threat. It's showing you what it keeps."

The staircase ended.

Not at a door. At a landing where the walls curved inward like the inside of a throat, and the air was thick and warm and smelled — God, this is the part that still wakes me up — it smelled like milk and dust and cedar and something underneath that was older than smell, the scent of a room that had been waiting so long to be a nursery again that waiting had soaked into the wood. Ahead of us, a doorway glowed with a light that came from nowhere, the yellow of a nightlight, the yellow of a lamp left on for a child afraid of the dark, except the child who was afraid of the dark had been dead for seventy-six years and the lamp had never been allowed to go out.

The song was coming from inside. All of it. Every verse, every voice, layered — I could hear them separately now, the way you can pick individual instruments out of an orchestra once someone shows you how. A woman's voice, 1961. A boy's, 1974. Danny Reyes, thirty-four years old and singing in a nine-year-old's register, because the song had kept him at the age it caught him. Dozens of voices. All the missing. All singing the lullaby they'd learned on Hollowell Street, looping it, missing the final verse, the way the song itself had looped for seventy-six years, missing its ending, the way Edmund had.

They sounded happy.

That was the worst part. That was the part I would carry out of there if I carried out anything at all: they sounded *happy*. Contented. Sung to sleep and never allowed to wake, mouths moving, and somewhere in that layered choir was a word being mouthed over and over, a word I wouldn't understand until I saw the mouths that made it.

"Ada." Wren's hand was shaking now. Not cold — shaking. "Once we go through, it can see us. All the way. There's no pretending in there. No naming things as pipes. No rational explanations." He looked up at me, eight years old and seventy-six years old and every year in between, and he said, very quietly: "You'll have to be seen. As what you are. It's the one thing you've never let anything do."

I thought about ten years of explanations. Pipes. Raccoons. Settling beams. A whole life built like a wall, brick by brick of *it's nothing, it's nothing, it's nothing*, and how every brick had been a door closing, and how the last door was about to open whether I knocked or not.

"Yeah," I said. "Well. I've been seen by worse. I've seen my own missing poster."

"That's not —"

"I know what it's not." I clicked off my flashlight, because the doorway was giving all the light a person should need, and some part of me understood that walking in there with a beam was like bringing sand to the ocean. I took out the ziplock with Edmund's diary. I touched it once, through the plastic, the way you'd touch a hand. "Come on, Wren. Let's go meet the neighbors."

The doorway breathed us in.

The nursery was bigger than the house. I don't mean it felt bigger. I mean that the room we stepped into had a ceiling lost in shadow and walls that ran back farther than the lot line, farther than the street, wallpapered floor to impossible ceiling in missing children, and in the center of it, suspended in the yellow light like specimens in amber, hung the collection — and one of them turned his head, eyes open, mouth moving, and mouthed the word I'd been hearing threaded through the choir since the stairs.

*Finish.*

Danny Reyes looked at me with eyes that were alive and emptied at once, and mouthed it again, and this time I read his lips clearly, and this time I understood that he wasn't asking.

He was warning.

Behind me, soft as a door closing on a sleeping child, the way in sealed itself — and the song stopped.

All of it. Every voice. Every year.

In the silence, something in the nursery turned to look at what had finally come home.
