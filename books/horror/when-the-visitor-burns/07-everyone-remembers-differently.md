## 7. Everyone Remembers Differently

Hazel came to Edith Okafor's cottage on the morning after the tape. He had not slept. The child's voice was still in his ears—*we plough the fields and scatter*—sung slightly flat, the way children sing when they are singing for themselves and no one else, and every hour since he had listened to it, the voice had grown a little more familiar, the way a face seen across a crowded room grows familiar until you realize you know it from somewhere you cannot name.

Edith's cottage sat at the crook of Fen Lane, its garden a riot of late marigolds gone leggy and wild. She was already at the door when he knocked, as though she had been standing there all night waiting for him to come.

"I saw your light," she said. "On and off. On and off. Like someone deciding whether to confess."

"Mrs. Okafor—"

"Come in before the street sees you. The street has eyes this week."

The inside of her house smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and something older underneath, something like the inside of a church. Every surface held photographs, and Hazel noticed—he could not help noticing—that none of them showed anyone under sixty. As if childhood itself had been edited out of the record.

She set down two cups of tea without asking whether he wanted one, and folded her hands on the table between them, and looked at him for a long time with eyes that were pale as pondwater and just as deep.

"Show me the rubbing again," she said.

He spread it out. Five small fingers, seared into plaster, each line distinct—the whorls, the creases, the tiny imperfections that made a print a person's own. Edith did not touch it. She leaned over it the way one leans over an open grave.

"That's her hand," she said. "That's Wren Halloway's hand. I'd know it anywhere. I held it once, crossing the ford, when the water was high." Her voice did not waver. That was the terrible thing about it—it was steady as a level, as if she had rehearsed this confession for fifty years and had only been waiting for an audience worth telling. "You've come about the bell. And the wall. And the tape."

"You know what's on the tape."

"I put the batteries in it myself," said Edith Okafor, "in June of 1974. And then I never let myself remember doing it. Until now."

* * *

Edith had been thirty-one that summer, and the fen had been dying.

Not dramatically—not the way death comes in cities, sudden and loud—but the way it comes to places that live by water and weather. The barley came up thin and grey-headed. The eels left the drains. Cows gave milk that curdled in the pail before evening. And the quarry pool, which had always been cold and clean enough to drink from if you were desperate, went still and black and smelled faintly, sweetly, of something rotting far beneath its surface.

"People need a reason," Edith said. "When the world goes wrong, they need a reason they can carry. A reason with a face is best of all."

Wren Halloway had a face. Eight years old, or nine—no one had ever been certain, because no one had ever been certain where she'd come from. A traveller family that passed through in '71 and moved on without her; a niece of the Halloways at the far end of the fen; a foundling left at the church door. The stories changed depending on who told them, and Wren herself, asked directly, would only smile and say she'd always been here, same as the reeds.

"She talked to birds," Edith said. "I mean she *talked* to them. Whistled little runs of notes and they'd answer. She could stand in a field and the crows would come down around her like a black cloak. And she knew things. Small things. Told Mrs. Cutler her well would go sour three days before it did. Told the Verrity boy to stay out of the drain culvert the very week it flooded. Little kindnesses, mostly. But people don't forgive you for being right, Mr. Ravensworth. They resent it. Being right is a kind of power, and power in a child's hands frightens adults worse than anything."

By August the crops were failing outright, and the harvest committee met in the vestry of St. Aldhelm's, and someone—Edith would not say who, though her eyes flicked, briefly, toward some memory she slammed shut—said the word *witch*, and no one laughed, and that was how it began.

"They didn't decide to kill her," Edith said. "I want you to understand that, because it matters, because it's the whole horror of it. They decided to *ask* her. To make her lift the curse. It was going to be a ceremony, a correction, almost gentle. And then she looked at them—all those grown men and women holding lanterns at the edge of the quarry—and she wasn't afraid. That was the unforgivable part. She looked at us the way you look at weather. And Tom Verrity's brother pushed her, or she fell, or—I was there and I still don't know, because we all agreed afterward not to know. The water took her. And the water was so quiet about it. That's what I can't get past, after all these years. How quiet the water was."

* * *

Hazel sat very still. The tea had gone cold in his cup and he hadn't touched it. Outside, a crow landed on Edith's fence and regarded the window with one flat eye, and Edith glanced at it and did not look away.

"Afterward," she said, "the village did what villages do. It closed like a fist. But a fist needs to hold something, or it forgets it's a fist. So we each kept a piece of her."

"A piece."

"One thing each. Taken from her, or given—no one will ever say which. Her shoes. Her coat button. Her song—she used to sing the harvest hymn walking home along the towpath, and Mrs. Aldous took that, took the singing right out of her throat somehow, and after that Wren never sang. Her name. Her reflection—there was a girl who swore she saw Wren's face looking back at her from the quarry water for weeks after, and one day it stopped, and the girl grew old without ever seeing herself properly again. Each of us kept one thing. And here is the cruelty of it, Mr. Ravensworth, the design of it: as long as everyone held their piece, no one could remember the whole. And no one could forget, either, because they were carrying their fragment around inside them like a stone. We built a forgetting that couldn't finish. Fifty years of half-sleep. And the bell—" her hand trembled now, the first break in the level surface of her voice, "—the bell rings to peel it open. Every toll takes a layer off. You've heard it. Don't tell me you haven't."

"I've heard it," Hazel said. "No one else has."

"No one else *can*. Not yet. It rings for whoever's carrying the last piece." She looked at him then, straight on, pondwater eyes meeting his, and Hazel felt something shift under him like ice on a winter pool. "And the prints on the vestry wall are yours. Aren't they. You pressed your own hand there without knowing why."

"Yes," he whispered.

"Because the last piece isn't in this village anymore. It left. It walked out of Marrow Fen forty years ago in the pockets of a man named Ravensworth."

* * *

Edith told him about his father then, and the telling was gentle, which made it worse.

Gerald Ravensworth had been the youngest man at the quarry that night, barely twenty, new to the parish, a surveyor's assistant who'd have done anything to belong. And belonging, in Marrow Fen, in 1974, meant taking your piece. Edith didn't know what Gerald had taken—only that within the year he'd married, and within two years there was a son, and the son was christened with a middle name that made old Mrs. Aldous, drunk at the christening feast, laugh until she cried and then go white as whey and leave without her coat.

"Hazel," Edith said. "They christened you Hazel Wren Ravensworth."

The room tilted. Not metaphorically—Hazel felt the floor drop, the whole architecture of himself swing loose on its hinges. His mother had always said the middle name was a family tradition, an old fen name, nothing more. His father had said even less; his father had flinched whenever Hazel wrote it out on school forms, had once torn a page out of a notebook because the boy had practiced his signature, *Hazel Wren Ravensworth*, over and over, the way children do, filling the margin with themselves.

Every signature. Every form. Every time he'd spoken his own full name aloud, he had been speaking hers. Carrying it. Keeping it warm. Forty years of a stolen name worn so long it fit like skin.

"He didn't keep it in a box," Hazel heard himself say. His voice sounded far away, underwater, quiet as the quarry. "He kept it in *me*."

"That's why the prints match your hand," Edith said softly. "It was never your father's guilt coming back. It was hers, calling in the debt. She doesn't want vengeance, Mr. Ravensworth—or if she does, it's a simple kind. She wants her name back. She wants to be called what she was called. And you're the only one alive who can say it, because you're the only one who's been saying it all your life without knowing."

Hazel stood up because sitting had become unbearable. The crow on the fence lifted off, unhurried, and wheeled away toward the church tower.

"The harvest moon," he said. "Friday. What happens Friday?"

Edith looked at her tea, at the photographs of the old and the absent, at the window where the light was beginning to fail though it was barely noon.

"On Friday the moon finishes rising, and whatever hasn't been returned gets taken instead," she said. "And there are people in this village—good people, kind people, people who bring you jam—who would rather take everything from you than remember what they did. Go home, Mr. Ravensworth. Lock your door. And whatever knocks tonight, don't tell it your name."

But when Hazel stepped out onto Fen Lane, the first door down the street was already closing, quietly, the way water closes over a stone.
