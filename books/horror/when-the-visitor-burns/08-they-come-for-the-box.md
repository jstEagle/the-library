## 8. They Come for the Box

Hazel had three hours left, and he spent the first of them doing something he hadn't done since he was a boy: he locked his door.

Not the casual lock of a man keeping out opportunists, but the full ritual—bolt, chain, the wedge of oak he'd bought for a cottage whose door had never once needed it. He checked the back door twice. He drew the curtains in the front room and then, feeling foolish and doing it anyway, in the bedroom too. The tape recorder sat on his kitchen table between Wren's shoes, and the name tag—WREN, in a child's careful capitals—lay on top like a label on evidence. He had considered hiding the box. He had considered burying it, driving it to the coast, dropping it in the canal. But every alternative was just another way of keeping, and keeping was the whole disease. So he let it sit in the open, in the lamplight, where he could watch it and it could watch him.

The scratching had followed him home. That was the thing he hadn't put into words yet, even to himself, even in the privacy of his own skull. For three nights now it had come from inside his walls—soft, patient, five-fingered, moving from the chimney breast to the corner above his bed and stopping there, directly over his pillow, at the exact moment he closed his eyes. He had pressed his palm to the plaster and felt warmth, faint as a hand withdrawn seconds before.

He made tea he didn't drink. He sat with the shotgun he'd inherited from his father and had never loaded, and finally put it back in the cupboard, because whatever was coming for him tonight was not coming through the door in a shape that lead would argue with.

At half past eight, the dogs started.

Marrow Fen kept dogs the way other villages kept gossip—indiscriminately and loudly—and Hazel had learned their vocabulary in his fifteen quiet years here. The yapping of a fox run. The complaint of a cat on a wall. This was neither. This was a low, continuous baying, dozens of voices braided into one, and it was not moving. It was holding still, somewhere down the lane, and holding still was worse.

He went to the gap in the curtains.

They came up Hollow Lane in no particular hurry, the way a tide comes, and that was the horror of it—not a mob, not a shouting, torch-lit thing out of a film, but the village itself, walking. He recognized coats. He recognized the way Mrs. Tredwell from the shop walked with her hip cocked from thirty years behind the counter, and how the two Denby brothers moved with the identical rolling gait of men who'd worked the same fields all their lives. There must have been sixty of them, seventy, more than he'd ever seen assembled outside of a funeral, and they carried no weapons and no torches and made no sound except the sound of feet on the lane's old tarmac, and the dogs had gone silent all at once, as if dismissed.

Tom Verrity led them, of course. Verrity in his good waxed jacket, hands in his pockets, face wearing the expression he wore at harvest fêtes and remembrance services: genial, unhurried, a man arriving at a friend's gate. He stopped at the low stone wall in front of Hazel's cottage and did not cross it, and the crowd stopped with him, filling the lane from hedge to hedge, and the strange thing—the thing Hazel's body understood before his mind did—was that they were all looking at the door. Not at the windows. Not at him. At the door, with the patience of people who knew it would open, because it had always opened, because doors in Marrow Fen opened for the village or they stopped being doors.

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. He made himself step away from the curtain, made himself cross the room, made himself unbolt the door, because the alternative was to stand in his own house like prey, and he had spent fifteen years being no one's prey.

He opened it. The cold came in with the smell of the fens—mud and rot and under it, faintly, the mineral smell of quarry water.

"Evening, Hazel." Verrity's voice was warm as a stove. "Thought we'd have a word."

* * *

"There's nothing to have a word about," Hazel said. "It's late. Whatever this is, it can wait for morning."

"I don't think it can." Verrity glanced back at the crowd—not for support, Hazel realized, but the way a conductor glances at his orchestra, confirming they're still with him. "Moon's at nine-tenths. You'll have seen it. We all see it. And we understand you've found some things. Up at the church." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "Things that aren't yours."

"They're not yours either."

"No." And here Verrity smiled, and it was the smile that undid Hazel a little, because it held no malice at all. It held something much worse: certainty. "They're ours. All of ours. That's the whole point, son. That's what you've not understood yet, poking about with your torch and your little rubbings. Those things belong to *everybody*. That's why they work. One person keeps a thing, it's just a keepsake. A whole village keeps a thing—" he spread his hands, taking in the lane, the dark fields, the drowned quarry beyond them, "—it's a covenant."

"Is that what you call it." Hazel's voice came out flatter than he intended. "A covenant. She was nine years old."

Something moved through the crowd then—not a murmur, nothing so honest as a murmur, but a shifting, a settling, the way a flock of starlings turns all at once. He saw faces he'd sold wasp-nest treatments to. The postman. The woman who ran the mobile library. Old Denby, who'd once bought him a pint and told him Marrow Fen was the last decent place in England.

"We don't want trouble, Hazel." Verrity again, patient, reasonable, a man explaining drainage to a stubborn tenant. "That's the truth. We like you. We've always liked you. You came here broken and we let you mend, didn't we? Nobody asked questions. Nobody pried. That's not nothing, in a village. That's *everything*, in a village. And all we're asking now is the same courtesy back."

"The courtesy of giving you a dead child's shoes."

"The courtesy," Verrity said, "of letting sleeping things lie." He reached into his jacket and produced an envelope, held it out across the wall. "There's five thousand in there. And there's a new contract—whole parish, vermin, five years, whatever you want. And after the moon's set, none of this ever happened. You keep your cottage. You keep your quiet. You keep—" and here his voice did something gentle and terrible, "—you keep your name, Hazel. Whatever it really is."

The lane was absolutely silent. Sixty people listening to a bribe with the attention of congregants.

And Hazel understood, standing in his own doorway, that this was the offer his father had accepted in 1974. Not money. *Belonging.* The warm weight of being inside the circle, of the lane nodding to you, of the village holding you the way a fist holds a coin. His father had taken a child's name and been given a life, a wife, a son, a place—and had carried the shoe in a box for forty years because the fist never fully opens.

"I know what you're offering," Hazel said. "I've seen it before. It came with a shoe in it."

Verrity's smile flickered—the first hairline crack in thirty years of practiced geniality—and Hazel watched it and felt no triumph at all, only a vast tired grief.

"I'm going to give them back," he said. "All of it. Her name, her shoes, her voice. Tomorrow night, when the moon finishes. And you can't stop me, because half of you—" he raised his voice, not shouting, just letting it carry down the lane, "—half of you *want* to remember. I've seen your faces at the bell. I've heard you humming the hymn in your sleep. You've been waiting fifty years for someone to take this off you."

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. The envelope hung in the air between them, and then Verrity withdrew it, slowly, folding it away like a man putting a knife back in a drawer.

"That's a pity," he said, and meant it, and that was the worst thing yet.

* * *

He shut the door. He bolted it. He stood with his back against the wood and his heart going like a trapped bird, and he waited for the shouting, the stones, the splintering.

It didn't come. What came instead was light.

It began at the edges of the curtains—a glow like a furnace door ajar—and Hazel turned and saw the handprints blooming across his windows from the outside, pressing through the glass the way frost presses through a pane. Five fingers each. Small ones. A child's hands, dozens of them, dozens and dozens, appearing one after another across every window in the cottage, searing themselves into the glass from the air itself, and through them he could see the lane: the crowd had not moved, had not raised a hand, had only *watched*, sixty faces lit orange by the prints' slow burn, and not one of them looked afraid. They looked like people watching a weather front come in. They looked like people who had seen it before.

The heat came through the glass and touched his face. Above his head, in the bedroom wall, the scratching had stopped.

And then, from the direction of the church, clear across the dark fields, the melted bell began to ring—though it was not yet midnight, though the moon was still climbing, though every record said it had been scrap metal for a hundred and thirteen years—and with the first stroke Hazel felt something loosen in his chest, some thread he hadn't known was knotted there, and he understood with perfect clarity that the prints on his windows were not a threat.

They were an invitation. *Come out. Come now. The water's waiting, and it's rising to meet the moon.*

He picked up the box. His hands were shaking, and the name tag sat on top—WREN—and he said it aloud, once, to the empty burning room, in the voice he'd been given it in.

Then he opened the door, and the crowd parted down the middle of the lane like a wound.
