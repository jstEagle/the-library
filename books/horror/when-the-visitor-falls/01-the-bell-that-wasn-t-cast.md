## 1. The Bell That Wasn't Cast

The bus let Theo off at the bottom of Hollowmere Hill at half past three in the afternoon, and the village did not so much appear as exhale itself out of the mist — a scatter of grey stone roofs, the dark spine of the church, and beyond it the long green shoulder of the mere, flat as hammered pewter. Forty-one years since he had last stood on this road. He was nine years old when he left, carried away in his aunt's Morris Minor with his few belongings in a biscuit tin, and he had spent every year since telling himself that what he remembered of this place was a child's invention: too green, too quiet, too full of listening.

It was worse than he remembered. It was exactly as he remembered.

He walked up the hill with the recorder bag heavy against his hip and the microphone case knocking his knee, past the well-house — still there, still squatting at the lane's bend like a toad that had learned patience — and felt the old cold move through him, the one he had carried since 1974 without ever once naming it aloud. Not here. Not yet.

* * *

Mrs. Ada Crick lived in the last cottage before the churchyard wall, in a room that smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender and something older underneath, something like the inside of a stone. She was eighty-nine and nearly blind, her eyes gone the colour of skimmed milk, but she found Theo's face with her hands the moment he sat down, cupping his jaw like a woman checking fruit for ripeness.

"Marchetti's boy," she said. "You've come back for the songs."

"For whatever's left of them," Theo said. "Before they're gone."

"Nothing's gone." She smiled, and her gums were pink and toothless and terrible. "Everything here is kept."

He set up the recorder on the table between them, the little red level-light glowing, and asked her about lullabies. She went still in a way that made him think of animals deciding whether to run. Then, slowly, she began to sing.

Her voice was ruined and beautiful, a thread pulled through a needle's eye, and the song was one Theo had never heard anywhere in thirty years of fieldwork — a descending line, five notes falling like water down steps, with words that were less words than shapes of words, worn smooth by mouths until only their shadows remained. Something about going down. Something about being called and answering. The hair rose along Theo's arms, and the professional part of him — the part that had archived four hundred hours of dying music across three counties — thought, with a cold thrill: *this is the oldest thing I have ever recorded.*

She sang it twice through. On the second pass, her blind eyes turned toward the window, toward the churchyard, and her voice thinned to almost nothing, and the last note came out shaped like a name.

Then her hand shot out and closed over the recorder, surprisingly strong, and pressed the stop.

"Delete it," she said.

"Mrs. Crick—"

"You'll delete it or you'll leave my house and never come back, and I'll know if you've kept it, boy, I'll *know*, we all know things here." Her milk-pale eyes were fixed on him with awful precision. "That song isn't mine to give. It never belonged to us. We just — we keep it fed, is all. We keep it sung so it stays where it is."

"Where it is?" Theo asked. "Where is it?"

Ada Crick took her hands off the machine and folded them in her lap like a good child at a funeral. "Delete it," she said again, gently now, almost kindly, "and then go and ask Edith Hale about the bell. She's the one who owes. She'll tell you what I won't."

Theo deleted the file while she watched with those sightless eyes, and the little screen said FILE DELETED, and he understood, with a lurch, that he had memorized all five notes anyway, that his own skull was now an archive no one could wipe. His hands were shaking. He told himself it was the tea, which was bitter and over-stewed, and drank it anyway because refusing hospitality in Hollowmere felt suddenly, unaccountably dangerous.

* * *

He walked out at dusk.

The light was doing something it should not have been able to do — coming up gold from under the mist instead of fading down into it, the whole valley lit from beneath as though the mere held a lamp. The harvest moon would be full in three nights; he'd checked the almanac on the train, some folklorist's reflex, and now the number sat in him like a swallowed stone.

Three nights.

He stood at the churchyard gate. St. Brannoc's was roofless, its tower a broken tooth against the sky, abandoned since before the war — everyone knew the parish had consolidated down in Lower Hollowmere, everyone knew the old church was left to the ivy and the jackdaws. And everyone knew, because it was in the county history Theo had read on the train with mounting unease, that the great bell of St. Brannoc's had been taken down and melted in 1911, its bronze sold to fund the new roof that never got finished. There was no bell. There had been no bell for a hundred and fourteen years. The tower held nothing but pigeons and weather.

At the moment the light failed — not gradually, but all at once, like a candle pinched — the bell rang.

One deep stroke, bronze-dark, rolling out over the fields and the mere and the low hills, a sound with weight and grain to it, a sound Theo felt in his back teeth and in the floor of his pelvis. Then silence. Then another stroke. Slow. Unhurried. Patient. Nine strokes, and Theo counted them the way you count anything when counting is the only thing keeping you upright, and after the ninth the silence came back so completely that the ringing might have been a hallucination, a trick of blood and memory.

Except.

Except that down in the lane, Mrs. Pym was standing at her gate with her shopping basket on her arm, motionless, head bowed. Except that two men outside the King's Head had stopped mid-sentence, caps in their hands, eyes fixed on nothing. Except that a woman walking a dog had knelt in the road, one hand pressed flat to the tarmac, and stayed there, kneeling, until the last stroke died.

No one looked up. No one looked at each other. No one said a word.

And Theo stood at the gate of a roofless church with no bell and felt the dread move through him like cold water through sand — and underneath the dread, shamefully, unmistakably, something else. Because the sound had been *home*. The sound had been his mother calling him in from the fields at dusk, had been the smell of bread and the tick of the parlour clock, had been every safe evening of his first nine years compressed into bronze and thrown across the valley. His body knew that bell. His body answered it the way a dog answers its name, and he hated it, and he could not stop it.

That was the betrayal, he thought, standing in the blue dark while the villagers filed silently indoors around him like figures in a pageant of grief. Not the bell. Not whatever slept under the fields and rang its melted throat across the dusk. The betrayal was that after everything — after the well-house, after the thing he had seen and sworn never to see, after forty-one years of careful forgetting — Hollowmere still fit him. Still knew his shape. He had built a life elsewhere, a career, a marriage, a daughter who called on Sundays, and all of it sat on top of this place the way a lid sits on a well, and tonight the bell had rung and the lid had trembled and Theo had felt, for one vertiginous second, that he had never left at all.

A door closed softly behind him. Edith Hale stood in her garden gateway across the lane, small and straight-backed in the last grey light, ninety years old and looking at him across forty-one years with eyes that had waited a long time for exactly this.

"You counted nine," she said. Not a question.

Theo's mouth was dry. "There's no bell in that tower."

"No," Edith Hale agreed. "There isn't." She opened her gate. "Come in, Theo Marchetti. You've got three nights, and there's a debt on this village older than your grief, and I'm the last one left who remembers how it was incurred. Come in, and I'll tell you what we took."
