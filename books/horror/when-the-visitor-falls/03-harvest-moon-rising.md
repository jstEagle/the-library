## 3. Harvest Moon Rising

The harvest moon rose the color of a peeled egg, and Theo Marchetti stood at his window in the old schoolhouse watching it climb over Hollowmere's crooked rooftops, and knew — the way you know a tooth is going to ache before it does — that tonight was the night.

He had spent three days with Edith Hale's confession sitting in him like swallowed glass. *We took it from the well-house. We named it Thomas and we loved it, God forgive us, we loved it so hard we never once said its true name aloud.* Three days of parish records spread across the kitchen table, three days of ink-faded entries that didn't add up: a baptism dated the ninth of October 1974 for a child whose birth was recorded nowhere, whose godparents were half the village, whose christening feast had been the largest anyone could remember. A village celebrating a theft. A village singing over the hole where a child used to be.

And beneath all of it, faint as a watermark, the song. The one Mrs. Alderby had begged him to delete. The lullaby with the second verse no one would sing, the verse that went down instead of up, that named the thing under the fields instead of blessing the child above them. He had transcribed it from memory that first night, hands shaking, and had not played the recording since.

The bell began at moonrise.

Not the church bell — there was no church bell, hadn't been since 1911 when they'd melted it down for the war effort and gotten, by every account, nothing but slag and a smell like burnt hair. This bell lived somewhere lower. It came up through the floorboards, through the soles of Theo's feet, through the water glass on the sill trembling into rings. Each toll was slow and vast and patient, the sound of something that had waited fifty years and could wait fifty more if it must.

Theo picked up the recorder out of habit and then set it down again. Some things you didn't record. Some things you answered.

Outside, Hollowmere was pretending again. That was the village's oldest art form, older than its songs: curtains drawn to exactly the width that let light show but not faces, televisions turned up loud enough to be heard but not believed, dogs shut inside so they couldn't howl at what they smelled rising out of the ground along the processional path — the old track that ran from the ruined churchyard down past the well-house, worn into the earth by centuries of feet that had once carried candles on this same night, this same moon, before everyone agreed to forget why.

Theo put on his coat. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Underneath the steadiness was a feeling he refused to name until he was out the door and walking, and then it named itself: *homecoming*. Fifty years and the village still fit him like a hand fits its own shadow, and that was the betrayal, wasn't it — that you could be complicit in a place's silence and still love the shape of its streets.

The processional path was open.

Not broken — *open*, the turf folded back like the pages of a book someone had pressed flat for half a century and now released. The soil underneath was black and soft as loam in a coffin, and it breathed. Theo could see it breathing, a slow swell and fall along the whole length of the path, from the churchyard gate down toward the well-house, in time with the bell.

It was standing at his door when he got back.

Small. That was the first wrongness — after fifty years it should have been grown, should have been old, should have been anything but *small*, a child of perhaps seven standing barefoot on the threshold with the harvest moon pouring over it like milk. Its skin was the grey-white of things raised in the dark, candle-wax skin, and its eyes were enormous and dark and wet, and its face —

Its face was familiar. That was the worst of it. Theo had been seven too, in 1974, sworn companion for one summer to a quiet child who lived near the well-house and hummed songs no one taught him, and Theo had watched the men come with a lantern and a blanket, and had said nothing, had said nothing for fifty years, had built an entire life on top of the saying-nothing the way Hollowmere had built itself on top of the thing below.

"You're the one who remembers," the child said. Its voice was the bell's voice, pitched small. "I've been listening to you listen."

"I'm sorry." The words came out of Theo cracked and ancient, older than he was. "I'm sorry. I watched. I didn't—"

"I know what you did." No anger in it. That was somehow worse than anger. "I know everything you did. I felt every year of it, down here, while they sang the first verse over my stolen name." It held out one wax-pale hand. "Sing me home. Sing the whole song. The second verse is mine."

Behind him, down the lane, torchlight. Voices. The elders coming up the street in their good coats with their grim faces, Alderby's son at their head holding a shovel the way his father had held one in 1974, and Theo understood with terrible clarity what they intended. Not violence, exactly. Something more respectable. A silence, offered again, renewed for another fifty years — feed it another name, another borrowed child's worth of forgetting, keep the ledger balanced in the currency the village had always paid in.

"It can't stay," Alderby's son said, stopping at the gate. He wouldn't look at the child directly; none of them would. They looked at Theo, at the door, at the moon, anywhere else. "It's not right, Theo. It's not natural. We put it where it belongs and it stays where it belongs, that's the arrangement, that's always been the arrangement—"

"The arrangement was theft," Theo said.

"The arrangement was *survival*—"

"Then survive differently." Theo looked down at the child, at the offered hand, and took it. Cold as well-water, cold as 1974, and yet — clinging, trusting, the grip of a seven-year-old who had decided, against all evidence, to trust him one more time. "I know the song. All of it. And I'm going to sing it where it should have been sung fifty years ago, in the churchyard, under the bell, with the whole village hearing or without them."

"You'll bring it up," the old woman at the back of the group whispered, and she wasn't warning him. She was pleading. "You'll call the rest of it up, boy. There's more down there than him. There always was."

"Then it's time Hollowmere met its neighbors."

He walked the child up the processional path with the elders falling back on either side like water around a stone, and the black earth breathed beneath their feet, and the bell tolled faster now, eager, and the moon climbed higher and turned from white to a deep bruised gold, and Theo found he was humming the lullaby's first verse as they walked, the safe verse, the one about sleep and shelter, and the child's cold hand tightened around his fingers and its voice joined his, thin and clear and terribly sweet, a voice that had not been used in fifty years and remembered everything.

The ruined churchyard received them. Roofless walls, gravestones leaning like bad teeth, the empty belfry overhead with its melted ghost of a bell — and below, below, below, the tolling that had no source and every source. Theo set the child down on the altar step, the one place in Hollowmere that had never been lied from, and opened his mouth, and sang the first verse loud enough to reach the farthest field.

Then he drew breath, and crossed the line no one had crossed since before the melting, and began the second verse.

The bell went wild.

* * *

It was not a sound anymore; it was a weather. The tolling broke apart into clanging, into shrieking bronze, into a rhythm like something hammering to get out of a room, and the black earth of the processional path heaved once, twice, like a sleeper turning over, and every dog in Hollowmere began to howl at once, and still Theo sang.

The second verse did not bless. The second verse *named*. It named the well-house and the lantern and the blanket; it named the ninth of October and the feast and the borrowed name; it named every silent witness, himself first among them, *I watched, I said nothing, I am the silence, hear me*; and as he sang it the words came up out of the ground to meet him, rising through the soles of his feet and out of his throat in a language half his own and half much older, the song teaching itself to him faster than he could learn it, because it had been waiting down there all along for one voice foolish enough and guilty enough to start it.

The child on the altar step was singing too, eyes closed, wax-pale face tilted to the moon, and where its voice touched the air the air went silver.

At the churchyard gate, the elders stood in a knot, and Alderby's son raised the shovel — and lowered it, because the old woman beside him had begun, involuntarily, horribly, to hum.

Down the processional path, the black earth split wide as a mouth.

And out of the singing, out of the silver, the child opened its eyes and looked at Theo with the moon swimming in them, and said, between verses, in the bell's patient voice:

"They'll have to sing too. One voice isn't enough. It was never enough. That's why it failed, fifty years ago — one voice, one witness, one silence." Its cold hand found Theo's and gripped. "Get them to sing with you, Theo Marchetti, or we both go down, and the village keeps its quiet forever."
