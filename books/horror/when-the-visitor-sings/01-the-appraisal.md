## 1. The Appraisal

The house had gained a door.

Cyrus Crane noticed it the way he noticed most things: by counting. He stood on the front porch at 7:42 in the morning, clipboard against his hip, and ran his thumb along the edge of the surveyor's pencil he'd owned since his first job out of college—a yellow Eberhard Faber, sharpened down to a stub, the wood dark where his sweat had soaked into it over eleven years. He counted the doors facing the drive the way his father had taught him before the funeral, before the hospice, before all of it. Front door. Kitchen door. Cellar hatch. Garage. The two French doors off the sunroom. The little door under the back stairs that everyone pretended was a closet.

Seven. Same as always.

But that wasn't the count that mattered. The count that mattered was inside, and he wouldn't have it for another hour yet, and the whole drive out from the city—ninety minutes of wet highway and radio static—he'd been rehearsing the ritual the way other men rehearsed apologies.

*Lights on. Doors counted. Then work.*

He let himself in with the key his mother had mailed him in a padded envelope, the kind of key that had been sitting in a drawer so long it had forgotten doors. The lock turned like it had been oiled yesterday. That was the first wrong thing, though he didn't log it as one. Grandmother had been dead nine weeks. Houses that sit empty nine weeks do not have smooth locks.

Inside, the air was cool and still and smelled of lavender and, beneath it, something older—well water, stone, the mineral breath of a cellar. Cyrus did what he always did. He walked the ground floor and switched on every lamp. The parlor lamp with the fringed shade. The brass floor lamp by the reading chair. The kitchen overhead, which buzzed like it was arguing with itself. The hall sconces, one after another, a small procession of light marching ahead of him into the dimmer parts of the house. His father had called it a superstition. His father had also, Cyrus remembered now, never once turned off a light in his own house before bed. Some inheritances skip the will and go straight into the blood.

Then the doors.

He started at the front hall and moved clockwise, pencil ticking against the clipboard, one mark per door. Front. Kitchen. Pantry. Under-stairs. Cellar. Cellar again—no, that was the old coal chute, but it had a door on it, so it counted; his system was strict about that, strictness being the entire point. Sunroom, two. Hall bath. Upstairs he went, the stairs complaining in the third step the way they always had, and counted the bedrooms, the linen closet, the attic hatch with its rope pull. Eleven.

He wrote it down. *Eleven doors.* He wrote the time. He wrote the date. He wrote the weather, because his grandmother had taught him that a record without conditions was a rumor.

Then he went back downstairs to fetch his laser measure from the car, and on his way back up, passing the linen closet, he counted again without meaning to. It was a reflex, like blinking. Front, kitchen, pantry, under-stairs, cellar, chute, sunroom, sunroom, bath, bedroom, bedroom, bedroom, closet, attic.

Fourteen. No. He'd double-counted the bedrooms. He started over, slower, touching each frame with two fingers the way his father had taught him, *feel the wood, boy, the eyes lie but the hand doesn't.*

Twelve.

Cyrus stood in the upstairs hallway with the laser measure humming faintly in his hand and made himself breathe. There were four bedrooms. There had always been four bedrooms. He walked them. Four. He walked the hall. One bath. One closet. One attic hatch. That was seven upstairs, plus the five downstairs, plus the front and the kitchen and—no. He was doing it wrong. He went back to the very beginning, to the front door, and counted the whole house in one continuous line, no segments, no categories, just doors, in the order his body met them.

Twelve.

He sat down on the top step. The laser measure's little screen glowed in the dim hall, waiting for him to point it at something. Below, through the floor, he could hear the house making its small empty sounds—the tick of cooling pipes that had no water in them, the settle of a structure holding its breath.

*You counted wrong the first time,* he told himself. *You were tired. You drove ninety minutes. You counted eleven when it was twelve all along.*

That was the professional explanation, and he was a professional, and he took it, the way you take a free mint, because it was there.

* * *

The appraisal of an estate property, Cyrus had told his mother on the phone, was mostly arithmetic wearing a suit. You measured the rooms. You noted the fixtures. You catalogued what the estate contained so the county could decide what the family owed for the privilege of having had it. He'd done two hundred of these houses—farmhouses, colonials, one lighthouse keeper's cottage that had made him uneasy for reasons he'd never written down—and the work had a rhythm that steadied him. Room by room. Number by number. The world, reduced to a form he could fill out.

He started in the kitchen, because kitchens were honest. He measured the counters, the window over the sink, the pantry—*pantry door, that's one, that's on the list, that's accounted for.* The morning light came in gray through the east windows and made the linoleum look like pond ice. On the windowsill sat his grandmother's watering can, still full. Nine weeks, and the water in it had not evaporated, had not gone cloudy, had not grown the skin of algae that standing water grows. It sat there clear as a held breath.

He did not write that down either. His clipboard was developing a habit of omissions, and he was beginning to notice the habit, which was worse than the omissions.

By ten o'clock he had the ground floor measured and was working through the parlor inventory—settee, walnut, fair condition; writing desk, cherry, good; photograph albums, four, contents unexamined—when he heard the sound.

It came from above. Not a footstep. A sound like a drawer sliding open, slow, in a house where every drawer was already open because he'd opened them. Then a sound like a drawer sliding shut.

Cyrus stood in the parlor with the inventory sheet in his hand and listened the way he'd been trained to listen, which is to say he counted the seconds between sounds. Four seconds. Then the house was quiet, and the quiet had a texture to it, a held quality, like a classroom when the teacher turns her back to the board.

He went upstairs. He checked every room. He checked the closet, the attic hatch, the space behind the water heater where a man could stand if a man were narrow and patient. Nothing. He stood in the hall with his pencil and counted the doors again, because counting was the only prayer he'd ever been taught.

Twelve.

*But which twelve?* The thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome, and it was the right thought, the professional thought, the thought a good surveyor asks about any measurement that won't repeat: *which twelve, in what order, and what changed?* He had counted eleven first. Then twelve. A house did not gain a door. A house was a fixed object. That was the whole premise of his profession, the load-bearing wall of his entire life: that things stayed put, that a wall measured today would measure the same tomorrow, that the world could be trusted to hold its number.

He went back downstairs and turned on the two lamps he'd turned off to save the bulbs, and then, for no reason he could defend, he turned on the porch light too, and the light over the garage, and stood in the front hall watching the windows go pale with the reflection of all that illumination, the house lit up like a vessel on display.

* * *

He broke for lunch at one, sitting on the front steps with a sandwich from the cooler in his trunk, and made himself review the morning's numbers like a man checking his own pulse. Eleven, then twelve. A sound like a drawer. Water that wouldn't age. None of it went on the appraisal form. The appraisal form wanted square footage, year of construction, condition of roof. The form did not have a field for *the house is counting me back.*

Because that was the feeling, and he finally let himself think it in full, sitting on the cold steps with the wet grass breathing against his ankles. All morning he had been measuring the house—every wall, every window, every fixture entered and valued—and the whole time he'd had the creeping sense, rising through him like cold through a floorboard, that the measurement was mutual. That somewhere in this house a ledger was open, and a pencil very like his own was moving down a column, and his footsteps were being marked the way he marked doors: *one, two, three, the tall one, the one with the tired eyes, the one who turns on the lights.*

He laughed at himself. It came out wrong, flat, swallowed by the yard. Then he went back inside, because the alternative was driving home, and he had a job to do, and besides—this was the thought he didn't examine—besides, the house had his name on the work order now. His name, his handwriting, his signature on the intake form. *Crane, C., surveyor of record.* It felt, suddenly, less like a credential than an entry in something.

* * *

The afternoon he spent upstairs, measuring bedrooms, and it was in the smallest bedroom—the one that had been his, summers, with the horse wallpaper he'd outgrown but the house never had—that he found the album. It was on the closet shelf, wrapped in a pillowcase, and it wasn't one of the four he'd catalogued in the parlor. This was a fifth album, and it was older, the cover soft as ear leather, and inside were photographs of people he didn't know: stiff families on porches, men with hats, women whose faces had gone the silver-gray of old film. He turned the pages with the care he'd give a boundary marker.

Every photograph had a chair in it.

Not in the pictures—*beside* them, at the edge of every frame, half in and half out, a plain wooden chair with a straight back. Different yards, different decades, different families, and always, at the margin of the image, that chair. He went back to the first page. Chair. He went to the last. Chair. He held one photograph up to the window light and looked behind it, as if the chair might be a stain, a flaw in the print. It wasn't. It was in the negative. It had been there when the picture was taken, or it had been there before the picture, waiting.

He checked the parlor albums afterward, all four, page by page, and found the family he did know—his mother young, Dana gap-toothed, himself at eight with a lizard held up in both hands—and there, at the edge of the birthday photo, at the edge of the Christmas photo, at the edge of the photo of Grandma in her garden, stood the chair. Plain wood. Straight back. Empty.

He had looked at these albums a hundred times in his life. He had *catalogued* them that morning. *Photograph albums, four, contents unexamined.*

Contents unexamined. He wrote the words again on his clipboard and then crossed them out so hard the pencil tore the paper.

Outside, the light was going. He walked the ground floor turning on lamps that were already on, and when he reached the hall he stopped, because the hall had a door in it that he had not counted that morning, a narrow door between the pantry and the under-stairs, painted the same white as the wall, with no handle that he could see. It had not been there at seven forty-two in the morning. He was nearly sure. He was as sure as a man can be whose whole life rests on the assumption that walls keep their promises.

Cyrus stood in front of the handleless door with his stub of pencil and did the only thing that had ever made the world hold still. He counted. Front, kitchen, pantry, the new one, under-stairs, cellar, chute, sunroom, sunroom, bath, bedrooms, closet, attic.

Thirteen.

He wrote it down. He wrote the time, 6:15 p.m. He wrote the conditions: *overcast, wind rising, house lit throughout.* And then, because his hand was steady in the way that hands go steady right before they shake, he added a line the appraisal form had no column for, in his smallest, most controlled handwriting:

*The house is not gaining doors. The house is gaining me.*

Upstairs, faintly, from a room he had measured and found empty, something slid shut like a drawer.
