## 10. The Verse Half-Learned

Odile Vesper had spent seventy-three years learning how to wait, and she had grown expert at it the way other women grew expert at knitting or gardening — patiently, and with a private bitterness no one was meant to see. She waited for the kettle. She waited for the doctor's letters, which arrived thinner each season. She waited for the house to breathe, because it always breathed, and pretending otherwise had never once made it stop.

But she had never waited for anything like this: a pest controller sitting in her grandmother's parlor, dust on their sleeves, looking at her grey leather hymnal as though it were a snake that might yet be charmed.

"Say it again," Wren said. "The part about singing."

Odile folded her hands in her lap. Her knuckles had gone the color of candle wax, and she was aware — distantly, the way one is aware of weather — that she was running out of time to be delicate about any of this. The diagnosis had given her months. The house had given her less.

"I'll say all of it," she said. "But you'll want to sit down, and you'll want to not interrupt, because if I stop in the middle I won't start again. That's the nature of this particular story. It only tolerates being told whole."

Wren sat. They were younger than she'd expected her heir to be — younger than she'd ever allowed herself to picture — and they had their mother's jaw, though Odile doubted they knew it yet. That jaw had sat across from Odile at this same table once, forty years ago, set at the same mutinous angle, and had walked out the same door Odile was now afraid Wren would use.

She began at the beginning, because the beginning was the only place the story still made sense.

* * *

"In 1798," she said, "my great-great-great-grandmother's family built this house around something they found in the ground. I don't know what they were doing — digging a well, a cellar, a grave, the records disagree, and the records were written by people who wanted them to disagree. What I know is that they found it, and it was asleep, and it sang in its sleep. Not with a mouth. With the ground. With the water in the ground. A song like a hum you feel in your back teeth."

Wren's face was carefully blank. Odile had seen that face before, on every Vesper who had ever sat where Wren sat. It was the face of a person deciding how much of what they heard to file under madness.

"The founders understood, eventually, what it was. Not what it *was* — no one has ever known that — but what it *did*. It fed on inheritance. On songs passed down. On the particular love that lives in a melody a mother teaches a child. It grew fat on that. And when it grew fat, it began to wake, and when it began to wake, things happened in Hollow Creek that the town has very carefully forgotten. Children who sang themselves hoarse and then stopped speaking altogether. Whole nights when every dog in the township faced the same direction. My family's records call it the Hungry Year, and then the pages after that are burned, deliberately, by someone who thought burning was kinder than remembering."

"And your family stopped it," Wren said. "How?"

"By learning its song." Odile let that sit. "That's the cruelty of it, you see. It sleeps to its own music. Someone must sing it *to* it — the verse it hums beneath the ground, the one it dreams to — and sing it faithfully, completely, generation after generation, or it stirs. We call it the keeping-song. It has four verses. The first three are written in this book, in my grandmother's hand, and her grandmother's corrections beneath, and hers above. The fourth verse was never written down."

"Never?"

"Never. It lives in a singer's memory and passes mouth to ear, one keeper at a time. My mother learned it from her mother in this room. I learned it from my mother on the cellar stairs, when I was nine years old, and I have sung it every night since I was seventeen. Twice through, every night. Once for the house and once for the ground beneath it. Sixty years of nights, Mr. — " she caught herself, and smiled, and the smile cost her something. "Wren. Sixty years of nights, Wren. You get to know a song that way. You get to know it the way you know your own pulse."

The light through the parlor window had gone the color of weak tea. Odile watched Wren's hands — they were a person who listened with their hands, she was learning, fingers curled loosely on their knees like a man holding reins — and she saw the exact moment the skepticism began to crack, not because of her words but because of the house. The floorboards had settled, as they always did at dusk, with a long exhalation that was almost, but not quite, a sigh.

"You felt that," she said.

"The house settles."

"It settles," Odile agreed. "And sometimes it agrees with you, to keep you comfortable. That's the other thing about it. It's not cruel, whatever the stories say. It's patient, and it's hungry, and it is *courteous*. Those are worse. Cruelty you can hate. Courtesy you start to need."

* * *

She rose, slowly — the illness had taken her knees first, then her breath, and it was working through the rest of her like a careful tenant — and led Wren down the hall to the cellar door. She counted the doors aloud as she went, because she always counted the doors aloud, and she heard Wren's footsteps falter slightly at the sound, and then join in, unasked, on *nine* and *ten* and *eleven*.

Eleven. There should have been ten. There had been ten for two hundred years, and then, three months ago, there had been eleven, and Odile had not told a soul because there was no soul in Hollow Creek left to tell.

The cellar stairs were stone and cold. She went down them one at a time, one hand on the rail her grandfather had polished with sixty years of the same grip, and at the bottom she lit the lamp that hung on its nail, and she showed Wren the wall.

It was an ordinary wall, if one did not look too long. Fieldstone, mortar gone dark with age, the damp stain in the corner that no amount of lime ever cured. But the stain was shaped, if one looked, like a mouth. And if one stood very still, in the hour after sundown, one could feel it — not hear, *feel* — a vibration in the stone like a note held by a throat too deep to imagine.

"That's where it is," Odile said. "That's where it has always been. And every night for sixty years, I have stood exactly here and sung the keeping-song into that stone, and it has stayed asleep, and Hollow Creek has stayed ignorant, and the world has stayed the shape the world is supposed to be. That is the covenant. That is all of it. We sing; it sleeps. No one thanks us. No one knows. The town traded its memory of the bargain for its safety before I was born, and I have made my peace with that, or something near enough to peace to keep my voice steady at night."

Wren was staring at the stain. Their face had changed — the professional blankness gone, replaced by something rawer, and Odile recognized it with a lurch: it was the face of someone hearing a melody they almost knew.

"You've heard it," she said quietly. "Already. Before today."

"I wake up humming," Wren said, and their voice was not steady at all. "Since the first night here. A tune I don't know. My — " They stopped.

"Your mother," Odile said. "Yes. I imagine you would."

* * *

They went back up to the parlor, because Odile's legs would not forgive her a long stay in the cold, and because what came next was better said with a lamp lit and a kettle on, the small domestic ceremonies that made terrible things survivable. She poured tea neither of them drank. She set the grey hymnal on the table between them, open to the last written page, where a child's careful hand had copied out the third verse's ending — *and down, and down, and down it keeps* — and beneath it, nothing. Blankness. The space where the fourth verse had lived for two hundred years in mouths alone.

"I am dying," she said. It was easier, she found, to say it to this near-stranger than it had been to say it to her doctor, or to the mirror, or to God. "The disease has a long Latin name and a short schedule. Months. Fewer, if the winter is hard. And when I go, the song goes with me, unless — "

"No," Wren said.

Odile had expected it. She had rehearsed her answers to it in the bath, in the garden, in the long white hours of three in the morning. But hearing it aloud still landed like a stone dropped down a well — she listened for it to hit bottom and it never did.

"You haven't heard the rest."

"I don't need to. You want me to learn it. To stand in that cellar every night for the rest of my life, singing into a wall, for a town that won't even say your name above a whisper. You want me to inherit — " Wren's hands had come up, gesturing at the room, the house, the whole suffocating weight of it. "This. Whatever this is. I clear wasp nests, Mrs. Vesper. I unplug drains. I am not — I didn't agree to this. I don't even believe half of it, and the half I believe, I want no part of."

"Then believe the part you've already heard," Odile said, and her voice came out gentler than she intended, which surprised her, because beneath the gentleness was a grief so old and so large she had built her whole life on top of it like a house on a grave. "Believe the fingerprints on the glass. Believe the doors. Believe the tune you wake up humming. It already knows your name, Wren. It said it on the tape. It doesn't say names for nothing. It says names the way a fisherman baits a hook — because it has decided what it wants, and it is very, very patient about getting it."

"Then I'll stay away from the house."

"It isn't the house."

"Then I'll leave Hollow Creek."

Something in Odile's chest went tight and cold. She had left, once — no. Her mother's sister had left. The erased daughter. The one whose name had been struck through in different ink. Odile had been a child when the scandal happened, and had understood none of it, and had understood all of it by the time she was old enough to stand on these stairs and sing. Running had not saved that woman. It had only delayed the accounting, and passed the debt to her child.

"Leaving doesn't work," Odile said. "I have two hundred years of family records that say leaving doesn't work. It waits. It is *made* of waiting. And when it gets tired of waiting, it stops being courteous, and I promise you — " her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the weakness in it, hated the dying body that produced it — "I promise you that you do not want to learn what it's like when it stops being courteous."

"Then what do you want from me?" Wren was on their feet now, and the parlor felt smaller, the lamplight thinner. "You want me to say yes? To kneel down in your cellar and take vows? I have a life. It's small, but it's mine. I didn't ask for — whatever this bloodline thing is. I didn't ask to be related to you."

"No," Odile said. "You didn't. Neither did I. Neither did any of us, back to the woman who first heard it singing in the ground and made the worst bargain in the history of this county. That's what inheritance *is*, Wren. It's the bill that arrives before the meal. Nobody asks for it. We are only asked what we do when it comes."

She watched them stand there, breathing hard, and she saw — God help her, she saw — her mother in them, and her grandmother, and the whole long line of reluctant singers stretching back into the burned pages of the record. And she saw something else too, something newer: the exact shape of the refusal that had made her aunt run, forty years ago, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, that she was watching the covenant die. Not in a year. Not in months. In the next sixty seconds, if she could not find the words.

There were no words. There had never been any words. There was only the song, and the willingness, and one of those she could give and the other she could not.

"Sing the first lines," she said. "That's all I'm asking. Not a vow. Not an inheritance. Just — learn how it starts. So that if the day comes when you have no choice, you aren't starting from nothing. Please." The word cracked in the middle. "I have never in my life begged anyone for anything. I am begging you."

Wren stood in the doorway for a long moment. The house held its breath — Odile could feel it holding its breath, all eleven doors and every wall between them, the whole patient architecture of two centuries of duty leaning in to hear.

And then, to her astonishment, Wren came back. Sat down. Pulled the hymnal closer with one finger, careful, as if it might bite.

"The first lines," they said. "That's all. And then I'm going home."

Odile's hands were shaking as she turned the pages. She told herself it was the illness. She knew it wasn't. It was hope, and hope was the one thing she had trained herself out of for seventy years, and it had come back at the worst possible moment, wearing the face of a pest controller who had already, in their heart, said no.

She sang.

She sang the opening of the keeping-song — four notes descending, then three held, then the long falling phrase like water finding its way down — and she watched Wren's face as the melody landed in them and found, as she had known it would, as it always did with the bloodline, something already there waiting for it. Their lips parted. Their breathing changed. On the fourth phrase, without seeming to decide to, they hummed — low, half a tone flat, but *there*, the shape of it correct, the inheritance waking up in their throat like a muscle remembering.

Odile stopped singing. She pressed her hand over her mouth.

"That's enough," Wren said, and their voice was strange, thick, frightened. "That's — I didn't mean to do that. Why did I know that? Why did I *know* it?"

"Because it's yours," Odile said. "It has always been yours. It was waiting for you the way the house waits. Wren — Wren, listen to me, that is the *first verse*, and you already carry it, and if you walk out that door — "

"Then I walk out that door." Wren was standing again, and there were tears on their face, and they were furious about the tears, she could see it, furious at her and at the song and at every dead Vesper who had ever passed this weight down like a hot stone from hand to hand. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry about your — I'm sorry about all of it. But I won't be the next one. I won't stand in a cellar every night until I die for something nobody chose. Find another way. Teach it to the ground. Teach it to God. But not me."

They went. She heard them count the doors on the way out — she heard their voice waver on *nine*, and steady itself by *eleven* — and then the front door closed, and the house exhaled, and Odile Vesper sat alone in her grandmother's parlor with an open hymnal and the fourth verse burning in her memory like a coal with nowhere to go.

She sat until the tea went cold. She sat until the lamplight was the only light, and the stain in the cellar began, faintly, to hum.

* * *

It was full dark when she heard the footsteps overhead.

Not Wren's — Wren had gone, she'd heard the truck start, heard it fade down the county road. These were slower. These were in the attic, where no one went, where the old things were kept: the ledgers, the burned pages' ashes in a biscuit tin, and the boxes of reel-to-reel tapes that her mother had recorded, night after night, year after year, singing to the dark so that there would be a record, so that someone, someday, would know they had tried.

The footsteps crossed the attic. Stopped. Something was being moved — a box dragged a few inches, the shriek of old floorboards.

Odile rose, took the lamp, and stood at the bottom of the attic stairs with her hand on the rail, and did not go up, because she was a keeper and keepers know which doors not to open after sundown.

"Who's there?" she said, and her voice, sixty years of nightly singing, did not shake.

The house answered her with silence — but it was a different silence than before, a listening silence, a silence with a shape. And from the attic above her, soft as a lullaby, something began to hum the first notes of the keeping-song.

Wrong. Slightly, horribly wrong. Sung in a voice she almost recognized, and could not, and would not.

Odile stood in the dark with her lamp and understood that she had run out of months.
