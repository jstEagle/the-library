## 1. The Wrong Sibling

The bus to Hollowmere ran once a day and arrived once a day, and both of those things happened at dusk, which Orsen Quintana had always suspected was not a coincidence but a policy.

They sat in the last row with the urn on their lap. It was heavier than they'd expected — heavier than a person should be after being reduced to what a person is reduced to — and it was warm in a way that made no sense, because the bus had been cold since the second hour and the urn had been sitting in an unheated cargo hold for most of that. But warm it was, faintly, like a stone left too near a fire, like the crown of a sleeping child's head. Orsen kept one hand flat on the lid and told themselves it was engine heat conducted through the seat frame, through their own body, back into the brass. There was no other explanation that didn't end somewhere they weren't ready to go.

The driver had asked them twice if they were all right back there.

"Fine," Orsen said, both times, and the second time the word came out with a small crack in it, and the driver's eyes found them in the mirror and stayed there a beat too long before returning to the road.

"You'll want to be off before the pond," the driver said, an hour later, apropos of nothing. "Road curves right along it. Some folk don't care for the view."

Orsen hadn't said anything about the pond. Hadn't said anything about Hollowmere at all beyond "one way" and "thank you." They looked out the window instead. The country unspooled wet and green, hedgerows running like stitches, and then the trees opened and there it was: the millpond, wide as a held breath, brown-gold in the failing light, so still it looked less like water than like glass laid over something that didn't want to be seen.

Something in Orsen's chest did a thing they had no name for. Not grief. Grief they knew; grief was the constant, the weather of the last three weeks. This was older and stranger — a recognition, almost, like passing a house you'd dreamed of and finding the dream had gotten the details wrong.

The urn warmed another degree under their palm.

*Stop that,* Orsen thought, and didn't say, because saying it aloud on a nearly empty bus felt like the kind of thing that got written down somewhere.

Hollowmere announced itself the way small villages do: a church spire first, crooked against the sky, then a scatter of roofs, then a road sign whose paint had been renewed recently enough to look embarrassed among its neighbors. THE VILLAGE OF HOLLOWMERE WELCOMES CAREFUL DRIVERS. Someone had scratched out *careful* and left the raw metal showing, and someone else had tried to fill the scratch with black marker, and the result read, if you squinted, WELCOMES DRIVERS, or possibly WELCOMES NOTHING.

Orsen got off before the pond, like the driver said. The doors hissed shut behind them and the bus pulled away, and the sound of the engine faded faster than it should have, swallowed whole, and then there was just Orsen standing on the verge with a duffel bag over one shoulder and their dead twin sister in their arms.

The village smelled like bread.

That was the first wrong thing, though it took Orsen a while to catalog it as wrong. Fresh bread, yeast and warmth, drifting from doorsteps and open windows, everywhere at once, at six in the evening in a village with no bakery that Orsen could find on any map. It was a good smell. It should have been comforting. Instead it sat in Orsen's nose like a question someone was waiting for them to answer.

The church stood at the head of the lane, and the graveyard lay behind it, and Orsen went there first because there was nowhere else to go and because some part of them — the part that had counted doors as a child, that checked locks twice and lights three times, that needed the world arranged before it could rest — wanted to see the plot before dark. Wanted the fact of it settled. Wren deserved a settled fact.

The grave was already dug.

Orsen stopped at the lychgate and stared. The hole gaped neat and rectangular in the soft earth, sides squared, spoil heaped beside it on a tarpaulin with the tidy precision of professionals. And at its head, already planted, already set, stood a headstone.

WREN QUINTANA
BELOVED TWIN
1994 – 2025

Not *beloved sister*. Not *daughter*. *Beloved twin*, carved in letters that had already weathered a season or two, moss starting in the grooves of the Q, lichen freckling the granite the way lichen takes years to do.

Orsen stood very still. A rook called somewhere. The bread smell drifted past, patient.

"That's new ground," said a voice behind them, warm as the bread. "We don't get many of those anymore."

The vicar was a broad, pink-faced man in a cassock gone gray at the cuffs, and he came through the lychgate like a man greeting family off a train, hand extended, smile creased deep. "You must be the survivor," he said, and shook Orsen's hand before Orsen could decide whether to offer it. His grip was dry and strong and slightly cold, like a hand that had been holding a windowpane. "Vicar Marsh. We've been expecting you such a long time."

"We've been—" Orsen heard their own voice come out thin. "I only confirmed the date last week. I only— she died three weeks ago. The cremation was Tuesday."

"Yes, yes." The vicar nodded along, kindly, the way you nod at someone repeating something well known. "And now you're here, and it's done, and doesn't it feel strange? It always feels strange. Coming back to a place after everything's settled." He turned to look at the grave with an expression of mild professional satisfaction, a man inspecting work finished ahead of schedule. "We dug her deep. She'd have wanted that, I think. You two always struck me as the sort who liked things deep."

Orsen's mouth was dry. "You knew Wren?"

"Oh, everyone knew Wren." He said it easily, fondly, and then something crossed his face — quick, gone before it could be named, a shadow moving under ice. "Everyone knew *both* of you."

"I've never been here in my life," Orsen said. "Neither of us has. Our mother was born here, maybe, we think, we never — the records are—" They stopped. Their voice had climbed somewhere embarrassing. The vicar watched them with that same kind patience, hands folded over his belly, and waited for them to finish the way you wait for weather.

"There now," he said, when Orsen ran out. "It's a lot, coming home."

"It isn't home."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Of course not."

He walked them through the churchyard then, because Orsen couldn't think of how to leave, and the vicar talked the whole way — names attached to stones, most of them Halloway, a few Quintana that made Orsen's skin prickle and which the vicar never once explained — and the whole time the light drained out of the sky and the bread smell thickened until it was almost a taste at the back of the throat.

At the gate he paused.

"You'll be staying at the cottage, then. Number four, Mill Lane. It's been kept ready." He smiled. "Your key is where it always was."

"I don't have a key."

"You will," said Vicar Marsh, "by the time you need one," and he patted Orsen's shoulder twice, gently, like tamping down soil, and went back inside the churchyard, and closed the gate behind him without looking back.

* * *

Number four, Mill Lane, was exactly where the letter said it would be, and the key was exactly where the vicar promised: hanging on a nail inside the lean-to by the back door, on a loop of twine, furred with dust except for the bright clean circle where fingers had recently gripped it.

Orsen stood in the lean-to for a long moment with the key in their fist.

The cottage was small and low-beamed and smelled of cold ash and lavender, and it was, unmistakably, prepared. Bed made. Kettle filled. A jar of wildflowers on the table — late-season, slightly wilted, cut within the day. On the hearth, someone had laid a fire ready to light, kindling stacked in the exact chevron pattern their mother used, the pattern Orsen had never shown anyone, the pattern Orsen had assumed died with her.

On the kitchen step, wrapped in a cloth still warm from an oven, sat a loaf of bread.

No note. No knock. No footprints in the soft ground of the yard, though the flowerbeds were churned and soft enough to hold them.

Orsen carried the loaf inside, set it on the counter, and stood looking at it. It was a good loaf. Crust scored with three slashes. Their mother had scored her loaves with three slashes. Everyone scored loaves with three slashes; it meant nothing; it was how bread was scored.

They put it in the cupboard, unopened, and told themselves they simply weren't hungry.

Then they unpacked, because ritual was the only rope they had left. Duffel on the bed. Clothes in the dresser. Toiletries on the shelf above the basin, arranged left to right in order of use. The urn they placed on the mantelpiece, centered, facing the room, because Wren had never liked having her back to a door.

The moment they set it down, the cottage felt occupied.

Not haunted — Orsen was too tired and too stubborn for haunted — just *occupied*, the way a room feels when someone has just stepped out of it and will be back. The silence rearranged itself around the urn. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard ticked as the house cooled, and the tick fell into a rhythm, and the rhythm was almost regular, and Orsen caught themselves listening for it to resolve into something and made themselves stop.

They counted the doors instead. Front, back, pantry, under-stairs, bedroom, bathroom, the little cupboard over the stairs. Seven. Seven doors, seven locks or latches or catches, and they went around checking each one, and by the third circuit their heart had slowed and their hands had steadied, and they stood in the middle of the kitchen in the dark they hadn't yet admitted to and let out a breath.

Outside, far off across the fields, the millpond caught the last of the light and held it, a coin of copper in the blue dusk.

Dusk came fully. The light went out of the coin.

And Orsen — tired, grieving, half-asleep on their feet — would spend the rest of their life certain of what happened next, and equally certain no one would believe it:

From the direction of the pond, faint and far and impossible, a bell rang.

One stroke. Deep. Wet-toned, somehow, the way a bell sounds underwater, the way a bell would sound if it were rung at the bottom of a lake by patient hands. The note rolled across the fields and through the walls of the cottage and passed through Orsen's chest, and every hair on their arms rose, and in the cupboard behind them — they heard it clearly, they would always hear it clearly — the loaf of bread shifted, softly, as if settling.

As if breathing out.

Orsen did not sleep that night. They sat up with every light burning and the urn on their knees and their thumb moving over the lid, over and over, the way you'd hold a hand.

"Wren," they whispered, at three in the morning, to a cottage full of lit rooms and locked doors. "What did you bring me to?"

The urn was warm. It had been warm for three weeks.

By morning, Orsen had decided the bell was a tractor, or wind, or a memory of a bell, some sound from childhood filed wrong. People heard things when they grieved. Doctors said so. By morning the bread smell had returned to the doorstep — a fresh loaf, again unsolicited, again warm, again with three slashes in the crust — and Orsen had decided that too: neighborly kindness, rural custom, nothing more.

They were carrying the second loaf inside when the child appeared at the garden gate.

She was perhaps eight, in a yellow raincoat too big for her, and she looked up at Orsen with the frank, terrible directness of children and said:

"You're the survivor."

"I'm—" Orsen crouched, mostly to make themselves smaller. "I'm Orsen. What's your name?"

"Tam's my brother. I'm Nell." She said it like the answer to a different question. Her eyes went past Orsen, into the house, into the dark behind the windows, and her brow furrowed with genuine puzzlement, the way a child furrows at a sum that won't come out. Then she looked back at Orsen, and her face cleared, and she smiled the sweetest smile Orsen had ever been unsettled by.

"You hum at night," Nell said. "Mum says don't listen to it. But I like it. It's pretty."

"I don't hum," Orsen said.

Nell tilted her head, considering this, weighing it, finding it wanting.

"Not you," she agreed, after a moment, kindly, the way you agree with someone who has misremembered a thing that matters. And she turned and walked away down Mill Lane, yellow coat bobbing, leaving Orsen standing on the step with a loaf of bread in their arms and the words catching up to them slowly, one at a time, like footsteps on stairs.

*Not you.*

Behind them, in the quiet cottage, on the mantelpiece, in the morning light, the urn sat warm and silent and full.

Orsen did not turn around for a long time. When they finally did, the house looked exactly as they'd left it. Every door closed. Every lock fastened. Seven doors, counted and recounted, all of them shut.

All of them shut, and one of them — the pantry door, the smallest, the one that latched with a click like a tongue striking teeth — very slightly ajar.
