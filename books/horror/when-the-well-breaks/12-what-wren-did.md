## 12. What Wren Did

The journal came up out of the ground the way teeth come out — reluctantly, with a sound like tearing.

I hadn't gone looking for it. I'd gone looking for Wren's ghost, who had stopped appearing after the night I found the score in the floorboards, and in her absence I'd done what she would have done: research. The sexton of 1911 was named Aldous Halloway, which meant his grave sat in the oldest row of the churchyard, under a yew that had been old when he buried the drowned. His stone read only ALDOUS HALLOWAY, KEEPER OF THE SILENCE. No dates. No verse from scripture. Just that one strange phrase, cut deep enough to survive a century of lichen.

I brought a spade because I didn't know what else to bring.

The ground over him was soft. Not freshly dug — nothing in Hollowmere was ever fresh — but soft the way bread left on a step is soft, the way things get when something beneath them has been breathing. I dug for an hour in the failing light, telling myself it was archaeology, telling myself a lot of things, and at the depth of two feet my spade struck not wood but tin. A biscuit tin, rusted orange, sealed with wax that had gone the color of old honey.

Inside: a journal bound in oilcloth, dry as a saint's finger. And folded around it, a page torn from a hymnal — the torn edge jagged, the fifth verse's absence pressed flat between the leaves like a flower someone had picked and then changed their mind about.

I sat down in the hole I'd made over a dead man and read by the light of my phone, which seemed, in that moment, like the most obscene thing I had ever done.

* * *

Aldous Halloway wrote like a man who knew he was running out of ink.

*They tell me the bell must come down. Parliament says scrap. The vicar says progress. I say we are lucky they only want the metal.*

*I have been sexton twenty-two years and I will tell you what no one asks me: the bell was never ours. We did not cast it. It was already in the tower when the first stone of this church was laid, and the men who found it would not say where. My grandfather's grandfather wrote that it rang before there were hands to ring it.*

*The pond was not always a pond. There was a church there first. Lower ground, older foundation, congregation of forty-one souls. In the flood year they went into their church to sing against the water, and the water came in anyway, and they sang until they could not, and the last thing that went under was the sound. Not the people. The sound. The people were finished. The sound was not.*

*Our bell is that sound, cast in bronze so it could be carried. You cannot drown a song twice, but you can hang it somewhere high and let it call its congregation home.*

*Today they melted it. I stood in the foundry yard and watched the bronze go liquid and I heard it scream — not metal-scream, mind you, though I told the men that's what it was. It sang. One note, held, going up as the heat took it, and every man in the yard hummed along without knowing, and I bit my tongue near through to stop myself.*

*It is not destroyed. You cannot destroy it. That is why I am writing this, and why I am burying it, and why I have torn the fifth verse out of every hymnal in this parish and burned the pages.*

*The fifth verse is not a verse. It is the end of the sentence the congregation never got to finish. Whoever holds it whole does not obey the bell. The bell obeys them.*

*I have taught my son the refusals. He will teach his son. One Halloway always stands at the water and says no. This is our penance for melting what we should have sunk.*

*If you are reading this, you have dug up a sexton, which means the bell is ringing again, which means someone has sung four verses at the pond, which means God help you, because I can no longer.*

The last entry was dated three days after the melting. After that, blank pages, all the way to the back cover, as if Aldous had kept writing in his head for the rest of his life and couldn't afford the paper.

I climbed out of the grave with the tin under my arm and dirt in my mouth and walked home in the dark without humming once, which cost me more than the digging had.

* * *

Wren was waiting.

Not in the cottage — in the doorway of it, standing exactly on the threshold, which I understood now was a rule. Ghosts in Hollowmere did not cross thresholds uninvited. She was more solid than I'd ever seen her, solid enough that the porch light made an actual shadow of her, thin as wire but *there*, and her mouth was open the way it always was, working around words that had been taken out of her like stones from a fruit.

"You sang," I said. "At the pond. Four verses."

She flinched. An actual flinch, shoulders drawing in, and I felt sick at how human it looked, how much it looked like the way she used to flinch when I caught her smoking behind the school, caught her lying, caught her being a separate person from me for the five minutes it took her to fold me back in.

"Show me," I said. "You've been trying to show me since the floorboards. So show me."

She raised one hand — translucent fingers, and I could see the porch light through them like light through earlobes — and pointed at the journal under my arm. Then at herself. Then she did something new: she held up four fingers, and then, slowly, closed them one at a time. Four becoming zero. Four verses becoming nothing. Becoming silence. Becoming *her*.

"The congregation took your voice."

She nodded, and the nod turned into something else, her whole body shuddering like a struck bell, and for a moment I heard it — not with my ears, lower than my ears, in the roots of my teeth — a single note of her actual voice, preserved somewhere inside the taking, still ringing.

Then she pointed at me. Both hands now, pressing the air toward my own throat, urgent, almost angry.

"No," I said. "No, I'm not singing anything. I've read the journal, Wren. I know what the fifth verse does. I know what it costs—"

She stamped her foot. Actually stamped it, a child's tantrum rendered in ghost-light, and then pressed both palms flat against the sides of her head as if to hold her skull together, and I realized she wasn't angry at me. She was frustrated the way she'd always been frustrated, since we were children sharing one language and half a vocabulary between us, when the words ran out and only the shape of the meaning remained.

She wanted me to understand. Not the plan. The *why*.

"Why did you come here?" I asked. "Before you died. Why Hollowmere? You never told me you were coming. You told me you were going to the coast to write."

Her hands dropped. For a long moment she just looked at me, and there was something in her face I'd spent thirty years learning to read across a breakfast table, and what it said was: *because you would have stopped me.*

* * *

Here is what I pieced together that night, sitting on the cottage floor with the journal open and the torn hymnal page flattened beside it and Wren kneeling across from me like a child at a Ouija board, gesturing yes and no while I guessed my way through her death:

We had a great-grandmother none of us ever met. Edith Quintana, born in Hollowmere, left before the war, never spoke of it. When Wren started digging into family history — her project, hers alone, the one part of our twinned life she'd kept private — she found Edith's name in a parish register online. And below it, in a list of 1911 confirmations, a marginal note in faded ink: *sang at the drowning; survived.*

Wren came to Hollowmere in April. Last spring. She stayed three weeks. She talked her way into the archive — Mrs. Halloway's face when I mentioned Wren's name finally made sense, that terrible pale recognition — and she found what Aldous Halloway had hidden and what his descendants had guarded: the truth about the fifth verse. Maybe she even found a fragment of it. The hymnal page in the tin was torn, but paper remembers shapes, and Wren had always been good at reading the negative space of things.

And then, on her last night, she went to the millpond and sang.

Four verses. The bell's song, the lure, the congregation's unfinished hymn — she gave them four verses of it, standing at the water in the dark, and the congregation rose to listen, forty-one drowned throats harmonizing with a living one, and for a few minutes Wren Quintana was the most beloved person in Hollowmere.

Then the song reached the place where the fifth verse should have been. The place where the sentence stops mid-word. And the congregation — patient, starving, a hundred years past their own ending — did what hungry things do.

They took her voice first. I know this because of what Tam sang, the fragment the child carried out of the pond: it was Wren's melody, bent wrong, sung in a child's voice. They were practicing with what they'd taken. Then they took the rest of her, gently, the way the pond takes everything, and the village woke the next morning having forgotten she'd ever visited, because forgetting is the pond's other gift, and Mrs. Halloway burned whatever record remained, and Wren went home to her city apartment and lived another eight months voiceless — *voiceless*, and I thought she was sulking, I thought she was depressed, I sent her texts saying *call me, you can't ignore me forever, we're twins, talk to me* —

God. *Talk to me.* Every message. I said it in every message.

She couldn't. She was carrying a hole in her throat shaped like a hymn, and she knew if she ever came back to Hollowmere the pond would finish the job, and she also knew — this is the part I understood last, lying on the cold floor while my sister's ghost watched me cry — that she was already dead. Whatever they take from you at the pond, they keep. Her body just hadn't been informed.

So she chose the manner of it. She came back. She walked into the water or lay down beside it, and the bell began to ring at dusk for the first time in a century, ringing *for her*, calling her home the way it called its congregation, and the village forgot her again, and dug her a grave with her name on it before her ashes even arrived, because the pond gives back what it's done with and the ground here keeps its appointments early.

They buried her twice. Once in the water, once in the earth. And I arrived to find the second burial already prepared, already grieved, already old news, because in Hollowmere my sister had been dying for a year and everyone knew except the one person whose whole life was her.

* * *

Near dawn, Wren touched the journal.

I don't know how. The rules of her had been loosening all week — the threshold, the shadow, the solidity — and maybe grief was wearing her thin places thinner. But she put one finger on Aldous Halloway's final line, the one about whoever holds the fifth verse whole, and dragged it slowly across the page until it rested on the word *obey*.

Then she looked up at me, and her face did something I had never seen it do in life or death.

It begged.

Not *learn it*. Not *avenge me*. Not any of the clean dramatic reasons I'd been rehearsing in my head. She begged the way you beg when you're watching someone stand on train tracks and you have no voice to shout with — pure, formless, *don't*. Because she knew what I was already thinking. She could see it in my face, the same way I could always see her plans forming, twin telegraphy, no wires needed. She could see that I was thinking: *if I learn the verse, I command the bell. If I command the bell, I can order it to give her back.*

And she knew — better than anyone dead or living — what the bell does with voices. What it keeps. What it doesn't give back.

"There has to be a way," I said. My voice cracked on *way*, and outside the window the sky was doing its bruised pre-dusk color even though it was morning, the light arriving wrong, the hours bending toward the pond. "There's a version where everyone lives, Wren. There's always a version. We used to find them."

She shook her head. Slow. Final. And then she lifted her hand one last time and pointed — not at the journal, not at the hymnal, not at my throat.

At the hallway.

At the doors.

I turned and counted them, the way I'd counted them every night since I was six years old, the ritual that kept the dark organized: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve—

Thirteen.

The thirteenth door stood at the end of the hall where no door had ever been, and I knew its wood, I knew its paint, I knew the chip in the lower panel from the time we'd moved furniture through it when we were nineteen. It was Wren's bedroom door, from the house where we grew up, three hundred miles away, standing in my hallway where no wall was.

And from behind it, muffled and patient and horribly sweet, something was humming the first verse.

Wren's ghost backed away from it, one slow step at a time, her eyes never leaving mine, and her mouth opened wider than a mouth should open, and though no sound came out, I heard her anyway, in my teeth, in the roots of me, the way you hear your own name called from another room:

*Don't open it. And Orsen — whatever you do — don't count to thirteen twice.*
