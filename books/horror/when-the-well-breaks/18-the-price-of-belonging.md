## 18. The Price of Belonging

The pond gave Orsen back to the bank at dawn, soaked to the ribs, with Wren's hand still cold against their wrist where she'd let go.

They sat in the reeds until the shaking stopped. The water lay flat and patient behind them, the way a dog lies flat when it has decided you are not going anywhere. Above the village, the bell tower stood empty against a bruised sky, and yet the sound was already gathering somewhere under the world, tuning itself for dusk.

*It takes what roots you.*

Wren had said it — or shaped it, mouthed it through the murk, her translucent face close enough that Orsen could have counted the freckles they'd both been born with. She had held up one finger, then pressed that finger into the mud at the waterline, deep, until it vanished.

Rooted. Planted. Held.

Orsen walked home dripping and did not dry off until noon, because drying off meant rituals, and rituals meant counting, and counting meant facing what they were about to count.

* * *

The cottage smelled of bread no one had eaten and lamp oil burned long past sensible hours. Orsen lit every light anyway — hall, kitchen, parlor, the landing, the little lamp in what had been their bedroom as children and was now just a room where they slept badly. Twelve bulbs burning. They knew the number without looking, the way they knew their own pulse.

Then they took a sheet of paper from Wren's old desk, sat down at the kitchen table, and began to make a list.

Not a list of things. A list of *belongings*. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole shape of the trap.

**The cottage.** They wrote it first because it was first. Stone walls their grandfather had pointed at, said *your family laid these.* A garden gone to seed. A roof that leaked over the stairwell in exactly one spot, so that Orsen had spent three nights moving a copper pot and listening to it fill — *plink, plink, plink* — a rhythm so like a slow bell that on the second night they had thrown the pot across the room. The cottage rooted them. It was the easiest entry on the list and therefore the least dangerous to write down.

**The doors.** Underneath: *the counting itself.* Twelve doors, checked morning and night, locks tested in order, always clockwise, always ending with the front door so the last thing their hand touched was the way out. Their whole life arranged around this liturgy. Other people had prayers; Orsen had hinges. Take away the counting and what was left? A person who slept badly in a house full of open mouths.

**The rituals themselves,** then — the lights left burning, the tongue-bite, the iron nail they now carried loose in their coat pocket like a rosary bead. These weren't separate from the doors. They were the same devotion wearing different clothes.

**Hollowmere.** The village. The pond road. Mrs. Halloway's empty chair by her window, which Orsen still glanced toward each evening out of habit, though the window had been dark since the night of the open doors. Tam's small solemn face. Even the neighbors who never knocked — they belonged to Orsen in some stubborn, involuntary way, the way a scar belongs to the body that earned it.

**The refusals.** The counter-rhythms Mrs. Halloway had beaten into them in her final weeks, coughing blood between lessons, saying *again, again, you hesitate and it hears you hesitate.* Orsen carried those rhythms now the way a soldier carries drill. They were not things. They were load-bearing walls inside the self.

Orsen looked at the list. Four entries. It felt incomplete, and the incompleteness had a specific gravity, pulling everything toward one line they hadn't written yet.

Because there was one more belonging. And it wasn't a thing at all.

* * *

Wren's ashes sat on the mantel in a tin biscuit box, because the funeral home's urn had cracked in transit and Orsen had refused to buy another. The tin had strawberries painted on it, faded. Their mother had kept sewing supplies in it once. It was the least sacred object imaginable, which was why Orsen had chosen it: Wren would have laughed.

Orsen stood in front of the mantel for a long time.

"You're the last line," they said aloud. "Aren't you."

The fire didn't answer. The fire never answered. But the air in the room went the particular kind of still it went when Wren was near — a stillness with a person inside it, the way a glove holds the shape of a hand.

Here is what Orsen understood, standing there, and here is what they wrote last, pressing hard enough to tear the paper:

**Wren.**

Not the ashes. Not the ghost. The *belonging* — thirty years of being half of a pair. Finishing each other's sentences before either had spoken. The private language of twins, built from nothing, needed for nothing, kept anyway. The certainty, since childhood, that however far apart they were, some part of Orsen was always turned toward Wren like a compass needle, and always would be, and that this was not love exactly but something older than love, something pre-verbal, something that had existed before either of them had words for anything at all.

That was the root. That was the taproot, going down past all the others, past the cottage and the doors and the village, down into the dark where the congregation waited with their mouths open in perfect unison.

And the bell took what rooted you.

Orsen sat down slowly at the table. The chair creaked. Somewhere below the floor, below the foundations, below the bedrock, something vast shifted its weight and settled, patient as sediment.

"Take the cottage," Orsen said to the empty room. "I'd grieve it and I'd live."

Silence, but an attentive silence.

"Take the doors. I'd stop sleeping well. I've stopped sleeping well anyway." Their voice sounded strange in the lamplight, level and thin, a voice reading terms and conditions at the edge of an abyss. "Take the village. Take the refusals. I'd learn new ones. People learn new ones. That's what people do."

The stillness deepened. Wren was closer now. Orsen could feel her the way you feel weather coming — not seeing it, just knowing the pressure had changed.

"But you don't want any of those," Orsen said. "Do you."

* * *

They found her in the parlor, standing where the thirteenth door used to stand, back when the cottage had grown impossible architecture overnight. She was more solid than she'd ever been — the pond had given her substance the way it gave back hymnals and shoes, dredging her up from wherever drowned things wait. Her feet cast a faint shadow. Her face was still wrong in one crucial respect: the mouth moved and no sound came, had never come, not once since the pond took her voice along with everything else.

She raised one hand. Not pointing. Offering.

"No," Orsen said. "Don't nod. Don't you dare nod. Let me do this properly."

Wren lowered the hand and waited, because Wren had always known how to wait — had waited out their mother's rages, had waited three years for Orsen to come home, had waited under black water for a century's worth of congregation to finish a song cut off mid-word. Waiting was the oldest thing she knew.

Orsen paced. Three lengths of the parlor, turning at the window, three lengths back. The lamps hissed. Outside, far off, a dog began to howl and thought better of it.

"It wants the twin-bond," Orsen said. "That's the price. That's what 'belonging' means to it — not property, not keepsakes. *Belonging.* Where you're rooted. And I'm rooted in you. I have been since before I was born. We shared a wall before we shared a language." They stopped pacing. "You know that's what it asked for. You've known since you dragged me to the water's edge. You showed me the trade and then you showed me the price and then you waited for me to do the arithmetic myself, because if you told me, I could refuse you. But if I work it out—" their voice cracked, and they hated it, and pushed through— "if I work it out myself, then it's mine. The choice is mine. You absolute *bastard*, Wren. You learned that trick from Mother."

Wren's expression did something complicated. Grief and pride and apology, all trying to fit through a door too narrow for them. She did not deny it.

"And here's the part I can't get past," Orsen went on, quieter now, because quiet was all that was left. "If I give it the cottage, the song stays unfinished. If I give it the doors, the song stays unfinished. If I give it *you* — the ashes, the ghost, whatever's left — it might take the shell and leave the bond. It might take the body of the thing and leave the root. Because the root isn't you. The root is *me-and-you*. The space between us. The thing that exists only because there are two of us and there always have been." They pressed the heel of their hand against their eye. "It doesn't want my sister. It wants my *twinness*. And you can't bury that in a tin with strawberries on it."

Wren crossed the room. Orsen felt the cold arrive before she did — the pond's temperature, carried in her outline like a draft through a keyhole. She stood in front of them, close enough that the lamplight passed faintly through her shoulder, and she lifted her hand again, and this time Orsen didn't say no.

She touched their chest. Not dramatically. Just two fingers, over the sternum, the way you check whether someone is still breathing.

And she nodded.

* * *

Orsen slept — or lay down and lost track of time, which amounted to the same thing now — and dreamed of the pond from underneath. In the dream the congregation stood in rows on the drowned church floor, pews furred with silt, faces tilted up toward a surface of hammered silver. They were not menacing. That was the worst discovery of the whole season, worse than the thirteenth door, worse than the bell tolling *Quintana*: they were only *unfinished*. A hundred years of holding a note that someone had snatched away mid-phrase. Orsen knew that feeling intimately. It was the feeling of a sentence interrupted. Of a phone call cut off before goodbye. Of a sister dead at thirty-one with everything unsaid.

The song didn't want singers. It wanted an *ending*. And endings cost what roots you, because you cannot finish a thing while you are still holding on to the reason you started it.

Orsen woke at gray dawn with the dream dissolving and one thought sitting in their chest like a stone dropped down a well:

Three nights. Wren would teach them the verse word by word, and each word would cost a memory, and the memories would come from the deepest shelf, and the deepest shelf was *them*.

They got up. They counted the doors — twelve, all present, all locked, clockwise, ending at the front door. Then they stood in the hallway and made themselves say it out loud, to the house, to the pond beneath the house, to the bell that was even now gathering its breath somewhere under the world:

"I'll pay it. But I choose the order. And I choose what it buys."

The lamps flickered once, all twelve, in unison — acknowledgment, or appetite, or both.

On the mantel, the tin with the strawberries sat in the early light, and beside it, faint as breath on glass, a handprint appeared in the dust. Small. Left-handed. Wren's.

Pointing, not at the tin.

At Orsen's throat.

* * *

By afternoon Orsen had done the last of the arithmetic, and it was crueller than the first sum.

Every word of the fifth verse would buy back a memory of Wren — not erase the fact of her, but erase the *feeling*, the warmth, the root-hold. At the end, Orsen would stand waist-deep in the millpond able to name their sister the way you name a stranger in a photograph: *this was Wren, she was born in March, she liked rain.* True. Weightless. A grave with no grief in it.

And the village would keep its lights on. Tam would grow up unmarked. The congregation would bow and go down like lamps going out one by one, finished at last, released after a century of drowning mid-word.

It was a good trade. It was an obscene trade. Both things were true, and Orsen had stopped expecting truth to choose sides.

They spent the daylight hours preparing the way you prepare for surgery: clean clothes folded, iron nail polished and pocketed, the score from under the floorboards laid out on the kitchen table with its four written verses and its torn fifth edge. They ate everything in the house, absurdly, stockpiling. They walked to the pond road at low sun and stood where Mrs. Halloway had kept her post and said the refusals aloud, all of them, perfect, without hesitating once — a report delivered to a dead woman, or a promise.

When they came back, the light was going amber and long, and Wren was waiting in the kitchen, solid as a living woman now except for the silence pouring off her, and on the table, next to the score, she had set out three objects Orsen hadn't seen in twenty years:

A matchbox car. A hair ribbon. A photograph of two children on a wall, arms slung round each other, squinting into the sun.

The first payments. Laid out in advance, like coins for a ferryman.

Wren looked at Orsen across the table, and her mouth shaped three silent words, and Orsen read them easily, because reading Wren's lips was the first language they'd ever learned:

*Ready? Begin.*

Outside, far under the ground, under the roots, under everything, the bell drew its first slow breath — and began, early, to ring.
