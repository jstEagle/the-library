## 20. The Singing

The flooded hour came early, the way Mrs. Halloway had promised it would, the way everything had been coming early since the night the doors opened. The sun had not yet touched the western hills when the light began to fail, and the bell — which had not stopped ringing for three days, which had rung through meals and sleep and the digging of a grave no one would fill — changed its voice.

It went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet, the way a held breath is quiet. The whole village stood in the hush of it, and Orsen felt the hush in their teeth, in the scarred place on their tongue where they had bitten down a lifetime of almost-humming, in the hollow where three nights of memories used to be.

They could not remember Wren's laugh. They had paid for the first word of the fifth verse with her laugh, and the second with the smell of rain on the schoolyard, and the third with the game they used to play in the back of the bus, counting license plates, and so on down through the whole small treasury of a shared childhood, word by word, coin by coin, until the verse sat complete in Orsen's mouth like a stone they were not allowed to swallow and not allowed to spit out.

*Hold it. Don't sing it. Not yet.*

The melody pressed against the backs of their teeth. It wanted out. It had wanted out since the first night, and Orsen had kept it the way they kept doors counted and lights burning — with discipline, with ritual, with the flat stubborn refusal of a person who had survived one twin by being the careful one.

They walked down to the pond.

* * *

The whole of Hollowmere was already there.

They stood in their nightclothes, most of them, though it was not yet full dark — the vicar with his shuttered windows left open behind him, Mrs. Petch in her dressing gown, Tam's parents holding Tam between them, his small hand gripped so hard the fingers went white. They had woken, all of them, at the flooded hour, and walked, and now they stood at the waterline in a half-circle, faces tilted toward the millpond, mouths slightly open, humming.

The sound came off the water in a low, wet chord. It was not a tune so much as a tide — it rose and fell, and it had no beginning that Orsen could find, and it was beautiful in the way that drowning is beautiful from the shore, all light and motion and no air.

And the congregation rose with it.

They came up out of the pond the way reeds come up, slowly, without breaking the surface — first the pale crowns of heads, then the shoulders, then the whole grey ranks of them standing in the shallows and beyond, out where the water deepened, dressed in their 1911 clothes, their coats heavy with a century of pond. Their mouths were open. Their mouths had always been open. They had been singing one song, cut off mid-word, for a hundred and eleven years, and now the bell above the empty tower was ringing their unfinished note across the whole valley, and they had come to finish it.

Orsen counted them. It was a habit; it was the only prayer they had. Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-three.

At the center of the line, a figure stood slightly forward of the rest, and Orsen's chest did the thing it had done every time since the photograph — the lurch, the wrongness, the recognition that arrived ahead of permission. Wren's posture. Wren's tilt of the head. But it was not Wren. It was one of the drowned, a woman in a waterlogged hat, and Orsen made themself look away, made themself look at the true figure instead: the ghost standing at the pond's edge, translucent in the last light, humming with the rest.

Wren had come down with the village. Wren, who could not speak, who had spent three nights pressing words into Orsen's hands one memory at a time, stood among the singers now, and her humming fit the chord perfectly, and Orsen understood that this was the thing they had been afraid of since chapter one of all of this — that the song would take her back, that the congregation would finish collecting what it had started collecting last spring.

The bell rang again. Orsen felt it in the pond mud under their shoes.

Tam broke free of his parents.

He was fast, the way children are fast, and he was singing — the fragment, the verse-and-a-half the pond had given him and then marked him for — and he ran straight at the waterline with his arms out like a boy running to a father coming home. His parents lunged. The villagers did not turn. The chord swallowed his small voice and made it harmony.

Orsen moved.

They did not decide to move. Their body simply did the thing bodies do when the counted doors are behind you and the thing you love is running into the dark — they crossed the grass, they caught Tam around the chest two steps from the water, and the cold coming off the pond went through them both like a hand. Tam fought. He was strong with the song's strength. Orsen put their mouth against his ear and did the only thing Mrs. Halloway had taught them that worked on the marked: they named him.

"Tam. Tam Wicker. Born in March. You bit through a pencil in class. Your mother keeps the bread on the left side of the step."

He shuddered. The humming in him stuttered, caught, and his arms came down, and he was a wet-eyed eight-year-old again, and Orsen shoved him backward into his parents' arms and turned to face the water.

The congregation had all turned to look at them.

Forty-three drowned faces, patient as furniture. And above the village, though the tower was empty and had been empty since 1911, the bell rang once more — and this time it did not ring a note.

It rang a name.

*Quintana.*

The sound of it went through Orsen like a nail through a door. The mark, transferred, called home. The villagers' humming changed key. Wren's ghost turned her face toward Orsen, and even without a voice Orsen could read her — could read her the way you read a twin, the way you read your own reflection — and what her face said was: *Now. Before it takes you instead.*

Orsen walked into the pond.

* * *

The water was warmer than it should have been. That was the worst of it, at first — the water at the flooded hour was blood-warm, bathwater-warm, the temperature of a body, and it closed around Orsen's ankles and knees with the gentleness of something that had been waiting a long time and saw no need to hurry anymore.

Up to the waist. Mrs. Halloway's diary had said up to the waist; the sexton's journal had said it; Wren's hands, shaping the words in the air over three wordless nights, had said it. The verse could not be sung from the shore. The verse was a door, and doors only work if you stand in the frame.

The congregation did not advance. They stood in their ranks, water to their chests, mouths open, and the chord rose around Orsen on every side, and the bell rang the name again — *Quintana, Quintana* — and Orsen stood in the warm water in the middle of the song and felt the last verse pressing at their teeth like a word held too long.

They thought about what it had cost. They made themself think about it, because Mrs. Halloway had written, in the last legible page of her diary — the page Orsen had found that morning, weighted under the cold iron she had kept by her bed for sixty years — *name what you pay, or the song will name it for you.*

Wren's laugh. The license-plate game. The smell of the schoolyard rain. The secret language they had invented at seven, every word of it gone now, though Orsen could see the shape of the hole where it had been. The night Wren had a fever and Orsen had sat up all night counting her breaths — gone. The promise they had made at fourteen, pinky to pinky, that whichever of them died first would wait — gone, gone, all of it gone, paid out word by word across three nights while Wren sat across the kitchen table mouthing the verse and Orsen repeated it back, holding it, never singing it, the discipline of a lifetime of counted doors.

And still, standing in the water, Orsen found that what remained of their love for Wren was not smaller. It was only older. Stripped of every anecdote, every keepsake, every shared memory — what was left was the bare fact of it, the way you are left with the bare fact of a leg after the wound: *this is mine, this is part of me, I move with it or I do not move at all.*

That was what the congregation wanted. Not the memories. The belonging itself. The bell took what rooted you, and Orsen's root was a person, and the person was standing in the water humming.

*Sing it,* said the chord, in no language at all. *Sing it and keep her. Sing it and command us. Sing it and the bell is yours, the village is yours, the doors will open only when you say.*

It was a true offer. That was the trap of it. Orsen could feel the truth of it in the water, in the mark on their chest, in the bell overhead ringing their name: sing the verse as a claim and it would work. The verse that was never written down was a key, and keys do not care what they unlock.

The congregation was not malicious. Wren had shown them that, dragging Orsen into the shallows, pressing the truth into them in fragments: *unfinished, unfinished, cut off mid-word, a hundred years of a held breath.* They were not demons. They were a congregation whose hymn had been interrupted, and everything they had done — the luring, the marking, the taking of Wren's voice and then Wren — everything was the reaching of a interrupted thing toward its ending.

They did not need a master. They needed a last verse.

Orsen opened their mouth.

* * *

They sang it alone.

That was the whole of the craft of it, the thing the sexton had understood in 1911 and drowned a church to prevent and his granddaughter had spent her life refusing to understand: the verse only worked unaccompanied. Sung by the village, it was a claim. Sung by the bell, it was a lure. Sung by one marked and grieving person standing waist-deep in a warm pond with no one — no one — joining in, it was what it had always been meant to be.

An ending.

The first word came out of Orsen like a tooth. The chord around them faltered — one beat, a stumble in a hundred years of tide — and the congregation's open mouths went still, and even the bell above the empty tower seemed to lean in.

The second and third words came easier, because Orsen had paid for them already; the verse was theirs, bought at retail, every memory a receipt. They sang, and their voice was not beautiful. It cracked, it wavered, it was the voice of someone who had bitten their tongue bloody rather than hum along, and it did not matter, because the verse did not want beauty. It wanted completion. It wanted the note that had been held since 1911 to be, at last, resolved.

The congregation began to sway.

Not toward Orsen. Not toward the shore. They swayed the way a congregation sways when the hymn is nearly done — the sway of people who know the last line is coming and are ready to go home after. And the chord that had held the valley for a hundred and eleven years began, note by note, to come apart into its separate voices, each drowned singer finding their own line again, their own pitch, their own self — and Orsen heard, underneath the wet grandeur of it, something almost ordinary. Voices. A village's worth of voices, singing a hymn to its finish.

The bell rang one more time.

Mid-peal, at the height of the swing, at the loudest point of the loudest note — it cracked. Orsen heard it the way you hear ice go on a pond: a single bright catastrophic line through the sound, and then the bell's voice changed forever, went ragged, went wrong, went *finished* — and the last peal came out broken and human and was the truest note the bell had ever rung, because it was a note that could not continue.

Orsen sang the last line.

The congregation bowed.

All of them, together, the bow of a congregation at the close of a service — deep, unhurried, a hundred years overdue — and as they bowed they went out. Not down. Out. Like lights going off along a street, one after another, each grey figure dimming from the feet up, and the last thing each of them did before the dark took them was lift their heads and look at Orsen, and their faces — Orsen would carry this, whatever else the cost had bought — their faces were grateful.

The chord ended. The pond went quiet. The water around Orsen's waist went cold all at once, ordinary cold, pond cold.

Wren was standing in front of them.

Whole. That was the only word. The translucence was gone; she stood in the last grey light as solid as the far shore, and her mouth was closed, because her voice was still gone — the congregation had taken it last spring and no ending could return what had already been spent — but she was *here*, all of her, and she was looking at Orsen with an expression Orsen could not read for the first time since the bus, since the grave, since the beginning.

Then she reached out and touched Orsen's cheek.

Her hand was cold. It was the first time she had ever managed to touch them, in all these weeks, and the cold of it was the cold of the pond, and the tenderness of it was entirely hers, and Orsen understood — with the part of them that still knew her, the bare fact of her, the root of her — that this was goodbye, and that she had known it since the first night she pointed at the torn edge of the score and then at Orsen's throat.

She had not been leading Orsen to a rescue. She had been leading them to a release. Hers.

Wren smiled. It was her old smile — no, it was a smile, Orsen knew it was a smile, they knew that much, whatever else was paid for — and she stepped back, and the water took her the way the congregation had gone: from the feet up, gently, like a light going out because the day has come, not because the power has failed.

Her face was last. Her face was last, and then the pond was only a pond.

* * *

Orsen stood in the water until the cold made their legs shake, and then they walked out.

The villagers stood at the waterline, blinking, in their nightclothes, like people waking from a nap they would not remember. Tam was crying without knowing why. Somewhere behind them, in the village, twelve doors stood open onto empty rooms, and no one would remember leaving them open, and no one would remember to close them, and it would not matter.

The bell did not ring again.

Orsen walked up the hill through the dusk with pond water running out of their clothes, and above the churchyard the empty tower stood against the last light, and it was empty, and it would stay empty — Orsen knew that the way they knew the count of doors, the way they had once known their sister's laugh: as a fact, held without proof, kept without needing it.

At the cottage, they toweled off, lit every lamp, and stood in the kitchen counting under their breath.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Twelve.

The thirteenth door was gone. The nails were gone from the pillow. Wren's bedroom door stood where a wall should not be, and behind it was a wall, and the wall was only a wall.

Orsen put their hand flat against it and stood there a long time.

They tried to remember what they were grieving. They knew that they were grieving — the ache was enormous and specific and it had a shape, and the shape was a person, a twin, a girl who had come to Hollowmere before her death and rung something and been taken — they knew all of it as knowledge, clean and cold and complete. What they could not find was the feeling underneath it. The laugh. The fever-night. The pinky promise. Paid. Spent. Sung.

But the ache remained. That was the strange mercy of it. Grief with its contents removed, aching in the shape of her, the way a missing tooth aches, the way an amputated limb itches in the air where the hand used to be.

Orsen went to the window. Below, in the dusk, the pond lay flat and dark and finished, and the village lights were coming on one by one, and the doors of Hollowmere were closing — not locked, not counted, just closed, the way doors close in places where nothing is calling.

The bell tower stood black against the sky.

And Orsen stood at the window with wet hair and an empty mouth where a melody used to live, listening — listening the way they had listened all their life, the careful one, the counting one, the survivor — for the dusk bell that would never ring again.

There was nothing.

There was nothing, and it was the most beautiful sound Orsen had ever heard, and they wept without knowing why, and the lights stayed on.
