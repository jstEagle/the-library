## 2. Grandmother's House

The farmhouse appeared the way it always had in Kestrel's memory—suddenly, all at once, as if the road had been holding it back and then let go.

One moment there was nothing but the gravel switchback and the black pines pressing close on either side, and the next the house stood in its clearing with its slate roof and its crooked chimney, exactly where sixty years of photographs said it should be. Kestrel pulled the car onto the weedy apron of the drive and sat with both hands on the wheel, engine ticking down into silence, and felt the strange double sensation that had followed them since Hollowbrook: that they were coming home, and that they were being expected.

The urn rode in the passenger seat, belted in like a small gray passenger. Maren Amberdale, 1931–2024. Kestrel had carried her through two airports and a ferry crossing and up a mountain road that their rental agreement had probably forbidden, and the whole time the urn had seemed lighter than it should have been, as though whatever had made Maren Maren had already gone ahead to wait for them.

"Okay, Grandma," Kestrel said aloud, because the alternative was not speaking at all, and the silence in this place had texture. "Let's see what you left me."

They gathered the urn against their chest and climbed out. The air smelled of cold stone and woodsmoke from some fire no one had lit, and beneath it something faintly mineral—the smell of well water, though the well was still a hundred yards off behind the house, hidden by the old pump shed. The light was going amber and long across the yard. Three hours of daylight, maybe less. October did not negotiate in these hills.

The front door was unlocked. Of course it was. Maren had never locked a door in her life, and when Kestrel had asked her why, once, years ago, sitting on this same porch shelling peas into a dented bowl, Maren had only smiled and said, *Locks keep people out, sweetheart. We've never had trouble with people.*

Inside, the house exhaled. That was the only word for it—a slow displacement of air that stirred the dust motes in the hallway light and smelled of cedar and old paper and, very faintly, of something burnt. Kestrel stood in the entryway with the urn in their arms and let the house look at them. Because it did look. They had felt it as a child and dismissed it as imagination: the sense of attention, patient and distributed, coming from everywhere at once. From the walls. From under the floorboards. From the deep dark of the stairwell that led down to the cellar and the cold room and the door that Maren had kept painted shut.

Kestrel set the urn on the hall table, right where Maren's reading glasses still sat folded over a half-finished crossword, and took out their phone to check the time. No signal. There hadn't been any since the ferry, but here it felt less like geography and more like policy.

The lawyer's letter was in their coat pocket. *Estate reading to be conducted at the property, Saturday, four o'clock.* Which meant Mr. Aldous would be arriving within the hour, which meant Kestrel had an hour to do the thing they had been avoiding since the funeral: walk through the house alone.

Maren's instructions had come by post three days before she died, which Kestrel still could not make sense of, no matter how many times they turned it over. A letter written in her strong, slanted hand, posted on a Tuesday, arriving Thursday—and Maren had died Friday morning, peaceful in her sleep, according to the neighbor who found her. The letter contained no farewell, no sentiment, none of the warmth that had run through every card Maren had ever sent. It contained instructions, numbered, in the tone of a woman leaving a checklist for someone she trusted to follow it without questions:

*1. Keep the lights on. Every room, every night, until the estate is settled. Do not let a single bulb burn out unattended; if one goes, replace it before you sleep.*

*2. Count the doors each evening after sundown. Write the number down. If the number is ever wrong, do not correct it. Do not search for the extra. Go to the kitchen, put the kettle on, and wait for morning.*

*3. Do not sit in the wooden chair.*

There had been more, but the last lines were smudged, water-damaged somehow, as though the letter had been written near something wet. Kestrel had read them a hundred times and could never force the ink to resolve into words. Just a gray smear, shaped almost like handwriting, like a sentence drowning.

*4.* Something. *5.* Something. And then, legible again, the final line, which Kestrel had memorized despite themselves:

*If you are reading this, I am sorry, and I am proud of you, and neither of those things will feel like enough. — M.*

* * *

Kestrel walked the house the way they used to as a child, fast, touching things. The kitchen with its blue enamel stove. The parlor with the taxidermy pheasant Maren had won in a raffle and refused to apologize for. The narrow stairs, thirteen steps, always thirteen, up to the three bedrooms and the linen closet and the window seat where Kestrel had spent whole summers reading while the valley went gold below.

Every bulb was burning. Kestrel checked—hallway, kitchen, parlor, all three bedrooms, even the cellar stairwell, where a bare bulb swung gently on its cord though there was no draft. Someone had replaced them all recently. The bulbs were new, bright, identical. Maren had done this herself, then, in her last weeks. Had climbed the ladder at eighty-three and screwed in fresh light against whatever darkness she'd believed was coming.

On the kitchen table lay a second letter, propped against the sugar bowl. Kestrel's name on the envelope. Their stomach dropped through several floors before they made themselves pick it up—it was dated, in Maren's hand, *to be opened upon arrival*. Not posthumous trickery this time. Just another instruction, waiting.

Kestrel didn't open it. Not yet. They told themselves it was because the lawyer would want to be present, or because they wanted tea first, or some other reasonable lie, and they put the envelope in their back pocket, where its weight sat against their spine like a hand.

Outside, tires crunched gravel.

Hollis Vane came up the drive in a truck older than Kestrel was, moving with the deliberate unhurried gait of a man who had learned that hurrying attracted notice. He was maybe seventy, lean as fence wire, with a face weathered down to its essential geometry—deep lines, pale eyes, a mouth that looked like it had forgotten most of its expressions except one. He stopped at the bottom of the porch steps and looked at Kestrel for a long moment.

"You're Maren's grandchild," he said. Not a question. "You've got her chin."

"Kestrel."

"I know." He said it flatly, without warmth, and something about the way he said it—as though knowing were a burden he'd been carrying a long time—made the hair rise on Kestrel's arms. "Hollis Vane. Next property over. Your grandmother and I—" He paused. Recalibrated. "We were neighbors a long time."

"You knew her well?"

"No one knew her well." He climbed two of the steps and stopped, and now his voice dropped, and Kestrel understood with a lurch that everything else he'd said had been preamble. "That's rather the point of her, God rest her. Listen to me, child. Are you staying the night?"

"The reading's at four. I'll need a few days at least, to sort the house."

"A few days." Hollis looked past Kestrel, through the open door, into the lit interior of the house, and his face did the thing his mouth had been saving. Fear, worn smooth by decades of practice into something almost like boredom. "How many lights you got on in there?"

"All of them. Per her instructions."

Something eased in him fractionally. "Good. Keep it so." He gripped the railing, and Kestrel saw that his knuckles were scarred, old white seams across all of them, like he'd spent years clenching his hands around something sharp. "I'll say this once and then I'll leave you to your grief, which I'm sorry for, truly. Whatever she told you to do—counting doors, keeping lights, all her little rituals—do it exact. Don't improve on it. Don't skip it because it feels foolish. And when the sun starts going down today, get in your car and drive to my place and sleep on my couch, because I'm telling you plain, Kestrel Amberdale: do not stay past dark. Not the first night. Not alone."

"Why?" The word came out smaller than Kestrel intended. "What happens after dark?"

Hollis held their gaze, and behind the pale eyes something moved—not madness, Kestrel realized, but arithmetic. A man doing sums he hated.

"Nothing happens," he said. "That's the horror of it. Nothing happens, and the house counts, and you'll lie awake listening for a sound you can't name, and in the morning you'll be fine, mostly, except you'll know the house was never empty. Not once. Not in sixty years." He stepped back down, away from the porch, as if the threshold itself had begun to lean toward him. "Your grandmother paid a great deal to keep you safe, child. Least you can do is not undo it out of stubbornness."

"Hollis—" Kestrel started down the steps after him. "What did she pay? Paid to whom? What is it you people aren't telling me?"

He was at his truck now, hand on the door, and he looked back at Kestrel with an expression that was almost tender, and almost pitying, and underneath both, guilty as a grave.

"Ask her," he said. "She left you everything, didn't she? She'll have left you that too." He climbed in, started the engine, and called through the open window, one last time, his voice cracking on it: "Sundown's at six-forty. My porch light burns all night. You'll see it from her kitchen window. If you're not on my couch by seven, I'll know you chose to stay—and I'll know whose choice it really was."

The truck ground away down the drive and was swallowed by the pines.

Kestrel stood on the porch a long while, arms wrapped around themselves against a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature. Sundown at six-forty. It was twenty past five. In the kitchen behind them, every light burned steady and bright, and the kettle—Kestrel was nearly sure they had not touched it—was just beginning, softly, patiently, to sing.

They went inside. They made tea because the kettle insisted. And as the light outside thickened toward gold and then toward copper, Kestrel found themselves drawn, the way you are drawn to press a bruise, to the hallway closet where Maren had kept the family albums—sixty years of Christmases and birthdays and harvest suppers, all of it waiting to be sorted, all of it waiting to be counted.

They carried the first album to the kitchen table, into the full brightness of the lights, and opened it to the oldest photograph. Maren as a young woman, laughing, arm around a man Kestrel didn't recognize, standing on this very porch.

Behind them, at the edge of the frame, stood a wooden chair.

Empty. Waiting.

And in the photograph, though Kestrel would have sworn on anything holy that the chair had never been there—that no such chair existed anywhere in the house—someone, or something, appeared to be just beginning to sit down in it.
