## 3. One Chair Too Many

The morning light came through the kitchen windows the color of weak tea, and Kestrel decided—deliberately, the way you decide to step into cold water—that they would not think about Edith Marsh's mouth forming *zero* like a key turning in a lock. They would sort photographs. Photographs were safe. Photographs were the opposite of counting.

Maren Amberdale had kept her albums in the cedar chest at the foot of her bed, and the chest smelled the way it always had: pencil shavings, lavender gone to dust, and beneath it all something older, mineral, like the air over a well. Kestrel knelt on the floorboards with their grandmother's quilt folded under their knees and lifted out six albums, each one bound in cracked leather, each one labeled in Maren's small, severe hand.

*Amberdales. 1961–1974.*
*The Farm. 1975–1988.*
*Hollis & Maren. 1989–1996.*
*Harvests.*
*Winters.*
*Kestrel.*

That last one stopped them. Of course there was an album with their name on it. Grandmothers kept albums of their grandchildren; that was what grandmothers did. But seeing it written there—*Kestrel*, in that hand that had also written *keep the lights on* and *count the doors*—made the word feel less like a name and more like a label on a specimen jar.

They started with the oldest album anyway. Start at the beginning. That was how you took a history, same as a patient: head to toe, system by system, looking for what was wrong.

The first photograph was dated August 1963, and it showed a family Kestrel knew only as faces from funerals. Great-grandfather Aldous standing stiff as a fence post in his Sunday coat. Great-grandmother Ruth with her hands folded. Two children Kestrel had never learned the names of, squinting into a sun that had set before Kestrel was born. They stood on the front porch of this very house, and behind them, through the open door, you could just see the dim interior, the hallway, the stairs.

And there, on the porch, at the edge of the frame—a wooden chair.

Kestrel frowned and brought the album closer to the window.

It was a plain chair, spindle-backed, the kind that came in sets of four from mail-order catalogs and outlived every table they were sold with. It stood slightly apart from the family, angled toward the camera, empty. Nobody leaned on it. Nobody acknowledged it. It simply existed in the photograph with the calm entitlement of furniture, and Kestrel felt the first cold thread go down through them, because they had grown up in this house, they had eaten Sunday dinners in this house, and there had never been a chair like that on the porch. There was no porch furniture at all. Maren had thought porch furniture invited loitering.

A trick of light, they told themselves. A shadow with edges. Old photographs lie; silver halide lies; your eyes lie most of all.

They turned the page.

Christmas, 1965. The living room, the tree hung with tinsel icicles, Maren herself a girl of maybe ten sitting cross-legged by the fireplace with a doll in her lap. And beside the tree, between the fireplace and the window where it had no business being, the same chair. Spindle-backed. Empty. Angled toward the camera.

Kestrel's mouth had gone dry. They licked their lips and tasted dust and something faintly metallic, like sucking on a penny, like the taste the air got in the ward when a monitor flatlined down the hall.

*Amberdales. 1961–1974.* The bargain was made in 1963. The journal said so—the journal they hadn't found yet, though its weight already waited behind the pantry wall like a stone in a shoe. Kestrel didn't know that yet. All they knew was that the chair appeared in 1963 and did not appear in any memory they owned.

They did what nurses do. They worked the problem. They carried the first album to the kitchen table, set it under the brightest window, and went back for the others, laying them out in chronological order across the scarred oak like a patient's chart. Sixty years of Amberdales, spread end to end. Then they walked the line of them slowly, twice, the way you'd walk a rash, checking margins.

The chair was in every photograph.

Every single one.

In the harvest pictures it stood among the corn shocks, absurd and domestic, waiting for a farmer who never came in from the field. In the winter pictures it sat in the snow with no tracks around it, snow settled undisturbed against its legs, as if it had fallen there from the sky or risen up out of the ground. In the wedding photo—Cousin Dell, 1991, everyone laughing, someone's beer raised mid-toast—the chair stood at the end of the receiving line, and Kestrel noticed, with a lurch, that the guests nearest it were all leaning very slightly away. Not looking at it. Leaning away from it, the way bodies lean from a smell.

Nobody ever looked at it. That was the thing that made the skin crawl slowest. Across sixty years and dozens of faces, not one person in one photograph turned toward the chair. It occupied space in every frame and attention in none, and Kestrel understood, with the particular horror of the observant, that they were looking at something that had spent decades learning how to be almost invisible—and that lately, it was getting worse at it.

Or better. Depending on what it wanted.

* * *

By early afternoon Kestrel had reached the last album. *Kestrel.* Their own name in Maren's hand. Their knees ached from the floorboards; the tea they'd made at noon sat untouched and cold, a skin on its surface. Outside, the light had begun to lean, the shadows of the maples stretching long fingers across the yard toward the house, and somewhere past the tree line the old well waited under its collapsed roof of rotted planks, doing whatever it did down there in the dark. Kestrel did not look toward it. You didn't check on things like that. Everyone who'd grown up rural knew that much: you didn't check.

They opened the album to the first page.

A hospital photograph, 1989. A newborn wrapped in a yellow blanket, face scrunched red and furious, held by a woman Kestrel barely remembered—their mother, who had left when they were four and whose face lived now mostly in photographs, which suddenly seemed like a much more ominous sentence than it used to.

Kestrel looked at the baby. The baby looked furiously back across thirty-odd years.

Behind the mother, in the corner of the hospital room, stood the chair.

Kestrel sat down hard on the kitchen bench. The bench scraped; the sound went through the quiet house like a shout. Because this was different. This was not a porch or a parlor or a snowy yard. This was a hospital room, three hundred miles from the farm, a room the chair could not have reached by any road—

Unless it had never needed roads. Unless it had never needed to reach anywhere. Unless it had always been wherever the Amberdales were, the way a shadow is wherever the body is, and every photograph Kestrel had ever been in had contained it, and Kestrel had never once seen it until today.

Why today?

The question arrived with its answer already attached, cold and complete, the way diagnoses sometimes do: *because today is the day you can see it. Something changed. Something decided you were allowed to see.*

They turned pages faster now, no longer methodical, a nurse losing a pulse. Birthday parties. First days of school. A gap-toothed kid holding up a fish at Miller's Pond, the chair on the bank behind them, patient as a heron. Kestrel at twelve, at sixteen, at twenty in their nursing school cap, and always, always, at the edge of every frame, the spindle-backed chair, angled toward the camera, empty.

Empty.

Until the last third of the album.

It was subtle at first, and Kestrel might have missed it if they hadn't spent the whole afternoon teaching themselves the chair's geography. In a photo from 2009—Maren's seventieth, the whole surviving family crammed onto the porch steps—the chair stood in its usual place at the frame's edge, but the seat was no longer quite empty. There was a darkness on it. Not a shadow; shadows don't have texture. Something like a smudge of soot shaped vaguely like a lap. Like someone had begun, very gently, to sit down.

In the next photograph, two years later, the darkness had shoulders.

Kestrel's hands were shaking. They noticed this the way you notice weather—as fact, unrelated to self. In the next picture the shape had a suggestion of arms resting on armrests, a suggestion of a head tilted, almost companionable, toward the family it declined to join. By the final page—a photograph Kestrel had never seen, taken apparently at some Amberdale gathering after Kestrel had moved away for work—the figure in the chair was nearly complete. Still blurred, still wrong, like a person seen through frosted glass, like a face remembered by someone who never loved it. But seated. Settled. Waiting with the terrible patience of furniture.

And it was turned toward the camera. Toward whoever held it.

Toward Kestrel, reading it now.

* * *

There was one photograph left, tucked loose in the inside back cover where loose photographs always hide. Kestrel drew it out with two fingers, careful, the way you'd draw a splinter.

It was a child, maybe seven years old, standing in the farmhouse kitchen in overalls, holding a jar of fireflies up toward the camera as if offering it light. The summer light poured gold across the floorboards. The child was grinning with a missing tooth, and the grin was Kestrel's grin—it had to be, the album was labeled *Kestrel*, the handwriting was Maren's—but Kestrel stared at the face and felt the floor tilt gently beneath the bench.

Something was wrong with it.

Not obviously. Not the way the chair was wrong. Wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the painter has met the subject only once, briefly, in bad light. The eyes were a shade too far apart. The smile pulled a half-millimeter higher on the left than Kestrel's smile had ever pulled. The jaw was almost right. The ears were almost right. Everything was *almost* right, assembled by something working from a description, from a report, from a list of features passed along secondhand in the dark.

Kestrel raised their phone and opened the camera, hands unsteady, and took a selfie without wanting to, without deciding to, the way you flinch. They looked at the screen. They looked at the photograph. They laid the phone on the table beside the loose print, side by side, thirty years apart, and compared.

Same face. Same face the way two keys cut from the same blank are the same key—one opens the lock, and one merely fits partway, and jams, and you stand in the dark outside your own door wondering why.

The child in the photograph was not Kestrel Amberdale.

The child in the photograph was something that had studied Kestrel Amberdale.

Outside, the light had thickened toward late afternoon, and the shadows of the maples lay all the way across the yard now, reaching the porch boards, reaching the door. Somewhere down the road an engine coughed and died—Hollis Vane's truck, though Kestrel couldn't know that yet, couldn't know he was sitting at his own kitchen table with a shotgun broken open across his knees and sixty years of guilt on his shoulders, working up the nerve to drive over and beg a stranger to burn down the house they'd inherited.

Kestrel looked at the last page of the album, at the nearly finished figure in the nearly finished chair, and understood that it had not been waiting for the family.

It had been waiting for them. Learning them. Practicing their face in the dark of every photograph they'd ever appeared in, patient as a hospice clock, counting down.

On the back of the loose photograph, in Maren's severe hand, there was a single line Kestrel hadn't noticed, small enough to hide in the margin:

*He asked for the one with your name. I told him it wasn't ready yet.*

Kestrel read it three times. The kitchen grew colder around them, though no window had opened, though the stove was off, though the afternoon sun still stood full on the western fields.

*I told him it wasn't ready yet.*

Which meant that someday, someone had said otherwise.
