## 2. The Second Call

The thing about working the overnight shift at a five-hundred-watt AM station is that you develop a very particular relationship with silence. It isn't empty. It's full — of furnace hums, of the tick of the transmitter cooling between songs, of your own breathing getting louder the longer nobody calls in. I'd spent four years learning to fill that silence with my voice, and I liked to think I was good at it. Warm but not cloying. A nightlight with a broadcast license.

So when the phone lit up at 2:14 a.m. on Tuesday and the board read CALLER: UNKNOWN, I did what I always do. I took a sip of cold coffee, thumbed the button, and said, "You're live with Felix Fairweather, it's *The Long Dark*, who's out there tonight?"

"Tell Harold the stove was never fixed."

I knew that voice. Not from life — from the obituary page of the *Cedar Hollow Courier*, which I read every Thursday because small-town journalism is a contact sport and I like to know who's winning. Maribel Doyle, sixty-eight, died six days ago of a heart that had been giving her fair warning for a decade.

"Ma'am," I said carefully, "if this is a prank—"

"He keeps saying he'll get to it. He won't get to it. He'll stand in front of it for twenty minutes and then watch television instead." A pause, and then, softer, with something underneath it that made the hair on my arms stand up and take notice: "Tell him it's all right. Tell him I never minded the cold kitchen. I only minded eating alone."

I should have cut her off. That's protocol, more or less — unidentified callers, possible hoaxes, the station's liability insurance has opinions about grief. Instead I heard myself say, "Is there anything else?"

"No," she said, almost kindly. "That was everything. That was always everything."

The line went dead in the old-fashioned way, the click of a receiver set down gently, and I sat there in the booth with my coffee going colder and the transmitter humming its one long note, and I told myself what any reasonable person tells themselves at 2:15 in the morning: prank. Grief-tourist. Somebody who'd read the same obituary page and thought they were funny.

Except pranks have a shape. They build. They want a reaction, so they escalate, they perform. This hadn't performed. This had *delivered* — flat, unhurried, like a woman reading a grocery list she'd had sixty years to memorize. And the detail about standing in front of the stove for twenty minutes before giving up — that wasn't a guess. That was an observation. You can't fake the specific texture of a marriage from the outside.

I played three songs in a row without talking, which for me is the equivalent of screaming into a pillow.

* * *

Wednesday night, 1:51 a.m., the board lit up again. UNKNOWN.

"Felix Fairweather," said a man's voice, gravelly and amused, like he already knew me. "Still doing the show. Good. Your grandfather said you would."

"I'm sorry — who is this?"

"Dale Pruitt. Died Friday. You'll find me in the paper under 'beloved,' which is generous, but the paper's always generous." He coughed, wet and real. "Message first, questions after. Tell my daughter Karen the money in the coffee can behind the paint cans in the garage is hers. Not her husband's. Hers. She'll know why."

"That's — Mr. Pruitt, I can't just—"

"You can and you will, son, because I can't, and believe me, I've tried. Been trying since Friday. Phones don't work where I am except yours." A chuckle. "Yours and one other, but we'll get to that."

My mouth had gone dry enough to store grain in. "Where are you calling from?"

There was a pause, and in the pause I could hear something behind him — a low sound, rhythmic, almost like water moving somewhere deep underground, or like a room full of people breathing in unison.

"Close," he said. "Closer than you'd think. Now. Your turn to answer a question, since you're the switchboard operator of the great beyond these days. When you were seven, what did you bury under the crabapple tree at your grandmother's place on Hollow Creek Road?"

And here's the thing — I knew. Instantly, completely, the way you know your own name. A tin box containing a plastic tyrannosaurus, three marbles, and a note in crayon that read TO WHOEVER FINDS THIS: I AM RUNNING AWAY BUT ONLY TO THE END OF THE DRIVEWAY. I had told no living soul about that box. My grandmother Edith had died when I was nine; the house had stood empty ever since; whatever I buried under that tree had stayed between me, the tree, and the worms.

"A dinosaur," I said. My voice came out thin as wire. "How do you know that?"

"Because she talked about it," Dale Pruitt said, gentle now, the amusement gone. "For years. Every time she passed that tree. *Felix buried a treasure there once.* She'd laugh. She loved you somethin' fierce, kid." Another pause, longer. The breathing-sound behind him swelled, just slightly, like a tide coming in. "She asked me to tell you something, if I got through. She said: *the well still knows your name.*"

The line clicked dead before I could ask the only question left, which was *what well*.

I sat in the booth until dawn. I didn't play music. At some point I realized I was holding the dead-air light button down with my thumb like a panic bar, keeping the silence off the airwaves, keeping it mine.

* * *

Thursday I called in sick. Then I felt ridiculous about calling in sick from a radio show — it's not like the microphone catches things — and drove in anyway, and spent the shift playing an entire album of ambient whale sounds because it required zero words from me. The request line stayed dark. I told myself the whales were why. I told myself a lot of things Thursday.

Friday night, 3:03 a.m. — the hour I privately call the soft spot, the time when the town is thinnest — the board lit up twice at once. Line one. Line two. Both UNKNOWN.

I stared at them the way you stare at two growling dogs. Then I answered line one.

"Tell Ruthie I found the earring," said a woman's voice, young, apologetic. "It was in the couch the whole time. She tore the house apart over that earring in 1987 and I let her blame the vacuum cleaner. Tell her I'm sorry. Tell her the vacuum cleaner deserves an apology too."

"And line two?" I heard myself ask, which was insane, which was inviting it, but some part of me had apparently decided that if the dead were going to use my show as a message service, I was at least going to run a professional operation.

Line two was an older woman, crisp as starched linen. "This is for the Fairweather boy. Tell him his grandmother kept the good china wrapped in the cedar chest, second shelf, under the winter quilts, and he shouldn't let the mice have it. Tell him the blue pattern. He'll remember the blue pattern."

I did remember. Sunday dinners. Gravy boats. Being allowed to hold one plate, with both hands, over the abyss between cupboard and table, feeling like a bomb technician.

"How do you all know me?" I asked. "None of you ever met me. I moved away when I was ten. I've been gone twenty years."

"Oh, honey," the crisp voice said, and there was such fondness in it that my eyes stung before I understood why. "We didn't need to meet you. We watched you grow up the way you watch a garden. From the ground." A pause. "You came back to town last month and you didn't visit the house. We noticed."

"We?"

But both lines had gone quiet — not dead, exactly. Quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone in it decides to stop talking. I could hear, faintly, on both lines at once, that low rhythmic sound again. Breathing, or water, or a congregation. Then a click, and nothing.

I put my head down on the mixing desk, cheek against the cool fader caps, and let myself shake for exactly one song's worth of time. Then I wiped my face, rolled the next track, and started making a list, because lists are how I keep the world from dissolving. Caller one: Maribel Doyle, stove, son named Harold. Caller two: Dale Pruitt, coffee can, daughter Karen. Callers three and four: an earring, and china, and a house on a road whose name I had been carefully not thinking about for two days.

Hollow Creek Road.

Under the list, my hand wrote, without consulting the rest of me: *the well still knows your name.*

* * *

Priya Chandra noticed on Monday, because Priya notices everything; it's her superpower and occasionally her crime. She manages KCHL the way harbor pilots manage tankers — quietly, constantly, with an unshakable conviction that disaster is always one careless move away. She appeared in the booth doorway at 4 a.m. holding two coffees, which she never does, and looked at me the way you look at a check-engine light.

"You've played the whale album twice this week," she said.

"It's a bold artistic choice."

"Felix." She set a coffee on the desk beside me. "Your talk breaks used to be little stories. Little jokes. This week you've said eleven words total, and six of them were 'that was Felix Fairweather, good night.' You flinched when the phone rang Saturday. Actually flinched. I watched you flinch at a telephone."

"It's nothing. Some weird callers."

"Some weird callers," she repeated, in the tone of a woman filing the phrase away for later cross-examination. She didn't push. That was the thing about Priya — she had a genius for knowing exactly how much rope a person needed, and she'd hand it over silently, coiled neat, and stand there holding the other end. "Take Wednesday off. I'll cover. I like the overnight shift. Nobody bothers me and I get the good vending machine crackers before Dale from maintenance eats them all."

"Priya, you hate the overnight shift. You told me it's like being paid to guard an empty parking lot."

"I contain multitudes." She paused in the doorway, backlit by the hallway's flickering fluorescent, and for a second she looked like she wanted to say something else — something with weight in it. What she actually said was: "Whatever it is, it's not nothing. But it'll keep till Wednesday. Drink your coffee."

She covered Wednesday. And Thursday. And when I came in Friday looking, I'm told, like a man who'd been exhumed and reburied hastily, she took one look, handed me her own coat because the booth runs cold, and said, "Go home. I've got it. Don't argue, I already changed the log."

She never asked why. Not once, those first weeks. I think she understood, in her harbor-pilot way, that some ships you don't question — you just steady them against the current until they're ready to tell you what's dragging on their hull.

* * *

Saturday night I couldn't stay home. Home was worse — home was quiet in the wrong way, quiet with a listening quality to it. So I drove to the station at midnight and sat in the booth with the lights low, not broadcasting, just existing among equipment, which is my version of hiding under blankets.

At 2:47 a.m., the board lit up. All eight lines. Simultaneously. Eight UNKNOWNs stacked up the screen like a column of black teeth.

I didn't answer. I want that on the record: I sat on my hands, literally, and did not answer.

They waited. Four minutes. Five. The lights pulsed once, softly, all together, like a slow blink. And then, without my touching anything, the speaker above the board crackled and a voice came through anyway — many voices, braided, men and women and at least one child, speaking in perfect unison, calm as a weather report:

"*Hollow Creek Road. The house with the well behind it. Come see where you're from, Felix. Come sit down. Dinner's been waiting a long time.*"

Then all eight lines went dark at once, and the ON AIR sign over the booth door — which I had not turned on — glowed red in the empty station, and somewhere far below the floorboards, or maybe just below the range of hearing, something hummed one low, patient note.

I sat there a long time. Then I picked up the phone and dialed the one number I'd been avoiding, and when Priya answered on the first ring — awake at 3 a.m., as if she'd been waiting — all I said was:

"I need to borrow your car. Mine won't start, and I have to go to Hollow Creek Road."

There was a pause, and then Priya Chandra, manager of KCHL, guardian of vending machine crackers, said the words that I'd remember for the rest of my lopsided life:

"I'm driving. Give me ten minutes. And Felix — bring the list."
