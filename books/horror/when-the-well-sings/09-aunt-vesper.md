## 9. Aunt Vesper

She arrived the way weather arrives in October—suddenly, and with a smell of cold stone that had no business being indoors.

I was at the kitchen table of my apartment above the laundromat, eating cereal at four in the afternoon because my body clock had given up on the concept of meals entirely, when the doorbell rang. Nobody rings my doorbell. The buzzer downstairs is broken, has been broken since I moved in, and the delivery drivers just leave things on the stoop like offerings to a small indifferent god.

The bell rang again. Three notes, patient as a hymn.

I looked through the peephole and saw a woman standing very straight on the landing, wearing a coat the color of wet slate and an expression I recognized from photographs I'd been taking down off walls all week: the Fairweather jaw, the Fairweather level gaze, the mouth set like it had been asked to smile once in 1953 and had politely declined.

"Open the door, Felix," she said, to the wood, without raising her voice. "It's cold out here, and I've been cold for a very long time."

Every instinct I owned said don't. Every instinct I'd developed since October said *especially* don't—the dead call me, the frost is writing letters to me, the house on Hollow Creek Road hums when I say hello. A woman who knows my name and stands outside my door smelling of winter is either the best thing or the worst thing that could happen, and lately the odds have not been kind.

But she said *Felix* the way family says it, with the second syllable softened, and God help me, I opened the door.

* * *

"You look like your grandfather," she said, stepping past me into the apartment as if she'd hung her coat on that hook a thousand times. She hadn't; there was no hook. She held the coat anyway, over her arm, like a woman waiting for a hook to be installed. "Around the eyes. He had the same look whenever someone told him something he didn't want to hear. Like a man reading his own bill."

"I'm sorry," I said, because it seemed like the thing to say, "who are you?"

"Vesper." She turned and looked at me fully, and up close the wrongness of her resolved itself into something specific: she was old the way a river is old. Her face was unlined. Her hands were not. There were decades in her knuckles, centuries almost, and none of it had bothered to travel north to her face. "Vesper Fairweather. Your grandmother Edith's sister. Which makes me your aunt, though I suppose nobody's been by to tell you so."

"There's no Vesper Fairweather," I said. "I checked. After—" I stopped. After the calls started, after Priya and I spent two nights with the county records spread across the station's break table like tarot cards, after we found Fairweathers going back to 1891 and one gap in the ledger where a name should have been. "There's no record."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "There wouldn't be."

She moved through my apartment the way you move through a house you grew up in—touching nothing, knowing everything. She paused at the window where the frost had come in the night before, the spiral pattern, the one that pointed toward Hollow Creek Road, and her expression did something complicated. Anger, mostly. But underneath the anger, something that looked horribly like guilt.

"You've been waking the house," she said.

"I don't know what that means."

"Don't you?" She turned. "The calls. The messages. You've been answering them. Delivering them. Going out to Hollow Creek and talking to the well like it's a cousin at a reunion. Do you know what the house has been doing since you started? It's been setting the table, Felix. Every night. Every single night since your first broadcast, it sets one more place."

My cereal sat abandoned in its bowl, going soft, and I thought about the photograph in my desk drawer—the one I'd taken from grandma's house against every instinct I possessed, the one with the extra chair that had followed me home and had been getting closer in every picture ever since. Last night it had been in the foreground. Last night I could see the weave of the chair's seat.

"Who are you really?" I asked. "Because the dead call my show, and I've learned what voices sound like when they're wearing something. And yours sounds—"

"Alive?" She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the corners, and it terrified me more than anything the well had done yet. "Yes. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

She made tea in my kitchen without asking where anything was, which meant she knew, which meant either she'd been watching me for weeks or the house remembered her hands, and I honestly couldn't decide which option I liked less. She set a cup in front of me—chamomile, no honey, exactly how I take it—and sat down across from me and folded those ancient hands together.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said, "and you're going to want to interrupt, because you're a broadcaster and broadcasters think silence is a hole they fell into. Don't. This story only works told whole."

So I drank my tea and let her talk.

"In 1891, this town was dying. Bad harvests, bad winters, children leaving and not coming back. Your great-great-grandfather Aldous Fairweather—he was a practical man, which is the most dangerous kind—found the spring under Hollow Creek and found what lived with the spring, and he made a bargain. Not a contract. Bargains with that thing aren't written down anywhere you could subpoena them. They're written in the family. In us." She touched her own sternum briefly, lightly, like knocking on a door. "One voice, freely given, each generation. In exchange: mild winters, full fields, a town that prospers while everyone else freezes."

"And the voice goes—" I couldn't finish it.

"Down," Vesper said simply. "Into the well. To keep it company. It isn't evil, whatever you've imagined. It's lonely. It was lonely before there were people, before there was language, and somewhere along the way it learned that a voice is the shape loneliness takes when it gets hungry. One Fairweather, every generation, sings it quiet. And the town forgets. That's part of the bargain too. Forgetting is a mercy, and it's also how the bargain keeps itself. Nobody remembers long enough to object."

"The ledger," I said. "In the archive. The last entry is unsigned."

Something flickered across her face—fast, gone, but I catch things for a living. Pauses. What people almost say.

"Yes," she said. "That would be mine."

* * *

Here is what she told me, in a voice that stayed perfectly level except at the very bottom, where it trembled like the last inch of water in a glass:

In 1991, it was her turn. She'd known since she was a girl—it's the kind of thing you know, the way you know your own birthday. And when the solstice came, and the house set the table, and her mother stood at the door of the pantry with tears running down her face and her hand outstretched, Vesper Fairweather ran. Out the back, past the well, down Hollow Creek Road, and she didn't stop until she was three states away, and she never came back, and she never aged, because the bargain doesn't release you. It waits. It holds you the way a debt holds you, in suspension, interest accruing.

"And Edith went instead," I said quietly.

"Edith went instead." Vesper looked at her tea. "My little sister walked down into that well in my place, singing, because she loved me, and because someone had to, and because the town would have frozen and starved otherwise. She went willingly. That's the terrible arithmetic of it—willingness is the currency, and love will make anyone willing." She looked up. "You should be proud of her. I've had thirty years to be proud of her and it hasn't helped at all."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below us, a dryer thumped through its cycle, ordinary as anything, and I wanted to climb inside it.

"So why are you here now?" I asked. "Thirty years late. Why come back?"

"Because you've been waking the house," she said again, and now the fury came through clean. "Because you took their calls and delivered their messages and went out to that road and spoke aloud, and the house heard you, Felix. The house has been asleep at the table for thirty years, waiting, and you knocked on the china. The chair is set for you now. It's been set since your first broadcast, and every week you sit behind that microphone you pull it a little closer, and at solstice—"

"At solstice it expects me."

"At solstice it collects you." She leaned forward. "Unless."

* * *

There it was. I'd been waiting for the *unless* since she rang the bell. Everything in this story comes with an unless. The town prospers, unless. The winters stay mild, unless. Somebody loves you enough, unless.

"Here is what I am offering," Vesper said, and her voice changed—softened, warmed, became the voice of every aunt at every holiday table, the voice of family, and I understood with a cold clarity that this was the most dangerous thing in the room, more dangerous than the well, more dangerous than the frost. "You leave. Before solstice. Get in your car tonight and drive until the radio stops picking up this town, and keep driving. The bargain wants a Fairweather, yes—but it wants a willing one, and it can't follow willingness across a county line. If you're gone, the claim lapses. It reverts."

"To whom?"

Her smile again. Warm corners. Teeth. "To the next in line. Which is me. I'll go down in your place, Felix. I'll sing the song. Thirty years of running, paid off in one night, and you get to live your life in some other town where the mirrors behave and the frost writes nothing. Isn't that generous? Doesn't that sound like mercy?"

And here's the thing—I wanted it. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only thing I've got left that the well hasn't learned to imitate. I sat there with chamomile going cold in my hands and I wanted to run. I thought about Priya covering my shifts without asking why. I thought about Mrs. Okafor on Maple Street, who wept when I told her the stove was never fixed, who hugged me on her porch like I was delivering her mother's ghost in person. I thought about all of it, and underneath all of it, I thought: *drive, drive, drive.*

"And if I leave," I said slowly, "you go down. Willingly."

"Gladly," she said, and the word came out a half-second too fast, the way words do when they've been rehearsed.

I set down the cup. "You lied to me twice in the last five minutes."

"Felix—"

"Once about being glad. And once—" I stood up, because sitting felt like agreeing. "The ledger entry is unsigned because you never signed it. Because signing it means accepting it. And the claim doesn't revert to the runner, does it? The bargain doesn't reward the person who fled. It holds them. If I leave, it doesn't get me, and it doesn't gracefully pass to you—it keeps *waiting*, and you keep not-aging, and the town keeps its mild winters on credit until somebody settles up. You're not offering to take my place. You're offering to hand me the bill and call it a gift."

For a moment—just a moment—the warmth went out of her face entirely, and what was underneath was thirty years of cold, and I understood why the house remembered her hands.

Then she laughed. Short, sharp, almost admiring. "Edith's blood," she said. "You listen like she listened. She caught me in exactly two lies our whole childhood, and both times she just stood up like that, like the truth required better posture." She rose too, unhurried, and collected her coat from the hook that still wasn't there. "Fine. Then hear the rest of it, nephew, and hear it plainly: if you don't leave, and you don't go willingly, the well takes what it's owed however it can. It's patient, but it isn't gentle. Ask the frost. Ask the callers. Ask yourself why your reflection has started looking at you like it's taking notes."

"It hasn't," I said, and then stopped, because it had. Half a heartbeat. Just half a heartbeat, lately, like the mirror was thinking it over.

"Mm," said Vesper, at the door. "Solstice is nine days away. Leave, stay, or find a third thing nobody's tried in a hundred and thirty years—those are your options, and I'll tell you for free that I only know how to survive the first one." She stepped onto the landing, into the gray light, and paused. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much, coming from me—I am sorry about Edith. She sang beautifully. The whole valley used to stop and listen."

The stairwell door closed behind her, and the smell of cold stone faded slowly, like a chord decaying.

I stood in my kitchen for a long time. Then I got my coat, and my keys, and the photograph out of the desk drawer—in which the empty chair now sat close enough that I could see, carved faintly into its backrest, a single letter: *F*.

Nine days. I picked up the phone and called the one person I knew who'd already decided to stand next to me in the dark.

"Priya," I said when she answered. "I need the archive open tonight. Grandma Edith left us something. I can feel it."
