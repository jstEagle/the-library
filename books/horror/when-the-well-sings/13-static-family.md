## 13. Static Family

Priya Chandra had run the overnight board at WQRT for eleven years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about the people who listened to radio at three in the morning.

One: they were almost never asleep because they didn't want to be.

Two: they were almost never alone because they wanted to be.

Three: if you gave them something to hold onto—a voice, a task, a reason—they would hold on with both hands and not let go until you pried them loose or the sun came up, whichever came first.

She was counting on all three of those things now, standing in the station's break room with a whiteboard she'd stolen from the supply closet and a marker that only half worked, explaining to Felix Fairweather why she had just invited forty strangers to spend the winter solstice in a haunted house.

"You can't do this alone," she said. "That's the whole point of what your grandmother wrote. Many voices, given freely. Not one martyr."

"I wasn't going to martyr myself," Felix said, in the tone of someone who had absolutely been considering martyring themselves.

"Priya, I've known you for two years. You alphabetize the tea bags. You once called the fire department because the microwave sparked. You are not the kind of person who lets people walk into wells alone." She capped the marker. "And I'm not the kind of person who lets her favorite host get eaten by a hole in the ground, so here we are."

Felix looked at the whiteboard. Priya had written VIGIL — SOLSTICE NIGHT in block letters, and beneath it, a list already forming: names, phone numbers, availability, skills. It looked less like an occult counter-ritual and more like a community potluck sign-up sheet, which, honestly, was the aesthetic she was going for. Terror was easier to carry when it came with a casserole.

"Forty people," Felix said quietly. "You actually got forty people."

"Thirty-eight confirmed. Two maybes. The maybe from the truck stop might show up late; he works till midnight." She tapped the list. "And these aren't strangers, Felix. Not really. These are the people who've been calling your show for years. The insomniacs. The widows. The guy who drives long-haul and just wants a voice in the cab. They've been your congregation since before either of us knew there was a church."

* * *

The first call had come in four days earlier, and Priya had almost hung up on it.

"—yes, hello, is this the station? Is Felix there? This is Dot. Dorothy Okafor, I listen every night, I never call but—" The woman's voice was thin and urgent, the way voices get when someone has been rehearsing a sentence for hours before working up the nerve to say it out loud. "I need to know if it's true. About the house on Hollow Creek. My sister heard from her neighbor that the dead have been calling in, and I thought—well, I thought it was nonsense, but then my husband—"

She stopped. Priya waited. On the board behind her, the ON AIR light glowed red over Studio B, where Felix was playing a record with hands that still weren't quite steady, even now, even after everything.

"My husband passed in March," Dot said finally. "And last Tuesday, the phone rang at two in the morning, and it was him. Or it sounded like him. And he said—" Her breath caught. "He said he'd been trying to reach me, and that the lines were open now, and that I should listen to the radio man. That the radio man needed help. So I'm calling to help. What do you need?"

Priya had stood there with the receiver against her ear and felt something crack open in her chest—not painfully, exactly. More like a window unlatching in a room that had been shut too long.

Because here was the thing about Priya Chandra: she did not believe in ghosts. She believed in signal integrity, in backup generators, in the FCC, in showing up fifteen minutes early. She had spent eleven years being the sane one in a building full of night people, and she had liked it that way. Sane was a load-bearing wall.

But she'd also heard the calls. All of them. She'd pulled the logs, cross-referenced the obituaries, driven out to the county records office on her day off like a woman possessed, and come back with a spreadsheet that said, in cold black cells, what her sanity kept trying to deny: forty-one callers over three weeks, all deceased, all buried within a mile of a well on Hollow Creek Road, spanning four generations. The data did not care what she believed. The data just sat there, patient as frost.

So when Dot Okafor asked *what do you need*, Priya took a breath and answered honestly.

"We need everyone," she said. "Can you be at the corner of Main and Hollow Creek on solstice night? Bring a thermos. And, Mrs. Okafor—bring anyone else who's gotten a call."

There was a pause, and then Dot laughed—a wet, startled, relieved sound. "Oh, honey. Everyone's gotten a call."

* * *

They came to the station instead of the house, at first. Priya hadn't planned it that way, but word traveled the way it always did in a town that size—one phone tree branching into ten—and by Thursday evening the lobby smelled like coffee and cinnamon rolls and faintly of wet wool, because it had started snowing and nobody wanted to leave their cars running.

Felix stood in the middle of it looking like a man who had walked into his own surprise party and couldn't find the exit.

Priya watched them work the room, because watching Felix work a room was one of her private pleasures, right up there with a clean signal chain and the first sip of morning chai. Felix had this way of listening—full-body, head tilted, like whatever you were saying was the most important audio they'd ever mixed. Half these people had poured their hearts out to that listening across a decade of dead air, and now here it was, embodied, blinking at them under fluorescent lights, asking how they were doing and actually waiting for the answer.

An old man in a barn coat gripped Felix's hand with both of his. "You told my wife the stove was never fixed," he said. "Maribel. You told her son, and he came home, and we got it fixed together, the two of us, before—" His jaw worked. "Before the frost got bad. She knew. Somehow she knew I'd need to know how to light the pilot myself."

"I just made a phone call," Felix said.

"You made forty phone calls," the old man said. "Don't you dare get modest on me now, young lady. Modesty is for people with time."

Priya snorted into her coffee. Felix shot her a look that said *you're enjoying this far too much*, and she raised her mug in cheerful confirmation.

By nine o'clock, the lobby had organized itself with the terrifying efficiency of church ladies and union stewards, which were frequently the same people. There was a sign-up sheet for shifts. Someone—Dot, inevitably—had appointed herself head of refreshments and was taking requests for solstice-night soup with the gravity of a general planning a campaign. The long-haul driver, whose name turned out to be Marcus and who was built like a vending machine, had volunteered to bring chains for the tires and a generator, and then had quietly added, "and I'll park my rig across the driveway, in case anybody needs to leave fast," and Priya had written *MARCUS — EXIT STRATEGY* on the whiteboard with genuine emotion.

It was around ten when a small woman with silver hair and a cardigan the color of eggplant approached the whiteboard, studied it, and said, "Who's keeping the list of names?"

"That would be me," Priya said.

"Good. Then write down Ruth Ellery-Coleman, and put me down for the singing part, because I directed the church choir for thirty-one years and somebody needs to keep forty amateurs on pitch at four in the morning." She peered at Priya over her glasses. "You do know it has to be sung, don't you? Not spoken. Sung. My grandmother used to hum it while she shelled peas, and she'd stop if you asked her about it, but I remember the shape of it. I can teach it. Whether I should is another question, but I can."

Priya wrote the name down. "Mrs. Ellery-Coleman, you're the best thing that's happened to this operation since I fixed the transmitter hum in March."

"It's Ruth. And the hum was the well, dear. Everything's the well." She said it mildly, the way you'd say *everything's the weather*, and wandered off toward the cinnamon rolls.

Felix appeared at Priya's elbow. "Did she just say everything's the well?"

"Everything's the well," Priya confirmed. "Also, we have a choir director, a generator, an exit strategy, and someone's cousin who does HVAC and thinks he can figure out the house's old furnace. We are, against all odds, becoming competent."

Felix was quiet for a moment. When Priya glanced over, they were looking at the room—at Dot refilling coffee cups, at Marcus showing a teenager how the generator worked, at Ruth already conducting an invisible choir with a bread knife—and there was an expression on their face that Priya had only ever heard through the headphones at 3 a.m., in the pauses between songs. Something raw and wondering. Like a person who had spent their whole life broadcasting into the dark suddenly realizing the dark had been full of people the entire time.

"They came," Felix said. "I keep thinking they won't, and then they keep coming."

"Of course they came." Priya bumped their shoulder with hers. "You picked up the phone for them every night for six years. What did you think was going to happen?"

* * *

The planning meeting proper happened at midnight, because of course it did; you could take the people out of the overnight shift but you couldn't take the overnight shift out of the people. Thirty-eight of them crammed into the studio, folding chairs scraping, and Priya stood at the front with the whiteboard and laid it out straight, because she figured they deserved straight.

"Here's what we know," she said. "On solstice night, the ritual that's kept—call it the thing under the well—quiet for a hundred and thirty years comes due. The original terms required one Fairweather to give themselves to it. Forever. Felix's grandmother went down in 1991, and the bill has come due again, and the house has been setting a place at the table for Felix ever since." Murmurs ran through the room. In the back, Marcus crossed his arms like a drawbridge closing. "But Edith Fairweather left instructions. Hidden ones. The seal doesn't have to be one voice given under duress. It can be many voices, given freely, sung over the well until dawn. Nobody goes down. Everybody sings."

"And if we're off-key?" someone called out, and got a laugh, and Priya let them have it, because laughter was load-bearing too.

"Then we're off-key together, and apparently that counts. Ruth will handle pitch. I'll handle logistics. Felix handles—" She paused, and looked at Felix, who was leaning against the mixing console with their arms folded, and something in her chest went tight and fond and terrified all at once. "Felix handles the broadcast. Because here's the part I need you all to understand. This isn't a vigil where we sit in a circle and hold hands. This is a broadcast. Every voice in that house goes out over the transmitter, live, all night. And every person in town who hears it—whoever's awake, whoever's scared, whoever's lonely—that voice joins whether they know it or not. That's the design. That's Edith's math. The more voices, the stronger the seal."

"So we're not just singing," Dot said slowly. "We're putting out a call."

"We're putting out the biggest call this town's ever heard," Priya said. "And knowing this town, and knowing you people—" she gestured at the room, at the thermoses and the blankets and the determined faces, "—I think they'll answer."

A hand went up near the front. A young woman Priya vaguely recognized from the diner, the one who always called in to request Patsy Cline. "What happens to the dead?" she asked. "The callers. After. Do they—do they go somewhere better, or..."

Nobody answered for a second. Then Felix pushed off the console and said, quietly, "I don't know. But I know they've been stuck. Forty-one people, four generations, all circling the same mile of ground, holding messages they couldn't deliver. If the seal holds, maybe they get to finish. Maybe they get to rest." They looked down at their hands. "I'd like to think the last thing the well takes is its prisoners."

Ruth Ellery-Coleman cleared her throat. "Well," she said briskly, in the tone of a woman who had sat through enough funerals to know when to move things along, "we'd best learn the hymn, then. Fetch me that piano in the corner and somebody turn the microphones on. If we're going to sing over a hungry hole in the ground, we are not going to embarrass ourselves."

* * *

They practiced until nearly four in the morning, and it was terrible, and it was wonderful.

The hymn was older than any of them—Ruth taught it phrase by phrase, humming first, then words in an English so old it barely qualified—and the strange thing, the thing Priya would lie awake turning over afterward, was how fast everyone learned it. Too fast. As if the melody had been waiting in them the way frost waits in the ground, needing only the season to arrive. Felix harmonized without being taught, eyes going wide halfway through the second verse, and Ruth pointed her bread knife at them and said, "That. Whatever that line is, you keep that. Don't ask me where it came from. Some harmonies you inherit."

When they finally broke up, bleary and warm, Priya stood in the parking lot with Felix and watched taillights scatter down Main Street. Snow was coming down soft and unhurried, and for a moment—just a moment—it fell in ordinary, random flakes, no spirals, no patterns, nothing pointing anywhere.

"Hey," she said. "For the record. Whatever happens out there. Whatever the house does, whatever the well does, whatever your aunt does—" Felix flinched slightly at Vesper's name, and Priya pretended not to notice. "—this isn't you alone anymore. It stopped being you alone the moment Dot called. You've got a congregation, Fairweather. Congratulations. They're weird and they can't sing and one of them brought a generator the size of a refrigerator. But they're yours."

Felix laughed—a real one, cracked and surprised. "Static family," they said. "That's what we are. People who found each other through the noise."

"Don't get sentimental on me, I'm dehydrated." But Priya was smiling as she said it, and she suspected Felix knew.

They were halfway to their cars when Felix stopped walking.

"Priya," they said, very quietly. "Look at the station window."

She turned. In the dark glass of Studio B, behind the glass and the ON AIR sign that glowed red even though nothing was broadcasting, frost was creeping across the inside of the pane. Spiraling. Deliberate. And in the center of the pattern, clear as print, three words:

**BRING THE CHOIR.**

Priya stared at it for a long moment. Then she pulled out her phone, photographed the window for the records, and sighed the sigh of a woman adding a line item to a budget that had already gone completely insane.

"Well," she said. "At least it RSVP'd."
