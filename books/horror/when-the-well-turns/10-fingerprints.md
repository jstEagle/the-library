## 10. Fingerprints

Emrys didn't sleep so much as surface from it, again and again, like a man treading water in a lake that kept pulling him under to show him things.

The farmhouse. The Aldous Crane place, out past the grain co-op, three weeks ago now — the first callout, the one that had started all of this. He'd almost forgotten it in the churn of everything since: the obituary, the house, the ledger, the well cap he still hadn't opened. But his sleeping mind hadn't forgotten. It kept dragging him back to that kitchen wall, to the section of plasterboard he'd cut away at Mrs. Crane's insistence because something had been *scratching*, she said, something inside the walls, and Emrys had knelt there with his flashlight and his pry bar and found the cavity empty.

Empty except for the marks.

He'd told her mice. He'd told himself mice. But even then, kneeling in the dust with the flashlight beam shaking slightly in his hand, some part of him — the part that had been doing this work for nineteen years, the part that knew the difference between a rat's gnaw and a raccoon's rake and a cat's bored clawing — that part had gone very quiet and very still, the way it did when he walked into a room and knew, without being able to say why, that something in it was wrong.

He got up before dawn and sat at his grandmother's kitchen table with the lights on — all of them, the overhead, the stove hood, the little lamp in the window that had burned every night of his childhood and which he now understood had never been a nightlight at all — and he made himself look at it properly. Not the memory. The *fact*.

Fingerprints. Inside the wall cavity. Pressed into the plaster dust on the inside face of the exterior sheathing.

Pressed *outward*.

* * *

He'd taken photographs. He always took photographs — Mrs. Crane had signed his standard service agreement, which gave him the right to document, and documentation had saved him from a lawsuit twice over the years. He found them on his phone, forty-three images from the morning of September 14th, and he scrolled through them with his coffee going cold beside him and his breath going shallow in his chest.

There. Frame nineteen.

The cavity wall, lit by his flashlight at an angle, and there in the fine gray film of construction dust that had settled on the inside of the sheathing in the forty years since the house was built: handprints. Not smears. Not the dragging trails a trapped animal leaves. *Handprints*. Full palms, all five fingers, pressed flat and held, pressed flat and held, dozens of them, overlapping, climbing the wall in a pattern that his brain had refused to parse that morning and which it now parsed with horrible, perfect clarity.

They were climbing. They were climbing *up*.

And they were the size of his hands.

He set the phone down on the table. His hands weren't quite steady. He picked it up again because not looking was worse, and he zoomed in until the image pixelated, and he looked at the ridges, the whorls, the individual fingerprints pressed into forty years of dust like a man pressing his face and palms against the inside of a window, looking out.

Mice didn't have fingerprints. Raccoons had prints, but small ones, and paw-shaped, and no raccoon on earth stood upright against a wall and pressed its palms flat the way a child presses against the glass of a locked door.

A child.

The word arrived in his mind and sat there, and he thought of the ledger under the basement stairs, the neat columns in his grandmother's handwriting — *June 1974. Thomas Voss. Paid in full* — and he thought of the child's shoe, small, leather, one lace still tied in a bow that some small hand had never learned to untie, and he thought: *the well is under the ground, and the ground is under the house, and houses sit on the ground the way lids sit on jars.*

"Oh," he said, out loud, to the empty kitchen, and the word came out very small.

The thing in the well wasn't trying to get *in*. It had never been trying to get in. The claw-marks in the walls, the scratching Mrs. Crane had heard, the eleven doors in a nine-room house — he'd been reading it all backwards, the way you misread a sentence until someone reads it aloud to you. Everything in him that knew pest control, that knew how animals behave, that knew the difference between something burrowing down and something clawing *up* — all of it had been telling him the truth from the very first morning, and he had overruled it, because the truth was worse than any infestation he'd ever been called to treat.

The thing had been trying to get *out*. For fifty years.

And the walls had held it.

* * *

He drove to the Crane place without deciding to. His hands made the turns; his foot made the pedal do what it needed to do. The morning was the flat gray kind that makes everything look like a photograph of itself, and the fields on either side of the road were stubbled and empty, and Emrys drove through them feeling the way he'd felt as a boy when he first understood that his grandmother kept the porch light on not for guests but for *something else* — that sudden vertigo of a world rearranging itself around a fact that had been in front of him all along.

Mrs. Crane met him at the door in a housecoat, squinting. "Mr. Thackeray. Lord, you look terrible. Come in, I've got coffee on."

"Mrs. Crane," he said, and heard his own voice come out level, the professional voice, the one he used for telling people there was nothing in their attic. "I need to look at that wall again. The one I opened. And then I need to ask you some questions, and I need you to answer them even if they sound strange."

She looked at him for a long moment. She was seventy-eight years old and she had the kind of face that had done its weeping decades ago and settled into something drier and harder. "You found something," she said. It wasn't a question. "I knew you'd found something. You got that look. My husband got that look, the last time the county man came out about the water." She stepped back to let him in. "Ask your questions, Emrys. I'm too old to be polite about strange things anymore."

* * *

The kitchen wall was patched — he'd sealed it himself with a square of plasterboard and a tube of caulk, standard practice, close the cavity, done — but the basement access was still open, and from below, with the flashlight pressed up against the underside of the floor, he could see the cavity's far wall. The sheathing. The dust.

The handprints.

Mrs. Crane stood at the top of the basement stairs with her coffee mug held in both hands, and she didn't ask what he was looking at, because she'd already looked. He could see it in her face. She'd looked years ago, or someone had told her, or she'd heard the scratching herself on some night in the last fifty years and lain awake doing the same arithmetic Emrys was doing now.

"Those aren't animal," he said. "You know that."

"I know that." She took a sip of coffee. Her hand was steady. "My husband patched that wall himself, back in '89, after the last time. Before your grandmother's people came and did whatever it is they do." She said it flatly, the way you'd report the weather. "He never slept right after. Said a house should creak, but it shouldn't *push*."

"Your grandmother's people," Emrys repeated. He climbed the stairs slowly, because his legs felt like they belonged to someone older. "What do you mean, her people?"

Mrs. Crane set her mug down on the counter with a small deliberate click. "Sit down, Emrys."

"I'd rather—"

"Sit *down*." There was nothing unkind in it. There was nothing kind in it either. It was the voice of a woman who had been carrying something for half a century and had recently decided the carrying was over one way or the other. He sat.

"Your grandmother came out here in the fall of '74," Mrs. Crane said. "I remember because the drought broke that same week and everyone was so busy being grateful they didn't ask why. She came with a man from the county — not the county, some office nobody's ever heard of since — and she walked every house on this road, and she put her hand flat against the walls, like this—" Mrs. Crane pressed her own palm against the kitchen table, fingers spread, and held it there, and Emrys's skin crawled so hard he nearly stood up again, "—and where she kept her hand longest, those houses got a visit. A service call, she called it. Free of charge. Sealing work, she called it."

"Sealing."

"Every cavity. Every gap between the studs. Every hollow space a house has, and a house is mostly hollow, Emrys, when you get right down to it. She'd have us clear out for the day, and she'd be inside for hours, and when we came home the walls were full of something — insulation, she said, a new fire-rated foam, county program — and the scratching would stop." Mrs. Crane looked at him with those dry, finished eyes. "For a while. Ten years, fifteen. Then it'd start again, softer, and she'd come back, and we'd clear out, and she'd fill the walls again. She did my house four times. The last time was 2009. She was eighty-one years old and she crawled under my kitchen sink with a flashlight and a bucket of that foam and she told me—" Mrs. Crane's voice did something then, a small fracture, the first one, "—she told me, *As long as they can't get out, they can't get at your children. That's the whole job, Alda. That's the whole job and nobody thanks you for it.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Outside, a crow said something to another crow and got no answer.

"They," Emrys said. "She said *they*."

Mrs. Crane's mouth twisted. "I've had fifty years to think about that word. Whether it's one of them or many. Whether it matters." She picked up her mug again, and he saw now that the steadiness of her hands was a kind of discipline, a thing maintained the way his grandmother had maintained the porch light — every single day, without fail, because the alternative was unthinkable. "Here's what I know, Emrys Thackeray, and I'll say it once and then I'll ask you to leave, because I can't have this in my house past noon, I've never been able to have it past noon. Whatever your grandmother put in these walls, it isn't insulation. And whatever she was keeping in, it was also keeping *in*. And the year she stopped coming — the year she died —" she nodded toward the wall, toward the basement, toward the handprints climbing the inside of the world, "—it started pushing again."

* * *

He sat in his truck in her driveway for a long time.

The gray morning pressed against the windshield. Somewhere down the road a dog was barking at nothing, the way dogs do, except that Emrys had spent nineteen years learning that dogs bark at nothing only when the nothing is very patient.

He thought about the foam. His grandmother, eighty-one years old, on her knees under a stranger's sink, filling the hollow places of a house with whatever it was she filled them with — and he thought about the ledger under the basement stairs, the payments, the maintenance, the terrible bookkeeping of it, and he thought about the well cap in the back field, concrete, poured smooth, and the shoe under the stairs, and the extra chair in the photographs, closer to the camera every year.

The chair was getting closer. He'd seen it. In the album, across fifty years of family photographs, the empty chair had been moving — slowly, patiently, at the speed of something that had all the time in the world because it was *in* the world, inside the walls of it, pressing outward.

And he had spent his whole career as the man who sealed walls.

The realization came to him not as a thought but as a sensation, cold water down the back of the neck: his grandmother hadn't been a well-keeper. That was only the half of it. She'd been something else too. She'd been the town's exterminator of a different kind — or its containment specialist, or its jailer — and the pest she'd spent fifty years managing wasn't in the fields or the foundations.

It was in the walls of every old house on this road. Pressing. Waiting. Leaving its hands flat against the inside of the world like a child left in a locked room, and every so often — every ten years, every fifteen — it pushed hard enough that someone had to come and push back.

And now the someone was dead. And the someone's grandson was sitting in a truck in a farmhouse driveway, and the grandson had a ledger, and a key to a cabinet, and a name in the ledger that matched a shoe, and a well in a back field with a concrete cap that he had not yet lifted, and every instinct he had — nineteen years of instinct, the instinct that had kept him calm in crawlspaces full of wasps and basements full of worse — every instinct was now telling him the same thing, in the same flat professional voice he used on customers:

*You're not the exterminator here, Emrys. You never were. You're the wall.*

He turned the key. The engine caught on the second try, the way it always did.

He was halfway home before he made himself think the rest of it, the part that had been waiting at the edge of his mind all morning like a dog waiting to be acknowledged: if the walls were the containment, and the walls were failing, and the house on Ashgrove land was the center of it — the oldest house, the well house, the house where the ledger lived — then the claw-marks he'd found in *that* house, the eleven doors, the hallway that was too long, the chair in the photographs getting closer to the camera — that house wasn't haunted.

That house was *breaching*.

And there was one more thing, one small terrible thing, that he only let himself think when he was parked in his grandmother's driveway with the engine ticking and the porch light burning in broad daylight because he'd never once turned it off:

The ledger's last entry. The one dated three weeks ago, the week she died, in handwriting gone thin and shaky: *Balance called due. It will take the boy unless someone settles.*

The boy. Denny.

The thing in the walls had been trying to get out for fifty years. And the thing in the well had finally found a way to make someone open the door — not by pushing harder, but by holding something the living couldn't bear to lose.

Emrys got out of the truck. The house watched him come with all its windows. Behind the kitchen wall, faint as a settling beam, he heard — or thought he heard, or his grandmother's training heard for him — a single soft sound.

Not scratching.

A palm, laid flat against the inside of the plaster. Holding still. Waiting to see what he would do.

He put his own hand against the wall, fingers spread, the way Alda Crane had shown him his grandmother used to, and he stood there in the lit kitchen with his heart going like a trapped bird, and he said, quietly, to whatever was on the other side:

"I know you're in there. And I'm coming down to the well."

The wall said nothing. But the porch light flickered once — acknowledgment, or warning, or grief — and stayed on.
