## 22. Church Records

The key Crane had given him was older than Emrys's father would have been — a flat iron thing with a bow worn smooth as river stone, stamped with no maker's mark, the kind of key that outlives the locks it was cut for. It sat in Emrys's jacket pocket all the way across town, and he was aware of it the way you're aware of a splinter: small, buried, and insisting.

St. Brigid's stood at the top of Kettle Hill, white clapboard gone gray at the corners, its steeple leaning two degrees east like a man cocking his head to listen. Emrys had driven past it his whole life without once going in. His family hadn't been church people. Grandma Ruth had been something else entirely — he'd understood that by now, understood it down in his bones where the cold had settled and refused to leave. She'd been a bookkeeper. A payer of accounts. And whatever faith she'd kept, she'd kept it at the bottom of a dry shaft on her own property, making deposits for fifty years.

The parking lot held one car, a Buick with a bumper sticker reading PRAY FOR RAIN in letters faded to the color of old bone. Emrys almost laughed. Pray for rain. This town had gotten its rain in 1974, and he knew now exactly what it had cost, and who had carried the invoice.

He let himself in through the side door — the records office, Crane had said, ask for Father Lindqvist or don't ask for anyone at all, whichever feels safer. The vestibule smelled of candle wax and dust and something faintly green, like ferns kept too long in a dark room. His boots left wet prints on the linoleum. Outside, October was coming down in a fine gray drizzle that couldn't decide whether it wanted to be fog or rain, and the whole world seemed to have agreed to keep its voice low.

"Mr. Thackeray."

The priest emerged from behind a filing cabinet like a man surfacing from deep water. Father Lindqvist was younger than Emrys expected — forty maybe, with the careful, tired face of someone who'd inherited a congregation full of secrets along with the building. He wiped chalk dust off his hands. There was a chalkboard visible over his shoulder, half-erased, covered in dates. Confirmation class schedules, probably. Or maybe not. Maybe every calendar in this town was a ledger wearing a disguise.

"Crane called ahead," Lindqvist said. "He does that. Calls ahead." He studied Emrys with an expression that wasn't unfriendly so much as braced, the way people look at weather they've been told is coming. "You understand what you're asking to see?"

"I understand I'm asking to see it."

"That's not the same as understanding what you're asking." The priest sighed and rubbed the back of his neck. "Come on back. I'll make coffee. You'll want something to hold while you read."

* * *

The parish archive lived in a room behind the sanctuary, a low-ceilinged space lined with metal shelving and boxes labeled in a dozen different hands spanning a century. Baptisms. Marriages. Funerals. Confirmations. First Communions. The whole machinery of a town's soul, filed chronologically, indexed by surname, kept dry by a dehumidifier that hummed in the corner like a hymn sung very badly.

Lindqvist set a mug of coffee on the worktable — real ceramic, chipped at the rim, warm enough to matter — and pulled a box from the second shelf. "1970 through 1979," he said. "I want to tell you something first, though, and I'd take it as a kindness if you heard me out."

Emrys wrapped both hands around the mug. "Go ahead."

"I've been here six years. Before me there was Father Ambrose, thirty-one years. Before him, Monsignor Havel, who served this parish from 1951 until 1981." Lindqvist leaned against the shelving and crossed his arms. "When I took over, Ambrose sat me down in this exact room and told me three things. One: never count the collection twice. Two: the Voss farm road floods even when nothing else does. And three—" He paused, and Emrys watched him choose whether to finish the sentence, watched him decide to. "Three: if anyone ever comes asking about June of 1974, give them the register and then call Aldous Crane, because Crane has been waiting his whole life for that question and it isn't fair to make him wait alone anymore."

The rain ticked against the little window high in the wall. The dehumidifier hummed. Somewhere out in the sanctuary, a loose shutter knocked once against its frame and went still.

"He's been waiting," Emrys said. Not a question.

"Fifty years," Lindqvist said. "He sits in the third pew, second row from the back, every Sunday, and he doesn't take communion, and afterward he stands in the parking lot like a man who's forgotten what car he drove. I used to think it was grief. Now I think it's a sentence he's serving." The priest pushed off the shelf and headed for the door. "Read what you need to read. I'll be up front. When you're done — if you're done — come find me. There's a second thing you should see, but you need the register first, or the second thing won't mean anything."

He left. Emrys stood alone among the dead town's paperwork and opened the box.

* * *

The register was bound in cracked leather the color of dried blood, its spine hand-lettered: *Funerals & Burials, St. Brigid's Parish, 1968–1976.* The pages inside were heavy stock, ruled in faint blue, filled with the confident looping script of Monsignor Havel. Emrys turned pages slowly. Names scrolled past — births and deaths in their ordinary proportions, the usual harvest of age and accident, heart failure and highway and cancer. Ordinary grief, ordinary arithmetic. A town losing its people at the rate towns lose them.

June 1974. He found the page and his finger stopped before his eyes did, some animal instinct pulling him up short, the same instinct that made him count doors twice.

There were four entries for that month. Three were routine. The fourth —

**Voss, Thomas Ellery. Age 6 years. Son of Gerald and Anne Voss, Kettle Road. Service conducted June 14th, 1974, 10 o'clock, side chapel. Interment: Section C, Row 7, Plot 12. Remains: not recovered. Committal per family request. Officiant: Mgr. J. Havel.**

Emrys read it four times. The words didn't rearrange themselves. They didn't blur or swim or do any of the things words did lately when the house got into his head. They just sat there in fifty-year-old ink, patient and terrible.

*A funeral held June 14th.*

Thomas Voss had vanished in late June 1974. Emrys knew the date from the missing-child report — June 21st, the day Ruth Thackeray had walked into the sheriff's office with her hat in her hands and reported a boy missing from the Voss place. He'd read the report three times in the county archive, had memorized the case number, had lain awake under it like a beam pressing on his chest.

But the funeral was dated a week *before* the disappearance.

Which meant one of two things, and only one of them was possible. Either Monsignor Havel had made an error — written the wrong month, transposed the dates, some clerical slip that had survived fifty years uncorrected in a document signed and countersigned and bound into the permanent record of a parish — or Thomas Ellery Voss had been declared dead and buried before anyone admitted he was gone.

Before the well took him. No. Emrys caught himself, corrected himself, felt the correction land somewhere deep and sickening. *After.* After the well took him. The men had put the boy in the ground — in the shaft — and then they had put a coffin in the earth at St. Brigid's, and the monsignor had stood over the empty box and spoken the words of committal, and God had been told a lie in His own house, with the whole town standing around the hole nodding.

They hadn't just fed a child to a hole in the ground. They'd built a bureaucracy around it. A paper trail. A cover story with a burial plot and a row number and a section letter, so tidy that a stranger fifty years later could run his finger down the column and feel how carefully it had all been arranged.

Emrys became aware that his hands were shaking, and that the coffee had gone cold in the mug because he'd been holding it without drinking, using it the way Lindqvist had known he would — as ballast, as something to grip while the floor tilted.

Section C, Row 7, Plot 12.

He was moving before he decided to move, box forgotten, out through the sanctuary where Lindqvist stood at the altar rail pretending to straighten hymnals, out the front doors into the drizzle, across the wet grass toward the cemetery that spread behind the church in terraces of granite and lichen.

"Thackeray." The priest's voice followed him, not alarmed, not surprised. "Section C is past the big maple. Take your time. I'll bring an umbrella."

* * *

The cemetery was old the way the town was old, which was to say it wore its age lightly, grass kept mowed, stones upright, names legible. Section C sat in the southwest corner beneath a maple gone gold at the edges, dropping leaves onto the rows like slow confetti. Emrys walked Row 7 with the rain spotting his shoulders, reading names, counting plots the way he counted everything now, because counting was the only prayer he trusted.

Row 7 ran along a low stone border. Plot 12 was near the end, and even before he reached it he saw that it was different. The other graves in the row bore headstones — names, dates, verses. Plot 12 bore a marker so small it was nearly a footstone, flush with the grass, and on it, carved shallow and already softening with weather:

**THOMAS E. VOSS · 1968 – 1974 · SUFFER THE LITTLE CHILDREN**

No verse about rest. No *beloved son*. Just the name, the span, and a line of scripture chosen, Emrys thought, by someone who wanted the stone itself to testify. *Suffer the little children.* Not a comfort. An accusation, set in granite, aimed at everyone who walked past.

And the grave was tended.

That was the detail that undid him. The grass over Plot 12 was shorter than the grass around it — clipped recently, clipped deliberately. Someone had been mowing this small square of ground, faithfully, for fifty years. At the head of the stone, someone had planted a ring of crocuses, long since bloomed and died back, their foliage yellowing neatly around the marker like a wreath drawn in green ink. And pressed into the soft earth at the base, half-dissolved by the drizzle, was a toy — a little die-cast truck, red paint gone pink with sun and rain, its wheels furred with moss.

Not one toy. Emrys crouched. Beneath the mat of grass at the edge of the plot he could see the ghosts of others — a shape that might have been a soldier, a rectangle of rust where a tin car had surrendered to the soil, the ghost-white curve of a plastic dinosaur long bleached to bone-color. Decades of toys. Someone had been leaving offerings here the way his grandmother had left payments at the well — flour, teeth, photographs — except these offerings weren't tribute. They were love. They were a mother, or a father, or an aunt, kneeling in the wet grass year after year, putting a toy truck on the grave of a child whose body had never been in it, knowing, or half-knowing, or refusing to know, and doing it anyway because the alternative was to stop.

Emrys knelt in the mud beside the grave and put his palm flat on the small warm-wet stone, and something in his chest came apart quietly, without drama, the way wet cardboard gives.

"They lied to you too," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the open air, rusty; he realized he hadn't spoken above a murmur in days. "Whoever you are. Whoever kept this. They lied to you and you knew it, and you stayed anyway. Fifty years of Sundays."

The rain thickened. An umbrella appeared over his shoulder — Lindqvist, holding it steady without being asked, saying nothing, which was its own kind of sacrament.

"The mother," Emrys said. "Anne Voss. Is she—"

"Plot 11." The priest nodded toward the neighboring grave. "Died 1989. Heart, they said." He paused. "Gerald Voss moved away in '75. Sold the farm to the Cranes' cousins. Nobody knows where he went. But Anne stayed in her sister's house on Delmar Street until the end, and every June 14th she came out here with a new toy, and every June 21st she came out here with nothing, and stood there for an hour, and nobody ever asked her why those were different days."

Because June 14th was the lie, Emrys thought. The funeral that preceded the death. And June 21st was the truth — the day the boy actually went into the ground, into the wrong ground, the dry ground, the hungry ground. She'd mourned both. The fiction and the fact. She'd spent fifteen years tending a grave she knew was empty, because the empty grave was the only piece of her son the town had left her, and even that piece was stolen goods.

"Monsignor Havel," Emrys said, standing, knees cracking. "Did he know? Did he know what was in that coffin?"

Lindqvist was quiet for a long moment. Rain drummed on the umbrella. Out beyond the wall, a truck ground past on the county road and faded.

"When I gave you the register," the priest said finally, "I told you there was a second thing. Come inside. It's time you saw it anyway, and I'd rather show you than have you find it the way I found it — alone, at night, with a flashlight, wishing I hadn't."

* * *

The second thing was in the sacristy, in a locked drawer of the desk that had belonged to Monsignor Havel, which the parish had never cleared out because no one had ever quite had the nerve. Lindqvist unlocked it with a small brass key from his key ring — the ring, Emrys noted distantly, held eleven keys, and he hated himself for counting, and counted anyway.

Inside the drawer lay a black cloth-bound notebook, smaller than a Bible, its cover stamped with a cross. Lindqvist set it on the desk but didn't open it.

"Havel's private journal. Ambrose showed it to me my first week, made me swear I'd only read it if the day ever came." He looked at Emrys, and for the first time the young priest's composure slipped, and underneath it Emrys saw a man who had inherited a haunted house and been told to dust around the ghost. "This is the day, I think. Read the entry for June of 1974. Then read the last entry in the book. In that order."

Emrys opened the journal. The handwriting was the same confident loop as the register, but here it moved faster, less guarded, a man writing to himself in the dark.

**June 13th, 1974.** Gerald Voss came to me tonight and asked to schedule a funeral for tomorrow morning. I asked him whose. He wept. He would not say. He said only: *it must be recorded, Father. If it is recorded, it is finished. If it is finished, it cannot be undone.* I have never heard a man beg for paperwork before. I have never heard paperwork described as absolution. I told him I would bury no one I had not seen dead. He thanked me. That frightened me more than the weeping.

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. He turned the page.

**June 14th, 1974.** I conducted the service. There was a coffin. It weighed nothing — I know the weight of coffins, I have carried too many, and this one weighed nothing, and every man who lifted it knew it and none of them flinched, which was worse than flinching. Anne Voss did not attend. The side chapel held nine men and no women. Nine men, and I counted, because counting is what I do when God has gone quiet. I spoke the committal over an empty box and I felt the words go into the air and come back to me unchanged, unblessed, like coins thrown down a well that never hit water. I have buried liars and thieves and I have given them God's words anyway, because that is mercy. This was not mercy. This was conspiracy, dressed in a surplice. I do not know what they have done. I know what they have made me do. Lord, receive a child I never met, wherever he is, and forgive Your servant, who has just taught this town that a record can stand in for a resurrection.

Emrys set the journal down. His hands were steady now, which frightened him more than shaking would have. Steady was what you got after the shock burned off and left only the shape of the thing underneath.

Nine men in the chapel. He thought of Crane's confession waiting for him — *he's waited fifty years to be asked* — and wondered how many of the other eight were still alive, still sitting in pews and trucks and kitchen chairs all over this town, carrying their ninth of the weight.

"Turn to the last entry," Lindqvist said softly.

Emrys turned to the last page. The final entry was dated decades later, the handwriting shrunken and tremulous, an old man's hand:

**October 3rd, 1994.** Ruth Thackeray came to see me today. First time in twenty years. She sat where you are sitting, if anyone ever reads this, and she asked me one question. She asked: *if a debt was paid dishonestly, does the debt die, or does it wait?* I am an old man and I have thought about that question every day since 1974 and I still do not know the answer. I told her: debts do not die. They wait for honest hands. She nodded like I had confirmed something she already knew, and she left, and I watched her walk down the hill in the rain and I understood, finally, what kind of woman has been living among us, and what she has been paying, and to whom. I have kept this book locked because some knowledge is a burden best carried by one man at a time. To whoever reads this: the coffin in Section C is empty. The grave is a lie. But the crocuses are true, and the toys are true, and the mother's grief is the truest thing this parish has produced in my lifetime. Start there. Start with what is true. It is the only currency Heaven honors, and I suspect — I have always suspected — it is the only currency anything else honors either.

The rain had stopped. Through the sacristy window, the clouds were tearing open over Kettle Hill, and a bar of pale light fell across the desk, across the open journal, across the word *currency* as if the afternoon itself were underlining it.

Emrys closed the book with great care, the way you close a door on a sleeping person.

"Lindqvist," he said. "The nine men in the chapel. Crane was one. Do you know who the others were?"

The priest was quiet for a moment. Then he reached past Emrys, took the black journal, and turned back to the June 14th entry, tapping one line with a blunt finger.

"Havel counted them," he said. "Havel always counted. Look at what he wrote next — the part everyone reads past."

Emrys bent to the page. Below the account of the service, in smaller script, cramped against the margin as if the monsignor had written it quickly and been ashamed of writing it at all, was a single line he had missed:

*Nine men. I will not write their names. But I will write that the ninth arrived last, and left first, and that he wore a game warden's coat, and that when I asked Gerald why a game warden, Gerald said: because someone has to know the land. Because someone has to know where the water goes.*

Emrys straightened up slowly. Somewhere below the floor, below the foundation, below the hill itself, he could feel it — not hear, not sense, but *feel*, the way you feel weather change in an old scar — the turning of deep water in a dry place.

A game warden. Someone who knew where the water goes.

He was halfway to the door before Lindqvist called his name, and when he turned, the priest was holding out the black journal.

"Take it," Lindqvist said. "Crane will want to see it. And Thackeray — whoever that warden was, he'll be eighty-something now, if he's alive at all. But wardens keep logs. State archives, county office, the conservation district on Route 9." He almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a man handing someone a shovel and pointing at the grave. "Someone has to know where the water goes. Go find out who they hired to know."
