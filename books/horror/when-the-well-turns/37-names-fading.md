## 37. Names Fading

The first thing to go was the mail.

Mara noticed it on a Tuesday, standing at the end of her gravel drive with the box open and the morning sun slanting through the oaks the way it did in October, all amber and long shadow. The box was nearly empty. Not empty the way it had been in the worst weeks after Dale died, when the world seemed to forget her address along with her grief — empty in a different, more surgical way. There was a utility bill with her name on it. A flyer for a church rummage sale. And nothing else. No letter from the county. No card from her sister in Cedar Falls. No envelope with the blocky, careful handwriting she had come to know better than her own — Emrys Thackeray wrote the way he did everything else, methodically, as if each letter were a door he was counting and confirming before moving to the next.

She stood there a long moment, the cold metal of the box biting her fingers, and told herself it was nothing. Mail was like weather. It came and it didn't. There was no reason on earth to stand in her driveway at seven in the morning feeling like the bottom had dropped out of something she couldn't name.

But she counted, in the days that followed, the way she had learned to count things since June — since the well, since the shaft, since the night they carried a six-year-old boy up out of the dark and Emrys stood at the rim with his hands empty and his eyes full of something she still couldn't look at directly. She counted the mail. And the mail kept not coming. Not for her — her box filled again within the week, catalogs and bills and a sympathy card from a woman at church whose name Mara had to squint at to remember. The mail kept not coming for *him*.

* * *

Emrys had moved into the Thackeray house by then. It had happened without discussion, the way the truest things happen. One evening in late September Mara had brought a casserole over — she was still bringing food, still unable to shake the habit of feeding people who had been down in dark places — and found him on the porch with a paint scraper, working at the flaking columns with the patient concentration of a man performing surgery on a friend. Denny had run up the steps ahead of her, and Emrys had looked up, and the look on his face when he saw them — that small, unguarded brightening, like a lamp turned up a notch — had made her decision for her before she'd even known she was making one.

"You should eat something that isn't from a can," she'd said, and that was that. He fixed the porch. He fixed the gutters. He fixed the hinge on the kitchen door that had shrieked since Mara was a girl, and when it swung silent for the first time she had laughed out loud in a way she hadn't since Dale was alive, and Emrys had looked pleased in his quiet, contained way, the way he looked when a door counted right.

He was there most evenings now. He ate at their table three or four nights a week. He had taught Denny to set a mole trap and to read the grain of old wood and to keep the porch light burning, though he'd changed the reason when Denny asked why. "Not because of the dark," he'd said, and Mara had heard the old fear underneath the new words. "Because people coming home should see that somebody's waiting up."

It was a good autumn. That was the thing Mara kept returning to, later, when she tried to make sense of it — how *good* it was, how the house felt full for the first time in years, how Denny had stopped sleeping with his door cracked open to the hallway light. They had paid a terrible price in June. She had thought the paying was done.

Then the mail stopped. Then other things began.

* * *

It was Denny who noticed the license.

They had driven into Corrick's Landing on a Saturday, the three of them, because Emrys needed washers for the faucets and Denny needed — in his own words — "to get out of the house before it grew a new room," which was the kind of joke that would have been funny a year ago and now landed with a small cold weight that all three of them pretended not to feel. They had stopped at the diner for lunch, and the waitress — a young woman Mara didn't know, new since summer — had taken their orders and smiled at Denny and at Mara, and then looked at Emrys with the pleasant, faintly baffled blankness of someone meeting a stranger.

"Y'all just passing through?" she'd asked.

"We live here," Mara said, before Emrys could answer. "Emrys grew up here. His grandmother's place is out past the Voss road."

The waitress's smile didn't falter, but something behind it shifted, like a curtain moving in a closed house. "Oh," she said. "Sure." And she wrote nothing down, because she'd already forgotten the name, and Mara watched her glance at Emrys twice while she filled the coffee cups, the way you glance at a word you can't quite read.

Emrys had gone very still. Mara had seen it — the particular stillness he had, the pest-controller's stillness, the stillness of a man who has learned that sudden movement in a quiet house is how you find out what else is in the room with you. He'd smiled at the waitress and thanked her and eaten his lunch and left a tip that was slightly too large, and none of them had spoken about it in the truck.

It was in the truck, at the hardware store, that Denny saw the license. Emrys had handed it across the counter for the return of a deposit — some small municipal thing, a deposit on a propane exchange — and Denny, standing on his toes beside him, had frowned at the little plastic rectangle.

"Emrys," he'd said. "Your picture's blurry."

"It's an old license," Emrys said. "They get worn."

"It's not worn-worn," Denny said. "It's blurry like — like when the TV loses the signal. Like it's *trying* to be a picture." He squinted. "Your face is still there. It's just... softer. Like somebody rubbed it with a thumb."

Mara had looked. She wished she hadn't. The photograph was Emrys — the jaw, the careful eyes, the small scar above the brow he'd never explained — but it was Emrys seen through a pane of dirty glass, through water, through fifty years of the same soft erosion that had eaten the faces off her grandmother's photographs. The name beneath it was worse. She could read *Emrys* if she already knew it was there. The last name had gone to static.

The clerk hadn't noticed at all. The clerk had looked at the license, looked at Emrys, and said, "Sure, mister," in the tone of a man who would not be able to describe the person standing in front of him five minutes later.

* * *

Mara began keeping a list. It was a habit she'd caught from Emrys, this compulsion to write things down where the world could see them and be held to account — the ledger had done that to all of them, she supposed, though none of them spoke of the ledger anymore, the way you don't speak of a surgery scar in mixed company. She kept the list in the back of Denny's old homework notebook, in pencil, in her small square handwriting.

*Oct. 4 — No mail for E. Third week.*
*Oct. 9 — Waitress at diner. Didn't know him.*
*Oct. 11 — License blurring. Denny saw it too.*
*Oct. 14 — Mrs. Hollis at the feed store called him "sir" three times and then asked if he was "the one renting the old Thackeray place."*
*Oct. 17 — County records. See below.*

The entry for the seventeenth took her three tries to write.

She had gone to the county office in Corrick's Landing to sort out the last of the estate paperwork — the transfer of the Thackeray property, which had stalled for weeks for reasons the clerk couldn't explain and didn't seem troubled by. While she waited she'd asked, casually, for a copy of the current tax roll, just to confirm the parcel. The clerk, a heavyset man with reading glasses on a chain, had pulled the book and run his finger down the column.

"Thackeray," Mara said. "Emrys Thackeray. He inherited from his grandmother, Edith Thackeray, back in the spring."

The clerk's finger stopped. He looked at the page. He looked at it for a long time, longer than reading took, and then he turned the page and looked at that, and then he looked up at Mara with an expression of genuine, helpless apology.

"I don't — ma'am, I'm not finding a Thackeray on this roll." He turned back a page. Turned forward two. "There's the Ellery parcel, and the Crane holdings, and — you said Edith? Edith Thackeray?" He pulled a different book, an older one, and found the name there, and his relief was almost comical, as if the name had been hiding from him personally. "Here we go. Deceased, March of this year. Estate of. But the heir — " He squinted. "It just says 'heir.' No name. That's odd. That's — huh." He tapped the blank space with a fingernail, twice, the way you'd tap a dead flashlight. "Well. Computer system's been eating entries all fall. I'll get it re-keyed."

He did not get it re-keyed. Mara checked a week later, and the week after that, and each time the space where Emrys Thackeray's name should have been was smooth and blank as a healed wound, and each time the clerk grew a little less certain that there had ever been anything to re-key.

* * *

She told Emrys on a night in late October, the first hard frost, when the two of them sat on the repaired porch with blankets and the porch light burning above them — burning because he'd asked her to leave it on, and because she'd learned not to ask why anymore, or rather, she'd learned that the answer had changed and the new answer was a private thing between him and the dark.

She told him about the mail, and the waitress, and the license, and the county roll, and she watched his face while she did it, and what frightened her most was not that he was surprised. It was that he wasn't. He listened the way a man listens to a diagnosis he's already read about in the pamphlet in the waiting room. He nodded at the right places. When she finished, he was quiet for a while, and the frost ticked in the dead flowerbeds, and somewhere down the road a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"I figured it would be something like this," he said finally.

"You *figured* — " Mara's voice came out sharper than she meant, and she reined it in. "Emrys. You gave them your *name*. Your whole — everything. Every record, every memory, every — " She heard her own voice crack and hated it. "You didn't even hesitate. You didn't even let me — "

"There wasn't time to let you," he said, gently, which was true and also a lie of emphasis, because there had been a moment — she remembered it, she would always remember it — when the house had gone still around them like a held breath, and the well had stated its terms in that voice that was not a voice, and Emrys had said *take mine* before Mara had finished drawing the breath to say it first. He had beaten her to it by half a heartbeat. She had carried that half-heartbeat ever since, like a stone in her shoe, like a debt of her own.

"It should have been me," she said.

"It should have been no one," he said. "That was the whole point. That was the argument you won, remember? You stood in that kitchen and told a house older than the county that its contract was rotten. You out-argued it. Denny closed the deal." A small smile. "I just did the paperwork."

"The paperwork," Mara repeated, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, wet and short. "Emrys, the county doesn't know you exist. The diner girl looked right through you. Your own *face* is going soft on your license. How long before — " She stopped. She couldn't say it. *How long before I do?*

He was quiet again. The porch light hummed. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, the voice he used for frightened animals and frightened children and, she suspected, for himself, at night, alone, in a house where every photograph had once held an extra chair.

"Come here," he said. "I want to show you something."

* * *

He led her inside, through the kitchen — where the child's handprint still marked the wall by the basement door, small and clean and pale as skim milk, and where neither of them ever wiped it, and never would — and down the hall to the front room, where Edith Thackeray's photographs hung in their old frames, straightened now, dusted now, kept now the way you keep a promise to the dead.

"Look at the family picture," he said. "The one from Christmas, 1981."

Mara looked. She knew the photograph. Edith young-ish and severe, Edith's husband long dead, a scatter of aunts and uncles, and at the edge of the frame a boy of about ten, dark-haired, squinting at the flash — Emrys, visiting from out of county, the summer-before-last of a childhood Mara had only ever heard in pieces.

Except the boy was fading.

Not gone. Not yet. But soft — soft the way the license was soft, the way the clerk's memory was soft, edges bleeding into the wallpaper behind him as if the photograph were a window and someone on the other side was breathing on the glass. She could see him because she knew to look. She knew, with a certainty that settled into her bones like cold, that a stranger would see a family picture with a smudge at the edge, and would not wonder about it, and would not remember wondering.

"It's slower than I thought it'd be," Emrys said. He didn't sound afraid. That was the thing that frightened Mara most of all — he didn't sound afraid, he sounded like a man reading a meter. "The well isn't eating me, Mara. It's not spiteful. It's just... balancing. I took a name off its books — I took Thomas home, I carried him, I *am* the record of him now, all of it, every day of those fifty years, I hold it so the world doesn't have to. And a ledger has to balance." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "So it's balancing."

"That's not balance," Mara said. "That's erasure."

"It's both." He said it simply, without self-pity, and that simplicity broke her heart more thoroughly than any wailing could have. "I knew the terms. I read the ledger for a year, remember? I know how it keeps accounts." He turned to her, and in the lamplight his face was steady, and she thought: *I could lose this face. I could lose it and never know what I lost.* "Ask me if I'd do it again."

"Don't," she said.

"Ask me."

"No." Her voice was fierce and low. "Because I know what you'll say, and I don't want to hear it, and more than that — " She stopped, pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and made herself finish. "More than that, I'm afraid the question is going to be answered for me. Not by you. By everyone else. By the world, one clerk and one waitress and one blank tax roll at a time, until there's nothing left to ask."

The house creaked — the ordinary creak of an ordinary old house settling against the frost, and both of them heard it, and both of them heard the other hear it, and for a moment the old fear moved through the room like a draft. Then it passed. The house was just a house now. It had been just a house since June. Whatever lived in the ledger lived in the ledger, and the ledger was blank, and the handprint on the wall was a child's, and the child was home.

But the world outside the house — the world of tax rolls and licenses and waitresses and mail — that world was still keeping its own books. And it was erasing him line by line.

* * *

Only she and Denny remembered. That was the truth of it, and it settled over the autumn like the first snow, soft and total.

Mara tested it, quietly, the way you test ice. She mentioned Emrys to Pastor Lund at church, and the pastor smiled the warm, vacant smile of a man being kind to a confused woman, and said, "Ah — your handyman? The fellow who's been fixing up the old Thackeray place?" and could not, when she pressed, recall ever having heard a first name. She mentioned him to Ruth Ellery at the market, and Ruth said, "Oh, the quiet one, sure, he was in just Tuesday — " and then, when Mara asked what he'd bought, Ruth's face went smooth and blank as the county roll. "Did he? Buy something, I mean. I can't — huh." Ruth laughed at herself. "Aging," she said. "It's aging."

It was not aging. Mara was fifty-one and could remember the color of the ribbon on a birthday gift from 1983. The world was not forgetting. The world was being *tidied* — gently, thoroughly, the way Emrys himself tidied a workbench, everything in its place, everything accounted for, one name removed from the roll and the space left smooth.

Only Denny remembered whole. She watched him do it sometimes with a fierce, wondering pride — at the diner, when the new waitress asked if it was just the two of them, and Denny said, "Three. This is Emrys Thackeray. He lives at his grandma's house on the Voss road and he taught me to trap moles and he keeps our porch light on," in the flat, carrying voice of a child stating facts to an adult who should already know them. And the waitress would blink, and smile at Emrys, and something would *almost* catch behind her eyes — a fish rising toward the surface of a pond and then, at the last instant, turning away — and she'd say, "Well. Nice to meet you, mister," and forget him before she reached the kitchen.

Denny never got tired of saying it. He said it at school, to his teacher, to the lunch ladies. He said it to the county clerk, once, marching into the office with Mara and announcing, "You lost a name off your tax roll and it's Emrys Thackeray and you should put it back." The clerk had been kind. The clerk had written it down. The next week the entry was blank again, and the clerk had no memory of the boy with the serious face, and Mara had driven home with her hands tight on the wheel and her jaw tight above them.

At night, after Denny was asleep, she would sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and add to the list, and sometimes she would add a different kind of entry — not what the world was losing, but what she was keeping. *He hums when he fixes things. He didn't know he did it until Denny told him. He takes his coffee with too much sugar and pretends it's one spoon. He cried at Thomas's grave and thought the dawn hid it. It didn't.* She wrote them because writing was a record, and records were what the well and the world both ran on, and if the world would not keep him, then by God she would. She would keep him in pencil in a homework notebook if that was the last ledger on earth that would hold his name.

One night in November, Denny came down past his bedtime and stood in the kitchen doorway in his socks, and Mara looked up from the notebook, and the boy said, in a small voice stripped of all his usual certainty:

"Mom. What if I forget him too?"

And Mara crossed the kitchen and knelt and took her son's face in her hands, and told him the truth, which was: "You won't. Because I won't. And because he's not a name, Denny. He's the porch light. He's the silent hinge. He's the man who went down the stairs in the dark and came back up carrying somebody home. You don't forget a man like that. You just keep saying his name until the world gives up and lets you."

Denny nodded. He believed her, mostly. He went back to bed.

Mara sat alone at the table a long while, the notebook open, the pencil in her hand. Through the window she could see the Thackeray house down the road, every window lit, every bulb burning, the porch light steady above the steps he had repaired with his own hands — lit the way it always was now, out of love instead of fear, though she sometimes wondered, watching it, whether Emrys himself knew the difference anymore, or whether the old fear had simply grown so quiet and so patient that it had learned to wear love's clothes.

And then she saw something that made the pencil stop moving.

Down at the Thackeray house, in the lit front window, there was a figure. Emrys — she knew his silhouette the way she knew Denny's, the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head. He was standing at the window, looking out, looking at her house, at her lit kitchen window, and even at this distance she could see that he was raising his hand — slowly, deliberately — and touching the glass.

And behind him, in the warm lamplight of the front room, at the edge of the frame where the wall met the window, stood an empty wooden chair.

It had been gone since June. It had vanished from every photograph, every room, every mirror. It was finished; it had gotten what it came for; the ledger was blank.

But there it stood, patient as fifty years, in the corner of the window, and Emrys had not turned around, and Mara's blood went cold and then colder as she understood that he could not — that he was looking at her, touching the glass, and the chair was behind him, and he did not know it was there.
