## 38. Staying Anyway

The first thing Emrys fixed was the porch.

Not because it needed fixing most — the roof shingles were curling like dead leaves, and the well cap, though sealed flush and silent, deserved a weekly inspection out of respect — but because the porch was where Mara had stood with her pie the day everything started, and he liked the idea of the house greeting people properly. He tore up the rotten boards one October morning when frost still silvered the rails, and he found, beneath the third plank from the left, a child's marble. Blue glass with a twist of white at its heart, worn smooth as a river stone.

He sat on the joists for a long time holding it. He didn't cry. He'd done his crying in the spring, in the dark of the shaft, with a cold small weight in his arms and the house screaming around him through every door it owned. This was something quieter. He put the marble in his shirt pocket, where the obituary clipping used to live, and finished pulling boards.

By noon Denny was beside him with a pry bar too big for him, wearing work gloves that swallowed his hands whole.

"You're supposed to lever from the nail side," Emrys said.

"I am levering from the nail side."

"You're levering from your own foot side."

Denny looked down, adjusted, and got the board up on the second try, beaming like he'd raised a barn. Eight going on nine now, all elbows and questions, hair that Mara couldn't do anything with. He'd lost the counting compulsion in stages over the summer — Emrys had watched it happen, watched the boy's eyes stop flicking to doorframes mid-sentence — but some habits stayed, transmuted. Denny counted everything now the way other kids collected things: steps to the bus stop, nails in a fence rail, the rings in the stump by the field. It wasn't fear anymore. It was just how he moved through the world, tallying it, keeping it honest.

"Forty-one nails in this one," he announced, holding up a board studded with rusted heads. "That's a lot. Was she mad about something?"

"Grandma? Probably." Emrys took the board, tested it, tossed it on the scrap pile. "She was mad about plenty. She just did her being mad quietly, into ledgers."

Denny considered this with the gravity he gave all matters concerning the dead. "Do you think she knows we fixed her porch?"

Emrys thought about the tap water running clear all summer. The radio that only played hymns now, softly, when nobody had touched it. The way the kitchen always smelled faintly of bread no one baked.

"Yeah," he said. "I think she's the first one who knows."

* * *

The trouble with not existing, Emrys learned, was mostly paperwork-shaped, which was to say it barely touched him at all.

His truck registration had lapsed in June and no notice ever came. His pest control license — the laminated card he'd carried for eleven years, the one with his squinting photo and the state seal — had gone blank in his wallet sometime around the burial at dawn. Not expired. Blank. Just white plastic where his face used to be. He kept it anyway, the way you keep a key to a house you've sold, and once a month or so he'd take it out and look at it and feel nothing worse than a kind of distant curiosity, like reading an old letter from someone he used to know.

The bank had stopped sending statements. The hardware store on Route 9 still let him run a tab, though Mr. Pell, who'd known him since he was twelve, now greeted him with the warm baffled smile people reserve for regulars whose names they've misplaced. "Morning, friend. What'll it be today?" Friend. Buddy. Sir. The town had developed a whole vocabulary of placeholders for him, and Emrys had come to find them almost tender — the world patting its pockets, looking for him, shrugging, and offering him what it had instead.

Mara had raged about it, early on. She'd driven to the county office with his birth certificate and a folder of documents and come back gray-faced and furious, having stood at the counter while a clerk scrolled through records that showed Thackeray, E., pest control license active, address current — and then blinked at the screen and asked her, politely, whom she was trying to help today. She'd tried three times. On the third try she came home and sat at his kitchen table — their kitchen table, though nothing in this house was anyone's alone anymore — and put her head down on her folded arms.

"They didn't erase you," she said, muffled. "They just... filed you somewhere I can't reach."

"It's okay," Emrys said, and meant it, which surprised him.

"It is not okay. You saved my son's life. You carried a dead child half a mile underground through a house that wanted to kill you, and the state of—" Her voice cracked. She lifted her head, and her eyes were wet and furious and beautiful, and Emrys understood in that moment that he would trade every record, every license, every trace of himself in every database on earth for exactly this: Mara Voss at his table, fighting for him harder than he fought for himself.

"Mara." He covered her hand with his. "I spent my whole life being findable. Licenses, invoices, name on the mailbox. You know what I was? A line item. Pest removal, per callout." He turned her hand over, traced the callus on her palm from years of farm work. "Now I'm a porch board and a pie crust and the guy who taught your kid to tell a rat hole from a woodpecker hole. That's more real than anything the county ever had on me."

She laughed despite herself, wiping her face with her wrist. "You're a terrible romantic."

"I'm a pest controller. We specialize in infestations. Your heart's infested. Nothing to be done."

* * *

Winter came down off the hills in December and settled over the property like a held breath, and Emrys taught Denny the trade.

It started as a joke — the boy trailing him around the barns asking what was in the walls — and became a curriculum. Emrys showed him how to read a building the way you read a face: the sag that meant water, the sawdust drift that meant carpenter ants, the greasy rub-mark at a baseboard that meant rats had been commuting there long enough to have opinions. He taught him the difference between a creature that wants in and a creature that's already home, and why the second kind deserves a conversation before it deserves a trap.

"The thing in the wall isn't the problem," Emrys told him, crouched in the Hendricks' barn with a flashlight, showing Denny a mouse run behind the feed sacks. "The thing in the wall is telling you something's wrong with the wall. You fix the wall, the thing finds better lodging."

Denny nodded solemnly, taking notes in a spiral notebook Mara had bought him, his pencil gripped wrong but ferociously. Then, without looking up: "Like the well."

Emrys went still. Snow ticked against the barn roof. Somewhere out in the white field, under two feet of frost-hardened ground, the cap sat flush and quiet, and deep beneath it — deeper than any shaft Crane's father had dug — water turned, slow and patient, going nowhere.

"Yeah," Emrys said finally. "Exactly like the well. Except we fixed that wall with the truth instead of lumber, and the tenant decided to stay peaceful." He bumped the boy's shoulder. "You'd have made a good exterminator, Denny Voss. Terrible bedside manner, excellent instincts."

"I'm gonna be lots of things," Denny said, with the terrifying confidence of eight-year-olds. "But I'm gonna keep doing this with you. On weekends."

Something caught in Emrys's chest, some small mechanism he hadn't known was loaded. He cleared his throat and blamed the dust.

* * *

The lights were the last habit to change.

For fifty years — first his grandmother's life, then his own childhood summers here, then these months — the rule had been simple: keep them burning. Every bulb, every room, dusk to dawn, because darkness was where the count went wrong, where doors multiplied, where the chair crept closer to the camera. He'd kept the lights on through the worst of it out of terror, and after the burial out of momentum, and one night in January he came downstairs at 2 a.m. for water and found himself, out of pure reflex, reaching for the switch in the front room.

And stopped.

The house lay dark around him, and it was fine. More than fine. The dark was just dark — cold, blue with moonlight, smelling of pine from the tree Mara and Denny had dragged in. No extra doors. No breathing in the walls. Through the window he could see the well field, silvered and still, and beyond it the road, and beyond that the ordinary sleeping town that no longer knew his name.

He stood there a long moment. Then, deliberately, he flipped the switch, and the front room bloomed warm and yellow, and he understood what he'd done differently.

Not fear. Love.

He went through the house then, room by room, turning on lamps — the kitchen where Mara's flour canister sat, the hall where Denny's boots dried, the little room that had been Thomas's chair-room once and was now a reading room with a good armchair and a lamp with a green glass shade. He lit them all, and the house glowed against the snow like something alive and content, and anyone driving past on the road would see it and think: someone's home. Someone's waiting up.

Which was true. Someone was always waiting up now. Him, usually, until Mara's truck crunched into the drive. Or her, when he drove out to Hendricks' place for a late callout. The lights weren't a ward anymore. They were a porch light the size of a house, burning for each other.

* * *

In February, the mail brought one envelope.

It was addressed to Occupant, which was accurate enough to make him laugh, and inside was a flyer for gutter cleaning. But tucked behind it, unmarked, unexplained, was a photograph.

He knew it immediately. It was the picture from the album — the 1974 family gathering on this very porch, everyone squinting into the sun, and at the edge of frame the empty wooden chair that had haunted fifty years of photographs. Except now the chair wasn't empty. A small boy sat in it, six years old forever, in a striped shirt, holding something up toward the camera with both hands.

A toy car.

Emrys carried it to the kitchen, where Mara was rolling pie dough and Denny was doing homework at the table, and set it down between them without a word.

Mara wiped her hands. Looked. Her breath went out of her slowly, like air from a valve.

"That's the grave," Denny said, leaning over. "That's my car. I left it at Thomas's stone in September. Mom said the ground would take it." He frowned, working it through. "So how does he—"

"He doesn't," Emrys said gently. "I think maybe the ground sent it back. As a thank-you. Or a receipt."

They stood around the photograph for a while, three people and one ghost's gratitude, while the pie dough warmed to room temperature and the snow came down soft past the windows. In the picture, Thomas was smiling. Emrys was almost sure of it. Fifty years of dread in that chair, and now the boy in it looked like a boy at a family gathering, showing off a present, wanting to be seen.

He was seen, Emrys thought. Finally, fully, named and buried and mourned. That was the whole currency. That had always been the whole currency.

Mara reached over and took Emrys's hand, and Denny leaned into his side, and outside the light in every window burned steady against the coming dark.

"Stay for dinner," Mara said, though he lived forty feet away, though he wasn't going anywhere, though there was no version of the evening where he left. Some invitations you say out loud anyway, just to hear the answer be yes.

"Yeah," Emrys said. "I'm staying."

He was. For dinner, for winter, for whatever the well turned beneath them and however long the world forgot to write his name down. The county had no record of him. The church register had no line for him. But there were two people at this table who knew him down to the grain — knew how he counted doors and why, knew what he'd carried up out of the dark, knew the sound he made when he laughed, really laughed, which was rare and getting less so.

Being forgotten by the world, he was learning, was a small price for being known by the right ones.

Outside, far below the snow and the roots and the sealed stone, the water turned. And in the warm yellow house above it, somebody's home.
