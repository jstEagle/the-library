## 39. One Year Later

Denny counted the doors on the walk to school the way other kids chewed gum — without deciding to, without being able to stop.

Front door of the house: one. Screen door: two. The little door on the porch cabinet where Mara kept the citronella candles: three. He'd stopped fighting it a long time ago, sometime in the gray weeks after everything, when his mom had found him standing in the hallway at midnight in his socks, lips moving, finger rising and falling like a tiny conductor keeping time with something only he could hear.

*Four,* he'd told her then. *There's four doors and there's supposed to be four.*

She had knelt down on the hallway floor in her robe and held his face in both hands, and her eyes had been wet and fierce all at once, and she'd said, "That's right, baby. That's exactly right."

Because that was the thing now. That was the whole strange miracle of it. Before, there had always been one extra. One too many, lurking at the edge of every room, under the gym at school, behind him in mirrors, patient as a held breath. Now there was never an extra. There was never one missing either. Doors were just doors, honest as spoons, and when Denny counted them they came out right, every single time, like the world had finally agreed to be the size it said it was.

He still counted anyway. Mr. Aldous Crane said that was fine. Crane had come over for supper back in the spring — brought a lemon meringue pie and ate two slices and cried a little into his napkin when nobody was looking, though Denny was always looking; looking was what Denny did best — and Crane had told him, in his papery old voice, "A man who counts his doors is a man who knows his house. Don't let anybody shame you out of knowing your own house, boy."

So Denny counted. School had nine doors if you counted the boiler room hatch, which Denny did, because a door was a door. The church had eleven. The hardware store where Emrys bought the good snap traps had six, unless you counted the little swinging door on the dog-food bin, which made seven, and Emrys always counted it too, without saying anything, their eyes meeting across the aisle for half a second like two people sharing a joke nobody else would ever get.

Emrys. Denny thought about him the way you think about weather, or breakfast, or the smell of your own pillow — constantly, comfortably, without surprise. Emrys was there. Emrys fixed the sagging hinge on the screen door and taught Denny how to tell mouse droppings from bat guano (mice poop wherever they walk, bats poop where they sleep) and kept every light in the house burning at night, not scared-lights-on anymore but love-lights-on, which Denny understood even though nobody had explained it, because kids understand things by feel long before adults get around to explaining them.

The mailman didn't know Emrys's name. That was the one weird leftover, the loose thread Denny wasn't supposed to have noticed but had. The mailman called him *the handyman*. Mrs. Petrelli next door called him *the young man who does your yard, dear*. Even the yearbook photo from Mom's old box — no wait, that wasn't right, Emrys didn't have photos anywhere, Denny had checked, quietly, thoroughly, the way he checked everything now. It was like the world had a hole shaped like Emrys Thackeray and had smoothly grown shut around it, skin over the wound, no scar.

But Mom remembered. And Denny remembered. And that, Emrys had told him once, sitting on the porch steps with the moths bumping the lit bulb above them, was enough. More than enough. "Two people is a pretty good country," he'd said. "You could do worse than a country with pie in it."

Denny hadn't fully gotten the joke then. He got it better now.

* * *

Saturday morning came up gold and slow, the kind of October morning where the frost burned off by eight and the air smelled like apples going soft somewhere out of sight. Denny woke before his alarm, which almost never happened, and lay in bed listening to the house.

Houses talked. He'd learned that. Not words, mostly — more like the way a dog talks, with creaks and settles and small sighs of wood. This house talked easy these days. Relaxed. The floorboards above the kitchen ticked as the sun warmed them. The water in the pipes murmured its low morning note. Nine rooms, nine doors, nothing extra breathing in the walls.

Except today the house felt — Denny searched for the word while the ceiling went pink with sunrise — *expectant*. Like a dog at five o'clock, sitting by the door, knowing.

He got up. He didn't need to check the calendar. He knew what day it was the way he knew his own birthday, except this wasn't his birthday. It was Thomas's.

Thomas Ellery Voss. Six years old. Liked toy cars — Denny knew this the way he knew his own name now, because for a whole season last year Denny had listened to a cold small voice come up out of a dark shaft and say so, and some things you hear once and keep forever. *Red one,* the voice had said, or Denny had always believed it said, in the memory that had gone smooth and certain with retelling. *I had a red one.*

Denny had a red one. Had had one for a year, actually, sitting on his shelf where nobody played with it, because it wasn't really his. He'd won it at the Harvest Fair ring toss two weeks after everything, a little die-cast Chevy pickup with chipped paint on the driver's side door, and the moment he'd pulled it from the prize bin his hands had known what it was for. He'd taken it home and put it on the shelf and waited.

Today was the day.

Downstairs, the kitchen was warm and yellow-lit even though the sun hadn't cleared the tree line yet, because Emrys had beaten everyone down and started coffee and eggs, and the radio was playing low — real music, not voices, not anymore, though sometimes late at night between songs the static still breathed a little, and Emrys would turn it off gently, like closing a door on someone sleeping.

"Morning, count-man," Emrys said. He said it every Saturday. It was their word, theirs alone, and it meant *I see you, I know what you carry, it's okay.*

"Morning." Denny climbed onto his stool. "How many doors did the kitchen have today?"

"Same as yesterday," Emrys said, sliding eggs onto his plate. "All of them."

"That's the right answer."

"I've been practicing."

Mara came down with her hair still damp, kissed the top of Denny's head, kissed Emrys's cheek — she did that now, easily, like she'd always done it, and Denny pretended to be grossed out and secretly wasn't — and looked at her son for a long second with that mother-radar look.

"You're going out to the cemetery," she said. Not a question.

"After breakfast." Denny speared his eggs. "Can I take the truck?"

Mom looked at Emrys. Emrys looked at Denny. Something passed between the three of them, quiet and enormous, the size of a whole buried year.

"He can ride along," Emrys said. "If he wants company. Or he can go alone, if he wants that instead. Either one's fine."

Denny thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything now. A year ago he'd have wanted company so bad his chest hurt. But he'd learned something in the well field, in the year of wildflowers, about how some errands are lighter carried alone.

"Alone," he said. "But you can drive me to the gate."

* * *

The cemetery sat on the hill above town, white stones scattered across it like teeth in an old jaw, and the morning fog was burning off it in ribbons as Emrys's truck rattled up the gravel lane. Denny had the toy truck in his jacket pocket, its weight familiar against his hip. Beside him, Emrys drove with one wrist on the wheel, quiet, letting the silence be a friendly thing instead of a heavy one. He was good at that. He'd always been good at that, even back when Denny first met him, back when Denny was counting doors under the gym and getting numbers that made teachers frown.

They passed the Voss plot's older stones — Great-Grandpa Aldous-adjacent names, farm families gone to dust — and then the newer section, and there it was.

The stone was granite, gray, modest. THOMAS ELLERY VOSS, it said. BELOVED SON. JUNE 1968 — JUNE 1974. And beneath the dates, added last spring at Mara's insistence, in letters the stonecutter had cut deep so they'd outlast everything:

*HE CAME HOME.*

They'd buried him beside his mother, in the plot the parish register had promised him fifty years ago and lied about. The coffin had been small. Denny hadn't looked inside and hadn't needed to; he'd stood at the graveside holding his mom's hand with one hand and Emrys's with the other, and the dawn had come up the color of peach skins, and the well cap three fields away had sealed itself flush with the earth with a sound like a book closing, and everybody had cried, even old Crane, especially old Crane, and it had been the saddest and most right thing Denny had ever been part of.

Now the grave was just a grave, tended and grassed over, and Denny knelt down in the dew and took the toy truck out of his pocket.

"Hey, Thomas," he said. Conversationally. That was another thing he'd learned: dead kids aren't scary. Dead kids are just kids who are far away. "It's me. Denny. You remember. Well — maybe you don't, now. Mom says it works different over there. But I remember for both of us, so it's okay."

He set the truck down at the head of the stone, wheels aligned straight, facing east, because cars should face somewhere.

"It's the red one. Well — mostly red. The paint's chipped 'cause it lived in a prize bin for a while, but Emrys says things that get used are worth more than things that don't, so." He shrugged. "I figured you'd want a used one. Real ones are better than perfect ones. That's like, the whole lesson of everything, I think."

The wind moved through the cemetery grass with a sound like long exhaling. Somewhere below, three fields down and forty feet under, Denny felt — not heard, *felt*, in his back teeth, in the counting place behind his sternum — the deep slow turn of water in the dark. Turning, not rising. Content. A settled account.

"We're okay, you know," Denny told the stone. "Me and Mom and Emrys. Emrys doesn't have a name anymore — like, officially, on paper — but he has us, and honestly? Paper's dumb. Paper lost track of you for fifty years, so paper can lose track of him. We keep better records than paper does." He tapped his own temple. "Right in here. Permanent file."

He sat back on his heels. The sun had cleared the trees now and lay warm across his shoulders, and for a while he just stayed there, the way you stay with a friend who doesn't need you to talk.

"One more thing," he said, standing, brushing off his knees. "Mom says I should tell you happy birthday, sort of. Since we only ever knew the day they took you. So." He grinned, gap-toothed still, one canine late coming in. "Happy taking-day-turned-home-day, Thomas. Enjoy the truck. Drive it somewhere good."

And — Denny would swear this later, on anything, on the whole permanent file in his head — as he turned to go, the little truck rocked. Just slightly. Just once. Like something had shifted its weight behind the wheel.

Denny smiled all the way down the hill.

* * *

The well field was the second stop, and he didn't ask permission anymore because permission had stopped being necessary sometime around midwinter. The field lay beyond the back pasture of grandma's house — the house was theirs now, all three of them, though the deed said things the deed didn't quite mean, since officially the estate had no heir and the county had eventually shrugged and let the property pass to *the adjoining household of record*, whatever that meant, paper being dumb.

A year ago the field had been bare. That was the famous thing about it, the thing the old-timers muttered about: nothing grew there, not in living memory. Dirt like a held breath. Grass seed died in it. Weeds wouldn't even cheat.

Now it was a riot.

Wildflowers everywhere — not planted, nobody planted them, they'd simply arrived that first spring like guests who'd finally been told they could come in. Black-eyed Susans and Queen Anne's lace and purple asters and something small and white that Mom said looked like baby's breath and Denny privately thought looked like the flowers they put on cakes. They grew hip-high in places, swarming right up to the edges of the field and stopping in a neat circle, as if the flowers themselves had agreed on a boundary line. Inside the circle, around the flush concrete cap, the growth was thickest — a green crowd pressing close, leaning in, the way people lean toward a fire.

Denny walked out to the cap through the bees and the butterflies. The stone was warm in the sun. It always was, even on cold mornings, which Mrs. Petrelli called *geothermal nonsense* and Crane called *something else entirely* and Denny called *him being comfortable.*

He sat down cross-legged on the cap, the way he did most Saturdays, and put his palm flat on the warm concrete.

Below him, far down, the water turned. Slow, deep, patient — a great dark wheel going around and around in the earth's quiet, not rising, never rising, just moving the way a sleeping person moves. Denny had asked Emrys once what it was doing down there, turning like that, and Emrys had thought about it for a long time before answering.

"Remembering," he'd said. "Or keeping watch. Maybe those are the same job."

Denny pressed his hand flat and listened with the part of himself that counted doors, the part that had always been able to hear one extra breath in any room. He heard no extra breath now. He heard the water, and under the water, faint as a radio between stations, something that might have been humming.

A kid tune. Skipping-rope rhythm. Happy, or near enough.

"You're welcome," Denny told the cap, grinning. "Tell Thomas the truck's a stick shift. He'll figure it out."

He stayed until his legs went numb, then got up, brushed flower petals off his jeans, and headed back across the field toward the lit windows of the house, where Mom was hanging laundry and Emrys was fixing the third step, hammer ringing steady as a heartbeat, and the whole scene glowed gold in the slanting afternoon light like a photograph — a real one, this time, with everybody in it and nobody extra and nobody missing.

* * *

It happened at dusk.

Denny was on the porch doing his homework — fractions, which were just counting with extra steps, so actually he was fine at them — when the light changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed.* Went amber and thick, honey-heavy, the way it does in photographs of summers that ended decades ago. The crickets stopped all at once, a held-breath hush rolling across the yard. And out past the pasture, in the well field, every wildflower turned.

Not wilted. Turned. All of them, thousands of small faces, rotating on their stems in perfect unison to face the cap — facing *down*, roots and petals alike, like a congregation bowing.

Then, from deep beneath the earth, came a sound Denny had never heard before and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own name shouted across a parking lot.

The water had stopped turning.

And it was rising.

Just a little. Just an inch of sound, a ripple climbing the dark throat of the shaft, gentle as a question. Denny stood up, homework sliding forgotten off his knees, and behind him the screen door opened and Emrys stepped out onto the porch, and Denny knew without looking that Emrys had felt it too, in his teeth, in his counting place, in the old scar the year had left on all of them.

"What's it doing?" Denny whispered.

Emrys stood very still, head tilted, listening the way only he could listen. The porch lights flickered on around them, every bulb, unasked, the house lighting itself like it used to — but softly now, warmly, not afraid.

"I think," Emrys said slowly, "somebody just said thank you back."

Out in the field, the flowers turned face-up again, all together, and the last of the light caught them, and far below the water began — very softly, very deeply — to sing.
