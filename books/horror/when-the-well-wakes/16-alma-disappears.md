## 16. Alma Disappears

I told myself I was going back to Alma Voss's place for professional reasons.

That was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, and I went anyway, because at that point in my life the lies were less about deceiving other people and more about giving my feet permission to move. You can't walk toward something if you admit what it is. That's not cowardice. That's physics. Ask any pest controller who's ever had to open a wall he already knows is full of wasps.

The official reason — the one I'd have given if anyone asked, and nobody did, because the town of Harrow Bend had collectively decided to stop asking me questions around the same time they'd decided to stop looking me in the eye — was that Alma had information I needed. She'd warned me off with that terrible courtesy of hers, *your grandmother kept her appointments*, and then she'd shut her door on me like I was a salesman. People who slam doors are people with something behind them. In my line of work you learn that early. The mouse doesn't care about your baseboards until you threaten its route.

So: professional reasons. Information retrieval. A follow-up visit, standard practice.

It was four in the afternoon and the light was already going the color of old dishwater when I turned onto Voss Road, and the first thing I saw was her porch light burning.

Now, understand. Alma Voss was the kind of woman who treated electricity like a moral failing. She'd told me once, through the gap in her door, that her husband had installed that porch light in 1978 and she'd left it off since his funeral as a kind of ongoing argument with him about waste. She was proud of it. It was the only thing she'd ever said to me that sounded like pride instead of dread.

And it was burning. Bright yellow, steady, no flicker, in full daylight, with the sky still holding a scrap of blue.

I sat in my truck for a while with the engine ticking and did the thing I do, which is count. Counting is how I pray, if I pray, which I don't, officially. I counted the distance from the road to the porch — forty feet, give or take. I counted the windows with lights behind them — two, upstairs, both warm. I counted the reasons not to get out of the truck and came up with eleven, which was a number I was starting to hate.

Then I got out of the truck, because stubbornness is just fear that's been given a job.

* * *

Her door was unlocked.

That was wrong twice over. Once because Alma Voss struck me as a woman who locked things — doors, cupboards, memories, all of it — and twice because when I tried the handle it turned with the smooth, oiled ease of a door that had recently been *attended to*. Everything in that valley had started feeling attended to lately. The farmhouse. The roads. Now this.

"Missus Voss?" I said, and my voice came out in the flat professional register I use for clients, which is to say it came out like I was announcing a treatment plan for carpenter ants. "It's Petra Nystrom. From the farm. We spoke."

Nothing. The house breathed around the word.

I stepped inside and stopped, because the first thing you do in any structure is orient, and orientation means counting, and I counted her hallway and got a number that made my stomach drop through the floorboards into whatever crawlspace God keeps under Harrow Bend.

Alma's house was a single-story clapboard, the plain kind, the kind with one front door, one back door, and maybe an interior door or two. I'd stood outside it. I'd looked at it. It was a small house. Four doors, five tops, if you counted closets.

I counted nine.

I stood in her hallway and counted them again, slowly, touching each frame the way I touch the frames at the farm, and there were nine, and every one of them was closed, and every one of them was warm under my palm like a hand resting flat against the wood from the other side.

"Okay," I said out loud, because saying things out loud is another rule. If you say it, it's real; if it's real, you can deal with it. "Okay. New rule. We don't open warm doors."

The house did not argue with me. That was somehow worse than arguing. My grandmother's house argued — creaked, knocked, rearranged itself with the passive aggression of a cat knocking a glass off a table. This house just *waited*, the way a trap waits, and I know traps, and the worst ones are the ones that don't need to spring fast.

I found her kitchen at the end of the hall, past doors six, seven, eight, and nine, and that's where I found the chair.

* * *

It was a wooden chair. Straight-backed, spindle-legged, dark with age and polish. I recognized it the way you recognize a face in a crowd you've been dreading — instantly, completely, with your whole animal body before your brain catches up. It was the chair. The extra chair. The one that had been creeping into the Nystrom family photographs generation by generation like a stain spreading through cloth, the one that had appeared in my attic three days ago, dusted and pulled slightly from the table as if set for a guest.

It was sitting in Alma Voss's kitchen, pulled slightly from her table, facing the doorway.

Facing me.

And across from it, in Alma's own rocking chair by the cold stove, sat Alma's cardigan. Just the cardigan, draped over the chairback, sleeves hanging down, shoulders slumped in a way that was so specifically *her* — so much the shape of a small, dry, careful woman — that my throat closed up. There was a teacup on the side table beside it. The tea was still warm when I touched the cup. Not hot. Warm. The temperature of something that had been set down recently by hands that were in a hurry, or hands that had been interrupted, or hands that were no longer attached to anything that needed tea.

There were fingerprints on the back of the empty chair.

Greasy, gray-brown, the size of a large man's hand but wrong in the proportions — fingers too long, spaced too wide, pressed hard enough into the wood grain that I could see the whorls. At exactly chair-back height. The same height as every print ringed around my grandmother's kitchen walls. The height of something seated. The height of something patient.

I didn't touch them. I want credit for that. Three weeks ago I would have touched them, because I'm the kind of person who has to poke the nest to confirm the wasps, and I've got the scars to prove it. But I'd learned something in my grandmother's house, learning curve or no: some evidence isn't left behind. Some evidence is left *for* you. A calling card. An invoice.

The house was collecting witnesses, and now it was leaving receipts.

* * *

I searched the rest of the house because searching is what I do, methodical, room by room, and because standing still felt like sinking. I checked the bedroom — bed made, hospital corners, a Bible on the nightstand with a ribbon marking Psalms. I checked the bathroom — mirror clean, too clean, wiped in a circle the way you'd wipe it if you wanted to make sure nothing could show itself there. I checked the little back room where Alma kept her preserves, rows of jars glowing amber and ruby in the last light, every label written in her cramped hand, and on the shelf at exactly the height of a seated person's shoulder, one jar turned backward so the label faced the wall.

I turned it around. The label read, in her handwriting: **FOR PETRA, WHEN SHE UNDERSTANDS.**

Inside was not preserves. Inside was a folded page, yellowed, soft as cloth from handling. I stood in that cold pantry and read it by the light of my phone, and my breath fogged the screen, and somewhere in the walls of Alma Voss's house something shifted its weight, gently, the way a guest shifts in a chair that's grown uncomfortable.

It was a letter. Undated. Alma's hand, younger, shakier:

*Marta —*

*You asked me once why my family left the valley and I lied to you and I think you knew it. Here is the truth, and after I give it you can decide whether we are still friends.*

*In 1954 my father was asked to sit. The Nystroms' arrangement was aging, the way arrangements do, and the valley understood — everyone understood, though we never said it above a whisper — that the seat wanted filling and the bloodline was thinning. My father went to the farmhouse on the appointed night and he sat in the chair and he stayed an hour, and he came home whole, and our crops came in fat that year, and my mother cried for a week and would not say why.*

*What it costs is not what they tell you. What it costs is this: while you sit, the thing looks at everything you love. It takes an inventory. And afterward you remember the inventory being taken. You remember it the way you remember a stranger walking through your house touching all your things. My father never slept a whole night again. He said the looking never stopped, even years later. He said it had memorized us, and that being memorized by it was worse than being haunted, because haunting ends.*

*We left in '61, after the boy. After Anders. My father said a thing that saves a child has already priced the child, and we would not be part of the payment plan. So we ran. And it let us run, Petra, do you understand? It let us go, because running families still talk, and talking towns still believe, and belief is a kind of invitation too.*

*I came back when your grandmother wrote to me, after her husband died. She said someone had to keep the appointments and she was tired and her hands shook. I did not sit. I want that written down somewhere that isn't my own guilty heart: I never sat. But I kept her books. I witnessed. And witnessing is its own seat, though no one warns you.*

*If you are reading this, then I am gone, and I am sorry, and I am more sorry than sorry, because I think I always knew the ledger would come due for those of us who watched without paying. Debts don't forget the audience.*

*Do not sit down. Whatever it offers — and it will offer, it offered your grandmother your mother's life once, or so she screamed at me drunk one Christmas — do not sit. The chair is not a punishment. That's the trap of it. The chair is a kindness. It is the kindest thing in that house, and it will fit you perfectly, and you will want to rest, oh, you will want to rest so badly—*

*—A.V.*

The pantry light — a bare bulb I had not seen a cord for — clicked off above my head.

I got out of that house at a speed I'm not proud of, letter crushed in my fist, and I did not count the doors on the way out, and when I looked back from the truck the porch light was still burning, steady and yellow and patient, over a home with nobody in it, and I understood with a clarity that felt like a hand around my throat:

It hadn't taken Alma because she'd broken a rule.

It had taken her because she'd *kept* one. Witnessing is its own seat. She'd sat at the edge of this thing for sixty years, watching, keeping books, keeping appointments secondhand — and the house had finally come to collect the audience.

And I was the last witness left.

* * *

I drove back to the farm with the windows down even though it was freezing, because I needed the cold to think against, the way some men need noise. The letter sat on the passenger seat like a passenger. Twice I caught myself checking the rearview for a shape at chair-height in the cab behind me, and twice there was nothing, and both times the nothing felt rehearsed.

At the farmhouse I did my ritual. Lights on — every bulb, every lamp, the stove light, the porch light, the useless attic pull-chain I'd rigged to a work lamp. Doors counted: eleven, in a nine-door house, and I chalked each threshold with the tired fluency of a woman writing lines on a blackboard she's stopped believing in. Salt at the sills. Iron filings in the door tracks, scavenged from the shed, rust-red and honest.

Then I sat at the kitchen table — my chair, the far one, the one with its back to the wall, because I am not an idiot — and I laid out what I had.

The ledger. The letter. The tape I hadn't listened to yet because I wasn't ready, and because some part of me understood that once Grandma's voice came out of that machine, this stopped being an investigation and became a conversation, and I wasn't sure I could survive hearing her answer me.

And underneath it all, the new fact, the fact that changed the arithmetic entirely: they had Alma now. Or it had Alma. Whatever grammar applied to a woman dissolved into a warm teacup and a draped cardigan.

Here's what that meant, and I made myself look at it straight, because looking away from numbers is how pest controllers die: I was no longer dealing with something that waited. The renewal clock was ticking — the ledger's final pages had made that much clear, a life per missed generation, the new moon coming like a bill collector — and the presence had started gathering. Witnesses. Seats. Inventory. It was tidying the valley the way it tidied my dishes, folding the loose ends, putting everything in its place before the appointment.

And every person it gathered was one more voice that would stand at that table when the moment came. One more pair of prints at chair-height. One more reason the town would come for me not with pitchforks but with casseroles and concern and gentle hands steering me toward a chair.

My phone buzzed on the table. I flinched hard enough to knock the salt dish.

Unknown number. Local prefix. I answered, because unanswered calls in horror stories are how you find out things too late, and I'd rather know.

For three seconds there was nothing but a faint, wet, rhythmic sound. Then a voice — Alma's voice, thin and papery and calm in a way Alma had never once been calm with me — said:

"Petra, dear. It's warm in here. There's a seat saved."

The line clicked dead.

I sat in the kitchen of my grandmother's house with every light burning and my heart going like a trapped bird, and I looked at the ledger, and the ledger lay open to its final page, and beneath the last entry — the one dated three days after the funeral, the word RENEWED in my grandmother's failing hand — a new line had appeared.

Fresh ink. Still glistening. Not my grandmother's handwriting, and not mine.

**WITNESSES: 1 REMAINING.**

And below that, in smaller letters, almost tender, almost apologetic, the way you'd write a note to a houseguest:

**APPOINTMENT AT THE NEW MOON. WE KEPT YOUR CHAIR.**

Outside, across the frozen yard, every window of the farmhouse lit up at once — including the windows I could see straight through, the ones with no rooms behind them — and in the glass of the kitchen window, briefly, in the fog of my own breath, a small hand drew a chair.

Then signed beneath it, in a dead boy's careful script:

*thank you*
