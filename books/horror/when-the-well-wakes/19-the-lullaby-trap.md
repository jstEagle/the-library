## 19. The Lullaby Trap

I want to say I slept badly because of the doors. That would be tidy — a professional woman kept awake by an anomaly, running the numbers, planning countermeasures. The truth is uglier. I slept badly because the house was warm.

Not stuffy-warm. Not radiator-broken warm. Warm like a body under blankets, warm like the kitchen of my childhood on a Sunday when somebody was always baking something I was never allowed to touch until it cooled. The farmhouse had no right to be warm. I'd cut the thermostat to fifty-five as an act of spite three nights ago, and the furnace had answered by going silent, and yet every room held that particular heat that lives in skin.

I'd chalked my lines before bed. Every threshold, every doorway, a fat white stroke with Petra's Pest Solutions stenciled across my knuckles in permanent marker, which is the kind of branding decision you make at twenty-three and regret forever. Eleven doors counted twice. Nine real ones. Two that hadn't existed last Tuesday, both locked, both warm as a sleeping animal's flank. I'd written the count on my forearm in ballpoint, the way I do on big commercial jobs: *11/9. Do not open 10 or 11.*

The lullaby started somewhere around three in the morning.

Here's what you need to know about me and lullabies: I don't have any. My mother didn't sing. My mother did a lot of things — smoked on fire escapes, dated men whose names she'd forget mid-introduction, left — but singing wasn't among them. My grandmother sang once, at the funeral, or rather she would have, except she was the one in the box, so instead there was a pastor with a voice like wet cardboard and a hymn nobody knew the second verse of.

So when the sound came drifting up through the floorboards, soft and wordless at first, a hum with a melody attached like a hook through the cheek of a fish, I didn't recognize it. That should have been the warning. A house full of someone else's memories shouldn't have had a melody I almost knew.

It resolved into words on the third pass. A woman's voice, low and smoke-roughened, singing in Swedish-inflected English about a boat on dark water, rowing, rowing, all the little ones asleep, and the oars don't make a sound, *the oars don't make a sound* —

My mother's voice.

I sat up in the guest room — my room now, apparently; the bed had been made with hospital corners I hadn't put in it — and my whole body went cold even though the air didn't. Because I knew that voice. You know your mother's voice the way you know your own heartbeat, whether you love it or not, whether you've heard it in eleven years or not. The specific grain of it. The way she swallowed her R's when she got gentle, which was rare, which was reserved for animals and dying things.

She used to sing that. Once. Maybe twice. On the drive up to this house when I was small enough to still be buckled into a car seat, some night when the roads were bad and she wanted me quiet. I had forgotten it completely. It had gone into the same landfill where childhoods go, and this house had dug through the trash and pulled it out and learned it.

"That's not hers," I said out loud, to the ceiling, to myself. "That's a recording. That's bait."

The voice didn't stop. It just got closer, or softer, which amounts to the same thing. It moved through the hallway below me the way a person moves when they're carrying something fragile and don't want to wake anyone — deliberate, weightless, kind. And God help me, the kindness was the worst part. My mother's voice had never been kind. Whatever was wearing it had studied the shape of kindness from the outside, the way I study termite damage, and it had gotten the proportions almost exactly right.

I got up. I'm telling you I got up, and I want it on the record that I told myself it was reconnaissance. Check the source. Locate the vector. That's what I said in my head, in my professional voice, the one I use on customers who want to argue about price. But my feet were bare on the floorboards and I hadn't grabbed the crowbar or the flashlight or the salt, and professionals gear up. What I was doing was following a song.

The hallway was dark except for the light under the attic door, which had been off when I went to bed. The stairs were cool under my feet. The voice came from the kitchen, and it had changed.

It took me four steps down to notice how it had changed, and another two to admit it.

It was my voice now.

My voice, singing a lullaby I had never learned, in a register I don't use, with a tenderness I have maybe deployed twice in my adult life and immediately regretted both times. It sang about the boat and the water and the sleeping children, and it sang the way I would sing if I were the kind of person who sang — if some better-distributed version of me had gotten the gentleness gene instead of the stubbornness one. It sounded like who I might have been if the Nystrom luck had run sweeter.

And here is the thing I will only admit once, here, in the privacy of my own head where nobody can subpoena it: it sounded good. It sounded like coming home after a long job, boots off, kettle on. It sounded like being taken care of by the only person alive who could do it right, which was me, because everyone else was dead or gone.

I reached the bottom of the stairs.

The kitchen door stood open. Beyond it, candlelight — actual candlelight, because the house knows I keep the lights burning and has apparently decided to be atmospheric about its ambushes — and the long table, and the chairs.

Twelve of them. I'd counted twelve yesterday, having accepted that counting was now a form of prayer. Twelve wooden chairs around a table built for eight.

Thirteen stood at the head, half-pulled out, waiting.

I don't know where chair thirteen came from. I don't mean metaphysically — I mean physically, mechanically, in what order its parts arrived and who assembled them. Yesterday the kitchen had twelve chairs and I had photographed each one like evidence, which is what I do, and today there were thirteen, and the thirteenth was old in the way the others weren't, dark wood worn pale at the arms where hands had gripped it for generations, and it was angled toward the door like a host pulling out a seat for a guest they've been expecting since before the guest was born.

The voice was coming from behind it. From inside the walls behind it, maybe, or from the space where a body would be if a body were sitting there, singing to itself, content.

"Petra," my own voice sang, between verses, conversational, sweet. "Come sit down, sweetheart. You're tired. Look how tired you are."

I was tired. That's the trap within the trap — the thing doesn't lie to you, it just tells you the truth in a voice that makes the truth unbearable. I hadn't slept properly in six days. My eyes felt packed with sand. My shoulders had climbed up around my ears and refused to come down. And my own voice, coming out of that warm dark kitchen, offered me the one thing I had never once been offered in forty-one years: rest without conditions. Sit down. Stop counting. Somebody else will keep watch tonight.

I took a step.

The floorboard under my heel was warm. Everything was warm. The warmth rose through my bare feet like a tide coming in, and I remember thinking, distantly, professionally, *warmth at floor level indicates a heat source beneath the structure, note for the report,* while the rest of me — the large majority of me, the part that doesn't file reports — drifted toward the kitchen door with the boneless compliance of a sleepwalker.

The lullaby swelled. My mother's voice again now, layered under mine, harmonizing, the two of us together for the first time in eleven years, and isn't that what I'd wanted, some bitter corner of me asked, isn't that the phone call I never made, the reconciliation I rehearsed in the shower and never dialed? Here it was. Here she was. All I had to do was walk nine more feet and sit down in a chair and let the boat carry us both across the dark water, all the little ones asleep, the oars making no sound.

Nine feet.

I got to five.

What stopped me wasn't courage. I want to be honest about that, because whatever Alma Voss thinks of me, and whatever I end up thinking of myself, the record should show that Petra Nystrom was not saved by bravery. She was saved by chalk.

My heel came down on the threshold of the kitchen doorway, and the chalk line cracked under it — a dry little snap, like a knuckle, like a branch — and a fine white dust puffed up against my ankle, and the warmth recoiled.

It recoiled. The way a hand pulls back from a stove. The tide went out of the floorboards all at once, and the cold rushed in behind it, and the cold was *mine*, the ordinary honest cold of a farmhouse at three-forty in the morning with the furnace off, and in that cold I heard the lullaby skip.

Just a hitch. A needle-jump. My voice and my mother's voice stumbling over the same syllable, catching, resuming — but the spell of it broke the way a dream breaks when you look at a clock, irrecoverably, and I stood on that threshold with white dust on my ankle and looked at chair thirteen and understood, with the flat clarity of the nearly-damned, that I had walked downstairs barefoot to answer an invitation.

"No," I said.

My voice, the real one, cracked on it. Fine. Cracked is still a word.

"No," I said again, louder, and turned around, and went back up the stairs the way you retreat from a dog you don't trust — facing it, slow, one step at a time.

The lullaby followed me. It didn't chase. Chasing would have been a mercy, some clear aggression I could fight. Instead it softened, verse by verse, the way a parent stops pushing when the child digs in, until by the time I reached my room it was barely a hum, and then it was the memory of a hum, and then it was nothing, and the silence it left behind was worse, because silence is what patience sounds like.

* * *

I didn't go back to bed. Obviously. I sat on the made bed with the crowbar across my knees like a security blanket with a felony record, and I re-chalked everything by flashlight, including the line I'd broken, double-width this time, and I added a new rule to the list on my forearm:

*Do not follow singing. Any singing. Even yours. ESPECIALLY yours.*

Then I sat there shaking, quietly, the way I shake after wasp jobs — adrenaline with nowhere to go — and I did the math I'd been avoiding.

The house had my mother's voice. Which meant the house had my mother somewhere in its archive, some true recording of her, some night on some bad road thirty-five years ago, and it had gone looking for it. Things don't search archives. People do. Whatever lived here wanted me lulled, and it had done its homework, and it had found the one sound on earth that could get me walking barefoot toward a chair.

But it had also made a mistake, and I held onto that mistake like a handrail. It had sung in *my* voice. Mine. Not my grandmother's, not Anders's, not some anonymous angel. It had reached into its collection and chosen the voice it calculated I'd trust most, and the calculation had been right — God, it had been right, five feet from correct — and it had still failed. Because underneath the wanting-to-rest, underneath the eleven years of unmade phone calls, there was something in me that heard my own voice coming out of a wall and thought: *that's wrong, that's counterfeit, I don't sound like that when I'm kind, I've never once sounded like that.*

The forgery was too good. That's what saved me. The real me doesn't sing to anyone.

I laughed at that, alone in the dark, and it came out wrong, half sob, and I let it, because there was nobody to see and the house had already heard everything anyway.

Toward dawn I went down for water — tap water only, I'd stopped trusting anything the house handed me warm — and I passed the kitchen doorway and made myself look. Chair thirteen sat where it had sat, patient, angled, its arms worn pale by generations of hands that had gripped them on their way down. On the seat, folded neatly, was a blanket. Hand-knitted. The colors had faded to the ghost of a pattern, but I knew the wool. Grandma's knitting. There had been a blanket like it folded over the back of her rocking chair my entire childhood, and it had gone into the ground with her, or I had assumed it had, because who steals a blanket out of a coffin?

The house, apparently. Or something wearing the house.

I didn't touch it. I wrote it down instead, in the ledger I'd started keeping opposite hers — date, time, observation, threat level — and my handwriting shook so hard the entry looked like it had been written during an earthquake, which, in fairness, it had.

At the bottom of the page, after the blanket, after the lullaby, after *5 feet*, I wrote the thing I'd been circling for six days without letting myself land on it:

*It isn't trying to scare me out. It's trying to sing me in.*

And then, because dawn was coming up gray through windows I no longer fully trusted to be windows, and because the exhaustion had receded just enough to let terror back in, I noticed the smell.

Bread. Rising yeast and browning crust, drifting up from the kitchen, warm and golden and absolutely impossible, because the oven was off, had been off, because I'd unplugged it along with everything else on the day I declared war.

I stood in the hallway holding my water glass and listened to the house bake.

On the kitchen table, when I finally made myself look, there were two place settings. Two plates. Two glasses. Napkins folded into shapes I didn't recognize. And at my place, laid carefully across the plate like a name card at a wedding, was my grandmother's amber brooch — the one she wore every day of her life, the one I'd watched the funeral director zip into a bag marked *personal effects* with my own eyes.

The fingerprints on the back of my chair were fresh. Still wet. Still glistening.

Dinner had been set for two, and one of us had been waiting a very long time.
