## 22. Mother's Phone Call

The phone rang at 4:17 in the afternoon, which was the first wrong thing about it.

I want to be clear about that, because everything else that followed might sound like the kind of story a woman tells after too many nights alone in a house with more doors than it should have. But I know phones. I've spent twenty years taking calls from panicked homeowners — *there's something scratching in my walls, how fast can you get here* — and I know what a phone sounds like when it's doing normal phone things. This one rang with a patience to it. Two long trills, a pause, two more. Like something on the other end had all the time in the world and wanted me to know it.

Also, nobody calls me. My clients text. Alma Voss doesn't own a working telephone and wouldn't trust one if she did. And my mother hadn't called me in eleven years, not since Dad's funeral, when she'd managed to make even the eulogy about her.

The landline in the farmhouse kitchen had been dead since before Grandma died. I'd checked. It's the kind of thing you check when your whole life is built on knowing where the exits are.

It kept ringing.

I stood in the kitchen doorway with a cup of coffee going cold in my hand and let it ring through four cycles, five, six, running the odds. Houses like this one — houses that are *trying* — don't usually announce themselves by phone. They prefer subtler channels: wallpaper, lullabies, chairs that arrive uninvited and dust themselves. A ringing phone felt almost crude. Almost human.

On the seventh cycle, I picked up.

* * *

"Petra."

My mother's voice. There is no mistaking it; it lives in the same part of my spine as the smell of cigarette smoke and the sound of a screen door slamming in 1998. She said my name the way she always did — like she was reading it off an invoice.

"Mom." I sat down at the table without meaning to, then caught myself and stood back up. Old reflexes. "Where are you calling from?"

"Does it matter?" A small laugh, dry as moth wings. "You never ask where I am. You just ask what I want. That's fair. I taught you that."

"I'm busy, Mom."

"You're in her house." Not a question. "I could tell. The line has a sound to it. That old wiring hums. You probably can't hear it anymore, but I can. I grew up inside that hum."

I looked at the phone jack on the wall behind me. The cord hung cut, frayed at the ends, the way it had hung for years — Grandma had gotten rid of the landline service when I was in high school, said the bill was robbery. The jack itself was empty. No plug. Nothing to unplug.

"Your line's dead," I said. "This house hasn't had phone service since—"

"Since before you were born, if you go back far enough," she said pleasantly. "And yet here we are. Isn't that something."

There was a quality to her calm that I couldn't place at first, and then I could: it was the calm of someone who has already decided. I hear it from clients sometimes, right before they tell me they're not going to fix the raccoon problem after all, they're just going to sell the house as-is and let it be somebody else's Tuesday. Decision takes the temperature out of a voice. My mother sounded like a woman standing in front of a thermostat she no longer intended to touch.

"Are you okay?" I asked, because some part of me is still nine years old and asks that question every time she calls, no matter how many decades pass between calls.

"I'm fine, sweetheart. Better than fine. I sleep well these days. First time in years." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gone soft in a way that frightened me more than shouting would have. "You should see how well I sleep."

"Mom—"

"She kept her appointments, you know. Your grandmother. Every generation, right on time. She used to write to me about it, back when we still wrote. Little notes between the lines of birthday cards. *The house is quiet. The chair is warm. He's so patient.*" My mother laughed again, and this time there was something underneath the laugh, something wet. "I thought she meant a man. Can you believe that? I was jealous of a man I'd never met. I spent thirty years jealous of him."

"Who, Mom? Who's *he*?"

"You know who he is. You've been living with him for three weeks." Her voice turned gently chiding, the tone she'd used when I left my boots in the hall. "You count his doors. He counts yours. Did you know that? He told me he counts yours. He said you have very steady hands for a woman who shakes so much."

The coffee cup rattled against the counter and I realized I was holding it again, gripping it like a handlebar. I set it down. I made myself set it down.

"What do you want, Mom?"

* * *

Here's what I knew about my mother, going into that call: she left when I was fourteen. Not dramatically — no note, no scene, just a gradual evacuation, weekends away that became weeks, until one day her side of the closet was a memory and Dad stopped setting out a third plate without ever saying he'd stopped. She moved to Spokane. She sent cards on birthdays with money in them, crisp twenties like tips. When Dad got sick she came back for exactly as long as the hospice required and then evaporated again, leaving me the estate paperwork and a voicemail that said, *You were always better at this than me, Petra.*

I'd built a whole personality out of not needing her. Traps over talk. Counting doors because doors stay counted, unlike mothers. I want to say I was over it. I want to say the wound had scarred into something useful, callus instead of cut.

But when she said *sweetheart*, some animal part of me rolled over.

That's the thing I understood later, standing in that kitchen with the dead phone warm in my hand: the presence didn't need to trick me with lullabies or phantom dinners. It had my mother's voice. It had eleven years of hunger wearing my mother's voice. All it had to do was dial.

"Just sit," she said.

I blinked. "What?"

"At the table. Just sit down, sweetheart. One sitting. That's all it's ever asked of anyone, and look how much it gives back. Grandma lived to ninety-four in that drafty old barn of a house. Ninety-four! Nobody in this family dies young anymore. Do you understand what that's worth? Anders drowned in 1961 and he's still—" She stopped herself. Started over, calmer. "You've seen him. In the glass. He looked good, didn't he? Healthy boy. Rosy. The water never touched him, not really."

"The water killed him," I said. "Whatever's wearing him isn't him."

"Oh, Petra." She sighed, and the sigh was so precisely my mother's sigh — the one that meant *you're being difficult, you've always been difficult* — that my eyes stung before I could stop them. "You always did need everything explained. Everything proven. Your father was the same way. Wanted receipts for love." Another pause. "I'm not asking you to prove anything. I'm asking you to sit in a chair. People sit in chairs every day. It's easier than what you're planning. Don't think I don't know what you're planning."

My mouth went dry. "Which is what?"

"Alma Voss has been filling your head. That woman's family ran from their turn, you know. Ran all the way to the county line and then crept back broke ten years later, hat in hand, and the house took its interest anyway. Ask her what happened to her brother. Go on. Ask her." A beat. "Or don't. It doesn't matter. None of the old stories matter now. What matters is the new moon, sweetheart, and what happens to this valley if you keep being stubborn."

"And what happens?"

Silence. Long enough that I checked whether the call had dropped. Then, quieter, almost tender:

"You'll find out. Or you won't, if you sit. See? One option comes with a tomorrow in it. I know which one I'd pick." And then, in a voice that cracked clean down the middle, suddenly and horribly young: "Petra, I'm tired. I'm so tired. It's been showing me things, and I can't — I can't hold them all. If you'd just sit, it would stop showing me things. Please. Please, baby. It's easier."

For one full second I almost said yes.

Not because I believed her. Because she was crying, and my mother never cried, and some buried girl in me heard that crack in her voice and thought: *finally, finally, she needs me.* Eleven years of crisp-twenties-in-a-card, and now she needed me, and all it would cost was sitting down. Sitting down. The easiest verb in the language.

Then I heard it. Underneath her voice, faint but unmistakable, keeping time like a second heartbeat: humming. A lullaby. The one the house had been singing through the walls for days, the one from Door Thirteen, the one in Grandma's tape that she used to sing to me before —

Before.

I pulled the phone away from my ear and looked at it. An old beige handset, cordless, the kind with a charging cradle that hadn't held a charge since the Clinton administration. Its little screen was lit. It shouldn't have had power. Nothing in this house had power except what the house gave it.

"Mom," I said slowly, putting the handset back to my ear. "What's the number of the house you grew up in?"

She didn't miss a beat. "Sweetheart, that's not important—"

"What street. What town. Say it."

A pause. Then, pleasant as a receptionist: "You know I've never been good with numbers. That was always your gift." And beneath her words, the humming swelled, sweet and patient, and I realized the lullaby wasn't coming through the phone at all. It was coming from the hallway behind me. From the direction of the stairs. From everywhere, the way warmth comes from everywhere.

I hung up. The handset went into its cradle with a click that sounded, in the silent kitchen, like a lock turning.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world stops making sense: I worked the problem.

The farmhouse had one phone jack, in the kitchen, and it was empty — I verified it with my own hands, fingers in the socket, nothing but dust and a spider that objected to the eviction. The cordless handset had no battery in it at all. I opened the compartment and shook it out over the table to prove it to myself. Nothing fell. The compartment was spotless, factory-empty, and the screen glowed anyway, soft and green, displaying a single line of text I hadn't noticed before:

**1 MISSED CALL — HOME**

Home. Calling home. The house calling itself, or my mother calling from inside it, or my mother *as* inside it — the categories had stopped meaning much by then.

I carried the handset outside by the porch rail and set it on the railing like evidence at a crime scene, which, in a sense, it was. Then I called Alma from my cell, pacing the yard, watching the upstairs window while I waited for her to pick up.

"Voss."

"It's Petra. Two questions." I kept my eyes on the glass. Behind it, at the top of the stairs, something stood very still, the way a person stands when they're listening to a conversation they already know the ending of. "First: what happened to your brother?"

The silence on Alma's end lasted long enough for me to count the fence posts between me and the road. Fourteen. There had been twelve yesterday.

"Oskar," she said finally, and her voice had gone flat and careful, a woman walking on ice she knew personally. "He sat in the chair in 1974. Our turn came around and Papa was in the hospital and Mama couldn't — she couldn't do it, her nerve was gone. So Oskar sat. Sixteen years old. He said it was just a chair." A breath. "He sat down at supper and he stood up at supper and the boy who stood up had Oskar's face and none of Oskar in it. He smiled at us across the table and thanked Mama for the meal and walked out into the fields and we never saw him again. But sometimes, on still nights, our kitchen smelled like him. For years. Like a boy who'd just come in from the cold." Another breath, shakier. "That's what happened to my brother. Now your second question."

"When my mother calls this house — what does she sound like?"

Alma was quiet for a moment. "Petra. Your mama's been dead six years."

The yard tilted. I gripped the fence post — post thirteen, iron-cold under my palm — and made myself breathe. "No. She's in Spokane. She sends—"

"She died in March of 'nineteen. Heart, in her sleep. I drove to the funeral because nobody else from the valley would go, on account of her leaving the way she did. Small turnout. I paid for the flowers myself, the yellow ones, because I remembered she liked yellow." Alma's voice gentled into something I'd never heard her use with me. "Girl, whoever you talked to today, it wasn't her. It wore her because it's worn everyone in this valley at one time or another. It keeps us all on file. Every voice that ever fed it."

I looked at the handset on the porch rail. Its green screen glowed patiently in the dusk.

**1 MISSED CALL — HOME**

"But it knew things," I said. "About Grandma. About the appointments. About what I'm planning. Alma, it said it knows what I'm planning."

"Of course it does. It's been reading this family's intentions for sixty years like a menu." A pause, and when Alma spoke again, each word came out placed like a stone in a wall. "One more thing, and you're not going to like it. You said it urged you to sit. To make it easy."

"Yes."

"That's new. It's never had to ask before. There was always an heir willing, or an heir scared enough. You're the first Nystrom who's said no and meant it." I could hear her swallowing. "Things that have always been fed don't ask, Petra. They ask when the pantry's empty and the bill's come due. Whatever it offered you — however much it sounded like love — remember that it was negotiating. And things that negotiate are things that are afraid."

I hung up and stood in the darkening yard, counting fence posts because counting was breathing and breathing was staying upright. Fourteen posts. Fourteen. The corn at the field's edge stirred without wind, row by row, like something large turning over in its sleep.

Behind me, in the kitchen, the phone began to ring again.

Two long trills. A pause. Two more. Patient as a metronome.

I didn't go inside. I stood at the fence with my back to my own house and listened to it ring — twelve times, thirteen, fourteen — and on the fifteenth ring the ringing stopped mid-trill, and through the open kitchen door, clear across the frozen yard, I heard my mother's voice, coming not from any phone now but from the heart of the house, warm and reasonable and terribly tired:

"That's alright, sweetheart. Take your time. We'll keep your plate warm."

And then, softer, in a child's pencil-scrawl voice I recognized from the wallpaper, from the layers beneath the layers, dated decades before my birth:

"She always comes home. They always come home."

The lights in every window of the farmhouse came on at once — every room, every floor, every door — glowing out at me like a face smiling too wide, and I understood, standing alone in the cold with my mother six years in her grave, that the next new moon wasn't a deadline anymore.

It was a dinner reservation.
