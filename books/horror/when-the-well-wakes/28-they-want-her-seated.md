## 28. They Want Her Seated

The grange hall smelled of coffee and floor wax and something underneath both, something like the inside of a root cellar that had been left shut too long. I'd come for answers. What I got was a folding chair with my name on it.

Not figuratively. Somebody had taped a strip of masking tape to the backrest and written PETRA in ballpoint, in careful block letters, the way you'd label a lunchbox. I stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long moment, and the thing I noticed — the thing my brain offered up instead of the screaming it should have been doing — was that the handwriting was neat. Considerate. Whoever had made that chair ready had wanted me to feel welcome.

That's the trick of this valley, I was learning. Everything horrible here arrives dressed as hospitality.

"Miss Nystrom." Dale Erickson, from the co-op, materialized at my elbow with a cup of coffee I hadn't asked for. He was a big man, soft-spoken, the kind who fixed your furnace and remembered your dog's name. There were bruises on his wrist. Four small ovals, dark as thumbprints in dough, arranged exactly where a hand would grip if a hand were guiding him somewhere he didn't especially want to go. "We're glad you came. We've been meaning to reach out."

"Have you." I took the coffee because refusing felt dangerous in a way I couldn't articulate. It was good coffee. Of course it was. "Dale, can I ask you something? Your wrist."

He looked down at it like he'd forgotten it was attached to him. "Bumped the doorframe," he said. "I'm clumsy."

"You're not clumsy. You've replaced my grandmother's gutters twice. You climb ladders like a man with a personal grudge against gravity."

Something flickered behind his eyes then — a quick animal panic, there and gone, like a bird hitting glass. For half a second I think *he* was home in there. Then it smoothed over and he smiled, warm as fresh bread, and said, "You should sit down, Miss Nystrom. You've had a hard week."

"I've had a hard week," I agreed, "which is why I'm going to stay standing."

* * *

Here's what I knew by then, or thought I knew: the Warden had been invited into this valley before it was ever invited into my family. The ledger's cipher had led me to the church's founding register, and there it was in faded iron-gall ink — VESSEL-WARDEN, entered among the names of elders and deacons like it belonged there, which apparently it once had. The town had made its bargain first. My grandmother's people had just been the ones who kept paying on time.

And the interest was everywhere, now that I knew how to look. Mrs. Halvorsen at the bake table had bruises at her collarbone, chair-height if you were sitting. The pastor's handshake lingered a beat too long, and when he let go, his palm was greasy. Not dirty — *greasy*, the way the kitchen walls at the farmhouse were greasy, the way everything the Warden touched was greasy, like it sweated lamp oil and patience.

"We're not asking you to do anything strange," the pastor said, gathering us toward the front of the hall. His voice had the practiced gentleness of a man talking someone off a ledge, except nobody was on a ledge; we were all just standing around a folding chair. "This has been our way for a hundred years. It works. Nobody starves. Nobody freezes. Nobody's child drowns in the creek and stays drowned." He paused, and I watched forty faces soften at once, like butter left in the sun. "Your grandmother understood that, God rest her. She kept her appointments for fifty years. All we ask is that you honor her last one."

"My grandmother," I said, "left a tape telling me never to sit down."

Murmurs. Not shocked murmurs. *Disappointed* murmurs, the sound of a congregation watching a child misbehave.

"She was tired at the end," Dale said gently. "Tired people say things."

"She was clear-eyed at the end. She hid the tape in a coffin vault, Dale. That takes planning and a crowbar."

"Petra." Alma Voss wasn't supposed to be there. She'd been missing two days, her porch light burning over an empty house, and every hour she stayed gone had been carving a little more room out of me. But there she was, near the back, coat buttoned wrong, one shoe untied, looking like a woman who'd been walking all night. Her eyes found mine and did something no other eyes in that hall did: they *apologized*. "Don't argue with them. Arguing feeds it. Just leave."

"She's not leaving," said Mrs. Halvorsen, pleasantly. "She hasn't had dinner."

It was four o'clock in the afternoon. Nobody laughed. That was the worst part — nobody laughed, because it wasn't a joke, it was a schedule.

* * *

I want to be fair to these people, because fairness is a trap too but I keep falling into it. They weren't monsters. They were farmers and teachers and the woman who'd sold me 4-H raffle tickets when I was nine. They were hostages who'd been held so long they'd started decorating the cell. A hundred years of mild winters and full barns and children pulled gasping out of creeks will buy a lot of loyalty, and the Warden had bought it on credit, generation after generation, and now the note was coming due and the whole valley had decided I was the collateral.

So when six of them closed around me — gently, so gently, hands at my elbows and shoulders like they were escorting an elderly relative across ice — I understood that they believed they were saving me.

Understanding didn't make their grip hurt less.

"There's a storm coming," Dale said into my ear, breath warm, voice kind. "Nobody should be alone tonight. Your grandmother always said—"

"My grandmother said a lot of things, and half of them are in a ledger in code."

"Then you'll want to be home," said the pastor, "where the ledger is."

They walked me out of the grange hall and down the county road toward the farmhouse, and the whole way, the town did its best impression of normal. Kids on bikes waved. Somebody's radio played through a screen door. The sun went down orange and ordinary over the tree line, and I counted my steps because counting is what I do, and I got to four hundred and twelve before I realized the road was wrong.

Not wrong like broken. Wrong like *longer*. I have walked that road my whole life — well, I had walked it in pieces, before the funeral, before all this — and it is one point three miles from the grange to the farmhouse gate. I know because I've measured worse things for work. By step five hundred we should have been turning up the drive. At seven hundred we were still walking between the same two fields, and the farmhouse sat ahead of us exactly as far away as it had been at step one, patient as a photograph.

"It doesn't like to rush," Alma said, falling in beside me. She was the only one not touching me. "It likes you to arrive rested."

"Alma." My voice came out low. "Two days. Where were you?"

Her jaw worked. "My kitchen," she said finally. "Mostly. I don't remember leaving it. I remember sitting down to wait for the kettle, and then it was Thursday and my tea was cold and there was a second chair in my kitchen that I have never owned." She showed me her wrists. Bruises, four apiece, neat as buttons. "It doesn't take you anywhere, Petra. It just holds you at the table until you agree to stay. And I'm old and lonely and it knows it, and I am telling you this because you are the only person in this valley who might still get out."

"Then help me get out."

"I'm trying." She glanced at the hands on my arms. "But they'll carry you the rest of the way if you run, and they won't even know they're doing it. Look at Dale's face."

I looked. Dale was smiling at the sunset like a man on his honeymoon. His feet moved in perfect time with mine.

* * *

The gate, when we finally reached it, was open. It is never open. My grandmother chained that gate in 1987 after a heifer got out, and I'd cut that chain myself with bolt cutters the week after the funeral because I'm the one who deals with what's left behind. The chain was lying in the grass, coiled neatly, links gleaming like it had been polished.

The yard was mowed. The porch light was on. On the porch, framed by the door, stood the wooden chair.

It had come out to greet me. That's the only way I can describe it — it stood on the boards facing the yard the way a host stands in the doorway, and though chairs don't have posture, this one had posture. It was glad we'd come. Somewhere under my sternum, my body understood that better than my mind did, and my legs tried to slow down without asking me first, the way a horse slows near its own barn.

"No," I said, to my own knees, quietly.

"Almost there," said Mrs. Halvorsen, mistaking me entirely. Or maybe not mistaking me. Maybe hearing me perfectly and translating.

They got me up the porch steps. I could have fought — I'm stronger than I look and meaner than I am strong — but there were six of them and they weren't enemies, they were neighbors, and some cowardly part of me still didn't want to break Dale's nose. So I stalled. Professional habit. When you can't win, you inspect.

"That's a nice chair," I said loudly, to nobody, to everybody. "Antique? Let me see the joinery."

And while they exchanged the puzzled glances of people whose script had just skipped a line, I dropped my weight, twisted out of the grip on my left arm, and drove my elbow backward into the softest target available, which happened to be Pastor Lindqvist's solar plexus. He folded with a sound like a bellows. I'm not proud of it. I'd do it again.

Then I ran — not down the steps, not for the gate, because the gate was a lie and the road was a lie and every direction this house approved of was a lie. I ran along the porch, past the chair, around the north side of the house where the siding rotted and nobody ever went, into the narrow dark between the farmhouse and the windbreak pines.

And there was a door.

There is no door on the north side of the farmhouse. There has never been a door on the north side. I have crawled under that house chasing a fisher cat, I have hung Christmas lights on those eaves, I know that wall the way I know my own teeth. But there it was: a plain wooden door set flush in the siding, painted the same tired white as the trim, with a latch worn bright from use.

Warm air breathed out around its edges. From behind it, faintly, came the smell of bread.

Every instinct I own said *not that one*. Every rule on my chalked thresholds said a door that appears overnight belongs to the thing that appeared it. But behind me I heard the porch boards creak under six sets of polite feet, and Dale calling my name with such terrible kindness, and I did the math the way I do it on every job: which exit costs least.

I went through the door that wasn't there yesterday.

* * *

It put me out into the corn.

Not through the kitchen, not through any room I recognized — the door opened directly onto the field behind the west shelterbelt, which is impossible, which I filed away with the eleven doors and the penciled name under the wallpaper, in the growing drawer of impossible things I'd deal with later if there was a later. The stalks were high and dead and rattling, frozen stiff since the first hard frost, and the sky above them was going the deep bruised purple of a winter dusk.

Behind me, the false door clicked shut and became, when I turned, a stretch of unbroken siding. Good. Honest architecture again.

Ahead of me, out in the corn, somebody began to walk.

I heard it before I saw anything: footsteps in the frozen rows, unhurried, evenly spaced, coming from the direction of the farmhouse. Then another set, farther out, flanking me to the north. Then a third, south. Polite footsteps. The kind that don't stomp, don't hurry, don't crunch any louder than they have to, because the people making them are conscious of disturbing you.

"Dale?" I called. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the one inheritance from my grandmother I actually value. "Pastor? This isn't funny."

No answer. Just the footsteps, converging, patient as a metronome — and I hated myself for the comparison even as I made it, because I'd heard that exact rhythm knocking from inside the root cellar, and metronomes don't knock, and whatever was walking in the corn was keeping time.

I ran.

Cornfields in winter are the worst country on earth for running. The stalks are brittle and they cut, the ground is rutted from harvest equipment, and every row looks identical, which is the point, I suppose, if you're a thing that wants a person to lose her bearings. I crashed through row after row, lungs burning, and the footsteps kept pace without hurrying, always thirty feet back, always thirty feet to either side, herding without touching. They never gained. They never had to. They knew something I was only beginning to understand: that the field itself was on the invitation list.

I stopped counting rows and started counting breaths. Four in, four out, the way I breathe through a wasp job. Then I stopped counting breaths and started counting doors, silently, in order, the way I chalk them every night — front, back, cellar, pantry, bath, three bedrooms, summer kitchen, the mystery eleventh that hadn't existed in September. Nine real ones. Eleven counted. The ritual settled over me like a coat, and under its cover my mind went cold and professional, and cold and professional is where I do my best thinking.

What does a pest controller know that matters? We know that animals follow edges. We know that a cornered thing panics and a routed thing thinks. And we know that every infestation has a structure — a nest, a route, a reason — and that the structure is always built around what the pest wants.

The Warden wanted me seated. Everything — the long road, the open gate, the chair on the porch, the neighbors' gentle hands — bent toward that single outcome. Which meant every path it left open was a funnel, and every obstacle it raised was a fence, and the way out of a funnel is not forward or back.

It's sideways, where nobody built anything.

I stopped running. I stood in the frozen corn, chest heaving, fog pouring out of me, and listened. Footsteps paused. Thirty feet back. Thirty left. Thirty right. Waiting to see what I'd do, courteous as ushers.

Then I dropped low and crawled.

Under the stalks, along the frozen furrow, on my belly like a man after a raccoon in a crawlspace, I crawled sideways across the rows, perpendicular to everything, through the one layer of the world nobody had thought to guard because nobody walks there. Dirt packed cold against my ribs. Stalk-edges slicing my jacket. Above me the footsteps shifted, confused, spreading, and for the first time they sounded less polite — quicker, querying, a little lost.

I crawled until the corn thinned, until the ground rose toward the fenceline, until I could see the black bulk of the shelterbelt and, beyond it, the pale scar of the gravel pit road. I rolled under the fence wire and lay in the ditch grass, gasping, bleeding from a dozen shallow lines, alive.

In the corn, the footsteps stopped.

All of them. At once. The silence they left was enormous.

And then, from the middle of the field, from the exact center of the exact row I'd fled, a voice spoke — not loud, pitched for me alone, carried on air that shouldn't have worked that way. It used my grandmother's voice. Of course it did. It had been practicing her longer than anyone.

"You'll come back, Petra. Everyone comes back. This valley is a table, and you were born into the place setting."

I lay in the ditch and pressed grandma's brooch through my pocket into my hip, cold as a verdict, and I said nothing, because arguing feeds it, Alma had said, and for once in my life I took advice.

The lights of the farmhouse came on across the field, every window, warm and gold and welcoming, a house holding its breath for my return. I got up on shaking legs and turned my back on it and started walking the long way to town, along the gravel pit road, counting my steps, four hundred and twelve, four hundred and thirteen —

At four hundred and sixteen, I passed Alma Voss's parked car, empty, driver's door open, engine ticking as it cooled.

Alma had told me she'd walked to the grange. Alma had been standing beside me an hour ago.

Her coat was folded on the driver's seat, buttoned side up, neat as laundry.

The car was pointed toward the farmhouse.
