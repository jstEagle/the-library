## 32. New Moon Eve

I woke to the smell of bread.

Not the sour, chemical ghost of a plug-in air freshener trying to pass itself off as cinnamon. Real bread. Yeast and warmth and the faint scorched sweetness of a crust that had come out of the oven maybe twenty minutes ago, in an oven I had unplugged three days ago along with everything else that fed this house.

I lay in my grandmother's bed — my bed now, I supposed, though I hadn't let myself say the word *mine* even in the privacy of my own skull — and listened to the house breathe. That's what it did at night. Not creaking, exactly. Creaking is structural. This was slower. Rhythmic. The sound of something very large and very patient inhaling through its walls.

The bread smell drifted up the stairs like an apology.

"Nice try," I told the ceiling.

The ceiling didn't answer, but the floorboards under my feet were warm when I got up, warm the way a dog's flank is warm, and I made myself walk across them anyway because if I started avoiding the warm spots I'd end up living in one corner of one room until the new moon came and took me where warmth couldn't follow.

In the kitchen: a loaf on a wire rack, golden-brown, split open along the top like a healed wound. Butter beside it in the dish Grandma kept for company — the good dish, the one with the blue rim that only came out of the cupboard twice a year. A knife laid across a clean plate. A glass of milk, sweating gently, as if someone had poured it moments before I walked in.

There are two kinds of fear. There's the kind that makes you run, and there's the kind that makes you sit down at your own kitchen table because your hands are shaking too badly to stand. I did not eat the bread. But I stood there for a long time with my hand flat on the counter, feeling the wood hum against my palm like a struck tuning fork, and I will not lie to you: part of me wanted to cry, because nobody had baked for me since I was nine years old.

That's the thing they don't put in the folklore books. The monster doesn't always roar. Sometimes it does your laundry.

* * *

I found the laundry at noon.

I'd been in the pantry again, going through the ledger for the hundredth time, running my thumb over the columns of dates and initials and that single recurring word — RENEWED, RENEWED, RENEWED — like a rosary made of debt. When I came back out, every piece of clothing I owned was folded. Not just folded. Folded *well*, corners squared, socks paired by weight and color, my work shirts stacked in the order I'd have chosen myself if I'd bothered to choose. My boots sat by the door, cleaned of mud, laces tucked inside, dried from the rain I'd slogged through yesterday. Someone — some*thing* — had taken the wet newspaper stuffing out of them and replaced it with cedar shavings.

I stood in the hallway holding one of my own shirts like it might bite.

"You don't get to do this," I said out loud. My voice sounded small in a house that had learned to be bigger than its walls. "You don't get to be *nice*. Nice is how you people — how you *things* — get invited in."

The hallway light flickered once. Not menacingly. Almost sheepish.

And here's the part I'm ashamed of, the part I'll deny under oath: I wore the shirt. Because it was clean, and because I was tired down to the marrow, and because a person can only fight a house for so many days before she starts accepting small mercies from it, and every mercy accepted is a hook set a little deeper.

Grandma knew that. Grandma knew all of it. Her tape was still in my jacket pocket, the cassette labeled FOR THE ONE WHO COUNTS DOORS, and I'd played it so many times the ribbon was going thin. *Don't sit down, Petra. Whatever it offers you, whatever it shows you, however tired you get — don't sit.* She'd meant to break the chain herself. She'd told me that much. She'd gotten as far as learning the rite and then she'd stopped, and I used to think it was cowardice. Now, standing in a hallway full of folded laundry that smelled faintly of lavender, I understood it better than I wanted to. It wasn't cowardice. It was arithmetic. She'd done the math on what uninviting would cost her, and she'd blinked.

Every memory of belonging. That was the price. Every memory of the family, burned away like frost off a window. You could speak the Warden's name and renounce the invitation and save yourself and the valley besides, and you'd stand afterward in an empty kitchen unable to say why there was a brooch in your pocket or why your face was wet.

I turned the brooch over in my hand. Grandma's garnet brooch, the one she'd worn to church and funerals and nothing in between. It had been waiting for me on the dinner table two nights ago, marking my place like a name card at a wedding. I'd pocketed it instead of sitting, and it had been cold ever since. Not cool. Cold. Cold as a verdict, cold as a stone pulled out of a well, and it hadn't warmed against my skin once in forty-eight hours no matter how long I carried it.

Maybe that should have frightened me more than it did. Mostly it felt like company.

* * *

The mirrors started around four in the afternoon.

I was in the bathroom washing my face when the fog on the glass began to move. Not run — *move*, gathering itself into lines, into shapes, into letters written in a child's careful hand:

**PLEASE DON'T GO**

I shut off the water. The words stayed.

"I know what you're doing," I said to the mirror. To him. "It won't work."

More fog gathered beneath the first line, slower now, the way Anders wrote when he was being careful with me, when he was trying to make each letter perfect:

**I WAITED 60 YEARS FOR SOMEONE TO TALK TO**

"Oh, that's low," I whispered. "That is genuinely low."

He appeared then, behind my reflection, the way he always did — a boy of about ten, dripping slightly, hair plastered to his forehead, wearing a white christening gown gone gray with pond water. He'd been dead since 1961. He'd been alive since 1961, too, which was the whole problem. Drowned in the farm pond on a Sunday afternoon and walking again by suppertime, and the family had knelt around his bed and wept and thanked God, and God had nothing to do with it. Something else had carried him back up out of the water, something the family had invited in generations before to save another dying child, and Anders was its receipt.

He smiled at me in the mirror. It was a sweet smile. It was a child's smile. It was also sixty years old and it did not reach eyes that had seen the bottom of a pond and whatever was underneath it.

I left the bathroom. He followed, mirror to mirror, hall glass to dresser glass to the dark window over the kitchen sink, and everywhere he appeared he signed a little more, hands moving in the fog, patient as tide:

**STAY**
**IT'S WARM HERE**
**NOBODY HAS TO BE ALONE**

"That's not true," I said, and my voice cracked on the last word, and I hated him for hearing it, and I hated the thing wearing him more. "People are alone all the time. People are alone their whole lives. It doesn't kill them. Being alone never killed anybody."

But standing there in the kitchen with the bread smell still hanging in the air and a drowned boy signing *stay* in every reflective surface, I couldn't actually remember believing that.

* * *

By dusk the house had stopped pretending to be subtle.

Dinner was laid when I came in from checking the chalk lines — I checked them eleven times a day now, out of habit, though the doors had settled back to nine and I didn't trust that either. Roast chicken. Potatoes. Green beans from a garden that hadn't been planted in fifteen years. Two place settings. My place marked with a napkin folded into the shape of a swan, which was a trick Grandma used to do, which meant the thing at the table had been paying attention for decades, which meant it knew her better than I ever had, and I stood in the doorway hating how much that hurt.

At the far setting sat the chair. The wooden chair from the photographs, the extra chair, the renewal chair, pulled out from the table at a welcoming angle. Empty. Waiting. Warm, I had no doubt, if I touched it. I didn't touch it.

Instead I ate standing up, at the counter, with my back to the room, and I could feel the empty chair behind me the way you feel a stranger reading over your shoulder. The chicken was the best thing I'd eaten in a week. That was the cruelest part. Everything it gave me was good. Warm bread, clean laundry, a hot meal, a boy in the glass who only wanted company. If horror had a flavor, it would be this: comfort, served by something that intended to digest you.

Afterward, Anders found me in the parlor. All the mirrors at once, and the black television screen, and the glass of the framed photograph of Grandma as a young woman — he stood behind her younger self and pressed his dripping palms to the inside of the picture and wrote:

**IF YOU GO IT TAKES ME TOO**

I stopped.

That was new. That was a card it hadn't played, and I stood there in the lamplight with my heart going like a trapped bird, because I'd spent weeks thinking of Anders as bait, as lure, as the wax on the hook — and it had never once occurred to me that he might also be a hostage. That the presence that saved him owned him. That if I spoke the Warden's true name tomorrow night and renounced the invitation, everything the Warden had made might go back into the ground with it.

Including a boy who'd been dead for sixty years and didn't know it.

"It takes everyone it made," I said slowly. "Doesn't it. You're not asking me to stay for you. You're asking me to stay because you're afraid."

The fog swirled. For a long moment nothing formed. Then, in letters smaller than any he'd written yet, almost shy:

**IS THERE A DIFFERENCE**

God help me, I sat down on the parlor floor right there, cross-legged, facing the photograph, and I talked to him for an hour. About the pond. About what he remembered — cold, then green light, then a woman's voice that wasn't his mother's saying *come along, love, you're late*. About whether it hurt. He answered in the fog, letter by letter, and somewhere in the middle of it I realized I was crying, and somewhere after that I realized the crying wasn't entirely mine — the house was crying with me, the walls damp with condensation, the windows weeping streaks down the glass, the whole structure grieving in sympathy like a tuned string.

It wanted me to believe we were already family. That was the trap. Not the chair. The belonging.

* * *

Near midnight I did the rounds. Lights burning in every room — my lights, my electricity, the generator I'd hauled in myself, because I'd told the house *I keep the lights on, not you* and I intended to keep telling it. Chalk lines fresh at every threshold. Iron filings poured in a thin gray seam across the cellar door. Salt in the windowsills.

At the root cellar, the knocking had changed. For days it had been patient, metronomic, three knocks at intervals. Tonight it was slow. Almost tender. Knock... knock... knock. Like a parent outside a child's bedroom door, giving fair warning before coming in to say goodnight.

I put my palm flat against the cellar door. It was warm. Under my hand, faintly, I felt the vibration of something humming — and I recognized the tune before I recognized what it meant, because Alma had sung it to me over coffee two days ago, haltingly, remembering: the lullaby Grandma used to sing, the one my mother sang, the one that had lived in this valley longer than either of them.

"Don't," I said quietly. "You don't get her voice. You don't get any of them."

The humming stopped. And then, through four inches of oak and sixty years of silence, something in the cellar sighed — a long, disappointed, almost human breath, the sigh of a guest who has done everything right and cannot understand why the hostess is being so cold.

Tomorrow was the new moon. Tomorrow night, at the table, before the marks of every witness it had collected — Alma's fingerprints among them, bruised into her skin at chair-height — I would speak its name. VESSEL-WARDEN. The ledger hid it in plain sight, hymn numbers and initials, and I'd rehearsed the renunciation until the words lived in my teeth. *This invitation is withdrawn. By the blood that offered it, generation upon generation, I uninvite you. Your welcome is ended.*

And then I would forget why I'd said it. Forget her face. Forget the funeral, the lullaby, the smell of bread, the shape of the swan napkin. Forget Anders, who was currently watching me from the dark kitchen window with his palms pressed to the glass, writing one last line in the frost:

**WILL IT HURT YOU**

"Yes," I told him honestly. "Yes, I think it will."

He nodded, slow, the way children nod when they've understood something adults try to soften. Then he lifted one dripping hand and — I will carry this to whatever grave I get, even if I don't know whose grave it is — he signed:

**THEN I HOPE IT'S QUICK**

I slept in the truck that night, engine off, doors locked, chalk dust on my sleeves and the brooch in my fist. Through the windshield I watched the farmhouse burn its lights against the moonless dark, every window gold, every room warm, keeping itself lit for me like a wife who won't accept that her husband isn't coming home.

Around three in the morning, I heard the front door open. Just once. Just wide enough.

And from the doorway, in a voice like warm bread and folded laundry and sixty years of patience, the house called my name — softly, kindly, the way you'd call a child in from the cold —

and waited to see if I'd answer.
