## 2. A Ledger of Small Doubts

The house gave Jonah a room that had never been theirs.

It was the blue room at the end of the east hall — Grandmother Odile's sewing room, once, though the sewing machine was gone and the walls had been repainted a gray so pale it seemed less like a color than an apology. Someone had made the bed. Someone had set a glass of water on the nightstand and a folded towel at the foot of the mattress, the way you prepare for a guest whose stay you hope will be brief.

Jonah sat on the edge of the bed in their funeral clothes with their shoes still on and listened to the house settle around them like a large animal rearranging itself in sleep.

Below, faintly, through two floors of old pine: voices. The family was still up. They were keeping vigil, Corinne had said, as if Wren's death were a storm they were all weathering together, hands joined, faces turned toward the same horizon. But the voices didn't sound like grief. They sounded like people waiting for a kettle to boil. Low, unhurried, occasionally punctuated by a laugh that stopped too quickly when it remembered where it was.

Jonah took off their shoes. Lay down without undressing. Stared at the ceiling, where a hairline crack ran from the light fixture to the corner of the room like a river on a map of nowhere.

*The hands are wrong.*

The thought arrived again, patient as water finding its level. It had been arriving all day, all through the drive from the airport, all through the receiving line where relatives pressed their mouths to Jonah's cheek and said *she's at peace* and *she looks so natural* and *you have her eyes, you know, it must be so hard for you, seeing yourself* —

*The hands are wrong. The scar is missing. That is not my sister.*

Wren had a scar. A thin white crescent on the back of her left hand, between the second and third knuckles, from the summer she was nine and climbed the fence behind the Kowalski place to steal apples and came back bleeding and grinning and holding four green apples against her chest like contraband treasure. Jonah had been there. Jonah had cleaned the cut with tap water in the garage while Wren hissed and swore and dared them to tell Mom. The scar had faded over twenty years but never vanished; Jonah had seen it last spring, at Easter, when Wren passed the potatoes and her sleeve slid back and there it was, the little white moon, faithful as ever.

The body in the casket had smooth hands. Unmarked. Manicured, even — and Wren had never in her life paid anyone to touch her nails. Her nails were bitten short, always, a habit she'd fought since college and lost every time.

And the face — the face had been *close*. That was the worst of it. Close enough that everyone else saw their sister, their niece, their cousin. Close enough that Jonah had leaned over the casket rail expecting the familiar asymmetry — Wren's left eyebrow a fraction higher, a trick of the womb, one twin crowded out slightly by the other before either of them had eyes to see with — and found instead a face like Wren's rendered by someone working from a photograph. Accurate. Flattering. Dead.

Jonah had not cried at the funeral. They had stood at the graveside in a wind that smelled like thawing dirt and felt the ground open beneath certainty itself — that vertiginous drop when the thing you know is true turns out to be the only thing no one else can see. Aunt Corinne had taken Jonah's arm afterward, her grip warm and dry and firm, and said, "You shouldn't have looked so long, sweetheart. It isn't good for the living."

*It isn't good for the living.* As if looking were the danger. As if the dead thing in the box might be disturbed by attention.

Now, in the blue room, Jonah got up. Sleep was not going to come; sleep would be a betrayal, a door left unlocked. There was only one thing Jonah Ravensworth knew how to do when the world stopped making sense, and they had learned it in this very house, at this grandmother's knee, decades ago.

They went downstairs to get their bag.

* * *

Odile Ravensworth had kept journals the way other people kept saints' candles burning — continuously, devoutly, without interruption. Forty-one years of them, Jonah knew, because as a child Jonah had found the shelf in the attic and spent one entire rainbound August reading them by flashlight while Wren kept watch at the attic door, bored and loyal, humming show tunes.

The journals were not diaries. Odile recorded almost nothing of feeling. She recorded *fact*: temperatures, times, visitors, purchases, the exact number of eggs the hens laid, the exact date the first frost came, the exact words spoken at the dinner table on the fourth of March 1981, attributed by speaker. She wrote in a small, upright, unhesitating hand, black ink, no crossings-out. When Jonah was ten and asked her why she wrote everything down, Odile had looked at Jonah for a long moment with those pale eyes — the eyes both twins had inherited, the family's one consistent feature — and said:

"Because memory is a liar, little bird. Memory tells you what it wants. Paper doesn't get to want anything."

Then she had closed the journal on the desk in front of her, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and given Jonah a butterscotch candy from the tin she kept for exactly such moments.

Jonah found their duffel in the corner of the blue room where they'd dropped it twelve hours ago, and dug out the notebook they carried everywhere — a cheap composition book, graph-ruled, half-filled with grocery lists and story fragments — and the phone, and a pen borrowed from the nightstand drawer that said HOLLOWELL FUNERAL HOME, SERVING THE VALLEY SINCE 1953 along its barrel.

They sat cross-legged on the bed, turned to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in letters small enough to be deniable:

**LEDGER OF SMALL DOUBTS.**

Underneath:

**11:47 p.m. — Arrived at conclusion: body in casket is not Wren Ravensworth. Evidence follows.**

And then, because documentation was a kind of prayer, and prayer was the only language Jonah had left, they began.

*1. Scar absent from left hand (Kowalski fence, June 1999). Verified at viewing, 2:10 p.m., present day.*

*2. Nails manicured. Wren has not had a manicure since her wedding-that-didn't-happen, 2019. She called it "surrender."*

*3. Left eyebrow symmetrical with right. Wren's left sits ~2mm higher. Confirmed at every shared birthday for 27 years.*

*4. Hands wrong. Calluses absent. Wren rows. (Or rowed.) Calluses do not posthumously heal.*

*5. Weight appears correct. Height correct. Hair color correct, length correct — grown maybe 4cm since I last saw her in April. Consistent with continued growth after death? Research needed. Or consistent with a body prepared by someone who studied her photographs.*

Jonah's pen slowed on that last line. Writing it made it real in a way thinking it hadn't. Somewhere below, the murmur of the vigil continued, unbroken, unruffled.

They got up and photographed things. This was the modern part of the rite, the part Odile could never have imagined: the phone's camera as a second ledger, indifferent and exact. Jonah photographed the funeral program — *In Loving Memory, Wren Iris Ravensworth, 1996–2025* — and noticed, photographing it, that the dates were right but the middle name was wrong. Wren's middle name was Iris, yes. But the program spelled it *Iris* with a lowercase i tucked inside the capital, a typo, or something else, some small failure of transcription, as if whoever printed it had been copying from a description rather than a record.

Small doubt. Filed.

They photographed the guest book in the front hall, forty signatures, and noted that Uncle Dale's entry was dated three days before the funeral. People sometimes signed early. People sometimes misremembered the date. Small doubt. Filed.

They photographed the casket spray — white lilies, which had been Wren's least favorite flower, a fact Jonah had mentioned to Corinne on the phone and been told, warmly, "Of course, sweetheart, lilies, we'll see to it" — and understood suddenly that Corinne had heard *lilies* and nothing else, had received the instruction like an actor receiving a prop list for a play she already knew by heart.

At 12:31 a.m., Jonah went down to the kitchen for water and found the vigil dispersed. The living room was empty, lamps off, chairs pushed back in. On the dining table, the funeral food sat under cling film like specimens: ham, rolls, a lemon cake with a single slice missing. And beside the cake, a stack of thank-you cards, already addressed, already stamped, the envelopes blank where messages would go but the handwriting on the fronts finished — *Mr. & Mrs. Hollis, The Delgado Family, Dr. Amara Osei* — in Corinne's neat hand.

Thank-you cards. Prepared in advance. For a funeral that had happened today.

People did this. Grieving people, organized people, people who coped by working ahead of the wave. Corinne was organized. Corinne had been executor of three estates before this one. There was an innocent explanation.

Jonah filed it anyway. **12:34 a.m. — Thank-you cards pre-addressed, pre-stamped, pre-funeral(?). Innocent explanation available. Using it anyway feels like lying down in traffic.**

They drank their water standing at the sink, looking out the dark window at the yard, at the swing set that had rotted to gray geometry, at the line of trees beyond the fence where the town ended and the reservoir began. Their reflection hung in the glass, faint, superimposed on the night — a face floating over black trees, watching them back.

"You're being crazy," Jonah told the reflection quietly.

The reflection agreed. The reflection always agreed. That was the whole contract with reflections: they did what you did, when you did it, forever, and asked nothing except that you keep existing to keep them company.

Jonah rinsed the glass, went upstairs, and wrote until nearly two.

* * *

Somewhere past 2 a.m., the entries began to contradict each other.

Jonah noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know — not immediately, but as a wrongness that accumulates until it can't be ignored. They flipped back through the pages they'd filled and found their own handwriting telling two different stories.

At 11:52 p.m., they had written: *Viewing began 2:00 p.m. I arrived at the casket approximately 2:15.*

At 1:38 a.m., they had written: *I looked at her face for the first time at 2:40 p.m. I remember because the church bell struck the half hour just as Corinne touched my arm.*

Both entries were in Jonah's handwriting. Both felt true. Both could not be true — unless Jonah had stood at the casket for twenty-five minutes without noticing, which was possible, grief did strange arithmetic, but Jonah didn't *remember* twenty-five minutes. They remembered minutes. Three, maybe four. Long ones, but countable.

They checked the photos. Timestamp metadata: the program photo, 2:07 p.m. The guest book, 2:19. The flowers, 2:33. All within the earlier window. So the later entry was the false one — except Jonah could *feel* the church bell, could feel Corinne's fingers on their forearm, warm and dry and firm, could feel the specific quality of that moment, the way you can feel a step you took yesterday.

One of these memories was not theirs. Or one of them was not real. Or —

Or the house was doing something to the record. Which was insane. Houses didn't edit notebooks. Houses didn't reach down overnight and rewrite ink.

Jonah sat on the bed with the composition book open on their knees and their heart going hard and shallow, and made themselves write the next line:

**2:14 a.m. — My own notes now disagree with each other. Two memories of the same hour, mutually exclusive. One of them is counterfeit. I cannot tell which. If my record can't be trusted, then what Odile said is worse than she knew: it isn't just memory that lies.**

They underlined it twice. Then, because the alternative to writing was screaming, they kept writing.

*2:20 a.m. — Counted doors on this floor: 7. Counted again: 7. Third time: 8. The eighth door is at the end of the hall, past the linen closet, painted the same color as the wall. In the morning I will check whether it exists. If it does not exist, why do I remember a brass knob cold under my hand?*

*2:26 a.m. — Smell in the blue room: pond water. Faint. Under the paint smell. No pond within a mile. Reservoir is close but downhill; smells don't climb.*

*2:31 a.m. — Sound: dripping. Located it to the bathroom at the end of the hall. Faucet tight, tub dry. Dripping continues. Rate: roughly one per second.*

Jonah got up and went to check the faucet themselves, because writing about a sound was one thing and hearing it was another. The hall was dark, lit only by the nightlight Corinne had plugged in near the stairs — a courtesy, or a marker, marking the route. The dripping grew louder as Jonah approached the bathroom, regular as a metronome, and stopped the instant Jonah crossed the threshold. Tub bone-dry. Faucet dry. Mirror over the sink holding a dim copy of Jonah standing in the doorway, phone raised, face washed gray.

"Okay," Jonah whispered to the mirror. "Okay."

The mirror said nothing, being a mirror.

But standing there, in the silence where the dripping had been, Jonah became aware of another sound, farther off, down at the far end of the house — the hallway clock. Grandfather clock, walnut case, Odile's, older than Odile probably, a tall dark presence at the turn of the stairs that had ticked through Jonah's whole childhood like a second heartbeat belonging to the house itself. Jonah could hear it now through two closed doors: *tick... tock... tick... tock,* slow and enormous, the sound of a building keeping time.

Except.

Jonah stood in the bathroom doorway and counted against it. Not consciously at first — their own pulse, their own breathing, the body's private metronome — and then deliberately, tapping one finger against their thigh, matching the clock's rhythm through the walls.

The clock was slow.

Not broken-slow, not winding-down-slow. Evenly, precisely slow, each tick landing a hair late, a hair behind the beat Jonah's body expected, so that listening to it felt like walking down stairs in the dark and finding one more step than your foot promised. Tick — and then, a fraction of a breath later, the *tock* that should have coincided with it. The whole mechanism running one second behind the world. Behind Jonah's watch. Behind Jonah's pulse. Behind, behind, behind.

Jonah walked out to the landing and stood in front of the clock. Its pendulum swung in the glass case, brass disk flashing, metronomic, serene. Its face read 2:44. Jonah's phone read 2:45.

One second. Maybe two. Jonah held the phone beside the clock face and watched the minute hand and waited for the changeover, counting under their breath, and the clock turned to 2:46 a full second after the phone did — lagging, obedient to some slower law, as if it weren't running late but running *elsewhere*, keeping time for a room adjacent to this one, a version of the house one beat out of phase.

**2:47 a.m.,** Jonah wrote, back on the bed, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages. **Hall clock runs exactly one second slow. Not drifting. Exact. Checked twice. Clocks drift randomly; they don't hold a deficit. Something is holding it back. Or something is holding the rest of us forward.**

Their hand was shaking. They watched it shake and documented that too, because if the record was going to be accused of lying, let it lie in full view, let every tremor be entered into evidence.

*Prayer,* Odile had believed, was the wrong word for it, but it was the word Jonah had. You wrote the world down so the world couldn't gaslight you. You fixed the facts to paper like butterflies to a board, and as long as the paper held, you existed, you were sane, the dead stayed dead and the living stayed knowable. That was the faith. That was the whole cathedral: *ink, timestamps, the neutral eye of the camera.*

And tonight the cathedral had developed cracks. The entries contradicted. The memories came in duplicate, one of them forged. The clock kept a debt of one second and paid it back to no one.

Jonah looked at the last clean page and, before closing the book, wrote the entry that mattered, the one they would build everything on tomorrow, the thesis statement of whatever this was becoming:

**2:53 a.m. — Working theory, stated plainly so I can't pretend later I didn't believe it: Whatever is in that coffin, Wren may not be dead. And whatever is happening to this house — the doors, the drips, the clock, my own doubled memories — it started before I arrived. The family is calm. Calm like rehearsal. Tomorrow I start on the house. Tonight I don't sleep.**

They closed the notebook. Turned off the lamp.

Lay in the dark of the blue room, listening to the hall clock lose its single stolen second, over and over, patiently, forever — *tick... tock* — and it was somewhere in the long shallows toward dawn, drifting despite everything, that Jonah heard the pendulum hesitate.

Just once. Just for the length of a heartbeat.

And in that gap — in the held breath between the tick and the tock — Jonah heard, distinctly, from the direction of the hallway, the sound of footsteps crossing the floor above.

There was no floor above.

Only the attic, where forty-one years of Odile's journals slept in their boxes, and where, Jonah was suddenly, completely certain, one page had been torn out.
