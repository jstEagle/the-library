## 4. The Ones Who Were Invited

The ledger lived in the vestry of St. Brannoc's, chained to a lectern like something that might otherwise wander. Mara had seen it before—had even photographed its cracked leather cover for the archive—but had never been permitted to open it. *Parish business*, Edith would say, in the tone of a woman closing a gate.

Tonight there was no one to close it against. The church was cold and smelled of candle grease and old stone, and Mara stood at the lectern with their phone's torch propped against a hymnal, hands trembling in a way they told themselves was only the chill.

They had come for 1919. That was where the ledger stopped—that was what they'd told themselves all day, pacing the narrow lane between the shop and the church while the excavation crew's floodlights burned behind them at the tithe barn, illuminating nothing anyone wanted to see. The entries stopped in 1919. The noises didn't. Five years of recordings, five years of dates matched against five years of parish minutes, and every scratch beneath the floorboards, every knock in the well shaft, every night the dogs refused to leave their kennels—each one fell on a date the ledger had once marked with a small, careful symbol Mara couldn't find in any heraldic reference: a circle with a line drawn through it, like an eye being closed.

But the ledger hadn't stopped in 1919. That was the first lie Mara had told themselves, and standing here now, they understood it had not been the last.

The final entry was dated the twenty-ninth of September, three years ago. Harvest moon. And beneath the date, in ink so fresh it still seemed wet:

*Invited: M. Yarrow.*

Mara did not move for a long time. The torch beam wavered on the page. Somewhere out in the dark, a rook called once and thought better of it.

*Invited.* Not *arrived*. Not *took residence at the old Hollis cottage*. Invited—as though Mara had answered something. As though some hand had extended itself up through the soil and the slate and the centuries and tapped them on the shoulder, and they had turned around, and said yes.

Their name was not alone on the page. It sat at the bottom of a column that ran backward through the book like a root system, and Mara turned the pages with fingers gone clumsy, following it down. 1987: *Invited: R. Okafor, teacher.* 1961: *Invited: T. Brennan, wheelwright.* 1934: *Invited: A. Kowalczyk.* 1902. 1876. 1849. On and on, back past the point where the handwriting stopped being confident, past the point where it stopped being English, until the vellum went soft and brown as autumn leaves and the names were written in a hand that pressed so hard it had torn through.

Every name an outsider. Every name someone who came to Hollowmere from somewhere else and stayed.

And beside each name, in a later ink, a second notation—the closed-eye symbol—and then nothing. No burial record. No departure. No death registered with the district registrar in Ashcombe, because Mara had spent two days last spring cross-referencing exactly those records for an article they never wrote, and had filed the gaps away as clerical sloppiness. Forty-one people across four centuries who had simply ceased to be accounted for, and no one in Hollowmere had ever once remarked upon it.

No one had ever needed to remark upon it. That was the thing Mara's stomach understood before their mind consent to. You don't remark on the tide going out. You don't remark on winter.

The torch flickered. Mara steadied it against the lectern and became aware, very slowly, of the sensation of being read rather than reading—the sense that the ledger lay open under their hands the way a mouth lies open, and that somewhere below the floor, below the crypt, below the water table, something had turned its attention toward the sound of pages turning.

They photographed everything. Their hands shook so badly the first three images were blurs; the fourth came out clean, and looking at their own name rendered in objective pixels made it worse, not better, because now it existed outside the church, outside the village, outside the story. Now it would follow them home.

Except, said a small cold voice at the base of their skull, this is home. Isn't it? Five years. The good chair by the fire. The way Mrs. Tregowan saved you the end pieces of the seed cake. The way you stopped flinching at the scratching, the way you learned to sleep inside the sound of it, the way you called it *rats* in your field notes with such confidence that you believed yourself—

Had they believed themselves? Or had they simply been kept?

Mara closed the ledger. The chain clinked. Out in the churchyard, the harvest moon was four nights off and already fat and wrong-colored, hanging over the yew like a lamp about to be lit.

* * *

Edith Prowse opened her door before Mara finished knocking, which meant she had been standing behind it, which meant she had known.

"Come in, then," she said quietly. "Kettle's just gone on."

The shop's back room was warm and close and smelled of paraffin and dried lavender, and Edith moved through it with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done every chore in it ten thousand times. She poured tea without asking how Mara took it, because after five years she knew, and that knowledge—which should have been a comfort—landed now like evidence.

"You've been in the vestry," Edith said. She sat down across the little table and folded her hands, and Mara saw for the first time how old her hands were, how the knuckles had gone wide and knotted like the roots of the yew. "I could tell you I'd rather you hadn't."

"Then say it."

"I'd rather you hadn't." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gentle and defeated. "There. Said. Doesn't change anything, does it."

"Forty-one names." Mara set their phone on the table between them, screen up, the photograph glowing. "Going back to before the parish registers were in English. All of them invited. All of them—" They stopped. Swallowed. "All of them unaccounted for. And mine. Three years ago. *Mine*, Edith. Before I ever came here. Before I ever answered that advertisement for a folklore survey. Before—" Their voice cracked and they hated it. "I chose Hollowmere. I researched it. I applied for the grant. I found it on a map."

"No," Edith said, without cruelty. "You didn't."

She rose, took down a biscuit tin from the shelf—of course, the biscuit tin, the most innocent object in England—and lifted out a folded paper gone soft with handling. She laid it flat and smoothed it with both palms, twice, like a woman making a bed for someone about to die in it.

It was a letter. Old-fashioned copperplate, undated, unsigned. Mara read it and felt the floor tilt gently beneath the room.

*To the keeper of the shop: when the moon ripens, the surveyor will come. Make ready the Hollis cottage. See that the roads are spoken of as flooded. Let them find the song.*

"The advertisement," Mara whispered. "The grant application. The—the whole survey, the reason I came—"

"I posted it," Edith said. "Well. I wrote it out fair, and Mr. Tregowan drove it to Ashcombe, and we put it where it wanted putting. Same as my mother did for the Okafor man. Same as her mother before her. It's not forgery, love, don't look at me like that—it's *bait*, and there's a difference, though I'll grant you it's a fine one." She refilled Mara's untouched tea, though neither of them would drink it. "You think a village like this survives four hundred winters on its own cleverness? We bargain. We've always bargained. And the bargain wants newcomers—not dead ones, it's particular, it wants the *living* ones, the ones with no roots, the ones nobody's missing. It chooses them. Has done since before the tithe barn had a roof. We just... hold the pen."

"It chooses," Mara repeated. The words came out flat, stripped of inflection, because if she put anything into them they would shatter. "Something under the village chooses who lives here."

"Aye."

"And I was chosen."

"You were chosen." Edith's eyes were wet and steady. "Three years ago, when the moon ripened, it reached out, and it found you—you, specifically, Mara Yarrow, sitting alone in a bedsit in a city full of eight million people, and it *wanted* you. Do you know what that's like, from our side? Watching it happen? Knowing that whoever it finds will be kind, and lonely, and easy to love, because that's what it goes for? I baked you a welcome loaf the day you arrived and I cried into the dough, and you ate it and said it was the best bread you'd had in years, and I have never forgiven myself for how pleased that made me."

Mara looked at their hands. Strangers' hands, suddenly. Hands that had unpacked books into shelves chosen by something else. Had slept in a bed positioned by something else. Had walked lanes laid out by something else, humming songs taught to them by something else, for five years—or three, or however long the reaching had taken, because God, God, maybe the loneliness itself had been arranged, maybe the bedsit, maybe the city, maybe the whole shape of the life that had made them reachable—

"The others," Mara said. "The ones in the ledger. What happened to them?"

Edith was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the hedges with a dry whispering, like pages turning.

"They stayed," she said finally. "That's what I can tell you true. They stayed. Longer than the rest of us, some of them." She met Mara's eyes. "The moon's in four nights. The door's open under the barn for the first time since my grandmother's grandmother, and whatever sealed it from the inside has been waiting for company. You're not the only one it's called, love. There's the gravedigger. And the chef at the Folly. And that young man up at the mast with his radios. It's been hungry a long while, and it's invited generously this year."

Mara stood. Their legs held, which surprised them.

"Why are you telling me this?" they asked. "Why warn me? Why not just let it—"

"Because my mother told me the same thing she told me about the song." Edith folded the letter away, back into the tin, back into the dark. "She said: the thing below does the choosing, but it doesn't do the *claiming*. Claiming's got to be given freely, or sung. And it's never yet managed to make one of them sing."

At the door, Mara paused. The night air came in cold and mineral, smelling of turned earth, of the barn, of the deep.

"Edith. The ones it invited this year. Does it always invite more than one?"

"No," Edith said. "Never has before."

Down the lane, in the direction of the radio mast, a light was burning that Mara had never seen burning before—steady, urgent, sweeping the fields in slow arcs, as though someone up there were searching the dark for something.

Or signaling it.
