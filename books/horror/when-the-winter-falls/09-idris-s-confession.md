## 9. Idris's Confession

Idris Vane had spent sixty years perfecting the art of not looking at the Sandoval house.

It was a discipline, like any other. He'd learned it young, the way other boys learned to whistle or spit or swim in the quarry pond. You did not look at the house. You did not count its windows — because the number changed, and counting was how it got its hooks into you. You did not walk past it after dark. And you never, ever sat on the fence at the bottom of its drive, no matter how good the fence was for sitting.

He was eighty-one years old now, and he had broken every one of his own rules this week.

The girl — Sandoval, she said her name was, though the name had a strange thin quality when she said it, like a coin worn smooth — had come to his gate on Tuesday asking about the property line. On Wednesday she'd asked about the town records. On Thursday she'd asked him, quite casually, whether he'd ever noticed anything odd about the photographs inside.

And Idris Vane, who had kept his mouth shut through two marriages, one divorce, four decades of postal service, and the entire slow collapse of Hollowmere's main street, had heard himself say: "Come in for tea. There's something you ought to know."

He'd regretted it before the kettle finished boiling.

* * *

His kitchen smelled of toast and pipe smoke and the particular staleness of a house lived in alone too long. He set out two cups — the good ones, the ones with the painted wrens on them, which was either a coincidence or sixty years of unconscious devotion, and he preferred not to examine which.

The girl sat across from him with her hands wrapped around her cup like she was trying to absorb the heat directly into her bloodstream. She looked tired in a way that sleep didn't fix. He recognized it. It was the look of someone whose explanations had all run out and who hadn't yet admitted it to herself.

"Vane," she said. "That's an old Hollowmere name."

"Older than me, which is saying something." He lowered himself into his chair with the care of a man negotiating with his own knees. "My grandfather built fences for half the county. Including, as it happens, the fence around your house."

"You've been watching it a long time."

"I've been *not* watching it a long time," Idris corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is most of my life."

She almost smiled. She had her great-aunt's way of almost smiling. That was the trouble. That was the whole trouble, sitting right there in his kitchen drinking his tea.

"Wren," he said. "Your grandmother's sister. Wren Sandoval."

The girl went very still. "You knew her?"

"I was going to marry her."

The words came out easier than he'd expected, after sixty years in the dark. Like a drawer that sticks and then opens all at once, spilling everything onto the floor.

* * *

Her name had been Wren because her mother said she sang like one, though Idris had known her for eleven months before he heard her sing a single note, and then only once, walking home from the Grange dance, some foolish song about a sailor's wife, half-whispered against the cold so their breath made little clouds that seemed to keep time.

She was twenty-two. He was twenty-one and certain about everything, which is the defining condition of being twenty-one. He worked at the feed store and saved for a ring and thought the hardest thing in his future would be convincing her father he could provide.

"She used to talk about the house," Idris said. "Not the way you'd expect. Not frightened. More like — resentful. The way you'd resent a chore. She salted the thresholds every Sunday and counted the chairs every night, and she talked about it the way you'd talk about mucking out a barn. Just work. Family work."

"Counting chairs," the girl repeated softly. Not a question. Which told him everything about what she'd already seen.

"There were supposed to be twelve. Dining room, twelve chairs, one for each place setting her grandmother insisted on even though nobody had eaten in that dining room since the war." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. Old habit. Old superstition. "Some nights there were thirteen."

"And the thirteenth—"

"Was closer to the head of the table than it should have been. Every year. A few inches at a time. She measured once, with her father's carpenter's rule. Said if it kept coming, it would reach the table by—" He stopped. Drank tea he didn't taste.

"By when?"

"By 1963. Which is when she decided to stop waiting for it."

* * *

Here was the thing Idris had never told anyone, not the priest, not his first wife, not the man who'd shared his foxhole in Korea and heard everything else: he had known what Wren planned to do, and he had let her do it.

Not let. Worse than let. He'd helped carry the books down from the attic.

"It was November," he said. "Cold enough that your breath crackled. She'd found the founders' ledger — up in the attic, behind a false panel her grandfather put in. Pages and pages of it. Instructions. A binding ritual, started in 1847 by the people who built the house, and left unfinished on purpose. Half a cage." He looked at the window, where the light was going gray and blue the way it did at three o'clock in a Hollowmere winter. "They figured half a job was better than none, and they'd let their children finish it when they were ready. Except nobody was ever ready. Generations just kept salting and counting and not finishing, and the thing under the house kept getting fed anyway, little meals, a name here, a memory there. Your family, mostly. It ate them slow."

The girl's face had gone white, but she didn't interrupt. She had Wren's stubbornness too. He was beginning to understand why the house had noticed her.

"Wren read that ledger cover to cover. Twice. And she came to me and said, 'Idris, somebody has to finish it, and I'm the one who knows the words.' And I said—" His voice caught on the old splinter of it. "I said, 'Then I'll help you carry the books.'"

"You couldn't have stopped her."

"I could have tried." He set the cup down harder than he meant to. "That's the arithmetic I've done for sixty years, miss. I could have tried. I could have burned the ledger. I could have carried her off to Buffalo and married her in a courthouse and let the house sit hungry and half-bound until the sun burned out. Instead I held the lantern while she read the words at the hearth. Held it steady, because my hands were steadier than hers. That's what I contributed. Steady hands and a coward's silence."

"What happened?"

"The solstice. Midnight. She spoke the founders' words — the whole passage, start to finish, exactly as written, because she trusted the book more than her own instincts, and why wouldn't she, she was twenty-two and the book was a hundred years old and written by people who built a whole town." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and cracked. "Except the book was written by people who *left it unfinished*. Who maybe wanted it finished. Who maybe weren't writing instructions at all. Maybe they were writing bait."

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere outside a crow said something rude and flapped off.

"She got halfway through," Idris said. "Halfway through the words, standing at the hearth, and the fire went green — not bright, mind, just green, like pond ice — and the chair came. Out of nowhere. Right beside her. And she looked at me—" 

He had to stop. Sixty years, and he still had to stop.

"She looked at me and she smiled. That same almost-smile you've got. And she said, 'Idris, hold the lantern,' and I held the lantern, and the chair took her. No violence. Nothing dramatic. One moment she was standing there reading, and the next she was sitting, and then she wasn't anywhere at all, and the fire was ordinary again, and the house was quiet in a way I'd never heard it be quiet. Satisfied quiet."

"Did you go in after her?"

"I ran to the door and it wouldn't open. Then it opened, eventually, hours later, and I went in with a constable and a search party and we found nothing. No Wren. No chair. No ledger — the pages from the final step were torn out, every one, neat as you please. The house had tidied up after itself." He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "They said she ran off. Cold feet. Ran off to the city. Her father believed it because believing it hurt less. Her mother half-believed it and died not knowing. And I—" 

"And you?" the girl prompted gently.

"I stood at that fence every night for a year like an idiot, waiting for her to come home. And then I stopped looking at the house, because I understood that looking was a door, and I'd already lost one person through it." He straightened his shoulders. "But here's the part that matters, and why I asked you here. Before she went to the hearth, Wren gave me her notebook. Her own notes. Corrections she'd made to the founders' text — things she'd noticed, things that didn't add up. She said, 'If it goes wrong, give these to whoever comes next.' And I didn't. For sixty years I kept them in a tin box under my floorboards like a coward, because giving them away meant admitting she was gone, and admitting the whole thing was real, and I—" He gestured vaguely at himself, at the kitchen, at the whole sagging architecture of his long careful life. "I chose the comfortable lie. Married a nice woman from Ashford. Sold insurance. Watched the house sideways for six decades and told myself the girl who took my heart down into that attic had simply run off to the city."

The girl was crying now, quietly, without drama, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like tears were an administrative inconvenience.

"I'm sorry," Idris said. "I'm sorry I waited. If I'd given those notes to your grandmother—"

"My grandmother counted chairs every night for fifty years," Freya said. "She salted thresholds. She told me not to finish it and never said what *it* was. If she'd had those notes..." She shook her head. "Maybe it wouldn't have mattered. But she'd have known she wasn't mad."

"No," Idris agreed. "She'd have known she was right, which is worse, but at least it's true."

* * *

He fetched the tin box from under the floorboard beneath the loose plank by the pantry — a floorboard he'd walked over ten thousand times without opening, the way you can live above a grave and call it a cellar. The box was rusted at the corners. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a notebook with a wren embossed on the cover, because Wren Sandoval had never met an irony she didn't enjoy.

He set it on the table between them and did not push it across. He found he couldn't. His hands stayed flat on either side of it, an old man bracketing the last piece of a girl who'd been dead or worse since before this child's parents were born.

"This is hers," he said. "Everything she figured out. What the ritual actually does, where the founders lied, what the chair is and who sits in it. She was smarter than the whole lot of them — smarter than me, certainly, God knows." He swallowed. "I should have given it to your grandmother. I should have given it to anyone. Instead I sat on it while your whole family got eaten a spoonful at a time, because opening that box meant Wren was really gone."

Freya reached out and touched the notebook's cover, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"Thank you," she said. "For keeping it safe. Even if it was for the wrong reasons."

"There are no right reasons left, miss. Only late ones." He finally slid it across the wood, and felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since Eisenhower. "Take it. Read every word. And then come back and tell me what she knew, because I've wondered for sixty years and I find I'm too old to wonder anymore."

She stood to leave, tucking the notebook inside her coat like something alive that needed warmth. At the door she paused.

"Mr. Vane — the notes. Did she figure out how it ends? How it's supposed to end?"

Idris looked at her, at Wren's almost-smile wearing a younger face, and gave her the only honest answer he had.

"She figured out how it ends," he said. "That's exactly what frightens me. Because the last page of her notebook isn't instructions, miss. It's a warning, underlined twice, in her handwriting: *'The trap isn't failing to finish it. The trap is wanting to.'*"
