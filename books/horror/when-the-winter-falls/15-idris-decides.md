## 15. Idris Decides

Idris Vane had spent sixty years learning the exact weight of a guilty conscience, and he could tell you this much about it: it does not lighten. It only gets better organized.

His lived in a small wooden box on the top shelf of his wardrobe, beneath a sweater he never wore because Wren had knitted it and the sleeves were too long and he could not bear to shorten them or to wear them dragging over his hands like a boy wearing his father's coat. The box held her notes. Her handwriting, quick and slanted, full of arrows and crossed-out words, as though she were always in a hurry to get to the truth before it got away from her.

He had taken them out three times since Freya Sandoval arrived in Hollowmere. The first time just to confirm they still existed — that was the kind of man he'd become, checking his guilt for damp and rot like a farmer checks his seed potatoes. The second time to read them properly, all the way through, which had cost him a night's sleep and most of his composure. And the third time was tonight, because tonight he had made up his mind, and a decision like that deserved to be made holding something of hers.

The kettle muttered on the stove. Outside, the frost had climbed the kitchen window in its careful rings, one more than yesterday, and Idris had watched it close another circle with the calm of a man reading a train timetable. Two days until the solstice. He'd done the arithmetic on his fingers twice, the way you do when you want the answer to change and know it won't.

Freya would come by in the morning. She always did now — a knock at the fence, then the gate, then the kitchen, trailing cold air and that stubborn surveyor's practicality she wore like armor. She'd sit where Wren used to sit, at the near end of the table, and she'd drink her tea too hot and pretend not to notice him watching her face for echoes of a girl dead sixty years.

And tomorrow morning, Idris decided, he was going to tell her what he intended to do about it.

* * *

She came early, which surprised him, and came through the gate without knocking, which surprised him more, and stood in his kitchen looking like a woman who had slept in her coat, which surprised him not at all.

"You look terrible," he said, and poured her tea before she could argue.

"Thank you. You're very kind." She wrapped both hands around the cup. "I couldn't stay in the house. It's started rearranging itself while I'm *in* rooms. I went up to bed last night and the landing was four feet longer than it's been all week. I measured it." A short, unsteady laugh. "I measured it, Idris. At midnight. With a tape measure. Like that was going to help."

"What did it say?"

"Four feet." She stared into her tea. "It said four feet."

"Well," Idris said, "at least it's honest about its length."

That earned him half a smile, which was the whole point. He'd learned this from Wren: when the fear gets big, you make it small enough to hold. Joke at it. Name it. A thing with a name is a thing you can argue with.

They sat a while. The frost on the window ticked as it grew. Freya told him about the ledger diagrams appearing in the glass, ring by ring, patient as embroidery. She told it plainly, without drama, the way she reported everything — square footage, water damage, an entity counting down to solstice — and Idris thought, not for the first time, that the house had picked the wrong woman if it expected her to come apart quietly.

Which was exactly why what he had to say next was going to be so difficult.

"Freya," he said. "I need to tell you something, and I'd take it kindly if you'd let me get to the end of it before you start arguing."

Her eyes came up sharp. "That sounds ominous."

"It is. Ominous is my middle name. Edith Vane chose it herself." He set down his cup so his hands would have something official to do. "I've been thinking about Wren's notes. About the ritual. About what it needs."

"A willing heir," Freya said. "I know. I've read it forty times. An heir has to speak the words and then sit in the chair, and if the binding's half-done it feeds instead—"

"And you're the only heir left," Idris said. "On paper. In blood. According to the old rules." He took a breath. "But here's the thing about rules written in 1847 by frightened men who couldn't spell 'conscience' — they assume everyone playing wants to win for themselves. They don't account for a person choosing to lose *on purpose*. For someone walking into that chair who isn't an heir at all. Who isn't feeding it anything it can use. Just... sitting down. Refusing to move."

Freya had gone very still. He pressed on before his nerve failed, the way he should have pressed on in 1963.

"I'll do it," he said. "I'll speak the words. I'll sit in the chair. I owe this house a debt sixty years overdue, and I'd rather pay it standing — well, sitting — than watch it collect from you."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Idris counted the ticks of the frost out of pure habit. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then Freya said, "No."

"I haven't finished explaining—"

"No." She set her cup down with a click that meant business; he'd heard that same click from her grandmother, thirty years ago, refusing a developer's offer on the orchard. "You're eighty-one years old. You have a heart that your doctor — you told me, Idris, you told me yourself — describes as 'a coin flip with a stethoscope.' You want to walk into a house that folds hallways like origami and sit down in front of a creature that eats souls, and your plan is *stubbornness*?"

"My plan is love, actually," Idris said, with some dignity. "Stubbornness is merely the delivery method."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. That's the terrible part." He leaned forward. "Wren sat in that chair because the notes told her an heir must. Because duty told her to. She went up those stairs in 1963 believing it was her sentence to serve, and I let her go, because I believed it too. I stood at the bottom of the stairs like a coward and let duty do my loving for me. Sixty years, Freya. Sixty years I've known exactly what I should have done, and every year it's gotten heavier, not lighter. This isn't sacrifice. This is the first morning in six decades I've woken up knowing how to fix something."

"That's guilt talking, not sense."

"Of course it's guilt talking. Guilt is the only part of me that's kept current on this situation." He spread his hands. "But think it through, since you're the professional. The ritual wants a Sandoval. It wants bloodline, inheritance, the slow siphon of a family being forgotten. What happens if it opens the door expecting an heir and finds *me*? A Vane. No claim on the house, no debt in the ledger, nothing it can count. I'm not a payment. I'm a wrong number."

For a moment — he saw it happen — she actually considered it. He watched the surveyor in her run the numbers, check the load-bearing walls of the argument. And then her face closed, and he knew he'd lost, and knew why.

"If you're wrong," she said quietly, "it takes you anyway. And I will have traded the one person in this town who remembers my name for a theory." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "Do you understand what you're proposing? Everyone else looks *through* me now. The clerk, the shopkeeper, my own bank. You are the single thread, Idris. If the Tallyman takes you, there is no one left holding the other end."

"There's you."

"There won't be a *me*," she said. "Not one anyone can find. That's the whole horror of it. I'll be a woman standing in a town that's never heard of her, in a house that eats names, and the only proof I ever existed will be an old man at a fence — and you want to hand that old man to the thing in the cellar as a *goodwill gesture*."

Idris sat back. Outside, the light was doing that thin December thing, silver and no warmth in it, and the frost rings on the window had completed their outermost circle sometime in the night. He looked at her — this exhausted, furious, brave young woman with tea going cold in her hands — and felt the old ache shift into something new. Not lighter. Better organized.

"All right," he said. "Then tell me what you see, when you imagine the solstice. Honestly. Both endings."

She was quiet a moment. "If I go," she said finally, "I finish it my way. Grandmother told me — *don't finish his sentence, write your own.* I think there's a way to bind it that isn't a feeding. I think the ritual's been misread for a hundred and seventy years. But I might be wrong, and if I'm wrong, it takes me, and the house goes back to waiting for the next Sandoval, and there aren't any, so it just... waits. Hungry. Forever."

"And if I go?"

"If you go and you're right, I live, and I spend whatever's left of my life knowing I let an old man die for me because I was too much of a coward to argue hard enough." She met his eyes. "If you go and you're wrong, we both lose everything. There's no version where your plan ends well, Idris. There's just versions where you feel cleansed first."

"Cleansed," Idris repeated. He almost laughed. "Is that what you think this is? A bath?"

"What else would you call it?"

"I'd call it *finishing*," he said. "There's a difference. A bath washes things away. Finishing is when you carry something the whole distance." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "You weren't there in '63. You don't know what it is to stand in a hallway and hear the house take someone and do nothing. Every night since, I've lain in bed and run that hallway again, and every night I stop at the same stair. So yes — part of this is guilt. But guilt, Freya, is just love with nowhere to go. For sixty years mine has had nowhere to go. Let it go somewhere."

"*No*," she said again, but softer now, and he heard what was underneath it, and it undid him a little: it wasn't anger. It was terror. She was terrified of losing him, this creaking old neighbor she'd known for three weeks, because he was the last hand holding hers above the water. "Please. I can't — Idris, I can't do this alone."

And there it was. The thing the ritual didn't account for. The thing the founders, in all their careful frightened arithmetic, had left off the ledger entirely.

Idris reached across the table and put his hand over hers, papery and warm.

"Child," he said gently, "you've had it backwards this whole time. You think refusing my help is protecting me. But listen to what you just said. *I can't do this alone.* Do you know what that is? That's not a burden. That's the first honest sentence anyone's spoken in that house since 1847." He squeezed her fingers. "The Tallyman counts. That's all it knows how to do — count what's owed, count who's missing, count the chairs. It understands debts and duties and heirs and sentences. It has *no idea* what to do with two people who simply refuse to let each other go. Wren went alone because we all believed alone was the price. Nobody ever tried it *together*."

Freya blinked at him. Something moved behind her eyes — the surveyor again, but working on a different building now.

"Together," she repeated.

"You talk to it at the hearth, you write your own sentence, and I stand behind you — not as a sacrifice, not as a substitute, just as a witness. Somebody whose job is to remember. That's all I've ever been good for, apparently. Remembering." His voice roughened and he let it. "You want to protect me? Then don't send me away to be safe. Keep me where the remembering does the most good."

For a long moment she didn't speak. The frost ticked. Somewhere in the walls of his old house, a pipe knocked twice, the way pipes do, or the way houses do when they're listening.

Then Freya turned her hand over and gripped his, hard, and said, "If you're at the bottom of those stairs on solstice night, Idris Vane, and anything comes down them that isn't me — you run. You run and you don't look back and you don't open that door again, no matter what you hear calling you. Promise me."

"That's a cruel promise to ask of an old man."

"It's the only one I'll accept."

He looked at her a long time — at the frost-ringed window behind her, at the two days left on the calendar of ice — and gave her the truth, because she'd earned it and because lying to her now would be one debt too many.

"Then I promise," he said. "But hear me, Freya Sandoval. Whatever you write at that hearth — write it knowing somebody's holding the other end of your name. That's not a detail. That may be the whole trick."

She nodded, and finished her cold tea in one swallow like medicine, and stood to go — and stopped, halfway to the door, as the light in the kitchen dimmed though no cloud had crossed the sun.

From the direction of the Sandoval house, faint but unmistakable across the frozen fields, came the sound of a chair scraping against a floor.

Moving closer.

Both of them heard it. Neither of them said a word. But Idris saw Freya straighten her shoulders the way soldiers do, and he thought: *two days*, and reached for the teapot, because whatever was coming, they would meet it warm.
