## 16. Solstice Eve

The last ring closed at dawn.

Freya knew it the way you know a song has ended — not by seeing the final note but by the silence after it. She had been sleeping in the kitchen for three nights now, wrapped in her grandmother's quilt on the old daybed beneath the window, because the bedroom upstairs had begun to feel like a throat. When she woke and looked at the frost on the glass, the pattern was complete: concentric rings of geometric lace, each one matching a diagram in the founders' ledger, each one a little more finished than the last.

Twenty-one rings. The twenty-second had filled itself in overnight, its final arc drawn in feathered white across the corner of the pane like a signature.

"Right," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the house, to whatever was listening. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which she supposed was the whole trick of being a Sandoval. "I see it. You don't have to be subtle anymore."

The kettle, sitting cold on the stove, ticked once — though there was no fire under it.

She made tea anyway. There was something about the ritual of it that kept her anchored: the weight of the kettle, the bloom of steam, the small domestic defiance of drinking chamomile while an ancient hunger counted down to midnight beneath your feet. Her grandmother had always said that a person who could still make tea was a person who hadn't lost yet. Freya suspected Edith Sandoval had said this specifically so that Freya would remember it someday, standing in exactly this kitchen, exactly this afraid.

Grandmothers were sneaky like that. It turned out love left instructions everywhere, if you knew how to read them.

* * *

By nine o'clock the house began to move.

Not dramatically — nothing so honest as walls groaning or floors tilting. It moved the way water moves, the way a dream moves, so that you only noticed afterward. Freya went upstairs to fetch Wren's notebook from the nightstand in what should have been the front bedroom, and found herself descending a staircase she didn't climb. She stood in the parlor holding a book she'd gone up to get, with no memory of the journey between, and the parlor's two windows now faced north when they had always faced east.

"All right," she said again. "That's new."

She tried walking deliberately toward the front door, counting her steps the way her grandmother had taught her — *count everything, Freya-love; what counts you can't cheat you* — and at step eleven she was in the dining room. At step nineteen, the hallway. At step thirty-one, she stood at the mouth of the cellar stairs, looking down into the dark where the original bargain-seal waited, and understood that the house wasn't trapping her.

It was *escorting* her.

Every corridor folded toward the hearth. Every hallway bent toward the cellar. The house was a funnel now, and she was the last grain of sand, and midnight was the hourglass turning over.

Fine. She'd known this was coming. Wren's notes had been clear enough: *On solstice eve the house will bring you where it wants you. Don't fight the walls. Fight the words.*

So Freya stopped fighting the walls. She let the house carry her — through rooms that rearranged themselves politely around her footsteps, past doors that opened onto other doors, along a corridor that was somehow both the upstairs landing and the garden path — until she arrived, unsurprised, in the great room where the hearth stood.

It was enormous. She'd measured it weeks ago, back when measurements still meant anything: seven feet of blackened fieldstone, a mantel carved with wheat sheaves, a flue deep enough to stand in. Now it seemed larger, as if the room had been built around a fireplace that had been built around a mouth.

The extra chair sat before it.

It had come downstairs sometime in the night. It stood on the hearthstone now, plain pine, no ornament, its seat dusted with frost despite the fire Freya hadn't lit yet. The handprint was still there — long fingers, splayed wide, pressed into the rime as if someone had gripped the chair from below, or as if the chair itself were reaching up through its own wood toward a hand that hadn't arrived yet.

"Don't flatter yourself," Freya told it. "I'm not sitting down yet."

The frost on the seat did not melt. But she thought — she chose to think — that the handprint curled slightly, like fingers drumming.

* * *

She spent the afternoon preparing, because preparation was the one language she spoke fluently. Surveyor's habit: measure twice, cut once, know where every load-bearing wall stands before you swing the hammer.

She laid out her materials on the dining table — which had obligingly relocated next to the hearth, the house apparently eager to help — and arranged them in the order she would need them.

The ledger first. Its leather cover was soft as skin now, and it fell open to the ritual pages without being asked, which she tried not to find creepy and failed. The founders' words ran down the page in Elias Sandoval's careful hand, written in 1847 in ink gone brown as old blood:

*What lies beneath, we name and bind.*
*What hungers, we feed by count.*
*A soul for the household, given freely—*

And there the sentence broke off, mid-word, because the final pages were torn out. A binding left deliberately unfinished. A door held shut with one nail instead of driven home. For a hundred and seventy years, every generation had been quietly eaten because nobody wanted to drive the nail.

Wren's notebook lay beside it, its pages soft with age and pencil-smudge. Wren had gotten further than anyone. In 1963 she had stood at this very hearth, Idris said, and spoken half the words before the house took her — taken her *mid-ritual*, which meant the binding failed, which meant the thing below got fed anyway and stayed hungry. Wren's corrections crowded the margins in her sharp, impatient hand:

*The words are a cage but the ledger's version is a leash. It wants obedience. Give it none.*

*Do not offer. CLAIM. There is a difference and the difference is everything.*

*If you falter, it will finish your sentence FOR you. This is the trap. It has been waiting 116 years for someone to say the last line out loud.*

And at the bottom of the final page, in letters pressed so hard the pencil had torn through:

*Don't finish his sentence. Write your own.*

Grandmother's words, too. They'd both said it. Two women, sixty years apart, leaving the same note for the same girl. Freya touched the torn place in the ledger where the ending used to be and felt something that wasn't quite fear. It was closer to anger, and anger was fuel, and she intended to burn every degree of it by midnight.

* * *

Idris came at four, letting himself through the garden gate with the ease of sixty years' habit. He carried a covered dish, which struck Freya as so profoundly, absurdly human that she nearly laughed for the first time in days.

"You're feeding me," she said. "On solstice eve."

"A person facing down a hungry house needs a hot meal." He set the dish on the table — shepherd's pie, still steaming, smelling of thyme and onion and ordinary life. "Wren always said the same. She'd laugh at me for bringing food to a haunting, and then she'd eat every bite." His voice caught on Wren's name, the way it always did, a stone in a stream he'd learned to talk over. "You eat. Then we go over it one more time."

They went over it one more time. The words from the ledger, which Freya had memorized and then deliberately garbled in her memory, learning them wrong on purpose so that if the house reached into her mind and pulled them out, it would pull out nonsense. The corrections from Wren's margins. The plan — such as it was. Speak the founders' opening. Refuse the offering. Claim the house in her own words. Reach the cellar. Break the seal. Rebind.

"And if any part of it goes wrong?" Idris asked quietly.

"Then I'll improvise. I'm good at improvising. I once talked a farmer out of evicting his own barn owls."

"That's not the same scale of problem."

"No," Freya agreed. "But it's the same skill. You find out what the other party actually wants, and you offer them something better." She looked at the hearth, at the chair, at the frost that crept along the floorboards in thin geometric veins. "It wants to be fed. What it actually wants — underneath, the way the seal showed me — is to be *known*. Correctly. It's been misremembered for a hundred and seventy years. Fed by bad arithmetic. Nobody's ever told it the truth about what it is."

"And you think the truth will hold it?"

"I think," Freya said, "that a story is stronger than a hunger. Hunger eats itself empty. A story gets retold." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, rusty. "Besides. I'm a surveyor. My entire job is describing things accurately enough that other people agree. If anyone can appraise that thing honestly, it's me."

Idris laughed — a short, startled bark — and then pressed his knuckles to his mouth, and Freya realized he was crying, quietly, the way old men cry, as if embarrassed by the plumbing.

"Wren said almost those exact words," he managed. "*Someone has to measure it fairly.* And then she walked into the house and I never—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm coming down with you. To the cellar. I know you said—"

"You're staying above ground," Freya said gently. "That's not negotiable, and it's not because I don't want you. It's because the ritual needs a witness who remembers. Whatever happens tonight, someone has to walk out of here carrying the true version. That's you. That's always been you — you've held onto Wren for sixty years when the whole world let her go. You're the best keeper of names I've ever met."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did at solstice, the sun giving up by half past three.

"When this is done," he said, "you'll tell me about the owls."

"It's a riveting story. There's a ladder involved."

* * *

At eleven, she bathed and dressed in clean clothes, because her grandmother had insisted that one never faced a threshold unkempt — *you present yourself, Freya-love; you are not presented*. She braided her hair the way Edith used to braid it, tight and practical, and put on the wool socks with the reindeer on them, because dignity was important but so were warm feet, and she refused to meet an ancient entity with cold toes.

At eleven-thirty, she stood before the unlit hearth with the ledger in one hand and Wren's notebook in the other, and the house gathered itself around her like a held breath.

The frost patterns on every window had begun to move — the completed rings rotating slowly, all together, like the face of a clock whose hour was almost come. The chair waited on the hearthstone. The handprint in its frost had spread, five finger-trails running down over the edge of the seat, as if the hand that made it was climbing.

Freya set the books on the mantel. She took a match from her pocket — an actual wooden match, because some rituals deserved analog equipment — and struck it against the stone.

The fire caught low and blue, then rose orange, and the shadows of the room leaned in around it.

Beneath her feet, so far down that she felt it in her teeth rather than heard it, something stirred. Something that had counted twenty-two winters' worth of rings. Something that had eaten a family name off the face of the earth, one memory at a time, patient as frost.

The clock in the hall — a clock she was certain hadn't been there an hour ago — began to strike twelve.

Freya Sandoval, who had maybe forty minutes left of being called that by anyone but herself, stepped forward to the hearth, looked directly into the dark of the flue, and began to speak.

"What lies beneath," she said, "we name—"
