## 21. Morning Without Her

I woke up in my car with my cheek against the steering wheel and a crick in my neck that felt like a moral judgment.

The first thing I noticed was the quiet. Not the Hollowmere quiet I'd spent three weeks learning to hate—the held-breath quiet of a village pretending to sleep—but an honest quiet. The kind a place earns. Birdsong, distant and stupid and cheerful. A dog barking twice somewhere and then thinking better of it. Wind moving through the churchyard grass without anyone conducting it.

The second thing I noticed was that my throat hurt like I'd swallowed gravel and sung about it.

I sat up. My hands were gray with dust—fine, pale dust, the color of old bronze—and there was blood on my lower lip, dried in a crescent, though when I touched it there was no cut underneath to explain it. My voice, when I tried it out, came out in two registers at once, one of them mine.

"Okay," I said to the windshield. "Okay."

Outside, the morning was aggressively ordinary. Low sun. Frost on the grass going translucent as the light found it. And across the lane, where St. Brannoc's had stood leaning into its own silence for a hundred years, there was now a heap of rubble wearing the church's shape like a coat two sizes too big. The tower had come down whole, folded inward, and the bell—

I got out of the car to look. I don't know why. Some part of me needed to see it the way you need to touch a bruise.

The bell lay in the wreckage in pieces, cracked down the middle like a walnut, its metal gone dull and ordinary. It looked like what it had always claimed not to be: an object. A thing someone poured and cooled and hung. There was no hum coming off it. No warmth. When I crouched and put my palm flat against the largest fragment, it was cold as a headstone and just as communicative.

Something about that made me want to cry, and I couldn't figure out why, which made me want to cry more, and I stood there in the churchyard frost being sad about nothing in particular until a voice behind me said:

"You shouldn't be standing in there, love. That's no place for feet."

* * *

Mrs. Tarnow looked smaller than I remembered her, which was strange, because I'd only met her—when? Three weeks ago? She was the gravedigger, I knew that much. Knew it the way you know things from a pamphlet. She stood at the churchyard gate in a cardigan the color of wet bark, squinting at me with the polite blankness of a woman trying to place a face she has no reason to know.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

And here is the thing I want to be honest about, because honesty is all I've got left and even that's secondhand: some part of me had been waiting for this question since I opened my eyes. Some part of me heard it coming through the frost and the birdsong like a train through a tunnel. But knowing a thing is coming doesn't stop it from arriving.

"No," I said. "I don't think so. I'm passing through. Car trouble." I gestured vaguely at my car, which had no visible trouble, and she nodded with the easy credulity of small towns everywhere.

"Passing through," she repeated, tasting it. "Nobody passes through Hollowmere. Nothing here but the church—" she glanced at the rubble and frowned, a small crease of confusion crossing her face like weather, "—well. What was the church. Funny. I keep thinking there was something I meant to do today."

"There isn't," I said, before I could stop myself.

She looked at me for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—not recognition, exactly. Recognition's cousin. The feeling you get when a stranger's laugh sounds like someone you used to know, and you can't say why, and it aches in a room inside you that you didn't know was furnished.

"No," she agreed slowly. "There isn't. Isn't that odd? I feel like I've been carrying something heavy for years and this morning I put it down and can't remember what it was." She smiled then, and it transformed her face, and I understood with a lurch that this was what she'd looked like before. Before 1968. Before whatever she'd clawed herself back out of. This was the original face, the unhaunted one, and it was kind.

"Well," she said briskly. "If your car's broke, young man, the Hollises will have a look at it. They're good with engines. Everyone's waking up chipper this morning—whole village slept like the dead, if you'll pardon the expression, and me with a spade in my hand saying it." She chuckled. "First proper night's rest I've had in—I want to say years. Isn't that funny? I feel ten pounds lighter and I don't know where the weight went."

"I'm glad," I said, and meant it, and hated how much I meant it.

She gave me a little wave and went on toward the churchyard, presumably to dig a grave for nobody, because that was her job and jobs persist even when their reasons don't. At the gate she paused.

"You've got a kind face," she said over her shoulder. "Sad, but kind. Like you lost something and decided to be decent about it." Then she shook her head at herself. "Listen to me. Morning philosophizing. You'll think I'm mad."

"No," I said. "I think you're the sanest person in this village."

She laughed at that—a real laugh, unguarded—and went inside the fence, and I stood in the road watching her go, and I did not tell her that she'd once had a daughter whose face she'd paid to forget, or that she'd once begged a stranger to leave before the harvest moon, or that she'd pressed a ledger into his hands with tears freezing in the wind. None of those things were true anymore. Or rather, they were true the way the bell was still a bell: technically, in fragments, with the sound gone out of them.

* * *

The village woke up around me while I walked it, and every single waking was a small miracle performed by people who would never know they'd been dying.

I watched the baker's son—I never did learn his name; he'd been the baker's son to me from the start, and now he was only that forever—come out of his family's back door with an armful of flour sacks, yawning, scratching his ribs, entirely and blessedly himself. He saw me looking and gave me a nod, the flat incurious nod of one stranger acknowledging another. His wedding ring was on the right hand. His own hand. His own life, his own memories, his own terror returned to him like confiscated property, and he carried his flour inside with it and complained to his father about the cold.

His mother came out after him and boxed his ear affectionately for sleeping past the alarm, and he yelped, and she laughed, and none of them knew that forty-eight hours ago the boy had woken up as his own father, ring and memories and mannerisms intact, screaming silently behind a borrowed face while everyone he loved smiled at him and saw no one home.

They sang in their sleep last night, I thought. All of them. And this morning they woke up as themselves.

Except the hymn wasn't in them anymore. I could tell by walking past the houses. Three weeks ago—or whatever it had been—the windows had leaked music at 3 a.m., every voice slightly wrong, layered like a recording of itself. Last night, apparently, they'd slept in silence. Ordinary human silence, full of snores and mattress creaks and one dog's dream. Whatever covenant had run under Hollowmere for a century had been paid off, discharged, torn up. The village was mortal now. It would get sick and flood and argue and age and bury its own, honestly, without feeding anyone to anything.

It was the best thing I'd ever done and I had no memory of doing it.

That's the joke, if you want it. That's the punchline I wake up inside of every morning now, though that particular morning I didn't yet know it would repeat: I had traded everything for this, and *everything* turned out to include the receipt.

* * *

I found the list in my coat pocket around noon, sitting on the sea wall at the edge of the village green, eating a roll I'd bought from the baker with money that still worked.

My handwriting. I recognized it the way you recognize your own walk—lopsided capitals, the way I cross my sevens. A single sheet, folded twice, soft at the creases like it had been handled a lot. Written fast, in the dark, maybe by candlelight. Maybe by the light of a moon the color of a wound.

At the top, underlined twice:

**THINGS I WILL LOSE TONIGHT — READ THIS FIRST, AUGUST, BECAUSE YOU WILL NOT REMEMBER READING IT**

Below that, a list. And below the list, a column of small tally marks, dozens of them, each one struck through except—

No. Let me do this properly, because it matters, because it's the closest thing I have to a scar.

Each line of the list read the same way: a memory, named. *Wren teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass, age six.* *The summer we mapped the quarry and called it Cartography.* *Her laughing at my funeral suit, age nineteen.* *The humming in the walls, and how she liked it.* On and on, twenty-some lines, a whole sister rendered in inventory. And every single line had been crossed out. Not scribbled—struck through with a single clean stroke, deliberate, final, the way you strike a name off a registry.

Except the last line. The last line was uncrossed, and underlined, and written slower than the rest, the letters careful, like someone making themselves legible to a future stranger:

**you had a sister, and you chose right.**

I sat on the sea wall for a long time with that paper shaking in my hands.

Here's what I could reconstruct, and reconstruction is a generous word for it—it was more like reading tea leaves in my own skull. I had a sister. Her name was Wren—that much survived, anchored somehow, maybe by the paper itself, maybe by the one line I'd chosen to leave unstruck. We were twins. I had come home to bury her, and she hadn't been dead, and something in this village had worn her face, and I had learned a verse that was never written down, and I had sung it backward into a bell, and the price of the ticket was every memory I had of her, and I had known that price in advance, itemized, in my own handwriting, and I had paid it anyway.

The crossed-out lines weren't erased. That was the terrible, precise detail. They were still *there*, ink on paper, the words legible. *Whistling through a blade of grass.* I could read the sentence. I understood every word. But there was nothing behind it. It was like reading a menu from a restaurant in a country I'd never visited. *Cartography.* A word. A game two children played, apparently, mine among them, and I felt about it the way you'd feel about a stranger's photograph found in a used book: mild curiosity, faint warmth, no claim.

Twenty-some memories, listed, priced, and spent. And the account balance was one sentence.

*You had a sister, and you chose right.*

I want to tell you I wept. Honestly, mostly I sat there chewing a roll I couldn't taste, doing arithmetic. Because here's the thing about grief with no object: it doesn't hurt less. It hurts *wrong*. Somewhere in me was a Wren-shaped cavity, and the cavity ached, and I kept pressing on it like a tongue finding a missing tooth, and all I ever found was smooth nothing and that one sentence, glowing at the bottom of the page like a pilot light.

*Chose right.* Chose what? Against what? I'd have liked to ask myself. Past-me had left no contact details.

But I noticed something else, sitting there. Something I probably shouldn't admit, because it makes me look worse than grief does. Underneath the hollow, underneath the wrong-ache, there was a kind of peace. Not happiness—peace. The peace of a man who has set down a weight he can't remember carrying. Mrs. Tarnow had said it herself that morning, not four hours ago, about a weight she couldn't name. The whole village felt it, I'd have bet money. Ten pounds lighter, all of us, and no idea where the weight went.

We'd bought our peace with a girl named after a bird, and none of us could remember the exchange rate.

* * *

I drove out of Hollowmere at dusk, because there was nothing left to do there and because staying felt like loitering at the scene of a crime I'd solved too well to prosecute.

The village shrank in my mirror—smoke rising straight from chimneys, lights coming on window by window, ordinary as arithmetic. Someone was laughing somewhere. A television murmured through an open door. As I passed the last house, an old woman in a garden looked up from her roses and raised a hand to me—not a wave, exactly. More like a salute between soldiers of some war neither of us remembered fighting. I lifted two fingers off the wheel and we were done with each other forever.

The list rode in my breast pocket. I'd tried leaving it in the car and lasted four miles.

Somewhere past the county line, at a red light with nowhere to be, I caught myself doing it again: pressing my tongue against the missing tooth. *Wren.* I said the name out loud to hear it. It fit my mouth perfectly, the way names you've said ten thousand times fit, and I had said it zero times that I could access, and both of those facts were equally true, and I sat at that light until it turned green twice, holding the word like a stone from a beach I couldn't find on any map.

Then I drove on. There was a chapel at the next crossroads—I'd see it soon enough, its door standing open, an old hymnal lying face-up on the pew as if waiting—and I'd stop, because stopping is what people like me do at chapels with open doors. But that hadn't happened yet.

That night, in a motel outside a town whose name I never learned, I slept nine hours without dreaming, and woke up rested, and lay staring at the ceiling with wet eyes and no explanation, and finally got up and took out the list and read the last line again.

*You had a sister, and you chose right.*

"Yeah," I told the empty room, in a voice that still had a ghost of harmony in it. "Thanks."

And somewhere far behind me, in a drowned quarry under a silent village, a mold shaped like a kneeling person held, in a dead girl's handwriting, the rest of the story—and waited, the way songs wait, for someone who could still read it.
