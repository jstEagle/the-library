## 22. The Song You Don't Remember Learning

The car smelled like someone else's life.

That's the first thing I noticed, sitting in the driver's seat with the engine ticking and the sun coming up over a village I no longer had any reason to name. The car smelled of coffee gone cold, of the pine air freshener I apparently didn't own, of a long drive and a longer night, and underneath all of it—faintly, like a watermark—of candle smoke and turned earth.

I sat there for a while. I'm not proud of how long. There's a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, like a man checking his mirrors. I checked my mirrors. All three of them, twice, and then the blind spot over my left shoulder, where there was nothing but the churchyard and its rubble and a woman I'd apparently known once, standing at her gate, watching me with the polite blankness of someone who has never seen me before in her life.

I raised a hand. She didn't raise hers back. She turned and went inside, and her gate swung shut behind her with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

Right, I thought. Okay. Drive.

I drove.

The road out of Hollowmere is the kind of road that doesn't believe in you. It narrows, it floods at the low places, it runs between hedgerows so old and knotted they look less like plants than like arguments the land is still having with itself. I took it at a crawl, because my hands were shaking in a way I couldn't account for and I didn't trust my reflexes above twenty miles an hour. There was no reason for them to shake. I'd slept—hadn't I? I'd slept in the car, outside the village, and I'd woken up stiff-necked and empty-headed and fine. Fine the way a house is fine after the furniture's been repossessed. All the rooms still there. Nothing in them.

At some point I caught myself trying to remember why I'd come.

Not why I'd driven this route—I could reconstruct that, sort of, the way you reconstruct a dream by its edges. There'd been a phone call. There'd been a funeral. There'd been a name on a form that I'd signed anyway, because leaving would have meant admitting something was wrong, and I had a lifetime's practice at not admitting things were wrong. But the center of it, the reason, the person—all of it was gone. Not blurred. Gone, with clean edges, like a word that's been cut out of a page and the page smoothed down after so you almost can't tell. Almost.

I pulled over at the top of the hill and looked back.

Hollowmere sat in its bowl of fields the way it must have sat for four hundred years, gray roofs and green water and the church standing at its center with no bell in it. The tower looked wrong without one. It looked like a man with his mouth sewn shut who is, for the first time in a century, not trying to say anything.

I couldn't tell you why my eyes went hot. That's the honest truth of it. I looked at a village I had no memory of loving and I cried like a man at a graveside, and I had no idea whose grave it was.

* * *

I stopped at the crossroads because my body told me to.

That's not a figure of speech. I was three miles past the turnoff for the coast road, which is where I'd decided—loosely, in the way of a man deciding things with a head full of wet sand—that I was going, when my hands did it for me. Indicator on. Brakes. Left, onto a lane I'd never seen, down between two fields to a chapel I didn't know existed, and I only understood I'd made a decision at all when the engine cut out and my fingers were already on the door handle.

The chapel stood at the meeting of four lanes, the way chapels used to, back when people believed crossroads needed guarding. It was small. Whitewashed, once; the wash had gone the color of old teeth. A slate roof furred with lichen. A door that had been painted green approximately during the reign of some king I couldn't have named, and which stood open, which is a thing doors in empty chapels should not do at seven in the morning.

I want to be clear that I knew, standing there, that I should get back in the car. I want to be clear that some part of me—the part that had spent fifteen years explaining away footsteps and humming and the particular creak of a house that knows your name—was screaming at me in a language I no longer spoke. I want to be clear that I heard it, and that I understood it was right, and that I went in anyway.

Because the humming had started.

It started, I think, at the top of the hill, back when I was crying at a village I couldn't remember. It had been going ever since, low and tuneless, under my breath, and I hadn't noticed it until the moment the car turned left, when the tune resolved into something with shape and intention, and I realized my mouth had been moving for three miles without my permission.

I didn't know the song.

I want that on the record, in ink, notarized. I did not know the song. It was a hymn, or the bones of one, in a minor key that leaned somewhere unexpected in the second phrase, the way a stair leans when the house has settled, and I had never heard it in my life. I knew this the way you know your own name. And I was humming it perfectly, every interval exact, the way you hum your own name too.

The chapel smelled of dust and cold stone and, beneath that, very faintly, of candle smoke that was not old enough to be there.

There were maybe twenty pews. They were worn smooth along the tops of the backs, in long pale ovals, the way wood gets when hands have rested on it for generations—and I noticed that, and noted it, and told myself it was nothing, because apparently I am the kind of man who tells himself things are nothing even after everything. A hymnal lay open on the third pew from the front. Just the one. Just one book, open, in an otherwise empty chapel, at the exact height of a resting hand.

You already know what I did. I did what you'd do. I picked it up.

The page was brittle and foxed with age, and the hymn had no title I recognized, just a number, and the verses ran down the page in that cramped old typeface that makes every hymn look like a legal summons. One. Two. Three. Four. And then the page ended, and the next hymn began, and there were—let me count them again, because I counted them four times that morning, standing in that cold chapel like a man checking a lock he'd already checked—there were four verses.

Four.

And my humming, which had followed me faithfully from the car, through the door, up the aisle, stopped. Not trailed off. Stopped, clean, the way a clock stops, at the exact place where a fifth verse would have begun.

I stood there holding a stranger's hymnal in a stranger's chapel and I understood two things with absolute, unshakable certainty, the way you understand fire is hot or that you shouldn't look down. The first was that this hymn was missing a verse. The second was that I knew it was missing a verse the way I knew my own name, and that I did not know my own name, not entirely, not this morning, because there was a shape cut out of me with clean edges and the shape had been a person.

I sat down in the third pew from the front, because my legs had opinions of their own now too, and I read the four verses that were there.

They were ordinary. That was the worst of it. Verses about sowing and reaping, about the Lord walking the furrows at evening, about the harvest gathered in and the laborers called home. Harvest language. The kind of thing that gets sung at services in October by people who mostly mean nothing by it. I read them twice and they slid off me like rain off slate, and all the while the absence of the fifth verse sat on the page like a hole in a photograph, like a chair pulled out at a table, like a gap in an alto line exactly the size of—

Exactly the size of what?

I put my hand to my face and found it wet.

* * *

Here is what I know about myself, as of this morning, as of the coffee I bought at a petrol station from a boy who called me "mate" and meant nothing by it and never would:

I am thirty-four years old. I drive a car with a pine air freshener. There is a funeral home receipt in the glovebox with my signature on it, dated two days ago, for the burial of a woman whose name I have read four times and whose face I could not pick out of a crowd, because I never saw it. There is a hotel keycard from a town I passed through on the way down, and a phone with no messages on it, and a wallet, and a life, and all of it fits together like a sentence with the middle clause removed.

And there is a piece of paper in my pocket.

I found it when I was looking for the receipt, sitting in the chapel pew, needing—God knows why—proof that I was a man who did ordinary things in an ordinary sequence. It's folded in eighths, soft at the creases, the way paper gets when it's been carried a long time or held a long time. It's in my handwriting. I know my handwriting the way I apparently know hymns I've never heard: intimately, and without knowing why.

It's a list.

*The summer she taught me to whistle through the gap in her teeth.*
*The night we buried the time capsule under the willow and she cried because we'd have to dig it up to remember it.*
*Her humming through the wall, every night, and how I stopped needing to explain it.*
*The way she said my name when she was laughing at me. (Both ways she said it.)*
*The quarry. The mold. The message. She waited for me. She knew I'd come.*
*The last verse. I took it from her mouth and I gave it back wrong, on purpose, and the bell broke, and it was worth it. It was worth it.*

The rest of the page is blank.

I sat in that chapel and I read that list and I understood, in the way you understand a thing from a great height, looking down at it, that I had written it to myself. That some version of me—the version who knew, who remembered, who had a sister—had sat down on the eve of something terrible and written down everything he could not bear to lose, knowing he was going to lose it, knowing the losing was the price and the price was right. And that the list had done what lists do, which is nothing. Which is only witness. The memories themselves were gone. All of them. Every line on that page was a headstone for something I couldn't visit.

Except.

Except the last line. *You had a sister, and you chose right.* That one isn't a memory. That one's a message, and messages are different, and I think whoever I was knew that. I think he knew the memories would burn and wanted one line to survive the fire, the way you write your name on the inside of a coat before you send a child off to a place where coats get mixed up.

You had a sister. And I chose right.

I read it maybe fifty times. I read it until the words stopped being words. And here is the thing I will tell you, the thing I have decided to believe, because a man has to decide to believe something or the morning gets him: I don't remember her. I don't remember her laugh or her humming or the gap in her teeth or either of the ways she said my name. But I know she was worth it. That came through the fire. That's the one thing that wouldn't burn, and I think—I think that's not an accident. I think love is the part of a memory that lives somewhere the song can't reach, and I think that's why the song has to take the whole memory to be safe, and I think the song knew it, and I think it took them all anyway, and I think it still didn't get everything.

I folded the list along its soft creases and put it back in my pocket, over my heart, which is where you put things you intend to keep.

* * *

The hymnal was still in my hands.

I'd forgotten I was holding it, which should have frightened me more than it did, but by then I'd spent a morning being a stranger to my own grief and my thresholds had all moved. The book lay open on my knees, four verses and a wound where the fifth should be, and the chapel was very quiet, and the quiet had a quality I recognized without knowing from where—the quality of a house that has stopped making noises because it is listening.

I started humming again.

I didn't decide to. That's the thing I need you to understand, whoever you are, whoever finds this, whatever this account ends up being: I did not decide to. The tune came up out of me like water finding a crack, the first verse, the sowing verse, and my voice knew the intervals the way my hands knew the steering wheel, without me, beneath me, and it was beautiful. That's the trap. That's the whole trap, I'd bet my erased life on it. It's beautiful, and it fits, and it feels like remembering something rather than learning it, and that is exactly how a song gets its hooks into a man who has sworn off songs.

I hummed verse one. I hummed verse two, the Lord walking the furrows. I got most of the way through verse three before I caught myself, and I stopped, and I sat in the cold with my heart going like a fist on a door.

Because I knew what came next. Not the words—I don't think there are words, or if there are, they've been kept out of books on purpose, because the verse is an instruction, someone I used to be wrote that down somewhere, someone I used to be knew that and it's one of the few things that survived the burning. But I knew where it went. I could feel the shape of it the way your tongue finds the gap of a missing tooth, the way a congregation leaves a gap in the alto line exactly the size of the person they've decided to take. The hymn leaned somewhere unexpected, and the lean was the beginning of the fifth verse, and I could have hummed it. I could have hummed it right now, sitting here, and I don't know where that knowledge lives or how it got in me or how to cut it out, and I am not going to hum it.

I am not going to hum it.

I closed the book.

It made a small sound, a sound like a mouth closing, like a held breath let go, and the chapel's listening quiet changed—eased, or pretended to ease—and the light through the green door looked like ordinary morning again instead of like an invitation. I stood. My legs held. I walked up the aisle and set the hymnal back on the third pew, open, to the same page, because some part of me that I've decided to trust said: leave it as you found it, leave it waiting for someone else, and some other part of me said: no, close it, don't leave the door open behind you. I compromised the way people do. I closed it, and then I couldn't make myself put it back where it had been, so it's in my coat pocket now, riding over my heart next to the list, one dead song and one dead sister, and I am aware of how that sounds, and I don't care.

At the door I stopped. I don't know why. Habit, maybe—the habit of a man who used to check rooms before leaving them, who used to listen to houses. I stood in the green doorway with the four lanes running away from me in their four directions and the fields going gold at the edges, and I listened.

Nothing. Birds. Wind in the hedges, arguing with itself. Somewhere far off, a tractor, which is a kind of hymn too, if you're the kind of man who can no longer hear a rhythm without flinching.

Nothing sang. Nothing hummed. The song was asleep, or seemed to be, and I stood there a long time in the doorway, and I want to tell you I felt free. What I felt was the way you feel on the first morning after a fever breaks: hollowed, grateful, and unable to stop checking your own temperature.

Because here's what I know, and I know it the way I know the hymn, which is to say without knowing how: songs wait. Songs are patient in a way people aren't. A song doesn't need you to remember it; it only needs you to be near it, and to have a mouth. It can wait in a book on a pew. It can wait in a village that sleeps soundly for the first time in a century. It can wait, I suspect, in the gap of a missing tooth, in the lean of a phrase, in the place where a man's humming stops one verse too soon. It doesn't hold a grudge, because holding a grudge requires wanting something, and the song doesn't want. It just is, the way water is, and water always finds the crack.

But so does loyalty.

That's the other thing I know, and I know it from the list, from the last line, from the one sentence that walked out of the fire still standing: whatever they took from me, they couldn't take the choosing. I came back for someone once. I don't know her face. I don't know her voice, except that she hummed, and that she said my name two ways, and that one of them was laughing. I don't know anything about her except that she was worth everything I had, and I gave it, and I would give it again, and that knowledge doesn't live in my memories at all. It lives somewhere deeper, somewhere the congregation's hands couldn't strip, and if the song ever wakes—and it will, it's only sleeping, songs only sleep—then it's going to find out what I found out this morning, standing in a doorway at a crossroads with an empty head and a full heart.

You can erase the man. The loyalty outlives him.

I got back in the car. The engine caught on the first try, which felt like a small mercy, and I've decided to take all the small mercies I'm offered, because I know now how the large ones are priced.

I pulled out onto the coast road. The chapel shrank in the mirror, whitewash and slate and the green door I'd shut behind me, and I watched it until the hedge took it, and then I watched the hedge, and then I drove.

Somewhere around the fifth mile, without deciding to, I started humming again. I let it go as far as the end of verse three. Then I stopped, one verse short, the way I will always stop, and I drove on into a morning that didn't know my name, carrying a closed book, an open wound, and a song I don't remember learning—humming everything except the last verse, which I know by heart, and will take with me, unspoken, all the way to the sea.
