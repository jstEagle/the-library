## 7. Striking Out the Names

The parish archive smelled of dust and candle grease and, beneath that, something older — the smell of paper that had been waiting a long time to be read. Zephyrine had let himself in with the vicar's spare key, which Mr. Ellery had pressed into his hand two days earlier with the expression of a man handing over a live adder.

"Lock up after yourself," the vicar had said. "And Zephyrine — whatever you find, remember the parish has kept its accounts faithfully for over a century. Faithfully," he'd repeated, as though the word were a rope thrown to a drowning man. Or perhaps thrown from one.

Now Zephyrine sat at the reading table with a single lamp turned low and the 1911 ledger open before him, and he did not feel like a drowning man. He felt like an accountant.

That was the horror of it, he was beginning to understand. Not the bell ringing in an empty tower. Not the counting. It was the *bookkeeping*. Somewhere under Marrowmere there was a system, tidy as a chemist's shelf, and every soul in the village was a line item, entered in a fair hand, reconciled on schedule. He had spent twenty years keeping notes on the dying — their appetites, their restlessness, their backwards counting — because he believed that attention was a form of love. He had never imagined that somewhere, something else was taking notes too. And that its handwriting was his own.

He turned to the final page.

Nine names remained unstruck. Ada Kettleby. Bell Kettleby. And seven others — Mrs. Prowse from the end cottage, old Tam Weller who still grew beans out of pure spite, the Hollis sisters, and three more whose faces he knew from market day and Sunday pews and the small kindnesses of village life. His pen hovered over them the way a hand hovers over a stove it already knows is hot.

"All right," he said quietly, to the ledger, to the drowned year, to whatever clerk was listening. "Let's discuss terms."

The ink bled before his pen even touched the page.

Not dramatically — nothing so honest as a scream. The first stroke of his nib went down clean across *Ada Kettleby*, a good firm line, the kind of line a man draws when he has decided something. And then the line began to move. The ink lifted off the paper like oil rising through water, gathered itself at the edge of the stroke, and slid back into the letters, restoring them. *Ada Kettleby* sat on the page untouched, refreshed almost, the ink darker than the names around it, as though the book had just re-inked its type.

Zephyrine sat back. His heart was going hard but his hands, thank God, were steady. Twenty years of hospice work taught you that: steadiness while everything else went to ruin.

He tried again. A second line, harder this time, the nib nearly tearing the page.

The bell rang twice.

It came through the floor of the archive, up through the foundations of the old schoolhouse where the parish kept its records, a double note — *toll, toll* — soft and close, like someone clearing their throat behind him. He did not need to look out the window to know the tower was empty. He did not need to check his watch to know it was three in the afternoon, broad daylight, a time when bells have no business and empty towers have less.

*Two rings for two strokes,* he thought. *It's counting my attempts.*

He tried a third time, on Tam Weller's name, using a different pen — a pencil, actually, graphite against iron-gall ink, surely a different substance entirely. The pencil line held for perhaps four seconds. Then it greyed, faded, and lifted off the page like breath off glass, and the bell rang twice again, patient as tide.

"All right," Zephyrine said, and heard his own voice crack on the second word, which embarrassed him even alone in a locked room with a haunted ledger. "You don't take deletions. Fine. I've edited enough manuscripts to know the type."

He thought of his own drafts — the one novel he'd written, abandoned in a drawer, where he'd learned the hard way that you cannot simply remove a character. Cut them out and the story bleeds; the narrative reaches around the gap and knits itself shut, and the character returns, changed maybe, but returned. Stories do not accept subtraction. Stories only accept *exchange*.

A name given freely.

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do — fully dressed, holding its hat, perfectly reasonable. The ledger would not let him strike a name. But a substitution — a trade, one entry for another, the oldest arithmetic there was — that the book might honor. That was how ledgers worked. Nothing vanished; balances merely moved.

And there was, sitting at this very table, a man with a name to give.

"No," he said aloud, immediately, to himself, to the lamp, to the nine names. Then, more quietly, in the tone he used with patients who wanted to discuss giving up: "No. We're not doing anything rash. We're going to think."

He thought.

He thought about Ada, who sang off-key while kneading bread and pretended she couldn't hear Bell laughing at her — laughing silently, shoulders shaking, the boy's laughter all the louder for having no sound attached. He thought about how she had come to Marrowmere fleeing something, though she hadn't yet told him what, and how she'd built a life here anyway, out of flour and stubbornness and a mute grandson she loved with a ferocity that made the word seem too small.

He thought about Bell, who pointed at the sky when the bell rang, who drew the tower over and over with figures standing in its doorway, who had not spoken since his grandfather died mid-number and had somehow, in that silence, become the only other person in Marrowmere who could hear the truth.

He thought about Mrs. Prowse, who saved the crossword until he visited so they could do it together, and about Tam Weller's beans, and the Hollis sisters' terrible sherry, and the way the dying counted backwards like children climbing down stairs in the dark, trusting someone to be waiting at the bottom.

Nine names. Nine lives. And the well rising, inch by measured inch, one soul per toll.

Zephyrine looked down at the ledger, at the tenth blank line below the nine, the one the book seemed to be holding open the way you hold a door.

"Not tonight," he told it. "But I see your arithmetic. I was a nurse before I was anything else. I know a triage decision when I'm standing in one."

The lamp flickered. On the page, faintly, the blank tenth line darkened — just slightly, just at its edges — like a mouth beginning to smile.

He closed the ledger firmly, the way you close a conversation you're winning by leaving.

* * *

Ada knew something was wrong before he got his coat off.

She always did. It was unnerving, that talent of hers — she'd look up from whatever she was doing with the expression of a woman who had already been informed, by some private wire, of exactly how much trouble you were carrying and would thank you to set it down by the door.

"You've been to the archive," she said. It wasn't a question. She had flour on her hands and bread dough rising under a cloth on the table, and Bell sat at the window drawing — the tower again, always the tower, though lately the figures in the doorway had begun to number nine.

"I've been to the archive," Zephyrine admitted.

"And?"

"And the book doesn't want to cooperate." He hung his coat on the peg, taking longer about it than any coat deserved. "Ink won't hold. Lines come back. Every attempt costs two rings." He managed a smile. "I'm racking up quite a bell debt."

Ada wiped her hands slowly. "Zeph."

Nobody called him that. Nobody ever had, before her. It had arrived one morning without ceremony, halfway through his second year in Marrowmere, and now it lived in her mouth as naturally as if she'd invented it.

"When I came here," she said, "seven years ago, I left a place where things counted too. Different things. Same arithmetic." She looked at the window, at Bell, at the tower he was drawing. "I promised myself I'd know the signs next time. And I do. And so do you. So don't stand in my kitchen telling me the book won't cooperate unless you already know what will."

The bread smelled wonderful. The light was going gold outside. Somewhere beyond the fen, faint as memory, the well was filling.

"There may be a way," Zephyrine said carefully. "Substitution rather than deletion. A name given freely, exchanged for—" He stopped.

"For?" said Ada.

"I'm not certain yet. I want to test the principle first. Properly. Before anyone gets hopeful." He was aware, distantly, of how smooth the lie was, how easily it left him, and hated it. "Give me a few days."

Ada studied him for a long moment. She had her husband's patience, or perhaps patience was simply what grief left behind after everything else burned away.

"A few days," she agreed. Then, turning back to her dough, softly: "Whatever it is you're not telling me, Zeph — I buried one man in this village already. Don't make me bury the habit of trusting you too."

Bell looked up from his drawing. For a moment his eyes met Zephyrine's — clear, grey, far too old for eight years old — and then the boy turned the sketchpad around and held it up.

The tower. The doorway. Nine figures inside.

And below them, drawn small and careful, a tenth figure — standing apart, at the foot of the tower, looking up. With a nurse's bag in one hand.

Zephyrine smiled at the boy, and took the sketchpad gently, and tore that last figure out of the page, and folded it into his pocket, where it lay against his heart like a prescription he had no intention of filling.

"That one's not finished yet," he told Bell.

The bell rang twice, somewhere over the fen, as if in agreement.
