## 9. The Last Entry

The water in the lanes had risen past the doorstones by midnight, and Zephyrine waded through it carrying the ledger against his chest like a child he meant to keep dry.

The flood was not rain. There had been no rain. The water came up out of the ground itself, out of the well, out of every drain and ditch in Marrowmere, as if the village were a cup being filled from beneath. It moved without current. It lay flat and black under the moon and reflected nothing — not the stars, not the church tower, not him. He tried not to look at it. Looking at it felt like being read.

The bell rang as he walked. Not the muffled night-ringing of the past weeks but a full-throated peal that shook droplets from the hedges, though when he tilted his head back to the tower there was only the empty belfry, the bare frame where a bell had hung until 1911, and inside that emptiness something enormous swinging.

*Eight,* said the bell.

He had stopped hearing the count as sound some days ago. Now it arrived the way his own heartbeat arrived — from inside, patient, inevitable. His patients counted with it. He could hear them through the walls of their rooms as he'd left the hospice: Mrs. Petch lying serene with her hands folded, counting; old Tam Weller, who hadn't spoken a full sentence since Easter, counting; all seven of them, in unison, in separate beds, like a tide going out.

Ada had been asleep when he slipped from the house. He had left the forged pages on the kitchen table deliberately — no. That wasn't true, and he made himself admit it while he waded, because if he was going to do this thing he would not also lie to himself about it. He had left them carelessly. He had wanted her to find them. Some cowardly corner of him had hoped she would find them and be angry enough to bar the door behind him.

She would come anyway. He knew Ada. She would come anyway, with Bell on her back, because that was what family did, and the whole point of what he was about to write was that they would arrive too late.

*Seven.*

The church doors stood open. Water lay an inch deep across the nave, perfectly still, and the drowned congregation of 1911 stood in it.

They did not look drowned. That was the worst of it. They looked *dried* — pressed flat by ninety years of patience, their clothes stiff with river-silt gone to dust, their faces smooth as paper left too long in a window. There were forty-one of them. He knew the number because he had read it in the ledger so many times the number had worn a groove in him: forty-one souls who walked into the mere on the last night of October 1911, willingly, in their Sunday best, because the bell called them home and home was down.

They stood in rows facing the tower, and they were not menacing. They were *waiting*, the way people wait for a service to begin, and one of them — a woman with a hymnbook fused to her hands — turned her head slightly as Zephyrine passed, the way you acknowledge a latecomer slipping into the back pew.

"I'm not joining," he told her. His voice sounded small and rude in the nave.

The woman smiled. It was a kind smile. That was somehow worse than menace would have been.

*Six.*

The tower stairs were slick but solid. He climbed with the ledger held high, absurdly, as though the flood might reach for it specifically, and perhaps it would have. At the top, the belfry opened to the night sky, and the empty frame stood against the moon, and the sound lived in it like a bird in a cage of air.

On the sill where the ringing rope should have been, the book-stand waited. Of course it did. The ledger had always known its way home before he did.

Zephyrine set the book down and opened it to the final page, and his hands were steadier than he expected them to be. Ninety years of nursing had taught him this much: the shaking comes after. During the thing itself, the body is strangely businesslike. You note the time. You check the dose. You hold the hand. You do what the moment requires, and grief is permitted later, in private, in the dark, over tea.

Nine names remained unwritten-over. Ada Kettleby. Bell Kettleby. And seven more — his patients, his crooked little congregation of the dying, who had trusted him with their last arithmetic. He read each name once, the way you read a chart before signing it.

Then he took out his pen.

The ink refused him twice before. In the archive, in the vicarage, the strokes had bled backward off the page like water running uphill, and each refusal had cost two tolls. But that had been refusal *for* them. This was different. This was a trade, and trades require both parties, and he had come to give his half freely.

He wrote beneath the nine names, in his own hand, in the handwriting that had already been in this book fifty years before he was born:

*In place of the above, I offer myself. Zephyrine Thackeray, nurse of Marrowmere. Willing. Counted.*

The ink sank in without argument. The page drank it the way dry earth drinks rain, and where the words settled they glowed faintly, like embers under ash, and then faded to ordinary brown-black, permanent, filed.

"Right," Zephyrine said aloud, because nurses talk to patients through the worst of it and he supposed he was his own patient now. "Right. Last entry."

He struck through Ada's name first. One clean line, corner to corner, no hesitation — hesitation was cruelty, he'd learned that from watching doctors deliver bad news badly. The line took. The ink stayed where he put it. Somewhere below him, in the flooded lanes, he heard — or imagined he heard — a door open, and a woman's voice calling his name across the water, and a child carried fast.

"Not yet," he murmured. "Nearly done. Hold on."

Bell's name next. Then Mrs. Petch. Tam Weller. Each stroke clean, each name surrendering its claim, and with every line the bell above him rang louder, angrier, *hungrier* — five, four, three — and the drowned congregation in the nave began, very softly, to sing. Not a hymn he knew. A hymn from 1911, worn smooth as their faces, and underneath the singing the sound of water rising in the tower stairwell, climbing patiently toward him, filling the belfry like a cup.

Two names left. He struck them both together, one motion, and stood back.

The ledger lay open on the sill. Nine lines crossed clean through. One new entry glowing faintly, waiting to be collected.

*One,* said the bell.

And here is the thing Zephyrine Thackeray understood in that last high room, with the water at his knees and the drowned singing below and Ada's voice getting closer and closer and never close enough:

The count was the mechanism. Forty-one souls in 1911 had counted down and reached zero, and zero was the door, and they had walked through it willingly because the bell asked nicely and they were tired and faithful and did not know they were allowed to refuse.

But a count requires the counter. He had watched death approach a hundred times and learned its one secret: it cannot take you until your number runs out, and the number is yours.

The bell swung in its cage of empty air. The water reached his waist. Below, splashing now, shouting his name — *Zephyrine, ZEPHYRINE* — Ada came up the tower stairs with Bell on her back, and Bell was pointing past her, pointing at him, mouth open around a word two years buried.

The final toll began to gather itself in the empty frame, vast and slow, a wave building far out at sea.

Zephyrine closed his eyes. He did not run. He did not bargain. He simply did what he had spent his whole life teaching the dying to do, the thing no one in Marrowmere had thought to try in ninety years, the thing that was his alone to author:

He counted with it. *Three. Two.* And then, at the very lip of it, with the whole of the bell's voice falling toward him like an axe —

— he stopped.

He did not say *one*. He let the number go unspoken, hanging, unfinished, a sentence with no period, a ledger line left open. He held the count at the threshold the way you hold a dying man's hand: present, unafraid, refusing to be the one who lets go first.

The toll broke over him.

And the bell — the bell that had not existed for a hundred and eleven years, the bell that had melted and been sold and scattered and still rung — *cracked*. The sound split down the middle like ice on a thawing pond, like a spine, like a heart finally allowed to stop holding, and the great voice of it tore in half and fell silent mid-note, and the water in the tower stopped rising, and the singing below cut off as one.

The drowned congregation stood in the black nave, forty-one of them, unfinished, unsung home, uncollected.

And Zephyrine Thackeray, keeper of the count, felt himself begin — gently, terribly, like a tide going out — to be taken anyway.
