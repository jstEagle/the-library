## 1. The Songs They Won't Sing Twice

The last bus of the season smelled of diesel and wet wool, and Tamsin Okonkwo had it entirely to himself. He sat at the back with his recorder on his knee and his field notebook open against the seat in front of him, watching the hills fold themselves around the road like a hand closing. Snow had begun an hour out of Ashcombe, fat unhurried flakes that didn't fall so much as assemble, settling on the hedgerows until the whole valley looked like something being carefully wrapped for storage.

The driver, a broad man with a red face and a name stitched on his cap—HOLLIS—glanced at him twice in the mirror.

"You'll be the folklorist, then."

Tamsin looked up. "News travels fast."

"Nothing travels fast here. It just travels." Hollis turned back to the road. "Mrs. Petch rang the exchange this morning. Said to expect you. Said to tell you the roads close Thursday and won't open till thaw, so if you're thinking of leaving before you've finished, don't bother planning it."

"I'm not planning it."

"Mm." The bus took a bend, and the manor rose out of the trees ahead—grey stone, black windows, a roofline like a broken jaw. It sat above the village the way a lid sits above a pot. "That's Marlowe House," Hollis said, without being asked. "You'll hear about it. Everyone hears about it. Best thing you can do is write down what people tell you and not what they don't."

"And what don't they tell me?"

The driver smiled at the road. "That's the spirit."

The village of Cellar Hollow announced itself first by smell—woodsmoke and cold stone and, underneath it, something older and sweeter, like apples gone to cider in a locked room. Then the houses appeared, hunched under their snow, each one with its windows lit amber against the early dark. The bus sighed into the square and stopped beside a post office with a green door and a hand-painted sign: PETCH & SON, POST OFFICE AND ROOMS.

"End of the line," Hollis said. "For everything, this time of year."

Tamsin gathered his bag, his recorder, his notebooks—four of them, each labeled in his small, exact hand—and stepped down into snow that came to his shins. The cold was total, democratic, indifferent. It went through his London coat as if the coat were a rumor. Behind him the bus turned, its lights sweeping the square, and he watched it go with the particular loneliness of a man who has just made a decision irreversible.

The door of the post office opened before he knocked. A woman stood there, sixty or so, small and wiry, with iron-grey hair pinned back and eyes the color of the slate on the hills. She looked at him the way a customs officer looks at luggage.

"Mr. Okonkwo."

"Mrs. Petch."

"You're taller than the letters made you sound." She stepped back to let him in. "Mind the mat. And mind your boots. And mind—" She stopped, as if the sentence had a third item she'd decided against. "Room's at the top of the stairs. Bath's Thursday. Supper's at six, and I don't cook twice."

The room was narrow and clean, with a bed, a washstand, a desk under the window, and a view of the square that took in the whole village at a glance: the church with its squat tower, the inn, the shop, the crooked row of cottages climbing the hill toward the manor. From this window, Tamsin thought, you could watch everything and be watched from everywhere. He set his recorder on the desk and his notebooks beside it, squared their edges, and felt the small, private settling he always felt at the start of a collection—the sense of a devotion beginning.

His mother had called it his religion. She hadn't meant it kindly. *You love the songs more than the people who sing them,* she'd said once, and he'd never found a way to answer, because it was true, and because the people had never made much room for him anywhere, and the songs had. A song didn't care where you were from. A song only wanted to be carried.

He came down at six.

Mrs. Petch had laid supper in the back parlor: mutton stew, dark bread, a bowl of stewed apple. She sat across from him with her own plate and watched him eat with frank, unembarrassed attention, the way he himself watched singers. He recognized the look. It was the look of a person cataloguing.

"You've had folklorists before," he said.

"One. 1971. A young man from the university. He stayed nine days." She tore her bread. "He left on the tenth."

"What happened on the tenth?"

"He asked the wrong question at the wrong house." She said it the way you'd report weather. "You'll want the songs, I expect. Everyone wants the songs. We've got more songs than we've got people, and half of them nobody remembers the ends of."

"Half?"

"More than half." She looked at him then, directly, and something in her face shifted—not warmth, exactly, but a kind of tired honesty. "You seem a patient young man. I'll say this once, and then we won't say it again. There are songs in this village that get sung once. Once a year, once a generation, once and no more. If you hear one, you write it down and you keep it shut in your book and you do not ask anyone to sing it again. Not for clarity. Not for the second verse. Not for science." She picked up her spoon. "The asking is the trouble. You understand?"

"I understand that I don't understand."

"That's a good start." Almost, almost, she smiled. "My mother had a voice on her. Sang at every wedding, every christening, every burying. There was a lullaby she sang me, and her mother sang her, and I'd sing it to my boy when he was small, God keep him in Leeds."

"Could you—" Tamsin kept his voice light, casual, the way you'd offer a hand to a bird. "If it's not one of the ones you meant. I'd be grateful to hear it. Just as a beginning."

Mrs. Petch was quiet for a long moment. The fire ticked. Outside, the wind moved along the square and something—shutters, perhaps, or a sign—knocked softly against a wall, in no rhythm he could catch.

Then she began to sing.

Her voice was low and worn and true, a voice like a path walked over for a hundred years. The melody was old—older than the language of the words, which were dialect, half-eroded: something about *down* and something about *sleep*, a child lowered into a warm dark, a mother's hands, a promise that the dark would keep the child the way a cellar keeps apples. Tamsin's pen was moving before he'd decided to move it, his shorthand spilling across the notebook, and the melody was lovely, was *strange*, was built on intervals that didn't resolve where a lullaby should resolve but leaned, always, toward a lower note, as if the whole song were a staircase.

She sang four lines. Five. Six. Her eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere warm and long ago, and Tamsin leaned forward, recorder running, pen flying, already hearing how the seventh line would rise—

She stopped.

Mid-word. Mid-breath. The last syllable died in her mouth like a moth in a jar.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Tamsin had ever heard in a kitchen.

Mrs. Petch had gone grey. Not pale—grey, the color draining downward from her cheeks like water through sand, and her hand had come up to her throat, and she was staring at the table as if the grain of the wood had rearranged itself into something she knew.

"Mrs. Petch—"

"That's enough of that." Her voice came out flat and steady, which was somehow worse than shaking. "That's enough sung. You'll not write the rest down, because there's no rest to write. There's just the part that gets sung and the part that doesn't."

"The song isn't finished—"

"No," she said. "It isn't."

She stood, gathered his plate though he was still eating, and stood there a moment with the plate in her hands, and when she looked at him her eyes had come back to the present and were hard and bright and frightened all at once.

"Six o'clock supper's at six," she said. "I'll bring your tea at eight. Don't go out after dark your first night. The village isn't used to you yet, and you're not used to it, and it's better that way till you are." At the door she paused, her back to him. "Mr. Okonkwo. What did I sing? Tell me what I sang, in your own words."

"A lullaby," Tamsin said slowly. "A mother putting her child down somewhere safe. Somewhere below. And promising it would be kept."

Mrs. Petch's knuckles whitened on the plate.

"Aye," she said. "That's one way of putting it."

She left. Tamsin sat alone in the parlor with the fire ticking and his notebook open, and read back his shorthand, and hummed the melody under his breath, and felt the lean of it, the staircase of it, the way every phrase bent downward toward a note she had never reached.

*The part that gets sung and the part that doesn't.*

He wrote that down too. Then, because he was who he was, he opened a fresh page and wrote at the top of it, in his small exact hand: *Cellar Hollow. Collection begun. Note: every song here seems to be missing the same thing.*

He did not yet know what the same thing was. He only knew that when Mrs. Petch had stopped singing, the silence she'd left behind hadn't felt empty.

It had felt *held*—the way a room feels held when someone in it is listening, and has been listening far longer than you have, and is waiting, patiently, for the verse you promised and did not deliver.

Upstairs, in his narrow room, Tamsin slept badly. Once, in the small hours, he woke to a sound below the window—voices, or a voice, low and rhythmic, rising from the direction of the church. He got up and looked out. The square was empty. The snow lay unbroken from the post office door to the churchyard gate, without a single footprint in it anywhere.

The singing, if it had been singing, had stopped.

But in the morning, when he came down, Mrs. Petch was standing at the foot of the stairs with his breakfast tray and an expression he would come to know very well in the weeks ahead.

"You slept all right?" she asked.

"Mostly," Tamsin said. "Mrs. Petch—last night, around two. I heard something from the church. Was there a service?"

She set the tray down on the hall table. Very carefully. As if the tray were fragile, or as if her hands were.

"No service," she said. "Church hasn't been used at night since my grandmother's day. Whatever you heard, Mr. Okonkwo—" and here she looked at him, and behind the hardness in her face was the grey fear of the parlor, still there, still waiting— "you'd do well to write it down and keep it shut in your book."

"Like the song."

"Like everything," said Mrs. Petch, "in this village."

* * *

The next three days, the snow came down and the village watched him back. He learned the rhythm of it quickly: doors that opened as he passed, curtains that shifted, the inn's conversation that dipped whenever he crossed the threshold and rose again, carefully louder, when he left. They were not unkind. They fed him, greeted him, told him where the shop kept its good cheese. They simply watched him the way a household watches a stranger holding something breakable.

And they sang for him—some of them, cautiously, in exchange for a pint or a pot of tea or the simple pleasure of being listened to. A ploughman's counting song with numbers that skipped from nine to eleven as if ten had never existed. A wassail tune that named every orchard in the parish except the one below the manor. A christening blessing whose last line everyone, everywhere, swallowed—and when he asked old Tam Reeve, ninety if he was a day, what the line was, the old man looked at him with bright, apologetic eyes and said:

"Don't know, lad. Never heard it. My father said his father said the same. It's not that we've forgotten it."

"Then what?"

Tam Reeve turned his cup slowly on its saucer.

"It's that we've *stopped*," he said. "There's a difference, and you'll learn it."

That night, Tamsin laid all his transcriptions side by side on the desk under the lamp, and looked at the gaps. The missing tenth number. The unnamed orchard. The swallowed blessing. The six lines and the silence of Mrs. Petch's lullaby.

They weren't separate omissions. They were the same omission, wearing different songs like coats.

Somewhere under all of it, one longer thing had been taken apart, and every piece in the village had been taught to stop at the same place—and the place they stopped was always just before the dark, just before the downward note, just before whatever came after *sleep*.

Tamsin picked up his pen, and wrote, in his small exact hand, at the bottom of the page:

*They are not forgetting a song. They are keeping one quiet. And tomorrow I mean to find out who taught them the difference.*

Outside, the snow went on falling over Cellar Hollow, over the church and the square and the crooked cottages and the manor above them all with its black windows, and the village lay silent under it, listening, the way it had always listened, for the verse someone, someday, would be foolish enough to finish.
