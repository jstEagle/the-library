## 1. The Appraisal

The county map put Hollowmere House at the end of Vane Road, past the last working farm, past the place where the asphalt gave up and became gravel, past even the gravel, which thinned into a pair of ruts that seemed less like a road than an argument the land was having with itself. Mirabel Thackeray had driven worse. They had appraised houses in towns where the post office doubled as the funeral parlor, where the only traffic light had been out so long it had become a local landmark. They were good at this work precisely because they did not flinch from decay; they catalogued it, priced it, reduced it to line items.

So when the house came into view through the October trees—three stories of gray clapboard gone silver, a steep roof like a folded wing, chimneys standing at odd angles as if leaning in to whisper—Mirabel felt only the familiar professional click of assessment beginning. Slate roof: original, failing. Foundation: fieldstone, likely dry-laid, probable water intrusion. Windows: twelve-over-twelve sashes, half of them clouded with interior condensation, which meant failed seals, which meant money.

They parked on the weedy apron of the drive, killed the engine, and sat for a moment with their hands still on the wheel, doing what they always did before entering a property: running the file in their head. Estate of Aurelia Thackeray, deceased 1974, property held in trust since, court-ordered appraisal pursuant to probate. No living heirs of record. Structure approximately 6,200 square feet per tax records. Last assessed 1989. Contents: unknown, presumed abandoned.

*Presumed.* That word would come back later, though Mirabel didn't know it yet. Presumed was a word you used when nobody had bothered to look.

They got out, slung the camera bag over one shoulder, and took the laser measure from its holster the way a doctor takes a stethoscope. The morning was cold enough to show breath. Somewhere behind the house, crows were making a sound like tearing paper.

The porch was the first thing wrong, though Mirabel wouldn't register it as *wrong* for another hour. It wrapped around two sides of the house, wide-planked fir gone soft and gray, and it sagged in the way old porches do—not dramatically, just a settling, a concession to gravity that any hundred-and-forty-year-old building is entitled to. But there was a step, the second one down, that caught Mirabel's eye because it was worn differently from all the others. Every board on the porch showed weathering: even, uniform, the gray patina of decades of rain and sun applied equally, like paint. Except this one board, which was worn *smooth*, dished in the middle into a shallow pale hollow, the kind of wear you see on church thresholds or the stone steps of castles—the wear of feet, thousands upon thousands of passages, concentrated in one small place.

Mirabel crouched over it, frowning. The rest of the porch said *nobody has walked here in years*. Weeds grew through the joints. Squirrel droppings dotted the rail. But this single board had been polished by use, and recently—the hollow's edges were clean, not crusted with lichen.

People don't wear wood like that in a decade, let alone thirty-five years of vacancy.

"Foot traffic," they murmured, and wrote it down, because writing things down was how Mirabel kept the world in its proper shape. *Step two, anomalous wear pattern, possible prior repair using salvaged material.* There. Explained. Filed. A salvaged board, brought in from some other doorway, already worn, installed here. Perfectly reasonable. They almost believed it.

The front door wasn't locked. This also went into the mental ledger without comment—vacant properties were broken into constantly, and locks failed—but the door swung open on hinges that made no sound at all, and that, Mirabel noticed. Hinges that silent after decades of disuse were either new or oiled, and neither made sense.

Inside, the smell hit them: dust, yes, and mouse, and the green-black breath of damp plaster, but under it something else, something almost pleasant. Beeswax. Old wood smoke. It smelled, faintly and impossibly, *lived-in*.

"Probate appraisal," Mirabel announced to the empty foyer, because they always announced themselves aloud on entries—it was procedure, it covered them legally, and if they were honest, it kept the loneliness of empty houses at arm's length. Their voice fell into the hall and did not echo. That was the thing about the sound in this house: it didn't bounce back. It just went somewhere and stayed.

The foyer was generous, twelve feet square by eye, with a staircase rising ahead in a turn and three doorways branching off. Mirabel started their standard circuit: perimeter first, then room by room, clockwise. Laser measure against the north wall. *Click.* Eleven feet, four inches. South wall. *Click.* Eleven feet, four inches. East, west, ceiling height nine-two. They photographed each wall, shot the trim profiles, noted the plaster damage in the northeast corner where a hairline crack ran floor to ceiling in a long, lazy curve—almost like a seam, almost like the wall had been stitched shut.

Parlor: fourteen-six by sixteen-two. Dining room: thirteen-one by fifteen-eight. Kitchen: eleven-eleven by fourteen-four, with a coal stove and a dry sink and a window over the basin that looked out on the overgrown garden, where something tall and dead stood in a tangle of briar—a trellis, maybe, or a clothesline post, though from this angle it looked uncomfortably like a figure waiting.

Mirabel worked. This was the gift of the trade: the work filled you up. Measure, photograph, note. Measure, photograph, note. The house could be as strange as it liked; every strangeness got converted into data, and data could be priced, and priced things could be managed. By noon they had finished the ground floor and climbed to the second, where the bedrooms opened off a central hall like rooms off a hotel corridor, and here Mirabel began the second pass.

The second pass was procedure too. You measured everything twice; tape measures lied, lasers drifted, and a court document with wrong numbers was a lawsuit waiting to happen. So Mirabel stood in the parlor again at one-fifteen and raised the laser to the north wall, expecting fourteen-six.

*Click.*

Fourteen-nine.

They lowered the device, checked the battery indicator, checked the unit setting. Feet, inches, fine. They re-measured.

Fourteen-nine.

Mirabel stood very still in the middle of the parlor floor. Three inches. Houses settled, but settling didn't add inches to a room between ten a.m. and one p.m.; settling took years and moved walls *inward*, toward collapse, never outward, toward generosity. They walked the perimeter slowly, hand trailing the wallpaper—faded roses, a pattern repeated so many times the flowers had become abstract, a suggestion of gardens rather than gardens themselves—and found no hidden door, no movable panel, no explanation. The room was simply three inches bigger than it had been at breakfast.

They measured the dining room. Thirteen-four by fifteen-eleven. Three inches more in both directions. The kitchen: twelve-two by fourteen-seven. The foyer: twelve-three square. Every room on the ground floor had grown by exactly three inches, uniformly, quietly, while Mirabel had been upstairs photographing bedroom ceilings.

There was a version of Mirabel Thackeray that would have packed the car right then. They knew this about themselves the way you know about a scar—you remember the event that made it. Eight years ago, in a farmhouse outside Corben County, they had found a child's shoe in a sealed chimney flue and had driven home shaking and had not taken a rural assignment for two years afterward. They had learned their own limits. They knew exactly how much wrongness they could metabolize before the professional calm cracked, and three-inch-growing rooms were flirting with the edge of it.

But the court order was in the bag. The fee was already half-paid. And the numbers, at least, were *consistent* now—all fourteen-nines, all matching. Whatever the house was doing, it had done it, and it was holding still. Mirabel told themselves this the way you tell yourself the dog that growled at you is probably chained up.

*Consistent,* they wrote in the margin of the survey sheet, and hated how much the word sounded like a plea.

* * *

They ate lunch in the car, engine running for the heater, and reviewed the photographs from the morning. This was the other ritual: scroll through, check focus, confirm coverage. The images loaded onto the little screen one by one—foyer, parlor, dining room, kitchen—and they were all correct, all ordinary, until Mirabel reached the series shot in the upstairs hallway and stopped scrolling.

In the background of the fourth photograph, at the far end of the hall, in the dimness beyond the reach of the flash, there was a shape on the floor. Small. Pale. Roughly the size and posture of a child sitting cross-legged.

Mirabel scrolled back. Third photo, same angle, taken ninety seconds earlier: empty hallway. Fifth photo, ninety seconds later: empty hallway. Only the fourth frame held the shape, and it was blurred—not motion-blur exactly, but soft-edged, the way things look in dreams, the way things look when the camera can't quite decide they're there.

They zoomed in until the pixels broke apart into mush. The shape resolved into nothing usable: a fold of light, a suggestion of knees, perhaps a head bowed over something in its lap. Drawing, maybe. The posture was unmistakably the posture of a child absorbed in drawing.

Mirabel deleted nothing—that was evidence protocol, though evidence of what, they couldn't have said—and set the camera face-down on the passenger seat, and finished their sandwich without tasting it, and told themselves, in the firm internal voice reserved for panicking clients and their own 3 a.m. doubts, that cameras in low light produce artifacts, that dust motes bloom into shapes, that the human brain is a pattern-matching machine that will find faces in electrical outlets and children in noise.

All true. All comforting. None of it quite landing, because of the porch step, and the silent hinges, and the beeswax smell, and the three inches.

At two o'clock, they went back inside, because the contract required interior measurements of the attic and cellar, and because Mirabel Thackeray had built a whole life on the principle that you finish the survey. You do not leave a job half-done. Half-done surveys are how appraisers get sued, and besides—if you run from every house that gives you a bad feeling, you end up unable to enter any house at all, and then what are you?

The attic stairs pulled down from the ceiling on a counterweighted hinge, and the ladder was solid, and the attic itself was a long low gable smelling of cedar shavings and cold. Mirabel measured it twice. Both times: forty-one feet, six inches. Identical. They noted this with something dangerously close to relief—a room that kept its size!—and was turning to go when they saw, tucked in the eastern gable where the roof sloped nearly to the floor, a small chair.

A child's chair. Wooden, handmade, its seat worn pale in the center. Beside it, on the floorboards, arranged with obvious care, lay a stub of red chalk and a piece of slate no bigger than a saucer, wiped clean.

Nobody had cleaned this attic. Nobody had cleaned anything in this house; the dust lay everywhere in gray velvet, undisturbed except—

Except around the chair. Around the chair, the dust was swept into a neat ring, a clear circle perhaps four feet across, as if someone had tidied it recently, or as if the dust simply declined to settle where a child might sit.

Mirabel photographed it. The flash fired; the image on the screen showed the chair, the chalk, the slate, the circle of clean floor. Then, unbidden, their thumb moved and took a second photograph without the flash, by ambient light through the gable vent.

On the screen, in the second image, the chair was empty and the circle was empty and everything was exactly as it should be, and Mirabel exhaled—right up until they noticed that in the second photograph, the slate was no longer blank. Something was written on it in a wobbly, childish hand, in white chalk lines that had not been visible to the naked eye:

*M i r a b e l*

Eleven letters, spelled correctly, spaced carefully, the way a child spells who has practiced.

Mirabel Thackeray did not run. They descended the attic ladder with steady, deliberate steps, closed the hatch, and stood in the upstairs hall with their heart going like a fist on a door, and they did the only thing their profession had ever taught them to do with the unbearable.

They wrote it down.

*Attic: child's chair, chalk artifacts, anomalous inscription on slate (subject name). Recommend further investigation.*

Then they looked at what they'd written, and understood, with a cold spreading clarity, that *recommend further investigation* was a sentence you wrote about mold and wiring, and that they had just filed their own haunting as a line item, and that the filing had not helped at all, because downstairs, faint but unmistakable, rising through the floorboards like heat, someone—or something—had begun to hum.

It was a tune Mirabel almost knew. It sat at the edge of recognition the way a name sits on the tip of the tongue, and Mirabel stood in the empty upstairs hall of Hollowmere House, hugging the survey clipboard to their chest like armor, and thought: *I have been here before. I just don't remember when.*

Outside, the afternoon light was starting to fail, and the porch step waited, worn smooth, for feet that were coming back down those stairs whether Mirabel liked it or not.
