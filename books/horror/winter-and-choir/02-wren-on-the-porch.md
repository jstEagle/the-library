## 2. Wren on the Porch

The porch step was wrong.

Mirabel had catalogued it twice already—once with the laser measure, once with the folding rule, because the numbers had disagreed and Mirabel trusted the rule the way some people trusted scripture. The step's tread was worn into a shallow crescent, a hollow polished by feet, and that was the wrongness, because the house had stood empty for eleven years and before that it had belonged to an estate sale and before that to a woman named Pell who had lived alone and driven nowhere and walked, according to her doctor, only as far as the mailbox.

Nobody had worn that step. And yet the wood was pale in the center of the hollow, pale as bone worn smooth by a thumb.

Mirabel wrote *tread wear, origin unclear* in the survey ledger, and the pen dragged a little on the second word, and they told themselves it was the cold.

October had come down on Hollowmere the way fog comes down on water—not arriving so much as being revealed, as though it had always been there and the morning had simply gotten honest enough to show it. The maples along the drive had gone the color of old bruises. Somewhere behind the house, a crow said one syllable and did not repeat itself.

Mirabel stood on the lawn with their clipboard and their breath making small ghosts, and they did not look at the porch swing.

They looked at it anyway.

* * *

Wren was sitting on the swing the way Wren had always sat on things: sideways, knees up, one shoe dangling, as though gravity were a rumor he'd heard but hadn't confirmed. He was small. He was seven years old, or he had the shape of seven years old—corduroy pants gone soft at the knees, a sweater with one button missing, hair the brown of river stones, and that same gap between his front teeth that had made his whistle sound like a leak in the world.

Thirteen years. Mirabel had been twenty-two when they'd last stood on this lawn, twenty-two and embarrassed at themselves for standing there, for the half-second where their eyes had gone to the empty swing out of habit. Thirteen years, and the boy on the swing had not gained an inch or lost a button. His corduroys had not faded. The missing button had not been replaced, because of course it hadn't; it had never been there to begin with, because Wren had never been there to begin with, because Mirabel Thackeray was a grown adult with a license and a ledger and a reputation for exactness, and imaginary friends did not wait on porches for over a decade like dogs who had been told to stay.

"Hello, Mira-bell."

He said it the old way. Not *Mirabel*, not the way teachers had said it, careful and clinical, and not the way their mother had slurred it in her last good year. He said it in three notes, *Mira-bell*, the second one rising, the third one falling like a small bell being set down gently on a table. Nobody living used that name. Nobody living had *ever* used that name, because Mirabel had invented it in the summer they turned seven and had never spoken it aloud to another soul, had never written it down, had buried it so deep in the childhood they didn't talk about that some days they doubted it themselves.

The clipboard did not fall out of Mirabel's hands, though it wanted to. The hands wanted many things. The hands wanted to run, and the hands wanted to reach, and the terrible thing—the thing Mirabel would lie awake cataloguing for nights afterward—was that both wants had the same weight.

"You're not real," Mirabel said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them, which felt like a betrayal of how much of them was screaming.

Wren considered this with the seriousness he'd once given to the question of whether the creek froze from the edges in or the middle out. "You measured the step wrong," he said. "It's worn on the left side more. You wrote it down like it's even."

"That's not—you were watching me?"

"I always watch you." He said it the way children say *I always brush my teeth*, a fact with no weight in it, a fact that had never once occurred to him could be frightening. "You got tall. I knew you would but you got *tall*-tall." He swung his dangling shoe. "Your hair's different. You look like someone who sleeps with the light on."

"I don't sleep with the light on."

"You do," Wren said kindly. "That's all right. I would too, if I were you."

The crow behind the house said its one syllable again, and this time it sounded less like a crow and more like a person imitating a crow badly, and Mirabel decided firmly, professionally, that they had not heard it.

They walked backward toward the car. It was not a dignified retreat; dignity had left the property some minutes ago, probably around the time Mirabel's own mouth had answered the boy instead of screaming. Backward, one hand out behind them like a man feeling for a wall in the dark, eyes never leaving the porch, because some animal part of them was certain that the moment they looked away, Wren would be *closer*—and another animal part, deeper and older and seven years old, was certain of the opposite: that the moment they looked away, Wren would be *gone again*, and that this would be worse.

"Where did you go?" Wren asked, and his voice did something then, a small fracture in the middle of it. "That summer. You just stopped coming. I waited and I waited and then the people came and took all the things out, and I thought—maybe you got lost. People get lost. I got lost once, I think. I don't remember where."

"I didn't go anywhere," Mirabel heard themselves say. "You went. You were—" *Imaginary. A game. A symptom, my mother said, a phase, my father said, a grief-response, said the school counselor with her kind worried voice, and Mirabel at nine had learned to say "I know he's not real" in the exact tone that made adults stop writing things down.* "You stopped."

"I'm not allowed off the porch," Wren said. He said it simply, the way he'd once reported that the pantry smelled like rain. "I tried. There's a line. You can't see it but it's there, it's like the edge of a pool. I put my foot over it once and the whole house went—" He searched for the word, and Mirabel watched a seven-year-old's face try to hold something that no seven-year-old should have a word for. "—*loud*," he finished. "So I don't do that anymore."

The car was behind Mirabel now. They felt the bumper against their palm, cold metal, real metal, the most reassuring thing they had touched all day. They got in. They locked the doors, all four, in sequence, an old ritual they had not performed since childhood, since the summer of the thing under the bed, and the familiarity of it made their stomach drop, because the hands remembered even when the mouth denied.

The key turned. The engine made a sound like a held breath and did not catch.

"Come on," Mirabel whispered. "Come *on*."

They tried again. The engine turned over, turned over, turned over, a sound like someone clearing their throat before a difficult conversation, and then stopped, and the dashboard stayed dark, and the radio, which had been playing nothing anyway, played less than nothing.

Through the windshield, Wren had come to the edge of the porch. Not off it. He stood at the top of the steps with his toes right at the lip of the boards, and he did not cross, and he did not try to cross, and something in the way he stood—hands behind his back, weight on his heels, a boy keeping a rule he had learned the hard way—was so precisely, so *unbearably* the way he had stood at the edge of the creek the summer Mirabel's mother left, waiting to see if Mirabel would come in after him.

"You have to say it," Wren called. His voice carried across the dead lawn without effort, without echo, as though the air between them had been tuned for it. "It's rude to leave without saying it. You taught me that. You said a hello is a door and a goodbye is a door and you have to close the ones you open."

"I didn't teach you that."

"You did. August. The lightning bugs were out. You said—" and he recited it, word for word, in Mirabel's own seven-year-old voice, a voice Mirabel did not have anymore and had not heard in thirty years, and hearing it come out of the boy's mouth was like hearing a recording of their own heartbeat. "*A hello is a door, Wren. You always have to open it back.*"

The engine turned over again, that same throat-clearing, and died.

Mirabel sat in the cold car with their breath fogging the glass and understood, with the slow horrible clarity that comes to appraisers and to the appraised, what the car wanted. What the house wanted. What the worn step and the dark dashboard and the boy at the edge of the porch were all, in their different grammars, saying the same sentence.

*Say hello. Open the door.*

"It's a trick," Mirabel said aloud, to the windshield, to the steering wheel, to whatever part of themselves was still taking minutes. "It's a hallucination. Stress. The estate. You're tired, you didn't sleep, the motel had that smell—" The arguments lined up like little soldiers and stood at attention and were shot down one by one by the simple fact of the gap between his teeth, the specific gap, the one Mirabel had invented, had *chosen*, on an afternoon in July, because they had decided their friend should have a flaw so he wouldn't be too perfect, and no hallucination of stress could reproduce a design decision made by a seven-year-old.

Wren was still waiting. He had sat back down on the swing, patient as furniture, and the swing did not move, and Mirabel realized with a lurch that the chains were not creaking because the boy was not actually swinging it, because the boy might not actually have weight, and that thought opened under them like a stair in the dark.

The tenderness was the worst part. That was the thing no one warned you about hauntings, if this was one: not the terror, or not only the terror, but the way terror and love could occupy the same chest at the same time and neither one would yield the room. Mirabel looked at the small shape on the swing and felt the old love rise up in them whole and undimmed, thirteen years of it, unaged, exactly as fresh as the boy himself, and it was the most frightening feeling they had ever had, because it meant some door inside them had never been closed either.

Mirabel turned the key one more time. The engine cleared its throat.

"Hello, Wren," Mirabel said.

They said it quietly, through the closed window, but the word did not behave like sound. It went out of them like something *let out*, like a bird from a hand, and the air between the car and the porch seemed to lean to receive it.

The engine caught on the next turn—no, on the *same* turn, still turning, the held breath releasing into a low, even, idling hum, and the dashboard lights came up all at once, and the radio said, mid-sentence, in a warm announcer's voice, "—and a fine harvest moon expected this month, folks, one of the red ones," and then static, and then nothing.

On the porch, Wren lit up. There was no other word for it. The boy glowed the way children glow, from some inner furnace, and he pressed both hands to his mouth and then took them away and called out:

"You came back! You said hello! Mira-bell, you said—" and then he stopped, and the glow flickered like a lamp in a draft, and his face did the thing that faces do when they remember something they weren't supposed to know yet. He looked past Mirabel. He looked at the house.

Mirabel looked too.

The front door of Hollowmere House was open. It had been locked—Mirabel had the key on their own ring, had locked it behind them an hour ago, had photographed the lock for the file. It stood open now by perhaps a foot, and the hallway beyond it was dark, and the darkness was not empty the way darkness is usually empty. It had texture. It had *attention*.

"That happens," Wren said, in a small voice. "It opens when it's happy."

Mirabel put the car in gear. The wheels turned. The gravel crunched, real and ordinary and sane, and in the rearview mirror the boy on the porch shrank and shrank and did not disappear, which was the problem, because imaginary friends were supposed to disappear, they were supposed to dissolve at the property line, at the edge of the dream, at the first honest glance from a grown-up's eyes. Wren did not dissolve. Wren watched the car go with one hand raised, and he did not wave, because he was still standing at the top of the steps, still not crossing, and his stillness had the quality of a rule being kept under great strain.

At the end of the drive, where the lane met the county road, Mirabel stopped the car and sat with both hands on the wheel and made themselves think like a professional, because the professional was the only self they had left.

Item: the boy matched a design from 1989 down to a deliberate imperfection.

Item: the vehicle's failure correlated exactly with a spoken social ritual, and resolved upon its completion. That was not how engines worked. That was not how *anything* worked.

Item: the house had opened its own door. Houses did not have keys. Houses did not have moods. And yet the word that had come to mind—unbidden, unwelcome, in their mother's voice, in *Wren's* voice, in a chorus of small voices they had spent thirty years not listening to—was not *haunted*. Haunted meant the past lingering, the dead rattling their old cups. This was not lingering. This was *waiting*. This was a house that had been holding a place at a table for thirteen years and had finally heard footsteps on the road.

Item: the contract. Mirabel pulled it from the folder on the passenger seat, the court-ordered appraisal, probate exhibit C, and read the clause they had skimmed in the county office because it was boilerplate, everyone said it was boilerplate: *The appraiser shall complete a full interior survey, including all rooms, in a continuous engagement, prior to the final filing date.*

*All rooms.* Including all rooms. As though the number of rooms were not a thing a house could have opinions about.

Mirabel looked at the folder. Inside, under the contract, was the survey ledger, and under the ledger was the site map, and the site map was a clean rectangle of rooms drawn in their own precise hand this morning, and Mirabel looked at it for a long moment before admitting what was wrong with it, which was that it felt incomplete. Not inaccurate—incomplete. The way a sentence feels incomplete. The way a name feels incomplete when you've only heard part of it.

The house had a room in it that Mirabel had not measured. Mirabel was certain of this the way you are certain of a word on the tip of the tongue. And behind the certainty, faint as a radio through a wall, something was humming.

Mirabel put the car in gear and drove back up the lane, because the contract said *all rooms*, because the court said *complete the survey*, because the engine had started when they said hello and they did not want to learn what it would do if they tried to say goodbye.

Behind them, in the mirror, Wren stood up on the porch and pressed his small hand flat against the air, against the invisible line he could not cross, and held it there, the way you hold your palm to glass.

The front door of the house stood wider now.

It had not been opened by wind.
