## 19. Loyalty vs. Truth

Sleep did not come. It circled the room like something on a leash, sniffing at the doorframe, and then it left.

Mirabel lay in the east bedroom—the one that had not existed on any blueprint until three nights ago—staring at a ceiling that breathed. Not visibly. Not enough that they could point to it and say *there, look, the plaster swells and falls*. But they could feel it, the way you can feel a sleeping animal beside you in the dark: the slow displacement of air, the warmth rising off surfaces that should have been cold, the sense of an enormous attention turned inward, digesting.

The hymnal lay under their pillow. They had moved it there from the coat lining two hours ago because they kept imagining the house finding it, and somehow against their sternum felt safer than inside a seam of wool. It was a child's logic. The house was already inside everything. It was inside the walls and the well water and the particular way the dark here had texture, like felt. Putting a book under a pillow would no more hide it from Hollowmere than hiding a coin from God.

But they kept it there anyway.

Below them—two floors down, behind the false wall in the basement that still smelled of lime and old blood when they pressed their ear to it—nine chairs waited in their half-circle. Nine chairs, nine objects, nine names in a ledger written by men who believed harmony was something you could build, the way you built a barn or a fence line, post by post, voice by voice. And one chair empty. One chair with no object at all, only dust shaped faintly like waiting.

*The tenth voice shall be given freely.*

Mirabel turned the phrase over for the thousandth time, and for the thousandth time it turned back over on its own, showing its underside: *given*. Not taken. Not lured. Given. The ledger's grammar was precise in the way of people who have thought hard about a terrible thing and decided to be honest about at least one part of it. Whatever happened in this house—and Mirabel now believed things had been happening here for a hundred and forty years—it required consent. It had always required consent. That was the horror of it, the part that made their teeth ache: not that the house stole voices, but that it asked, patiently, generation after generation, and someone always said yes.

And tonight, lying awake in a bed that fit them too well, in a room that had rearranged itself around their childhood the way water rearranges around a dropped stone, Mirabel Thackeray understood—with the flat, cold clarity that comes only after midnight has stopped pretending to be early—that they were being asked.

Not yet. Not in words. But the question was being assembled somewhere below them, plank by plank, note by note, and it would be finished by moonrise, and it would be addressed to them, and every night they spent under this roof without leaving was already a kind of answer.

* * *

They got up because lying still had become unbearable, and because Wren was on the porch.

That was the other thing. Every night since the counter-verses had begun to take shape in Mirabel's mind, Wren came to the porch at some hour past counting and sat on the top step, knees drawn up, watching the yard go silver and then black. Wren could not come inside. Thirteen years on that threshold and never once across it—not until the house had briefly permitted it, recently, for reasons Mirabel did not want to examine—but the porch was theirs, worn smooth by small feet that had never grown, and every night Wren sat there like a sentinel made of patience.

Mirabel found them exactly where expected. Moonlight came through the elms in broken coins, and Wren sat in the middle of all that scattered silver with their chin on their knees, humming.

Mirabel stopped dead on the threshold.

It was the lullaby. Their mother's lullaby—the one from chapter six of this nightmare, the one that had risen out of the walls while Mirabel lay rigid in the dark, harmonized by voices that had no business knowing it, because their mother had never lived here, had never, as far as Mirabel knew, set foot in this county. And now Wren was humming it, softly, the way you hum something you've known so long you've forgotten learning it.

"Wren." Their voice came out rougher than intended. "Where did you learn that song?"

Wren looked up. In the moonlight their face was the same face from thirteen years ago—round-cheeked, gap-toothed, ageless in the specific way of things that are not permitted to change—and for a moment something flickered across it, something older than the face, something tired.

"I don't know," Wren said simply. "I know lots of songs I don't remember learning. Don't you?"

"No."

"Maybe you do and you just don't remember not remembering." Wren scooted sideways on the step, making room—an invitation thirteen years old, offered ten thousand times, never once refused until now. "Sit. You look like a folded-up letter."

Mirabel sat. The wood was warm beneath them, which was wrong; October nights in Hollowmere bit through coats, and the porch boards should have been cold as pond ice. Everything about this house ran a few degrees warmer than it should, like a body running a fever it refused to admit.

For a long time neither of them spoke. A wind moved through the elms and the coins of light slid across the yard.

"I can't do it," Mirabel said finally. It came out before they'd decided to say it, which was how the truest things always arrived. "I can't stop thinking about it. The choice."

Wren didn't ask what choice. Of course they didn't. Wren had read the hymnal over Mirabel's shoulder for three evenings, following the marginalia with a finger, asking questions a child shouldn't have been able to ask—*what does 'unmake' mean, does it mean die, is dying the same as unmaking*—and Mirabel had answered each one honestly, because lying to Wren had begun to feel like the one sin left that mattered.

"The counter-song," Wren said now, quietly. "If you sing it, everyone goes free. Edith's sister. All the ones in the walls. Even—" a pause, very small, "—even me."

"Even you."

"And if I don't sing it—if we stay quiet—then everyone stays. Kept. Singing in the dark forever. And eventually you get tired, or lonely, or the house makes you an offer you can't refuse, and then you sit in the tenth chair, and then it's just me out here on the porch again. Except this time nobody's coming back." Wren said it all without drama, the way children recite multiplication tables, and that was the worst part. Wren had done the arithmetic. Wren had been doing it for days.

"Yes," Mirabel said. "That's the shape of it."

"So don't sing it."

Mirabel turned. "What?"

"Don't sing it." Wren was looking at the yard, not at them. "Stay quiet. Stay with me. We'll figure something else out. There's eleven days. Maybe we find another way. Maybe we just leave and the house gets bored. Houses get bored, probably. Probably."

Every word of it was nonsense and both of them knew it. The roads looped. The contract bore a signature Mirabel didn't remember giving. Edith Vane had vanished into her own kitchen two nights ago, leaving two warm teacups and a silence where her sister's singing used to be—a silence that was worse than the singing, so much worse that Mirabel woke up listening for it. Eleven days was not a reprieve. Eleven days was a countdown wearing a kind face.

But Wren was asking. Wren, who had waited thirteen years on a porch step for a friend who had driven away and not come back, was asking them to stay quiet, and Mirabel felt the request go into them like a splinter, working deeper with every heartbeat.

"Wren." They made themselves say it plainly. Made themselves not soften it. "If I stay silent, I'm choosing the house. Maybe not today. Maybe not tomorrow. But silence is how it survived a century. Do you understand? Nobody had to say yes out loud. They just had to not say no. Generation after generation of people who heard the humming and pulled the blankets up and told themselves it was pipes. That's the whole trick. That's the entire architecture. It isn't built of wood. It's built of people deciding not to make a fuss."

Wren's hands had gone still in their lap.

"I know," Wren said.

"You know?"

"I've had thirteen years to think about what keeping means." Now Wren looked at them, and the moonlight did something unkind to their face, hollowing the cheeks, aging the eyes. "I'm kept, Mirabel. I'm the most kept thing here. I stand on this step and I wait and I can't cross the line and I can't remember anywhere else, and every year you didn't come back, I told myself you would, and that was—I think that was my version of pulling the blanket up. Hoping is how you stay quiet without noticing you're doing it." A breath. "So I know better than anyone what you're describing. I've been living inside it."

The words landed in Mirabel's chest and stayed there, heavy as stones dropped into a well.

"I won't let it keep you," they said. "Whatever it is—whatever you are—I won't."

"But if you sing the song, you might not have a choice. The book says the kept voices go free. Free could mean gone." Wren's voice cracked on *gone*, just slightly, a hairline fracture in the child-voice, and underneath it Mirabel heard—briefly, terribly—something older. A woman's timbre. Aurelia's ghost living in the throat of the thing wearing Wren's shape. "You'd be trading me for everybody. That's the trade. Say it plain."

"It's not a trade. It's not currency. You're not—"

"Say it plain," Wren repeated, and the older voice was stronger now, and Mirabel realized Wren was doing it on purpose, borrowing Aurelia's gravity because a child's pleading couldn't carry this weight. "One voice for many. That's the exchange rate. I did the math first, remember? I'm allowed to name it even if you're not."

Mirabel looked out at the dark yard. The elms stood like witnesses. Somewhere far off, down toward the creek, a bird made a sound that was almost a word and gave up.

"Yes," they said. "That's the trade. If it comes to it. If the song works the way the margins say it works."

"Okay." Wren nodded slowly. "Okay. Then here's my answer, and I need you to actually hear it, because I've been practicing." They took a breath, and when they spoke again the child's voice was back, but steadied, braced, like a small boat that had decided on its heading. "Free me anyway. If it comes to that. Don't you dare keep me quiet to keep me. I'd rather be true and gone than kept and comfortable. I've been comfortable for thirteen years and it's the worst thing I can imagine, now that I know there's another word for it."

Mirabel's throat closed. They reached out and took Wren's hand—small, cool, solid as anything real—and Wren gripped back with a strength that had no business fitting in those fingers.

"This is why it asks," Mirabel said, mostly to themselves. "This is why it needs consent. Because consent given by someone who loves you is worth more than consent taken from someone who doesn't. It wants you to agree to give me up, or me to agree to give up you. Either way it gets fed on the love itself. That's the meal. That's what it's been farming all along."

"That's horrible," Wren said, with admiration.

"It's worse than horrible. It's patient." Mirabel squeezed the small hand once and let go. "A century of patience. And you know what the awful thing is, Wren? Tonight, for about four minutes, between two and three o'clock, I wanted to say yes to it. Not to the chair—to the silence. I wanted to fold the hymnal shut and sit here with you until the sun came up and pretend the rest of it wasn't real. Four minutes. I timed it by the clock in the hall. And in those four minutes I was happier than I've been since I was seven years old."

Wren considered this with grave attention.

"Four minutes isn't a decision," they said at last. "Four minutes is just wanting. Everybody wants. The house counts on everybody wanting. But wanting isn't signing." A pause, and then, softer: "Is it?"

Mirabel thought of the contract in their bag with the signature they didn't remember giving. Thought of a seven-year-old in this same yard, some summer night decades ago, holding a pencil in a fist too small for it.

"No," they said, and heard the doubt in their own voice, and hated it. "Wanting isn't signing."

* * *

Wren faded with the deepest part of the night, the way they always did—not leaving, exactly, but thinning, going translucent at the edges like paper held to a flame, until the porch held nothing but moonlight and the memory of a small weight on the boards. Mirabel stayed a while longer anyway, because leaving the porch felt like another small silence, another blanket pulled up.

Then they went inside, and the house was waiting.

Not obviously. Nothing so crude as a voice or a vision. Just—the hallway had changed again. The clock that had hung beside the stairs for as long as Mirabel had been trapped here was gone, and in its place hung a mirror, tall and dark-framed, that had not been there at dusk. Mirabel knew every object in this house now, had inventoried them twice, had measured the halls to the eighth of an inch. There was no mirror in the inventory.

They approached it the way you approach a dog of unknown temper.

The glass showed the hallway, the stairs, themselves—rumpled, gray-faced, three days unwashed. Ordinary. Except that the reflection's mouth was moving, slightly, out of rhythm with their own, shaping words they couldn't hear. And except that behind the reflected Mirabel, at the top of the stairs, stood a figure in old-fashioned clothes—a woman, tall, dark-haired, her face turned away—waiting, with the posture of someone who has been waiting a very long time and has grown excellent at it.

Mirabel spun. The stairs were empty.

They turned back to the mirror. The woman was closer now—or rather, she had turned her head, though her body hadn't moved, and the face coming around into view was a face Mirabel knew from a locket in a basement chair, from a photograph in a county archive, from a hidden room full of refused writing.

Aurelia Thackeray smiled at them through the glass. Her lips moved, and this time—this once—the sound came through, faint and clear as a voice carried up a well:

"There are things you should know before you decide," she said. "Come downstairs. I'll tell you what your mother wouldn't."

The mirror went dark, and somewhere below, behind the false wall, nine chairs scraped gently against stone—as if settling in to listen.
