## 1. The Counting Ward

The night shift at Rosewood Hospice began, as it always did, with Marguerite Iversen sharpening her pencil.

Not a pen. Pens were permanent, and permanence was a kind of arrogance. A pencil could be corrected. A pencil admitted, in its soft graphite way, that a person might write something down and later learn it was wrong, or incomplete, or true in a way they hadn't yet earned. Marguerite had been keeping her journals in pencil for thirty-one years, and she had never once trusted ink.

She sat in the nurses' station at 11:47 p.m. with the leather journal open on her knees—journal number eleven, its spine cracked to softness, its cover gone the color of weak tea—and she waited for the building to settle. Rosewood settled the way old bodies settle: a groan in the heating ducts, a tick in the ice machine, the elevator sighing down to a floor where no one had called it. The hallway lights were dimmed to their night setting, amber pools spaced at intervals like stations of some private cross, and beyond the windows the October dark lay against the glass the way a hand lies against a cheek.

Room 9 was where Alma Brandt lay dying.

Not dying quickly. Not dying the way the pamphlets said people died, with stages and closures and dignified goodbyes. Alma was dying the way a clock dies, which is to say she was still keeping time, only backward.

Marguerite had first noticed it on Tuesday. She'd been doing her rounds, checking the morphine pump, and she'd paused in the doorway because Alma's lips were moving. This was not unusual. The dying talked to people who weren't there, to mothers and husbands and the children they'd lost or never had. Marguerite had learned to hear it as weather. But Alma wasn't talking. Alma was counting.

"Ninety," she said. Then, after a pause long enough that Marguerite thought she'd finished: "Eighty-nine."

Marguerite had stood in that doorway for eleven minutes, her hand resting on the doorframe, listening to an eighty-six-year-old woman with metastatic cancer count backward from ninety with the unhurried certainty of a metronome. And here was the thing—the thing that had lifted the hair on Marguerite's arms and made her feel, for the first time in her professional life, like an intruder in a room she was paid to enter:

Alma did not breathe.

Not between the numbers. Not during them. Marguerite had watched her chest. She had leaned close enough to smell the lavender lotion she applied to Alma's hands each evening, had watched for the flutter and fall of the sternum, the small labor of ribs that even the dying cannot escape. Alma's chest did not move. Her lips parted, her voice came—thin, papery, but *there*—and her lungs lay still as two folded maps in a drawer.

"Ninety," she'd said. "Eighty-nine." No breath. "Eighty-eight."

Marguerite had gone back to the station, sat down, opened journal eleven to a fresh page, and written:

*Oct. 14, 11:52 p.m. — Rm 9, Alma Brandt, 86. Counting backward from 90. No respiration observed between or during numbers. Voice present. Chest still. Counted to 84 before I left the doorway. She did not pause. She did not breathe.*

Then she had sat with what she'd written, the way she always sat with what she wrote, letting the words do their work. This was the ritual. This was the whole of her religion, though no one at Rosewood knew it and no one ever would: that a thing written down was a thing held still. That the page was a jar with a lid. That if you could describe the terrible thing precisely enough—its number, its hour, its temperature, the exact quality of its wrongness—you could fold it flat and file it among the other terrible things, and it would stay where you put it.

Her mother had taught her the opposite lesson, in the way mothers teach their worst lessons, which is by example. Her mother had kept nothing. Had written nothing. Had let the bad year—the well year, though Marguerite's memory of that year was a hallway with a door at the end that her mind simply declined to open—exist only in the body, where it grew like a root through a foundation. Her mother had died at fifty-three with her fists clenched around nothing at all.

Marguerite had decided, at nine years old, that she would not be her mother. She had begged a leather journal off her father that Christmas—*for my observations, Daddy, I'm going to be a scientist*—and she had filled it by the following winter, and she had filled ten more since. Eleven journals in a hatbox under her bed, wrapped in a pillowcase, each one a year of the world pinned like moths to a board. The dying counted backward and Marguerite wrote it down, and between the two of them—the counter and the keeper—the night stayed where the night belonged.

* * *

At 12:20 a.m., she went back to Room 9.

Alma's room was the last one on the left, and it had been decorated by her daughter with the desperate cheerfulness of the bereaved-in-advance: a silk poinsettia though it was October, a crocheted blanket the color of margarine, photographs on the dresser of a woman who had once been round-faced and laughing and was now folded into the bed like a letter someone had given up trying to read. Alma weighed perhaps eighty pounds. Her hands on the coverlet were elegant still, the hands of a woman who had played piano, though no one had visited who would know that.

Marguerite checked the pump first. Morphine at the ordered rate, the little green light steady, everything as it should be. Then she stood at the bedside, and Alma's eyes opened.

They were pale eyes, nearly colorless, and they found Marguerite with a directness that the dying usually saved for ceilings.

"You're the writer," Alma said.

Marguerite's hand went, without her permission, to the pocket where the journal was not. She kept it in the station. "I'm your night nurse," she said. "Marguerite."

"I know who you are." Alma's voice was a dry leaf of a voice, but there was iron under it, old iron, the kind that outlasts the sword it made. "My grandmother was a writer too. Of a kind." Her eyes went to the window, where the dark pressed. "You keep the books."

"I keep notes," Marguerite said carefully. "About your care."

Alma smiled. It was not a comforting smile. "That's what they all called it. Care." She turned her head on the pillow with the slow grinding effort of a rusted hinge, and her colorless eyes came back and pinned Marguerite in place. "Ninety," she said. "Eighty-nine. Eighty-eight." Her chest did not move. "You've heard it. Don't lie to me, girl. I can see it on you. You've got the listening face."

"The listening face."

"My grandmother had it too." Alma's hand crept out from under the blanket, and Marguerite, trained by a thousand nights into reflex, took it. The skin was cool and fine as paper. "She used to say the counting starts when the door gets thin. Ninety is when it starts. She said—" and here Alma stopped, and something moved behind her face like a fish under ice, and for a moment Marguerite thought she had lost her, thought the count had taken her somewhere with no numbers in it. Then Alma's grip tightened on Marguerite's hand with a strength that had no business living in that arm.

"She said the song keeps it. Not the book. The *song*. The book is just—" Alma's breath caught, an ordinary human breath, the first Marguerite had heard her take all night, and she spent it on a word: "—*notes*."

"What song?" Marguerite asked, and heard her own voice come out level, professional, and knew that Alma heard the lie in it, the same way you can hear a hollow wall if you knock.

But Alma had already turned away. Her eyes had gone to the ceiling, to whatever appointment she was keeping up there, and her lips began to move again, and the numbers came, thin and papery and unhurried, and her chest lay still beneath the crocheted blanket like a frozen lake.

Marguerite stood there holding her hand until the count reached seventy-one, and then she let go, and walked back down the amber hallway on legs that felt borrowed, and sat down at the station, and opened journal eleven, and wrote:

*Oct. 14, 12:41 a.m. — Alma awake, lucid. Said her grandmother was "a keeper of the books." Said "the song keeps it, not the book." Would not say what song. Would not say what "it" is. Chest motion absent during counting. Counted 90→71 during my presence. No respiration.*

Then, beneath it, in smaller letters, the way she wrote the things she did not want to read later but could not bear not to:

*I have heard that song. I don't know where. I don't know when. There is a year I cannot open and a name on the tip of my tongue that starts with W, and when Alma counted just now, some part of me was counting with her, and that part already knew the next number.*

She looked at that last sentence for a long time. The pencil hovered. Then, because the ritual was the ritual and the ritual was all she had, she did not erase it. She closed the journal. She laced the pencil through the loop in the spine. She sat in the amber light with her hands flat on the cover, the way you rest your hands on a chest that is still breathing, and she felt the building settle around her, groan and tick and sigh.

* * *

At 3 a.m., the count reached forty.

Marguerite knew this without checking, the way you know a storm has changed direction from the smell of the air. She had been charting—real charting, the institutional kind, the kind that kept the state satisfied—and the knowledge simply arrived in her, cold and complete: *forty*. She set down the pen. She walked the hallway. Room 9's door was ajar, and through the gap came Alma's voice, steady as a drip of water in a cave.

"Forty. Thirty-nine. Thirty-eight."

Marguerite stood in the doorway and did not go in. There was a rule forming in her, some new law of her own body, that she should not be in the room when the numbers got small. She did not know where the law came from. It felt old. It felt like it had been waiting in her for thirty years for a night exactly like this one, and now it stood up inside her and made itself known: *do not go in when the numbers get small.*

"Thirty-six," said Alma. "Thirty-five."

"Alma," Marguerite said, and her voice sounded strange to her, too soft, the voice of a much younger woman, a girl on a porch at dusk. "Can you hear me?"

"Thirty-three." A pause. The first pause in three nights. "Thirty-two." Another pause, and then, without turning her head, without moving anything but her mouth, Alma said: "Don't let me finish it."

"Finish what? The count?"

"Don't let me finish." Now Alma's hand came up off the blanket, reaching, and Marguerite made herself cross the room and take it, made herself ignore the law standing in her chest with its arms folded, because the patient came first, the patient had always come first, that was the whole architecture of her life. "My grandmother finished hers," Alma whispered. "She was found in the garden with her mouth full of dirt and the last number in her teeth. Nobody ever knew which number. Nobody wrote it down." Her colorless eyes rolled toward Marguerite, and there was terror in them, genuine, animal, the terror of a woman who had been afraid of one specific thing for eighty years and had finally, at the end, been introduced to it. "You keep the books. Write down where I stop. Promise me. Write it down so it stays stopped."

"Alma—"

"*Promise me.*"

"I promise," Marguerite said, and felt the words go into her like a hook going into a fish, clean and deep and meant.

Alma smiled then, a real smile, and closed her eyes, and her hand went slack in Marguerite's, and the numbers resumed, softer now, almost content: "Thirty. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight."

Marguerite wrote it down at the station with a hand that was almost steady.

*Oct. 15, 3:12 a.m. — Patient request: document where the count stops. Promise given. Grandmother "finished her count" — found dead, garden, "last number in her teeth." Unknown number. Patient fears finishing. Chest still during count. Counted 40→28.*

She read the entry twice. Then she added the line she would think about, later, at dawn, on her porch, when everything she believed about the shape of her own life had already begun to come apart:

*She is counting toward something. Not down. Toward.*

* * *

At 5:40 a.m., the sky over the parking lot went the gray of old dishwater, and the count reached twelve.

Marguerite stood at the station window with her coat over her arm, watching the light come up thin and colorless over the sycamores, and she listened to the numbers come down the hallway like footsteps on stairs. She had not slept. She would not sleep. There was a shift change at six and a handoff report to write and a promise in her chest with a hook in it, and she stood at the window and waited for eleven, and ten, and nine.

They came. They came in Alma's papery voice, down the hall, through the closed door, through the wall, through the window glass, through the dishwater sky, and Marguerite stood with her hand pressed flat against her sternum as though she could hold something in, some door in her, some hallway with a room at the end she had not opened since she was nine years old.

"Eight," said Alma Brandt, alone in her room, in the gray light, in the last hour of the last night. "Seven."

Marguerite opened journal eleven one more time. The page was nearly full. She wrote the numbers as they came, in her small careful hand, each one pinned to the page like a moth, and beneath them, as the sky went from dishwater to pearl, she wrote the last thing she would write before she went home to her house and her porch and the morning that was waiting there for her with something small and unchanged standing on it:

*Oct. 15, 5:47 a.m. — Count at 6. She is counting toward something. I promised to write where it stops. I keep the books. I have kept eleven of them. I have kept everything. I have kept it all my life, and I do not know why, and this morning I understood that I have never once asked.*

*The count is at 5. I am going home now.*

*Something is waiting for me to stop writing.*
