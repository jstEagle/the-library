## 1. The Appraisal

The house had been waiting for him longer than he'd been willing to admit.

Rafael Iversen knew this because he'd done the math in the car, somewhere past the second cattle grid on the county road: forty-one years since his grandmother had last invited anyone from the family out here, twenty-three years since he himself had stood on this porch as a boy with grass-stained knees and a soda he wasn't allowed to finish. The house had sat through all of it—through the probate filings, the lawyer's letters, the three years of silence after Grandma Edith's stroke and the eleven months of silence after her death—with the patience of a thing that had never once needed to hurry.

He parked the surveyor's van at an angle that said *temporary*, though nothing about today was temporary. Clipboard. Laser measure. Tape measure with the cracked housing he refused to replace because it fit his hand. Two pens, because one pen was amateur hour. He was a property appraiser with nineteen years of experience and a reputation among estate attorneys for reports so clean they were practically notarized twice. He did not get rattled by old houses. Old houses were inventory.

The porch boards announced him in a language of creaks that felt, absurdly, like recognition.

"Right," he said to no one, and let himself in with the key the lawyer had mailed him, still in its envelope, still smelling faintly of the firm's toner.

* * *

The front hall smelled of cedar and cold ash and something underneath both—a sweetness gone slightly wrong, like fruit remembered rather than tasted. Dust hung in the light from the transom window in columns so still they looked poured. Rafael set his clipboard on the hall table, clicked his pen, and wrote the date at the top of a fresh page in his small, square, unimprovable hand.

**IVERSEN PROPERTY — 14 HOLLOWMERE LANE. PROBATE APPRAISAL. R. IVERSEN, CERT. #4471.**

Then, because he was who he was, he measured the hallway first.

Twelve feet, four inches. He wrote it down. Then, out of habit—the habit that had made his name, the habit of never trusting a single reading—he measured it again, walking back the other way.

Twelve feet, five inches.

He stopped. Looked at the tape. Looked at the wall. The laser measure's little screen glowed its red dot against the baseboard, patient as an eye.

Houses settled. Houses breathed with temperature; joists swelled in damp weather and shrank in dry. A one-inch variance over twelve feet was within tolerance for any structure older than the invention of the level, and this house was older than the invention of the level by a comfortable margin—it had that look, the look of houses whose foundations predated their own addresses. He'd appraised hundreds of them. He wrote *12'4"* and drew a small line through the second figure, the way he always did when discarding an outlier, and moved on to the parlor.

The parlor measured sixteen feet even, north wall to south. Then sixteen-one. The kitchen: fourteen-six, then fourteen-seven. The stairwell run: nine-two, then nine-three. By the time he reached the upstairs landing he had eleven rooms logged and eleven second readings, every one of them exactly one inch longer than the first, and the pattern had stopped being a coincidence somewhere around the dining room and started being a sentence he couldn't read.

He stood on the landing with the tape hanging slack from his fist and made himself say it out loud, because saying things out loud was how you took their power away. Nineteen years of practice.

"The house is settling unevenly," he told the empty corridor. "Or my technique is drifting. Fatigue. Dehydration." He hadn't brought water. He noted that too, in the margin, in the column where he usually noted defects: *surveyor error possible.*

The house said nothing. But the silence had a texture he didn't love—the silence of a room where someone has just stopped talking when you entered.

* * *

He worked the ground floor methodically, because method was the load-bearing wall of his entire personality. North parlor. South parlor. Dining room, where he counted the chairs without thinking about it—twelve, walnut, seats worn pale in the centers—and wrote *12* beside *dining furniture, antique, fair condition,* and did not think about why counting chairs felt suddenly like a task worth doing carefully. Kitchen, with its blackened range and its linoleum curling at the edges like a page turning. Pantry. Back porch, screened, sagging two degrees toward the garden, which was itself losing a slow war with the treeline.

It was a good house. That was the thing he kept bumping into, professionally speaking. Under the dust and the dead smell it was a *sound* house—plumb lines true, sashes smooth in their tracks, floors that gave back your weight without complaint. Somebody had maintained this place with a devotion that bordered on religion, and the somebody had been a woman in her nineties with bad hips. He found the evidence everywhere: new caulking around the tub in a bathroom nobody used, a replaced pane in the pantry window matched so perfectly to the old glass you'd only catch it by the absence of bubbles. Grandma Edith had spent her widowhood keeping this house alive, and Rafael had spent that same widowhood not visiting, and the difference between those two facts sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He measured the study twice. Fourteen-eight, then fourteen-nine.

"You know what," he said, and put the tape away, and used the laser only, and wrote down the first number and refused to take a third reading, which was its own kind of surrender and he knew it.

* * *

Lunch was a sandwich in the van, eaten watching the house in the rearview mirror the way you watch a dog you're not sure is friendly. It looked fine. It looked like a photograph of a house, almost too composed, gable and chimney and ivy all agreeing with each other. There were no photographs of it anywhere in his mother's albums that showed the back of it, he realized. Only the front, always the front, always from the same angle at the gate, as if the family had collectively agreed that was the side the house showed people.

His phone had one bar. His mother had left a voicemail at 7:04 that morning: *"Rafi, honey, just—if anything seems strange over there, you call me. I know you'll say it won't. Just call me anyway."*

Strange. He turned the word over. His mother was a woman who considered a mislabeled spice jar strange. Whatever she meant by it, she'd meant more than spice jars, and she'd chosen to say it to the son with the clipboard instead of the sister with the feelings, which meant she wanted data, not comfort. He filed the voicemail under *pending* and went back inside, because the upstairs wasn't going to appraise itself, and because the alternative to working was standing in a driveway feeling watched by a building, and he was forty-six years old and had left that feeling behind in childhood along with the belief in the thing under the bed.

The thing under the bed, incidentally, had lived in this house. He remembered that on the stairs. He remembered being seven, and this staircase, and the specific terror of the fourth step from the top, which dipped, and which he had once been certain was soft. Not broken—soft. Like standing on something that could notice you.

The fourth step from the top dipped. He stepped on it deliberately, adult and professional, and it gave under his boot with a sound like a held breath released, and he wrote *stair tread 14, deflects, repair estimate minor* on the clipboard with a hand that was completely steady, thank you very much.

* * *

Four bedrooms. A sewing room that smelled of lavender gone to dust. A linen closet with sheets folded so precisely they looked geological, strata of other decades—floral prints fading downward into something woven before his grandmother's time. In the smallest bedroom, the one that must have been his mother's, the wallpaper was a pattern of tiny birds, and half of them had faded while half hadn't, so the walls looked like a flock caught mid-vanishing.

He measured each room. Each room gave him its inch.

By the sewing room he had begun taking the second measurement first, then the first, on the theory that order might matter, and the house adjusted without comment. By the linen closet he tried measuring corner to corner, diagonally, where settling couldn't reach. The diagonal came out long by an inch too. There was no geometry in which that was possible unless the room simply *was* an inch bigger than itself, and he stood in the closet breathing sheet-dust and understood that he was now having thoughts that belonged to a different kind of man—a man with a dowsing rod, a man who believed in the fourth step—and he hated that man and his stupid, reasonable, frightened heart.

"Probate doesn't care about your diagonals," he reminded himself. Probate cared about square footage, condition, comparable sales. Probate cared about numbers that could be defended in a courtroom. And the numbers were defensible. Every one of them. He would write 12'4" and 16'0" and 14'6" and no judge on earth would ever know about the ghost inches trailing each figure like a shadow with better posture.

Except he would know. That was the trouble with a mind built for precision: it kept everything. Somewhere in the warehouse of his head there was a shelf where this day was already being stored, labeled and cross-referenced, and he could feel it filling up.

* * *

The attic hatch was in the ceiling of the upper hallway, on a pull-cord that had gone the color of weak tea. He hadn't planned to go up there—attics were excluded from gross living area, attics were a footnote—but the cord hung there, and the afternoon was getting long, and there was a part of him, the part that had climbed this exact ladder at seven years old on a dare from a cousin whose face he could no longer recall, that wanted to stand up there and be taller than the house for once.

The ladder unfolded with a shriek of dry hinges. Above, the hatch swung open onto darkness that exhaled warmth—not attic heat, not yet, the day wasn't warm enough. This was the warmth of a breath, if breaths could be a hundred years wide.

He went up with his flashlight and his clipboard because leaving the clipboard behind felt like leaving part of his armor.

The attic was vast and clean. That was wrong immediately—attics were chaos, attics were the landfill of family life, but this one had aisles. Boxes stacked square. Furniture under sheets, draped so neatly the shapes beneath looked curated, museum pieces between exhibitions. At the far end, under the round window, stood an easel, and on the easel, facing the wall.

Rafael stood in the aisle with his flashlight beam on the easel's back and felt his pulse do something unprofessional.

An easel. Facing the wall. In a house where everything else faced outward, presented, arranged for viewing. He thought: *portrait, probably family, probably valuable, note it.* He thought: *turn it around, log it, go home, write the report, bill the estate, never come back.*

He crossed the attic in nine steps—he counted, because counting was the flashlight he had—and turned the frame around.

A girl. Oil paint, old varnish gone amber, hair the color of wet bark, a dress from another century buttoned to the throat. She was maybe ten years old. She was painted with terrible skill, the kind of skill that makes you feel the painter had stolen something to achieve it. Her hands were folded. Her mouth was a thin, serious line. Behind her shoulder, painted into the shadows of the background with a subtlety that took a moment to find, stood a wooden chair.

There was a card tacked to the frame's bottom rail, yellowed, the ink brown and fine:

**WREN — 1911**

"Wren," Rafael said aloud, and the name went into the attic air and did not echo, which was impossible, attics were the echoing-est rooms in creation, and the word just—stopped, the way a word stops when someone catches it.

He wrote on the clipboard: *Oil portrait, unsigned, approx. 19th c., subject "Wren," condition good, appraise separately.*

And then, because he was thorough, because thoroughness was the whole architecture of his survival as a person, he raised the flashlight again to check the canvas for a signature, some mark, anything—

Her head was turned.

Not much. Three degrees, maybe four. Enough that her eyes, which in the first looking had been aimed politely past him at the window, were now aimed at him. The thin mouth had not moved. The folded hands had not moved. Nothing else in the painting had changed, and he knew—knew with the flat, cold certainty of a man who measures things for a living—that nothing else *would* change, that the house had spent all day teaching him its grammar one inch at a time and this was the first full sentence.

Rafael Iversen stood in his grandmother's attic holding a clipboard full of honest numbers and did not move for a long time. The flashlight trembled a half-circle of light across the floorboards. Somewhere below him, in the walls or the pipes or the spaces between, the house made a sound that was almost a sigh, and the warmth in the dark leaned closer, attentive, the way a host leans toward a guest who has finally, finally begun to arrive.

On the clipboard, in his small square hand, beneath the appraisal notes, without deciding to write it, he added one line:

*Subject appears to be awake.*

* * *

He got down the ladder fast, clipped the hatch shut, and stood in the upper hallway with his back against the wall, doing the breathing exercise his doctor had taught him for the blood pressure. Four counts in. Six counts out. The hallway was dim now; the afternoon had gone amber through the little birds of the wallpaper, half the flock vanished, half remaining.

There was a rational explanation. There was always a rational explanation. He'd seen the painting before—as a child, obviously, and memory had superimposed the frontal image, and fatigue plus low light plus expectation had produced the illusion of movement. It was called pareidolia. It was well documented. He would eat dinner, sleep in the van if necessary, and in the morning re-examine the canvas in full daylight and find her facing forward, and laugh, and call his mother, and tell her about the funny old picture.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It had numbers in it.

He pushed off the wall to go downstairs and make it real, and that was when he noticed the door at the end of the hallway—the small door, knee-high, the one to the space under the eaves that he had not measured, because it hadn't registered as a room, because it was barely a closet.

It was open six inches.

He had passed it four times today. It had been shut every time. He knew it had been shut, the way he knew the parlor was sixteen feet, the way he knew his own signature, and the gap of darkness at its edge lay against the hallway floorboards long and thin and deliberate as a line drawn by a careful hand.

From inside the little room, softly, in a voice like paper remembering how to be a mouth, something asked:

"Are you the one who's come to stay?"
