## 3. The Song That Refuses to End

By the fourth morning in Marrowfen, Ines Galloway had developed a system, and the system was the only thing standing between him and the creeping suspicion that he was losing his mind.

The system went like this: every door in the cottage got counted at dawn and counted again at dusk. Front door, one. Back door, two. The little door under the stairs that opened onto nothing but a shelf of the previous tenant's jam jars, three. Eleven windows, though windows were windows and not doors, and he was careful about the distinction, because a folklorist who blurred categories was a folklorist whose notes were worth less than the paper they were scrawled on. Lamps lit at sundown — the oil lamp in the parlor, the candle stub in his bedroom, the hurricane lamp he kept by the tape machine like a guard dog. And the rule above all rules, the one he'd learned in the fens of Lincolnshire and the hollows of the Pennines and had never once had reason to regret: *never sing along*.

He could hum. He could tap his boot. He could write the melody down in his careful notation and feel very clever about it. But the mouth stayed shut when a source sang. That was the trade. You took their song with your ears and gave them nothing back, because a song given back is a song shared, and a song shared is a song that belongs to you now, and there were things in the world that tracked their property by ear.

He'd learned that from a woman in Norfolk who had then refused to speak to him for the rest of her life, which he'd taken at the time for a personal failing on her part. He was beginning, in Marrowfen, to revise his opinion.

The cottage came furnished — "fully furnished," the letting agent had said with a warmth that should have been his first warning — including a reel-to-reel machine that belonged to the cottage's previous occupant, a Mr. Ellery Voss, whose name was still inked on a luggage tag wired to the handle. Ines had written to the agent about returning it. The agent had written back to say Mr. Voss had no forwarding address, and wasn't that a shame, and did Ines need anything else, because Mrs. Tarn next door was ever so good about eggs.

The eggs, at least, were excellent.

He spent the fourth evening doing what he did every evening: cataloguing. Three days of field recordings, each reel labeled in his tidy hand. *Tarn, Mrs. E. — hummed fragment, refuses words. Pell, the butcher — fragment, different key, refuses words. Children at the well — fragment, same key as Pell, refused words with more giggling.* The whole village, it seemed, carried pieces of the same tune the way other villages carried chilblains: universally, quietly, and with a firm collective agreement never to discuss it.

It was a good tune. That was the devil of it. Ines had transcribed the fragments onto a single stave and the melody assembled itself like a jigsaw someone had designed to be finished — a rising figure, a turn, a fall that felt less like an ending than like a held breath. Every folklorist dreamed of songs like this. Complete, coherent, *waiting*. And every fragment stopped at the same point, mid-phrase, the way a child stops a story when the parent walks in.

"They don't sing it twice," he murmured to the lamp, and the lamp did its usual flicker of acknowledgment, or perhaps it was just the oil.

He threaded a fresh reel. He set the needle to the Tarn recording — the longest fragment, forty seconds of Mrs. Tarn humming in her kitchen while she shelled peas into a bowl, the peas making a soft percussion underneath, her cat purring somewhere near the microphone — and he let it play at low volume while he worked on his transcription, because there was a comfort in it, the domestic sounds, the peas, the cat.

He was halfway through correcting a note when he heard the second voice.

At first he took it for the tape. Old machines made old noises; he knew every groan and hiss this one produced, had catalogued them too, in his way. But this was not a groan. It was underneath Mrs. Tarn's humming — beneath the peas, beneath the cat, beneath the hiss of the tape itself — a low, even tone, patient as water finding a level. It was singing the melody. Not the fragment. The melody *onward*, past the place where every villager stopped, continuing the phrase that Mrs. Tarn's humming leaned toward and then, with the discipline of a lifetime, abandoned.

Ines sat very still.

He rewound. He played it again, louder, and turned off the parlor lamp so he could listen better, which was, he would reflect later, exactly the wrong instinct, though at the time it seemed sensible — darkness for hearing, light for seeing, a tidy division of the senses.

The voice was still there. Low, unhurried, and — this was the part that made the back of his neck prickle in a way he'd have been embarrassed to admit to any colleague — *polite*. It wasn't growling. It wasn't whispering. It sang the way a good guest sings: quietly, in the key of the room, deferring to the host. It waited for Mrs. Tarn's humming to fall silent at the end of her forty seconds, and then it went on alone for eleven more seconds, completing the phrase, resolving it, and stopping precisely where a second verse would begin.

Then the tape ran on into silence, and the silence had a texture to it, the way a room has a texture when someone has just left it.

Ines wrote in his notebook, in handwriting that was only slightly less tidy than usual: *Undervoice on Tarn reel. Unidentified. Continues melody past conventional stopping point. Duration 11 sec. Pitch consistent, no vibrato. Not Voss (presumed). Not Tarn. Not any source recorded to date.*

Then, because he was a folklorist and honesty was the whole of his religion, he added: *Not any source recorded to date that I know of.*

He checked the other reels. Pell's butcher-shop recording, made with the microphone discreetly wedged in a barrel of sawdust: there it was, beneath the humming, the same low voice, the same eleven seconds. The children at the well — and he felt genuinely ill listening to that one — the voice was there too, singing under the giggles, patient as ever, as if eleven seconds was no time at all to wait, as if it had waited longer for less.

Every reel. Every single reel, including the one he'd made of the churchyard at dawn, which contained no human voice at all, only rooks and wind — and beneath the rooks and wind, faint but unmistakable, the melody, going on.

"Well," said Ines to the empty parlor, in the tone of a man who has just found a footprint in a house he lives in alone. "That's not ideal."

* * *

He did what any reasonable scholar would do. He made tea. He decided that the sensible interpretation was a technical fault — crosstalk, print-through, the previous recording bleeding through the tape — and he tested this theory rigorously by playing a reel of a folk festival in Cambridge that he'd recorded two years ago and brought along for comfort. The festival reel was clean. No undervoice. No melody. Just a man named Dennis playing the spoons, bless him, forever.

He tested it again with a reel of his own voice, recorded the first night in Marrowfen as a calibration tone and a spoken log. His own voice said: *Tuesday, the eleventh. Arrived. Cottage satisfactory. Doors: three. Windows: eleven. The letting agent has an alarming number of teeth and I intend to say nothing about it.*

Beneath his own voice, low and patient and polite, something sang.

Ines set down his tea with great care, because his hands had opinions of their own, and the tea deserved better than to wear his opinions.

"Right," he said. "Options."

He was fond of options. Options were how he kept the world at a manageable size. Option one: the machine was haunted, which was absurd, machines were haunted only in the sense that they were old and vindictive about it. Option two: the tape had been recorded over something, some earlier tenant's session, and the machine was faithfully reproducing layers, in which case Mr. Ellery Voss had recorded a great deal of the same eleven seconds, which raised the question of what had happened to Mr. Ellery Voss, who had no forwarding address. Option three —

Option three was the one where he stopped making options, because option three was: the voice was on the tapes because the voice was in the room, and it had been in the room every time he'd recorded, and it was in the room now, and it had been singing along with his calibration log the way he had spent his whole career *refusing* to sing along with anyone, and there was a symmetry there he did not care for at all.

The lamps guttered.

Not the dramatic gutter of wind through a cracked window — the cottage was sealed, he'd checked, doors counted, windows latched. It was the gutter of a flame deciding, briefly, whether to continue. The parlor lamp bent. The candle stub in the bedroom, visible through the open doorway, bent the same way, in the same instant, as if they'd rehearsed it.

In the darkened room, the reel kept turning. His own recorded voice had finished. The tape ran on into its hiss, and the hiss, very softly, resolved into the melody, going on, past the phrase, past the place where Mrs. Tarn stopped, into the eleven seconds and then past *them*, further than he'd heard it go, further than any fragment, the second verse beginning now, low and patient and — he could think of no other word for it — *expectant*. As though it were singing to him. As though it were leaving space beneath the tune, room in the harmony, a part for a second voice.

Ines's mouth stayed firmly, heroically shut. He bit the inside of his cheek to be sure.

The tape ran out. The take-up reel spun against nothing with a small, forlorn flapping sound, and in the silence after, the lamps straightened themselves and burned steady again, and the cottage was a cottage once more: jam jars under the stairs, rain on the latched windows, the ordinary dark of an ordinary fenland night.

Ines sat for a long moment. Then he stood, and did the thing he did at dawn and dusk, because ritual is a handrail and he needed the handrail more than he needed dignity.

Front door, one. Closed. Back door, two. Closed. The little door under the stairs —

Three. Open.

He stood looking at it. It was a door that had never once, in four days of twice-daily counting, been anything but shut. It opened onto a shelf of jam jars — damson, he thought, or something damson-adjacent — and there was no draught in the cottage to move it, and no reason for it to be ajar, standing open perhaps four inches, the darkness behind it exactly as dark as a cupboard under the stairs should be, and no more.

Except that as he watched, the darkness behind it seemed to settle, the way a listener settles in a chair.

Ines closed it. He counted it aloud — "three" — in a voice that only shook a little. Then he went back to the parlor, took out his notebook, and wrote, beneath his careful entry about the undervoice:

*The song does not end. Every fragment stops where they stop it. The voice underneath does not stop. I believe the fragments are not incomplete songs. I believe they are held ones. And I believe something has been holding the rest of it for a very long time, and that it has noticed me taking notes.*

He underlined the last four words, then crossed out the underlining, because melodrama was for novelists.

Outside, far off across the fields, from the direction of a church that had had no bell since 1911, something began — very faintly, very patiently — to toll.

Ines looked at his watch. It was four minutes to midnight.

He put on his coat, took the hurricane lamp, and went out into the dark to count the church's doors.
