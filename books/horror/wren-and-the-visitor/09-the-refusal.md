## 9. The Refusal

The well crested on a Thursday.

Ines knew it the way you know a kettle is about to boil—not by looking, but by listening. All day the water behind the church had been making a sound like someone clearing their throat before an important speech, and by dusk the sound had become a hum, and by full dark the hum had become the song, sung low and complete by every throat in Marrowfen except his own.

He had counted his doors one last time. Eleven. He had lit every lamp in the cottage, including the one in the cupboard that Mrs. Tarn had once called "a bit theatrical, dear," and he had stood in the middle of his small kitchen with his hands flat on the table like a man steadying himself against weather, and he had said aloud, to no one:

"No."

It was the first word he'd spoken in two days that was entirely his. It felt strange in his mouth. Good-strange. Like bread baked from your own recipe.

Then he picked up the ash-written verse folded inside his coat—the paper itself was blank now; the words lived only behind his eyes, where the old mute woman had written them and scrubbed them away while gripping his wrist hard enough to leave bruises—and he walked out into the night to the well.

* * *

The village did not try to stop him. That was the worst part, or perhaps the best. They stood in their doorways as he passed—Mrs. Tarn among them, holding her lantern high like a woman seeing off a son at a station—and they said nothing, because half of them no longer could. Old Petchey opened his mouth when Ines went by and what came out was not a word but a shape of a word, a mouth moving around a hole where "good luck" used to live. He settled for raising his cap instead. Ines raised his back. Some courtesies survive the loss of language. Perhaps all of them do.

The churchyard grass was wet and silvered under a moon that seemed embarrassed to be watching. The bell above him rang without pause now—not tolling so much as ringing the way a struck glass rings, one long unbroken note that Ines felt in his teeth and in the backs of his knees and, he suspected, somewhere more private than either.

And the well—

The well was full. Not brimming. Full the way a cup is full, right to the lip, black water trembling with the bell's note, and rising still, a skin of water climbing the old stones millimeter by patient millimeter, the way dread climbs.

Ines stood at its edge and looked down into it, and the well looked up into him.

He saw faces in the water. That was expected; he'd been warned by the ledger, by Tarn, by his own tapes. What hadn't been warned was how *kind* the faces looked. They were everyone who had ever sung—keepers going back past 1911, past memory, their features worn smooth as river stones—and they were smiling at him with enormous gentleness, the way you smile at someone coming home after a long journey. Come in, the smiles said. You've carried this so far. Put it down. Put yourself down. We'll hold the rest.

"You're very persuasive," Ines told them. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "I want you to know I've thought about it. Properly. I made lists."

The water shivered. The faces waited.

"Pros of joining you," he continued, because talking kept the fear at arm's length and he had always talked when frightened, ever since he was a boy locked in a church vestry during a storm. "No more counting doors. No more lamps. Everyone I've lost would be down there, presumably, singing harmony. The village gets its words back. Mrs. Tarn stops looking at me like I'm a lamb she's fond of." He paused. "Cons: I stop being me. Which, granted, is a short list, but it's a load-bearing item."

The bell rang on. The water rose another finger's width.

He knelt at the lip of the well. The stone was cold through his trousers. Below him the Visitor gathered itself—a darkness beneath the darkness of the water, vast and unhurried, wearing its choir of stolen voices like a coat made of people—and Ines felt it begin, gently, expertly, to try his throat from the inside. A hum started in his chest that he had not begun. His lips wanted to part. Somewhere in his mouth, a first note assembled itself out of his own breath, warm and familiar, the opening line of the song every villager had hummed at him since the day he arrived.

This was the moment, he understood, that every keeper had reached. This exact stone. This exact note. And every keeper before him had opened his mouth and let the song come, because the song was easier than silence, because joining is so much less frightening than refusing, because a door held open by a hundred friendly hands is very hard to close alone.

Ines thought of the ledger entries ending mid-sentence. He thought of the mute woman scrubbing ash off a floor with shaking hands, keeping her refusal silent for fifty years because she had no voice left to refuse with—or perhaps because she had spent it all in one night, here, and been spared, and never spoken again by choice. He liked that version better. He decided to believe it.

He put both hands on the well's rim, leaned over his own reflection, and sang the last verse.

Alone.

* * *

It did not go beautifully. Let the record show that.

His voice cracked on the second line. He had learned the verse from ash, memorized in terror, and the melody came to him crooked, half-remembered, more spoken than sung in places. The verse was short—that was its whole nature, brevity as architecture, a song built like a door built like a wall—and the words were simple almost to the point of insult. No names. No summons. Just the closing of something: *this voice is mine, this debt dies here, the door shuts from my side, and what waits may wait forever.*

But here is what Ines Galloway understood, kneeling at the well with a stranger's hum dying in his chest: the verse had never needed to be beautiful. It only needed to be unsurrendered. Every keeper who sang the summons had sung it *with* the Visitor, in chorus, their voice braided into the many. The last verse could only work sung alone—one throat, one stubborn mortal note, offered to the thing below not as tribute but as a closed hand.

So he sang it alone, cracked notes and all, and meant every syllable the way you mean a vow, the way you mean *no.*

The effect was not dramatic. That was the terrible, wonderful thing about it. There was no lightning, no shriek from the deep. What happened was smaller and worse and better: the Visitor stopped.

All that gathering patience, all that rising water, all those kind smiling faces—it simply stopped, the way a conversation stops when someone says something true. The hum in Ines's chest faltered. The note assembling behind his teeth dissolved. And the darkness below the water drew back—not fleeing, nothing so honest as fleeing—but recoiling, the way a hand recoils from a stove, the way a guest recoils from a host who has finally, politely, unmistakably shown him the door.

Because that was what the verse was. Not a weapon. A refusal. A door closed from the other side. For a hundred years and more the Visitor had risen on invitation, fed on consent, grown fat on keepers who chose the bell—and it had forgotten, if it had ever known, that a voice could be withheld. Ines gave it the one thing it could not digest: a note that would not join.

The faces in the water looked at him. The smiles went first—drained away like tide leaving a shore—and underneath them the faces were only faces, tired and old and human, and then they were only reflections, and then the water was only water.

The bell, above him, rang one final time—a single clean stroke, almost relieved—and stopped.

The silence afterward was so total that Ines heard his own heart, heard the wet grass dripping, heard, distantly, in the village, a child say the word *bread* for the first time in three days, and a woman laugh, and old Petchey's voice crack across the square calling a name—his dog's, probably, or his late wife's, or possibly just testing it, saying *Margaret, Margaret* like a man tasting rain after drought.

Ines stayed kneeling a long while. The water in the well began, slowly, to sink—an inch, a foot, a yard—draining away down into whatever deep place it had come from, taking its choir with it, or sending them ahead, or letting them go. He chose not to look too closely. Some accounting is best left unread.

When he finally stood, his legs shook and his throat was raw and he felt lighter than he had any right to feel, given everything. He patted the well's rim the way you'd pat the shoulder of an adversary you'd come to respect.

"That," he said hoarsely, "is the last verse anyone sings in Marrowfen."

The well said nothing. Wells are good at that.

Behind him, footsteps on the churchyard path. Mrs. Tarn, lantern raised, her face wet and open in a way he'd never seen it, and beside her the whole village spilling out of its houses into the moonlight, mouths working, words returning one by one like birds after a hard winter. She stopped a few feet away and looked at him—at the empty well, at his ruined voice, at the whole impossible fact of him still standing there, singular, uncopied, his.

"Well," she said, and her voice broke on it, and she laughed, and it was the most beautiful sound Ines had heard in months. "Well, Mr. Galloway."

"Yes," he agreed. "Quite."

She offered her arm. He took it. Together they walked out of the churchyard while the village murmured around them like a hive waking, and if Ines noticed—as they passed under the silent bell tower—that his own humming of an old walking song had acquired, very faintly, underneath it, a second voice, low and patient and waiting, he said nothing about it.

Not yet.

Some debts, he was beginning to suspect, are not cancelled. Only carried.

He walked on anyway, out toward the road, humming.
