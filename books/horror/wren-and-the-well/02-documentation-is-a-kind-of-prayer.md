## 2. Documentation Is a Kind of Prayer

Felix had photographed four hundred and eleven funerals before this one. He knew the number because he counted everything now — a habit that had begun as professional discipline and calcified, over the years, into something closer to superstition. If he could record it, it was real. If it was real, it could be survived. His editor at the *Meridian* called it his rosary: the camera raised to his eye, the shutter's small dry click like beads slipping through fingers. Felix had never corrected her. There were worse prayers.

This morning the prayer failed him almost immediately.

The light was wrong for October — flat and yellowish, like old varnish — and it fell across Hollowmere Cemetery in a way that made every headstone look recently scrubbed. That was the first thing he catalogued, standing at the gate with his camera bag heavy on his shoulder and Wren's death certificate folded in his inside pocket like a letter he couldn't stop rereading: *the stones are too clean.* He photographed the gate itself, its wrought-iron arch gone green with lichen except where hands had worn two bright paths into the metal, as if generations of mourners had gripped the same spots entering and leaving.

*HOLLOWMERE CEMETERY — EST. 1851.*

He noted the date against the town charter in his head. Then he stepped through, and the click of the shutter began, and did not stop for three hours.

* * *

There were forty-two mourners. He counted them twice from different positions to be sure — once from the back of the gathering, once from beside an obelisk whose inscription had weathered to a smear of gray. Forty-two, in a town whose population sign at the highway turnoff read 3,900. For a Brightwater, one of the founding names, one of the twins of the founding family, forty-two was not grief. It was attendance.

He worked the crowd the way he'd worked flood zones and factory collapses, moving along the edges first, then spiraling inward. The lens made people honest in one direction and dishonest in another; they forgot their faces but performed their bodies. And their bodies here were performing something Felix had seen only once before, in a company town in West Virginia where everyone knew what the water table held: a uniformity of condolence so precise it might have been rehearsed.

It *had* been rehearsed. He became certain of it by the ninth mourner.

"I'm so sorry about Wren," said a woman in a blue coat, gripping his hand with both of hers. "She was such a bright girl."

"She was a lovely person," said a man with a farmer's forearms. "So sorry about Wren."

"Such a bright girl," said a teenager who could not have been older than fourteen when Wren left Hollowmere twelve years ago and who therefore had no possible memory of her. "I'm so sorry."

Felix photographed each of them mid-sentence. Later, reviewing the frames on the little screen, he would notice what he'd only half-registered live: none of them looked at his face. They looked at his collarbone, or past his ear, or at some fixed point over his shoulder — the same point, all of them, as though something stood there that they preferred to address. In frame after frame their eyes converged on empty air behind him, and their mouths shaped the same six words, and their hands did the same two-handed clasp, and it was like watching forty-two clocks all set to the same wrong hour.

He wrote it down standing under a yew tree, in the waterproof notebook he'd carried since his first assignment: *Condolences verbatim identical. Eye-line consistent, off-subject, approx. 6 ft behind speaker. Rehearsed? Or instructed?*

Underneath, after a moment, he added: *Or compelled.*

Then he crossed out *compelled*, because he was a journalist, and journalists did not write words like that in notebooks, and because crossing it out felt like closing a door he wasn't ready to open.

* * *

The casket sat closed through the service. This was, the funeral director had told him yesterday, standard practice — "given the circumstances." When Felix had asked what circumstances, precisely, the man had smiled with the lower half of his face only and said, "The drought's been hard on everyone," which answered nothing and was somehow meant to answer everything.

Felix had signed whatever needed signing. He had paid whatever needed paying. He had done this much correctly, this much by the book, because the alternative was to say aloud what had been sitting in his chest since the phone call: *That's not my sister you found.* He hadn't said it. You didn't say things like that to a funeral director in a town of 3,900 without evidence, and Felix Brightwater did not operate without evidence. Evidence was the only inheritance his family had ever given him that he wanted.

So he photographed the casket instead. From eight angles. He photographed the flowers — lilies, white, arranged in a fan pattern he'd never seen at any other funeral, each stem cut to exactly the same length. He photographed the funeral director's shoes (polished, dry) and the ground beneath the casket's trestles (dust, no damp, though it had supposedly rained the night they found her). He photographed the minister, a young man with a county seminary ring, reading from a card rather than a Bible, and got a frame of the card itself, blurred but legible enough later: not scripture. A list of dates.

He zoomed until the screen steadied. Four dates. 1897. 1924. 1961. 1998.

No explanation. No context. Just the dates, read aloud in a gentle voice while forty-two people bowed their heads, and Felix understood that he was the only person present who did not know what those years marked — and that everyone else's stillness was not reverence but recognition.

His hands were steady. He noted that too. *Hands steady. Pulse high. Functioning.* It was the kind of note a man took when he was afraid that if he stopped taking notes he would start screaming, and Felix had learned long ago that the notebook was cheaper than the screaming.

* * *

It was during the lowering that he noticed the Vanes.

Not their absence — absence is hard to photograph — but the shape of it. He'd been circling the graveside, shooting wide frames of the assembly, and in the third or fourth frame his eye caught the layout of the older stones beyond the recent section, rising up the low hill like teeth. He walked up after the service, while the mourners dispersed with the strange briskness of people leaving work early, and began reading.

VANE. VANE. VANE. VANE.

They dominated the hill. Not scattered among the Brightwaters and the Merricks and the Coles — dominant. The oldest legible stone read *ABIGAIL VANE, 1809–1897*, and beside it *EDMUND VANE, 1801–1897*, both dying the same year, both with inscriptions worn smooth in a way that suggested not weather but touch — thousands of fingertips over a century and a quarter. Below Edmund's name, nearly erased, a line remained: *HE KEPT THE COVEN—* and then nothing, the stone flaking away as if the word had been deliberately chiseled out and time had been hired to finish the job.

Felix photographed it from four distances. He put his thumb against the chisel marks and felt the violence in them. Someone had wanted that word gone badly enough to attack granite, and someone else — many someone elses, over many decades — had wanted to read it badly enough to wear the wound smooth again.

And yet: no Vanes below, among the forty-two. He checked. He walked the whole gathering's perimeter twice, shooting faces, running names in his head against the ledger of the town he'd grown up in. Brightwaters — himself, and a distant cousin who avoided his eyes. Merricks. Coles. Ashfords. The mayor, Dill Harrow, who shook his hand and said, "Such a bright girl," with the exact cadence of the woman in the blue coat, and whose palm was cold as well-water even in the yellow sun.

No Vane. Not one. A name carved into more stone than any other in this cemetery, a family that had plainly owned this hill, and not a single representative at the burial of a Brightwater twin.

He wrote: *Van(e) absent. All others present. Ask why.* Then, beneath it, because the notebook was also where he kept the things he couldn't say anywhere: *Wren would have noticed first. She always noticed first. I'm doing her job for her and she isn't here to make fun of me for it.*

The pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next page.

* * *

The trail he found at half past noon, and it was the trail that turned the day from wrong to unbearable.

He'd gone back to the grave one last time — the crew had finished filling it with a speed he chose not to think about — and stood at its edge doing the thing he did at the end of every assignment: a final sweep, frame by frame, telling himself it was thoroughness when it was really a bargain. *Look carefully enough and nothing will be missed. Look carefully enough and she'll still be dead, but the death will have been witnessed properly, and witnessed things stay put.*

The fresh earth was already cracking on top. Dry as biscuit. Of course it was — the whole county was dust, three years into a drought that had emptied the reservoirs and cracked the church foundation and driven the drilling crews into town. Except —

Except for a line of dark soil leading away from the grave's western edge.

He crouched. Touched it. Wet. Genuinely, unarguably wet, cool against his fingertip, smelling of iron and something underneath the iron — a sweetness, mineral and old, like the air in a cave that has been closed a very long time. The wet line ran maybe eighteen inches wide, meandering slightly, downhill through the grass toward the cemetery's far fence, and the grass on either side of it was brown and dead while the grass *in* the line lay flattened, slicked down, as if something heavy and damp had been dragged, or had crawled, or had poured itself along the ground.

Felix followed it. He photographed every ten feet, noting landmarks — the yew tree, the Cole plot, the leaning stone of some infant dead in 1874 — building a chain of evidence he could retrace blind. The trail passed through a gap in the back fence where boards had rotted away, and beyond the fence the land dropped into the old well field: a depression of perhaps twenty acres where, according to everything Wren had told him on the phone last spring, laughing about it, the town's founders had sunk their first wells and then, one by one, abandoned them. She'd hiked out there as a teenager. She'd said the wells weren't capped so much as *shut*, boarded and bolted and in one case bricked over, and that the town treated the field the way you treat a room where something bad happened — not forbidden, just never mentioned, never picnicked on, never developed. "Hollowmere's haunted acreage," she'd called it. "Every town has one. Ours just won't admit it."

Now he stood at the fence gap and looked out at it under the varnish-yellow light, and the field looked back the way a held breath looks.

The wet trail continued. Down the slope, past the first collapsed well — a ring of stones like a broken crown — past the second, its boards black with age, and on toward the third, the far one, the one set apart from the others near a stand of dead oaks. Felix raised the camera and shot the sequence, and it was only when he lowered it that he understood what the trail was doing.

It ended at the third well. But it ended *wrong*. It didn't stop at the rim. It went to the rim, circled it once — a full, deliberate orbit, like a signature — and then ran straight back toward the cemetery, fading as it went, drying into ordinary dust as if the ground were embarrassed by what it had shown him.

As if something had come *from* the well, walked to his sister's grave, and returned.

Felix stood in the dead oaks' shade for a long time. His breath came in careful, metered pulls. He thought about the closed casket. About the funeral director's dry shoes. About the four dates on the minister's card, and the chiseled-out word on Edmund Vane's stone, and forty-two mouths saying *such a bright girl* in perfect unison while their eyes fixed on the empty air behind his shoulder.

He thought: *I am the only person in this town acting like something is wrong, and either I'm insane, or everyone else has agreed not to know.*

He knelt at the well's rim and photographed the circle the trail had made, and then, because the light was good and because thoroughness was his religion and religion was what you practiced hardest when you were most afraid, he leaned over the edge to shoot straight down.

The well was dry. Of course it was dry. Everything was dry; the drought had taken every drop in the county.

But thirty feet down, at the bottom of the shaft, the stones glistened. Wet. Black and gleaming, catching the sunlight he sent down after them like coins thrown into water, and at the very limit of what the lens could resolve, at the mouth of a darkness deeper than the shaft's own depth should allow, something pale caught the light and held it.

He did not get the frame sharp. His hands, steady all day, were not steady then.

Felix backed away from the rim, walked backward the whole way to the fence gap without turning around — he would deny this later, and would never quite manage to believe the denial — and only at the cemetery gate let himself look at the camera's screen, at the last image, the blurred pale shape at the bottom of the dry earth.

He opened the notebook and wrote the date, the time, the GPS coordinates, and beneath them, in handwriting he barely recognized as his own:

*Something came up. Something attended her funeral. Something went home.*

Then, after a silence in which the wind moved nothing in the well field at all:

*Find the Vanes. Find out what Edmund kept.*

He clicked the notebook shut, raised the camera one last time, and photographed the cemetery gate from the outside — the arch, the date, the two bright-worn paths in the iron where a century of hands had gripped coming in and going out.

He intended, when he took hold of it, to grip somewhere new.
