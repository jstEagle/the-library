## 4. What the Ledger Says

The Hollowmere town archive occupied the basement of the old municipal building, and it smelled the way Felix imagined a drowned library would smell — paper left too long in damp air, ink gone soft at the edges, the faint mineral tang of water that had come and gone and left its signature behind. A single fluorescent tube buzzed overhead, flickering at intervals like something trying to remember how to stay lit. The stairs down were narrow and worn concave by a century of feet, though judging by the dust on the railing, few of those feet had belonged to anyone recent.

Felix had called ahead, or tried to. The clerk who answered the municipal line had a voice like warm milk, pleasant and utterly without texture, and had told him the archive was open to the public during business hours, that no appointment was necessary, that she hoped he found what he was looking for. He'd written down the time of the call, the number dialed, the duration — four minutes, eleven seconds — because documentation was the only prayer he knew how to say, and he said it constantly now, compulsively, the way some people touched wood or crossed themselves.

*Tuesday, October 14th. 9:47 a.m. Archive basement, Municipal Building, 2 Founders Row.*

He wrote it on the inside cover of his notebook, dated it, and descended.

The archive was one long room, shelving running floor to ceiling along both walls, boxes stacked in the aisles where the shelves had run out of patience. Fluorescent light did strange things down here; it flattened everything, drained the color from the cardboard spines until the whole room looked like a photograph of itself, an archive of an archive. Somewhere at the far end a dehumidifier hummed and gurgled, and Felix found himself grateful for the sound. Silence, in Hollowmere, had begun to feel less like absence and more like attention.

He hadn't come for the ledger. Not exactly. He'd come because of the hands.

Two days since the funeral, and he could still see them when he closed his eyes: Wren's face above a stranger's hands, calloused and broad-knuckled, one nail torn back to the quick as if it had spent its last hours clawing at something. The funeral director had been so smooth about it. *Embalming can affect the extremities, Mr. Brightwater. Grief does strange things to perception.* But grief didn't put dirt under a dead woman's fingernails, and grief certainly didn't make her hands forty years older than her face.

So Felix had done what Felix always did. He had started pulling threads.

Wren's disappearance was officially a drowning — the reservoir, three years ago, though they'd never recovered a body, which meant there'd never been a death certificate, which meant the casket last week had been *closed*, officially empty, until someone in the council office had decided otherwise. Someone had produced a body. Someone had buried it with Wren's name on the stone. And nobody — not the mayor, not the police chief, not the funeral director with his soft deflections — would tell Felix where that body had come from.

But towns keep records. Towns are made of records. And if Hollowmere had decided to lie, then the lie would have seams, and seams could be photographed.

He started with the finding aids — typed index cards in long wooden drawers, each drawer labeled in fading hand-lettering: *Council Minutes. Property & Tax. Water Rights.* That last drawer stuck when he pulled it, and gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Water rights. In a town dying of drought, the water rights file should have been the thickest thing in the building. It wasn't. It held perhaps two dozen folders, most of them recent — permits for the excavation crew's drilling operation, correspondence with a state hydrology office, a petition from 2011 about lawn restrictions. All surface. All shallow. Nothing below 1990, nothing below 1970, nothing—

Nothing except a single transfer slip, carbon-copied, dated June 3rd, 1987:

*Item: One (1) ledger volume, Brightwater family accounts, 1897–1987. Transferred from private holding (Brightwater estate) to municipal custody per standing arrangement. Location: Restricted shelf, Bay F. Access: By authorization of Council.*

*Standing arrangement,* Felix wrote in his notebook, and underlined it twice. *What standing arrangement?*

Bay F was at the back of the room, past the dehumidifier, where the fluorescent light gave out entirely and a single caged bulb burned amber over a short run of shelving. The restricted shelf was not locked. It was not even marked, really — just a small brass plate, green with age, screwed to the upright: *BAY F*. As if the restriction were understood rather than enforced, a secret everyone in town carried so naturally they'd never needed a sign.

The ledger sat where the transfer slip said it would, wrapped in acid-free paper that had gone brittle as autumn leaves. Felix unwrapped it over the reading table beneath the amber bulb, and his hands were steady, and he noted that they were steady, and hated himself a little for noting it.

It was a ledger. That was all. Green cloth boards, corners bumped white, the spine cracked in three places from a century of opening. *BRIGHTWATER — ACCOUNTS* in gilt that had mostly flaked away. His family name, in his grandfather's house, on a book his grandfather had kept, sitting in a town basement under a restriction no one had ever explained to Felix or to Wren, who had grown up in that house and never once seen this book.

He opened it the way you open a door you already know is going to show you something wrong.

The early pages were ordinary. Farm accounts, mostly — seed corn, fencing wire, a mule named Duchess, feed, labor, the small relentless arithmetic of a dryland farm in the 1890s. The handwriting changed hands twice in the first decade, father to son, the script tightening, modernizing. Felix turned pages with a pencil — he'd brought cotton gloves and chose not to wear them; he wanted to feel the paper, wanted the truth of it against his skin — and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, page after page, year after year.

Then October 1897.

The entry began mid-page, following a note about winter wheat, in the same hand, in the same ink, as if it were the same kind of thing:

*Oct. 12 — To the resident below, $4.00.*

Felix read it three times. He read it aloud, quietly, just to hear whether the words behaved differently in air. They didn't. *To the resident below, $4.00.*

There was no explanation. There was never any explanation. November: *To the resident below, $4.00.* December, the same, and then January 1898, and February, a metronome of payments marching down the page month after month, decade after decade, through changes of handwriting and changes of ink and changes of century. Through the war years, when every other entry shrank and strained — sugar rations, tire stamps, a son's allotment sent home — the payment to the resident below continued, unwavering, adjusted only for inflation. $4.00 became $6.00 became $15.00 became, somewhere in the fifties, $40.00, the figure climbing in careful increments like a rent negotiated by people too frightened to renegotiate.

*Resident.* Not debtor. Not tenant. *Resident.* As if the ledger were acknowledging a neighbor. As if whatever lived below had a right to the money the way a landlord has a right to rent — established, customary, unremarkable.

Felix photographed every page. Flash, shutter, log entry. *Page 34, entry dated Oct. 12, 1897. Page 35, Nov. 1897.* The camera's whir sounded enormous in the silent room, and once — he would tell himself later it was the dehumidifier cycling — the silence after each click seemed to lean in slightly closer, attentive, interested in what he was keeping.

He reached the signatures near the end of each fiscal year and understood what he was actually looking at. Each December, beneath a summary line — *Paid in full, twelve months, to the resident below* — a single name, signed. Elias Brightwater. Then Josiah Brightwater. Then a gap during some illness, filled in a different hand but countersigned. Then Abraham. Then Edmund. The names marched down the decades like the payments themselves, generation after generation of Brightwater heads of household, each one taking up the pen and taking up the obligation, none of them — apparently, across ninety years — writing so much as a marginal note of protest, or question, or shame.

Until Edmund Brightwater. Felix's grandfather. The man who had taught him to fish at the reservoir, who had died when Felix was nine, whose hands Felix remembered as gentle and whose voice he remembered singing hymns off-key in the truck.

Edmund's final signature sat at the bottom of a page dated December 1986. Beneath it, in fresher ink, a different hand had added a note — smaller, neater, administrative:

*Arrangement transferred to municipal stewardship per agreement of March 1987. Payments to continue via Council budget line 14. Signatory duties assumed by M.V.*

*M.V.*

Felix sat very still under the amber bulb. The dehumidifier gurgled. Somewhere upstairs, a phone rang and rang and stopped.

Budget line 14. He'd seen budget lines before — he'd requested the council's public financials by email two days ago and received, within the hour, a cheerful attachment so clean and so complete it had felt rehearsed. He opened his laptop now, right there at the table, and scrolled. Line 14: *Groundwater Stewardship & Aquifer Maintenance.* Annual amount: $41,600. Disbursed monthly: $3,466.67.

$3,466.67 a month. To a maintenance contract for an aquifer that had been dry for six years. Paid to whom? The disbursement records listed a payee: *Hollowmere Water Trust.* No address. No tax ID. No board members. Just a name, and a number, and a monthly transaction stretching back — he checked, scrolling faster now, heart climbing into his throat — back thirty-seven years without a single missed payment. Through recessions. Through the drought that had killed the town's crops and half its businesses. Through Wren's disappearance, which had happened in June, and there it was, July's payment, disbursed on schedule, four weeks after she vanished, as punctual as a heartbeat.

They were still paying. His town, his neighbors, the parents of kids he'd gone to school with — they were still paying, every month, to *the resident below*, and they had laundered the word *resident* into *stewardship* and the fear into a budget line, and the payments had never stopped, not once, not in a hundred and twenty-seven years.

And the signatures. Felix flipped back through the ledger, back and back, and saw it now, the thing he'd missed: the payments didn't begin in October 1897. They began in *September* 1897 — but September's entry was different. September's entry read:

*Sept. 30 — Being the first payment, freely promised, $4.00. Witnessed and agreed, all parties.*

*Freely promised.* He wrote the phrase down and stared at it until the letters seemed to swim. Freely. Promised. Contract language. This wasn't extortion recorded by a victim; this was a *term sheet*. Something had been offered, something had been accepted, and the Brightwaters — his blood, his name, the people whose photographs hung in his mother's hallway — had sat down with whatever lived beneath their land and *made terms*.

He thought of the well field west of town, the old Brightwater parcel, fenced and posted and empty for as long as anyone could remember. He thought of the wet trail he'd photographed leading from the cemetery toward it, gleaming in the morning sun like something dragged. He thought of Wren's last voicemail, three years ago, breathless and half-laughing: *Felix, I found something in the county records, you're not going to believe — call me back.*

He had called back. She hadn't answered. He had told himself, for three years, that there was nothing to have caught in time.

The fluorescent tube at the front of the archive flickered, steadied, flickered again. The amber bulb over Bay F dimmed — not brownout-dimmed, but deliberately, the way a person dims a lamp to look more closely at something. Felix looked up. The light held low and yellow, and the shadows between the shelves seemed longer than they had been, and the dehumidifier had stopped humming without having stopped, the silence arriving so smoothly he couldn't find the seam of it.

He photographed the September page again. And the December page with Edmund's signature. And the transfer note with its neat initials, *M.V.*, and he logged each frame aloud in a whisper, date and content, because saying it out loud made it real, and making it real was the only defense he had against the vertigo rising in him like floodwater.

Because that was the thing. That was the sick, tilting heart of it. It wasn't horror at strangers. It was *inheritance*. Every one of those signatures was a hand from his own house. His great-great-grandfather had promised. His grandfather had paid, for fifty years, and then had handed the debt to the town the way you hand a mortgage to your children — carefully, quietly, with the full expectation that they would keep paying and never ask what for. Felix had eaten breakfast in that kitchen. He had slept under that roof, above whatever the payments were feeding, and never known, and the not-knowing had been arranged for him, maintained for him, *budgeted* for him.

Complicity didn't require his signature. It only required his silence, and his silence had been inherited too, pre-signed, waiting for him in a green cloth book on a restricted shelf.

The amber light came back up. The dehumidifier resumed mid-hum, seamless, as if it had never paused. Felix exhaled and discovered his shirt was damp at the small of his back.

He copied the ledger's final page one more time, and it was copying the final page that he saw it — the thing that turned the vertigo into freefall. Beneath the 1986 summary, beneath Edmund's signature, beneath the transfer note, the ledger did not end. The ruling lines continued, blank, for another page and a half. And at the very bottom of the last ruled page, in pencil so faint it might have been accidental, someone had written a single line:

*She counts the steps. Ask her how many.*

Felix's mouth went dry. *She.* Wren had tapes — he'd found them yesterday in her house, her voice counting footsteps in a hallway that couldn't hold them. He hadn't listened past the first tape yet. He hadn't dared.

He looked at the penciled line for a long time. Then he photographed it, logged it, and closed the ledger with the gentleness owed to a loaded thing.

As he rewrapped it in its brittle paper, his phone buzzed against the table. An unknown local number. A text message, from a number he didn't recognize, in a thread with no history:

*You shouldn't be down there alone.*

Felix stared at it. Then, slowly, he raised his head and looked around the empty archive — the flat gray shelves, the humming dark at the end of the room, the stairs going up into ordinary daylight.

The message had been sent from inside the building. The signal strength icon showed one bar.

And beneath the text, still typing, the little bubble pulsed: three dots, appearing, disappearing, appearing — someone, or something, deciding what to say next.
