## 1. Off the Survey

The padlock on the Almeida homestead's back door was a joke, and Elodie Almeida told it to herself every time: *a joke with my name on it.* She had the bolt cutters through the hasp in four seconds, and the door swung inward on hinges that hadn't screamed in years because rust had eaten them into silence. Cold air rolled out to meet her, carrying the smell of a house that had been holding its breath since 1987.

She clicked her headlamp on and stepped inside without wiping her boots.

That was the first tell, if anyone had been watching. Urban explorers wiped their boots. They respected the decay, photographed it reverently, left nothing but footprints they'd apologize for later. Elodie walked through the mudroom like a woman walking through her own kitchen, which—technically, legally, in a deed filed under a trust she'd spent six years pretending not to understand—she was.

The client's email had been specific. *Map the cellar. Full dimensions. Note any anomalies against the 1974 municipal survey. Payment on delivery.* No name, no company letterhead, just an address that routed through three forwarding services and a payment structure that smelled like someone with more money than patience. Fine. Anonymous clients were half her business. People wanted to know what was under things before they bought them, or before they tore them down, or before they dug. Elodie didn't ask why. Asking why got you involved, and involvement was the one thing she'd sworn off when it came to this house, this town, this name stitched into her like a wire she couldn't cut out.

She moved through the kitchen with her camera running, narrating timestamps under her breath the way she always did. "Entry, 11:47 p.m. Interior condition: dry rot throughout, north wall buckled. No signs of recent habitation." Her voice sounded wrong in here. Too flat. The house ate the echo and gave nothing back, and she noticed herself lowering her volume to compensate, which was ridiculous, because the house was empty.

It was empty. She checked.

The hallway was where she stopped narrating.

Not because of anything. Because of the mirror at the end of it, the tall cheval glass with its silvering gone black at the corners like frost creeping across a window. It stood where it had always stood, angled toward the stairs, and Elodie caught her own shape in it—dark figure, headlamp halo—and felt the old childish certainty rise up her throat: *it's watching you count.*

She'd been seven the last time she'd stood in this hallway. Grandmother Maren had covered every mirror in the house with bedsheets that summer, all except this one, which she'd turned to face the wall. Elodie remembered asking why, and Maren had said, "Because some of them are tired," in the tone of a woman explaining weather, and then made her hot chocolate with too much salt and never spoke of it again.

Elodie turned her headlamp away from the glass and kept walking. She did not look back. She noted, in the mental ledger she kept instead of feelings, that she had not looked back, and filed the note under *discipline* rather than *fear*.

* * *

The cellar stairs were stone, worn into shallow bowls by a century of feet, and Elodie took them fast, glad to be out of the hallway's sightlines. Down here the air changed—cooler, mineral, wet in a way that wasn't damp so much as *deliberate*, like the ground itself was sweating. Her headlamp swept across shelving gone soft with mold, jars of something dark that had eaten their own labels decades ago, a workbench with tools fused to it by corrosion.

And there, along the east wall, the cistern.

The survey showed the cistern. It showed everything down here with the tidy confidence of a 1974 draftsman: cistern, eight feet by ten; coal chute; root cellar alcove; load-bearing brick pier. Elodie unfolded her printed copy and pinned it against the pier with her elbow, checking reality against ink. Cistern, check. Coal chute, check. Root cellar—

She lowered the paper.

The root cellar alcove wasn't there.

There was wall where the drawing said there should be an arched opening with wooden doors. Not new wall, either. Old brick, old mortar, stained the same amber-brown as the rest, laid in the same herringbone pattern. It had never been a sealed doorway. It had been built as wall, from the start, over something the surveyor had once seen and then been paid—or persuaded—to forget.

"Okay," Elodie said quietly, to no one, because saying things out loud made them smaller. "Okay."

She circled the room slowly, measuring tape whispering out behind her, and that was how she found the seam: a vertical hairline in the mortar behind the cistern itself, hidden from the doorway by the tank's bulk, running floor to ceiling with a precision no settling crack would bother with. She ran her thumbnail along it. Behind the plaster dust, the mortar in the seam was softer than its neighbors. Repointed. Someone had opened this wall and closed it again, and they'd been good enough at masonry to fool a county surveyor and careless enough to leave the joint a half-millimeter proud.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself the way she noticed everything, clinically, from a slight distance: *hands steady, pulse elevated, breathing controlled.* The distance was the inheritance too, probably. Maren's flat voice lived somewhere in her chest.

The pry bar went into the seam with a sound like a tooth coming out.

* * *

The wall came apart in slabs, easier than it should have—the mortar keyed to fail, she realized, mortared to be reopened, a door disguised as a decision already made. Behind it: darkness that her headlamp couldn't price. Cold poured out of it, cold with weight, and beneath the mineral smell was something else now, faint but unmistakable.

Water. Standing water, deep below, and under the water-smell, something organic and old, like the inside of a bell.

Elodie crouched at the threshold and played the light down.

A shaft. Hand-dug, the walls scarred with pick marks in overlapping generations, dropping straight down past the reach of her beam until the light just gave up. Iron rungs were set into the near wall, forged, not store-bought, each one furred with orange rust—and the topmost rung was bright. Polished metal gleaming under her lamp, worn smooth in the center the way a stair tread wears under feet that use it often.

Recently. Recently used often.

"No," Elodie said, and the shaft said it back to her a half-beat late, in a voice that didn't quite have her pitch.

She sat back on her heels. Pulled out her phone. No signal, obviously—there never was in these houses, though she'd never thought to wonder why *this* house in particular drank signal the way it drank echoes. She switched to the laser measure and dropped the dot down the shaft. The readout spun: 12 meters, 15, 18, and then the number started climbing back down as the beam found a far wall that shouldn't have been there, because a straight shaft doesn't have a far wall.

Tunnels. The dot was hitting something horizontal. Something that spread.

She got out the municipal survey again, hands still steady, still noting their steadiness. The 1974 sheet showed bedrock under the homestead, unbroken, annotated "no subsurface voids." She had the county's newer digital surveys cached offline too—utility corridors, storm sewers, the whole subterranean bureaucracy of a town that had been mapping itself since 1890. She scrolled through all of it, zoomed into the block, held the screen beside the shaft's mouth like a priest comparing scripture to the actual sky.

Nothing. Every map agreed: solid rock under the Almeida land. Every map, going back a hundred and thirty years, drawn by men who had surveyed every culvert and privy vault in town, had looked at this property and seen nothing worth drawing.

Which meant the tunnels weren't missing from the record.

They'd been removed from it.

Elodie sat down fully on the cold floor, back against the cistern's brick flank, and let herself do the thing she never let herself do in the field: think about the name. *Almeida.* The name on the deed, the name in the trust, the name she'd shortened to "L. Almeida" on her invoices and finally to just "L." because clients kept asking questions she didn't want to answer. The name her grandmother had said only twice in Elodie's memory with anything like warmth, both times at funerals, both times followed immediately by: *"We keep what's ours quiet."*

She'd thought it meant land. Money. The usual quiet that wealth buys.

She looked at the shaft, at the bright-worn rung, at the dark that her light could not finish describing, and revised the thought with the calm of a woman reading a contract she should have read years ago.

*We keep what's ours quiet.* Whatever was down there, the family had kept it. Kept it off maps, out of surveys, behind walls mortared to reopen. And someone—something—had been climbing those rungs recently enough to polish them.

Her phone buzzed against her thigh and she nearly drove it through the cistern wall. Signal, suddenly, one bar, down here where signal went to die. A single message from the anonymous client, timestamped ninety seconds ago:

**Did you find the hatch? Don't go down. Whatever you hear, don't go down. Wait for me.**

Elodie stared at it. Then at the shaft. Then, because she was who she was—an Almeida, which apparently meant a person who checked locked rooms her family had paid to forget—she leaned over the mouth of the well and listened.

Far below, in the dark where her light drowned, water moved.

And under the water, patient as a held breath, something began—very softly, in a voice worn smooth as the top rung—to hum a tune she almost knew.
