## 8. Anselm's Circle

The winter of 1968 I learned that a language can be kept alive the way a fire is kept: by feeding it things you will miss.

I was twenty-three and worked at the co-op weighing flour for people who had never once asked me my name. My mother had died the previous spring, and with her went most of our conversations — she had been the only person left who spoke to me in the old tongue without translating herself first, without that small apologetic pause people make when they are embarrassed by their own mouths. After the funeral I caught myself thinking in the majority language, thinking in the language of the radio and the tax office, and it frightened me more than grief did. Grief at least had words. Mine were leaving.

So when a notice appeared on the co-op board — *Study circle, old speech, all levels welcome, Glasshouse Road, Thursdays* — I copied down the address before I had decided to go. That is how Anselm got me. Not through persuasion. Through the simple fact that he was holding the door open to a room I already lived in.

* * *

The glasshouse on Glasshouse Road had belonged to a nursery that failed sometime in the fifties. The glass was clouded, panes missing here and there, patched with tar paper and one piece of an advertising hoarding showing half a woman's smiling face above a slogan no one could read anymore. Inside, the benches still stood in rows, and someone — Anselm, we learned later, with his own hands, in the months before any of us arrived — had cleared them of debris and set out lamps and folding chairs and a blackboard salvaged from a school.

There were nine of us that first Thursday. I remember because Anselm counted us aloud, in the old tongue, and then said, "Ten is a proper number. We will find our tenth." He said it as though the tenth were a fact waiting to occur, like weather.

He was not what I expected. I had imagined some professor type, tweedy, apologetic, a man who would teach the language the way you teach a dead thing — respectfully, from behind glass. Anselm was younger than that, thirty perhaps, with forearms brown from actual work and a way of looking at you that made you straighten your spine without being told. He spoke the old language first and explained second, and when he explained he did it in the majority language but reluctantly, as if each translated sentence cost him something.

"Everyone here has lost someone to silence," he said that first night. "A grandmother. A mother. A whole childhood. The language didn't die of natural causes. It was starved. Deliberately. And starvation can be reversed."

I want to be fair to him. This is the difficulty of telling this story now, at my age, with my hands the way they are: I want to be fair, because fairness is the only apology I have left that isn't worthless. So let me say plainly: everything Anselm told us in the beginning was true. The language *had* been starved. The schools *had* beaten it out of our parents' generation; the employers *had* fined men for speaking it in the yards; the radio *had* made our own words sound like mud and rust until we were ashamed to use them in front of our children. He named the mechanisms. He had documents. He stood in that cold glasshouse and described the slow murder of everything my mother had whispered to me, and I sat in the third row and felt something I hadn't felt since her funeral, which was that I was in the right place.

That is the seduction, if you want to call it that. It wasn't flattery. It was accuracy. He simply told the truth about the wound, and then positioned himself as the only bandage in the world.

* * *

By spring we were fourteen. The tenth member turned out to be Vera.

She came in April, late, wet from rain, carrying a loaf wrapped in cloth as an offering, which was an old custom none of the rest of us knew. Anselm knew it. He received the loaf with both hands and said the blessing over bread — three lines of it, archaic, the kind of thing that survives only in remote valleys and very old women — and Vera's face when she heard it. I have thought about that face for fifty years. It was the face of someone hearing a door open in a house she'd believed condemned.

She was a year older than me, quicker than any of us, with a laugh that arrived before her sentences did. She'd grown up two counties over, in a family that had held onto the language longer than mine had, and within a month she was correcting Anselm's grammar — gently, once, and then less gently, because he took correction the way a stove takes water. But he admired her for it too, or seemed to. "This is what we need," he told the circle. "Not students. Keepers. People who will fight me." We all wanted to be the person he pointed at when he said that.

The lessons themselves were good. I will say that until I die. He taught us the mourning vocabulary first — the words that have no neighbors in any other language, the ones that only exist where people have been losing things for a thousand years. There is a word for the grief of a language leaving a family, a word so specific it names the exact shape of the empty chair at the table where the stories used to be told. There is a word for remembering someone wrongly. There is a word — I teach it now, to Casper, who writes everything down — for the moment you realize you are the last one who knows something, and it is not a sad word exactly. It is a word with weight in it, like a stone you're handed and expected to carry uphill.

Anselm taught us those words and we wept, all of us, the first time, sitting among the seed trays with frost on the glass, crying in a grammar nobody around us could follow. You cannot imagine what that is like unless you have done it. To cry in a language and be understood. It is the opposite of loneliness. It is the thing I spent the rest of my life trying to get back to, and the thing I destroyed in order to keep.

* * *

The change came slowly, which is how such changes always come. If it had arrived all at once, wearing its true face, even I would have run.

First it was only enthusiasm. "The language lives between speakers," Anselm said. "Every hour you spend outside this room, it starves a little more." Reasonable. True, even. So we began staying later. Then he arranged work for some of us near the glasshouse — he had connections, a cousin with a warehouse — so that our days and our lessons folded into one another. Then communal meals on Saturdays, cooked in the old style, eaten with the old blessings, and it would have been churlish to refuse, and why would you refuse? The food was good. The company was better. For the first time since my mother died I was not translating myself for anyone.

Then, one Thursday in autumn, he said: "Some of you are still going back, after lessons, to houses where nothing of this is spoken. You might as well carry water in your hands."

He didn't forbid anything. Anselm never forbade. He *grieved*. He would look at you with those patient, disappointed eyes and say, "I understand. It's hard. The world pulls at us," and the shame of disappointing him was worse than any rule would have been. Marta stopped visiting her sister. Old Henrik sold his boat because the men he fished with mocked the blessings he said over the catch, and Anselm told him the sea understood the old words better anyway. Little by little, our lives outside contracted to the size of the glasshouse, and inside it we were luminous. That was the trade. Everything smaller, everything brighter.

Vera saw it first. Of course she did. She was the quickest of us, and quickness includes seeing the trap while standing in the middle of it.

"It's a circle," she said to me one night, walking home along the canal, our breath smoking. "It's supposed to be a circle, Ilse. Circles don't have a head."

"He's not a head," I said. "He's the one doing the work. Who else would rent the glasshouse? Who else has the books?"

"Books he won't lend out," she said. "Have you noticed? Everything written stays in his case. We learn by mouth, he says. From him." She kicked a stone into the water. "My mother told me about men like this. In the old country, she said, every village had one. The man who loves the tradition so much he becomes the gate, and then the wall."

"You sound ungrateful," I said, and I am ashamed of it now — ashamed of the sentence itself, the cheap little knife of it. Ungrateful. As though belonging were a debt and doubt a theft.

Vera looked at me for a long moment. "You sound afraid," she said, not cruelly. That was the worst part. She was never cruel about it. "You're afraid that if he's wrong, then the room goes dark again."

I didn't answer, because she was right, and because the canal was cold and the walk home was long and I did not want to spend it alone with the truth.

* * *

By the following winter — the winter everything turned — the circle was twelve. People had drifted away, the ones with families, the ones with doubts they trusted more than their hunger. Those of us remaining called ourselves, without irony, the keepers. We had our own names for the days of the week in the old tongue. We had begun, at Anselm's suggestion, to write down our dreams, because dreams, he said, were where the language went when it fled our waking hours, and reading them aloud on Thursdays had become the heart of the meeting. I dreamed in the old tongue by then. I dreamed of my mother, and she spoke, and I woke fluent and grieving and grateful, and read my dream to twelve nodding faces while Anselm watched from the blackboard with an expression I mistook, then, for tenderness.

It was tenderness. I want to be fair. It was tenderness with ownership inside it, the way a deed of title is tender toward a field.

That January he announced the vow. He didn't call it a vow — he called it "the deepening" — but a vow is a vow whatever you dress it in. Each keeper would speak only the old language among ourselves, permanently. Each would bring their personal papers, letters, photographs, family records, into the common archive, "so that nothing of ours remains scattered in the world of forgetting." And each would accept that the circle came first — before employment that conflicted with it, before acquaintances who mocked it, before, he paused here, and the pause was the whole speech — before ties that pulled away from it.

"The language survived a thousand years," he said, "because people were willing to be sealed vessels. We are the vessel now. All of us together. Nothing gets in, nothing leaks out."

And eleven voices said yes. Mine was the fourth or fifth loudest. I remember the heat of the lamps, the smell of damp plaster and seed soil, Vera's hand lying flat and still on her knee beside me, and the terrible warm certainty that I was finally, finally home.

Vera did not say yes.

She said, quietly, in front of everyone, in the old tongue so that it would be perfectly clear and perfectly deniable: "A sealed vessel is for the dead. Living things need air."

The silence afterward lasted four seconds. I counted. I have counted it many times since, in insomnia, in hospitals, in the long gray afternoons of my old age, and it has never gotten shorter.

Anselm smiled at her — that was the frightening part, the smile, warm as a lamp — and said, "We will find our tenth again," as though she had already gone, as though the sentence had already fallen on her like weather.

* * *

What happened next belongs to another chapter of this story, and I am tired, and the light in the glasshouse is going the way it does in February, thin and blue and final. But I will set down the shape of it, because Casper will read this, and she deserves the shape even before the details:

Within the year, Vera tried to leave — truly leave, with more than herself. And I, Ilse Vann, who owed her a loaf of bread and a door opened in a condemned house, stood in this same glasshouse and said nothing while a man I loved in the way you love a father, a general, and a fire declared her a traitor to everything we were.

I chose the circle. I chose the warm bright shrinking room.

Fifty years later a girl walks in here with her mother's chin and her notebooks full of unsaid things, and asks me questions I have been rehearsing answers to since before she was born. The language is nearly gone now. Anselm saw to that in his way, and I saw to it in mine, and the two ways were more alike than either of us ever admitted.

But the girl keeps coming back. And last week she asked me about the photograph — the one of Vera and me, young, squinting in this very light — and I heard myself begin to tell the truth for the first time in fifty years, and it came out in the old tongue, naturally, the way confessions do.

There is a word for what I am feeling as I write this. We had it. Of course we had it. It means: the fear of the account coming due, mixed with relief.

Tomorrow I will teach it to her.
