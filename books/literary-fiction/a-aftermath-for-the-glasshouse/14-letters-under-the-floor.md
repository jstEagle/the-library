## 14. Letters Under the Floor

My mother's house had always been a museum of postponed decisions. The broken umbrella stand kept because it might be mended. The tax returns from 1994 kept because they might be audited. The good scissors kept in the junk drawer among the bad ones, so that every act of cutting was a small gamble. I had catalogued all of it, of course — that was what I did, what I had always done, though I was only beginning to understand that the notebooks were not a record of my mother so much as a substitute for her. You cannot lose a filing system. You can only lose a person, and I had hedged accordingly.

Now I was in her house alone, and the filing system had failed me.

Vera had been moved to the care facility eleven days ago, on a Tuesday that smelled of rain and disinfectant. The move itself had been mercifully quick — a social worker with kind, tired eyes, two orderlies, one suitcase. What remained was the sorting, and I had been putting it off with the discipline of a professional procrastinator. I told myself I was waiting for her to be settled before I disturbed her things. I told myself a great many things. The notebooks recorded none of them, which was itself a kind of entry.

But I had come back for the photograph. Or not for it — for the absence of it. The one she used to hide in the drawer of the sewing table, the one I'd written about months ago in my careful, cowardly shorthand: *She keeps a picture face-down. She checks it is face-down. She does not know I have seen her check.* The drawer was empty now. Of course it was empty. Vera had spent her last lucid months distributing her secrets like a woman settling debts, and I had no way of knowing which of us had received which.

So I did what any reasonable person does when a drawer fails them. I started on the house.

* * *

There is a particular intimacy in searching a parent's home that no amount of childhood there prepares you for. You know the geography — the loose brick in the garden path, the cupboard over the stairs that smells of turpentine and decades — but you have never had *permission*. As a child, permission is granted in increments, doled out like sweets. As an adult child of a parent who is disappearing, permission arrives all at once, retroactive and terrible, and you find yourself opening drawers with the guilty tenderness of a burglar who loves the house.

I began sensibly. Desk first: bills, insurance documents, my father's death certificate in a folder labeled with his name in her handwriting, which stopped me longer than the certificate did. Her handwriting had changed over the years — I could see the whole arc of it in the folder labels, the confident slant of the seventies giving way to something more careful, more effortful, and finally to the shakily monumental capitals of the last two years, letters like small monuments, like things being carved rather than written. Language as excavation. I had spent months learning a dying language from Ilse Vann, and all the while my mother had been writing her own in the margins of utility bills.

Nothing in the desk. The wardrobe: winter coats, and in the pocket of the grey one, a cinema ticket from 2009 — she had gone alone, I realized, to a film I'd told her wasn't worth seeing, and kept the ticket, and I could not decide whether this was pathetic or the saddest, bravest thing I'd ever found. I put the ticket in my coat pocket. Some evidence you keep.

The kitchen. The bathroom cabinet, with its archaeology of creams. The boxes in the attic that I'd always been told were *just papers*, and which were, in fact, just papers — my grandmother's ration book, a child's drawing of a house with too many windows, done in a hand I didn't recognize and couldn't stop looking at.

By evening I had searched everywhere a reasonable woman hides things, and found nothing but the ordinary sediment of an ordinary life, and I was standing in the middle of the sitting room feeling foolish and hollow when I noticed the floorboard.

It was the third board from the window, and it was proud — raised slightly at one end, the way boards get when they've been lifted before and not seated quite properly afterward. My mother had lived in that house for forty years. She noticed everything. A proud board in her sitting room was not an accident. It was a sentence.

I knelt. The board came up with a sound like a held breath released, and underneath, in the shallow cavity where pipes should have run but didn't, was a biscuit tin. Not a decorative tin — the kind shortbread actually comes in, dented, the lid rusted at one corner. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me. I catalogued the shaking. I did not stop it.

Inside: letters. Perhaps forty of them, bound in bundles with the rubber bands that had perished into sticky threads, and beneath the bundles, flat and face-down, the photograph.

I knew her at once — the young woman in the photograph. Not because I'd seen the picture before, but because I had spent a winter learning her from the outside in. Vera, twenty-something, standing in front of the glasshouse with its panes fogged and green with age, her arm around another young woman — Ilse, unmistakably, though rounder then, her hair long, both of them squinting into a sun that had set fifty years ago. They were laughing at something outside the frame. The laughter was the unbearable part. I had never seen my mother laugh like that. I'm not sure I'd ever seen her *expect* to laugh like that.

I set the photograph aside with the care you'd give a wounded bird and turned to the letters.

* * *

The earliest were dated 1971. I know because the dates were the first thing I looked for, the way you check the exits in a unfamiliar room. The handwriting on the envelopes was small and exact and beautiful, the handwriting of a man who believed his penmanship was an argument, and the return address was the glasshouse — no street name, just *The Glasshouse, Vann Property*, as if the building were its own municipality.

I should describe the man before I describe the letters, because the letters only make sense if you understand what Ilse had told me and what she had carefully not told me. Anselm Reiter. The teacher. The charismatic center of the study circle that had gathered in that conservatory in the late sixties, young people learning a language that everyone else had agreed to let die, and Anselm telling them they were not students but *vessels*, that they were the language's second chance, and that a second chance comes with obligations. Ilse had described him to me in her careful, halting way — she always spoke about him in the old language first, as if he were safer there, smaller there, and only translated him reluctantly into the majority tongue, where he came out larger and colder. *He believed the language needed protecting from the people who spoke it,* she had said. *He was right, in the way that a fire is right about needing wood.*

I had assumed Anselm was a chapter in other people's history. I had assumed, with the comfortable arrogance of a translator, that my work was to carry his story across into my own and set it down.

The first letter was two pages. I will not reproduce it whole; my notebooks have it entire, and even there I have edited the worst of it, and I am a person who prides herself on not editing. But the shape of it: Anselm wrote to my mother the way a creditor writes to a debtor, with courtesy stretched over menace like skin over bone. He wrote that she was missed. He wrote that the circle had suffered from her absence — that her *ear* was irreplaceable, her instinct for the old words. He wrote that he understood she had *chosen the world*, and that the world was a generous employer but a poor family. And then, in the final paragraph, in the same beautiful hand, the same measured cadence, he wrote that he hoped she would return soon, because there were matters — her *circumstances*, he called them — that the circle had agreed to treat with discretion, but that discretion, like all virtues, required cooperation to survive.

*Circumstances.* I sat on the cold floor of my mother's sitting room and read the word four times before I let myself understand it. I was the circumstances. I was two years old and living somewhere with my mother, away from the glasshouse, away from the circle, and a man I had never heard of was writing to her that her discretion was appreciated and her cooperation anticipated, in the tone of a man discussing the weather in a country where it always, eventually, rains.

The letters ran through 1971, 1972, 1973. They got shorter. The menace got less upholstered. *You know what I know. The child deserves to be raised among her own. Return, and we will say the leaving was a study abroad. Stay, and I cannot answer for the others' discretion, though I will answer for mine as long as I can.* And once, in a letter dated the winter of 1973, the sentence that I have since transcribed and transcribed again, looking for a way to soften it and finding none: *A language survives by claiming its children. You of all people should not stand in the way of a survival.*

I put the letters down. I got up and walked to the window and stood there for a long time, watching the street do its ordinary business — a man walking a dog that was clearly winning, a delivery van reversing with its patient electronic complaint — and I thought: *I am forty-one years old and I have just learned that my childhood was a negotiation I was the terms of.*

Here is what I did not think, and I want to be honest about this because the notebooks are supposed to be honest and this is the closest thing to a notebook I have: I did not think, *poor Mother.* My first thought was smaller and uglier. My first thought was, *she knew. She knew all of it, every year of my childhood, every time she switched languages at the market, every time she flinched when I asked about before — she knew, and she let me believe the silence was temperament, when it was strategy.* She had built a fortress out of forgetting and called it a personality, and I had spent my whole life taking it personally, and it had never been personal. It had been a wall. I had been living inside a wall and calling it a mother.

And then — because I am my mother's daughter, and the second thought is always the expensive one — I thought: *she won.* Whatever Anselm wanted, whatever claiming he intended, it never happened. I grew up outside. I grew up free of him, free of the circle, free of every word of the old language until it had shrunk in me to a handful of pebbles I couldn't even name. She had paid for that freedom with fifty years of silence, and with a daughter who resented her for the silence, and she had paid it anyway, without ever once telling me the price, because telling me the price would have been letting the war through the wall.

I went back to the floor. There were more letters. There was also, at the bottom of the tin, beneath everything, a single sheet in a different hand — my mother's, younger, the confident slant of the seventies — and it was not a letter. It was a list. Names, seven of them, and beside each name a word in the old language that I recognized with a lurch, because I had learned it only weeks ago in the glasshouse, in a lesson about grief vocabulary, and Ilse had said it was a word with no clean translation: *the debt you owe to the place that made you, paid by never returning.*

Six names had the word beside them. The seventh — the last, at the bottom, in ink pressed so hard the paper had nearly torn — was *Anselm*.

She had been keeping her own inventory. All these years, all these rooms away, she had been running the same accounting I run, the same ledger of what people are owed and what they take, and she had concluded — my mother, my cautious, silent, fortress-building mother — that the man who threatened her, the man who wanted to claim her child for a language like a landlord claims rent, was the one who owed the debt. That he was, in the grammar of that word, the one who had never come home.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat on the floor. Long enough that my knees had gone from protest to numbness, which felt appropriate. Long enough that the light through the window moved from amber to the grey-blue of early evening, and the room became a place where things were only shapes, and I had to hold the letters close to read them, and their small exact handwriting swam.

I should have called Ilse. That was the thought that finally got me upright — not grief, not rage, but the translator's reflex: *take this to the native speaker, ask her what it means.* Ilse had been there. Ilse had stayed — *the one who stayed*, my mother had called her, in one of the last sentences she'd ever given me, with an intonation I'd spent months failing to parse. Ilse knew what the discretion was. Ilse knew what the others' discretion might have cost. Ilse knew whether the child in the photograph — whether *I* — had ever been in actual danger, or whether Anselm's letters were the paper tiger of a small man in a glass house, roaring at a woman who had simply walked away.

But there was a problem, and the problem was the argument. Three days ago I had walked out of the glasshouse into the rain, and the last thing Ilse Vann had said to me was that I wanted the language without its cost, and the last thing I had said to her was something I had been rehearsing ever since, trying to make it more defensible, and failing, because it wasn't defensible, it was just true and cruel: I told her that she had spent fifty years guarding a museum and calling it a living thing. I told her that a language kept the way she kept it — behind glass, in labeled trays, in the hands of one dying woman — was not a language at all. It was a specimen.

She had looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and said, in the old language, the word for *go*, and I had gone.

And now I had a biscuit tin full of proof that the cost was real. That the cost had a name and an address and beautiful handwriting and had spent three years trying to collect my mother's child as payment. That the museum, if that's what it was, had been built over a bunker.

I put the letters back. Not all of them — I took the 1973 letter, the one with the sentence about claiming children, and the photograph, and my mother's list. The rest I returned to the tin and the tin to the cavity and the board to the floor, pressing it down until it sat flush, until the room was a room again and not an excavation. Some evidence you keep. Some evidence you keep *close*.

At the door I looked back at the sitting room, at the third floorboard, at the whole architecture of postponed decisions, and I thought about what my mother had done with her one wild, unrepeatable act of defiance. She hadn't written it down. She hadn't told a soul. She had lifted a floorboard in the night, put the war underneath it, and spent the rest of her life walking over it, and when the forgetting came for her, it took the surface first — the bills, the birthdays, my name on some afternoons — and left the floorboard proud, left the tin waiting, left the sentence in the wood for whoever loved her enough to kneel.

The care facility was twenty minutes away. It was nearly nine. Visiting hours were over, and my mother would be asleep, or sitting up in the dark doing whatever it is she does now in the country her mind has moved to — the country where her sister is alive and the language is whole and I am someone else, someone younger, someone who maybe never left.

I didn't go tonight. I went home, and I opened a notebook — not the inventory, a new one — and I wrote at the top of the first page the word Ilse had taught me, the debt-word, and beneath it I wrote Anselm's name, and beneath that I wrote my own, because I no longer knew which side of the ledger I was on. The daughter of the woman who fled, or the child a language had been promised. The translator, or the thing that gets translated — handed over, claimed, made to mean what other people need it to mean.

In the morning I would go to the glasshouse. I would stand in the doorway with rain or without it, and I would put the photograph on the potting bench between the labeled trays, and I would say to Ilse Vann, in whatever mixture of tongues I could manage: *You knew what he wrote to her. You knew what he wanted. Tell me what my mother bought with her silence — and tell me whether I'm the price, or the purchase.*

And if she refused — if she gave me one more partial story, one more carefully rationed truth, the way she rationed everything, the way my mother rationed, the way the whole dying language seemed to exist only in fragments and refusals — then I would open the notebook and read her the letter aloud, every beautiful, menacing word, and watch what the last fluent speaker of a language does when she hears the voice of the man who nearly buried it, speaking again after fifty years, in ink.

I fell asleep with the light on and the 1973 letter on my chest, rising and falling with my breath like something alive. In the dream, the glasshouse was full of people I almost recognized, and they were all speaking at once, and I understood every word, and I woke at dawn having understood none of them, with the paper stuck to my skin and the sentence about claiming children printed faintly, in reverse, on my collarbone — as if the letter had been trying, all night, to write itself on me.
