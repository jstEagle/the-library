## 22. First Words for Strangers

The glasshouse has new glass now, which is the kind of sentence that sounds like a joke until you've spent a winter watching a man in coveralls fit each pane by hand, swearing softly in two languages and neither of them ours. The restoration crew found Ilse's seed trays under forty years of ivy and labeled them all *unknown* in their inventory, which I suppose is technically accurate and spiritually bankrupt. I bought the lot for a nominal sum and spent a week with my mother's old dictionaries trying to give them back their names.

Some of them I got. Some of them I invented, which Ilse would have called betrayal and then, after a decent interval, survival.

It's March when the class starts — small, as promised to no one in particular. Seven people on the first night, which felt like a crowd. A retired postman named Aldous who heard about us from an obituary, which is a sentence that would have made Ilse laugh her dry paper laugh. Two sisters in their sixties who grew up hearing their grandmother speak it over the phone and understood nothing but the tone, which they describe, both at once, as "scolding" and "lullaby." A linguistics student who takes notes on a laptop and whom I have decided not to hold against the profession. A man named Tomas who doesn't say why he's here and whose accent, when he finally speaks, makes my chest hurt, because it's the accent of the river valley, the one my mother tried to sand off herself and couldn't.

And a child. Marta, eight years old, granddaughter of one of the sisters, who comes because there's nowhere else to put her on Thursday evenings and stays because — she informs me with the gravity of a customs official — she likes the words better than the ones she already has.

I don't argue. I have stopped arguing with people about which words are worth keeping. It took me fifty-odd years and two funerals to learn that lesson; she arrived at it before nine.

* * *

The notebook is new. That was the decision, the one I made standing in Ilse's kitchen the morning after she died, holding her last dictation tapes against my sternum like something that might be confiscated. The old notebooks — eleven of them, green and grey and water-stained, the whole catalogued architecture of everything unsaid between me and Vera — those went into a box. Not thrown out. I'm not a saint. But boxed, taped, labeled in the majority language: *personal, do not open without me.*

The new notebook is red. Cheap. Spiral-bound, the kind schoolchildren use. On the first page, where I used to write *Inventory*, I wrote only:

**Things said.**

The entries are different now. Where the old notebooks recorded silences — *she didn't tell me about the photograph; I didn't ask about the father; neither of us said the word for stay* — this one records sentences. Actual ones, transcribed in the old alphabet, which my hand still writes slowly, like a person learning to sign her own name again.

*Entry one:* Vera, at the home, when I told her Ilse had died: *"Held sie gut?"* Did you hold her well? And I said yes, and she said, *"Dann ist es genug,"* then it is enough, and turned back to her window, and I wrote it down before the tears could blur the letters, because that's the discipline now — not cataloguing what's missing, but catching what arrives.

*Entry four:* Aldous, second class, mispronouncing the word for bread so badly that the sisters laughed until they had to sit down, and then teaching him the right way with the patience of a man who has waited his whole life for someone to teach him anything.

*Entry nine:* myself, saying aloud, to seven strangers and one child, the opening line of Ilse's story — the one about the girl who counted the greenhouse panes instead of sheep — and hearing the room go quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when a language that was almost buried moves through it alive.

That entry I wrote twice. Once in the old tongue, once in English underneath, and then I crossed out the English, not because translation is shameful but because I wanted the record to show which version came first. For once in my life, the original came first.

* * *

People ask, sometimes, gently, the way you ask someone about a scar — whether it's strange, teaching the language that was used against us. Whether Anselm's ghost doesn't sit in the corner of every lesson. I've learned to answer honestly, which is its own kind of discipline.

Yes. Some evenings he does. Not the man himself — the scholar's archive project collapsed after my recording of our conversation made its way, through channels I didn't ask about, to two newspapers and a very embarrassed university ethics board — but the shape of him. The temptation he embodied. The idea that a language is a treasure and treasures need guards, and guards need rules, and rules need someone to enforce them on the people who love the thing wrong.

Anselm believed the language belonged to the faithful. Ilse spent her last winter teaching me that it belongs to whoever grieves in it. Those are not the same religion. I know which congregation I'm sitting in.

So we have exactly one rule in the glasshouse, and I announce it the way Ilse announced hers, standing under the repaired ridge of the roof with the seedlings behind me: *Nobody here owns anything.* Not the words, not the stories, not the grief. You take what you need and you leave it changed and you pass it on or you don't, and either way the door is unlocked.

Tomas cried the first time I said it. He told me afterward, in the parking lot, in the valley accent, that he'd spent thirty years believing he'd been stolen from, and hadn't realized until that moment that he'd also been doing the stealing — hoarding the few phrases his grandmother left him like a miser, refusing to teach his own children because it felt too much like spending. He starts teaching his daughter next month. I gave him my spare dictionary. It felt like handing someone a lit match and trusting them with the fire, which is, I'm told, how all of this was supposed to work from the beginning.

* * *

Vera comes on Thursdays now, when she's able. The home sends a driver. She sits in the chair by the door — Ilse's old chair, though nobody says so — and mostly she watches, her face doing that thing dementia does, where expression arrives and departs like weather over water. Some weeks she knows me. Some weeks she knows her sister, long dead, and asks Casper — meaning someone else, someone 1954 — whether the frost has come off the pansies yet.

But the language reaches her where nothing else does anymore. That's the finding I can't publish anywhere and don't need to. When the sisters sing the planting song, Vera's mouth moves around the shapes of it. When Aldous mangles the word for bread, she corrects him — sharp, clear, *bröda, not broda, boy, were you raised in a barn* — and then looks astonished at herself, and laughs, and the laugh is my mother's laugh from before everything, from the photographs, from the years Anselm's circle nearly ate.

Last week she stayed after class while the others filed out into the cold, and she looked at the seed trays — my relabeled ones, the rescued names — and read three of them aloud without hesitation, fluently, like turning over in sleep, and then said to me, in the old tongue, *"Du hast ihre Handschrift,"* you have her handwriting. Meaning Ilse's. Meaning the way I form the letters, the slant of them, the stubborn little hooks.

I said thank you. In the old tongue. She nodded, satisfied, and asked again about the pansies, and I told her the frost was off, and both of those things were true.

*Entry twenty-two:* *Du hast ihre Handschrift.* Underneath, in English, because some things deserve to exist in both: *You have her handwriting.* I am choosing to believe she meant it as inheritance and not accusation. With my mother, the two were always printed on the same page anyway.

* * *

Marta asks questions the way water asks questions of stone — constantly, patiently, with total confidence in the eventual answer.

Tonight, near the end of class, while the sisters practice introductions and Aldous wrestles with the past tense like a man fighting a deckchair, she tugs my sleeve. She has the word written on her palm in ballpoint, copied off the board earlier, the letters slightly wrong in the way that shows she copied them by eye rather than understanding.

"What does this one mean?" she says. "*Blíva.*"

I look at the word on her hand. The word for *stay*. The word Ilse taught me in her last lesson, dawn coming up grey through the broken panes, her voice going thin as paper, telling me: *record everything, child, even this, especially this.* The word Vera never said to my father. The word Anselm weaponized into a cage. The word I carried in a closed box for a year, thinking it was too heavy, too loaded, too much mine.

Ilse would have wanted it guarded. I know that. I loved her, and I know that, and I do the other thing anyway.

"It means *to stay*," I say. "To remain somewhere. To keep being somewhere when leaving would be easier."

Marta considers this with the seriousness of a supreme court. "Like staying at Grandma's?"

"Exactly like staying at Grandma's."

"But bigger."

"Bigger," I agree. "It's the word you use when staying costs something. When you could go, and you don't."

She frowns at her palm. Then she looks up, and there is something in her face — bright, unguarded, eight years old and already fluent in the tone if not the grammar — that makes my throat close.

"So it's a hard word," she says.

"It's the hardest one we have," I tell her. "Which is why we're giving it away."

She nods, satisfied, and copies it more carefully onto the back of her hand, and runs off to interrupt the sisters' introductions, shouting *blíva, blíva* like a small revolutionary, and Aldous asks what the child is on about, and the sisters explain, and within a minute the whole room is using it, wrongly, joyfully, in sentences that would make Anselm rise from whatever circle of hell reserves seats for men who mistook custody for love.

I open the red notebook. I write:

*Entry thirty-one. Blíva. Given away, Thursday, 7:40 p.m., to everyone.*

Then I put down the pen, and turn on the lamp, and begin the lesson again — from the top, from the first word, from hello — because the light is still on, and the door is unlocked, and for the first time in the history of this language, there is nowhere any of it needs to be hidden.
