## 1. The Pitch of Empty Rooms

The piano had gone flat in the night, the way it always did when the house was grieving.

Marguerite noticed it before she noticed anything else — not the cold on the kitchen floor, not the black dress hanging on the closet door like a woman waiting to be let in, but the sound. A432 drifting toward A428, the middle C sulking a quarter-tone below where it belonged. Ten years of tuning this instrument had taught her its moods the way other people learned their spouses'. It went flat when the humidity dropped. It went sharp in August. And it went flat in the night, without weather's permission, whenever something in the house broke.

Her mother had died at 3:14 in the morning. Marguerite knew because she'd been awake, sitting up with the last of the morphine and the last of the conversation, and she'd heard it: a single low knock from the radiator in the hall, the one that sounded like a knuckle rapping twice on wood. *Someone's here,* that knock said. Or maybe, *someone's leaving.* The house had never been clear on which.

Now it was six-forty in the morning, the day of the funeral, and Marguerite stood in the parlor in her robe with her tuning kit open on the carpet like a surgeon's tray, because there was nothing else to do with her hands.

"You could just let it be flat for one day," Robert had said last night, on the phone from Boston, his voice already rehearsed into funeral smoothness. "People aren't coming for the piano, Marguerite."

"People are coming for the room," she'd said. "And the room is out of tune."

He hadn't understood. He never had. But then, Robert had left this house at eighteen and only ever touched it with money — the roof shingles one year, the oil bill every winter, checks that arrived like apologies from someone who didn't intend to stop doing whatever needed apologizing for. He heard a piano. She heard a room. That was the whole difference between them, distilled.

She pressed the damper pedal and struck C4, hard, letting the string speak. Then she leaned close, mute in hand, and listened past the note itself — past the fundamental, down into the overtones where the truth lived. Every tuner knows a piano tells you what's wrong with it if you listen long enough. A false beat in the tenor means a loose winding. A howl in the treble means a bad bridge pin. And underneath all of it, if you were Marguerite Dunmore, in this particular parlor, you could hear the radiators answering.

The old steam system ran through the walls like a second nervous system. In deep winter it sang — a whole choir of hisses and knocks, each radiator with its own voice, the parlor one a baritone that wheezed on cold mornings, the upstairs hall one a soprano that whistled through a valve nobody had replaced since Eisenhower. Her father — her father — had claimed he could tell the temperature by ear. Marguerite had gone further. She could tell the *weather* of the family by ear. Grief had a pitch. So did lying. Both of them lived somewhere around the low B flats, a muddy, unresolved interval that made the pipes knock at odd hours as though the house itself couldn't settle.

This morning the parlor radiator gave a long, low exhalation, steam finding its way through a pipe joint that had been weeping since 1997, and Marguerite thought: *there you are.* The house knew. The house had known since 3:14.

She worked the tuning hammer gently, raising the C by degrees, feeling the string resist and then give. There was a comfort in it no grief counselor would have prescribed — the pure mechanical honesty of it. A string is either in tune or it isn't. Nobody has ever successfully lied to a tuning fork.

*Say it,* the piano seemed to say, in the wordless way pianos say things. *You've been holding this note for ten years. Let it resolve.*

But she didn't. She tuned instead. E above middle C, then F, then the small climb up the octave, her hands steady even as her chest did that thing it had been doing for a decade — the tightening, the almost-speaking, the sentence that began *I* and ended nowhere.

By eight the piano was true again, and the parlor held its note like a held breath, and Marguerite went upstairs to put on the black dress that fit her exactly as well as it had at her father's funeral eleven years ago, which is to say like a costume borrowed from a stricter woman.

* * *

The house filled at noon the way a tide fills a cove — gradually, then all at once, and smelling faintly of other people's perfume.

They came in their dark coats with their covered dishes and their careful faces: the neighbors from the point, the women from St. Brendan's who had sat with her mother through the last Lent of her life, the man who plowed the driveway and had apparently decided this constituted a friendship deep enough to require a casserole. They said things like *she's at peace now* and *what a long road* and *you're a saint, Marguerite, an absolute saint*, and Marguerite received each phrase the way she received out-of-tune notes — politely, internally wincing, making the small adjustment required to keep the room sounding right.

Robert arrived at noon-thirty with his wife Diane, both of them Boston-polished, both of them wearing grief the way they wore their wedding rings — correctly, visibly, and without much thought anymore. He embraced her at the door. "You look tired," he said, which was his love language, such as it was.

"It's been a long ten years, Robert."

"I know." He squeezed her shoulder. "It's over now."

And there it was — the thing under the thing, surfacing for air. *It's over.* Not her mother's suffering. The *ten years*. The caretaking, the house, the whole slow sinking of her life into this place that he had long ago written off as a liability with good bones. He was already, she could feel it, doing arithmetic in the back of his skull. Square footage. Waterfront footage. What a buyer from away would pay for four acres and a view of Penobscot Bay.

She smiled the smile she'd practiced. The service hadn't even happened yet.

Father Dooley kept it brief, mercifully. Her mother had requested it — "No Latin, no twenty-minute homily, Marguerite, I'll be dead, I won't learn anything" — and so they got twenty minutes of psalm and eulogy, Robert reading a passage about the Prodigal Son that he had chosen, Marguerite suspected, without any awareness of its irony. The parlor piano waited silent in the corner, freshly tuned, holding its ground. Marguerite had offered to play. Robert had said the organist would handle the music at the church, and Marguerite had not argued, because arguing with Robert about music was like arguing with a man about whether water was wet while he drowned.

It was after — back at the house, the casserole hour, the hour of ham and Jell-O salad and murmured condolences — that the stranger came.

She saw him first from the hallway, standing at the edge of the parlor with his coat still on, looking not at the mourners but at the radiators. Actually looking at them, the way she looked at them. His hand was raised near the parlor radiator, hovering an inch from the iron, the way you'd hover a hand near a sleeping animal or a fire.

Then he turned, and she saw he was perhaps thirty-five, tall, with her mother's jaw — no. With *a* jaw. With a jaw she recognized from photographs in the attic she'd never been allowed to sort. Dark hair going gray early at the temples. Eyes that moved around the room cataloguing it, and stopped on Marguerite with an expression she could only call recognition, except that she had never seen him in her life.

He crossed the room to her. Conversations dimmed around him the way conversations do when someone moves through them with purpose.

"Mrs. Dunmore," he said. "I'm sorry for your loss. I'm Elias Vann." He shook her hand. His grip was cool and careful, like someone handling an instrument. "I knew your brother. Years ago."

"Robert?" She glanced across the room. "I didn't think Robert knew anyone from—"

"Not Robert." Something moved behind his face, quick as a fish under ice. "I'm sorry. This isn't — I'm not here to intrude. I'm here because I owed your mother a debt I never paid."

"We didn't know my mother owed anyone either."

"No," he said. "I don't imagine you did."

Across the room, Robert looked up from a conversation with the insurance man, and Marguerite watched the color leave her brother's face in real time — not gradually, not politely, but all at once, like a tide going out. He set down his glass. He started toward them and then stopped, caught between two instincts, and in the half-second of his hesitation Marguerite saw something she had never seen in her brother in forty-some years of knowing him.

Fear.

"Mr. Vann," Robert said, when he finally arrived, and his voice came out wrong, pitched too high, a false beat in the tenor. "I didn't expect—"

"I know you didn't." Elias Vann smiled, and it was a kind smile, which somehow made it worse. "It's been a long time, Uncle Robert."

The room did not go quiet. Rooms in Maine at funerals never fully go quiet; there is always a casserole dish clicking, always someone breathing. But Marguerite felt the whole house shift around her, the way it did when the temperature dropped fast — every pipe in the walls ticking, adjusting, trying to find a new pitch.

Because Vann wasn't a name from anywhere she knew. But the way the stranger had said *uncle*, easy and worn like a coin handled for decades —

That was a Moreau's voice. Her grandmother's voice, her father's voice, the name that had been sanded off the family like an old maker's mark, the name nobody used, the name her mother had once started to explain and then, catching sight of Marguerite's father's face across the kitchen, had folded away like linen and never taken out again.

Elias Vann turned back to her, and the parlor radiator knocked twice behind him, soft, like a knuckle on wood.

*Someone's here,* it said. Or maybe, *someone's leaving.*

Marguerite, who had spent ten years not asking questions, opened her mouth — and found, for the first time, that the question had gotten there ahead of her.
