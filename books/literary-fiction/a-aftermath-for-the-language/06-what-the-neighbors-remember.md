## 6. What the Neighbors Remember

I made a list. It was the only way I knew how to do anything anymore — the funeral had taught me that, the way it had taught me that grief, properly administered, could be folded into a schedule like any other appointment. The list had fourteen names on it. Musicians, mostly. People who had played with my mother in the years when Lagos still smelled of diesel and possibility, when her voice — *their* voice, I was beginning to suspect, though I refused yet to say so even to myself — had moved through the clubs on the Island like weather.

I called them in the order of the list, because alphabetizing felt like a small mercy I could perform for a woman who had never once, in my memory, been disorganized. She had kept her scores in manila folders labeled by year. She had kept her grief, apparently, in sealed envelopes.

* * *

The first was Baba Ekwueme, the old guitarist, who received me on the sagging veranda of his house in Surulere with a fan that turned more out of habit than effect and a pot of tea that had been stewing, by the taste of it, since the funeral. He was ninety-one and his hands, which had once made a Fender Telecaster sound like rain on a tin roof, now shook so badly he held his glass with both of them, like a man drinking from a river.

"Your mother," he said, when I told him why I'd come. "Ah. Ngozi Okafor." He said the full name the way you set down something heavy. "The most disciplined musician I ever played with. And the coldest."

"Cold?"

He waved the word away, or tried to; his hand described a small exhausted circle in the air. "No, no. Not cold like ice. Cold like — a room that has been swept so clean there is nothing left in it. You understand? She would arrive at rehearsal with everything written out. Every part. Every harmony. Written, you understand, not just remembered. Other bandleaders, they carry the song in here—" he tapped his temple, missed slightly, tapped his cheekbone instead, "—and it comes out different every night, alive. Your mother's songs came out the same every night. Perfect. Like photographs of songs."

I wrote that down. *Photographs of songs.* It seemed important, though I couldn't have said why, in the way that all of it seemed important now — every stray adjective from every old mouth, each one a splinter of light I was trying to assemble into a window.

"Was she happy, in those years?" I asked. "The eighties. The studio years."

Baba Ekwueme looked at me for a long time. Outside, a generator coughed to life somewhere down the street, and the fan turned, and turned.

"Happy," he said finally, "is not the word I would use. Careful. Careful is the word. She was the most careful woman I ever met. A careful woman is a woman who is hiding something, or hiding from something. Sometimes both."

* * *

The second interview was Mrs. Adaeze Nwosu, who had sung backup on the early sessions and now ran a small shop of school supplies in Yaba that smelled of chalk dust and new exercise books. She was smaller than I remembered her from childhood — she had been at our house once, when I was perhaps seven, and I remembered mostly her laugh, which had filled our mother's carefully swept rooms like a bird loose indoors.

"She was a genius," Mrs. Nwosu said flatly, before I had finished my first sentence. "Say whatever you like, write whatever you are writing, but write that. Ngozi Okafor was a genius."

"And was she—" I hesitated, choosing the word carefully, which is to say, dishonestly, "—generous? With her music?"

"Generous." Mrs. Nwosu turned the word over like a coin she suspected of being counterfeit. She was arranging exercise books on a shelf as we talked, squaring their edges with a precision that reminded me, unpleasantly, of my mother's manila folders. "She paid the band well. On time, every time, in an industry where musicians wait months to be robbed. She was generous with money." A pause. The books were squared. "Music is not money, Isandro. You ask a musician if another musician was generous, we do not think of naira. We think of — the song. Who the song belonged to. Whether she shared it or hoarded it. Whether, when she stood on the stage and the people shouted her name, the name was the right one."

My pen stopped.

"What do you mean, the right one?"

Mrs. Nwosu looked at me then, really looked, and I saw her decide something — decide against it, I think, though I would not know that for certain until later. "I mean," she said, "that fame is a photograph, and photographs crop. Ask her friends. Ask her enemies, if she had any. Ask—" and here she stopped herself, pressed her lips together, and picked up another stack of exercise books as though our conversation were inventory that needed putting away. "You should talk to Emeka. Emeka Obiora. He played trumpet. He will talk. Emeka always talks."

There was a warning in it, I realized afterward. There had been a warning in it, and I had driven straight through it, the way you drive through a yellow light because you are late and the road is empty and you believe, the way the young believe and the guilty believe, that nothing is coming from the other direction.

* * *

Emeka Obiora received me in the evening, in a bar off the Ikorodu Road that had once been a nightclub and was now a kind of museum of itself — the same mirrored pillars, dulled; the same stage, reduced to a shelf for a television playing football with the sound off. He was drunk in the methodical way of men who have been drinking since afternoon, which is to say he was drunk the way other men are sober: with full command of his grievances.

"Ngozi's boy," he said, or possibly girl — he squinted at me with the frank taxonomic curiosity of the very drunk — "Ngozi's child. Sit. Sit. You want to know her. Everyone wants to know her now that God has taken her. Where were all you wanters when she was alive, eh? When she sat in that house like a queen in a freezer?"

I sat. I ordered him another of whatever he was having. I had learned by then that the fastest route through a musician's memory was not a question but a drink.

"She was the best of us," Emeka announced to the bar at large, to the muted football, to the boy wiping tables who had never been born when the music was made. "The best and the worst of us. The most talented and the most afraid. You know what she was afraid of?" He leaned across the table. His breath was a weather system of beer and years. "Being forgotten. That was her whole life, that fear. Everything else was furniture arranged around that fear."

"And the music?" I asked. "How did she work? Did she collaborate?"

"Collaborate." He laughed, and it was a terrible laugh, a laugh with something broken at the bottom of it, like a bottle dropped down a stairwell. "You sound like a lawyer. Did she collaborate. Yes. She collaborated the way the ocean collaborates with the river."

I waited. I had gotten good at waiting; the house of sealed letters had taught me that too. The bar boy brought the fresh drink and Emeka wrapped his hand around it and did not drink, holding it like a prop, like something he had put down on the table to mark his place in the story.

"There was a woman once," he said, quieter now, and the bar noise seemed to lean in around us, or I leaned in, or both. "Early days. Before the albums, before the name was a name. There was a woman who was always at the rehearsals. Always. She had a voice like — what is the thing that cuts? Not a knife. The other one."

"Razor?"

"Blade," Emeka said, satisfied. "A voice like a blade. And she and your mother would sit at the piano for hours, and I would stand there with my trumpet getting cold, because nobody tells the trumpet player anything, the trumpet player is furniture — and they would build the songs. Together. I watched them build them. Brick by brick. Your mother at the keys, the woman singing, and the two of them arguing over a line the way you argue over a child's name, because it matters that much, because the song is a child—"

He stopped. He drank, finally, a long swallow, and when he set the glass down his hand was not steady, and it was not only the drink.

"What happened to her?" I asked. "The woman."

Emeka looked at me. And here is the thing I will carry, the thing that sits in my chest now like a swallowed coal: he looked at me with pity. Not guilt, exactly, or not only guilt. Pity, the way you look at someone standing in a house whose foundations you know are rotten, and they are asking you about the paint.

"Your mother didn't write everything she signed," he said.

The bar noise came back all at once, like water rushing into a hole. The television flickered. Somewhere a bottle cap hissed open.

"Say that again," I said.

But he was already retreating, I could see it happening, the sober sentence being dragged back down into the drink where he kept his dangerous things. "I said what I said. Old men say things. Old men are furniture, nobody tells the furniture anything." He waved at the boy for another drink. "Ask the lawyer. Ask the papers. Ask—" and he made a vague, collapsing gesture toward the whole world, "—whoever keeps what was kept."

"Emeka." I put money on the table, more than the drinks cost, and he looked at it and then at me. "Her name. The woman's name. Please."

He stood, slowly, gathering his jacket, his dignity, his decades. At the door he paused, and did not turn around, and said, so quietly I almost lost it under the football commentary:

"We called her Ada. But that was not all of her name. Her name had more in it. Like a song with a verse nobody sings."

And then he was gone into the Lagos night, which swallowed him the way it swallows everything, kindly and completely, and I sat alone at the table with my notebook full of other people's memories, none of which agreed with each other, all of which were true.

* * *

I drove home with the windows down because the car felt sealed, and everything sealed felt like her now — the envelopes, the folders, the swept room of her. The city poured past in its usual river of light and dust, danfo buses preaching their painted gospels, and I thought: *a genius; the coldest; careful; afraid of being forgotten; the ocean collaborating with the river; a woman called Ada whose name had more in it.*

Four interviews. Four women and men who had loved her or feared her or both, and every account a different portrait, and the portraits did not hang together on any wall I owned. Hagiography, I thought. I had looked the word up that afternoon, unable to remember where I'd first seen it. The writing of saints' lives. The making of the holy out of the human, by leaving things out.

That was the part that sat in me all the way home, that turned over and over like a key trying doors: not what they said, but what they had all, in their different ways, declined to say. Mrs. Nwosu pressing her lips shut over a name. Baba Ekwueme's careful room. Emeka's pity, aimed at me like a lamp aimed at something he didn't want me to see yet. They had all spent forty years leaving things out, and they had done it — this was the thought that arrived as I pulled into the street, that arrived with the terrible gentleness of a truth you have known for a while and only now permit to sit down — they had done it *for her*. Out of loyalty. Out of love, maybe. Or out of the simpler economy of the music business, where everyone's silence is someone's salary.

And I had spent the day collecting their silences and calling it research.

At the house, I went straight to the desk, past the boxes of photographs I had not yet opened, past the piano with its dust like snowfall, and took out the notebook — her notebook, the one with the songs in her handwriting — and set it under the lamp. And then, because I could not help it, because the day had gotten into me like dye into water, I went and fetched the boxes anyway, and knelt on the floor of her study, and began pulling photographs.

I was looking for a woman with a voice like a blade. I did not yet know her face. But I knew, with the certainty of a man who has just been told the foundations are rotten, that she would be there — at the edge of the frames, in the margin of the years — and that my mother's scissors would have found her first.

In the third box, under a 1987 calendar and a bundle of receipts, I found an envelope of photographs from a studio session I had never heard mentioned: *Studio 2, Apapa,* someone had written on the front. I spread them out on the carpet under the lamp.

And at the edge of the second photograph, half-turned away, half-cut by the frame itself — a woman at a second microphone.
