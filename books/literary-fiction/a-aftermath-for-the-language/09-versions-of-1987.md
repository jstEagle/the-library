## 9. Versions of 1987

From Isandro's notebook, undated:

*Two women. One year. I have now heard the story three times, from three mouths, and I no longer believe there is a single 1987 waiting somewhere to be found. There are only versions, each one polished smooth by decades of handling, each one true in the way that a key is true — it fits a lock, but only its own. What follows is my attempt to lay the versions side by side without choosing among them. I am not a judge. I am the child of one of the witnesses. That disqualifies me and obligates me at once.*

* * *

Adaora's version, told on a Thursday evening after the children had gone, over tea gone cold in mismatched cups:

"We met at a funeral, of all things. 1984. Her aunt's, in Surulere. I was playing the organ because nobody else could be persuaded, and afterward she came up to me and said, 'You play grief like it's an argument you're winning.' I thought, this woman is either going to be my best friend or my worst headache. She was both. She was both for eleven years."

Adaora's hands wrapped around the cup. Isandro noticed the knuckles, broad and scarred, a working musician's hands, nothing like their mother's slender, ringless ones.

"We wrote everything together. Everything. She had the polish — she'd been to the conservatory, she knew her theory like a lawyer knows his loopholes. But I had the ear. The raw ear. She'd bring a melody that was beautiful and dead, like a bird in a glass case, and I would find the thing inside it that could fly. 'Eziokwu' — you know that one? Everyone knows that one. The bridge, the part where the key lifts? That was mine. She knew it. She'd sing it at me across the piano and laugh and say, 'Adaora, what would I do without you?' And I'd say, 'You'd be fine, but you'd be boring.' We laughed. God, we laughed."

"Then the contract," Isandro said.

"Then the contract." Adaora set the cup down with a small, precise click. "Highlife Records wanted her. Just her. They said a woman fronting her own name was already a risk, two women was a novelty act, and novelty acts don't sell past one album. She came to me with the paper and said, 'Sign as a session musician, it's just for the lawyers, we'll know the truth between us.' And I —" her voice caught, not with tears but with the effort of an old anger lifting something too heavy, "— I was pregnant. Did you know that? No. Of course you didn't. Nobody knows. I was four months pregnant, alone, my father dead, my mother in Enugu with nothing. I signed. I signed because she promised me the second album would carry both our names, and because a promise from Ngozi Okafor felt like scripture. She had that voice. When she said a thing, the world arranged itself around it."

"And then?"

"And then the album came out and my name wasn't on it. Not in the liner notes, not in the credits, not anywhere. I called her. She said the label had cut it without telling her. I believed her — that's the part that shames me, I believed her for a whole year. Then the second album. Same thing. And when I went to her house, to her face, she said —" Adaora looked out the window at the dying light over the community hall's cracked yard. "She said, 'Adaora, be reasonable. What would your name on it change? The money is the same. The music is the same.' As if the music were a loaf of bread we were dividing. As if my name were a garnish."

She turned back. Her eyes were dry and very bright.

"I stopped speaking to her in 1988. I heard the third album from a market stall in Onitsha and every song on it had my fingerprints on it, and my daughter —" the pause here lasted four full seconds, and Isandro learned later what filled it, though Adaora did not say it that night, "— my daughter was two months dead, and Ngozi Okafor was on the radio being called a genius. So. That is my version. You wanted it. Now you have it, and you can carry it home like a stone."

* * *

Isandro's notebook, that night:

*I carried it home like a stone. Set it on the kitchen table next to the notebook, next to the photograph with 'Adaora — do not contact' on the back. And then, because I could not sleep, I did what I have always done with unbearable things: I made a list.*

*Adaora's claims:*
*1. Co-authorship of the entire early catalog, uncredited.*
*2. A promise of shared credit on album two, broken.*
*3. A pregnancy in 1987 — a daughter, apparently, now dead.*
*4. My mother's direct refusal to credit her, in person, not once but twice.*

*Against this I have only my mother's silence, which until this week I mistook for modesty. I need her side. But the dead do not testify. Or — I wrote this and then sat for a long time looking at it — or they testify only in the documents they leave behind, and I have a house full of documents I have not finished reading.*

* * *

The second version came from the letters. Not the sealed ones — those Isandro had cataloged but not yet opened, a discipline that was becoming its own small torture — but from a folder at the bottom of the piano bench, misfiled under tax receipts. A letter, typed, on Highlife Records letterhead, dated March 1987, addressed to N. Okafor:

*Miss Okafor — The label has reviewed the demo sessions and confirms its position. Solo billing is a condition of the advance. We are aware of the contribution of the other party and consider it a work-for-hire matter to be settled between you. We will not be party to disputes over authorship. Sign where indicated or the deal reverts.*

And beneath it, in the margin, in his mother's small, upright hand, a single line that Isandro read perhaps forty times before the words stopped rearranging themselves:

*She will understand. She has to. There is no other way to get us both out.*

*Us both,* Isandro wrote in the notebook. *Not 'me.' 'Us.' She believed, in March of 1987, that she was saving two people. That the theft was a bridge. That Adaora would cross it with her and understand on the other side.*

*Adaora did not cross. Adaora was left standing on the near bank with a signed contract and a swelling belly, watching the bridge burn behind a woman who called it a door.*

*Both things can be true. That is the discovery that keeps me awake: both things can be entirely true, and the space between them is where a friendship died.*

* * *

The third version came from Chidi, of all people, though not willingly.

They met at the house — the house was still technically shared estate, still half theirs each, though Chidi had not slept in it since the funeral — and Isandro had not planned to ask. But Chidi was in the kitchen making jollof the way their mother used to, badly, too much pepper, and something about the smell loosened him.

"She came to Lagos with nothing, you know," Chidi said, stirring, not looking up. "Before us. Before Daddy. She used to tell us she slept three nights in the studio because she couldn't afford the bus home to Yaba. Whatever you're digging up, Isandro, whatever it is you think you've found — remember that she earned every note. Nobody gave her anything."

"Did she ever mention someone named Adaora?"

The spoon stopped. One second. Two. Then it resumed, slower.

"Once. I was maybe ten. A woman came to the house while you were at school — I was supposed to be doing homework but I was listening at the door, the way I listened at every door in that house. She was crying. Not loudly. The quiet kind, the kind that's worse. And Mummy stood in the doorway with her arms folded and said, 'You signed, Adaora. You signed.' And the woman said, 'I signed because you told me to,' and Mummy said, 'I told you because it was the only way,' and then she closed the door, and I ran back to my homework, and I never asked. You didn't ask Mummy questions. You know that better than anyone."

Chidi finally looked up. His face was doing the thing their mother's face used to do — the composed thing, the curated thing, grief filed under a calm so practiced it looked like peace.

"Whatever you've found," he said, "ask yourself what she was carrying. A woman alone in Lagos in 1987, pregnant — no. Not her. But a woman alone, with a label holding her whole future in one clause. You think she had choices? You think any of us have the choices we pretend we had?"

"She had one choice she didn't take," Isandro said. "She could have told the truth later. Any year after. There were twenty years after."

Chidi turned back to the pot. The pepper smoke rose between them like a curtain.

"Later is a country nobody can afford to live in," he said. "You'll learn that."

* * *

Isandro's notebook, three days later:

*I have begun interviewing the old circle — the trumpeters, the bassists, the one surviving producer — and their accounts are hagiographies with the saints' names worn off. Everyone agrees my mother was brilliant. Everyone agrees she was generous. No one can quite agree on what she was generous with. One man told me she "wrote like the songs came through her from somewhere else." Another said, "She wrote like a woman being chased." I am starting to think both were right, and that what chased her had a name, and the name was 1987.*

*But here is what troubles me most. Not the contract. Not even the pregnancy, though God, the pregnancy — a daughter, a dead daughter, a whole grief I never knew existed in that house. What troubles me is the margin note. 'There is no other way to get us both out.' She wrote it as if she were the rescuer. And I believe she believed it. That is the horror that isn't horror: not a villain, but a woman who committed a lifelong theft and filed it, in her own ledger, under sacrifice.*

*Adaora's version makes my mother a thief. My mother's version makes her a martyr. Chidi's version makes her a survivor. The truth, if there is one, is probably a fourth thing — smaller and more ordinary than all three: a frightened woman in a room with a contract and a friend, choosing the contract, and then spending forty years not looking at the friend's face.*

*I need the paper. The original. Not memories, not margins. The contract itself — whose name, whose signature, whose seal. If I'm going to do what I think I'm going to do, I will need something no one can argue with.*

*I called the lawyer this morning. He was careful on the phone, the way lawyers are careful, but he said a thing I haven't stopped hearing since: "The estate files are extensive, Mr. Okafor. Your mother kept everything."*

*Yes, I thought. She did. That was always the problem.*

* * *

That night, unable again to sleep, Isandro sat at the untouched piano — the upright no one had played since the funeral, since long before the funeral, since some year Isandro could not name — and opened the fallboard and looked at the keys without touching them. On the music rack, someone had left a single sheet, yellowed, in a plastic sleeve. Not sheet music. A photograph, kept where a musician keeps the thing she cannot stop looking at.

Two young women at a piano, laughing. The camera had caught them mid-laugh, heads thrown back, one hand each on the same keyboard as if playing a four-hand piece that existed nowhere but between them. On the left, unmistakably, a younger version of his mother. On the right, a woman with broad scarred knuckles and a laugh you could hear through forty years of paper.

Adaora. Before the cutting away. Before the cropping. Whole, and touching the same keys.

Isandro turned it over. On the back, in his mother's hand, a date — *June 1987* — and beneath it, smaller, almost erased by the pen's hesitation:

*The last good month.*

Isandro sat in the dark with the photograph until the streetlight outside went from amber to grey. Then they wrote one line in the notebook, and closed it, and in the morning called the estate lawyer and asked, for the first time, to see everything.
