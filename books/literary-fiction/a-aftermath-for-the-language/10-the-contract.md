## 10. The Contract

The lawyer's office smelled of toner and old carpet, the particular staleness of a room where important things are kept and no one lives. Isandro arrived at nine sharp on a Tuesday in late October, carrying a canvas tote bag they had bought specifically for this purpose — plain, unbranded, large enough for paper — because some part of them had already decided that whatever they found today would need to be carried out into the world.

"Mr. Okafor." Mr. Adeyemi rose from behind his desk with the careful courtesy of a man who had spent forty years handling other people's griefs and had learned to move slowly around them, the way one moves around a sleeping animal. "I've prepared everything you requested. The estate files, the correspondence logs, the — " he paused, adjusting his glasses — "the business papers. Publishing agreements, royalty statements, that sort of thing."

"How much is there?"

"Four boxes." He said it without inflection, but Isandro heard the weight beneath it: four boxes was a lot of paper for a woman who had supposedly lived a quiet life, a woman whose public story — the one printed in the obituaries, repeated by the radio hosts, recited by Chidi at the funeral with such practiced fluency — was of a private artist who wrote for herself, who shunned the industry, who gave her songs to the world reluctantly and rarely.

Isandro's mother, Ngozi Okafor, had been many things to many people. To her children she had been a silence with a body attached, a woman who answered questions with questions, who could sit through an entire Sunday dinner without offering a single unprompted sentence about herself. To the small circle of musicians who still spoke her name with reverence, she had been a genius of restraint. And to whoever ran the late-night community station that played her song to strangers — she had been, apparently, a thief.

Isandro set the tote bag down. "Where do we start?"

* * *

They started with the boxes themselves, which told their own story before a single document was read. Box one was orderly: folders labeled in Ngozi's narrow, upright hand, dates in sequence, nothing out of place. Box two was nearly as tidy. But box three had been packed in haste or in avoidance — papers shoved in sideways, envelopes stuffed with clippings, a rubber band around a bundle of receipts so brittle it crumbled when Isandro touched it. And box four was sealed with packing tape, wrapped twice around, and marked on top in the same narrow hand:

**DO NOT OPEN UNTIL AFTER.**

After what, the note did not say. After the funeral, presumably. After her death. After — Isandro thought, running a thumb along the tape — after I am no longer here to stop you.

Mr. Adeyemi watched from across the desk. "She left instructions that the fourth box be released only to you," he said. "Not your brother. You."

"Why me?"

"She didn't say." He folded his hands. "Though I will observe that she was very specific. She came in person, twice, to make sure I understood. That was unusual for her. Most clients give instructions over the phone. Your mother came and sat where you're sitting and read every line before she signed."

Isandro looked at the box. It was not heavy, exactly, but it had the density of things long undisturbed, paper settling into itself like sediment. They did not open it. Not yet. Some instinct — the same instinct that had made them catalog the house rather than ransack it, that had turned grief into inventory — said: *in order*. Start at the beginning. Let the documents testify in the order they were born.

So they opened box one instead, and the morning became an archaeology.

* * *

Paperwork has a texture that memory does not. Memory is soft-edged, forgiving, prone to flattering its subject. Paperwork is merciless. It records amounts and dates; it initials amendments; it keeps carbon copies of things people said with pens instead of mouths. By eleven o'clock Isandro had learned more about their mother's career than forty years of family dinners had yielded, and almost all of it contradicted the legend.

There had been a label. Not a major one — a mid-sized operation called Meridian Sound, run out of a converted warehouse in Surulere, its letterhead featuring a stylized record that was also, if you squinted, a rising sun. There had been contracts, plural: session agreements, mechanical licenses, a distribution deal with a French company that had gone bankrupt in 1991 owing Ngozi what the statements suggested was real money. There had been tours — three of them, regional, documented in hotel receipts and per diem forms. The woman who "shunned the industry" had filed expense reports.

And there had been songs. Dozens of them. Isandro counted the titles listed across the royalty statements, matching them against the notebook they now kept in the tote bag like a second passport. Song after song, title after title, each one credited in the paperwork to **N. OKAFOR**, sole author, sole signatory, sole recipient of every payment line.

Isandro had grown up hearing these titles the way other children grew up hearing scripture. *"Ekwueme." "Water Finds Its Level." "The Long Rain."* Songs their mother hummed while cooking, never performed, never explained. Songs that had appeared on two albums released in the late eighties and then vanished from print when Meridian collapsed — which meant, Isandro realized with a slow cold feeling, that most of the world had never heard them at all. Except one. Except the song from the radio, the song that had started all of this, playing last month out of a tinny speaker at midnight while Isandro sat in a parked car outside the estate lawyer's building and felt the curated past crack down its middle.

*"Egwu Mmiri"* — the water song. Credited on air, the DJ had said, to Ngozi Okafor. Word for word identical to the notebook, including the verse crossed out with a single decisive line, as though the pen had wanted to kill it cleanly rather than wound it.

Isandro pulled the notebook out and laid it beside the royalty statement. The crossed-out verse. The verse Adaora Eze had not yet explained, though Isandro had begun to understand, from the shape of the silence around it, that it belonged to someone.

"Mr. Adeyemi," Isandro said. "The publishing agreement. For the first album. Do you have the original?"

He did. He lifted it from the second box with both hands, the way one lifts something that might be fragile or might be dangerous, and laid it flat between them.

* * *

It was a handsome document, in the way that instruments of consequence are handsome. Heavy cream paper, typed on a machine with a slightly failing *e*, four pages fastened with a brass brad that had oxidized green at the edges. MERIDIAN SOUND (NIG.) LTD. PUBLISHING AND AUTHORSHIP AGREEMENT. Dated the ninth of March, 1987. Isandro read it once quickly, the way you read anything you already fear, and then again slowly, word by word, because the fear had been correct and now required witnesses.

Clause 3 defined the Work: a catalogue of twenty-two compositions, listed on Schedule A. Clause 4 assigned copyright. Clause 7 divided royalties. Clause 11 bound the Author to warrant that the Work was original, that no other party held any claim to it, that the Author was —

Isandro stopped.

Clause 11, sub-section (b): *The Author warrants that he or she is the sole creator of the Works herein described and that no other person, partnership, or entity holds any right, title, or interest therein.*

Sole creator. Sole. The word sat on the page with the confidence of a fact, and facts, Isandro had learned in the past month, are just stories that won paperwork arguments.

But it was the signature block that made the room go quiet inside Isandro's chest. Because Isandro had spent weeks now looking at their mother's handwriting — on the sealed letters, on the unlabeled photographs, on the note taped to the fourth box — and knew it the way you know a face. Narrow, upright, disciplined. A hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath.

**NGOZI OKAFOR**, the signature block read. One name. One signature. One date.

And below it, a second block, printed and ready, waiting since 1987 for ink that never came:

**CO-AUTHOR:** ______________________

The blank line stretched across the page like an absence with a shape. Someone had been expected. Someone had been provided for, in the architecture of the document, in the foresight of whoever drafted it — and then, somewhere between the drafting and the signing, that someone had been removed. Not crossed out. Not struck through, the way the verse in the notebook had been struck through. Simply never filled in. Erased so early and so completely that even the erasure left no mark.

Except one. At the bottom of the final page, in the space reserved for the notary, a stamp and a signature: **B. A. NWOSU, COMMISSIONER FOR OATHS**, and a date, and a registration number.

"You're frowning," Mr. Adeyemi said gently.

"There should be two names here."

"I couldn't say. I wasn't—" He began, then stopped himself, and Isandro watched him make the small professional calculation of a man deciding how much truth a client can hold. "Actually," he said, "I can say one thing. When your mother executed her will, she asked me to verify the chain of title on her catalogue. Standard practice — you can't bequeath what you don't clearly own. I reviewed these agreements myself, years ago." He tapped the blank co-author line with one finger, lightly, as if it might bite. "I noticed this. I asked her about it."

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "What did she say?"

"She said the second author declined to sign." He paused. "That was her exact phrasing. *Declined.* As though it were a choice freely made. As though someone had been offered a share of a lifetime's work and walked away from it." He took off his glasses and cleaned them on his tie, a gesture Isandro recognized as the lawyer's equivalent of a shrug. "I didn't press. It wasn't my place, and frankly, the agreement was valid either way. A warranty clause doesn't require a second signature to function. It requires only that the first signature be telling the truth."

"And was it?"

Mr. Adeyemi put his glasses back on. Behind the lenses his eyes were tired and kind. "Mr. Okafor, I draft documents. I don't audit souls." He straightened the contract on the desk, aligning its edges with the desk's edge, a small act of order against disorder. "But I'll tell you what I can tell you. This notary — B. A. Nwosu — was well known in those days. Half the music business in Lagos swore oaths in front of him. He retired, oh, fifteen years ago now. And he is, as of quite recently, in the St. Agnes care home off the Badagry road. My firm handles his affairs too, as it happens." He met Isandro's eyes. "His memory comes and goes, they tell me. But men near the end often find their memories come back very clearly indeed. Something about having less future to store things in."

Isandro looked down at the contract. The brass brad. The cream paper. The blank line where a name should have been, waiting thirty-seven years for someone to ask it a question.

"What's wrong with him?" Isandro asked, though the lawyer had already said it — dying, the word had been *dying*, dressed in euphemism.

"Emphysema, I believe. And age. He's ninety-one." Mr. Adeyemi slid a copy of the contract across the desk — he had prepared a copy, Isandro noticed, before Isandro ever asked, which meant the lawyer had understood what this was becoming before Isandro had admitted it to themselves. "You should know that whatever you intend to do with this, the document itself proves very little. A blank line proves an absence, not a theft. Warranties are broken every day and discovered every decade. If you want more than an absence—" he gathered the folder, squared it, handed it over— "you'll need a witness."

* * *

Isandro walked out into the October heat with the tote bag against their hip and the city doing its indifferent noon business around them: okada horns, a hawker's tray of groundnuts glinting, the smell of asphalt and frying oil and rain that hadn't fallen yet. They stood on the pavement for a full minute without moving, and let the truth of the morning settle into its final shape.

Justice, Isandro had always believed, was an abstraction — a fine word for funerals and opinion pieces, a word that lived comfortably next to *closure* and *moving on*. Their mother had raised them on softer words. *Let it rest. Some things aren't worth the trouble. Peace is better than being right.* Isandro had inherited those words along with the piano and the notebooks and the smile that ended conversations, and had carried them for forty years like furniture too heavy to move.

But justice was not abstract. Justice was a cream-colored document with a brass brad and a blank line. Justice was a registration number and a notary's stamp. Justice had a file number, a chain of custody, a schedule of exhibits. Justice, it turned out, had paperwork — mountains of it, decades of it, signed and stamped and filed away by ordinary men in ordinary offices who assumed no one would ever come asking.

Someone had come asking now.

In the taxi home Isandro called St. Agnes from the passenger seat, phone pressed to their ear against the road noise, and asked for a Mr. B. A. Nwosu. A pause. Shuffling. Then a voice, young and apologetic: visiting hours were two to four, family only, but — the voice lowered — for a man like that, at this stage, most rules bent. He didn't get many visitors. His mind wandered, they should warn me. Some days he thought it was 1988. Some days he asked for people who had died years ago.

"Some days," the voice added, almost as an afterthought, "he talks about the studio. About the singers. We just let him talk."

Isandro closed their eyes. Through the taxi window the city streamed past, and somewhere in it, in a care home off the Badagry road, a ninety-one-year-old man was waiting — perhaps remembering, perhaps already back in 1988, back in a Surulere warehouse with its rising-sun letterhead, back in the room where a blank line stayed blank and a stamp came down on cream paper and made a theft look, forever afterward, like a fact.

Two o'clock tomorrow, Isandro told the voice. I'll be there at two.

The call ended. In the tote bag, against Isandro's knee, the copied contract lay flat and cool, its blank line facing upward — an absence with a shape, waiting, like everything else in this inheritance, to finally be named.
