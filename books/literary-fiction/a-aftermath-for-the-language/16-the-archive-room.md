## 16. The Archive Room

The retired engineer's name was Emeka Bassey, and he smelled of pipe tobacco and machine oil, a combination that had probably been illegal in the building he was about to take me into for the last twenty years of his career there.

We sat in his car in the lot of a shopping plaza that had died sometime in the previous decade — a pharmacy with paper over its windows, a nail salon hanging on by its neon fingernails — and he laid out the plan the way he must once have laid out tape splices: flat, precise, without flourish.

"Third floor of the Meridian building," he said. "Archive room. Climate-controlled, which is why the tapes are still playable at all. The door has an electronic lock now, but the frame is original to 1989, and the strike plate is held by four screws some contractor stripped out years ago. A strong shoulder and a blanket will do it."

"You've thought about this," I said.

"I've thought about it every night since you called me." He looked at his hands on the wheel. They were enormous hands, the knuckles like walnuts. "I ran sessions in that building for thirty-one years. I heard things go into those archive boxes that never came out again. Songs that were recorded beautifully and then — nothing. Not rejected. *Erased.* There's a difference. Rejection is a decision. Erasure is a policy." He turned to look at me, and his eyes were wet in a way that embarrassed us both, so we pretended they weren't. "Your mother's voice was on half of them. And underneath her voice, most nights, was another voice. I used to think it was a ghost. Then I met Adaora Eze at a wedding in 1994 and I understood what ghosts actually sound like when they're still alive."

* * *

He was seventy-eight years old and insisted on driving, so I sat in the passenger seat of his dented Corolla at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday night while he navigated the empty avenues toward the Meridian Building with the unhurried confidence of a man who had driven this route ten thousand times and resented none of them until recently.

"Why now?" I asked him. "Why help me? You could have stayed quiet. You stayed quiet for thirty years."

He took a corner slowly, both hands on the wheel, as if the car were a difficult instrument.

"When my wife was dying," he said, "she made me promise three things. One, I would finally fix the gutter. Two, I would learn to cook something beyond rice. Three—" He stopped at a red light even though the streets were empty, and I understood that this was a man who did not break rules lightly, which meant breaking one cost him something real. "Three, I would stop keeping other people's secrets for free. She said secrets kept for free are just debts you owe strangers. I didn't understand her then. I do now. The label paid me well for thirty-one years, Isandro. But they never paid me for the silence. That part I gave away. Like a fool. Like a man giving away his own blood."

The light changed. We drove on.

* * *

The Meridian Building at midnight was a different organism than by day. By day it was a mid-rise of glass and beige ambition, home to insurance brokers and a dental practice; by night it exhaled through its ventilation system a low mechanical sigh, and the lobby lights ran on timers that left pools of amber at intervals down the corridor, like stage lighting for a play with no audience.

Emeka badged us through the side entrance with a card that should not have worked and did — "they never deactivate the old engineers' cards," he murmured, "it would be an admission that we existed" — and we rode the freight elevator up through the building's dark throat, standing side by side among the smell of dust burning off the elevator's heating elements.

Third floor. The corridor here was carpeted in something wine-dark and industrial, and the doors along it bore the names of departments that had been reorganized out of existence: A&R DEVELOPMENT, ARTIST RELATIONS, TAPE OPERATIONS. The last door was ours. It was steel-faced, windowless, and bore a card reader glowing a patient red.

Emeka pressed his palm flat against the door, the way you'd touch the flank of a horse.

"The lock is new," he said. "The door is not. Everything in this company that matters is old and pretending to be new." He produced from his bag a folded moving blanket and a rubber mallet, and handed me the mallet. "You're younger. Hit where I tell you. Aim low, near the strike plate. The blanket keeps it quiet-ish. Nothing keeps it quiet entirely."

My hands were shaking. I want to record that honestly, because everything afterward depended on it: my hands were shaking so badly that Emeka put his own over mine for a moment, steadying them, the way you'd steady a child learning to hold a hammer.

"Fear is correct," he said. "Fear means you know what you're doing is yours to answer for. People who break in without fear are the dangerous ones."

I hit the door three times. On the third, something inside the frame surrendered with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung open onto darkness and cold.

* * *

Cold — that was the first thing. The climate control hummed in the dark at some temperature chosen for cellulose rather than human beings, maybe sixteen degrees, and our breath showed faintly in the light of Emeka's pen torch. The room was long and narrow, lined floor to ceiling with gray archival boxes on metal shelving, each box labeled in fading marker with catalog codes. Along one wall stood the playback equipment under dust covers: reel-to-reel decks, a console, monitor speakers with their grilles yellowed like old teeth.

It was a morgue. I had prepared myself for a library, but it was a morgue — the careful, refrigerated preservation of things declared dead by people who had decided their deaths meant something.

"Catalog system," Emeka whispered, though there was no one to hear. "Everything pre-1990 is on this wall. Sessions are filed by date, then artist code. Your mother's code was OKF-7. Everyone knew her as OKF-7. She hated it, I think. Or she loved it. With her you couldn't tell what was hatred and what was love wearing hatred's coat."

We found the boxes within minutes. OKF-7, 1986 through 1989, eleven boxes total, each heavier than grief. Emeka pulled the first one down and set it on the worktable in the center of the room, and lifted the lid, and there they were: reels of two-inch tape in cardboard boxes, each labeled in pencil in a hand I recognized before I understood why I recognized it.

It was my mother's handwriting. She had labeled her own session reels. *Take 3 — keep. Take 5 — Adaora lead. Do not log.*

"Do not log," I read aloud, and my voice came out strange, flattened by the cold.

"That phrase," Emeka said quietly. "I saw it more times than I can count. 'Do not log' meant the session went into the box but not into the database. No entry, no index, no evidence. The tape existed only if someone remembered it existed. And the someone who remembered was always paid to forget."

* * *

We worked for hours. Emeka threaded the first reel onto the deck with movements his hands still knew after fifteen years of retirement, fingers finding lacing paths worn into muscle memory, and when the motors spun up, the room filled with a hiss like distant surf, and then —

A piano. My mother's piano, the phrasing unmistakable, that slight hesitation before the downbeat that I had grown up hearing through the floorboards of our house. And then a voice above it, and it was not my mother's voice.

It was a voice I had heard only once before, cracked and older, in a community hall on a Thursday evening, correcting a child's chord with unbearable tenderness. But younger here — thirty years younger, warm and dark and sure of itself, singing a song I knew. Not the radio version. Rougher. Truer. A demo, sung straight into a single microphone, no polish, no production, just a woman and a piano and a song being born.

And beneath her voice, harmonizing, threading through it like a second river under the first: my mother.

They were singing together. That was the thing the tapes made undeniable in a way no document ever could — you cannot fake two voices breathing in time with each other, anticipating each other's phrases, laughing together between takes. Take four broke down because one of them — I couldn't tell which — lost the lyric to laughter, and the engineer's voice said, off-mic, amused, "Again, ladies?" and the recording caught both women laughing, the sound overlapping, braided, inseparable.

I sat down on the concrete floor because my legs stopped being interested in holding me.

"They were good," Emeka said. He said it simply, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Not talented. Good. Talented is what you have. Good is what you become with someone who pushes you. Neither of them was that good alone. I've listened to your mother's solo sessions from after. Technically flawless. Dead as stone. Whatever lived in her music, it lived in that room, with that woman, and when the room ended, the living part of it ended too."

* * *

We copied everything. Emeka had brought a portable digital recorder and a playback chain, and we transferred reel after reel in real time — you cannot hurry tape; it plays at the speed it was recorded, and so justice, that night, moved at exactly fifteen inches per second. Four hours of it. The cold got into our bones. Emeka drank tea from a thermos and narrated sometimes, softly, naming dead producers, dating sessions by the smell of the tape stock, and mostly we let the voices fill the room.

Near three in the morning we found it. Box seven, a reel marked in my mother's pencil: *For A. — do not log.*

"For A," I said. "Adaora."

Emeka threaded it without comment.

It was the song. The song from the radio, the song from the notebook — but longer. It contained the verse that had been crossed out in the notebook, the verse I had not yet been given the meaning of, sung in full, sung twice, and Adaora's voice on the second pass broke — not from error, from weight — and my mother's voice came in underneath hers and carried her, held her up through the line the way you hold up someone at a graveside, and the take was kept. Someone wrote *keep* on that box. Someone wanted it preserved and also wanted it buried, and both of those anyones were my mother, and I sat in the freezing dark of the archive room and understood that I would never fully solve her, that she was not a puzzle but a weather system, and that loving her and indicting her were going to be the same lifelong labor.

I copied that reel twice.

* * *

At quarter past five we finished. Every reel transferred, every transfer spot-checked, the files on two drives — one in my coat, one in Emeka's bag, so that neither of us carried the whole truth alone. We reboxed everything, reshelved it, closed the wounded door as best we could. In the elevator going down, Emeka watched the floor numbers descend and spoke without looking at me.

"In the morning, a security guard will find the frame. There'll be an incident report. The label's lawyers will pull the badge logs and find my name, and they will come for me, and I am an old man with nothing they can take except a pension I have already spent most of, so I will enjoy watching them try." He smiled — a small, dry smile, the smile of a man setting down something heavy. "But they will come for you harder. You have things they can take. I want you to walk into that knowing. What you have on those drives is worth money, and money is the only language they speak fluently."

"I'm not selling it," I said.

"No," he agreed. "I didn't think so. That's why I helped you. Thirty years I waited for someone to come asking who wasn't asking about value." The elevator reached the ground floor. The doors opened onto the gray beginning of morning, the lobby lights switching off around us on their timers, one pool of amber at a time, as if the building itself were closing its eyes on what we'd done. "One more thing, Isandro. The reel marked 'For A.' I mixed that session. I was there the night they recorded it. And I'll tell you what I have never told anyone." He paused at the exit, hand on the push bar, morning traffic hissing past outside. "After the take, your mother asked me to make her a copy. Just one. I asked her what it was for. And she said —"

He stopped. Outside, a bus went by, and its light swept across his face and away.

"She said, 'In case I'm ever brave enough.'"

The door opened. Cold morning air came in, smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"And she never was," Emeka said. "Go home. Make copies of your copies. And call the lawyer — because by tonight, they'll already know whose daughter broke in."

* * *

I drove home through the waking city with the drive in my inside pocket, against my chest, like a second heartbeat. At a red light I caught my own face in the rearview mirror — hollow-eyed, unshaven, terrified — and behind the terror, unmistakable, something new. Not my practiced smile. Something rawer and less useful and more honest.

My phone buzzed at the first intersection. An unknown number. Then a text:

**This is Chidi. We need to talk before you do anything else. I know where you were tonight.**

The light turned green. I didn't move. Behind me, a horn sounded, impatient, ordinary, a world going on with its errands. I sat there holding the wheel with shaking hands, certain anyway.
