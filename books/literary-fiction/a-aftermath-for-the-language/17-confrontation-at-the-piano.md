## 17. Confrontation at the Piano

**Isandro**

The tapes rode in the passenger seat like a second person.

I had wrapped them in a wool scarf — my mother's scarf, though I no longer knew how to feel about anything of hers being mine — and every red light, I found myself checking on them the way you check on a sleeping child. Fourteen reels and two cassettes, copied over three nights in the retired engineer's damp basement while his dehumidifier wheezed like an asthmatic choirboy. *Session tapes, 1986 through '89,* he'd said, blowing dust off a spool. *Unreleased masters. And listen here* — he'd threaded one up with hands that still remembered everything — *that's not your mother singing lead.*

It was not my mother singing lead.

I had listened to that voice for six hours before I could name what it did to me. It wasn't better than my mother's voice; that was the terrible part. It was *different* in a way that made my mother's sound like a beautiful room and this sound like the open window in it. Adaora Eze sang the way rain sounds on a tin roof when you are safe inside and guilty about being safe. I had grown up on these melodies wearing someone else's skin.

Now I drove them to Enugu road, past the fuel station with its leaning sign, past the church where I'd once attended a wedding and remembered nothing but the heat. The community hall announced itself the way Adaora did, according to the neighbors: without apology and without decoration. A concrete box painted the green of old currency, a corrugated roof gone rust-brown at the seams, a hand-lettered sign — *THURSDAY EVENINGS: MUSIC FOR CHILDREN, ALL WELCOME* — with the W repainted twice, each time slightly larger, as if the word itself kept trying to escape.

Her car wasn't there. Her bicycle was, chained to a post, its basket holding a single umbrella and a bag of oranges.

I sat in the parked car for forty minutes. This is a thing I have learned about myself in the last months: I am my mother's child in all the ways I once denied. I rehearse. I curate. I arrived with the tapes arranged in chronological order and a speech drafted in three versions — contrite, formal, plain — and none of them true. The truth had no draft. The truth was: *I have proof, and I don't know what you want me to do with it, and I'm afraid that whatever I do will be another theft.*

When she finally came walking up the road from the direction of the market — I recognized her gait before her face, that unhurried stride of a woman who has decided long ago that the world can wait or not — I got out of the car too fast and stood there holding the scarf-wrapped bundle like an offering at a shrine I hadn't been invited into.

She stopped maybe four meters away. She looked older than seventy in the flat afternoon light, and younger than seventy in the eyes, which is a trick I have stopped trying to explain.

"You found something," she said. Not a question. She could read me the way weather reads a field.

"Session tapes," I said. "From the label's archive. Demos. You're — " My voice did something humiliating, caught between whisper and crack. "You're on them. Singing lead."

For a long moment she said nothing. A goat crossed the road behind her with the confidence of a landlord. Then she turned and walked toward the hall's side door, and over her shoulder, without stopping:

"Bring them, then."

* * *

**Adaora**

The boy who mangled the chord yesterday — Emeka, small hands, stubborn heart — had left a pencil under the piano bench, and I bent to retrieve it and stayed bent, because the piano was there, and it was *there*, the way a grave is there.

Chinedu Okafor's upright. I would know it blindfolded by the third step's complaint, by the smell of the felt and the old wood, by the hairline scar across the treble C where a ring caught it in 1984 and Chinedu laughed and said the piano would remember us even if Lagos didn't. It had followed her out of the city somehow — sold, shipped, resold, and then bought back, I learned later, by a woman who never played it, who kept it tuned in a house where no one touched it. A museum of one exhibit. All those years I imagined her living large on what she took from me, and some part of her had spent money she didn't have to keep a silent piano breathing in her front room.

I had sworn I would never touch it again. Vows are easy when the object is theoretical.

Isandro stood in the doorway holding the bundle against their chest. I made them wait while I opened the windows — the hall holds heat like a grudge — and while I moved a bench, and while I did all the small administrative tasks of cowardice. Then there were no more tasks.

"Play it," Isandro said. "Please. I brought headphones, I brought a player, you don't have to—"

"I know what's on them." I set the bundle on a desk without opening it. "I've known since the night the radio played that song. You think I burned your letter because I didn't want to know? I burned it because I already knew everything except whether any of you would ever say it out loud."

"And now?"

"Now," I said, "you brought boxes of tape instead of words. Which is more than she ever did." I looked at the piano. Forty years is a long time to hate a piece of furniture. "But I'll be damned if I let her instrument tell the story alone."

I sat down.

The stool was wrong — too low, someone had sawn it down for children — and my knees complained, and my hands hovered over the keys the way they hover over a door you're afraid has changed since you last knocked. I pressed middle C. The piano answered, a little dusty, a little sour, alive.

And then — I cannot fully account for this, and I am too old to lie about things I cannot account for — my hands began to play.

* * *

**Isandro**

She played the introduction first, the one everyone knows, the eight bars that have soundtracked a thousand weddings and a thousand funerals and at least one president's inauguration, and hearing them come out of this piano in this hall was like watching a famous painting being repainted stroke by stroke in front of you. Familiar and wrong. Familiar and *more* right.

Then she went somewhere the record never went.

A bridge I had never heard. A modulation that climbed instead of descended. And then her voice — untrained now, thinned by decades of chalk dust and children and grief, cracked on the ascent and glorious anyway — began the verse.

The crossed-out verse.

I recognized it instantly, the way you recognize a face in a crowd twenty years later, because I had spent weeks with that notebook page, decoding the strikethrough, holding the paper to the light. *Sleep, my small one, the drummers are done / the river keeps what the river has won.* Words written, I understood now with a force that buckled something in my chest, for a child who never drew breath. Words my mother had crossed out with a single ruler-straight line and then recorded around, built the song into a hit by amputating its heart.

Adaora sang it whole. She sang it the way you sing at gravesides, eyes half-closed, one hand flat on the piano's lid as if steadying herself against a wind only she felt. The hall's tin roof ticked in the heat. Somewhere outside a motorcycle passed and faded. She sang the verse twice, and the second time her voice broke on *river*, and she let it break, and kept playing, and the broken note hung in the room like a bird deciding where to land.

I did not realize I was crying until the tears hit the back of my own hand.

She finished. The last chord decayed into the hall's concrete silence. For a while neither of us moved. I think we were both afraid that speaking would collapse whatever had just been built, the way a wave collapses when you name it.

"She wrote that verse," Adaora said finally, quietly. Her hand was still on the lid. "Don't mistake me. That verse was hers. She wrote it the night we lost Chinwe — my daughter — she held my head in her lap until dawn and then she went to the piano and wrote it, and it was the truest thing she ever wrote, and I loved her for it. That's the part nobody understands." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes were dry in the way that is worse than wet. "I loved her. That's why it cost so much."

"Why did she cross it out?"

"Because grief doesn't sell." She said it without bitterness, which frightened me more than bitterness would have. "The label wanted a love song. She gave them a love song with its heart cut out and kept the heart in a notebook for forty years. I used to think that made her a coward." A pause. The roof ticked. "Now I think it made her a woman standing in a burning house, saving what she could carry."

* * *

**Adaora**

He — they; I am learning, slowly, the grammar of respect this child of hers carries like a passport — Isandro knelt and unwrapped the scarf on the desk, and laid the reels out in order, and I saw my mother's handwriting on the labels in Chinedu's slanting script: *Ady — take 3. Ady — keep this one. Ady — don't let them cut the verse again.*

"Don't let them cut the verse again," I read aloud. My voice sounded like someone else's.

"She fought for you," Isandro said. "On paper, at least. These are her notes to herself. Or promises to you."

"Promises." I picked up a reel and weighed it. Forty years of anger does not dissolve because of evidence; I want to be honest about that, because everyone in this story deserves honesty, including the dead woman who ruined my life and wrote the truest verse I know. The anger didn't dissolve. But it *moved*. It shifted its weight like an old dog settling, making room on the mat for something else. "Do you know what I planned to do if you ever came here? I had speeches. Forty years of speeches. I was going to tell you what she cost me — the credits, the royalties, the years when my name was a rumor and hers was a monument. And I was going to watch your face to see if you were her."

"And?" Isandro asked. "Am I?"

I looked at this person kneeling among the reels of my stolen voice, crying without wiping their face, who had driven four hours with tapes wrapped in a dead woman's scarf, who had chosen truth over inheritance at an age when most people are still learning to choose their breakfast.

"No," I said. "You smile like her. But you use it differently. Hers ended conversations. Yours—" I almost said *begins them*. I didn't. Some gifts should be discovered, not handed over. "Yours hasn't decided yet."

We threaded the first reel onto the hall's ancient machine — a relic from the days when the community hall hosted dances, before the roof rusted and the town's money left — and pressed play, and the room filled with 1987. Two voices, hers and mine, young and braided together on a demo no one was supposed to hear, laughing between takes. Laughing. I had forgotten we laughed. Memory keeps the wound and discards the weather around it, and here was the weather: the joke about the producer's shoes, the false start, Chinedu counting us in with a mouthful of groundnuts.

Isandro watched me listen. I watched the tapes turn. Neither of us apologized. I want that on the record, whoever ends up keeping it: neither of us apologized, and neither of us needed to yet, because apology is a transaction and what was happening in that hall was something slower — two people standing in the wreckage of the same fire, discovering they'd been burned by the same flame, sorting through the ash to see what could still be carried.

Halfway through the third reel, Isandro said, "There's going to be a remastered edition. Anniversary release. Her catalog, her credits, permanent. The lawyers sent letters already — to me, about the archive. They know what I have."

I stopped the tape.

"They'll bury you," I said. "They buried me with less effort."

"I know."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

It wasn't a question either. This child of hers, reading my fields the way I read theirs.

"The concert," Isandro said. "The memorial. In three weeks. I want you there. I want you to sing the verse. Not as a guest. As the author."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, and the hall filled with that amber hour when dust becomes visible and everything ordinary looks briefly consecrated. I thought of Chinwe, who would have been thirty-eight. I thought of the notebook page with its ruler-straight line. I thought of Chinedu Okafor, who stole my name and kept my piano and wrote *don't let them cut the verse again* in the dark to no one.

I put the reel back on the machine.

"Three weeks," I said. "Then you'd better teach me the ending. She changed it in '88 and I've been singing the old one out of spite ever since."

Isandro smiled — and I saw it then, clearly, in the amber light: not the mother's smile, not the wall, not the door closing. Something newer, still wet, still learning to walk.

Something that begins conversations.

And far away, in a lawyer's office in the city, a telephone was already ringing.
