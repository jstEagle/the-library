## 19. Memorial Concert

The hall held a hundred and forty people and smelled of dust warmed by old radiators, of varnish and cold coffee, of the particular staleness that gathers in rooms where nothing has happened for years and then something does. Isandro had chosen it precisely because it was small — the Unity Hall on Adeola Street, with its water-stained ceiling tiles and its folding chairs and its stage so shallow a pianist could touch the back wall without rising from the bench. A cathedral would have been a lie. Their mother had never been a cathedral. She had been a room like this one: modest, well-kept, full of things no one was allowed to move.

By six o'clock the chairs were filling. Isandro stood at the back in a dark suit they had bought for the funeral and not yet returned to the tailor, watching the crowd assemble itself out of their mother's life. The old musicians came first, of course — the survivors of the Lagos sessions, men and women now in their seventies who moved like furniture being rearranged, slowly, with complaint. Then the students from Adaora's Thursday class, herded in by two teenage girls who had appointed themselves ushers and wore matching yellow sashes. Then strangers, dozens of them, drawn by the flyer Isandro had papered the neighborhood with: *A Memorial for Ngozi Okafor — Songs, Stories, and the Truth About Both.* Chidi had called the phrasing inflammatory. Isandro had kept it.

Chidi himself sat in the third row, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man attending the trial of a relative. He had come anyway. That was something. Isandro chose to count it.

Backstage — which was to say, behind a curtain that didn't quite reach the floor — Adaora Eze sat with her hands flat on her knees, breathing the way she taught her children to breathe before a difficult passage. Four counts in. Four counts out. She was seventy-three years old and dressed in indigo, and she had told Isandro an hour ago, in a voice stripped of all its classroom authority, "If my hands shake, don't look at them."

"They'll shake," Isandro had said. "Mine are shaking now."

"Good," she'd said. "Then we're honest from the start."

The retired engineer, Mr. Balogun, had insisted on running sound though his hearing aids whistled when he laughed. He had copied the session tapes twice over, once for safety, once because he said archives burned down and you were a fool to trust any single building with the truth. He fussed at the mixing desk now like a man grooming a nervous animal. On the stage, under a single warm lamp, stood Ngozi Okafor's upright piano — Isandro had paid three men to move it from the house, and it had arrived with one leg shimmed by a folded photograph none of them had noticed until later.

At seven, Isandro walked to the microphone.

The hall quieted the way water stills after a stone. Isandro looked out at the faces — Chidi's set jaw, the trumpeter Sunday already weeping quietly into a handkerchief he'd clearly brought for the purpose, the children in the second row swinging their legs, Adaora's indigo shoulder visible at the curtain's edge — and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that there would be no version of this evening they could take back. Whatever happened next would be the record. It would be what people hummed at weddings and argued about at funerals. The thought did not frighten them. It steadied them, the way a ledger steadies an accountant.

"My mother died in March," Isandro began. "She died the way she lived, which is to say privately, in a house full of locked drawers. For most of my life I believed the silence around her was dignity. I catalogued her belongings like a customs officer cataloguing cargo — sealed letters, unlabeled photographs, an untouched piano — and I mistook my own control for grief, and my grief for understanding." A pause. Somewhere in the hall a chair creaked. "I was wrong about nearly everything, and tonight I'm going to tell you about one of those things, but first we're going to do what she would have wanted, which is play some music."

They played. Sunday's trumpet opened with a slow, cracked rendition of "Market Rain" that turned, halfway through, into something neither written nor improvised but remembered — the whole hall leaning toward it like grass toward light. The children from Thursday class sang "The Palm Tree Song" with the fearless wrongness of children, and Adaora, watching from the wings, corrected nothing, because there was nothing to correct. An old harmony trio reunited for two numbers and got lost in the third and laughed, and the laughter was somehow more moving than the song. Between pieces Isandro read aloud from the notebook — the early lyrics, the marginalia, a grocery list in the same hand as a chorus that half the city knew by heart. People chuckled at the grocery list. Grief, given permission, will laugh at anything.

At eight-fifteen, during the interval, Isandro stood again at the microphone, and this time they did not smile.

* * *

"I want to show you something," Isandro said, and nodded to Balogun, who lowered the lights and started the tape.

What came out of the speakers first was silence — real silence, tape hiss and room tone, the sound of a room in 1987 holding its breath. Then a count-in, a woman's voice, warm and slightly amused: *"One, two — Ngozi, you're rushing, I can hear you smiling."* Laughter. Piano. And then a voice singing "A Aftermath for the Language" — the title track, the signature song, the melody every obituary had named — except the voice was not Ngozi Okafor's voice. It was higher, rougher at the edges, with a grain in it like wood that had been left out in weather, and it carried the melody the way a river carries a boat: effortlessly, entirely.

Isandro let it play through the first verse and the chorus, then raised a hand and Balogun killed the tape.

"That was recorded on the fourteenth of June, 1987, at Eko Sound Studios," Isandro said. "The voice you heard belongs to Adaora Eze. The piano belongs to my mother. The song belongs to both of them. For thirty-eight years it has been credited to one."

The hall made a sound Isandro had never heard a crowd make before — not a gasp, not a murmur, something between the two, a collective intake that seemed to change the air pressure. In the third row, Chidi uncrossed his arms very slowly, as if his body had become evidence too.

Isandro told it plainly. The contract with the sole signature. The notary, dying now in a care home in Surulere, who had confessed to witnessing the removal of a name and being paid for his silence across four decades. The photographs with a figure cut away at the edge. The letter addressed to Isandro, never sent, that spoke of *a debt I could not pay in money*. They did not perform the telling; they read it like a customs manifest, item by item, because plainness was the only register equal to the material. When they finished, they said only: "Adaora Eze wrote half of everything you think my mother wrote alone. Tonight we're going to give it back to her. Not because the law requires it — the law, as of this week, does not. Because it's true."

And then Isandro stepped back from the microphone, and the curtain moved, and Adaora Eze walked onto the stage.

The applause began uncertainly — a few hands, then more, then Sunday on his feet with his trumpet raised like a torch — and Adaora stood in the middle of it looking, Isandro thought, like a woman wading into cold water she had decided long ago to enter. She reached the piano. She touched its lid the way you touch the shoulder of someone you once loved and haven't seen in forty years. She sat down. Her hands, as promised, shook. She looked at them, and then out at the hall, and said, in the voice she used on Thursdays, "We're going to begin again from the top. This time you'll hear the whole thing."

She played the introduction alone — and Isandro, standing at the wing, realized they had never heard it played this way, with the second voice woven into the left hand, a counter-melody that had been there all along in the notebook's notation, waiting. Then she sang. The first verse was hers. The second verse was Ngozi's — and here Adaora paused, lifted her chin toward the wings, and Isandro walked out and stood beside the piano and sang their mother's lines in a voice that was not trained and did not need to be, because the song had always been a conversation, and conversations require two people willing to be imperfect.

The hall did not breathe. The hall listened the way the dead listen, completely.

And then Adaora reached the place where the recording ended, where the demo had stopped and the published song had always stopped, and she did not stop. Her hands found the modulation the notebook had marked with a single asterisk, and she leaned into the microphone, and she sang the crossed-out verse.

*"Sleep, my daughter, the market is closed,*
*the traders have gone home, the lamps are cold.*
*I sold everything, I sold even my name,*
*but what I could not sell, I will carry — *
*I will carry you home."*

It was a lullaby. It had always been a lullaby, hidden inside an anthem, the way a grave is hidden inside a garden. Adaora sang it with her eyes closed and her spine straight, seventy-three years old and twenty-six again, in a decaying hall on Adeola Street, and when she finished there was no applause at first — only a silence so complete that Isandro could hear the radiator ticking, could hear Balogun's hearing aids whistling faintly, could hear, somewhere in the dark, Chidi crying without any attempt to hide it.

Then the hall rose. Not all at once — first the children, who understood instinctively that something sacred had happened and that standing was the correct response, then the musicians, then everyone, the whole room on its feet while Adaora sat at the piano with her hands folded, receiving it the way stone receives rain.

* * *

Afterward, the hall emptied slowly, the way a tide goes out, leaving things behind. People clustered near the stage wanting to touch Adaora's sleeve, to say words that mostly failed them. A young woman with a phone asked if she could post the video. Adaora looked at Isandro. Isandro said, "Post everything. All of it." The young woman's thumbs were already moving.

Chidi came backstage last. He stood in the doorway for a long moment, and Isandro braced for the fury, the lawyers, the word *defamation* — but what Chidi said, quietly, was: "She sounded like Mum's records. The ones from before." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I don't know what I'm supposed to do with this, Isandro. I really don't."

"You don't have to know tonight," Isandro said.

"No," Chidi agreed. "But the label will. By morning." And he left through the fire door, and the cold air that came in behind him smelled of rain beginning.

Adaora and Isandro packed the piano together — sheet music into a wooden box, the shim removed from the leg, the folded photograph from 1987 laid on top where it belonged, both women visible at the frame's edge, neither cropped. Adaora hummed while they worked. It took Isandro a moment to recognize the tune, and another to understand why Adaora had chosen it: it was the lullaby, but hummed lightly now, unburdened, a thing being carried home rather than buried.

Outside, the rain had settled in for the night. The streetlamps made small amber rooms in the wet air. Adaora paused at the curb and looked back at the hall, its sign buzzing, its windows going dark one by one as Balogun shut things down inside.

"Well," she said. "That's done."

"It's not done," Isandro said. "It's just begun. By morning everyone will have heard it. By noon the label's lawyers will have heard it. And they won't send flowers."

Adaora smiled — a small, dry, formidable smile, the smile of a woman who had waited thirty-eight years and could wait out a law firm too. "No," she said. "But they'll call. And when they do—" she patted Isandro's arm, once, the way she corrected a child's chord, gently and without letting go, "—we answer together."

Her phone buzzed in her handbag before they reached the car.
