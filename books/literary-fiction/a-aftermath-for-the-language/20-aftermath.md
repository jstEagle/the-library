## 20. Aftermath

The letter from the label's lawyers arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope so thick Adaora could feel its weight through the door slot, as if paper itself had grown heavier with money.

She let it sit on the hall table for two days. She made tea she didn't drink. She watered the ferns that lined her windowsill — the ones she'd kept alive through every decade that had refused to keep anything else alive for her — and she watched the envelope the way you watch a dog you don't yet trust.

On Thursday she opened it with a kitchen knife, slitting the seam in one clean motion, because forty years of waiting had taught her at least this: never fumble at the moment that matters.

*Settlement Agreement and Mutual Release*, the first page said, in type so small it looked like insects arranged in rows. The figures were on page four. She read them twice, then set the pages face-down on the table and went to stand at the window, where the afternoon light came through the glass in a warm, indifferent slab, and she pressed her palm flat against the cool pane and breathed until her hands stopped shaking.

It was not a fortune. It was enough. It was, she understood with a strange, floating clarity, exactly the amount a company pays when it has done the arithmetic and decided that fighting would cost more than confessing. No apology in the letter. Apologies were not billable. But there was the word *acknowledges*, used four times, and there was a schedule attached — *Amended Credits, Full Catalog* — listing song after song after song, each one bearing now two names where for decades there had been one.

She read down the list like a woman reading a census of the dead.

*"Morning Market." "Salt Road Lullaby." "The Long Rain."*

Her handwriting had been on those demos. Her voice was on those tapes, Isandro had told her, lead vocal, first take, the engineer's marks still penciled on the boxes. She had sung them into a microphone in 1987 while a young man named Emeka adjusted levels and a woman named Ngozi Okafor stood behind the glass with her arms folded, watching, and Adaora had believed — God help her, she had been twenty-nine and pregnant and glowing, and she had believed — that they were building something together.

She touched each name on the schedule with one finger, the way you'd touch the faces in a photograph of people who can no longer be called back.

Then she put the kettle on, because grief and vindication both require tea, and sat down, and read the whole thing properly, twice more, before signing.

* * *

The reissue came out in March.

Isandro sent her the first copy by hand — walked it across town to the community hall on a Thursday evening, interrupting her class, holding the record sleeve out with both hands like an offering at a shrine. The children craned their necks. Adaora took it and turned it over and there it was, printed in silver on matte black:

**NGOZI OKAFOR & ADAORA EZE — *THE LONG RAIN* (REMASTERED)**

And beneath, in smaller letters, the liner note Isandro had insisted on, the one the lawyers had fought over for six weeks: *"All compositions co-written by Ngozi Okafor and Adaora Eze. Original credits corrected posthumously at the request of both families."*

*Both families.* As if Chidi had lifted a finger. As if the dead woman herself had asked. But Isandro had fought for that phrasing, Adaora learned later, had spent three days of phone calls refusing to sign off on any language that implied the correction was a courtesy rather than a debt.

"It's yours," Isandro said now, standing in the doorway of the hall with sawdust on their sleeves and the smell of the outside world clinging to them. Behind them, twelve children were supposed to be practicing scales and were instead eavesdropping with the shameless totality only children possess.

Adaora held the record at arm's length, the way you hold a mirror when you're not sure you want to see what's in it.

"Play it," she said finally. "Track six."

Isandro blinked. "That's—"

"I know what track six is."

So Isandro carried the record to the old player in the corner — the one Adaora kept for teaching, its needle worn soft as an old dog's mouth — and set it spinning, and the room filled with crackle, and then with piano, and then with two voices, hers and Ngozi's, braided together across a distance of nearly forty years, young and sure and utterly unaware of what was coming for them.

The children went quiet. Even the littlest ones, the ones who couldn't sit still through a single scale, felt it — the way animals feel weather coming.

And then the verse came. The crossed-out verse. The one written in a hospital corridor in 1988 for a daughter who had lived nine days and whose name appeared nowhere on any document except the inside cover of a notebook, in ink gone brown with age: *Chiamaka. She was here.*

Adaora had not heard it since the funeral-that-wasn't, since the day she'd stood in the rain outside the church and refused to go in because the coffin was too small and small coffins make sounds when you carry them, a soft shifting sound, and she had never forgiven herself for hearing it.

She did not cry. She had used up whatever crying was, years ago, the way a well goes dry not all at once but bucket by bucket. She stood very straight with her hands clasped behind her back, a teacher's posture, and listened to her own young voice sing her own dead daughter's verse, credited at last, and found that underneath the vast slow relief of it there was a stone that had not moved.

Vindication, she discovered, is a room with one window. However large the room, however long awaited the light — you can still stand in the corner where the light doesn't reach, and you will know exactly where that corner is.

When the song ended, Isandro turned the volume down and looked at her with that careful, unguarded face of theirs — the face that had learned, somewhere between the funeral and the archive room, to stop performing.

"Does it help?" Isandro asked. Not *are you happy*. Not *isn't this wonderful*. Just: does it help.

Adaora considered lying, out of habit. Then she said, "It helps the way a cast helps a broken arm. You're glad it's there. It doesn't stop it having been broken."

Isandro nodded slowly, and Adaora thought: *this one listens the way your mother never did.* And immediately hated herself for the thought, and let it stay anyway, because at seventy-three she had earned the right to hold two true things at once without dropping either.

* * *

Fame, it turned out, was a weather system that passed over you.

It began with a journalist from one of the national papers — a young woman with a recorder and excellent manners who sat in Adaora's front room drinking ginger tea and asking questions with the gentle ruthlessness of her profession. *How does it feel, after all these years? What do you say to people who wonder why you waited? Was Ngozi Okafor a thief or a victim?*

Adaora answered carefully. She had rehearsed nothing, but forty years of silence had composed the answers for her, and they came out level and clean.

"She was my friend," Adaora said. "That's the part nobody wants to print, because it makes everything else harder. If she were a monster, you could file this story and sleep. She wasn't. She was a frightened, brilliant woman who signed her name on a line where mine should have been, and then spent the rest of her life paying everyone but me to keep quiet about it."

"And you forgave her?"

"I haven't decided," Adaora said, and the journalist laughed, thinking it wit, and wrote it down as wit, and it ran under the photograph of Adaora in her Sunday blouse looking serene and wise, which was the second lie about her published that month.

The interviews multiplied. Radio, then television, then a podcast recorded in a studio that smelled of coffee and carpet glue, where a host half her age asked her to describe "the moment everything changed."

"The moment everything changed," Adaora repeated, "was a Tuesday in September 1987, and it changed so quietly that I didn't notice for eleven months."

People loved that line. It got clipped and shared. Strangers quoted it back at her in supermarkets.

There were photographs now, too — the excised woman restored. Someone at the label, some archivist with a conscience or a deadline, had located the uncropped negatives from the Lagos studio session, and suddenly there was Adaora at the frame's edge everywhere: in the papers, on screens, leaning against a piano with her head thrown back mid-laugh, twenty-eight years old and luminous, beside a woman whose smile, seen now at full resolution, did not reach her eyes.

Adaora studied that photograph longer than any interviewer ever studied her. She looked at Ngozi's hands on the piano keys — elegant, ringless in those days, the nails short for playing — and remembered how those same hands had once rested briefly on her shoulder in the corridor of the studio, a weight of five fingers, and how she had thought: *I am known. I am finally known.*

She had been known. That was the terrible thing. Ngozi had known her completely — her voice, her griefs, the shape of her ambitions, the name of her stillborn child — and had erased her anyway. Erasure, Adaora came to understand, is not committed by strangers. Strangers cannot erase you; they never had you to begin with. Only love holds the pen.

The fame lasted, in total, about five weeks. A football scandal arrived, as football scandals do, and the news cycle turned its great blind eye elsewhere, and the interview requests thinned to nothing, and one morning Adaora woke and the world had moved on and her name was hers again — printed correctly now in the catalogs, spoken correctly in the occasional documentary, but hers again, ordinary, unwitnessed.

She expected to feel robbed a second time.

Instead she felt, unmistakably, relieved.

* * *

But there was one thing. There was always one thing.

It started at the memorial concert's echo — a clip of the performance circulating online, grainy phone footage of a small hall, an old woman singing a verse most of the audience had never heard, and a caption someone had added: *the verse Ngozi Okafor tried to bury.* Adaora watched it once and closed the laptop and sat with her tea going cold.

Then the letters began.

Not lawyer's letters. Ordinary letters, handwritten, on lined paper and stationery and the backs of envelopes. From strangers. From women, mostly.

*Aunty Adaora, I lost my son in 2019 and I have not been able to say his name out loud since. I have played your verse forty times. I am saying his name now: Kelechi. Thank you for showing me it can be done.*

*I sing your song to my daughter every night. She thinks the last verse is about her. I have never corrected her.*

*I am ninety-one years old. I remember when the record first came out and something in it felt unfinished, though I could not say what. Now I know. It was waiting for you.*

Adaora read them at her kitchen table in the mornings, one or two at a time, rationing them like sweets from a tin that must last the winter. She answered every one. It took hours. She wrote in her careful schoolteacher's hand, telling each stranger a piece of the truth sized to what they could carry: that the verse was for a daughter named Chiamaka; that grief does not shrink with time, it only learns to live in a smaller room of the house; that singing a name aloud is not a betrayal of composure but an act of architecture — you are building a place where the person can continue to exist.

And then, one Saturday in April, she went to the market.

She had avoided the big Saturday market since the concert — too many faces, too much recognition, the exhausting theater of being briefly famous. But she needed thread and plantain and the particular honey from the stall near the fishmongers, and so she went, wrapped in her plainest headwrap, anonymous by intention.

She was at the fabric stall, running indigo cloth between her fingers, when she heard it.

A voice, first. Young, female, unhurried, coming from the stall two rows over where a girl sold roasted corn from a steel drum. The girl was singing while she worked — the way people sing when they think no one important is listening, loose and low, a private soundtrack. And the melody was familiar in the way your own heartbeat is familiar, and Adaora's hands went still on the cloth.

The girl reached the bridge. And then, without pause, without ceremony, without knowing that the woman who wrote it was standing eight meters away fingering indigo cotton, she sang the verse.

*Sleep, my small one, the river is long,*
*and I will walk it carrying your song—*

She sang it slightly wrong — a passing note flattened, a phrase rushed — and it did not matter, it did not matter at all, because the words were crossing the open air of a Saturday market, riding over the noise of haggling and motorbikes and frying oil, out of the mouth of a girl born fifteen years after Chiamaka died, who had learned them from a remastered record or a grainy video or her own mother's nightly ritual, and who sang them the way you hum while working: casually. Easily. As if the verse had always existed. As if it belonged to the world.

Adaora stood among the bolts of cloth and wept.

Not the dry, finished weeping of funerals. This was sudden and undignified and hot, tears arriving faster than she could govern them, and the fabric seller — a broad, kind-faced woman — silently pressed a handkerchief into her hand and asked no questions, which is sometimes the deepest courtesy one human being can pay another.

Because this was the thing. This was the one thing.

The money was money. The credit was ink. The interviews had been a season of weather. None of it had reached the stone in her chest, the one that had not moved since a corridor in 1988.

But this — a stranger's voice, casual and unguarded, carrying her daughter's name in melody through a Saturday market — this reached it. Not moving it, perhaps. Not dissolving it. But warming it. Making it, for one impossible moment, less like a stone and more like a hand she was holding.

Chiamaka had lived nine days. She had left no photographs, no recordings, no grave marker legible past the rain. For thirty-seven years her entire existence had been contained in a crossed-out verse in a stolen notebook — one woman's secret, then another woman's theft, then a legal exhibit, then a liner note.

And now it was a song a corn-seller hummed at work.

Adaora paid for her cloth with shaking hands. She bought corn she didn't want just to have a reason to pass the girl's stall, and up close she saw the girl was maybe nineteen, with chipped nail polish and earphones dangling around her neck, entirely unaware of what she had done.

"You have a good voice," Adaora managed.

"Ah, thank you, ma." The girl grinned. "It's my mum's favorite song. The old one. She says the last part always makes her cry, but she won't tell me why."

"It's about a baby," Adaora said. "One who didn't stay."

The girl's grin softened into something older. "Oh," she said. "Yes. My mum says that too."

Adaora walked home the long way, along the road where the jacarandas were dropping their purple, holding the bag of corn she hadn't wanted, and she understood that the healing she had waited forty years for was not going to arrive the way settlements arrive, all at once, with signatures. It was going to arrive the way songs travel: one voice at a time, one stranger at a time, slightly wrong each time, and truer for it.

* * *

In May, a package came from Isandro. Inside was a photograph — an original print, not a copy — of the two of them in the Lagos studio, 1987, uncropped: Adaora laughing, Ngozi at the piano, both young, both whole.

On the back, in Isandro's careful hand: *Found this in the bench, with the rest. Thought she'd want you to have the one where neither of you is cut away. — I.O.*

And beneath that, smaller, hesitantly, as if the writer had paused mid-sentence: *There's something else in the bench. Something addressed to you. I haven't opened it. I think it should be you. Come when you're ready. No hurry. There's tea.*

Adaora turned the photograph over twice. Read the note a third time.

Something addressed to her. In Ngozi's hand. Hidden in the piano bench all these years, beneath the dust and the sheet music and the silence — while Adaora had burned, raged, grieved, and grown old believing the silence was the final word.

She sat for a long time in the fading light, the ferns shadowing the wall, the photograph face-up on her knee, two young women laughing in a room that no longer existed.

Then she went to the coat hook, and took down her good shawl, and reached for her keys.

No hurry, Isandro had written.

But Adaora had waited forty years for the mail to come, and she was not going to wait one more night than she had to.
