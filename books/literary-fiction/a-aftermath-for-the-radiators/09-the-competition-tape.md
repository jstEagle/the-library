## 9. The Competition Tape

The basement of Halloway Conservatory smells like a library that drowned and was never properly mourned. Paper rot, machine oil, the sweet chemical ghost of whatever they sprayed for mold in the eighties before the budget died and the mold won. I have cleaned down here exactly twice in four years of employment, both times under protest, both times with a mop I later retired with honors.

I go back on a Tuesday because Wren asks me to, and because I have discovered that my resistance to Wren operates on roughly a forty-eight-hour delay. She says *jump* on Saturday; by Tuesday I am airborne, resentful about it, but airborne.

"There's a storage cage in the sub-basement," she'd said, spreading a photocopy of one of my mother's letters across the cafeteria table like a tarot reader who'd learned her trade from an insurance adjuster. "Letter nineteen. She mentions 'the room behind the machines' and something about 'the year we can listen to again whenever we're brave.' That's a direct quote. Whenever *we're* brave, Rafael. Plural."

"She wrote to you for six years," I said. "You've developed a taste for other people's bravery."

"I've developed a professional interest in unlabeled boxes," Wren said. "It's the same thing."

So: Tuesday, 6:40 a.m., before the day staff arrive, flashlight in my jacket pocket because half the basement fixtures have been dead since the Obama administration. My official reason for being here—I drafted it carefully on the walk over, the way I draft all my alibis—is that the demolition notice means everything below grade gets gutted first, and as the conservatory's custodian of record (a title I awarded myself, consisting of a mop and a grudge) I should inventory what's down here before the wreckers do their curatorial work.

This is what I tell myself. Terror dressed in a janitor's uniform, carrying a clipboard. It's amazing how much fear you can get done before nine if you call it maintenance.

* * *

The sub-basement is reached by a staircase that descends with the enthusiasm of a man walking to his own sentencing. At the bottom, the building's organs are exposed: boiler, breaker panels, the great dead furnace that used to push heat up through the radiators—the actual radiators, the ones my family named ourselves after, though nobody alive except me remembers why anymore, and I've been rationing that memory like wartime sugar.

The storage cage is where letter nineteen said it would be. Chain-link and a padlock so corroded it looks decorative. I could probably open it with a firm look, but I brought bolt cutters anyway, because a man should commit to his burglaries.

Inside: shelving units sagging under decades of institutional sediment. Boxes marked CHOIR 1974–79. ORCHESTRA PARTS, DO NOT DISCARD (discarded). A filing cabinet labeled FACULTY GRIEVANCES, which I chose not to open, on the principle that some doors exist specifically to ruin your afternoon.

And on the bottom shelf, wrapped in a garbage bag that someone had knotted with unusual care—my mother's knots were always surgical, a trait I inherited along with her hands and her talent for silence—a cardboard box, soft with age, marked in her handwriting:

**R.A. — RECORDINGS — DO NOT PLAY WITHOUT ME**

I sat down on the concrete floor. Not dramatically. My legs simply filed a formal request and were granted it.

DO NOT PLAY WITHOUT ME. Six words, and every one of them doing overtime. Because she wasn't here. Because she had been not-here for eleven days now, permanently, irreversibly, and the instruction had outlived its author and become a kind of haunting. You could read it two ways. One: don't listen until I'm with you. Two: I'll be with you when you listen. My mother spent her whole life making sure those two sentences meant different things.

I unwrapped the box anyway. Of course I did. Obedience was never our family's instrument.

Reel-to-reel tapes, maybe fifteen of them, each in its cardboard box, each labeled in that same hand. Most were dated—recital performances, studio sessions, things I barely remembered giving. And at the front, positioned like a hostess greeting guests, one box labeled simply:

**RAFAEL — NATIONAL FINALS — JUNE 14**

Not my childhood name, exactly. Not the name on my janitor's badge, either—Amberdale is a fiction I purchased at twenty-two, a surname chosen from a map because it sounded like a place where nothing had ever happened to anyone. But it was the name from before. The name the household used. The name that belonged to the Radiators.

June 14. I know the date the way you know your own blood type. The day I played Rachmaninoff's Second Sonata in a hall in another city, in front of three jurors and four hundred strangers, and came off stage having won second prize and lost everything else. The injury happened after—that's the version everyone got. A wrist, a fall, a tragedy of overuse, poor gifted child, such a shame. The story had a shape because stories need shapes, and this one had been shaped by three people who agreed never to discuss it again, which is the most efficient way to preserve any lie: freeze-dry it and store it in siblings.

Thirty years, and the tape has been sitting in a basement forty minutes from my apartment, labeled in my mother's handwriting, waiting.

I did what any reasonable person does with a haunted object. I put it back in the box, put the box back on the shelf, and told myself I'd come back with proper equipment. Archival handling. White gloves. A trained professional. Wren.

Then I stood up, walked four steps toward the stairs, stopped, and swore with a fluency that would have gotten me expelled from this institution twice over.

Because here's the thing about practicality: it's the last refuge of people who are terrified, and I recognized the dialect immediately, because I speak it natively. *Proper equipment.* As if the obstacle were cotton gloves. As if what stood between me and that tape was a matter of humidity control rather than the fact that hearing it means hearing the last time I was ever fully myself in public, followed by the sound of the world reorganizing itself around a lie I helped maintain for three decades.

I walked back. I picked up the box. I carried it upstairs like a man transporting his own heart in a grocery sack.

* * *

There is a working reel-to-reel deck in the media room on the second floor, which survives because no one under sixty knows what it is and no one over sixty has the energy to advocate for its removal. I checked it Thursday night—Thursday, because Tuesday's courage only lasts so long and needs time to recharge—and it turned over, belts intact, heads dusty but functional. I cleaned them with isopropyl alcohol and cotton swabs, with the tenderness of a man grooming a horse he intends to ride into battle.

Friday, 9 p.m. The building empty except for me and the radiators ticking their small metal thoughts into the dark. I set the tape on the deck. Threading it took three tries because my hands, hands that once dispatched octave runs at tempo without a flicker, were shaking hard enough to qualify as weather.

Here is what I know about June 14, in the fragments I permit myself:

I was seventeen. I wore the blue blazer Maren bought me because our mother refused to come shopping and Tomas refused to come at all—he had a shift, he always had a shift, the eldest-child tax paid in hours. Our mother sat in the third row. She had driven us four hours in a car that smelled like hot vinyl and her nerves. Before I went on, she held my shoulders and said, "Play it the way you play it for me." Which sounds, out of context, like love. In context, it was a detonator.

I played. By every account but mine, I played magnificently. Second prize. The juror whose name I will not write down even now, not in a notebook, not anywhere—his face I remember perfectly, his hands I remember better—came to the reception and told my mother I was "the real find of the competition" and asked whether Rafael might benefit from private study. Summer study. At his house. His summer house.

I remember my mother's smile. I remember that it reached her eyes, which was rare, and that it frightened me, which was rarer.

Two weeks later, my wrist was injured. Two weeks after that, I withdrew from the conservatory's young artists program. Two months after that, I never played in public again. The official story—wrist, tragedy, shame—was constructed jointly, ratified by all three children, and maintained ever since with the discipline of a state religion. Maren believes one version. Tomas believes another. I believe the third, the true one, the one that lives in my chest like a swallowed stone: that I saw something on June 14 that a seventeen-year-old cannot unsee, and that my body made the decision my mind couldn't afford to make, and that my mother let the body decide because it was easier than explaining.

That's the version. Roughly. There are rooms in it I've kept locked even from myself.

And now a cardboard box on a shelf, labeled in her hand, offering me the door.

I pressed PLAY.

* * *

You should understand what happens in the first seconds of a recording like that. Before the music, there's the room. Tape hiss like static snow. Then the murmur of an audience settling—not individuals, just the ocean-sound of four hundred people becoming one animal. A chair scraping. A cough, somewhere in the cheap seats. And then footsteps, mine, seventeen years old, crossing a stage toward a piano, and the particular acoustic signature of a hall going quiet, that held-breath hush that every performer chases their whole life and only finds a few times, the silence that means: *we came here for you.*

Hearing your own footsteps from thirty years ago is an experience I recommend to no one. You hear how light you were. How quick. You hear a boy who hasn't yet learned that the world keeps receipts.

Then the bench creaks. Then a pause—two seconds, three—while the boy arranges his hands, and in that pause I heard myself breathing, actually breathing, the little nasal catch I still do before first notes, and thirty years collapsed like a folding chair.

Then the opening chords.

I gave it thirty seconds. I want that on the record, since apparently records are the theme of my life now: thirty full seconds. The opening statement of the sonata, that dark, plunging first page, and the boy playing it not perfectly—there's a smudge in the left hand at bar nine, I winced, some things never change—but playing it *alive*, playing it like a person setting fire to a building he plans to live in, and the hall around him holding still, and somewhere in the third row a woman breathing audibly through her nose, my mother's listening breath, a sound I would know underwater, a sound I hadn't heard in thirty years and heard now like a hand closing around my throat—

I hit STOP.

The reels spun down with a soft mechanical sigh, the way machinery apologizes.

I sat there in the media room with the lights off and the tape hanging between head and spool like a thread pulled loose from a hem, and I thought, with tremendous calm, *well. That's enough for today.* Practical. Sensible. Thirty seconds of archival verification confirms the recording exists and is playable; further review can be scheduled at a later date; the custodian will note it in the log.

Terror, in other words, wearing its little clipboard.

But here's what the thirty seconds did, the thing I couldn't stop thinking about while I rewound the tape with shaking hands and boxed it and carried it back down to the basement and locked it in the cage and stood there a moment too long with my palm flat against the chain-link:

The performance was good. Not good—*true*. Whatever happened after, whatever was broken or ended or buried, the boy on that tape was telling the truth at the top of his voice, and four hundred people heard it, and one of them was my mother, and she kept a recording of it in a basement for thirty years, labeled in her own hand, next to instructions that said *do not play without me.*

She wanted to be in the room when I heard it. Or she knew I couldn't survive the room without her. With my mother, these were always the same sentence.

I climbed the stairs. I signed out for the night in the logbook, entry reading, in my best bureaucratic hand: *Basement inspection. All items accounted for.*

All items accounted for. Except the one item that accounts for everything else—the tape, and the thirty seconds I'd heard, and the remaining twenty-two minutes I hadn't, and the question underneath both, the one I've been circling for three chapters of my life like a moth around a bulb:

What did she hear on June 14 that made her spend the rest of hers keeping it?

Saturday morning I called Wren. She answered on the first ring, as if she'd been sitting by the phone for thirty years too.

"I found the tapes," I said. "I need to show you something. And then—" I looked at the basement door, at the staircase going down into the dark where my childhood was filed under R.A., "—there's a locked room behind Hall C's stage. Letter twenty-three. Bring the letters."

A pause on the line. When Wren spoke, her voice had gone careful and bright, the voice of someone trying not to startle a wild animal—or a witness.

"Rafael," she said, "letter twenty-three isn't about a room. It's about a juror."
