## 5. Inventory of Unsaid Things

Priya found the notebooks on the third day, in the third place she looked, which felt about right for a marriage like theirs.

She hadn't been snooping. That was the thing she would keep returning to, later, turning it over like a stone to see what lived underneath. She had been looking for the funeral home's invoice because the estate lawyer had asked for it twice, and Oscar had said *it's in my bag* the way he said everything now — flat, preoccupied, his eyes already back on the window where the fog came off the harbor in long gray ropes. His bag was in the closet of the rental house. The invoice was not. The invoice was in a manila folder, and the manila folder was in a box, and the box was full of notebooks.

Forty-one of them. She counted.

They were the cheap kind, marbled covers, the sort sold in drugstore racks for a dollar ninety-nine, and every one had a date range inked on the spine in Oscar's small, level hand. *1998–2000. 2001–2003.* The earliest predated her. The most recent was three-quarters full, its last entry dated nine days before Marion Halloran died.

Priya stood in the closet with the box in her arms and her own reflection ghosted in the closet mirror behind it, and she understood, before she opened a single one, what she was holding. She had married a man who measured bridges. Of course he had measured her.

She told herself she would only look at one page. One page, to confirm, the way you press a bruise once to see how bad it is.

The entry was dated March 14, 2011 — the year they'd bought the house on Delancey, the year she'd thought they were the happiest they had ever been.

*P. laughed at her own joke before she finished it tonight. Third time this week. She doesn't know she does it. Filed under: things I love and have not told her.*

Priya sat down on the edge of the bed with the notebook still open in her lap. The rental house was quiet. Oscar had gone to the funeral home again, or the lawyer, or the harbor — somewhere a man goes when there is nothing left to arrange and he cannot bear the room where his mother's handwriting sits in an envelope nobody can read. Down the hall, the foghorn sounded, patient as a pulse.

She read another entry. Then another. She did not cry, which surprised her. What she felt was closer to vertigo — the specific vertigo of finding out the floor you have walked on for twenty years has been glass all along, and someone has been underneath it the whole time, taking notes.

*P. asked if I was happy. I said yes. The answer was true but incomplete. Filed under: things I said that were not the whole thing.*

*P. cried after the call from her sister. I put my hand on her back. I did not ask what was wrong. Filed under: failures.*

*Anniversary, 2016. Twenty years. She wore the blue dress. I said she looked beautiful. Filed under: true things, said too late in the evening to land.*

He had a filing system. That was the part that undid her, more than any single confession. Not the omissions themselves — she had her own, God knew, omissions packed like winter clothes in the attic of her — but the *taxonomy*. He had spent two decades turning their life into an inspector's report. Load ratings. Deficiencies. Items flagged for monitoring. And the cruelest, gentlest joke of it: he had never filed anything away. The notebooks weren't a record of things dealt with. They were a record of things *deferred*. Every entry was a maintenance item scheduled for a repair that never came.

She was still reading when his key turned in the lock.

She didn't hide the notebook. She wanted that on the record, wherever Oscar kept his records — that she did not hide it. It lay open on the bed beside her, spine up, when he came into the room with fog still beaded on his jacket and stopped as if he'd walked into a door he couldn't see.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Down the street a gull made its one complaint. Oscar looked at the notebook, then at her, and Priya watched him do the thing he did — the calculation, the load assessment, the quick silent engineering of how much weight this moment could bear.

"Don't," she said.

His jaw tightened. "Don't what?"

"Do the math. I can see you doing it. Deciding how much of it I read, how much it weighs, whether the structure holds." She patted the bed beside her. Not a command. An invitation, which was harder for him, and she knew it. "Sit down, Oscar. I'm not going to yell. I'm too tired to yell and you're too sad to be yelled at. We're just going to talk. Maybe for the first time."

He sat. He sat the way he stood on bridge decks — braced, weight distributed, ready for the span to move under him.

"Forty-one notebooks," she said.

"Forty-three. Two are in the car."

She laughed — a short, broken-off sound, nothing like her old laugh, the one he'd once recorded in 2011 as a thing he loved and never told her. "Of course they are. Of course there are two in the car." She turned the notebook toward him, to the March 2011 entry. "Read it. Out loud. I want to hear you say it in your voice, not just in mine."

"Priya—"

"Please."

He read it. His voice stayed level through *filed under* and cracked, very slightly, on *love*. He closed the notebook and set it down with the care of a man handling evidence at his own trial.

"When did you start?" she asked. "The filing."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Before you. My mother. I told you she kept lists. Grocery lists, weather in the margins, tide tables. I thought everyone's interior looked like that — a ledger. It wasn't until I was older that I understood she was doing it too. Writing down the things she couldn't say." He looked at the box. "She just never taught me the cipher. She taught me the habit."

"The habit of not saying things."

"The habit of *recording* not saying things. Which is worse, probably. It's like—" He searched for it, and she let him search, because she had learned in twenty years that Oscar's sentences, like his bridges, needed time to carry their load. "It's like inspecting a joint every year for corrosion and writing *monitor* in the report. Every year. For decades. And calling that maintenance."

"And our marriage was a joint you were monitoring."

He flinched. It was small, and he was too good an engineer to let it become anything bigger, but she saw it, and she let it stand, because it was true and because she was done pretending she hadn't seen things.

"I don't want a divorce," she said, and watched relief and disappointment cross his face in the same second — relief that the structure wasn't condemned, disappointment that she hadn't condemned it, because part of him, the part that wrote *failures* in the margins, believed he deserved the sentence. "I want something harder. I want you to say one thing a day that you would have filed. Out loud. To me. Starting now."

"That's—"

"Hard. Yes. I know. That's the point. Bridges get inspected, Oscar. People get talked to. You don't get to keep me in a ledger anymore."

The foghorn sounded again. Oscar looked at his hands, at the box, at the window where the fog was lifting off the water in the late afternoon light, and Priya saw him arrive somewhere — saw it the way you see a crane swing into position over a job site, the first visible sign that something is actually going to be built.

"One thing a day," he said.

"Starting now."

He was quiet for a long time. Then, slowly, he reached into his jacket pocket and took out a folded piece of paper — creased soft with handling, not new. He smoothed it on his knee.

"I've been carrying this for two days. I didn't file it. I didn't write it down anywhere. I've just been holding it." He turned it so she could see: a photocopy of the cipher sentence, Marion's strange looping characters, photographed and enlarged. "Wren's mother's name was in my mother's diary. I found it last night. And there's a house on the point — the Ferrow house. Wren wants to go inside. He wants me to go with him." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and terrified and, for the first time in years, entirely uncalculated. "Here's the thing I didn't file, Priya. I'm scared that whatever's in that house explains why I'm like this. And I'm more scared that it won't."

Priya put her hand over his — the same gesture he had logged, in 2014, as *insufficient*, and she held it there, deliberately, until it was something else.

"Then go," she said. "And come back and tell me everything. Out loud."

* * *

He left at dawn, and she watched from the window as he and the stranger walked the shore road toward the point, two silhouettes in the gray light, one tall and one taller, moving like men who had not yet agreed to be related. Then she went back to the box, took out the oldest notebook, 1998, and began at the first page — because if she was going to live in this marriage honestly, she intended to read the whole report.

Halfway down the second page, in her dead mother-in-law's handwriting, in the cipher nobody could read, was a single line. And beside it, in her husband's hand, dated the week of the funeral: *Found this tucked in the cover. Monitor.*

Priya sat very still.

Then she picked up the phone and called Wren.
