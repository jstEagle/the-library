## 8. Rising Water

The storm had a name before it had a face — some meteorologist on the radio calling it Odette, giving it a woman's name the way men name things they expect to be beautiful and then blame for everything. Wren heard it from the porch of the Anchor Inn, where he'd been staying since the funeral, watching the harbor go gray and then white and then disappear entirely into a wall of moving water.

The Ferrow house sat on the point, exposed to all of it.

He'd been inside once, with Oscar. They'd broken in together like boys, like burglars, like brothers who didn't know the word yet, and Oscar had run his fingers along the window frame where the cipher key was carved — small notches, a child's alphabet, Marion Ferrow at eleven years old inventing a language because nobody in her house spoke hers. Wren had watched him touch it and understood something he couldn't say: that this house was a body, and they were both standing inside its chest cavity.

Now the storm was coming to close that chest.

* * *

The innkeeper told him to stay put. The police drove the point road at four o'clock with their lights going and a loudspeaker telling everyone the causeway would flood by dark. Aunt Dee called his cell twice and he let it ring, because what was he going to say — I'm fine, I'm just going out to watch my mother's childhood drown?

Marion wasn't his mother. That was the sentence he kept trying to make true. She was the woman who wrote to him every month for twenty-two years, postcards signed *your mother* in handwriting that slanted right like something leaning into wind. She was the voice on the phone every second Sunday, asking about the boat, about his hands, about whether he was eating. She was the woman whose dementia had taken her from him eight months ago mid-sentence, mid-letter, the ink trailing off the page like a tide going out.

She was not his mother. His mother was Lorraine Ferrow, dead at thirty-one, a photograph and a headstone and nothing else. He had made his peace with arithmetic like that a long time ago. One mother given back by the sea; one given forward, secretly, in envelopes with no return address.

But the house. The house was the only place her whole life still existed — the notches in the window frame, the water stain shaped like a running horse on the ceiling of the room she'd been born in. If the sea took the house tonight, it took the last original document.

Wren told himself he was only going to look. He put on his foul-weather gear, the good set, the one he wore hauling traps in weather that sent other boats home. He told himself he knew water, that he'd grown up reading it the way Oscar read bridges, that no man who respected the sea ever drowned in it.

That last part wasn't true. Men who respected the sea drowned in it constantly. Respect didn't save you. It just meant you died knowing exactly what you'd underestimated.

* * *

The point road was already gone when he got there — not washed out, but underwater, black water moving fast over the asphalt, carrying somebody's dock chair and half a lobster trap. He parked on the rise above Dee's old place and walked in, which was stupid, and he knew it was stupid, and he did it anyway, because the house was still standing when he crested the hill and he needed to see it closer, needed to know if the windows were holding, if the water had found the foundation the way water always eventually finds everything.

The wind hit him sideways at the top of the dune. Rain came so hard it didn't fall anymore, it just traveled horizontally, stinging like gravel. He got to the house by way of the lee side, shoulder against the shingles, and got the door open against the pressure of wind trying to keep it shut, and stepped inside into darkness that smelled like salt and mildew and forty years of closed rooms.

It was almost peaceful in there. That was the trick of storms — outside they screamed, but inside a solid old house there was just the groan of timbers, the rattle of glass, the sound of the sea working at the foundations like a patient animal. Wren stood in the front hall with his light off and felt the house breathing around him and thought: she stood here. Eleven years old, inventing alphabets. Seventeen, falling in love with a boy named Halloran. Thirty-something, coming back to stand in this hall after her father died, deciding what to bury.

The water came up the beach road faster than any forecast said it would. He heard it before he saw it — a change in the sound under the wind, a deeper note — and when he looked out the kitchen window the yard was gone. Not flooded. Gone. Replaced by moving sea, brown-white and muscular, and the first of it was already curling around the foundation stones.

He should have left then. There was maybe a minute when leaving was still possible, and he spent it doing something unforgivable and completely human: he went upstairs. He went into her room, the birth room, and took the piece of window trim — the section with the cipher notches — out of the frame with his pocketknife, prying it loose while the house shook. Because paper could be replaced, letters copied, but this was wood her own hand had cut, and he was not going to let the ocean have it.

When he came back down, the water had come through the door.

* * *

Later, he would reconstruct it in pieces, the way you reconstruct anything that happens too fast to witness. The water in the hall was knee-high and moving. He turned for the back door because the front was now a river mouth, and the current took his legs out from under him before he finished the turn. The cold was total, immediate, a full-body fact. His boots filled. His gear, which floated him fine in open water, snagged on a doorframe, then on something below the surface he never identified, and suddenly he wasn't swimming, he was being held — held under, held down, the house's own wreckage wrapping around him like arms.

He remembered thinking, with strange clarity: *so this is the undertow.* The town's name, finally explained to him from the inside.

He remembered thinking about the postcards. Twenty-two years of them, in a shoebox in his truck, dry, safe, on high ground. *Your mother.* He remembered being furious at himself for dying before he showed Oscar the box properly, before they figured out the sentence, before —

Then hands. Hands on his collar, hauling, and a voice he knew saying words he couldn't parse, and he broke the surface into rain and streetlight-colored chaos and coughed half the Atlantic out of his lungs.

Oscar had him by the shoulders. Oscar, soaked to the skin in jeans and a rain jacket with no gear at all, no rope visible, no plan visible, standing chest-deep in a flooded yard holding a drowning man the way you'd hold a thing you'd carried your whole life without knowing it.

"You idiot," Oscar was shouting, or maybe Wren was, maybe both of them, the words torn away as fast as they were made. "You absolute — there's a road, there's a reason people don't —"

"The window," Wren got out, and pushed the piece of trim against Oscar's chest, and Oscar took it without looking at it, without question, tucked it under his arm like it was a child.

They fought their way up the rise together, the water pulling at them the whole way, the house behind them making sounds Wren chose not to translate. At the truck, Oscar shoved him into the passenger seat and then stood in the rain for a moment, breathing, looking back at the point, where the Ferrow house was still standing, barely, lit by lightning like something caught mid-confession.

"I saw your truck," Oscar said finally, getting in. "On the rise. Nobody parks there in a storm unless they're walking somewhere they shouldn't."

"You followed me."

"I've been following you since the wake." He said it flatly, staring through the windshield. "I just hadn't admitted it yet."

* * *

Dee's old place still had power — one lamp, a gas fireplace, the essentials of a woman who'd learned to ride out decades of storms alone. She wasn't there; she'd evacuated inland with friends, and she'd left the key under the third flowerpot the way she'd left it under that flowerpot for fifty years, a detail Oscar mentioned with a small helpless laugh that cracked in the middle.

They stripped wet clothes and wrapped themselves in blankets from a cedar chest that smelled of mothballs and someone else's history. Wren's hands wouldn't stop shaking. It wasn't cold, or not only cold. It was the aftermath — the body finishing the argument the mind had started and lost. He sat with the blanket around his shoulders like an old man and watched steam come off his forearms and tried to think of something to say that wasn't thank you, because thank you seemed too small and also too enormous.

Oscar put the window trim on the table between them. Water had gotten to it anyway — the wood darkened, swollen slightly — but the notches held. Little cuts in a straight line, a child's careful alphabet, legible to no one alive except possibly the two of them, together, if they worked at it.

"That's why you went in," Oscar said. Not a question.

"It's the only thing her hand touched that isn't paper."

Oscar nodded slowly. Then he laughed — one short, broken sound, nothing funny in it. "You know what I did when I was twenty-six? I drove past this town. On my way to a job in Rockland. I saw the sign — UNDERTOW, POP 1,840 — and I kept driving. I had three hours of daylight and I kept driving." He looked at the fire. "I spent my whole life measuring distances. Expansion joints. Gaps. How much a thing can move before it fails. And I never once measured the distance between here and everywhere I actually lived."

Wren pulled the blanket tighter. "You want to know what I measured? Tide tables. For twenty-two years I checked the mail like it was a tide table. Second Sunday, phone call. First of the month, letter. When the letters stopped, I told myself it was weather. I told myself that for eight months, Oscar. Weather."

The word hung there between them — *Oscar*, plain and unarmored, the first time either of them had used the other's name without hostility attached.

"She stopped writing because she couldn't hold the pen," Oscar said quietly. "Dee told me. The last coherent thing she wrote was addressed to you. Dee found the envelope. Sealed. Couldn't bring herself to open it, couldn't bring herself to burn it." He reached into the pocket of his dried jacket, hanging by the fire, and set a second object on the table beside the window trim.

An envelope. Soft with handling, sealed, Marion's handwriting slanting right across the front like something leaning into wind.

Wren stared at it. The shaking in his hands moved up into his chest. "That's —"

"Unopened. Eight months." Oscar turned it over once, like a man checking a load rating. "I can't read what's inside. Neither can you. Not yet. But whatever it is, she wrote it to you, and you nearly died tonight carrying a piece of her window through a flood, and I'm done pretending we're strangers negotiating over a woman we both loved."

The fire ticked. Outside, the storm was easing the way storms do — not ending, just spending itself, the wind dropping register by register.

"We're not strangers," Wren said. His voice came out wrecked, half seawater. He cleared his throat and tried again. "My mother used to say blood is just salt water that learned to stay in one place. I used to think that was just something boat people say." He looked at Oscar across the table, across the notched wood and the sealed letter, across forty years of a lie neither of them had built but both of them had inherited. "Brother."

Oscar didn't flinch. He looked at the word the way he looked at everything — testing it, weighing it, finding its load rating. Then he exhaled, long and slow, and some structure in his face that had been holding for forty years visibly settled.

"Brother," he said. It sounded like a joint taking weight for the first time and holding.

Wren reached for the envelope. Oscar's hand came down over his — not stopping him, just resting there, steady as a pier.

"Not tonight," Oscar said. "Tonight we almost drowned. Tomorrow we translate."
