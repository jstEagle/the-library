## 7. Concerto for One Hand

The wardrobe contents were spread across Adaeze's kitchen table when I arrived, and I knew before I saw her face that she had opened it. I knew because the house smelled different — cedar and old paper instead of the lemon polish our mother had applied to every surface like a second skin — and because Adaeze had made tea without asking if I wanted any. In our family, tea unasked-for was a summons.

She didn't say hello. She just moved aside so I could see the table.

A violin in a case gone soft at the corners. A stack of letters bound with string. And a ledger, green cloth covers worn to grey at the edges, open to a page of figures in my mother's small, upright hand.

"Cyrus." My sister's voice was careful, the way you'd speak to a man standing on ice. "Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit *down*, Cyrus."

I sat. The chair complained. Everything in that house complained; it had been complaining for forty years and nobody had ever fixed it, because fixing things meant admitting they were broken, and we did not do that in the Okonkwo household. We polished. We performed. We cropped photographs.

I looked at the ledger because looking at the violin felt dangerous, and looking at the letters felt impossible, and the ledger was at least arithmetic, and arithmetic had never lied to me. Payments. Dates I recognized the way you recognize your own heartbeat. September 1989 — the year of my first competition. June 1993 — the summer before the conservatory audition. A column running down through my entire childhood like a spine holding up a body.

Hale. Every line of it. Sir Edmund Hale, patron, benefactor, the great warm sun around which my talent had been permitted to orbit.

"It's not what it looks like," I said.

Adaeze laughed, one short note, no humor in it. "Cyrus. It's exactly what it looks like. That's what's unbearable about it."

"She was keeping us alive."

"She was selling you."

"She was *saving* me—" and I heard my own voice go up, go loud, go somewhere I hadn't let it go in twenty years. "You think anyone pays for a boy from our street to touch a Steinway? You think Hale woke up one morning feeling charitable? She went to that man and she traded something, yes, fine, she traded something, and I got to *play*. I got out. Are you telling me you'd have preferred we stayed honest and poor?"

"I'm telling you she watched us grieve a father who never left us," Adaeze said, quiet now, which was worse than shouting. Quiet was where Adaeze kept her knives. "Every Sunday. Every anniversary. She let us hate him, Cyrus. She let *you* hate him. You built your whole life on the shape of that absence."

"My whole life is the shape of that absence," I said, and stood up, and knocked the tea over, and did not clean it, which would have killed our mother twice over.

* * *

Here is what I could not make her understand, walking out of that kitchen with the ledger's numbers burning behind my eyes: the lie was not decoration. It was not a coat you could take off and hang in the hall. It was load-bearing. Take it out and the whole structure came down.

Because here was the story we had all agreed on, the story I had told interviewers and donors and, eventually, myself: a widow of iron will, who took her grief and hammered it into opportunity, who sacrificed everything so her son could have a piano. Noble mother. Grateful son. Beautiful, marketable, true enough to survive scrutiny.

And what had I given up for it? Only everything. The hand — and we will come to the hand, we are always coming to the hand, I keep it in my inventory like a debt I cannot pay — the hand I gave to the piano, or the piano gave back ruined. Twenty years of practice rooms and tendinitis and finally the surgery that failed and the surgeon who shrugged. If my mother's sacrifice had been noble, then my sacrifice meant something. We were two halves of one accounting. Subtract her nobility and all I had left was a man who destroyed his body for a patron's vanity and called it art.

I drove nowhere for an hour. Then I drove home, and the letters came with me, because Adaeze — infuriating, correct Adaeze — had pressed them into my hands at the door and said, "They're addressed to you. Ten years old, Cyrus. They're addressed to a ten-year-old boy who's fifty-one now, and somebody has to read them."

* * *

I did not read them.

What I did was this: I put them on the hearth. I took the box of kindling matches we kept for the wood stove though neither of us had lit it since the funeral. I struck one, and held it, and watched the flame walk down toward my fingers, and thought about how fire is very good at its job. Fire doesn't need a narrative. Fire doesn't need anything to be noble. You give it paper and it gives you ash and everyone can start again.

My left hand ached. It always ached now, a low weather-system of ache, but tonight it ached specifically, pointedly, like a finger tapping my shoulder. *You're going to burn your father's voice,* the ache said. *The voice you've been pretending you didn't miss for four decades.*

I shook out the match.

I told myself it was because the smoke alarm needed new batteries. This was even partly true.

* * *

The topmost letter was dated March 1990. I was nine. I don't remember him writing letters. What I remember is hands — big hands on the steering wheel of a car that smelled of petrol and peppermints, hands that turned me upside down by the ankles, hands that clapped at my first recital so hard and so long that the woman in front of us turned around. I remember the sound of his clapping more than I remember his face. Isn't that something? Grief keeps the strangest inventory.

*My dear Cyrus,*

*Your mother tells me you have begun to play scales with a metronome and that you call it "the tick-tock monster," which is the correct attitude toward all discipline in art. Fear it, respect it, and secretly enjoy annoying it.*

*I must tell you something, and I want you to read this when you are older, though knowing you, you will read it tonight under the blankets with a torch, as I did with my own father's letters, and God help us both.*

*There are men at the conservatory who want me to write numbers in books that are not true numbers. They are polite men. They wear good shoes. They have explained to me that this is how things are done, and that a man with a family should be flexible. But I think about you learning scales against the tick-tock monster, hour after hour, counting honestly whether anyone is watching or not — and I find I cannot do it. A house where the counting is false has no floor. I would rather we were poor on a real floor than rich on air.*

*Whatever happens next, know this: I did not leave you. If one day you are told I ran away, look at the date on this letter and ask yourself what kind of coward writes love letters to his son while he runs.*

*Practice your scales. Annoy the monster. Count honestly.*

*Your father,*

*Nwachukwu*

I read it three times. On the third reading I understood that I was crying, which surprised me, because I had assumed — twenty years of assumption — that whatever tears I had were used up, spent on the hand, budgeted and closed.

He counted honestly. That was the whole of him, in the end. A man who counted honestly, erased by men whose books balanced beautifully.

And my mother had known. Had sat across from Hale, year after year, signing for the payments, letting the lie stand between her children and their father's name like a locked wardrobe. I wanted to hate her. I reached for hate the way you reach for a light switch in a familiar room.

What I found instead was worse. I found a woman in 1990 with two children and no floor of her own, offered a bargain by men in good shoes, and taking it. Taking it the way you take a breath. And then spending thirty years dusting the wardrobe and forbidding anyone to open it, because she knew exactly what was inside, and she loved us too much to show us, and too much, also, to throw it away.

Rage broke against longing like a wave against a seawall. The wave loses. The seawall stands. But oh, the sound it makes.

* * *

Near dawn I carried the violin case into the conservatory, past the night porter who nodded at me the way porters nod at janitors, invisible and essential. I set the case down on the stage of the recital hall — my recital hall, once — and opened it.

The violin was smaller than I remembered. Child-sized. His, from before me, or mine, bought for a future that got cancelled. I lifted it out. I am not a string player; my hands know eighty-eight keys and nothing else. But I held it under my chin the way he must have, and drew the bow across the strings once, badly, a sound like a cat filing a complaint.

It was terrible. It was the worst sound ever produced in that hall, and I have heard some sounds in that hall.

I played the single note again anyway. Then I took out my notebook — the inventory, the catalogue of unsayables — and beneath twenty years of entries I wrote a new line:

*Item: one letter. Read. He counted honestly.*

Then, because dawn was coming and the floors would need doing and some rituals hold a man together:

*Item: tomorrow, Sir Edmund Hale. Ask him what a dead man's name costs per pound.*

I closed the notebook. Somewhere upstairs, the conservatory's first early student was already playing Chopin, badly, hopefully, and I stood on the stage of my old life holding my father's violin, and waited to see what I would do next.
