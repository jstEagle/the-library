## 1. Expansion Joints

The joint at Pier 14 had grown four millimeters since spring, and Dominic Okonkwo wrote the number down the way he did everything: precisely, without comment.

He knelt on the deck of the Kessler Viaduct with his calipers open like a heron's beak, one knee on cold concrete, and let the instrument tell him what it always told him — that things move, that movement is measurable, and that measurement is not the same as action. Four millimeters. In bridge language, that was a whisper. In marriage language, he suspected, it was a verdict.

"Anything?" called Marta from the far end of the span, her clipboard held up against the wind off the river.

"Four mils of drift at fourteen," he called back. "I want to come back in October."

"You want to come back in October," she repeated, in the tone she reserved for men who wanted things they would not permit themselves to have. She'd worked with him nine years. She knew his calendar better than he did.

The expansion joint itself was a beautiful piece of failure-in-progress: two slabs of concrete that had been poured as one continuous thought in 1961 and had spent sixty years disagreeing about temperature. Steel fingers interlocked across the gap, comb teeth waiting for a hand. When a truck crossed, the fingers flexed and sang a low note you could feel through your boots if you stood in the right place. Dominic had stood in the right place for most of his adult life.

He understood joints. That was the thing nobody warned you about competence — it made an excellent hiding place. A man who could read the drift of a structure to within half a millimeter could spend decades reading the drift of his own life and then file the readings somewhere no one would ever act on them. There were forty-one inspection reports on the Kessler Viaduct in the county archive, each one noting the widening at Pier 14, each one recommending *monitoring*. Monitoring. The most respectable word for doing nothing ever invented.

At home there was a different archive. Seven years of it. He kept it in the same place he kept everything — behind his sternum, indexed, current.

*Adaeze has stopped humming while she cooks.* Logged, roughly, three years ago.

*She sits facing the window now, not the room.* Two years ago, maybe less.

*Last week she said "we" about a future event and caught herself, and we both pretended the sentence had ended naturally.* That one was recent. That one was still warm.

He closed the calipers with a click and stood, and his knee announced its own drift, and he thought, not for the first time, that he should see someone about that too, and knew he wouldn't.

* * *

The house on Delacroix Street still smelled like Sunday, even on a Tuesday. Adaeze cooked the way other people prayed — steadily, without needing an audience — and the kitchen gave off pepper and stock and the sweet burnt edge of onions done properly, which is to say longer than patience suggests.

He came in through the garage door because he always came in through the garage door, and hung his jacket on the hook because there was a hook, and said, "Something smells illegal," which was his joke, twenty years old, and she said, "It's just soup," which was hers, and between them they maintained this small customs office where nothing new was ever allowed through.

Two plates. He noticed them the way he noticed everything, which is to say helplessly and without permission. She set the table with a care that would have looked, to a stranger, like love. But Dominic had spent seven years learning to read the fine print of his wife's hands, and what he saw was ceremony. The plates went down equidistant from the centerpiece. The napkins were folded into identical triangles. The water glasses were filled to the same height, measured by some internal instrument of hers more accurate than anything in his truck.

Strangers setting a table do exactly this. They are polite with geometry because they cannot be intimate with anything else.

"How was the viaduct," she said. Not a question mark on it. A statement wearing a question's clothes.

"Widening at fourteen. I'll go back in October." He sat. "How was the school?"

"They've cut the strings program again. Third year running." She ladled soup — egusi, because it was Tuesday and Tuesdays belonged to his mother's recipe, another treaty neither of them had signed but both observed. "Nia Okafor cried in the hallway. That girl has hands like you wouldn't believe. Mr. Augustin used to say—" She stopped. The ladle hovered. Then she finished the motion, set it down, and said, "It's hot. Careful."

Mr. Augustin. The name went through the table like a truck over the joint at Pier 14 — a low note you felt in your boots. Neither of them had said it aloud in months. It lived in the same drawer as the brother, and the catalog, and the houseboat, and the wake, and every other document in the estate of the life they'd had before the life they had.

"He shouldn't be alone out there," Dominic said, to the soup.

"He isn't alone. He has Voss." She said the name the way you'd say the name of weather coming in. "Voss counts as company the way a lien counts as a gift."

Dominic almost laughed. This was the cruelty of their marriage now — that they were still funny together. The humor worked like the steel fingers on the viaduct, flexing under load, holding two slabs together across a gap that only widened.

"I'm going out Thursday," Adaeze said. "To see him. You could—"

"I have the Corley Street culvert."

"On Thursday."

"On Thursday."

She nodded and ate, and he ate, and the meal proceeded with the ritual care of strangers, and underneath it Dominic ran the rehearsal. He always ran the rehearsal. It had become, over the years, a private liturgy with many verses:

*Adaeze, I know we've been drifting.* No. Accusatory. Drifting implied she was moving and he was anchored, when the truth was they were both anchored, in different rivers.

*Adaeze, I miss us.* Better. True. But small. Missing was passive. Missing was monitoring.

*Adaeze, I watched my brother die believing I hated him, and I have spent nineteen years making sure you never get the chance to believe the same thing, and the way I chose to do it was to say nothing, ever, about anything, and I am only now understanding that silence is not protection, silence is just the wake, again, every night, forever.*

That one he had never gotten all the way through, even alone, even in the truck with the radio off. He got as far as *brother* and then his hands wanted the calipers, something to measure, something with a number at the end of it instead of a person.

Tonight he got further than usual. Tonight, watching her fold her napkin back into its triangle — she always refolded it, why did she always refold it, as if the table might be inspected later by someone who kept records — he got as far as:

"Adaeze."

She looked up. Her face did the thing it did, the quick rearrangement, hope being put away before it could be seen using.

"The soup's good," he said.

"It's your mother's recipe," she said. "It can't be bad. It's contractually obligated."

And they smiled at each other across the measured distance, four millimeters or maybe four years, hard to say, and the moment passed through the house and was gone, and the joint widened by an amount too small to see but not too small to record.

* * *

Later, he stood at the kitchen window with the last of his tea going cold, and watched the streetlight do its work on the wet pavement, and admitted the thing he admitted nightly and acted on never: that he was a man who documented his life instead of living it. That somewhere in him was a boy who used to sing — badly, loudly, in a houseboat on the river with his brother hammering out chords and Augustin laughing that great landslide laugh — and that boy had been filed away under *monitoring* along with everything else.

His brother had written songs the way water finds cracks. Nineteen ninety-eight. The summer of the yellow legal pads. He could still see the handwriting, the arrows and crossed-out chords, the title scrawled and rescrawled at the top of the page. None of it recorded. None of it played for anyone outside the boat. And then the river took the summer, and the winter after it took the boy, and the songs went wherever unrecorded songs go, which is nowhere, which is the saddest nowhere there is.

Three people alive had heard them. Maybe fewer now. Augustin, dying by inches out on the water. Himself. And—

He counted again, out of habit, and stopped, because the third name was Adaeze's, and saying even her name in his head had begun to feel like reading an old inspection report. Accurate. Outdated. Filed.

Upstairs, the shower ran. She was singing in there — he could hear it faintly, the way you hear weather through walls. Something low and looping, a melody he almost recognized, from a life with a different floor plan.

He rinsed his cup, set it in the rack at the exact angle hers had been set that morning, and told himself tomorrow he would say it. Whatever *it* was. Tomorrow he would find the words, measure them, and this time deliver them.

Tomorrow, said the report. Recommend monitoring.

Then the phone rang — the landline, the old wall phone, the one only two people still called — and the voice on the other end was thin and river-worn and laughing at something before it even said hello, and Augustin said, "Dominic. My boy. Put on the radio. Right now. Station's 91.3. Trust me."

And out past the dark viaduct, past the river, the rain started up again like applause.
