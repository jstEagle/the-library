## 4. Voss

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day the viaduct's north span got its ultrasonic testing, and Dominic had come to associate Tuesdays with things that looked fine and weren't.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than his lunch. *Okonkwo, D.* — no first name offered as a courtesy, just the initial, like a man who couldn't be bothered to finish a handshake. Inside: one page of cover letter, one stapled packet of maybe twenty pages, and a business card that said **VOSS & ASSOCIATES, ESTATE ADMINISTRATION** in letters so confident they didn't need a serif.

*Dear Mr. Okonkwo,* the letter began. *As executor of the estate of Augustin Reyes, I am authorized to extend to you a settlement offer regarding the musical catalog...*

Dominic read it standing in the kitchen, still holding his keys. Adaeze was at the sink behind him, running water over dishes he'd already washed, which was its own kind of sentence — the two of them performing chores at each other like people leaving notes.

"Who's it from?" she asked, not turning around.

"The lawyer." He heard how flat his voice came out. "Augustin's lawyer."

The water shut off. Adaeze dried her hands on the towel with the faded herons on it — a wedding gift from Augustin himself, back when gifts between them were still a language they spoke — and came to stand beside him. She read faster than he did. She always had. He watched her eyes move down the page and stop at the number.

"That's real money," she said quietly.

"It's blood money with good kerning."

"Dominic."

"I know." He set the letter down. "I know what it is."

But here was the thing he couldn't say out loud, not yet, maybe not ever: the number had landed in his chest like a key finding a lock he'd forgotten he owned. Seven years of drift measured in millimeters, seven years of a marriage conducted through the medium of correctly loaded dishwashers — and here was an exit ramp, paved and lit, offering to convert all of it into something clean. Sign here. Take the money. Stop being the man who owed a dead man's brother-shaped debt.

He almost called Voss that night. He had the card in his hand, the phone in the other, and then Adaeze came downstairs in her reading glasses to say goodnight and he put both away, because some part of him understood — dimly, the way you understand weather coming — that whatever he signed this week would sign something else along with it.

* * *

Voss turned out to be a small man who occupied space the way a staple occupies paper: completely, and with an implication of permanence. His office was on the fourth floor of a building that had been a bank and still believed it was, all marble and brass and the faint smell of other people's money changing hands. The diplomas on his wall were arranged with geometric precision. There were three of them, and none of them, Dominic noticed, mentioned music.

"Mr. Okonkwo." Voss rose, extended a hand dry as a filing cabinet. "Thank you for coming. Can I get you coffee? Water? We have water."

"We have water," Dominic repeated. "That's very generous."

Something flickered across Voss's face — the brief recalculation of a man deciding whether he'd been insulted — and then he smiled, professionally, the way a door closes. "Please, sit."

The conference table between them was long enough for eight people and set for two, which gave the meeting the atmosphere of a dinner where everyone else had already died. Voss opened a leather folder. He wore cufflinks. Dominic tried to remember the last time he'd worn cufflinks and concluded it had been at Chidi's funeral, twenty-six years ago, when he was nineteen and didn't know yet that grief was going to be a career.

"Before we begin," Voss said, "I want to say that Mr. Reyes spoke of you often. Fondly."

"Did he."

"Oh, yes. In the early files, especially." Voss said this without irony, which was somehow worse. "You were close. Which is precisely why the estate is motivated to resolve your interest quickly and fairly. Grief is complicated, Mr. Okonkwo. Paperwork shouldn't be."

Dominic sat with that for a moment. It was, he had to admit, a beautiful sentence. It had the ring of something rehearsed in front of a mirror, polished until it sounded like kindness. *Grief is complicated. Paperwork shouldn't be.* As if the whole problem with death was that it generated too many forms, and Voss was here, heroically, to reduce the human heart to a signature block.

"You knew Augustin forty years," Dominic said. "What did he call me?"

Voss's smile held. "I'm afraid client communications are privileged."

"He called me 'the quiet one.' Even after Chidi died. Especially after." Dominic leaned back. "He used to say the quiet ones are the dangerous ones, because you never hear them coming. He said it about bass players, mostly. But he meant it generally."

"I'm sure he did," Voss said, in the tone of a man moving a chess piece past a story. He slid the folder across the table. "Let's look at terms."

And here is what Dominic learned that morning, sitting in a dead bank building across from a living one: temptation doesn't arrive wearing horns. It arrives wearing Helvetica. The settlement was elegant. Page one laid it out in plain language — someone had clearly been paid to make cruelty legible. The catalog, all of it: the recorded works, the publishing rights, the mechanical licenses, the sync rights, the whole architecture of Augustin's forty years of music, transferred to the estate's designated vehicle. In exchange: the number. The number from the letter, plus a modest escalation clause, plus — and this was the line that made Dominic's throat tighten — *full release of all personal obligations, emotional or otherwise, between the parties.*

Emotional or otherwise. Someone had written that. Someone had sat at a keyboard and typed the words *emotional obligations* into a legal release, and another someone had read it and let it stand, and now it sat there on page four like a tumor found on a routine scan.

"What does that mean?" Dominic asked, tapping the line. "*Full release of all personal obligations, emotional or otherwise.*"

"It means closure," Voss said smoothly. "It means neither party can come back to the other later with claims. It's standard."

"Claims of what? Feelings?"

"Mr. Okonkwo—"

"Because I'd hate to owe Augustin an apology and have it legally extinguished. That seems like the kind of debt you'd want to keep."

For the first time, Voss paused. It was a small pause, half a second, but Dominic had spent twenty-two years listening to bridges for sounds nobody else could hear — the hairline crack singing inside solid concrete — and he heard it. Somewhere under the marble, the man had a hairline crack too.

"It's boilerplate," Voss said. "May we continue?"

They continued. And it kept getting better, which should have been the warning. The houseboat — *the vessel known as the Josephine, currently moored at Pier 9* — would pass to Dominic free and clear, title unencumbered, a gesture the estate was *pleased* to make given his history with the property. The cash. The clean break. Voss walked him through it with the warmth of a man describing a cruise itinerary, and Dominic, God help him, felt himself leaning in. He could see it already: the Josephine at some new dock, mornings with coffee on the deck, Adaeze — maybe Adaeze, someday, if a man could learn to say things — reading in the stern while the river did its slow work. All of it purchased with a signature. All of it guilt-free, per page four.

"Any questions before we proceed?" Voss produced a pen. A good pen. A pen with weight to it, the kind designed to feel like consequence.

"One more thing." Dominic flipped to the schedule of assets, the itemized inventory of everything Augustin had left. Recordings, masters, lead sheets, notebooks. And then, near the bottom, a category he hadn't expected: *Unreleased compositions and fragments (approx. 40 items), including derivative works thereof.*

"Derivative works," Dominic read aloud. "Of what?"

"Of the unreleased material. Any arrangements, adaptations, recordings, or performances derived from those compositions would vest in the estate." Voss said it lightly, the way you mention a fence around a park. "Standard protective language."

"Protecting against what?"

"Against ambiguity." Voss folded his hands. "Mr. Reyes taught for thirty years. Students, workshops, that youth program by the river. Material may have circulated. If any of it surfaces — a recording, a performance, anything built on the unreleased songs — the estate needs clear title. Otherwise we spend years in litigation with every kid who ever hummed something in a rehearsal room."

Every kid who ever hummed something. Dominic stared at the phrase *derivative works thereof* and felt the temperature of the room change. Because he had watched Chidi write songs. He had watched him write them the way other men prayed — alone, at night, muttering — and he had watched Augustin collect those songs afterward like a man gathering eggs, careful, devoted, keeping them warm. Unreleased. Forty items. Songs that existed only in notebooks and in the memory of three people alive, one of whom was dying, one of whom was sitting in this chair, and one of whom —

One of whom had been on the radio last week, singing a song that had never been recorded, under a title Chidi never used.

Dominic kept his face still. Twenty-two years of bridge inspection teaches you that: the outside stays calm while the structure talks to you. But his pulse had moved up into his ears, and the settlement — the number, the boat, the beautiful guilt-free exit — suddenly reorganized itself in his mind. It wasn't an exit ramp. It was a purchase order. Sign here, and every note Chidi ever wrote became inventory. Sign here, and the song on the radio — whoever was singing it, however they'd learned it — became an infringement. Sign here, and the estate's lawyers would go hunting through rehearsal rooms with clipboards.

"Who else knows about the unreleased material?" he asked. "Students, I mean. Did Augustin teach any of it?"

Voss's pen hovered. "I couldn't speak to that."

"But you wrote a clause about it. You don't write clauses about nothing."

"Mr. Okonkwo." Voss leaned forward, and for a moment the professional warmth dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was not cruel exactly — it was efficient, which is crueler. "You've had a difficult month. Your mentor is dying. You're grieving. This offer is generous, and it is time-limited, and frankly, the longer this sits, the more complicated it becomes for everyone, including you. Take the number. Take the boat. Let the professionals handle the rest." He slid the pen across the table. It stopped, precisely, in front of Dominic's hand.

Dominic looked at the pen. Then at page four, at the words *emotional or otherwise*, at the little box waiting for his name like an open mouth.

He thought about the radio. Rain on the windshield. A voice singing his brother's melody in a town sixty miles south, and the strange, unbearable comfort of it — evidence someone else survived it. Evidence the song had gotten out. Was out. Was alive somewhere, in somebody's mouth, right now, whether or not he signed.

If he signed, it would belong to the marble and the brass and the man with the dry handshake. If he signed, Nia — though he didn't know her name yet, wouldn't for days — would owe the estate for every note.

"I need to read the full schedule," Dominic said. "All forty pages. At home."

"Of course." Voss retrieved the pen without a flicker, capped it, and smiled again, and the smile had teeth in it now, fine and even and numerous. "Take your time. Though I should mention — the offer expires Friday. After that, the estate proceeds to public administration, and matters become..." He spread his hands, a small shrug of infinite regret. "...less tidy."

Dominic gathered the folder. At the door, Voss said, pleasantly: "Mr. Reyes really did think highly of you. It would be a shame to disappoint the estate he built."

*The estate he built.* Not the music. Not the students. The estate.

Outside, the rain had started again, soft and steady, the same rain that had been falling since the night on the highway. Dominic stood under the bank building's stone awning with twenty pages of someone else's certainty against his chest, and thought about the sentence he'd swallowed at the wake, twenty-six years ago, still lodged in him like a joint that wouldn't close.

Friday, Voss had said. Everything untidy after Friday.

Dominic turned up his collar and went to find out what, exactly, Augustin had given away — and to whom.
