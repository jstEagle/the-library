## 5. Houseboat Reckoning

Dominic had been on the houseboat for eleven minutes before he touched anything, which he considered a personal record. The boat sat where it had always sat, moored off Cutter's Landing with the patience of a thing that had decided long ago not to go anywhere, its hull wearing a beard of green moss like an old man who had stopped shaving on principle.

"You can touch things," Adaeze said behind him. "It's an inventory, not a museum."

"I'm cataloguing with my eyes first. It's a technique."

"It's stalling."

"It can be both."

She brushed past him into the cabin, and he caught the smell of her — cardamom and rain, the same as always, a fact that annoyed him in a way he couldn't file anywhere useful. Seven years of marriage and four of estrangement-that-wasn't, since neither of them had signed anything or left, they had just... drifted, like the expansion joints on the Kessler Viaduct, measured annually, documented meticulously, addressed never.

Augustin's lawyer had given them a key and a clipboard and the phrase "personal effects, to be itemized," which was lawyer for *go find out what the old man left you and fight about it.*

The cabin was exactly as Augustin kept it: a museum of the almost-finished. Sheet music in drifts. Coffee mugs with fossilized bottoms. A metronome on the shelf, its arm frozen mid-swing like a conductor who had died mid-downbeat, which, knowing Augustin, was a joke he'd have appreciated.

"He kept everything," Adaeze said softly. She was holding a shoebox of ticket stubs from gigs in towns whose venues no longer existed. "Look. The Blue Heron. 1994. We were *there*, Dominic. You spilled wine on a state senator."

"I redirected wine toward a state senator. There's a difference."

"There is not."

They worked. That was the thing about them — they were good at working side by side. Dominic made lists in his small inspector's handwriting, precise as survey data. Adaeze opened drawers and closed them again with the gentleness of someone handling a sleeping animal. For an hour the boat was almost warm.

Then he opened the locker under the bunk and found the guitar case.

Not his brother's guitar — that had gone into the ground with Emeka, against every argument Dominic had made at the time. This was Augustin's spare, the beat-up Yamaha with the sticker of a fish wearing sunglasses. And taped to the case, in Augustin's slanted hand, a label: *FOR D — WHEN HE'S READY.*

Dominic put the case down the way you put down something that might be asleep.

"He left me homework," he said.

"He left you a *guitar*."

"He left me homework *in guitar form*. There's a difference."

"There is not," Adaeze said again, but softer now, and he heard the thing underneath it, the thing she'd been carrying like a stone in her shoe for years, and he knew — with the dread of a man watching a crack widen in a load-bearing wall — that today the wall was coming down.

* * *

Adaeze had loved this boat before she'd loved either of the brothers, though she'd never said so out loud, because saying things out loud had never been the family style. The family style was *implication*. The family style was setting two plates with ritual care and meaning a hundred things by it and saying none of them.

She had been twenty-three when Augustin took her in, a music student with a broken lease and a broken something-else she didn't have vocabulary for yet, and Augustin had said, "There's a bunk and there's a rule: whoever cooks doesn't wash up, and whoever sulks does both." It was the first house she'd ever been in where the rules were funny.

Emeka had arrived a month later with a guitar and a laugh like a door swinging open. Dominic arrived last, the serious one, the bridge man, the one who loved things by measuring them.

She had married the measuring one. She'd known what she was doing. She'd thought love was a structure you could inspect and maintain, and Dominic was the most thorough inspector she'd ever met. What she hadn't understood — what she'd taken eleven years to understand — was that some things in a house don't want to be inspected. They want to be *lived in*. They want you to put your feet on the furniture.

Now she watched him circle the guitar case like it was an unexploded ordnance, and she thought: *here it comes, the crack, finally, thank God.*

"Dominic."

"Mm."

"The wake. The night Emeka died. You said something to me in the kitchen, or you *started* to, and then Augustin came in with the casserole, and you never finished it."

His hands went still on the clipboard. "I don't remember that."

"You remember everything. You have a laminated card in your wallet with the torque specifications for anchor bolts. You remember."

"That's different. That's *steel*."

"And this is what, Adaeze? What is this that you get to forget and I don't?"

He set the clipboard down with exaggerated care, which was how he did anger — carefully, the way he did everything, as if the world were load-rated and he alone was responsible for not exceeding the tolerance. "I don't *forget* it. I just don't — there's no percentage in reopening it."

"Percentage." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You talk about our marriage like a maintenance schedule. *Annual inspection of feelings, no deficiencies noted, joint is widening but within acceptable limits.*"

"That's not fair."

"Isn't it? Dominic, I set two plates every night for seven years. Do you know what I've been saying with those plates? I've been saying *come back*. I've been saying it in *ceramic*. And you eat the food and you wash the dishes and you never once hear the sentence."

"What sentence?" he said, and his voice cracked on it, and she saw — finally, actually saw — that he wasn't cold. He was terrified. He'd been terrified for so long the terror had fossilized into something that looked like calm.

"The one from the wake," she said. "And the one from every night since."

* * *

The rain started while they were fighting, a soft gray curtain against the cabin windows, and Dominic stood in the middle of Augustin's museum of the almost-finished and felt the sentence rising in him like floodwater.

Because he *did* remember the wake. He remembered everything. That was his curse — he kept records no one had asked him to keep.

The kitchen. Augustin's casserole. Adaeze in black, her eyes swollen, and Dominic opening his mouth to say: *It was my fault. I was supposed to drive him that night. I swapped shifts. If I hadn't swapped shifts, he'd be alive, and I have never told anyone, and I need you to know so that someone knows.*

And then Augustin had come in with the dish and said "Eat something, both of you, grief is heavy and you need your strength," and Dominic had swallowed the sentence whole, and it had sat in him for eleven years like a bolt never torqued, working loose.

"I swapped shifts," he said now, out loud, in the present tense of a man finally saying it. "The night Emeka died. I was supposed to drive him to the gig. I took the inspection instead because the Kessler report was due and I thought — I thought the bridge needed me more than he did. And he drove himself. And the road was wet."

Adaeze had gone very still. "Dominic."

"I practiced telling you. For eleven years I practiced. In the truck, in the shower. I had it down to a paragraph. And then you'd hand me a plate, and the paragraph would just — dissolve. Because if I say it, it's real, and if it's real, then I did it, and if I did it, then maybe everything after is my fault too. The silence. The plates. All of it."

"Dominic, listen to me." She crossed the cabin and took his face in both hands, the way she had at their wedding, the way she had exactly never since. "Emeka borrowed my car that night because yours was in the shop. He *wanted* to drive. He loved driving in rain — he said it was like the wipers were conducting. You didn't kill him. A wet road killed him, and a drunk man in a pickup, and eleven years of you not saying one sentence out loud is the only thing that's still killing anybody."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere on the shelf the metronome sat frozen mid-swing, and Dominic thought: *even the boat is waiting for the downbeat.*

"I rehearse sentences," he admitted. "That's the whole marriage. I rehearse and you wait. It's the worst possible duet."

"It's terrible," she agreed, and her voice broke into something that was half laugh, half the other thing. "We're the worst band either of us has ever been in."

"And we've been in Augustin's bands."

"*Especially* Augustin's bands."

And then — because rupture, it turned out, was just another word for opening — she was holding him, or he was holding her, in the middle of the dead man's cabin, both of them crying in the undignified way of people who have finally put the clipboard down.

It was Adaeze who pulled back first, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, and it was Adaeze who said, "There's something else. I almost didn't come today because of it."

"What?"

"Tuesday. Driving back from Augustin's. The radio." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "Dominic, they played Emeka's song. The one he wrote the summer he died. The one that was never recorded. Somebody was *singing it*."

The rain ran down the windows. The boat rocked, gentle, insistent, like a hand on the shoulder.

Dominic looked at the guitar case with its label — *FOR D — WHEN HE'S READY* — and heard Augustin's voice as clearly as if the old man were sitting in his frozen-metronome cabin: *Music hoarded dies with its holder, boy.*

"Then we'd better find out who's singing it," Dominic said.
