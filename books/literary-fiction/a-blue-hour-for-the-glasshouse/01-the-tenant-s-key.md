## 1. The Tenant's Key

The piano at the Fenwick School for Girls was a Bösendorfer, and it had a cold in its left hand.

Silas Eastwick heard it before they touched the instrument — a thickening in the lower register, the way a voice goes when the lungs fill. They knelt and opened the fallboard, breathed the felt-and-brass smell of it, and pressed middle C. The note came back dull, apologetic. Somewhere in the bass strings, a winding had loosened its grip on the pin, and the whole chord sagged a quarter-tone flat, like a person leaning against a doorframe they no longer trusted to hold them.

"Is it bad?" asked the music teacher, a young man with chalk dust on his cuffs.

"It's not bad," Silas said. "It's tired. There's a difference."

They worked for two hours in the empty recital hall, tuning fork against ear, ear against string, and all the while the building talked to them. Buildings always talked. Most people had simply been taught not to listen. This one spoke through its radiators — the old iron kind, finned like the spines of some patient animal — and what it said was: *steam in the east wing, a valve sticking, third floor, someone's been letting the pipes bang and no one has asked them why.* Silas made a note on the invoice. *Radiator, east wing, valve.* They would not fix it. It wasn't their building. It was never anyone's building, not really, not in the way that mattered. But they wrote it down the way you'd write down a stranger's cough on a train — because someone should have it on record that the thing was hurting.

By four o'clock the piano was honest again, and Silas packed their kit — the mutes, the felt strips, the tuning hammer worn to the shape of their palm — and took the bus home.

*Home.* The word snagged on the way down, the way a coat snags on a nail. Silas had spent fifteen years sanding that word smooth so they could say it without bleeding. They did not own the Glasshouse. They *rented* it — that was the story, the story they told the tax office and the woman at the hardware store and, in the small hours, themselves. A private arrangement. A generous landlord. Month to month.

The landlord was dead. The landlord had been dead for fifteen years, and the deed sat in a locked drawer in a locked desk in a locked room, and Silas paid rent into an account no one monitored, and the house let them.

The Glasshouse stood at the end of Marrow Street where the city ran out of nerve — a conservatory-estate from the 1890s, all iron ribs and clouded glass, built by a shipping magnate who had wanted to grow oranges in a climate that laughed at oranges. It had failed at oranges and succeeded at atmosphere. Ivy had taken the east wall. The glass in the great dome was patched with plywood the color of old teeth. And inside, the house breathed: warm air rising through the atrium, the radiators ticking their slow morse, the floorboards announcing every footstep to every room, a gossip with perfect pitch.

Silas came in through the garden door, as always, and stood in the mudroom, as always, and listened.

The house said: *the boiler's low, the third-floor bath drips, the glass in the orangery weeps at dusk when the temperature drops and no one wipes its face.* Silas said nothing. They hung their coat on the hook that had held their coat for fifteen years and did not look at the other hooks — four of them, empty, each one a small brass accusation.

Two rooms only. That was the arrangement, the covenant Silas had made with themselves and never spoken aloud. The kitchen, where the stove worked and the table was scarred with someone else's initials. And the old music room on the garden side, where the upright piano lived — a Kimball, unremarkable, kept in tune with a devotion Silas extended to no other object on earth. Every other door on every other floor was locked. The locks were Silas's own installation, brass and deliberate, and the keys hung in the mudroom on a ring Silas did not carry. The rooms stayed shut the way you keep a trunk shut: not because the contents are dangerous, but because you already know exactly what's inside, and knowing is the wound.

They ate standing at the kitchen counter. Soup. Bread. The radiator under the window sang a low B, and Silas found themselves humming the third above it, and stopped, because the interval was the opening of something, and they did not finish things.

Afterward they made their rounds — caretaker's rounds, though they'd have denied the word. Checked the boiler. Bled the radiator in the hall. Wiped the condensation from the orangery glass, one pane, the one that wept the worst, and stood a moment in the blue dark looking out at the garden gone feral and lovely, the wisteria strangling the pergola, the fountain full of leaves. Somewhere under all that growth was a stone bench where a girl used to sit with a notebook and write songs no one was allowed to hear.

Silas went to bed in the music room, on the daybed under the window, and lay listening to the house settle its accounts. A floorboard overhead. A pipe contracting. The Kimball's soundboard breathing with the temperature, a faint creak like a sleeper turning over. Fifteen years of this. Fifteen years of being the house's only audience, and the house's only prisoner, and calling it tenancy.

* * *

The diner on Delancey was open all night and lit like an aquarium, and Silas went there on the nights the Glasshouse hummed too loudly, which is to say, most nights. Booth by the window. Coffee they didn't drink so much as hold. The waitress knew to leave the radio on the classical station and say nothing, and Silas would sit with their hands around the warm cup and let the city press against the glass and not come in.

It was past midnight when the announcer's voice changed.

"—and now something unusual for this hour. A listener submission, no title given, sent in on paper, no return number. We don't know who wrote it, and we'd love to. It came to us from up the coast. Here it is."

Silas's cup stopped halfway to their mouth.

Piano. Solo, unadorned, recorded close enough to hear the bench creak. And it was — it was the *blue hour piece*. The one Wren used to play at the window when the light went the color of drowned glass, the one she'd never written down, the one she'd made up out of a chord Silas had shown her wrong on purpose, so she'd have to find her own way around it, and she had, she'd built a whole melody out of Silas's mistake, and no one else had ever heard it. No one alive. Silas had never played it, had never written it down, had spent fifteen years not humming it, and here it was, note for note, mistake and all, coming out of a diner radio at one in the morning like a hand reaching through the wall.

The waitress looked over. Silas must have made a sound. They couldn't have said what.

The song was four minutes long. Silas counted every second of it. Near the end, Wren's little signature — the descending third, the pause, the third again, her way of knocking twice and once — and then the bench creak, and silence, and the announcer saying something bright and useless about local talent.

Silas was already at the counter. "The address. The station. What station was that."

The waitress blinked. "The classical one? KCHP, I think. Little station, up the coast a ways. Why, you know the piece?"

"No," Silas said. "Someone I used to know wrote it."

"Must be nice, hearing it like that. Out of nowhere."

Silas stood very still. The coffee was going cold behind them. The radio was playing Brahms now, indifferent, and the city pressed its face to the glass, and somewhere up the coast a town called Harrow Point was sitting in the dark holding a song that had been buried with a family fifteen years ago.

"Up the coast," Silas said. "How far up?"

"Hour and a half, two. Why?"

But Silas was already reaching for their coat, already seeing the mudroom, the ring of keys, the locked doors, the drawer with the deed in it — and underneath the dread, under the fifteen years of careful silence, something in their chest was turning over like a radiator valve finally, finally being asked to open.

Wren had knocked twice and once.

Silas was going to knock back.
