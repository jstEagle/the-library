## 1. The Tenant's Key

The word for *freshness* had left him.

Ivo Sandoval sat at his kitchen table at eleven at night with a draft of advertising copy glowing on his laptop—*Crisp. Clean. Like the first morning of a new life*—and reached, as he always did now, for the Spanish. *Frescura.* The word arrived on schedule, well-behaved, secondhand. But underneath it, somewhere in the flooded basement of him, there had once been another word, a saltier one, a word his grandmother Rosa used for the air off the harbor before the tourist season, when the water smelled like iron and kelp and something almost alive. He could feel the shape of it the way you feel the shape of a key you've lost: you know the lock it opened, you know the weight of it in your palm, but the teeth of it are gone.

He typed *frescura* and billed the client four hundred euros and did not think about the lost word again for eleven minutes.

That was the discipline of it. That was the whole architecture of his life, if he was honest: a building with the basement sealed. Thirty years of careful construction. He translated from Spanish into English and English into Spanish, two languages that sat in him like tenants in separate flats who had agreed never to speak in the stairwell. And beneath them both, on the ground floor, the first language—the one he'd learned at Rosa's knee, the one with the word for harbor air and the word for the specific silence a fisherman's wife makes when she sets down a bowl of soup in front of a man she's furious with—that language he had evicted so long ago he'd forgotten what color he'd painted the walls.

The radiator clicked. The refrigerator hummed its one note. Outside, the city did its night business: a siren, a couple arguing in French, the elevator in the building across the alley groaning up and down with someone else's life.

He was forty-six years old and he lived in a rented apartment on the fourth floor of a building he could not have named the architect of, though he'd walked past the plaque in the lobby a thousand times. He could name the architect of the Gare du Nord. He could name the architect of a hotel in Lisbon he'd stayed in for three nights. He could not name the man whose name was stamped in brass four meters from his own door. This seemed to him, suddenly, at eleven at night, over a finished invoice for the word *frescura*, to be the most damning fact of his life.

He got up and made tea he didn't want.

* * *

The apartment had come furnished, which was how he'd justified never furnishing it. Eleven years now. The sofa was the landlord's sofa, a gray thing with the posture of a waiting room. The art on the walls—two prints, a lighthouse, a bridge—came with the lease, and he had never once looked at them on purpose. When friends visited, and visits were rare enough to be events, they said *it's so clean* in the tone people use for hotel rooms, and he always laughed and said something self-deprecating about being a man of simple needs, and the lie sat between them like a third guest no one acknowledged.

The truth was simpler and worse: he had never unpacked. Oh, his books were shelved—two walls of them, dictionaries and novels in four languages, the visible evidence of a life's work, the respectable part. But in the closet there were still three boxes he had never opened, taped shut the week he arrived, and he knew without looking that they held the things he'd carried out of his mother's house at nineteen: a football scarf, a tin of fishing hooks, a stack of letters tied with string. The past, boxed. He had carried it across the country like an organ he'd decided he could live without, and then never had the surgery, and never looked at it either. It just rode along inside him, taped shut, taking up space.

His phone lit up on the table. MARISOL.

He looked at it for a long time. His sister called twice a year, at Christmas and on their mother's birthday, and both of those dates were months away. The phone lit up again. MARISOL. Then a third time, and a text: *Ivo. Answer. Please.*

He answered.

"It's Rosa," Marisol said, and then she couldn't say it, and he heard her breathing, and he understood, and something in his chest performed a maneuver he had no word for in either of his working languages.

* * *

Rosa. His grandmother, who was ninety-one and had been dying with such patience and such slowness that the family had stopped speaking of it, the way you stop mentioning weather that never changes. Rosa, who had raised him for the first seven years of his life while his mother worked doubles at the cannery. Rosa, whose kitchen smelled of garlic and bay and the particular scorched sweetness of sugar left a moment too long. Rosa, who had sung him to sleep in a language that was not quite Spanish, that the schoolteachers frowned at, that his father forbade at the dinner table with a look rather than a word.

Rosa, whom he had visited four times in fifteen years. Whose calls he let go to voicemail more often than not, because each call was an ambush of that other language, the ground-floor language, and he could feel himself stumbling in it like a man walking on a frozen lake, hearing the ice report under his weight.

"When?" he said.

"Saturday," Marisol said. "The funeral's Saturday. But you should come before. Come Thursday. Come and—" She stopped. He heard her choose. "Just come early, Ivo."

"Is Dad—"

"Dad is Dad," she said, which was the family's entire biography of Aurelio Sandoval, and it had been enough for forty years.

They hung up, and Ivo stood in his rented kitchen with the tea going cold in his hand, and he noticed that he was rehearsing condolences. In Spanish first—*mi más sentido pésame*—the phrase arriving smooth and dead, a coin rubbed featureless. Then, absurdly, in the other language, the ground-floor one, and there his mind simply presented him with a white space, a hole in the page where the phrase should be, and around the edges of the hole, fragments: his grandmother's voice saying something to a neighbor woman in a doorway, decades ago, both of them holding their hands the same way, palms up, and the sound of it, the music of it, grief in that language apparently had a music, and he could hear the music and not one word of the lyric.

He put the tea down and sat on the landlord's sofa and did not turn on a lamp, and the city went on outside, and he felt what he always felt in this apartment and had never once said aloud, not to the friends who called it clean, not to the women who had stayed a season and gone, not to himself in the honest hours: that he was a guest here. That he had been a guest in his own life for thirty years, staying in a place kept nice for him by his own carefulness, never moving a chair, never hammering a nail, ready at all times to leave without a trace.

The key was his own. That was the thing about tenants—the key is your own, the door is not. He had a key to a door that belonged to someone else, and he had carried it so long he'd forgotten it wasn't his door.

* * *

He worked, because that was what he did with everything. He opened the file for a client in Zurich, a hotel chain, and translated a paragraph about the lobby's *sense of arrival*, and the Spanish came out fluent and empty, and he billed for it, and closed the laptop.

Then he did the thing he did maybe once a year, the thing he would have denied under oath: he went to the closet, moved the coats, and knelt in front of the three taped boxes, and put his hand flat on the top one. The tape had gone amber and brittle. Under his palm, through the cardboard, he could feel the tin of hooks, or believed he could, the way you feel a scar.

He didn't open it. He never opened it. He knelt there for a while in the dark of the closet like a man at a grave he was too cowardly to tend, and then he stood up, and his knee cracked, forty-six years old, and he went to bed and lay awake listening to the refrigerator hum its one note.

* * *

Thursday morning, the coastal city came up out of the sea haze the way it always did, the way it had for a hundred years, the train running along the waterline with the light going from pewter to gold in the space of four minutes. Ivo watched it from the window with his bag on the seat beside him and his reflection ghosted over the water, and for a moment he could not tell which one was the city and which one was him.

He had not been back in three years. Before that, five. The city had a genius for making you feel that no time had passed and all of it had. The harbor was the same harbor. The cranes were the same cranes. The gulls were the descendants of the same gulls, screaming the same complaint. And the streets where he'd grown up—those he did not look at. The train conveniently did not pass them. The train, he thought, had been rerouted years ago, like everything else, like everyone.

His phone buzzed. A message from Marisol: *House is chaos. Everyone's here. You can stay with us, the sofa bed—*

He typed: *Booked a hotel.* Then, after a moment, because it looked monstrous in black and white: *Closer to the cemetery. Easier for the mornings.*

She read it and did not reply, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was his sister's raised eyebrow, a thing he could see from forty kilometers away through a phone screen.

* * *

The hotel was new, built in the last decade on the bones of something older—there was always something older, in that city, under everything, a Roman wall in the parking garage, a Moorish arch in a nightclub—and the young man at the desk spoke to him in English before Ivo had said a word. Something about him, some residue of the abroad, some way he stood. He answered in Spanish, and the young man switched without embarrassment, and Ivo signed the registration card, *Ivo Sandoval*, the name he had used for thirty years, the name on his passport and his contracts and his tax filings, the only name anyone in the world had for him.

The room was on the sixth floor, overlooking the sea. It was a beautiful room. It looked exactly like every other room he had ever stayed in, in eleven countries, and he stood in the middle of it with his bag unopened and felt the familiar vertigo of the professional guest: here was a room that was not his, that would not remember him, that would be reset to zero an hour after he left, and he had chosen it, he had chosen it over the sofa bed in his sister's house, over the smell of his childhood in the hallway, over all of it, and the choice was so old and so worn that it no longer even felt like a choice. It felt like gravity.

He unpacked. That was the one ritual: in a hotel, he unpacked everything, hung every shirt, lined up his shoes. In hotels he was meticulous. It was only in his own home that he lived out of a state of suspension. He had noticed this years ago and never once asked himself what it meant, and standing there in the beautiful anonymous room, hanging his funeral suit in a stranger's wardrobe, he asked himself for the first time, and the answer arrived with the simplicity of arithmetic: because in a hotel, leaving is the plan. You can commit to a room you're already scheduled to leave.

His phone rang. Marisol again.

"Tomorrow," she said. "The viewing is tomorrow at six. But Ivo—" and he heard her lower her voice, heard the household behind her, cousins, aunts, the television somebody had left on, the whole loud machinery of a family in grief, a family he was standing outside of in a hotel six floors above the sea. "Come at five. Before the others. I want—I want to talk to you before everyone gets there."

"Talk to me about what?"

A pause. The television murmured. Somewhere in his sister's house, a door closed.

"About what she left," Marisol said. "About what she *left*, Ivo. Nobody knows what to do with it."

And then she hung up, or the signal did, the coast doing what the coast did to signals, and Ivo stood holding the phone, looking at the wardrobe where his black suit hung in the beautiful borrowed room, and outside the window the blue was coming up in the water, that hour the city did better than any city he knew, the hour between the day's gold and the night's black when everything looked like a photograph of itself.

He did not know yet about the shoebox. He did not know about the postcards, or the name, or the bus. He knew only that his grandmother was dead, that his sister wanted to talk to him before the others arrived, and that somewhere in the sealed basement of him, a word for the harbor air was waiting, patient as water, for him to come down and open the door.
