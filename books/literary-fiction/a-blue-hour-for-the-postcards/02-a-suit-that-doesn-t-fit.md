## 2. A Suit That Doesn't Fit

The train came down out of the hills and the sea appeared all at once, the way it always had—a sheet of hammered tin thrown across the horizon—and Ivo felt his body recognize it before his mind agreed to. His shoulders dropped. His breathing changed. Thirty years of living inland, of living in a language that wasn't his first, and still the salt smell through a cracked train window could reach into him and turn a switch he didn't know was wired.

He pressed his forehead near the glass and watched the city assemble itself: the container cranes first, then the white apartment blocks climbing the ridge, then the old quarter huddled at the water's edge like something the city was embarrassed to be seen with. Somewhere in there was the house on Calle del Faro. Somewhere in there was a coffin.

The suit was wrong. He'd known it since the hotel mirror in the capital that morning—the jacket a half-size generous in the shoulders because he'd bought it for a funeral nine years ago and then not gone, and trousers that bagged now at the knee because grief apparently burned weight off him even before the funeral started. He'd stood in front of the mirror rehearsing. *Lo siento mucho.* Then, in the other language, the one that lived deeper down where the light didn't reach: *Siento tu pérdida.* Both versions sounded like advertising copy. Both sounded like a man reading from a card.

*My condolences.* *Mi pésame.* He'd said them aloud in the empty room until they stopped being words at all, which was, he supposed, the point of rehearsal—to sand the meaning off a thing until you could carry it without cutting yourself.

At the station the air hit him like a hand: diesel, brine, fried dough from the stand by the taxi rank that had been there when he was nine. The same woman? Impossible. Her granddaughter, then. Or her granddaughter's granddaughter. Cities kept their smells the way families kept their silences—handed down without explanation.

He took a taxi, not because he couldn't walk but because he wanted to arrive composed, and walking would give the city time to work on him.

"Hotel?" the driver asked.

"The Meridional."

A glance in the rearview mirror. Not quite surprise, but its cousin. Nobody stayed at the Meridional who wasn't passing through or hiding from someone, and often both. "Visiting?"

"A funeral."

The driver nodded and let it lie, which Ivo appreciated. In this city everyone knew everyone's business within an hour, but there were courtesies about how fast you collected.

The Meridional hadn't changed either, which unsettled him more than change would have. Same green tile in the lobby, same reception desk with the brass bell worn to a shine on one side where forty years of thumbs had struck it. He signed the register—Ivo Sandoval, the name he'd been given at birth and had never once thought of as anything other than complete—and took the key to a third-floor room facing away from the sea, because facing the sea cost more and he didn't trust himself alone with that view tonight.

The room smelled of lemon polish and old cigarettes. He hung the suit in the wardrobe, where it swung slightly, empty-armed, like a man waiting to be filled.

He sat on the bed and dialed Marisol.

She answered on the second ring, as if she'd been holding the phone all day. "You're here." Not a question. Little sisters developed radar; his had never deactivated, even across three decades and four hundred kilometers.

"At the Meridional."

A pause with a shape he recognized. "Not staying at the house."

"I thought—" He looked at the ceiling, at the water stain shaped like nothing in particular. "I thought it would be easier. For everyone."

"Easier," Marisol repeated, flat as the sea at noon. Then she softened by a degree, the way she did, anger folded quickly away like laundry. "The wake starts at seven. Papá wants everything simple. No priest, just the civil words, and Tía Carmen is handling the food, so come hungry or don't come at all, those are your options."

"Marisol."

"What."

"How is he?"

Another pause, longer. Through the phone he heard her breathe, heard behind her the sounds of a kitchen—water running, a pot set down hard. "Papá is fine," she said, in the voice people use for *the patient is stable*, which means alive, which means nothing else has been promised. "He's arranging chairs. He arranges things when he can't arrange himself. You know how he is."

I did know, Ivo thought. Arranging chairs instead of feelings. Counting spoons instead of counting losses. His father had turned grief into logistics sometime around Ivo's childhood and never filed for a permit to change it back.

"Seven o'clock," Marisol said. "Don't be late. And Ivo—" She stopped herself. He waited. He knew her pauses the way he knew chord changes in songs he no longer listened to. "Wear the dark suit. Not the gray one."

"I only have the one."

"Then wear it well," she said, and hung up.

* * *

He walked to the house.

He told himself it was for the exercise, the evening light, the need to learn the streets again on foot—but the truth arrived somewhere around the second block: he wanted to approach the way a stranger approaches, so that the moment of recognition could be survived gradually rather than all at once.

The city had renovated itself in patches, like a man shaving after a long illness. New glass fronts beside shuttered ones. A café where the hardware store used to be, its menu board promising *brunch* in letters that looked borrowed from another country. The palm trees along the boulevard had grown enormous, indifferent, their trunks scarred where posters had been pasted and torn away for decades—layers of the city's announcements, none of them addressed to him.

His feet knew the way anyway. That was the terrible thing. He could have closed his eyes at the corner of the market and his legs would have carried him home, some map drawn in childhood still running underneath the newer, better maps, the way a river runs under a paved-over district, occasionally rising up through the grates to remind everyone it was never actually gone.

Calle del Faro. The lighthouse street, named for a lighthouse that had been decommissioned before he was born. Number fourteen had new paint—a pale yellow he didn't recognize—and a security camera above the door like a small blind eye. But the door itself was the same door. The same ironwork, the same dent low on the left panel where he'd slammed it against a bicycle in 1987 and caught hell for a week.

He stood across the street for a full minute, doing nothing, being no one, letting the house look at him.

Through the windows: movement. Chairs. Voices. The choreography of a family preparing to grieve in public. He saw Marisol pass behind the curtain carrying a stack of plates, and something in his chest pulled sideways—not longing exactly, more like the ache you feel when you see a photograph of yourself at an age you can't remember being.

He didn't go in. Not yet. Instead he turned and walked the length of the street to the corner, and then down toward the water, because there was one thing he needed to see before he could sit in a room of mourners and say sentences in two languages.

The harbor.

It was smaller than memory insisted. Everything was smaller than memory insisted—that was the fine print nobody read until it was too late. The fishing boats had thinned to a handful; the rest of the moorings held pleasure craft with names in English. The fish market building still stood but its doors were chained, its signage replaced by a notice about *urban redevelopment opportunities*. He read it twice. The words were in his working language, crisp and administrative, and he understood every one of them, and understanding them felt like nothing at all.

There had been a word for the smell of this place at dusk. Fish and tar and cold stone. There had been a whole vocabulary for it—the specific word for the wet rope smell, the word the fishermen used for the hour when the boats came in, a word that wasn't dusk or evening but something between, belonging only to harbors. He reached for it and found the shelf empty. Dust where the word should have been. He tried again, harder, the way you press on a bruise to check if it still hurts.

Nothing. The word had gone wherever words go when nobody says them for thirty years.

He stood at the rail while the light went long and coppery, and he understood, standing there, that he had not lost the language the way you lose a key—somewhere between pockets, retrievable if you retrace your steps. He had lost it the way you lose a limb. The body had learned to balance without it. The absence had become a kind of architecture. And now the funeral would put him in rooms where that missing limb would be noticed by everyone who remembered him having two.

* * *

He arrived at six fifty-five, in the suit that didn't fit, holding a bottle of wine he'd bought from a shop where the clerk spoke to him in the local way and he answered in the national way and both of them pretended this was normal, which it was, which was the problem.

Marisol opened the door before he knocked.

She was fifty-one and looked like their mother, which she hated hearing and which was true: the same dark eyes set slightly deep, the same mouth that went firm at the corners when it was deciding whether to laugh or scold. She looked at him for three full seconds—cataloguing, he knew, the gray in his hair, the lines, the evidence of the years—and then she stepped forward and hugged him, hard, one sharp embrace like a stamp on a document.

"You look tired," she said into his shoulder.

"You look well."

"Liar." She pulled back and held him at arm's length, and her eyes did the thing they'd done since they were children—scanned him for damage, for lies, for whatever he was carrying that he hadn't declared. "Come in. Everyone's here. Try to look sad in the ordinary way."

The hallway smelled of Rosa. That was the ambush he hadn't prepared for. Not flowers, not incense, but the actual smell of his grandmother—camphor and wool and the lavender sachets she sewed into every drawer of every house she'd ever lived in—as if the house had been keeping her scent in reserve for exactly this occasion, released now that she could no longer replenish it.

Tía Carmen emerged from the kitchen with the force of weather. She took his face in both hands, examined it, pronounced him too thin, and steered him toward the food table with a grip that admitted no appeals. Around the room, relatives assembled themselves into constellations he almost recognized: cousins grown strange and tall, uncles stooped into new shapes, children he was meeting for the first time and who stared at him with the frank suspicion children reserve for adults who appear from nowhere claiming kinship.

"Papá's on the balcony," Marisol murmured, appearing at his elbow with two glasses of wine and handing him one. "Take your time."

Aurelio Sandoval stood at the balcony rail with his back to the room, looking out at the street, at the harbor beyond it, at the whole glittering diminishing city. He was smaller than Ivo remembered. That was the sentence that kept repeating, uselessly, like a stuck hinge: *he is smaller than I remember.* Seventy-eight years old and he arranged chairs and counted spoons and stood at rails, and he was smaller.

"Papá."

His father turned. His face did something complicated and brief—grief and relief and something older, warier, moving across it like cloud shadows over a field—and then settled into the expression Ivo knew best, the neutral one, the one that had governed every difficult conversation of Ivo's childhood. Aurelio embraced him once, briefly, firmly, the way men of his generation embraced: a contract, not a conversation.

"You came," his father said.

"Of course I came."

"It's far."

"Not so far."

His father looked at him a moment longer than the exchange required, as if checking the translation. Then he nodded toward the room. "Your grandmother's friends are here. Some of them. The others are dead or far away. That's what happens." He said it without self-pity, a meteorological fact. "Tomorrow, eleven o'clock, the civil ceremony. Short. She wanted short. She wrote it down years ago—there's a paper, Marisol has the paper."

"I know. Marisol told me."

"She tells you things." Something moved under the words, not quite resentment, not quite envy. His father picked up his own glass, found it empty, and looked at it with mild betrayal. "We'll speak after. When this is finished. There are—arrangements. Papers. Her papers were not in order, or they were in order, but in her order, which is different."

"I can help with the papers."

"Yes." His father said it quickly, and then, quieter, looking out at the darkening street: "Yes. Maybe. We'll see what the papers say."

Inside, the room had filled and warmed. Someone had put music on low—an old record, a singer Ivo half-remembered from kitchens and car radios, a voice like smoke. He circulated. He performed the condolences. *Lo siento mucho,* he said, and *mi pésame,* and once, to an ancient aunt with hands like origami, he attempted a phrase in the other language and felt it come out bent in the middle, stress on the wrong syllable, and watched her hear it—watched the flicker cross her face, the small involuntary arithmetic of *ah, this one has been away, this one has let it go*—and she covered for him graciously, taking his hand and saying the response slowly, clearly, the way you speak to a child or a foreigner, and he thanked her in the working language because he couldn't trust himself to do it in hers.

By ten the room had thinned. Marisol found him washing glasses at the sink, because in every house there is one person who washes glasses to have something to do with their hands, and in this house it had always been him.

"You're staying at the hotel," she said. Not a question again. She dried while he washed, their rhythm intact across the decades, and for a moment they were just two siblings at a sink, and the grief was a thing happening nearby rather than to them.

"For tonight," he said. "Maybe the week."

"The house has beds."

"The house has—" He stopped. Set down a glass. "It's easier this way, Mari."

"Easier." She handed him another glass, harder than necessary. "You fly four hundred kilometers, you stand in Mamá Rosa's hallway, you eat Tía Carmen's food, and then you go sleep in a hotel with strangers, and it's *easier*."

"For everyone."

"For you," she said. "Say it correctly. It's easier for you." She set down the towel. Her voice dropped, and something in it made him turn. "Ivo. Tomorrow, after the ceremony, there will be people here you don't know. Old friends of hers. From—before." A hesitation, tiny but seismic. "Just—when they talk to you, when they say things, don't ask questions in front of Papá. Whatever they say. Whatever names they use. Let it pass."

"Names," Ivo said. "What names?"

Marisol looked at him with an expression he'd spent thirty years learning to read from a distance: the exact face of someone standing on a trapdoor they'd promised themselves was solid floor.

"No names," she said. "That's the point. Forget I said it. Go to your hotel, Ivo. Sleep. Be a guest tomorrow, and then go home."

*Home,* he thought, walking back through the renovated, half-shuttered streets an hour later, past the café selling brunch, past the chained fish market, the word ringing false in every direction. He let himself into the Meridional, climbed the stairs, and lay down in the narrow bed facing away from the sea.

Sleep came slow. And when it finally came, it brought the smell of camphor and lavender, and a voice speaking a language he could almost, but not quite, understand.
