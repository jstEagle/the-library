## 14. The Coded Garden

The orangery smelled of wet stone and old oranges, and in the grey morning light it looked less like a ruin than like a body that had decided, against all advice, to keep living. Vines had come in through the broken ridge-glass and made themselves at home along the iron ribs. A fig tree — the fig tree, Gideon said, older than either of them, older than the fire, older than the family's name on the county rolls — stood in the center of it all in a terracotta tub the size of a grave, its branches bare and patient, its roots pressing against the drainage holes like fingers testing the bars of a cell.

Sebastian sat at a table that had once, he supposed, been used for grafting. Now it held Lucia's letters, spread out in the order he'd learned to read them, and a jar of Gideon's cold coffee, and the small brass key Gideon had produced from his coat pocket an hour earlier without explanation, setting it down among the papers like a man laying a card face-up.

"Start with the one from March," Gideon said. "The one about the auriculas."

Sebastian found it. He had read it so many times now that the paper had gone soft at the folds, the ink slightly blurred where his thumb had worried the crease. *Dear G., The auriculas are thriving. I've moved the grey one to the cold frame, the one you said looked like rain about to happen. Mrs. V. admired it Tuesday. She does admire things right up until she wants them gone. The pelargoniums are a trial this year — root rot in the third bed, I think. I may have to dig the whole bed out and start again. L.*

"It's about auriculas," Sebastian said. "It was always about auriculas. I spent eleven years thinking my sister liked flowers."

"She did like flowers," Gideon said. "That's the cruelty of it. Or the mercy. She never wrote a single false sentence. She just wrote true sentences that meant more than they said." He turned the letter toward himself, and his finger — long, gardener's fingers, still dirt in the nails no matter how he scrubbed — traced the lines. "Auriculas were their word for the deeds. The forged ones. The grey auricula in the cold frame — that was the 1897 deed, the one they greyed up, aged with tea and a hot iron, the one that moved the west meadow into Vane hands. Your grandmother's meadow, if the Marchettis came from here the way I think they did."

Sebastian's throat tightened. "They did. My mother used to sing a song about a meadow. I always thought she'd invented it."

"Nothing here is invented," Gideon said. "That's the whole horror of the valley. Everything was real once, and then it was purchased."

* * *

They worked the way archaeologists work, or the way two people defuse something work: slowly, in near silence, each afraid of the wrong movement. Gideon knew the garden's private language because he had helped build it — that was the confession underneath all his confessions, the one he could only approach sideways, the way you approach a dog that bites. The letters had been written to him, in a cipher the two of them had grown together over three years, a language of beds and frames and rootstocks, and every horticultural instruction was a fact, a date, a name, a document.

"Root rot in the third bed," Sebastian read aloud. "Dig the whole bed out."

"The third parcel," Gideon said. "The Harrow field. She's saying the deed for the Harrow field is rotten — that she's seen the forgery, that she could show where the ink had been matched wrong, two different iron-gall batches, the kind of mistake a forger makes when he's rushed. And *dig the whole bed out and start again* — that's her saying she intends to expose it. To pull the whole thing up by the roots. Not just the one deed. All of them."

"How many were there?"

"Eleven parcels," Gideon said. "Forty years of quiet acquisition. My mother called it consolidation. The solicitors called it estate management. What it was, was a machine for turning other people's grandmothers into our lawn."

Sebastian looked at the letters spread across the grafting table and felt the vertigo come again, the same vertigo he'd felt the day Tomas Ferro set the tin box in his hands. Forty letters. He had spent eleven years writing to a burned address, and all that time the answers had existed — not answers to him, never to him, but answers to something, a correspondence running parallel to his grief like a second rail, and the two rails had never met until now. His sister had been conducting a war in the language of flowers, and he had been writing her postcards about the weather.

"She was three moves ahead of all of us," he said. "Even then."

"Always." Gideon said it with a tenderness that had rusted at the edges. "You never played cards with Lucia. She used to let me win. I didn't know that until it was too late to matter."

* * *

By the second afternoon they had the shape of it. Sebastian had brought Lucia's unsent replies — the forty letters he'd kept hidden for eleven years in a biscuit tin inside a suitcase inside a wardrobe, the twin of the tin box, though he hadn't known it was a twin until now — and when the two sets of correspondence lay side by side on the table, the cipher cracked open like a pod.

Because Lucia's replies to Sebastian were not in code. They were in flowers too, but a simpler dialect, a child's dialect, the one they'd invented as children in their mother's garden: *the beans are climbing* meant *I am well*; *the slugs got the lettuce* meant *I am sad, don't ask why*; *I've saved seed from the poppies* meant *I am keeping something safe for you.* She had written to her brother in the nursery language and to her lover in the operational one, and both languages had been growing from the same root the whole time, and neither brother nor lover had seen the other plant until the ground was opened.

"She was protecting you," Gideon said, reading one of Sebastian's postcards — Lucia's last, the one that had arrived three days before the fire. *The fig has fruited. I'm taking the first ripe one to the house tomorrow. If it goes well I'll save you some, but you know how it is with figs — you have to be there the day they're ready or you miss them entirely.*

"She was telling me to come," Sebastian said. His voice came out strange, flattened, as though someone else were speaking through him. "She was telling me — the fig's ready, come now, be here, because if you're not there the day it's ready—"

"—you miss it entirely." Gideon closed his eyes. "She asked you to come. Three days before."

"I didn't come. I had a shift. I had a *shift*." Sebastian heard the laugh in his own voice and hated it, the practiced laugh, the one that ended conversations. "I sent her a postcard saying I'd visit at Christmas."

The orangery held the silence the way the fig's tub held water, without letting it go. Somewhere in the ridge-glass a pigeon shifted its weight.

"She knew you'd say that," Gideon said at last, gently. "That's the thing you have to let yourself understand, Sebastian. She planned around the man you were, not the man you should have been. She loved him anyway. Both things at once. She was very good at both things at once."

* * *

It was the fig that finished it.

Gideon had been circling it all morning, literally circling, walking his slow orbit around the terracotta tub, and on the third morning he stopped and put both hands flat against the trunk as if taking a pulse.

"She wrote about this tree more than anything else in the garden," he said. "In the coded letters. *The old fig wants thinning.* *The fig has been here longer than the house and will outlast us all.* *A fig doesn't fruit from new wood — only from the old, only from what's already lived.* I thought she was sentimental. She was never sentimental." He looked up, and for the first time since Sebastian had arrived there was something in his face that wasn't guilt or grief — something quicker, brighter, dangerous. "She was leaving instructions. Sebastian. She was leaving *gardening instructions*."

"Thinning," Sebastian said. The word went through him like cold water. "Root pruning. You thin the roots of a container fig or it gets pot-bound. You lift it. You cut into the root ball. You—"

"—you dig," Gideon said.

They did not have a spade. They had a hand fork and a trowel from Gideon's tool locker, and a broken half of a slate they used as a scoop, and between the two of them they lifted the fig — it took both their weights on the trunk, rocking it, the tree coming loose from its tub with a sound like a held breath released — and laid it on its side on the sacking Gideon spread on the flags, and knelt, the two of them, the lover and the brother, at the roots of the tree that had been here longer than the house.

The root ball was dense, matted, silver-grey, older than either of them. Gideon worked the fork into it with the tenderness of a surgeon and the ruthlessness of a man who had decided, and cut.

The first thing they found was wax. A lump of it, candle-wax, worked deep into the root mass, sealing something. Then oilcloth, black with age, and inside the oilcloth, wrapped in a further layer of the same, a bundle that clinked faintly when it moved — glass. A preserving jar, wax-sealed at the lid, and inside the jar, dry as the day they'd gone in, a sheaf of papers.

They did not open it on the floor. Some instinct, old and shared, made them carry it to the table first, lay it down among the letters, and stand over it for a moment the way you stand over a sleeping thing you're afraid to wake.

Gideon broke the wax seal with the brass key he still hadn't explained.

Inside: eleven documents. Sebastian counted them twice, his hands shaking too much the first time to be sure. Eleven deeds — or rather, eleven *pairs*, the originals and the forgeries laid together, the true and the false, the meadow and the machine that had eaten it. And beneath them, folded small, four pages in Lucia's hand, the writing upright and unhurried, the writing he had known since he was six years old.

Gideon stepped back. "They're addressed to you," he said. "I can't. You should."

Sebastian unfolded the pages. The orangery light came down through the surviving panes of glass, green-tinted, aquarium-slow, and the words rose up at him out of eleven years.

*My brother, who will smile and say nothing —*

He had to stop. He had to put one hand flat on the table and breathe. Gideon did not touch him, which was, Sebastian understood dimly, the correct and brotherly thing to do, and he hated him a little for knowing it.

He read on.

*If you are reading this, then you finally came to the fig, and I am not there to see it, and I need you to know that I knew that might happen and I forgave it in advance, the way I forgive everything you do, which is a habit I refuse to break. Here is what they did, all of it, in order, with the papers in the order I found them. Here is the name of the solicitor who drew the forgeries. Here is the name of the man who was paid to be elsewhere on the night I was meant to leave — I will not write it here because he has children, and this is not their fault, but you will find it in the ledger if the ledger survives, and I believe it will, because Mrs. Vane never throws anything away that proves she won. Here is what they offered me, and here is what I said, and here is why.*

*I am not going to run. I want that written down somewhere besides my own head. Everyone will tell you I ran, or that there was an accident, or that girls like me are careless with lamp-oil and November. Let them. You will know how to smile and say nothing. But the fig will hold what I hold, and figs outlast houses, and one day the house will fall and the tree will still be standing, and you — my slow, kind, careful brother — will dig.*

*Save seed. Always save seed. That's the whole of the inheritance I have to give you.*

*Your sister, three moves ahead,*
*Lucia*

The last page of the orangery light had gone amber by the time Sebastian looked up. Gideon was standing at the window with his back turned, his shoulders doing something quiet and terrible, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"She knew," Sebastian said finally. "She knew about the ledger. She knew about the fire before there was a fire. She knew they'd burn the evidence, so she planted the evidence in the one thing on this estate they'd never destroy, because it was older than them, because it was *theirs*, because destroying it would have been admitting—" He stopped. Started again. "She hid the truth inside the family's own heirloom. She made the Vane name the vault for the proof against the Vanes."

Gideon turned around. His eyes were wet and he made no apology for it. "There's more," he said. "The seed archive. The cold store behind the potting shed — my mother had it bricked up after the fire, but brick is only brick. Lucia had keys to everything. If she hid the deeds in the fig, the thing she called *the seed I'm saving for you* — the thing she told you about, in your language, in the poppy letter — it'll be there. And whatever she put in the seed archive, Sebastian, she put it there to survive even the fig. Even if the tree died. Even if the glasshouse burned twice."

Sebastian looked down at the four pages, at his sister's upright, unhurried hand, at the sentence she had written for him and only him: *you will know how to smile and say nothing.*

He had spent eleven years proving her right. He intended, starting now, to spend whatever remained proving her wrong.

"We dig tomorrow," he said. "Before she hears we have the deeds. Because the moment Mrs. Vane understands what's been sitting in her own conservatory all these years—"

Across the valley, down the long lawn, in the lit windows of the house, a curtain moved.
