## 21. Postcards, Forward Address

The postcard came to him before he wrote it, the way bread comes to a table before anyone is hungry.

Sebastian found it in the stationer's on Harbour Street, three months and eleven days after the burning, wedged between a rack of birthday cards for nieces and a carousel of magnets shaped like lobsters. It was nothing. A photograph of the valley in early spring — the river a grey ribbon, the hills still holding their winter brown, and in the foreground, where the Marchetti glasshouse had stood for a hundred and forty years, a field of turned earth and, at its edge, the blackened iron ribs of the old house rising like the skeleton of a whale that had chosen to die on land.

Someone had printed it as a postcard. Someone in the village had stood in the cold with a camera and thought: *this is worth sending to someone.*

He bought ten.

The woman at the till rang them up without comment, though she glanced at the image and then at him, and he understood that she had done the arithmetic — the man, the ruin, the resemblance of grief to something else. The valley had been in the newspapers all winter. *VANE ESTATE DOCUMENTS PROMPT COUNTY INQUIRY.* *FORGED DEEDS, DISPLACED TENANTS, A FIRE REOPENED.* His name had appeared twice, in small type, spelled correctly both times, which he had decided was the strangest honour of his life.

He carried the postcards home to the flat above the chandlery, set them on the table beside the tin box — the tin box, which he had not been able to leave in the valley, which had crossed the country in his rucksack like a sleeping animal — and sat down to do what he had done every week for eleven years and would now, it seemed, do differently.

* * *

The flat was small and smelled of salt and paraffin, and he had never once thought of it as a place he was hiding in until the winter taught him otherwise. For eleven years the flat had simply been where the postcards were written. Now it was a place he had chosen to leave every week in order to stand at a counter in a coastal town and post a card to an address that had burned down before he was born — no. Before Lucia disappeared. He was getting the chronology right at last; it had taken him most of a lifetime and a fire to get the chronology right.

The address was the strange part. It had always been the strange part, and he had never examined it, the way you don't examine the load-bearing wall of a house you live in. *Lucia Marchetti, The Glasshouse, Marchetti Row, Vane Estate, Ashcombe Valley.* A house that had never existed as a dwelling. A row that was not a row. He had written it in his careful, upright hand — the hand she used to say looked like it had been printed, not written — every Sunday evening for eleven years, and the postman of that coastal town had collected the cards every Monday morning without a single question, because the post office does not judge, it only forwards, and there is a mercy in that, a bureaucratic mercy, that Sebastian had leaned on like a crutch and only now understood had been holding him up.

Eleven years of postcards. He had never counted them until the lawyers asked. Four hundred and eighty-one, give or take the weeks he'd been ill or drunk or simply unable. Four hundred and eighty-one small rectangles of cardstock, each bearing a message of two or three lines — the weather, the fish, the neighbour's cat, a book he'd read, a lie about how he was — each one a knock on a door that had burned down, each one a way of saying *I am still here, are you* without ever having to hear the answer.

He had burned none of them. That was the thing the journalists never quite got right when they wrote about him. He had never sent them — he had posted them, which is different — and the post office had returned them, undeliverable, addressee unknown, and he had kept every returned card in a shoebox and then two shoeboxes and then, after the fire in the valley, in the tin box itself, because it seemed right that they should finally live with her letters, the answered and the unanswered, the two halves of a conversation that had been going on all along in a house he'd thought was silent.

Because that was the vertigo that had never fully left him, not through the digging of the fig roots, not through the reading of her confession, not through the night he'd stood in Mrs. Vane's drawing room and used the smile as a weapon for the first time and watched it fail to work on a woman who had invented it: the vertigo of learning that his ritual had never been a monologue. Lucia had been writing too. Gideon had been answering. The glasshouse had been full of voices the whole time he'd been standing outside it in his coastal town, posting silence into a slot, and the silence had only ever been his.

* * *

He wrote the first of the ten postcards on a Tuesday, because Sunday had come to feel like a costume.

*Lucia — the valley has had its first real thaw. Tomas says the fig is taking, which he says the way his father never said anything, out loud, in daylight, to a witness. I keep expecting to feel finished. I am not finished. I don't think that's the point anymore.*

He read it over. He almost crossed out *I don't think that's the point anymore* — it was too much, too naked, the kind of sentence he'd spent eleven years training out of himself, the kind of sentence that invited a reply. Then he left it. That was the whole discipline now, the only one he practised: leaving the sentence in. Saying the thing and not smiling it away.

He did not write the old address. His pen hovered over the line — the muscle memory was still there, the *Glasshouse, Marchetti Row* rising in him like a tide — and then he wrote, instead:

*Lucia Marchetti, c/o The New Orchard, Vane Estate, Ashcombe Valley.*

It was not a real address either. There was no post office in the valley that delivered to an orchard. But Gideon had promised to walk down to the gate every week, and Tomas had promised to check, and between the two of them — the heir who had forfeited his name and the groundskeeper's son who had refused his settlement — the post would find its way, the way water finds its way, the way Lucia's words had found their way through forty years of earth and one wardrobe's hollow wall to a demolition crew with a crowbar and no idea what they'd struck.

He posted it on the Wednesday. He stood at the counter and watched the slot take it, and for the first time in eleven years the small mechanical *thock* of card hitting the collection bin sounded not like a knock on a sealed door but like a seed going into ground.

* * *

He went back to the valley in April. He told himself it was for the inquiry — there were statements to give, dates to confirm, the long grey machinery of the county investigation grinding through its gears, and his testimony was load-bearing, the solicitor had said, in the way of a man who means it as flattery and means it as fact. But the inquiry took two days, and he stayed eleven, and he knew what the extra nine were for.

The estate was unrecognisable and exactly the same. The great house stood shuttered now, its windows blank, its drive weeding itself at the edges — Mrs. Vane had been moved to a care facility two counties over in February, after the inquiry's second phase began, and Sebastian had declined the solicitor's offer to arrange a final meeting. He had said what he needed to say to her in her drawing room, with the smile, with the refusal. Everything after that was administration, and he had decided, with a firmness that surprised him, that Lucia's story would not end in an administration.

The glasshouse footprint, though. The glasshouse footprint was where the life was.

He walked out on his second morning through the wet grass with his collar up, and there was Tomas, on his knees in the blackened earth, and there was the fig.

It was not much to look at. A whip of a sapling, knee-high, staked with hazel, its roots sunk into the exact spot where they had dug up the tin cylinder Lucia had buried inside the rootball of the heirloom fig forty years ago — evidence wrapped in oilcloth, deeds and a confession, hidden in the living architecture of a garden, because Lucia Marchetti had understood that the safest vault is the one that grows. Tomas had taken a cutting from the old fig's surviving rootstock before the burning, had grieved over it through the winter in his father's old potting shed, and had planted it out the week the frost lifted, in the footprint, at the centre, where the old house's heart would have been.

*She'd want it growing in the ruin,* Tomas had said. *Not in a memorial garden. In the ruin. That's where she hid things — in places that looked like nothing.*

He was on his knees beside it now with a watering can and a hand lens, examining a leaf for the early signs of the fungal spot that figs get in wet valleys, and when he heard Sebastian's boots he didn't look up, just said, in the tone of a man continuing a conversation that had been running for three months:

"New growth. See — here and here. It took the transplant better than I had any right to expect."

"You had every right," Sebastian said. "You're the one who kept it alive."

"I'm the one who didn't kill it. There's a difference. My father taught me the first one and Lucia's notes taught me the second." He sat back on his heels, wiped his hands on his knees. His father had died in January — quietly, in his sleep, after finally saying everything, the whole ledger of it, the sums and the dates and the word *witnessed* — and Tomas wore the loss strangely, like a coat that fit better than anything he'd owned before. "She wrote propagation instructions into the letters, you know. In the cipher. I've been reading them like scripture. *Take the cutting at the waning moon, wound it deep, let it callus in the dark.* She was talking about cuttings."

"And about everything else."

"And about everything else." Tomas stood, joints cracking, forty-two years old and built like his father, which was to say like a man who had carried weight up hills for a living. "There's a letter I haven't shown you. I wasn't hiding it. I just — needed it to be mine for a while."

"Tomas—"

"It's not evidence. The solicitor's seen it. It's nothing." He looked at the sapling, at the black iron ribs beyond it, at the sky doing its grey valley thing. "It's the last letter she wrote before the fire. The real last one, not the confession. She wrote it to Gideon and it never got sent because Gideon was — " he paused, chose the word with care, " — away. It says: *Tell my brother nothing and everything. He will smile and say nothing, and one day the nothing will be too heavy, and he will put it down, and when he does, plant something in the spot.* "

Sebastian stood very still. The wind moved the wet grass. Somewhere up the valley a woodpecker was knocking on the world like it wanted in.

"She wrote that forty years ago," he said finally.

"She wrote it in November."

"And you're telling me now because—"

"Because it's April," Tomas said, "and things are planted, and it seemed like the season for it."

* * *

Gideon was in the east beds.

Sebastian found him there on his fourth morning, because he knew the geography of it now — the converted orangery at the estate's flank where Gideon Vane had lived his half-forgotten years, and the beds beyond it where he had begun, in March, doing a thing that no Vane had done in living memory, which was to work the soil of his own inheritance with his own hands.

He was planting seeds. That was the plain, absurd, enormous fact of it. Gideon Vane, sixty-eight years old, in a canvas coat too big for his diminished frame, kneeling at a prepared bed with a wooden seed tray and a pencil and a row of paper envelopes, each one labelled in a hand that shook and did not care that it shook. Lucia's seed archive. The collection she had built across her years at the estate — heirloom beans, valley peas, a lettuce the seed catalogues had given up on in the 1950s, the whole endangered congregation of a kitchen garden's memory — sealed in waxed packets and hidden, like everything she loved, in the walls of things.

"You're supposed to be resting," Sebastian said. The doctors had said it too; he'd heard the discharge summary read aloud over the phone, the litany of what the winter and the guilt and the signing-away had done to Gideon's heart.

"I am resting," Gideon said. "This is what resting looks like when you've spent your whole life doing it wrong." He patted the bed beside him. Sebastian sat down on the cold ground, because you did, with Gideon now; the hierarchy had burned with the glasshouse, and what remained was two old men on the dirt.

They worked in silence for a while. Sebastian opened envelopes. Gideon sowed. The drill was simple: a furrow with the pencil's flat, seeds pinched out between finger and thumb — *gently, they're asleep* — soil drawn over, a label, a date. Gideon wrote the dates like a man keeping an appointment with the future.

"Forty years in the dark," Gideon said, holding up an envelope of scarlet runner beans, the paper soft as cloth with age. "And she kept them viable. She dried them properly, sealed them properly. She thought about the day they'd be needed." He shook his head, and the shake became something else, the tremor that lived in him now, grief and palsy indistinguishable. "I think about that. That she prepared for a future she must have suspected she wouldn't be in. That's not hope, Sebastian. That's something sterner than hope. I've been trying all winter to find the word for it."

"Faith," Sebastian said.

"Tending," Gideon said. "I think the word is tending. Hope sits and waits. Tending gets its hands dirty on behalf of a day it will never see." He pressed a bean into the earth, drew the soil over it, and set the label: *Runner, scarlet. Sown 14 April. By G.V., for L.M.*

They sowed the whole east bed that morning. When they were done, Gideon sat back and looked at the long dark stripe of turned earth with its regiment of labels, and said, without preamble, without the deflection that had been his native tongue for forty years:

"I've signed everything. The solicitor came Tuesday. The statement's with the inquiry, my name on it, my mother's arrangements in it, all of it. When it's done I'll have the cottage and my pension and nothing else, and I want you to know that I have never in my life been this frightened or this — " he searched, and found it, and said it plainly — "this light."

Sebastian looked at him. This was the man Lucia had loved, the G. of the forty letters, the boy who had chosen the inheritance and spent four decades paying for the choice, and Sebastian had spent the winter learning not to hate him, which had been harder than learning to hate Mrs. Vane, because hatred of Mrs. Vane had come easily, had come pre-installed, whereas this — this forgiveness-adjacent thing, this recognition of another man's ruin as a room he himself had lived in — had to be built by hand, like everything worth having.

"I'm going to write to her about it," Sebastian said. "All of it. The inquiry, the beans, the fig. She always liked the beans."

"She did," Gideon said. His voice went strange. "She used to say a bean is a small miracle that will feed you if you just get out of its way."

* * *

He wrote the last postcard on the Sunday, in the potting shed, because Tomas had gone to market and left him the key and the table by the window, and because it seemed right — the shed had been his father's, and Tomas's childhood, and now, in some unspoken way, all of theirs.

The postcard was one of the ten from Harbour Street. The valley in early spring, the ribs of the glasshouse black against the sky. He turned it over and looked at the empty lines for a long time.

Eleven years of postcards, and every one of them had been a way of not saying the thing. *The weather is fine. The fish are running. I am well, I hope you are.* Four hundred and eighty-one small acts of cowardice dressed as devotion, and he did not regret them — he had learned that too, in the winter, that regret was just another way of making the past about himself — but he understood now what they had been for. They had been a glasshouse. A small transparent structure he had built around his grief so that it could have light and not escape, so that it could grow in a controlled climate, so that he could walk around it every Sunday and admire the shape of his own loss and call it love.

Lucia had never needed a glasshouse. Lucia had planted things in the ground and in the walls and in the roots of trees and in the hearts of two men who didn't know they were soil, and she had done it in the certain knowledge that the truth, like a bean, only asks to be gotten out of the way of.

He wrote:

*Lucia — Last one to the old address; all the ones after this go to the orchard, care of the beans. Gideon sowed your archive on the fourteenth. Tomas has the fig in the footprint and it's taken, and he reads your cipher like scripture and argues with you in the margins, which I think you'd have loved. The inquiry has your name in it now, spelled correctly, in the big type. They're calling the fire suspicious. They're calling a lot of things a lot of things. I put the nothing down, Lu. It was heavy, like you said. I planted something in the spot. Come spring next year there will be glass here again — Tomas has a plan, Gideon has the salvaged panes stacked and numbered, and I have, of all things, money, which I am going to spend on windows. I am well. I hope you are. I hope you are where the beans are.*

He read it over. He left every sentence in.

Below the message, in the address block, he wrote — carefully, uprightly, the printed-looking hand she'd teased him about — *Lucia Marchetti, c/o The New Orchard, Vane Estate, Ashcombe Valley.* And then, because the card was the last of the old ritual and the first of whatever came next, he added a line beneath the address, small, in the corner where the stamp would go, where only the postmark and the patient machinery of the world would ever see it:

*Forwarding address confirmed.*

* * *

He posted it Monday morning at the village post office, where the landlady's sister worked the counter, and where the free-meal smile had been retired sometime in February and replaced with something rawer and more human, which was to say: a face that didn't know what to do and let you see it.

The valley took the card from him with its small mechanical *thock*, and Sebastian Marchetti stood a moment on the step with his hands in his pockets, looking down the street at the hills, at the river, at the black ribs on the skyline with their new crop of scaffolding — because the scaffolding had gone up that week, Tomas's cousin's firm, timber and glass to come, the first drawings of a thing they were already calling, in the village, in the careful new way the village had of saying things it used to refuse to say, the Marchetti glass.

He was still standing there when a car he didn't recognise came up the street and stopped, and a woman got out of it — young, city coat, a portfolio under her arm and the look of someone who had driven a long way on the strength of a newspaper clipping — and stood in front of him, uncertain, and said:

"Excuse me — I'm sorry — are you from here? Do you know anything about the glasshouse? The one that burned? I'm writing about it — about the woman who — I've been reading everything I can, and nobody in this village will talk to me, they just — " and here she did it, the smile, the exact smile, the one that ends conversations, deployed out of fear, out of training, out of the same old valley air he'd breathed it from — and then she saw his face, and the smile faltered, and she asked, quieter, "Do you know her? Did you know Lucia Marchetti?"

And Sebastian, who had spent a lifetime ending conversations, heard himself say:

"I knew her better than anyone alive. Would you like to hear it from the beginning?"
