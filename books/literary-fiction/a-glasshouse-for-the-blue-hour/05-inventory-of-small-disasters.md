## 5. Inventory of Small Disasters

The clipboard was a kind of shield. Marisol had learned that in her first year at the county office, when she'd discovered that if her hands were full of forms and her face was arranged into the neutral competence the job required, people left her alone with her thoughts. A clipboard said: *I am working. Do not mistake me for a person who might be feeling something.*

She stood at the edge of the Reyes lot at seven-forty in the morning, before the fog had finished lifting off the water, and told herself exactly that. *This is work. Lot 14, Parcel B, structure fire of record dated August ninth, eleven years past. Salvage assessment per bank protocol.* The words went through her like a rosary, each bead smooth and meaningless, and she gripped them because the alternative was to look at what she was actually looking at.

The glasshouse frame still stood.

She hadn't let herself think about it on the drive over from the motel—hadn't let herself think about much of anything since the handshake yesterday, since Jonah Reyes's hand had closed around hers and Marisol had felt ten years collapse into the space between their palms like a lung going down. She'd driven back to the Sea Breeze Motel, eaten half a sandwich she couldn't taste, and slept badly in a bed that smelled of industrial lavender, and now here she was, clipboard up, pen uncapped, doing her job in the place where her whole life had split into before and after.

*Structure: steel and wrought-iron glazing frame, approximately four hundred square feet, original construction attributed to Daniel Reyes, contractor. Fire damage: total loss of glazing; frame integrity surprisingly sound. Corrosion present but not structural.*

Her father's handwriting would have been neater. Her father had appraised properties for the same county office for nineteen years before her, had trained half the people she now worked alongside, and every form he filled out looked like it could be framed. She wrote small and slanted and pressed too hard, so that the third page beneath always carried a ghost of whatever she was writing on top. Her father used to tease her about it. *You're carving, mija, not writing.*

She did not think about her father. She cataloged.

*Outbuildings: none remaining. Perimeter fencing: chain-link, rusted, approximately sixty percent intact. Vegetation: invasive ice plant, blackberry bramble, one surviving Monterey cypress at northwest corner, estimated age forty years.*

The cypress had been there when they were girls. It leaned out over the lot like a woman listening at a window, and Marisol remembered—she made herself remember, because avoidance was just another way of carrying a thing, only heavier—how they used to climb its lowest branch together, Jonah below and Marisol above, because Jonah was afraid of heights and Marisol was afraid of nothing then, or thought she was. They'd sit up there some summer afternoons and watch the fishing boats come in and invent lives for themselves that all ended in the same place: this town, grown old, side by side, two houses with a gate knocked through the fence between them.

Marisol lowered the clipboard and looked at the glasshouse properly for the first time.

It was worse than a ruin and better than nothing. The fire had taken every pane, of course—the glass had gone in the first minutes, she'd been told, a sound like a thousand small bells falling downstairs—and the heat had warped the iron ribs so that the whole structure listed slightly eastward, toward the sea, as if leaning to watch the water. But the bones held. Daniel Reyes had built it the year Jonah was born, everyone said, a gift for his wife who missed the gardens of wherever she'd come from, and he'd built it the way he built everything, to outlast the person who wanted it. It stood there in the gray morning light like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down here to die and changed its mind halfway.

Inside the frame, the ash lay ankle-deep and even, drifted against the foundation stones like snow that had fallen indoors. Someone—Jonah's mother, probably, or the bank's cleanup crew—had hauled out the large debris years ago, the beams and furniture and the terrible unidentifiable things. What remained was the fine silt of a life: ash, broken glass fused into pebbles by the heat, shards of ceramic, the occasional pale fragment of bone-dry wood.

Marisol stepped over the low sill into the glasshouse, and the ash took her boot print, and she felt the trespass of it all the way up her arm.

*Interior contents,* she wrote. *See attached itemized inventory.*

The itemized inventory took her until noon.

She worked methodically, quadrant by quadrant, because that was how her father had taught her—you start at the door and you go clockwise and you don't skip, because the thing you skip is always the thing that matters—and she photographed and tagged and described. A hinge, brass, heat-blued. A drawer pull. The cast-iron leg of something, maybe a potting bench, maybe a stove. A child's rubber boot, fused to its own sole, size too small to be anyone's anymore. Each object went into a labeled bag, and each bag went into a bin, and each bin went onto the list, and the list grew, and the list was the point, because a list was finite and grief was not, and as long as she was writing *ferrous bracket, corroded, loc. NW quad* she was not writing anything else.

Around eleven the fog burned off and the light came through the empty ribs of the roof and fell across the ash floor in long bright bars, and Marisol stopped, because for one disorienting second the glasshouse looked almost the way it must have looked when it worked—light everywhere, falling through, unhindered—and her chest did something complicated and painful that she dealt with by writing down *glazing absent; natural light penetration total* in her smallest, hardest handwriting.

This was the trick of the work. This was the armor. You turned feeling into data. You turned the worst afternoon of your life into Parcel B, Structure Fire of Record, and you filed it, and filing was a kind of burial, and burial was a kind of peace, or the nearest thing to peace the county office offered in its benefits package.

At noon she sat on the low stone wall at the lot's edge and ate the other half of yesterday's sandwich and did not look at the sea, which was a discipline she'd perfected years ago and could perform anywhere, on any coast, without effort.

That was when she saw the tin.

It was partly buried at the base of the northeast corner post, where the ash had drifted deepest, and the tide of her surveying had washed around it twice already—she'd logged the corner post itself, *steel, anchor bolts intact*—but the light had shifted at noon and hit the tin's edge at an angle, and it threw back a flash of color, absurdly vivid, a coin of blue in all that gray.

Marisol set down the sandwich.

She knew before she dug. That was the cruelty of objects; they waited under the ash of eleven years keeping their colors, and your body recognized them before your mind consented to. Her hands were moving before she decided anything, brushing the ash away in careful sweeps the way she'd been trained to handle evidence, though this wasn't evidence, this was worse than evidence, this was—

A tin. Rectangular, hinged lid, once held hard candies from the shop on Delgado Street, the kind with the paper wrappers her grandmother saved in a jar. The paint was scorched along one edge and the hinges were seized with corrosion, but the front of it still showed its picture: a sailboat, red-sailed, on a blue sea, and beneath the boat, in cheerful looping script, the word *SALTWATER TAFFY* though the tin had never held taffy in its life.

Marisol knelt in the ash and held the tin in both hands and did not open it for a long time.

She knew whose it was. Of course she knew. There had only ever been one tin like it within a mile of this lot, and it had lived on the windowsill of the Reyes sunroom, and it had belonged to a girl who kept her treasures in it the way other children kept theirs in shoeboxes: a bird's feather, blue-jay, found whole. Four sea-glass pieces, ranked by color. A movie ticket stub from the summer they were twelve, when they'd seen something forgettable and held hands in the dark without either of them acknowledging it, because acknowledging it would have required a language neither of them had yet.

And marbles. Forty-one marbles, counted and recounted, traded over and negotiated for across three summers, the currency of their entire childhood economy. There was the big swirlie they called the Ocean because of the way the blue curled inside it like a wave caught mid-break. There were the two cat's-eyes Jonah had won off Tommy Okafor and immediately regretted winning, because Tommy cried, and Jonah Reyes had never in her life been able to stand anyone crying, which was either her best quality or the crack everything eventually fell through. There was the chipped aggie Marisol herself had given Jonah, deliberately, secretly, the last week before her family left—a bad trade, a marble with a flaw, given away like it was treasure, because Marisol had wanted to leave something of hers behind in that house and hadn't known how to say so.

The hinges wouldn't give. She worked them with her thumbnail, then with the flat of her appraisal pen, and the tin opened with a small grinding complaint, and the smell that came out was nothing—ash and salt and metal, no preserved childhood, no ghost of candy—but the sight of the contents went through her like a hook through soft tissue.

Marbles. Not forty-one. Eleven, maybe twelve, scattered in the ash dust at the bottom of the tin, and one of them was the Ocean.

They must have been in the sunroom windowsill when the fire came. Everything else in that room had gone—curtains, cushions, the wicker chair where Jonah's mother read, the photographs on the shelf—but the tin had been metal, and metal keeps, and somewhere in the roar of that night the little box had dropped or rolled or been thrown by the collapsing heat into the corner where it waited eleven years for someone with a clipboard and a legal obligation to kneel down and find it.

Marisol sat back on her heels in the ash of the Reyes house and wept.

Not gracefully, not the single photogenic tear of the movies. She sobbed, ugly and open-mouthed, the clipboard abandoned beside her with its neat columns of *ferrous bracket, corroded*, and she cried for the marbles and the girl who'd owned them and the ticket stub and the feather and the gate they were going to knock through the fence, and underneath all of it, underneath everything, she cried for the reason she had not opened this particular door in eleven years, which was standing thirty feet away from her yesterday offering a professional handshake with eyes like a struck match.

Because it had been easier to believe Jonah had chosen silence. It had been easier to build a life on the assumption that her best friend had watched the Vega family get driven out of Port Merrow on a tide of suspicion and pity and had simply—let it happen. Had stayed. Had kept her house and her mother and her town and her future, and had weighed all of it against one Vega girl, and found the arithmetic acceptable. Children do that math all the time, Marisol knew; she'd done versions of it herself. But knowing didn't make the sum hurt less, and she had carried the sum for a decade the way you carry a stone in your pocket—forgetting its weight until you reach in for change.

And now she was kneeling in the ashes of the house that arithmetic had built, holding a tin of marbles, and the stone in her pocket had never felt heavier.

*Item:* she wrote, later, when she could write. Her hand shook and she steadied it against her knee. *One (1) tin container, lithographed, heat-damaged, containing assorted glass marbles (approx. 11), condition fair. Recovered NE corner, beneath ash stratum. Probable original owner: J. Reyes.*

She looked at those last three words for a long time. Then she added a line beneath, in the small hard handwriting her father used to tease her about, pressing so hard the ghost of it would show through three pages:

*Recommend return to owner.*

She bagged the tin. She tagged it. She placed it in the bin with the hinges and the drawer pulls and the fused boot, all the small disasters of one household, and she lidded the bin, and she sat on the stone wall until the shadows of the glasshouse ribs stretched long and blue across the ash, and she told herself the day was done and the feeling was filed.

But walking back to the car she passed the Monterey cypress, still leaning, still listening, and she stopped, and she put her hand flat against its bark the way you'd touch the shoulder of someone sleeping, and she heard herself say, out loud, to no one, in a voice scraped raw:

"She kept the marbles."

Which meant the girl had expected to come back. Which meant the girl had never treated any of it—not the house, not the town, not, God help her, possibly not Marisol—as finished. And Marisol stood there with her hand on the tree and her armor dented clean through, and understood that tomorrow at nine a.m. she was contractually obligated to walk this lot again with Jonah Reyes at her side, and that the appraisal schedule, that innocent bureaucratic document, had just become the most dangerous thing in her life.

She got in the car. She did not start the engine for a while.

In the bin behind her, in the dark, the Ocean marble held its wave.
