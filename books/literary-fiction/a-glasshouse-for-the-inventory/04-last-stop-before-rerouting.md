## 4. Last Stop Before Rerouting

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were the city's day for bad news — budget cuts, water main repairs, the annual letter reminding Oscar that their building's fire escape had been condemned twice and was now, presumably, condemned in perpetuity. So when Oscar stepped off the 47 at the courthouse stop and saw the laminated sheet zip-tied to the pole, they knew before reading it what it would say.

**PUBLIC NOTICE: ROUTE 47 SERVICE CHANGE**

*Effective [date], Route 47 will be rerouted via Meridian Avenue to serve the new Riverside Transit Center. Stops along Corliss Street, Foundry Road, and the Elgin district will be discontinued.*

The Elgin district. Two words doing the work of a eulogy.

Oscar stood there long enough that a woman with a wheeled cart of oranges asked if they were all right, and Oscar said *sí, gracias* — out loud, on the street, where anyone could hear — and then got back on the same bus they'd just exited, because the alternative was standing at a bus pole grieving a piece of paper, and they had testimony to soften at nine o'clock.

All day in Courtroom 4C, the notice sat folded in their breast pocket like a stone. A witness described a break-in at her cousin's shop, and Oscar translated her rapid, furious Spanish into something measured and adjectivally restrained for the judge: *he entered without permission* became *the defendant gained access*; *he took everything, everything, even my mother's radio* became *various items were removed from the premises.* The witness glared at Oscar once, mid-sentence, as if she could tell. They usually could tell. It was the one part of the job Oscar had never learned to make peace with: not the softening itself, but the way people watched their words come out smaller than they'd gone in.

At lunch, in the stairwell where the vending machine hummed like a hive, Oscar unfolded the notice again and read it properly this time. Six weeks. After that, the 47 would swing east along Meridian, past the new condos with the balconies nobody sat on, and the old route — Corliss, Foundry, the whole spine of the neighborhood where Oscar had grown up two doors from a glasshouse nursery — would be severed from the grid like a branch pruned for the tree's own good.

Six weeks was nothing. Six weeks was how long it took to forget to do something.

They thought about the song again. They'd been thinking about it for eleven days now, since the night bus and the DJ with the smoke-and-honey voice who had played four minutes of untitled piano and voice off a tape labeled only *found*, and Oscar had sat up so fast they'd cracked their head on the handrail. Their mother's lullaby. The one she sang exactly once, in the kitchen, with the window open, and then forbade — forbade — anyone from writing down or humming into a recorder, because *some things have to live in bodies, mijo, or they don't live at all.* And here it was, living in a body after all: the radio's body, the city's airwaves, broadcast to strangers who didn't know whose throat it had come from.

Eleven days, and Oscar still hadn't figured out how a song with no address had found one.

But the bus had. Or the bus was losing its address, which amounted to the same thing.

* * *

That Friday, Oscar did something they hadn't done since childhood: they rode the 47 to the end of the line.

Not the new end of the line — the old one, the loop through Elgin that would die in six weeks. They boarded at the courthouse at 7:40 p.m., deliberately missing the last direct transfer home, and took a window seat near the back, and told themselves this was research. Due diligence. A translator's habit of checking sources. It was none of those things. It was a man sitting vigil with a bus.

The driver was a broad woman named Odalys — the name stenciled on a little placard by the fare box, because drivers on the 47 had names, had always had names; you couldn't run a route through a neighborhood for thirty years anonymously — and she gave Oscar a look in the big mirror as they settled in.

"You lost?" she asked.

"Riding to the end," Oscar said.

"End of the line's been the end of the line for forty years." She pulled out from the curb. "Now it's going to be the end of me, too. You hear about the reroute?"

"I read the notice."

"The notice." She said it the way you'd say *the diagnosis*. "Six weeks. Then I'm driving tourists to the Riverside Transit Center so they can buy candles that smell like 'heritage.' You know what heritage smells like? Diesel and plantains. That's it. That's the whole scent profile."

Oscar laughed — a real one, surprised out of them — and Odalys caught their eye in the mirror and seemed satisfied, the way drivers are when they've extracted a laugh from a stranger at no extra fare.

The bus moved north along Corliss Street, and Oscar watched the neighborhood go by like footage of a country they'd emigrated from without ever leaving.

The laundromat on the corner had become a phone-repair shop, but the tile floor was still the same checkerboard, worn gray at the door. The bakery was still a bakery — miracle number one — though the sign now said ARTISAN and the bread cost more per loaf than their mother used to spend on a week of groceries. The barbershop was still the barbershop, its pole spinning its useless, beautiful lie. A church Oscar half-remembered attending for other people's weddings had a banner out front: LAST SUNDAY IN THIS BUILDING — ALL WELCOME. All welcome, apparently, including the wrecking ball.

Every block had a notice. Zip-tied to poles, taped to windows, flaking off the shelter glass. PUBLIC NOTICE. PUBLIC NOTICE. PUBLIC NOTICE. The city had been thorough, at least. It had informed everyone, in advance, in writing, in three languages, that it was done with them.

"That's the third church this year," said a man across the aisle, to no one, to Oscar, to the bus. He had a toolbox on his lap and the forearms of someone who lifted heavy things for a living or for therapy. "St. Brigid's, then the Pentecostal place on Foundry, now this. God keeps getting rezoned."

"They should put him on the historic register," Oscar said.

"He'd qualify. Old, drafty, everybody claims to love him, nobody shows up." The man snorted at his own joke and then looked briefly ashamed of it, and Oscar understood: that was the local dialect. Grief spoken fluent, with humor as the accent.

The bus turned onto Foundry Road, and there it was.

Oscar hadn't meant to look. Had specifically arranged their sightline toward the window on the wrong side, a maneuver requiring genuine commitment. But Foundry curved, and the bus curved with it, and the glasshouse rose up behind its chain-link fence the way it always had — sudden, green, impossible. Even derelict. Even in the dark. The streetlight at the corner caught the ridge of the roof and lit the panes like a lantern with most of its candle burned away, and ferns had climbed the south wall so thickly that the structure looked less abandoned than *reclaimed*, as if the plants had filed the paperwork and were simply waiting for the city to process it.

And hanging in the open doorway — Oscar would swear later, would swear under oath if asked — something small and metal turned in the breeze and threw back the light. Chimes. Someone had hung chimes in a dead nursery.

The bus didn't stop there. Nobody got on, nobody got off; the stop had been flagged out of service months ago, a plastic sleeve over the sign like a hood. But Odalys slowed anyway — slowed, Oscar noticed, the way you slow passing a cemetery or an ex's house — and for three seconds the glasshouse hung in the window, whole and lit and listening, and then it slid backward into the dark.

Oscar's chest did something unscheduled.

* * *

The end of the line was a turnaround by the old freight depot, a loop of cracked asphalt where the bus idled for eight minutes before beginning the return trip. Odalys announced it the way she must have announced it ten thousand times — "Elgin depot, end of the line, bus goes back in eight minutes" — but tonight she added, almost gently, "Last month you'll ever hear me say it."

Three other passengers remained: the toolbox man, an elderly woman with a shopping trolley, and a teenager asleep against the window with his headphones leaking a thin metallic music. Nobody moved to get off. Nobody lived at the depot. The end of the line was a place you passed through on your way back to somewhere real, and perhaps that was why the city felt safe cutting it — no one was *from* the terminus. The terminus was just where the neighborhood ran out.

Oscar stayed on. Of course they stayed on. Riding to the end and then riding back was the entire point, though they'd have struggled to articulate it to anyone. *I rode a bus in a circle* was not a sentence that survived translation into adult purpose. But their body understood. Their body remembered being seven years old, riding this exact route with their mother after her shift at the laundry, her hands smelling of starch and bleach, her humming — never the lullaby, never that, but other songs, borrowed songs, songs with addresses — while the windows fogged and the neighborhood scrolled past like a film she narrated in a low voice: *that's where Doña Elena fell off her roof and lived, that's where the riots started and ended in one afternoon, that's the glasshouse, don't ask, we don't go there anymore.*

We don't go there anymore. Her voice had done something strange on those words every time. Not fear. Something adjacent to fear. Regret wearing fear's coat, maybe, so regret could walk around outside without being recognized.

The return trip began. The teenager woke, confused about the decade. The woman with the trolley argued cheerfully with Odalys about a fare increase that had happened in 2019 and showed no signs of resolving. And Oscar pressed their forehead to the cool glass and let the neighborhood scroll past again, backward this time, rewound, all the notices un-zip-tying themselves in reverse, and thought about six weeks.

Six weeks until the 47 forgot this route. Buses didn't forget, exactly — but routes died, and when a route died, the knowledge died with it: which corner flooded, which light was timed wrong, which driver took the turn on Foundry wide because of the sunken drain grate. Thirty years of accumulated intimacy between a vehicle and a street, deleted by a laminated notice. The city called it optimization. The neighborhood would call it, if the neighborhood bothered calling it anything, the second time it had been erased — the first having been quieter, and involving a producer named Aurelio Sanch, and a shoebox full of tapes, and names peeled off the masters like labels off fruit.

Oscar had spent ten years not thinking about Aurelio Sanch, and had gotten extremely good at it, the way you get good at anything with practice.

But the song had broken the seal. Four minutes of piano on a late-night frequency, and now the whole archive of not-thinking had sprung a leak, and things were getting out. The photograph on Marisol's family fridge — four children squinting outside the glasshouse, faces half-turned away, one of them Oscar's, one of them belonging to a person Oscar had spent a decade not forgiving. The sound of the tapes being carried out of the community hall in Sanch's crates, that summer day, the particular scrape of vinyl on cardboard. Their own silence, ten years old, holding.

And now the bus. Now the deadline made physical: six weeks, stamped and laminated, ticking down on every pole.

Here was the thing Oscar understood somewhere below language: erasure never announces itself as erasure. It arrives as efficiency. As rerouting. As *service change*. As a translator smoothing a witness's fury into adjectives. Nobody burns the library; they just discontinue the line that runs to it, and let the ferns do the rest.

The bus crossed back over the canal. On the far bank, the new transit center glowed like a cruise ship docked in a wheat field, all glass and blue LEDs, serving routes to places that already had routes.

"Hey," Odalys said, at the courthouse stop, as Oscar stood. "You get what you needed?"

It was such an odd question — drivers didn't ask it, drivers announced stops and adjudicated fares — that Oscar answered honestly, which was unlike them.

"No."

"Yeah." She nodded like this confirmed a theory. "Nobody ever does at the end of the line. That's kind of the point of it." She checked her mirror, signaled, and added, before pulling out: "There's a lady been fixing up that old greenhouse, you know. Out on Foundry. People say she's got tapes in there. Whole boxes of them. Music nobody's heard in years."

Oscar's hand was on the pole. The doors hissed.

"She hangs those little fork-chimes in the doorway," Odalys said. "You can hear them from the stop, some nights, if the wind's right. Sounds almost like somebody tuning up."

The doors closed. Through the glass, Oscar saw Odalys catch their expression in the mirror and smile — small, private, the smile of a woman who had delivered a passenger exactly as far as the route allowed and no farther.

The 47 pulled away toward Corliss Street, its lights shrinking down the avenue, and Oscar stood at the courthouse stop in the dark with the folded notice in their pocket and the word *tapes* ringing in them like a struck fork.

Monday, they told themselves. Monday they would figure out who she was.

Their feet, which had never once in ten years obeyed a schedule, were already turning toward Foundry.
