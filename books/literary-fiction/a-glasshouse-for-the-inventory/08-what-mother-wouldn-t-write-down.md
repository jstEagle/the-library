## 8. What Mother Wouldn't Write Down

The summer Oscar turned eleven, their mother decided to teach them the lullaby.

Not on purpose. That was the thing about Adaeze Villalobos — she never did anything on purpose that mattered. The important things happened sideways, in the margins of ordinary afternoons, the way a letter arrives inside an envelope addressed to someone else. She was repotting ferns in the kitchen — the glasshouse nursery had closed by then, but she had smuggled home three of its children and refused to call them anything but *the orphans* — and she was humming, and then she wasn't humming anymore. She was singing.

Oscar stopped halfway through the doorway with a glass of orange juice sweating in their hand and stood very still, the way you stand still when a bird lands near you.

The song had no words they knew. It moved between two languages like a person crossing a river on stepping stones — half of it in the soft Yoruba-inflected tongue their mother used only when she was tired or praying or talking to the plants, and half of it in something older than either language, something that was mostly melody pretending to be speech. Her voice was not what anyone would have called beautiful. It was thin at the top and gravelly at the bottom, and it cracked once in the middle of a phrase, and the crack became part of the song, so that afterward Oscar could never hear the phrase without expecting the crack, and when it didn't come, the phrase felt undressed.

She sang it through twice. On the second pass, her hands kept working the soil around the fern roots, patting it down, and Oscar understood even then — with the strange double vision children have, seeing both the surface and the enormous thing underneath — that the song and the potting were the same act. She was tucking something in. She was pressing something down where it would hold.

Then she finished, wiped her hands on her apron, and said, "You'll forget that."

"I won't," Oscar said.

"You will." She took the juice glass from them and drank half of it herself, which was a liberty she took with everything of theirs and no one else's. "That's fine. Forgetting is how it stays clean."

Oscar did not forget. Not one note. They carried it up to their room that night like contraband and lay on the bed replaying it against the ceiling, testing each phrase for wear, astonished that something so large could fit inside a person without showing from the outside.

* * *

The trouble started three days later, when Oscar wrote it down.

They couldn't help it. This was the summer the school librarian had called them *meticulous*, which Oscar had looked up in the dictionary at home and decided was the finest word ever coined. They kept notebooks. They transcribed things — bus schedules, overheard arguments, the names of clouds from a library book. Their mother called them *el escribano*, the scribe, and meant it as a tease, mostly.

So on a Tuesday afternoon, sitting cross-legged on the floor of their room with the door shut, Oscar wrote out the lullaby. It took two hours. The words in her language were hard to spell because nobody had ever taught them how; Oscar spelled them the way they sounded, which made them look wrong on the page, like the song wearing someone else's clothes. The notes they marked with numbers under the lines, a system they invented on the spot and were very proud of. When they finished, they read it back silently, sang it through twice in a whisper, and felt the particular satisfaction of a person who has caught a bird and put it safely in a cage so that nothing bad can ever happen to it.

Their mother found the notebook four days later.

Oscar never learned how. She didn't go through their things — she had a horror of that, having grown up in a house where drawers were searched — but mothers have other instruments. Perhaps she heard them practicing. Perhaps she simply looked at their face across the dinner table and saw the secret sitting behind it like a guest who'd taken off his shoes.

She asked to see the notebook, and Oscar, eleven years old and proud, handed it over like a tribute.

She read it standing in the kitchen, under the light that buzzed, and her face did something Oscar had never seen it do. Not anger. Anger would have been survivable. What crossed her face was closer to grief arriving early — the look of a woman reading her own obituary in draft form.

"Where did you get this?"

"You. I wrote it down. From you."

"Who told you to write it down?"

"No one. I wanted to keep it."

She closed the notebook gently, which was worse than slamming it. Then she went to the stove, lit the burner — the old gas one, with the pilot that had to be coaxed — and held the open notebook over the flame until the corner of the page browned, curled, and went up.

"Mama—"

"Some things," she said, holding the burning notebook over the sink, turning it so the fire ate it evenly, page by page, all two hours of Oscar's meticulous work, "must live in bodies or not at all."

The smoke smelled like ink and like oranges, somehow, though there were no oranges in it. Oscar watched the numbers of their invented notation system flare and vanish and thought, with a clarity that felt like being struck: *she has burned me.* Because the transcription had been theirs — their spelling, their system, their pride. She hadn't just destroyed a copy of the song. She had destroyed the first thing Oscar had ever made, and she had done it without asking, in their own kitchen, under the buzzing light.

"Why?" Their voice came out smaller than they wanted.

"Because paper can be taken." She ran the tap over the ashes. "Paper can be sold. Paper can end up in a drawer in some man's office with your name peeled off it. A body can't be stolen. A body can only be silenced, and silencing takes work, and most men are lazy."

"But now if you die, the song dies."

She turned off the tap and looked at them, and for a moment she seemed about to laugh — her mouth did the thing it did before laughing, the left corner lifting — and then it didn't lift.

"Yes," she said. "Now you understand."

She rinsed the ashes down the drain and went back to the ferns as if nothing had happened, and Oscar stood in the kitchen holding the shape of a rule they would spend thirty years resenting: *some things must live in bodies or not at all.* A beautiful sentence. A cruel one. It meant the song lived in her body alone, and when her body stopped, it would live in Oscar's body, unshared, unwritten, a whole country with no maps — and it meant that every time Oscar opened a notebook after that, some small clerk in their chest checked the pages first, to make sure nothing of hers had slipped in.

* * *

Here is what Oscar understood later, much later, too late to say it to her: she wasn't protecting the song from the world. She was protecting the world's claim on her.

Adaeze Villalobos had come to this city with a voice and nothing else, and within five years there were men with tape recorders at every gathering, men who smiled like Aurelio Sanch smiled, men who said *sing it again, slower, we'll fix you a contract,* and the contracts fixed nothing except the men's ownership. She had watched names come off music the way labels come off jars in a steamy kitchen — slowly, completely, until the jar holds something anonymous that anyone may claim. She had decided that the only archive that couldn't be raided was the one walking around inside her daughter.

But eleven-year-olds do not receive explanations. Eleven-year-olds receive burnt notebooks and rules, and they file the resentment away in the same body where the song lives, which means the song and the resentment grew up together, entangled, like the fern roots she used to separate with such patience — *careful, careful, they've grown into each other, you'll wound them both if you pull.*

* * *

There was one more time. Oscar has never told anyone this, not Marisol, not the notebook, not the dark.

It was the winter before she died, and she was already thin in the way that frightened people who loved her. Oscar, twenty-six by then and working in the courts, translating witnesses into softer versions of themselves, came home on a Sunday with a cold coming on. She made them tea the way she made everything — too strong, too sweet, administered like medicine whether you wanted it or not — and while the kettle worked itself up to its opinion, she sang.

Quietly. Half of it. The part in her own language, the stepping stones, the crack in the middle phrase arriving right on schedule like a train.

And when she finished, she looked at Oscar across the small kitchen and said, "Do you have it?"

"In my body," Oscar said. "Yes."

"Good." She poured the tea. "Don't write it down. Promise me."

And here is the thing Oscar has carried like a stone in the shoe ever since: they promised. Out loud. Standing in her kitchen with the tea going cold in their hands, they promised a dying woman they would let the song die with her rather than commit the crime of writing it down — and they meant it, and they hated meaning it, and some traitorous clerk in their chest was already drafting the notation system, improved, in pencil, in a notebook they would buy the following spring and never fill.

She died in March. The song survived in exactly one body, minus the parts grief ate, which turned out to be considerable. There were phrases Oscar lost within the year — whole verses gone soft at the edges, like handwriting left out in rain. They began keeping a private ledger, years later, of what remained, and the ledger's earliest entries were written in a fury of conservation, and every entry ended the same way: *do not write down the song itself. Only count what's missing.*

A rule curdling into a superstition. A promise curdling into a prison. Their mother had meant to build them a vault, and instead she had built them a cell whose door only locked from the inside.

* * *

So when the radio played it — years later, on the night bus, credited to a found tape, a tape that should not exist, a tape that could only exist if somebody had broken the rule, somebody with a recorder and no reverence, somebody who had taken the one unstealable thing and stolen it anyway — Oscar did not feel only grief.

They felt robbed a second time. And beneath the robbery, arriving a half-second behind it like thunder after lightning, they felt something they were ashamed of:

Relief.

Because a recording meant the rule was broken. And if the rule was broken, then the prison door stood open, and whatever came next — whoever had taped it, whoever was playing it, whatever reckoning waited at the end of that broadcast like a debt waiting at the end of a route — Oscar would not be the one who had betrayed their mother.

Someone else had done the unforgivable thing first.

Oscar sat on the night bus with their forehead against the cold glass, watching the old district slide past, and hated themselves for the relief, and followed it anyway, all the way to the last stop, all the way to the chain-link fence, all the way to the glasshouse where the wind chimes made of tuning forks were ringing a note that matched, almost exactly, the crack in the middle phrase.

And inside, waiting among the ferns and the seized tapes, was a woman from an old photograph — and beside her, pinned to a corkboard like evidence, a photograph Oscar recognized down to the crooked frame: four children outside the glasshouse, faces half-turned away.

One of the faces belonged to a boy Oscar had spent ten years not forgiving.

His name, they were about to learn, was Teo.
