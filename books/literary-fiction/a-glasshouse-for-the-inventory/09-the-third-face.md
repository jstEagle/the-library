## 9. The Third Face

The photograph stayed on my kitchen table for three days, the way a difficult letter does — read, reread, and still not answered.

I had turned it over so many times that the tape residue on the back had begun to collect lint like a small gray animal. Four children outside the glasshouse nursery, summer light blowing out the details of everything except their faces, which were all half-turned away, as if someone behind them had just said something worth hearing but not worth facing.

Marisol I knew now. The girl with the braid and the scowl she'd apparently been practicing since age nine. Myself I knew too well — the smallest one, standing slightly apart, hands folded like a child at a funeral they didn't understand. And the fourth child, the tall boy on the end with his chin lifted and his hand already halfway into his pocket, reaching for something — a notebook, I'd always assumed, because even then he was writing things down.

Teo.

The name arrived in my mouth before I decided to think it, the way a word arrives when you've been translating all day and your own language starts leaking through. Teo Ferrán. Who used to keep minutes for our childhood parliament of four, who wrote down what everyone said because he believed, even at eleven, that things not written down could be taken away from you. Who was wrong about that, as it turned out, in the most painful possible way. Some things not written down get taken anyway. My mother's song had no address and it was stolen regardless.

I hadn't seen Teo since the funeral. He had sent a card — cream stock, embossed initials, a sentence of condolence so carefully constructed it could have survived cross-examination. *Your mother was a woman of rare conviction.* Not: I loved her singing. Not: I remember the glasshouse. A woman of rare conviction, as though she were a case law citation.

He was a lawyer now. Of course he was. The boy who took minutes became the man who drafted terms.

* * *

It was Marisol who told me, in her way, which is to say by accident while meaning to wound.

I had come back to the glasshouse on a Thursday, carrying a thermos of coffee I mostly brought as an offering, the way you bring flowers to a hospital room of someone who doesn't want you there. Marisol was cataloguing tapes at the potting bench, which served as her desk, her shelves sagging under labeled cassettes in shoeboxes and biscuit tins. She didn't look up when I came in. She never looked up immediately; making me wait was part of the architecture of our reconciliation, such as it was.

"You heard," she said, after a while.

"Heard what?"

"Sanch is dying." She said it flatly, the way you report weather. "Pancreatic. Months, maybe weeks. And now he wants to *return* everything." She made the word sound like something you'd scrape off your shoe. "On his own terms."

"That's—" I started.

"Wait." She held up a hand, still not looking at me. "You'll want to sit down for who's brokering it."

"Who?"

She turned then, finally, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't parse — pity and accusation sharing a single face, which was very much her specialty.

"Teo Ferrán," she said. "Your friend from the photograph. He's Sanch's lawyer. Has been for years. Estate work." She let the word *estate* sit in the humid air between us, among the ferns swallowing the broken benches. "He's drafting the donation agreement right now. University gets the archive, Sanch gets his name on the plaque, and we get to be grateful."

* * *

I want to say I was surprised. I wasn't, and the not-surprise was its own kind of grief.

Here is what I remembered about Teo, from before: he was the one who understood systems. When we were children, he was the one who explained why the nursery owner locked the greenhouse at night, why the buses ran on the schedule they ran, why Mrs. Adeyemi's fruit stand kept getting moved further from the corner each season until it disappeared entirely. He explained these things without malice, the way a weatherman explains a storm, and we were grateful, because explanation felt like power. It was only years later that I understood the difference between explaining a system and questioning it — and that Teo had chosen the first with the whole heart of a boy who wanted, more than anything, to be on speaking terms with the people who ran things.

When the tapes were taken — when Aurelio Sanch came through the neighborhood with his reel-to-reel and his contracts and his smile, recording everyone's uncles and aunties and mothers, keeping the masters, erasing the names — Teo was fourteen and already talking about law school. I remember him saying, *at least the recordings will exist,* and Marisol throwing a mango at his head, and missing, and both of them pretending it hadn't happened.

At least the recordings will exist. That was the seed of everything, planted early. You can grow a whole career from a sentence like that. A whole defense.

* * *

He called me on Friday. Of course he did. Teo always knew when the minutes needed taking.

"Oscar." His voice was exactly as I remembered it and completely different — the same careful diction, but sanded down now, professionalized, every rough edge planed off until what remained was smooth and characterless, like a conference table. "I hear you've been out to the glasshouse."

"News travels."

"Marisol called the firm asking whether a demolition notice could be contested on heritage grounds." A pause, and I could hear him choosing his next words with tongs. "I recused myself from anything touching the neighborhood, obviously. But I hear things."

"Obviously," I said.

Another pause. I pictured him in an office with good lighting, a framed degree, probably a plant he watered on schedule. I pictured him younger, outside the glasshouse, hand halfway into his pocket.

"I'd like to see you," he said. "There are things you should know. About Sanch. About what he's proposing. Before Marisol frames it for you entirely her way."

"And which way would that be?"

"The accurate way," he said, and then, catching himself, or hearing how it sounded: "Oscar. I'm not the enemy here."

"No," I agreed. "You're just being paid by him."

Silence. Then, quietly, with something that might have been the old Teo surfacing for air: "Saturday. The diner on Calloway Street. Please. Ten years is long enough to owe someone an explanation, and I've owed you this one since the funeral."

I should have said no. I want to be honest in this account, wherever honesty leads, and honesty says part of me wanted to say yes from the moment Marisol said his name — wanted it with the same dread-dressed-as-curiosity that had made me dig through the shoebox in the first place. There is a particular hunger for the explanations of people who have hurt you. It masquerades as closure. It is actually just appetite.

"Saturday," I said. "But I'm buying my own coffee."

"I'd expect nothing less," said Teo, and hung up before I could decide whether that was warmth or technique.

* * *

That night I did what I always do with words I can't say yet: I rehearsed them.

This is my oldest habit, older than the courtroom, older than the softening. As a child I used to stand in front of the bathroom mirror practicing sentences for my mother — *Mama, the tape recorder, where did it go, why won't you sing it again* — and lose my nerve by morning, so that the mirror got the best of me and everyone else got the edited version. Professionalism, I call it now. It has a better ring than cowardice, and I chose the term myself, which makes it almost true.

So I stood at my kitchen window with the photograph propped against the sugar bowl and I rehearsed.

*I know what you did, Teo. You watched the tapes leave and you wrote down the license plate number and you did nothing with it, because even then you were saving things for the right moment, and the right moment never comes, it only gets invoiced.*

Too much. Too cruel. He was fourteen.

*I understand, Teo. Systems are heavy and you were small.*

Too kind. He is forty-three and he drafts agreements that put dead men's names on living music.

In the end I settled on nothing, which is my native language after all. I washed the coffee cups. I put the photograph face-down, then immediately face-up again, because hiding it felt like lying, and I am trying — God help me, I am trying — to stop lying in the small ways first.

* * *

Saturday came gray and drizzling, the kind of weather that makes the city look like a rough draft of itself. The diner on Calloway Street had been there forever, all chrome and laminate and a pie case lit like an altar. I arrived early, which was also old habit — translators arrive early; you can't soften what you haven't heard.

Teo was already there anyway, because of course he was, sitting in a corner booth with his coat folded beside him and two coffees already ordered, because of course there were. He stood when he saw me, an automatic courtesy that looked strange on him, like a suit borrowed from a bigger man.

"Oscar." He gestured at the second cup. "I took a chance."

"You took several chances over the years," I said, sliding in across from him. "One more cup of coffee barely registers."

He smiled — briefly, wincingly, a man accepting a fair hit — and sat. Up close he was grayer than the photograph suggested, thinner, with new glasses that gave him a permanently startled expression, as if the world kept presenting him with documents he hadn't reviewed.

"You look well," he said.

"I look tired. Let's not translate each other, Teo. We're both fluent."

That landed. I watched it land — a small flinch, quickly governed. And underneath the flinch, something else, something that surprised me: relief. The relief of a man who has been speaking diplomatically for so long that someone addressing him directly feels like a massage.

"All right," he said. "Straight, then." He wrapped his hands around his coffee cup but didn't drink, a lawyer's gesture, buying time with props. "Aurelio Sanch is dying. He knows it. And he has come to me — has *been* with me, on and off, for six years — because he wants to settle his estate in a way he considers honorable."

"Honorable," I repeated.

"His word. I want you to hear his word, because you'll be hearing a lot of it soon, and I'd rather you heard it from me first, with context, than from Marisol, with none."

"Context," I said. "Meaning excuses."

"Meaning history." He leaned forward. "Oscar, do you know what Sanch actually did? Not the story Marisol tells — the actual mechanics of it?"

"He recorded the neighborhood. He kept the masters. He erased the names."

"He recorded the neighborhood," Teo agreed, "because nobody else was. Because the radio stations wouldn't touch it, the labels wouldn't sign it, and within a generation every one of those musicians would have been dead and their music with them, unwritten, unsung, gone. Your mother among them." He said it gently, which made it worse. "He kept the masters because that's what producers did — that's what the entire industry was built on, ownership, however ugly. And he erased the names because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because the contracts were bad and he knew it, and the names were evidence."

The pie case hummed. Somewhere behind the counter a radio played something with a steel drum in it, faintly, like a memory leaking through a wall.

"So his defense," I said slowly, "is that he stole everything so beautifully that theft was conservation."

"His defense is that the alternative was oblivion."

"There was another alternative." My voice came out lower than I intended. "It was called asking. It was called credit. It was called paying people. My mother sang that song once, Teo. Once, and told no one to write it down, and he put a microphone in the room anyway, and now it plays on late-night radio credited to *found tape*. Found. Like a wallet. Like a glove."

Teo looked down at his coffee. For a moment — just a moment — the lawyer's surface cracked and I saw the boy from the photograph, the one with his hand halfway into his pocket, holding whatever he'd written down, unable to either use it or burn it.

"I know," he said. "I know that's what happened. I've known for thirty years, Oscar. Why do you think I took his business?" He looked up, and his eyes behind the startled glasses were wet at the rims, furious at themselves. "You don't get inside unless someone lets you in. I let him believe I was his kind of person. Six years of estate planning, of listening, of being useful. And now he's dying, and frightened, and he wants to give it all back with his name attached, and I am the only person alive who knows where every document is buried — including the ones that prove the names he erased."

My heart did something complicated. "Then give them to Marisol."

"It's not that simple—"

"It never is. That's the whole profession, isn't it? Making it not simple."

"*Oscar.*" He said my name like a ruling. Then, quieter: "There's a proposal. A real one, on paper, ready to sign. The archive goes to the university — climate-controlled, funded, permanent, everything Marisol's shoeboxes will never be. Every musician named in the liner materials. Public access. And Sanch's name on the founding gift, because that's the price, and it's a price I can't talk him below, because his name is the last thing he has and he knows it."

"And if we don't sign?"

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the rain thickened against the window, and the 47 bus went by — rerouted already, its detour signs blooming along the old route like weeds — carrying strangers past the glasshouse instead of neighbors.

"If you don't sign," Teo said, "the collection goes to his nephew, who has expressed interest in selling it to a private buyer in Europe. Piece by piece. No names, no access, no archive. Just inventory." He spread his hands, and I noticed they were trembling slightly, and that he was letting me see it, which from Teo was a confession. "That's the choice. That's the honest shape of it. I hate it. I've hated it for six years. But I need someone from the photograph in that room when it's signed, and Marisol won't come, and your mother isn't here, so it has to be you."

The radio behind the counter changed songs. For one disorienting second I thought — hoped, feared — it might be hers. It wasn't. It was advertising, bright and meaningless, selling something neither of us would ever buy.

"Saturday," I said. "You said Saturday. This is Saturday. So ask me."

Teo straightened his glasses, reached into his briefcase — the same motion, I realized, the same reach into the pocket, thirty years uninterrupted — and drew out a folder, square and heavy, tied with string.

"Sign the release," he said, "and everyone gets the music back. Legally. Forever. Refuse, and it goes to Europe in boxes." He slid the folder across the laminate between us, into the space where my coffee cup made a small wet ring, like a signature neither of us had authorized yet. "Read it first. But Oscar — read it knowing I wrote it, and knowing what it cost me to write it this badly."

I put my hand on the folder. It was warm from sitting near him, which seemed, somehow, the most obscene thing about it.
