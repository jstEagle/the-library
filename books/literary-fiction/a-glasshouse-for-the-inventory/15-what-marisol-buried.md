## 15. What Marisol Buried

The radio station kept its transmitter on a hill above the old district, and for eleven years Marisol had driven past it every Sunday on her way to her mother's grave, thinking: *that's where the voice comes from.* Not God's voice. Not the city's. The small, warm, insomniac voice of a man named Dev who played whatever he wanted between midnight and four because nobody important was listening.

She had chosen him the way you choose a lock to pick — carefully, after long study. She'd listened to his show for three months from the glasshouse office, headphones on, cataloguing his habits. He never announced track titles twice. He read listener letters aloud if they were strange enough. He had a soft spot for things that sounded like they'd been rescued from a fire. And once, memorably, he'd said into the microphone, at 2:40 in the morning, "If anybody out there has a tape that shouldn't exist, this is your hour."

That was the sentence she'd built her whole crime on.

* * *

Now, standing in the glasshouse among the ferns with Oscar Villalobos looking at her like she'd grown a second head, Marisol found that the confession she had rehearsed for months — in the shower, on the bus, over her mother's grave — had abandoned her entirely. What came out instead was:

"I bribed a DJ with flan."

Oscar blinked. "You what?"

"Dev. The overnight host. KXLR, 101.3." She said it fast, the way you pull off a bandage. "I made flan every Sunday for six weeks and left it in the station's mail slot with notes. Unsigned. The notes got longer until finally one of them said, *Play this. Don't ask where it came from. Just play it once, late, and say it's from a found tape.*" She gestured vaguely at the shelf behind her, at the reel-to-reel machine with its worn knobs. "I dubbed it off Exhibit One. Off your mother's song. I've had the equipment to do it for years. I just — hadn't had the nerve."

The greenhouse held its breath around them. Somewhere overhead the tuning-fork chimes stirred without wind, which Marisol privately believed was the building's way of clearing its throat before someone said something true.

"You put her on the radio," Oscar said slowly. Each word arrived separately, like luggage on a carousel. "Without asking anyone. Without asking *me*."

"Yes."

"She forbade recordings. You know that. Everyone in this neighborhood knew that. It was practically scripture."

"I know what it was," Marisol said. Her voice came out harder than she intended, so she let it stay hard; softness would have been a lie right then. "I sat on her kitchen floor when we were nine and she told us both — you and me and Teo, all four of us crammed in there — *some songs are for bodies, not boxes.* I was there. I heard the rule from the source."

"Then how could you—"

"Because rules are for the living, Oscar, and your mother has been dead nine years, and the man who stole her voice was going to die with it in his basement, and *somebody had to do something.*" The words came up out of her like water finding a crack. She hadn't meant to say any of this yet. She'd planned to ease into it, offer him tea first, maybe the good chair. But here they were, and the truth had apparently decided it was done waiting. "I couldn't finish it alone. That's the whole ugly confession. Eleven years of building this archive, eleven years of listening parties and name-restoring and writing letters to lawyers who wrote back in circles, and I could not get the photograph's children through that door. Teo sold himself to the other side. You vanished into the courts and turned into a suit with a dictionary. And I stood in here every week talking to ferns about justice."

A pause. A drip somewhere in the irrigation line that no longer irrigated anything.

"So I buried a copy of the lullaby in the one place I knew it would be dug up," Marisol said. "Late-night radio. Bait. For you specifically. Because you were the one I couldn't stop thinking about. Because you were the one whose face in that photograph is turned away, and I needed to know why."

* * *

Here is what Marisol had not told anyone, not even the ferns:

The night she mailed the first tape, she had sat in her car outside the station for two hours with the engine off, holding the package on her knees like it was a bomb. Which, in a way, it was. Inside was her mother's best friend's voice. Inside was Doña Inés singing the harvest song that Sanch had pressed onto acetate in 1979 and filed under *miscellaneous*. Inside were forty years of people who had trusted a neighborhood producer with their Sunday afternoons and gotten silence back.

And underneath all of it, third on the cassette, was the lullaby.

She had recorded it herself, years ago, illegally, secretly, with a handheld recorder hidden in her coat pocket, the single time she'd heard it as an adult. Not from Oscar's mother directly — by then it was too late for that — but from a neighbor, Mrs. Adeyemi, who had heard it once through an open window in 1988 and carried it in her body ever since, the way the rule demanded, and sang it to her own grandchildren with the words slightly wrong and the melody exactly right. Marisol had asked her to sing it into the recorder. Mrs. Adeyemi had refused three times. The fourth time, she'd said, "If it lives only in me, it dies when I do. That woman's rule was grief talking. Grief makes bad law." Then she sang, and her voice shook, and the recording caught a bus passing outside, and Marisol had listened to it so many times she could hear the bus in her sleep.

So technically, the version on the radio wasn't even the original. It was a copy of a memory of a copy. When Oscar's mother had burned the written score, she'd imagined she was protecting the song. What she'd actually done was set it adrift, and it had survived the way songs survive — badly, imperfectly, in other people's mouths, wearing other people's accents.

Marisol understood this now. She had not understood it at two in the morning, six weeks into the flan campaign, when she'd finally handed Dev the cassette and driven home shaking.

* * *

"You used her," Oscar said. Not shouting. Worse than shouting — quiet, precise, the courtroom voice, the one Marisol had spent ten years imagining across a witness stand. "You used my mother's voice as bait. Like a lure. Like chum."

"I did."

"And you didn't tell me. I sat in this room. I drank your terrible coffee. I translated testimony for your listening party — real testimony, from real people — and the entire time, the reason I was standing there was a trick you played."

"The reason you were standing there," Marisol said, "is that you heard your dead mother singing on the radio and got on a bus."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly the point. Oscar. Look at me." She waited until he did. His eyes were wet and furious and thirty years old all at once, and she thought, absurdly, *you still have the same cowlick.* "I am not going to pretend this was clean. It wasn't. I broke the one law your family had. I took something sacred and I weaponized it, and if you want to hate me for it, hate me properly, all the way, because I will tell you something worse: I'd do it again tomorrow. I'd do it tonight. I lay awake for a month afterward wondering if the song would come back to haunt us, if breaking the rule would break the song, and you know what happened? Nothing. It played at 1:17 a.m. to maybe four hundred insomniacs and one translator on the 47 bus, and the song didn't die. It *arrived.* Your mother's rule said some things must live in bodies or not at all. Well." She spread her hands. "It lived in yours. It got you here. The rule worked. I just had to break it to prove it."

The chimes stirred again. Down at the far end of the glasshouse, a pane of glass that had been hanging crooked since the nineties chose that moment to slide loose and shatter against a bench, and they both jumped, and then — Marisol saw it happen, watched it arrive on his face like weather — Oscar laughed. One broken syllable of a laugh, quickly strangled, but real.

"The house agrees with you," he said bitterly. "That's the worst part."

* * *

They ended up sitting on the old potting benches because there were no chairs, drinking the terrible coffee out of mismatched mugs, and Marisol told him the rest. All of it. The flan schedule (Sundays, caramel on the bottom, because Dev had mentioned once, apropos of nothing, that his grandmother made it that way). The note campaign (fourteen notes, escalating from *play this* to a full paragraph of fake provenance involving a fictional estate sale). The night she'd almost confessed to the whole listening party and lost her nerve when Mrs. Ferreira brought her accordion. The morning she'd heard, through the static of the office radio, Dev read a letter on air: *"To whoever found that tape — I think I know that song. I think it's my mother's. Please, if you're out there—"*

"He read it at 3 a.m.," Marisol said. "I was here alone. I turned the radio off and I sat on that floor and I cried like an idiot, because it worked, it actually worked, and working felt exactly like sinning. That's the thing nobody tells you about doing the wrong thing for the right reason. It doesn't feel righteous. It feels like theft with good intentions."

Oscar was quiet for a long time, turning the mug in their hands. Marisol let them turn it. She had learned, running this archive, that silence was also evidence, and that you didn't rush it.

Finally Oscar said: "Mrs. Adeyemi's version. On the tape. Was it wrong?"

"Wrong how?"

"The words. My mother's words. Did she get them right?"

Marisol hesitated. "Some of them. The middle verse was different. She sang 'sleep, the river knows the way' where I think—" She stopped, because Oscar had closed their eyes.

"'Sleep, the moon will hold the door,'" Oscar said quietly. "That's the verse. My mother's verse."

"The river knows the way," Marisol repeated. "That's what's on the tape. That's what went out on the radio. That's what pulled you home." She felt her throat tighten. "I'm sorry. I broadcast a translation of a translation. I sent you a copy of a ghost."

But Oscar was shaking their head, slowly, and when they opened their eyes they were smiling, and it was such a strange, unguarded smile that Marisol nearly looked away from it.

"No," Oscar said. "Don't you see? She changed it. Mrs. Adeyemi changed it, carrying it all those years, and it *still worked.* It still recognized itself. The song isn't the words, it's — it's whatever survives the carrying." They laughed again, that same broken syllable, less strangled this time. "My mother burned the score because she thought the song would die if it touched paper. And it didn't die. It got on a bus. It got *me* on a bus. You didn't kill the rule, Marisol. You tested it. And it passed."

Marisol stared at him. Ten years of rehearsing this conversation, ten years of drafting confessions and burning them, and in none of the drafts had the verdict been absolution delivered via musicology.

"You're not angry," she said suspiciously.

"Oh, I'm angry." Oscar set down the mug. "I'm furious. I'll be furious for weeks. You manipulated me with baked goods and broadcast piracy. There will be a formal complaint. Possibly in writing." A pause. "But Marisol — I have spent ten years translating other people's pain into softer versions of itself because I was too much of a coward to feel my own. And the thing that finally cracked me open wasn't courage. It was a stranger's voice on a late-night radio station, singing my mother's song with the wrong words." They looked at her straight on. "Thank you. Thank you for burying it where I'd dig."

* * *

Later — much later, after the coffee was gone and the light through the fogged panes had gone amber and then gray — Marisol walked Oscar out to the chain-link gate, and there she stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in her chest all day like a stone in a shoe.

"There's a reason I picked you," she said. "Out of the three of you. Teo I'd given up on. But you — I picked you because of the photograph."

Oscar went still.

"In that picture, all our faces are half-turned away. Except mine. I'm looking straight at the camera like a little sergeant. And I used to think that meant something — that I was the honest one, the one who faced things. But I've had ten years to look at that photo, Oscar, and here's what I see now: you're not turned away from the camera. You're turned away from *the glasshouse door.* Where the tapes are being carried out. Where Sanch's van is parked." She said it gently, as gently as she knew how, which was not very. "You were nine years old. There was nothing you could have done. I know that. I've always known that. But I needed you to come back and *say* it, in your own mouth, in your own language, at the reckoning — because I can't speak for you. Nobody can. That's the one thing this archive can't do."

Oscar looked at the ground for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was rough.

"The hearing. The one you're planning in here. You want me to translate. Fully. Both directions. No softening."

"I want you to speak," Marisol said. "Translation optional."

They stood there in the doorway of the glasshouse, two halves of an old photograph finally facing the same direction, and Marisol was about to say something else — something about Thursday, about the tuning forks, about whether he wanted flan, she had a batch going — when Oscar's phone buzzed.

They glanced at it. And Marisol watched the color leave their face the way color leaves the sky before weather.

"What?" she said. "What is it?"

Oscar turned the screen toward her. A message, short and terrible:

**Teo. Diner on Delmore, 8pm. Bringing Sanch's lawyer. He wants to talk terms.**

Marisol read it twice. Then she laughed — a short, hard, unholy laugh that scattered a pigeon off the roof.

"Terms," she said. "He steals forty years of our voices and sends a lawyer to discuss *terms.*" She pushed the gate open. "Get your coat, translator. Whatever they're selling, we're about to find out the price."

Behind them, in the darkening glasshouse, the tuning forks chimed once, all together, like a jury being sworn in.
