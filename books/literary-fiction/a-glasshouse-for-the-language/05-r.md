## 5. R.

The county records office smelled of dust and institutional tea, a combination that suggested the two had been in negotiation for decades and neither side had won. Felix signed the visitor's book at nine-fifteen on a Tuesday morning, printing their name in careful capitals as though the clerk might otherwise suspect them of being someone interesting.

"Family history?" the clerk asked, in the tone of a man who had heard every possible answer to that question and believed none of them.

"Something like that," Felix said. "I'm looking for a person. R. Thackeray."

The clerk's eyebrows performed a small, noncommittal dance. "Initials only?"

"Initials only. It's a long story."

"It usually is." He waved Felix through to the reading room with the weary generosity of someone who had long ago stopped charging admission to other people's mysteries.

The reading room was long and low-ceilinged, lit by windows set high enough that the light arrived already tired. Felix had been coming here for four days now, working methodically through the parish registers of the villages within reach of the estate — baptisms, marriages, burials, the whole bureaucratic biography of a family that had, as far as Felix could tell, spent three generations trying to be born less often than they actually were.

The notebook came with them everywhere now. It had started as an inventory of silences, a catalogue of everything their mother never said, and it had become something else — a case file, almost, though Felix resisted the word. *Case* implied a solution. *File* implied an ending. What Felix had was a growing conviction that the woman they had buried three weeks ago had been, in some essential way, a fiction maintained by everyone who loved her, and that Felix had been one of its most devoted custodians.

*Item:* she never spoke of her childhood. Not once, in forty-one years, did she volunteer a single memory that predated her wedding.

*Item:* there were no photographs of her before the age of twenty.

Felix had discovered that second item only after the letters. They had gone back through the albums — the heavy leather ones on the study shelf, the ones their mother had dusted but never opened — and counted. Every photograph of their mother as a child, every one, had been removed. Not lost. Removed. The albums were full of cousins and aunts and uncles and landscapes and one long, inexplicable sequence of dogs, and where a young Margot Thackeray should have been there were only the pale rectangles of absence, the ghost-outlines of pictures peeled away with such care that the backing paper had survived intact, like skin after a bandage comes off.

Someone had wanted her gone from the record. Or — and this was the thought that kept Felix up at night — someone had wanted the record to show that she had never been there at all.

* * *

The breakthrough, when it came, was not dramatic. Felix had imagined, in the small hours, a moment of revelation — a name surfacing from a register like a body from a lake, trumpets, etc. Instead it was a Tuesday, and a cup of vending-machine coffee, and a volume of baptismal registers for the parish of Little Wexcombe, 1946 to 1958, and a page so ordinary it took Felix a full minute to understand why their hands had stopped moving.

*Thackeray, Ruth Eleanor, born 14th March 1947, daughter of Edmund Charles Thackeray and Winifred Anne, née Pargeter. Sponsors: the parents. Baptised privately at home, 2nd April, on account of the child's frailty.*

Felix read it three times.

Ruth. *Ruth Eleanor.*

The letters — all forty-odd of them, still in their biscuit tin, still sealed, because Felix had read only the first and then found themselves unable to continue, as though the rest were a meal too rich to eat standing up — the letters were addressed to *R.* And here was an R, born two years after Felix's mother, baptised at home because she was too frail to bring to church, daughter of the same Edmund Thackeray whose portrait still hung — hung, past tense, Felix reminded themself; the house was coming down — in the front hall.

But a name in a register proved existence, not relationship. Felix wanted more. Felix wanted the whole architecture of the lie.

They asked the clerk for the confirmation registers, and the marriage registers, and then, on an impulse that felt less like research and more like prayer, the burial registers for the Thackeray family plot.

Ruth Eleanor Thackeray was not buried there. That was the first thing. The plot held Edmund (1981), Winifred (1974), and a scatter of great-aunts, and — Felix checked twice, feeling the clerk's patience thinning — no Ruth.

The second thing was in the marriage register for 1971. *Ruth E. Thackeray, of this parish,* married one Dennis Aldous, farm mechanic, in a service witnessed by two people whose names meant nothing to Felix. She had married. She had lived. She had, presumably, gone on living somewhere, under a name no one in Felix's family had spoken aloud in fifty years.

Felix sat back and let that settle. An aunt. A living, breathing, possibly-still-alive aunt named Ruth, erased so thoroughly that Felix had reached adulthood believing their mother was an only child — believing it so completely that when the solicitor had read out the list of next of kin at the funeral arrangements, and Felix had asked, half-joking, "No siblings?", their mother's old friend Jean had said, "No, love, she was all on her own," with such certainty that Felix had never thought to doubt it.

All on her own. As though the aloneness were a fact of nature and not a construction project.

* * *

The address book was Felix's own idea, and they were absurdly proud of it.

Their mother had kept an address book — a small, fat, maroon thing with a broken spine, stuffed into the drawer of the telephone table for as long as Felix could remember, updated with a diligence that bordered on devotion. Every Christmas card recipient, every doctor, every plumber, every friend of every decade, entered in a handwriting that grew smaller and more angular as the years went on. Felix had catalogued it in the first week of packing, one entry among hundreds, and had thought nothing of it.

Now they took it out of the box marked *STUDY — MISC* and sat on the floor of the emptied front room with it, turning pages.

There was no Ruth. Of course there was no Ruth. But address books are honest in ways their owners are not, because they are revised rather than rewritten. Felix went through it the way an archivist goes through a palimpsest, looking not for what was written but for what had been.

On the page for *R*, between *Radcliffe, Dr.* and *Reid, Margaret (bridge club)*, there was an entry that had been inked out. Not crossed out — inked out, thoroughly, the pen pressed so hard the paper had embossed, and then the whole thing gone over again in a second layer of ink, and then, years later by the colour of it, a third. Whoever had done it had wanted that entry not merely deleted but *unrecoverable*, and had returned to the project at least twice, as though the name kept trying to grow back.

Felix held the page up to the window. The light came through the paper and, for a moment, the pressure-marks of the original penstrokes stood out in relief, the way footprints show in a lawn at evening.

Felix could not read it. But they could *feel* it — four letters, maybe five, and beneath them the ghost of an address, longer, in a different hand.

They photographed it anyway, with the phone's torch raking across the page at an angle, and got nothing but a beautiful abstract image of embossed paper, which they stared at on the train home with the particular frustration of a person who has found a locked door and no key.

* * *

It was Jean who cracked it, though not on purpose.

Felix had gone to see her on the Thursday — Jean Pemberton, their mother's oldest friend, seventy-six and sharp as a tack wrapped in a cardigan, who had helped at the funeral and cried at exactly the right moments and volunteered nothing. Felix brought a lemon cake and an innocent expression, and for an hour they talked about nothing: the flowers, the vicar, the scandal of the new parking charges at the garden centre.

It was only when Felix stood to leave that Jean, fussing with the cake tin at the counter, said it.

"You know, she'd have hated all this. The house coming down. She never liked change, our Margot." A pause, a small laugh. "Though I suppose she got used to it early. Her family moved her about enough when she was young."

Felix turned around very slowly. "Moved her about?"

"Oh — well." Jean set down the tin. Something moved behind her face, quick as a fish. "After her mother died, you know. She went to stay with an aunt for a while, down the coast. I lost touch that year — we were sixteen, these things happen — and when she came back she was different. Quieter. I always thought—" Jean stopped. Recalibrated. Felix watched her do it, watched the whole sentence being dismantled behind her eyes. "I'm rambling. You'll want to be off."

"Jean." Felix sat back down. "Which aunt?"

"It doesn't matter, love."

"It matters to me."

"It was a long time ago."

"Jean, please. My mother left forty letters to someone called R. behind a wardrobe, and I have just learned that she had a sister named Ruth who was never mentioned in my entire life, and I am trying very hard not to feel like I was raised inside a lie, so if you know anything at all — anything — I would genuinely rather hear it now than spend another month finding it out in pieces."

The room went very quiet. On the mantelpiece, a carriage clock ticked with the smugness of a device that had seen this kind of thing before.

Jean looked at Felix for a long moment, and Felix watched the loyalty drain out of her face and something older and sadder take its place.

"Oh, love," she said. "Sit down properly, then. And don't blame her. Whatever you find — don't blame her. She was the kindest coward I ever knew."

* * *

Here is what Jean knew, in the end, delivered in the halting way of a person dismantling a wall she had personally helped to build:

Margot and Ruth had been close. Close the way sisters are close when there are only two of them and the house is cold. When their mother died in 1974, Margot was twenty-six and already engaged; Ruth was twenty-seven and still at home, keeping house for their father, who by every account had been a tyrant with a beautiful voice and a genius for grievance.

"And then something happened," Jean said. "Margot wouldn't ever say what. She came back from that house one last time and she was — grey. Like something had been switched off. And she said to me, the only time she ever said anything: *'Jean, if I ever seem to forget I had a sister, let me. It's kinder to everyone.'* And I said, what on earth do you mean, and she said, *'Just let me,'* and I did." Jean's hands were folded very tightly in her lap. "I've regretted it for fifty years, if that helps you at all. I let her."

"Where did Ruth go?"

"Down the coast. There was a husband — Dennis, a lovely man, dead now I think. She had the aunt's old cottage, or part of it. Margot sent things, sometimes. Not letters — she'd have died rather than write a letter — but parcels. A card at Christmas, unsigned. I posted one for her once, in 1990-something, and she watched me write the address and then made me burn the paper I'd copied it onto." Jean smiled, and it was a terrible smile. "She used to say Ruth was the only honest Thackeray. I never knew what she meant until now, and I still don't, not really. But you do, don't you. You know something."

"I know there are letters," Felix said.

Jean closed her eyes. "Oh, Margot," she said, to no one, to the ceiling, to a woman three weeks dead. "You absolute fool. You brave, stupid, wonderful fool."

* * *

Felix walked home through the village in the long gold light of early evening, and the anger arrived not all at once but in instalments, like furniture being delivered.

First, at their grandfather — the dead tyrant at the centre of the letters, the man whose portrait had watched over every Christmas of Felix's childhood, whom the family still referred to as *Edmund* in the hushed tone reserved for saints and the recently deceased. Whatever he had done, he had done it to both his daughters, and he had done it so thoroughly that one of them had spent fifty years pretending the other had never existed.

Then, more uncomfortably, at their mother. Because erasure is not passive. Erasure is work. Someone had inked out that address book entry — three times, with escalating violence. Someone had peeled the photographs from the albums. Someone had answered Felix's childhood questions — *Mum, who's that in the picture?* — with the smooth deflections of a person who had rehearsed. Their mother had not merely endured the silence. She had *maintained* it, dusted it, kept it in good repair, the way she kept everything in that house in good repair and nothing in it alive.

And then, last and worst, at themself. Because Felix had lived in that house for eighteen years and never once wondered why there were no pictures of their mother as a girl. Never once asked why the family albums read like a novel with chapters torn out. Children believe the version of the world they are handed; that is what childhood is. But Felix was thirty-four years old, and they had needed a biscuit tin behind a wardrobe to make them ask a question that had been sitting in the hallway their whole life, wearing a hat, waiting.

Felix stopped at the gate of the estate — the estate, soon to be a development called *Thackeray Meadows*, which Marcus had chosen, Marcus who understood that nothing sells like a family name with the family scraped off it — and stood looking up at the house. The windows were dark. The glasshouse loomed beyond it, a black ribcage against the last of the light.

*Someone did this,* Felix thought. *Someone sat down with a pen and made a person disappear, and then made the disappearance permanent, and then handed the finished product to her children and called it a family.*

The anger was still there — Felix could feel it in their teeth, in their hands — but it had changed shape. Anger, Felix was learning, is a fuel that burns fast and leaves nothing. What was left after it burned was something slower and more durable. Not quite resolve. Closer to direction. The feeling of a compass needle stopping its trembling and settling, finally, on north.

They went inside, past the portrait of Edmund — *hello, you* — and up the stairs to the bedroom where the wardrobe still stood against the wall, guarding its empty cavity. The biscuit tin sat on the bed where Felix had left it, forty sealed letters in their careful stacks, sorted by year.

Felix sat down beside it and opened the notebook to a fresh page. At the top they wrote: *RUTH ELEANOR THACKERAY, b. 14 March 1947. Married Dennis Aldous, 1971. Last known address: coastal — Jean knows the village. She stopped saying it before I could ask, but she knows.*

Then, underneath, because the notebook had begun as an inventory of unsaid things and Felix saw no reason to change its habits now, they wrote:

*Item:* I had an aunt, and I did not know it, and someone decided I should not, and the someone was the woman whose funeral I attended, and I have decided that deciding is not the same as being right.

They looked at that sentence for a while. It was not elegant. It was true, which was better.

Then Felix turned back to the tin, and took out the earliest letter — the one dated 1968, the one they had read standing in the dust, the one that began *Dear R., you'll laugh when you hear where I'm writing from* — and beneath the return address, in their mother's young, round, unguarded hand, there it was. It had been there the whole time, on every envelope, fifty years of a secret sitting in plain sight.

*Aldous Cottage, Gullmere-on-Sea.*

Felix reached for the car keys.

The house could wait thirty days. The truth had waited fifty years, and it was four hours' drive away, and it was, as far as anyone knew, still alive.
