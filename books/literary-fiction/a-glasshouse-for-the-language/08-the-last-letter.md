## 8. The Last Letter

The biscuit tin held forty-one letters. I know because I counted them that first night, and then counted them again the next morning as if the number might have changed overnight, as if grief were the kind of thing that could be audited. Forty-one sealed envelopes, each addressed in my mother's hand to a single initial — *R.* — each stamped, none postmarked. Forty-one small paper coffins, and I had dug every one of them up with a butter knife and a torch, kneeling on the floorboards of her bedroom like a gravedigger who'd taken a wrong turn.

I had read eleven by the time the light went. I read the rest over the following week, in order, because it seemed suddenly obscene to skip ahead — these were not documents anymore, they were a life, and you do not skim a life. You sit with it. You let it rearrange your furniture.

I learned things about my mother that I have no right to have learned.

I learned that at nineteen she had called herself "a girl who laughs too loudly for this house," which was a sentence I could not reconcile with the woman who had shushed me across two decades of dinners. I learned she had once won a prize for chrysanthemums at a county show and had been so pleased about it that she wrote three paragraphs about the ribbon's color — "not quite red, R., more like the inside of a plum" — and I sat in the kitchen holding that page and thinking: *I did not know she noticed colors. I did not know she had an inside-of-a-plum in her at all.*

I learned that she had been funny. That was the worst part, if I'm honest. Not the secrets, not even the money — we'll come to the money, God help us, we will absolutely come to the money — but the jokes. There was a whole register of her voice that had been switched off sometime before I was born, or perhaps switched off only when I entered the room, and either possibility was its own small wound. In the early letters she teased Ruth about a boy named Dennis who wore his collar up. She described our grandmother — my grandmother! whom I knew only as a photograph of disapproval — as "Mum, armed with a trifle and a grievance." She signed off, in those first years, *Your own Edie*, which is a thing I cannot type without stopping.

And then, somewhere around letter fourteen, the warmth began to bank itself like a fire going down for the night. The sentences shortened. The jokes thinned. By the letters from my childhood she was writing the way she spoke to me: careful, weatherproofed, a woman sending dispatches from behind glass. *I am well. The garden is quiet. Felix has started school and does not cry, which everyone says is a good sign.* Everyone says is a good sign. Even in 1987 she was translating other people's verdicts into her own correspondence, still asking permission to feel things.

The last letter was at the bottom of the tin, where last letters go.

* * *

I should explain how I found it, because I nearly didn't. On the ninth day of cataloguing — my notebook by then had pages headed *Things She Never Said About Father*, *Things She Never Said About the Glasshouse*, and one bleak column titled simply *Dinners* — I took the tin out into the garden, because the house had begun to feel like a lung I was breathing for. It was October and the light came in low and gold and made even the dead borders look briefly ambitious. I sat on the back step with the envelopes fanned across my knees like a losing hand of cards.

That's when I saw that the bottom envelope was different.

Not sealed. All the others were sealed — glued shut with a thoroughness that suggested my mother had wanted those words to travel exactly nowhere. But this one lay open, its flap tucked in rather than stuck, as though she had sealed it and then, hours or years later, thought better of the seal and opened it again to check the contents. As though she'd needed to confirm the words were still there. People do that with wounds, too.

The date on it stopped me before I read a word. Two weeks before she died. Fourteen days. My mother had written this letter while I was driving down twice a week to sit with her in that beige room, holding her hand, discussing the traffic on the A-road and whether she was eating enough, and the entire time there had been a sentence forming in her like weather.

It said:

*R. —*

*I am ready now. Come and I will tell you everything.*

*— E.*

That was all. Nine words and two initials. After forty letters of accumulated silence — after decades of *I am well* and *the garden is quiet* — she had finally, finally unscrewed the jar, and what came out was an invitation. Not a confession. An RSVP requested.

She never sent it. Of course she never sent it. The stamp was there, licked and pressed, ready — I could see the faint shine of old gum on the flap — and then some final caution had closed over her like water. Or maybe not caution. Maybe she'd been waiting for courage to arrive by post, and it kept missing the collection time.

Two weeks. She had been ready for two weeks, and I had spent those two weeks talking to her about parking.

* * *

You want to know what I did next, and I'm almost embarrassed to tell you, because it wasn't dignified. I didn't weep dramatically among the roses. I sat very still on the step for a long time, long enough that my tea went cold in the way tea does when it understands the situation, and then I laughed. One short, appalled bark of a laugh, out there alone in the garden, loud enough that a wood pigeon left the yew tree in protest.

Because of course. Of course this was how it ended. My mother had spent sixty years not saying a thing, and when she finally said it, she said it to a person who couldn't hear her, in a letter she couldn't send, and then she died with the sentence still in her mouth. If silence were an art form, Edith Thackeray would have had retrospectives. There would have been a blue plaque: *Here lived a woman who was ready now.*

I put the letter back in the tin. Then I took it out again. Then I walked around the garden with it, which achieved nothing but felt necessary, the way pacing feels necessary to people waiting for news.

The terrible arithmetic of it kept presenting itself. Two weeks. She'd been ill for months — we'd all known, in the way families know and don't say, another item for the notebook — and somewhere in those months something had shifted in her. Something had tipped her from *I am well, the garden is quiet* to *come and I will tell you everything*. What does that? What happens to a person at the end that makes the vault finally seem less safe than the truth? I thought about her in the hospice bed, small and amused as ever at the nurses, and I tried to locate the moment. Was it a Tuesday? Had someone said something? Had she dreamed of Ruth?

Ruth. Even the name felt contraband. For fifty-odd years my mother had written to this person — loved this person, from the sound of the early letters, loved her with the specific ferocity of sisters — and had managed to raise a child, me, without ever once letting the name slip. Not in anger, not in drink, not in the loose-mouthed hour past midnight when mothers tell their truths. Nothing. A whole sister, amputated so cleanly that I hadn't even grown up with a scar.

And here was the thing I kept circling, the thing that made the laugh come back at odd moments for days afterward: my mother had done the same thing to me that her father had done to Ruth. Erasure ran in the family like eye color. Grandfather Thackeray had cut his daughter out of photographs and wills; my mother had cut her out of conversation, out of memory, out of me. She hadn't escaped the silence. She'd inherited it, maintained it, dusted it weekly. And then at the end, dying, she'd tried to hand it back — *come and I will tell you everything* — and missed.

Missed by days. Missed by a stamp.

* * *

That night I did something I hadn't done since the funeral: I cooked properly. Not toast eaten standing up over the sink, which had been my entire domestic repertoire since September, but an actual meal, onions and garlic and the good pan, because grief apparently expresses itself in me as an urge toward soup. I set the table for one and then, feeling ridiculous, moved the place setting anyway so I wouldn't have to look at the empty chair.

The letters were stacked on the kitchen table in their chronological line, forty-one envelopes marching from 1963 to two weeks before her death, a paper timeline of a silenced woman. I ate with them beside me like dinner guests. I found myself introducing them mentally. *This is Edie at twenty-two, full of opinions about buses. This is Edie at thirty-five, learning to write around a secret. This is Edie at seventy, down to four sentences a year.*

And this — I picked up the last one again, the open one, the nine-word one — this is Edie with the door unlocked at last, standing in the doorway, waving at someone who isn't coming.

Here is what I understood, sitting there with my soup and my ghosts: the letter wasn't really unfinished. That's what I'd thought at first — that it was truncated, that death had interrupted her mid-sentence. But no. *Come and I will tell you everything* is a complete communication. It's just not a complete *transaction*. It requires a second party. It requires Ruth to walk through a door that my mother built and then locked behind her for half a century. The letter was a bridge with no road on the far side.

But roads can be built. That thought arrived quietly, between spoonfuls, wearing work clothes.

Because Ruth might still be alive. I did the arithmetic on that too, over the washing-up: if Ruth had been, say, nineteen in the earliest letters — young enough for collar-up boys, old enough to be leaving home — she would be somewhere in her late seventies now. Seventy-eight, perhaps. Seventy-nine. People lived to those numbers all the time now; they did yoga about it. There was every chance that somewhere in England a woman in her late seventies had spent fifty years wondering why her sister stopped writing, why the letters turned to stone, why the family door closed — and that she was alive *right now*, possibly this evening, making her own supper in her own kitchen, not knowing that the answer to everything had been sitting in a biscuit tin behind a wardrobe, nine words long, waiting for someone to build the far side of the bridge.

My mother had missed her window. I hadn't missed mine yet. That distinction felt like the only useful fact I possessed.

* * *

I went back to the earliest letter — the first one, Edie at nineteen, the one I'd read standing in the dust with the torch in my teeth — and this time I read it not for her voice but for her address. Because the early letters had return addresses; my mother had been scrupulous about that, even in her most guarded years, as if some part of her always kept the door marked. And the first one, the plum-colored-ribbon era, the *your own Edie* era, bore an address in a coastal town whose name I'd heard exactly once in my life, mentioned by our grandfather in a tone usually reserved for wet weather, and never explained.

I copied it into my notebook, under a new heading. The notebook had pages of *Never Said* by now, columns of omissions like a negative inventory, everything subtracted from a life laid out in my cramped handwriting. But this page was different. This page had a direction in it.

Under the address I wrote: *If she's alive, she deserves to know she was ready-to-be-forgiven-for. And Mum deserves to have the letter delivered. Late. Fifty years late, but delivered.*

Then, because I am my mother's child after all and honesty costs me something, I added: *Also I need to know. Also I am furious. Also I miss someone I apparently never met.*

I looked at the demolition notice pinned to the kitchen corkboard — the developer's letterhead, the cheerful deadline, the word *clearance* doing its bland violent work — and I thought: they can take the walls. Walls are just silence with windows.

But the letter goes out. One way or another, this letter gets delivered.

I put on my coat.
