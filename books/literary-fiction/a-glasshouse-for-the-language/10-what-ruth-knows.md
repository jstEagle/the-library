## 10. What Ruth Knows

The kettle in Ruth Thackeray's kitchen had a whistle like a seagull with opinions, and she let it sing longer than necessary because the noise filled the room better than she could.

Felix sat at her table with both hands around a mug they hadn't yet drunk from, the way young people did when they needed something to hold. Ruth knew the look. She'd worn it herself for fifty years, mostly while holding teacups in empty rooms.

"You don't have to tell me anything," Felix said.

"No," Ruth agreed. "I don't." She poured the tea anyway, milk first, the way their mother—*her* sister, God help them both—had always done it wrong, milk after, and Ruth had spent her girlhood correcting her about it until the day she left and never corrected anyone again. "But you drove four hours on roads I wouldn't wish on my late husband's driving, and you're sitting in my kitchen looking like your mother's photograph come back to haunt me. So we may as well start somewhere."

She sat down across from Felix and folded her hands, and was surprised to find them steady. She had expected them to shake. She had expected, if she were honest, to be shaking altogether by now, ever since the tall stranger with Marion's jaw had appeared at her garden gate that morning and said, *I think you might be my aunt,* as though delivering a parcel.

"Ask me what you came to ask," Ruth said.

Felix looked into their tea. "What happened?"

Ruth laughed—a short, dry sound, like a drawer sticking. "What happened. That's a question with sixty years of answer in it, love. You'll want more than one cup."

"I've got nowhere else to be."

"Well. That makes two of us."

* * *

She began, as she had begun telling it to herself a thousand times over the decades, with the weather. It was easier. Everything was easier if you started small.

"It was 1963. February. Cold enough that the pipes in the big house froze and Father complained about it for a fortnight as though the plumbing had betrayed him personally. I was seventeen. Your mother was twenty-one. And our brother Gerald—" she paused, because she still could not say his name without her mouth going strange around it, "—Gerald was thirty, and he had just become, through no effort of honesty whatsoever, a very important man."

Felix didn't move. They had their mother's stillness, Ruth noticed. Marion could sit through anything without twitching. It had been her gift and her ruin.

"You'll have heard of Halloway & Thackeray," Ruth said. "Or you'll have heard of the money it became. What you won't have heard is that it started as two men and a shed. Arthur Halloway and my father, equal partners, equal shares, drawn up proper by a solicitor who charged them eleven pounds and threw in the ink. Arthur was the ideas man. Father was the one who could make an idea sit up and behave. For twenty years it worked like a marriage—the good kind, which even then was rare."

"And then?"

"And then Arthur Halloway went to sea."

She watched Felix's face. Nothing there yet. Good. She would get there slowly; she'd earned the right to be slow.

"A fishing trip, it was meant to be. Off the coast near Whitby. There was a storm—not a freak one, not a once-in-a-lifetime thing, just an ordinary North Sea bad temper—and the boat went down, and Arthur drowned, and that is what the newspapers said and what the coroner recorded and what was read out at a funeral where Father cried beautifully, I'll grant him that. He always could cry beautifully. It was one of his talents, right alongside forging a signature."

Felix's breath caught. Ruth nodded, almost kindly.

"There it is. I saw you flinch. Good. Flinching means you're still capable of being surprised, and I'd begun to worry, from the look of you, that this family had got to you already."

"The ledger," Felix said quietly. "I found the ledgers. In the solicitor's files."

"Then you know some of it. But ledgers only tell you the arithmetic. They don't tell you the *afternoon*." Ruth wrapped her own hands around her cup now, needing the heat. "So. The afternoon. I'll give you the afternoon, since nobody else will."

* * *

It had been a Sunday. She remembered that with absurd precision, because she'd been supposed to go to evensong with her friend Pat and had begged off with a headache, and had been lying on her bed reading a library novel she was too young for when she heard the front door and then heard her father's voice saying her name—not calling it, *saying* it, low, to someone else, the way you say a thing you're checking off a list.

She'd gone down the stairs the quiet way, past the third step that announced visitors, and stood in the hall where the coat rack hid her, and seen her father and Gerald at the study door. Gerald had papers under his arm. Her father had said, "You understand it has to be dated before he sailed," and Gerald had said, "I understand perfectly, Dad," in a voice so pleasant, so reasonable, that for one full second Ruth had thought they were discussing a delivery or a subscription or something harmless, something with dates in it that ordinary families discussed.

Then her father had said, "And the widow signs Monday?" and Gerald had said, "Monday," and Ruth had understood, all at once and completely, the way you understand a fall only after you've landed, that the papers were about Arthur Halloway, who was three weeks dead, and that whatever was being signed on Monday would make the dead man's share of the company belong to the living ones.

"I didn't see the documents themselves," Ruth said. "Not that night. I saw enough. A partnership dissolved by agreement, a buyout clause exercised, a signature witnessed—all of it dressed up legal, because Gerald was clever that way. He never forged a thing crudely. He made forgery look like paperwork. That's the trick of it, love, if you ever want to know how a family rots: not lies, exactly. *Documentation.*"

"What did you do?"

Ruth smiled, and it was not a warm smile, and she was glad there was no one in the kitchen but Felix to see it.

"What did I do. Seventeen years old, in my school shoes, standing behind a coat rack. I did what children of liars do. I waited to find out what I was allowed to know." She drank her tea. It had gone lukewarm and she drank it anyway. "And then I made the mistake of being brave, later, when bravery was no use to anybody."

* * *

Because that was the part she had rehearsed least and would now have to say plainest, and her hands, the traitors, finally began to tremble, so she set the cup down before it could embarrass her further.

"Arthur Halloway had a daughter," she said. "Ada. She was nine when he drowned. Nine years old, and her father's half of everything went into the company's accounts and stayed there, growing, year on year, wearing our name instead of hers. And when I was nineteen—two years after, when I'd found my courage in a drawer somewhere—I wrote to her. One letter. Told her I believed her father had been wronged, told her I'd seen things, told her if she ever wanted to ask questions I would answer them."

Felix leaned forward. "Did she write back?"

"She never got it." Ruth's voice went flat and careful, the voice of a woman walking on ice she already knew was thin. "Marion found it. Your mother. Found it in the post tray before it went out—she was home between terms, helping with the household, and she read it, and she took it to Father. Not to stop me. To *report* me. She was twenty-three and engaged by then—to your father, the house, the whole shining future—and she came to my room that night and she sat on my bed and she said, 'Ruthie, you can't. You'll ruin everything. Not just for us. For yourself.' And I said, 'It's the truth,' and she said—" and here Ruth stopped, because fifty years had not worn the sentence smooth, it had only polished it, "—she said, 'The truth is expensive, and you can't afford it.'"

The kitchen clock ticked. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside a gull made its opinions known.

"And did you send another letter?"

"I did not. I was nineteen and I had watched what happened to people who crossed my father—he ruined a foreman once over a disagreement about overtime, quietly, completely, the man ended up sweeping floors in Leeds—and I told myself I'd write to Ada when I was older, when I was safe, when I was free. And then I married Tom Iverson, who was a good man and a poor one, and I moved here, forty miles and a whole lifetime away, and every year I thought, next year. Next year I'll write. And the years did what years do." She spread her hands. "You know the rest of it better than I do, probably. You lived inside the version where none of this existed."

"Marion never mentioned you. Not once. Not a photograph, not a name. I found you in a parish record and an address book with your entry scratched out."

"Scratched out." Ruth considered this. "That would have been Father's pen. Or Gerald's. They were thorough, those two. When I testified—oh yes, don't look so startled, there was one moment of testimony, 1971, when Ada Halloway, grown by then and armed with a solicitor of her own, petitioned to reopen the estate, and I gave a statement, a small one, hedged round with *I believe*s and *it seemed to me*s, and it went nowhere because Gerald's paperwork was beautiful and my word against paper is no word at all—but after that, after I put my name to even a hedge-shaped truth, I stopped existing. Photographs cropped. Christmas cards unreturned. Marion changed her surname in her own letters to me, did you know that? Signed herself Marion Ashworth for years, as though Thackeray were a disease we might not share."

"Why did she do it? Choose him. Choose the silence."

Ruth looked at Felix for a long moment—at Marion's jaw, Marion's stillness, Marion's hands around a cup—and felt the old anger shift, as it sometimes did in her seventies, into something more like sorrow, which was worse.

"Because she was loved," Ruth said simply. "That's the answer nobody wants. Father adored Marion. She was the pretty one, the easy one, the one whose approval he courted. Nobody adores the child who asks questions. I asked questions. So I got the truth, and the truth came with a bill attached. Marion got comfort, and comfort came with a bill too—it just arrived later, in installments, over a whole life. She paid it the way she paid everything: quietly, and without letting anyone see the cost." Ruth's eyes stung and she let them, mostly. "You want to know why she never spoke? Because speaking would have meant admitting she'd chosen. And she chose at twenty-three, and by fifty the choosing was load-bearing. Pull it out and the whole house comes down. Well." She gestured vaguely eastward, toward the estate, toward the glasshouse, toward the machines waiting. "You're pulling it out."

* * *

They sat a while. Felix drank their tea at last, cold, and grimaced, and Ruth found herself smiling despite everything, because Marion had made that exact face over cold tea her entire life, and grief, Ruth had learned, was largely a matter of recognizing gestures.

"The letters," Felix said. "Behind the wardrobe. Did you know she kept writing?"

"I stopped expecting them in 1980," Ruth said. "That was the last one I received—or rather, the last one that was sent. After that, nothing. I used to imagine her writing and burning them. It seemed like something she'd do. Write the truth down where it couldn't hurt anyone, then destroy the evidence, because evidence was dangerous in our family. Evidence was the enemy." She shook her head slowly. "And instead she sealed them and hid them behind a wardrobe for fifty years like a woman burying treasure she was ashamed to spend. That's more hopeful than burning. Burning means you've given up. Hiding means some part of you is waiting for permission."

"She wrote to you about leaving home," Felix said. "Early ones. She was funny in them. I didn't—" Their voice snagged, and Ruth watched this child of silences try to say the thing and fail and try again. "I didn't know she was funny. I didn't know her. I catalogued her silences for three weeks thinking that was all there was, and then I opened a biscuit tin and met a stranger who laughed."

Ruth reached across the table—slowly, giving Felix time to withdraw, and Felix didn't—and laid her hand over theirs.

"Then you mourned her twice," Ruth said. "Once for the mother you had, and once for the one you didn't. That's the family tax, love. We all pay it. I'm still paying mine, and I've had fifty years of installments." She withdrew her hand and stood, and began gathering the cups, because feeling was bearable in this house only if her hands were moving. "But here's what I'll tell you, since you've driven all this way and since you've got that look, the look of someone assembling a case. You're not the first person to come asking about Arthur Halloway."

Felix went very still. "No?"

"Ada Halloway wrote to me. Fourteen years ago. After Tom died—she'd seen the notice, or someone sent it to her, I never learned which. One letter, polite as a bank statement, asking whether I still stood by what I'd said in 1971." Ruth set the cups in the sink and turned around. "I wrote back. We've corresponded, off and on, ever since. She's ninety-one now, and sharp as broken glass, and she has spent her entire adult life petitioning, writing, filing, asking—for recognition, for restitution, for someone official to say aloud what we both know. And every door has closed in her face, because doors close for women like her and open for families like ours."

She dried her hands on the towel, unhurried, watching Felix absorb it.

"She lives in a care home in Scarborough," Ruth said. "An hour's drive, if the traffic's kind. And I'll give you her address, Felix, gladly—but you should know what you'd be carrying to her door. Because she doesn't want apologies. Apologies are cheap and she's had a lifetime of cheap. What Ada Halloway wants—" Ruth paused, and chose the words the way you choose stones to skip, "—is for the truth to be said somewhere it counts. Out loud. On the record. Before she dies. And I am seventy-eight years old, and tired, and I have hidden behind hedges long enough."

She slid a piece of paper across the table—an address, written that morning in her careful schoolroom hand, prepared before Felix had even knocked, because some part of Ruth had known, the moment she'd seen Marion's face at her gate, exactly where this road went.

"So," she said. "Decide what you're carrying, and who you're carrying it for. But decide soon. She's ninety-one, and the truth has already missed one funeral in this family. I don't intend to let it miss another."
