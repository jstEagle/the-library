## 12. Cousin Marcus Arrives

Marcus Thackeray-Hale had a theory about bad news: it traveled like weather. You felt the pressure drop days before the front arrived. So when his cousin Priya called from Singapore to say that Felix had been "asking odd questions at the county office," he was not surprised so much as disappointed — the way one is disappointed by a forecast of rain on a wedding day.

"Odd questions," he repeated, turning the word over. "What sort of odd?"

"The sort that make clerks call their supervisors," Priya said. "Probate records. The old company ledgers. Something about Grandfather's business partner."

"And who told you this?"

"Aunty Bex heard it from her solicitor, who heard it from theirs. You know how it goes."

He did know how it went. The Thackeray family communicated the way a hive communicates — no single bee knew the whole plan, but everyone knew when something had disturbed the comb.

"I'll handle it," Marcus said, and hung up, and sat for a moment in his study in Hampstead looking at the framed photograph of his grandfather on the mantel: a stern man with a beautiful jawline and, if you believed half of what Felix was apparently digging up, a flexible relationship with other people's property.

"Well, Grandfather," he said to the photograph, "it seems we're doing this again."

* * *

He drove down on a Thursday, because Thursdays were noncommittal. Arriving on a Friday suggested eagerness; arriving midweek suggested crisis; Thursday said *I happened to be in the area*, which was a lie so comfortable it practically ironed itself.

The estate looked worse than he remembered, which was saying something. The house wore its scaffolding of neglect like an old man wearing last decade's suit — technically still dressed, but nothing fit anymore. The glasshouse at the back had gone properly feral, its panes clouded green with algae, its iron ribs rusting into lace. Developers would flatten the lot by spring. He'd signed off on that months ago with genuine relief; the place had been bleeding money since Aunt Margaret died, and sentiment was a luxury item he'd learned early never to buy retail.

Felix's car was in the drive — a sensible grey hatchback with a parking ticket wedged under the wiper. Marcus smiled at that. Felix had always been the responsible one, the one who read the terms and conditions. It made what he'd come to do both easier and harder.

He knocked, waited, knocked again, then let himself in with the key every cousin still carried, because the family had never met a boundary it respected.

"Felix?" he called, into the hall of dust sheets and echo. "It's only me. Try to contain your joy."

From somewhere above: a thump, a pause, footsteps. Felix appeared at the top of the stairs holding a cardboard box against their hip, hair tied back, sleeves rolled, wearing the particular expression of someone interrupted mid-thought. There was dust on their cheekbone like a beauty mark.

"Marcus."

"Cousin." He spread his arms. "Come on, then. Hug. I've driven four hours and my suspension is filing a grievance."

Felix came down and submitted to the hug with the stiffness of a cat being held against its will. Over their shoulder, Marcus surveyed the hall: boxes labeled in Felix's neat hand, a notebook lying open on the side table, and — his stomach performed a small, controlled drop — a biscuit tin, lid slightly askew, beside it.

"You've been busy," he said lightly.

"Cataloguing." Felix stepped back. "Everything has to go somewhere before the demolition."

"Mm. And does everything include trips to the county records office? Because I hear you've been making friends there. Asking after Grandfather's ledgers. The old partnership papers." He kept his voice pleasant, conversational, the voice he used for negotiating contracts — warm enough to seem generous, level enough to suggest that warmth was optional. "Priya rang me. And Bex. And, I suspect, half of Gloucestershire by now."

Felix set the box down slowly. "I didn't realize my questions required clearance."

"They don't require clearance, darling. They require *context*." He smiled his best smile, the one that had closed three mergers and one very messy divorce settlement. "Which is why I'm here. Shall we have tea? I find difficult conversations go better with tea. Or worse, but at least pleasantly."

* * *

The kitchen was the only room Felix hadn't sheeted, and Marcus remembered why: it had been Aunt Margaret's room, her citadel. The Aga still ticked faintly, keeping a ghost-warmth. Felix made tea without being asked, moving around the kitchen with the unconscious fluency of someone raised in it, and Marcus sat at the scrubbed pine table and laid his leather folder down between them like a card player showing the deck.

"What's that?" Felix asked.

"Probate papers. The sale agreement. The demolition consent forms." He tapped the folder. "Also a letter from our solicitors, which I'll summarize for free: sign these, and your share of the sale clears within the month. Don't sign them, and everything enters review, which means delay, which means costs, which means the estate eats itself alive while lawyers bill us hourly for the privilege of watching."

"I'm not stalling the sale, Marcus. I signed the demolition order weeks ago."

"You did. Thank you for that, genuinely." He wrapped both hands around his teacup. "But signing the demolition order isn't the same as closing the estate, and closing the estate isn't the same as — how shall I put this delicately — going through the family's dirty laundry in public buildings."

"So it *is* true, then."

He blinked. "What is?"

"That there's laundry." Felix sat down across from him, and Marcus saw — with the mild professional alarm of a man who read people for a living — that his cousin wasn't angry. They were calm. Calm was much worse. "I wondered if Bex's version had embellished it. But you just confirmed it. There's something to hide."

"There's nothing to *hide*. There are things that are *private*, which is different, and things that are *dead*, which is most different of all." He stirred his tea though he took no sugar. "Felix. Sit with me a moment. Let me tell you about families."

"Oh, I can't wait."

"Humor me." He leaned back. "Every family is built on something. Ours happens to be built on a company, and the company was built by a man who is now dead, and whatever he did or didn't do in 1963 is buried with him, along with everyone who could testify. What remains is money, houses, and about forty living relatives whose pensions, school fees, and mortgages sit quietly on top of that foundation like furniture on a floor. Now—" he raised a finger— "you may kick the floor all you like. But understand that when floors go, everything on them goes too. Not just the guilty. Everyone."

"Is that what we're calling it? A floor?"

"I'm calling it a foundation, because I'm being precise, because precision matters when people start throwing words like *fraud* around in public offices where clerks take notes."

Felix's eyes flickered — just barely — toward the doorway, toward the stairs, toward wherever the biscuit tin lived upstairs. Marcus caught it. He was good at catching things; it was most of what he did.

"You've found something," he said softly.

"I've found some letters."

"Letters."

"Family correspondence."

"Between whom?"

Felix was quiet for a moment. Outside, a crow argued with another crow about something on the lawn, and the sound filled the pause like a dropped glass fills a silence.

"Does the name Ruth mean anything to you?" Felix asked.

And there it was. The pressure drop. Marcus kept his face perfectly arranged — mild interest, slight puzzlement, the face of a man searching his memory — but inside, an entire filing cabinet fell over.

"Ruth," he repeated. "No. Should it?"

"It's just a name I came across."

"Ruth what?"

"Just Ruth."

He laughed, warm and easy, and reached across to pat Felix's hand. "You've been alone in this mausoleum too long. It's making you cryptic. Come up to London next week, stay with us, let Sofia feed you until you can't move. We'll go through all of it together — the papers, the questions, all of it. Family shouldn't do this kind of thing alone."

"We're not really a family that does things together, Marcus. We're a family that does things *separately, in silence*, and then meets quarterly to agree nothing happened."

"Sofia makes a lamb shoulder that would change your mind."

"*Marcus.*"

He sighed, and let the charm drop — not all the way, never all the way, but enough that Felix could see the machinery underneath, the way you glimpse the clockwork when a clock's face cracks. It was important they see it. Charm alone made people suspicious; charm plus one honest inch of steel made them reasonable.

"Let me be plain, then, since plainness seems to be the weather today," he said. "Whatever you think you've found, I promise you it cost our grandparents' generation something enormous to bury it, and it will cost ours something enormous to dig it up. Scandal doesn't stay tidy. It doesn't affect only the dead. There are trusts. There are settlements. There are cousins — children, Felix, actual children — whose lives are financed by the arrangement you're poking at. If it unravels publicly, it doesn't unravel neatly. It unravels like a sweater, from one thread, and everyone's left holding wool."

"Or," Felix said, "someone was robbed sixty years ago, and the robbery paid for all those school fees, and maybe the fees were never ours to spend."

Marcus set down his cup with great care. "You don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Felix agreed. "Not yet. That's rather the point of asking questions."

They looked at each other across the table, across the tea, across forty years of shared Christmases and shared silences, and Marcus understood two things simultaneously: first, that Felix was not going to be charmed, bought, or frightened today; second, that he had badly underestimated his quiet cousin, who had apparently spent a lifetime learning to wait in exactly the way the rest of the family had spent a lifetime learning not to speak.

"All right," he said, standing, gathering his folder. "Think about what I've said. Sign the papers. And Felix — if you find any more *letters*, any more names, anything at all — bring them to me first. Before anyone else. Before any offices." He smiled, and this time the smile was entirely honest, which somehow made it worse. "Family closes ranks. It's the one thing we've always been good at. I'd hate for you to end up outside the rank."

"Is that a threat?"

"Heavens, no. It's an invitation." He shrugged into his coat. "The threat comes later, if you need one. I'm told I'm very charming about it."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and glanced back at the staircase, at the dust sheets, at the whole sleeping weight of the house.

"Whoever Ruth is," he said quietly, "leave her where she is. Some doors are load-bearing, Felix. Open them and the whole wall comes down on whoever's standing nearest."

Then he smiled again, waved cheerfully, and drove away down the rutted drive with his heart hammering and his face doing what it had been trained since boyhood to do, which was nothing at all.

In the rearview mirror, the house shrank behind him, and behind the house, half-hidden by yew trees, the glasshouse caught the afternoon light and threw it back — a hundred broken panes flashing all at once, like something winking.

Marcus turned the radio on loud and drove toward London, already composing the email to the solicitors, already planning the calls, already — though he would have denied it under oath — listening, for the first time in years, to the sound of the family's long-held breath, and wondering how much longer it could possibly hold.
