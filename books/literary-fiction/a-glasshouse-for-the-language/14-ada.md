## 14. Ada

The care home was called Rosemont Court, which Felix thought was the kind of name that got chosen by committee, the way funeral homes were called Peaceful Rest and budget airlines were called things like Skyrise. It sat behind a row of pollarded limes on the edge of a market town, a low brick building with a conservatory bolted onto the front — a small, domestic, well-loved glasshouse, kept full of geraniums and a lemon tree that had clearly given up on lemons but refused to give up on itself.

Felix sat in the car for eleven minutes.

This was becoming a habit. Eleven minutes outside Ruth's gate, eleven minutes outside this car park, eleven minutes outside every door that led toward the truth. They were beginning to suspect that the eleven minutes *was* the truth, or at least the antechamber of it — that everything important in their family had always happened in the pause before someone worked up the nerve to speak.

They checked the ledger pages in the folder on the passenger seat for the fourth time. Photocopies, because the originals were now evidence, or would be, if Felix ever found the courage to make them evidence. Grandfather Thackeray's neat, upright hand. The dates. The small, damning arithmetic of a man who had discovered that a dead partner was cheaper than a living one.

*You're going to put a ledger in front of a ninety-one-year-old woman,* Felix told themselves, *and ask her to relive the worst year of her life.*

*Yes,* came the answer, in a voice that sounded suspiciously like their mother's. *You are. Because she asked to be asked. Because nobody ever asked.*

They went in.

* * *

The woman at the desk — a young man with a lanyard and the serene competence of someone who had seen every possible human mood before lunch — checked his list. "Ada Kessler? She's in the conservatory. She's usually in the conservatory. She says the rest of the building is for people who've given up." He smiled. "Fair warning: she'll pretend she doesn't remember you're coming. She thinks it keeps her hand in."

"Her hand in?"

"Card sharp," he said, with the reverence of the young for the old and dangerous. "She runs a whist table on Thursdays. Two of the residents won't play with her anymore."

Felix found her exactly where advertised, in a wicker chair among the geraniums, a cardigan the color of marmalade over her shoulders, a paperback face-down on the armrest. She was small and upright and sharp-eyed, with white hair cut short and practical, and hands that were folded in her lap with the stillness of someone who had learned, long ago, that hands give you away.

"Miss Kessler? I'm Felix Thackeray."

Something happened in her face. It was very fast and very controlled — a flicker, like a pane of glass catching light — and then it was gone, replaced by an expression of mild, elderly vagueness that Felix recognized instantly, because their mother had worn the identical one for forty years.

"Thackeray," Ada said. "Now that's a name I haven't heard spoken out loud in a long while. People mostly write it down and then burn the paper." She gestured at the chair opposite. "Sit down, then. You've driven a long way to sit in my conservatory. I can always tell the long-distance ones. They sit like the chair might be taken away."

Felix sat. The lemon tree stood between them, gallantly leafless.

"I brought something," Felix said. "I wasn't sure I should. I've been carrying it around for two days, and I've taken it out of my bag in three car parks and put it back in twice."

Ada's eyes sharpened. The vagueness fell away like a coat.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

Felix laid the photocopies on the little glass table between the chairs, in order, the way they'd arranged and rearranged them at the kitchen table at three in the morning. Ada put on her glasses — gold half-moons on a chain — and read.

She read the way Ruth read, Felix noticed. Slowly. Completely. With her thumb moving under each line as if to hold the words down, in case they tried to leave.

The conservatory was quiet except for the tick of the radiator and, somewhere inside the building, a television playing a game show to an audience of one. Outside, a blackbird was conducting its evening business in the limes.

Ada turned the last page. Set it down. Aligned its edge with the others, precisely, the way their mother had aligned everything — cutlery, photographs, the edges of her silences.

Then she laughed.

It was not what Felix had expected. It was a short, cracked, genuinely amused laugh, the laugh of a woman who has just been shown a card trick she saw through at fourteen.

"He underlined it," she said. "Look. He *underlined* the entry. 'Adjustment — K. & H. dissolution — £1,400.' Underlined. As if underlining would make it legitimate. As if the pen were a notary." She tapped the page. "My father kept his accounts in pencil, you know. He said a pencil was honest because you could see where you'd changed your mind. This man — your grandfather — he went over his in ink and then underlined the lie. That's not fraud, that's *furniture*. That's a man who intends to live in what he's built."

Felix found they couldn't speak. They had prepared, in the eleven minutes and in the two days of car parks, a kind of speech — an apology, an explanation, a framing of themselves as messenger rather than inheritor. It had all evaporated. What remained was a person sitting in a warm room with a stranger, holding a stranger's grief out in front of her like a tray.

Ada looked up at them over the half-moons.

"You have her face," she said. "Not exactly. But the way of it. The way of holding still." She removed her glasses. "I met your mother once. Did Ruth tell you that? No, of course not. Ruth wouldn't know. It was at the funeral — my father's funeral, the one your grandfather stood at with his hat in his hands looking like a man at the funeral of a man he had personally and thoroughly killed. Which, as it turns out —" she tapped the ledger again, gently, almost fondly, "— he had."

"Your father died before the dissolution," Felix said. "The records say—"

"The records say he died of a fever, alone, in a boarding house in a town where he had no reason to be, three weeks after he told my mother he'd found something in the books he couldn't account for and was going to make it right." Ada's voice was level. Practiced level — the levelness of a thing checked so many times it had worn smooth, like a stair rail. "I was eleven. I remember the fever story. I remember it the way you remember a nursery rhyme — it has a rhythm, and the rhythm is wrong."

The blackbird outside said something emphatic and left.

"I spent fifty years not saying any of this out loud," Ada went on. "You get very good at it. It's a skill, like whist. You learn to hold your cards and keep your face. My mother died still calling it a fever. I let her. What was the alternative? Tell her the man she'd married had been murdered by paper — that his own partner had written him out of existence with an entry in a ledger and a doctor in a strange town? There was no version of that that didn't break her. So I kept the cards. I kept the face." She folded her hands again. "And then I got old, and I discovered that the face doesn't come off anymore. It's grown on. Like ivy."

"I'm sorry," Felix said, and heard how small it was, and said it again anyway, because it was true even if it was insufficient. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry that it was — that we're the ones — that the money that raised me—"

"Is his," Ada finished. "Yes. Some of it is. I did the arithmetic myself, years ago. I was quite good at arithmetic. It's why my father liked having me in the shop." She smiled, and it was the first smile that reached all the way, and it was terrible and warm at once. "I worked out what my share would have been, with interest, compounded. I did it on the back of an envelope in 1987 and then I put the envelope in a drawer, and I've moved house four times since, and the envelope has come with me every time. My daughter thinks it's a recipe."

Felix laughed — a startled, wet-eyed laugh, entirely against their will — and Ada looked pleased, as though she'd been trying for exactly that.

"What do you want?" Felix asked. It came out blunt, and they were glad of it. "I've been asking myself what I owe, and I keep arriving at numbers and hating all of them. But you're the one owed. So — what do you want? Money? The house is being demolished; there's a settlement; I could—"

"Stop." Ada raised one hand, and it was a small hand and a soft hand and it stopped Felix as effectively as a wall. "No. Sit back. Breathe. You've come all this way to hand me a cheque, and I'm going to tell you what I want, and it isn't that, and you'll be relieved and disappointed in equal measure, which is the correct mixture."

She leaned forward. The marmalade cardigan slipped; she ignored it.

"I am ninety-one years old," she said. "I have a bad hip, an excellent card game, and what the doctor calls a 'watchful' heart, by which he means he watches it and hopes. I do not need money. I need money the way the lemon tree needs lemons. What I need—" and here her voice did something, dropped its practiced levelness for one syllable, one crack in the rail, "—what I need is for it to be *said*. Out loud. Somewhere official. Somewhere with a stamp on it, or a clerk, or a record book, or whatever it is they use now. I need it written down by someone whose job it is to write things down, that my father, Henry Kessler, was defrauded of his share of Kessler and Thackeray by Edmund Thackeray, who falsified the record of his death to claim it. I need it in the world's own handwriting."

She sat back.

"Sixty years," she said. "Sixty years it's been a story I tell the teacup. And a story told to a teacup is just — weather. It doesn't *land* anywhere. I want it to land. I want it on a record, in an office, where a clerk will file it and a stranger might someday read it and know that Henry Kessler existed, and was honest, and was robbed, and had a daughter who noticed." She looked at Felix steadily. "Can you do that?"

"I think so," Felix said. "Ruth will testify. She heard him say it — your father, she means; she heard our grandfather say it. She was a child, but she heard it. And there are the ledgers, and the solicitor's files, and—"

"Ruth." Ada said the name the way you test ice with one foot. "Ruth Thackeray. The sister who vanished from the photographs." She was quiet a moment. "I looked for her once, you know. Years ago. Wrote to the old address. Nothing came back. I assumed—" She stopped, and started again. "I assumed she didn't want to be found. It's remarkable, isn't it, what we assume. I assumed she was hiding. Perhaps she was only buried."

"She was buried," Felix said. "By our family. Deliberately. She's seventy-eight, she lives on the coast, she grows tomatoes with an intensity I can only describe as theological, and she has been waiting fifty years for a letter that was never sent." They heard their own voice shake. "I have the letters. My mother wrote them for forty years and never posted one. The last one says she was ready. It's dated two weeks before she died."

Ada was silent for a long time. In the glass around them the evening was coming down, blue and soft, and the geraniums went from red to a deeper red, like embers.

"Two weeks," she said at last. "Two weeks." She shook her head slowly. "You know what I think, Felix Thackeray? I think silence is a room, and everyone in a family gets shut in it separately, and the cruelty of it is that you can all hear each other breathing through the wall and none of you can find the door." She picked up the ledger pages, squared them, and held them out. "Take these back. Keep them safe. And when you say it — out loud, officially, wherever it is they say such things — you say my father's name. Henry Kessler. Not 'the partner.' Not 'the deceased.' Henry. He had a laugh like a gate hinge and he let me stamp the invoices when I was six and he is *not* going to be a footnote in his own murder."

Felix took the pages. Their hands weren't steady, and Ada saw, and did them the kindness of pretending not to.

"I'll say it," Felix said. "I don't know exactly how yet, or where, or what it costs — my cousin is already circling with probate papers and the word 'scandal' — but I'll say it. Before—"

"Before," Ada agreed, briskly, "I die. Yes. Everyone says it that way, as if it were morbid. It isn't. It's a deadline. Deadlines are a mercy. I've never finished anything in my life that didn't have one." She reached over and patted Felix's hand once, briefly, with those soft dangerous fingers. "Now. You'll stay for supper. It's shepherd's pie on Wednesdays and it's better than it has any right to be, and you'll want your strength, because I can see you've got a long way still to go and you're carrying more than papers in that bag."

"How can you tell?"

"Because you keep touching it," Ada said, "the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. Whatever's in there, it's warm and it's yours now and you haven't decided whether to feed it." She raised her voice toward the corridor. "Nurse! Two for supper! And bring the good vinegar, we've got an occasion — a Thackeray's come to give me back my father."

* * *

They ate shepherd's pie at a small table in the conservatory while the dark came up, and Ada told stories about Henry Kessler — the gate-hinge laugh, the pencil accounts, the day he'd let six-year-old Ada stamp INVOICE RECEIVED on forty invoices and she'd stamped one on her own hand and worn the bruise like a badge for a week. Felix laughed until their face hurt, and then, somewhere in the middle of a story about a runaway delivery cart, found themselves crying quietly into a napkin, and Ada passed the salt and said nothing, which was the correct answer.

It was nearly nine when Felix stood to go. Ada walked them as far as the conservatory door, one hand on the lemon tree's pot for balance, and stopped there.

"Felix," she said. "One more thing. Free advice, from a card player." Her eyes were bright and level in the lamplight. "Whatever you're still carrying in that bag — the thing you keep touching — when you find it, don't sit with it in a car park for eleven minutes. Some doors you have to walk through while your legs still remember how to be afraid. After that, they just remember how to open."

Felix drove home through the dark with the folder on the passenger seat and Ada's voice in their ears, and it wasn't until they were pulling into the estate's empty drive, past the dark hulk of the glasshouse, that they understood she was right, and that the thing in the bag — the cassette, labeled in their mother's hand, *For R, if she ever asks* — had been warm the whole way home.

They carried it into the study. They did not sit in the car.

They found the old machine in the drawer where the telephone directories lived, and their hands were shaking, and they pressed play.
