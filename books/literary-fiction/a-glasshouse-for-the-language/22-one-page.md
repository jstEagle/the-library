## 22. One Page

The frame cost eleven pounds from a charity shop on the high street, and Felix spent more than that in time choosing it. Too ornate and the letter would look like a relic. Too plain and it would look like a mistake. In the end they chose a frame of pale ash wood with a thin gilt edge, the kind of compromise their mother would have called *sensible* and their mother's sister would have called *cowardly*, and Felix hung it above the desk in the small flat in town where the light came in slantwise through the morning and caught the paper at the hour when Felix sat down to write.

The letter behind the glass was the first one. Dated the fourteenth of June, 1963, in a hand Felix had spent a lifetime seeing on birthday cards and shopping lists and never once *reading*. *Dear R,* it began. *I have packed two dresses and my anger, and I am leaving on the morning train. If you come with me I will carry your suitcase the whole way, even the hill. If you don't come, write to me anyway, and I will forgive you before the ink dries, because that is what sisters are for, apparently, though no one asked my opinion.*

It had never been sent. It had spent sixty years behind a wardrobe in a house that was now a rectangle of cleared earth with a fence around it and a sign that said FUTURE DEVELOPMENT in a font that suggested optimism was mandatory. Felix had kept one pane of glass from the glasshouse — the pane over the propagation bench, where the moss had grown thickest — and it leaned in the corner of the flat like a window to a country that had stopped issuing visas. Sometimes, on bad days, Felix caught themselves checking it for weather. As if the glass remembered the seasons better than they did.

The rest of the letters had gone to Ruth, all of them, in a wooden box lined with the good tissue paper Felix had bought for the purpose and then agonized over, because there was no protocol for this, no card at the stationer's that said *SORRY YOUR SISTER NEVER POSTED ANYTHING, INCLUDING HER LOVE*. Ruth had taken the box in both hands the way you take something hot out of the oven, and she had read the first letter standing in her greenhouse among the tomato plants, and she had laughed at the line about the hill, a short cracked sound like a branch giving way, and then she had sat down on her own potting stool and read the second one, and Felix had let themselves out and watered nothing and waited in the car for forty minutes, which felt correct.

They met monthly now. First Saturday. Ruth's greenhouse, ten o'clock, tea in mugs with faded roses on them. It was not forgiveness. Felix had learned the difference the way you learn the difference between a door and a wall: by walking into one of them. Ruth was warm, and Ruth was funny, and Ruth could still, four months in, go quiet in the middle of a sentence about their mother — about *Marion*, she said now, *Marion* instead of *your mother*, as if the name were a door she was practicing opening — and the quiet would sit between them like a third person at the table. Felix had stopped trying to fill it. The quiet was doing work. You didn't interrupt a surgeon.

Ada wrote back. That was the thing Felix had not expected, though perhaps they should have; ninety-one years teaches a person to answer letters promptly, because the alternative is running out of time to. Ada's handwriting was enormous and confident, the handwriting of someone who had learned penmanship in an era that believed in penmanship the way some people believe in God. Her first letter ran four pages and contained, among other things, a full account of her father's greenhouse — *he grew orchids, Felix, orchids, in Yorkshire, out of pure spite against the climate* — and a single line, set off on its own, that Felix had copied into the notebook and then copied out again onto the flyleaf of a book they no longer remembered the title of: *You cannot give a person back their life, but you can stop charging them rent on the truth.*

The official statement had been filed in October. The county records office smelled of radiator dust and old glue, and the clerk had taken Felix's declaration with the weary courtesy of a man who had heard every kind of confession a county could produce, and Ruth had given her testimony in a voice that did not shake until the very end, and then shook only a little, and then stopped, and Ada had been present by arrangement, in a wheelchair, wearing a hat that could only be described as victorious. When it was done, Ada had reached up and taken Felix's hand and said, *Well. That's the rent paid,* and laughed until the nurse came over, concerned, and Ada waved the nurse off with the imperiousness of a woman who had waited six decades to laugh in a records office and did not intend to be hurried.

The Thackeray fortune, such as it remained after the settlement and the sale and Marcus's lawyers, had been divided in ways that would have made the probate solicitor weep into his files. Felix had taken the smallest lawful share and directed most of it to Ada, and to a scholarship at the horticultural college their mother had attended and never mentioned. Marcus had not spoken to Felix since. There had been a letter, eventually, from Marcus's solicitor, three paragraphs of frost with a fourth paragraph at the end that Felix read six times, because buried in the legalese was a sentence that could only have been written at Marcus's direct instruction and could only have been written by a man who had, against every interest of his own, read the tape transcript Felix's lawyer had sent him: *Our client wishes it noted that he listened to the recording in its entirety.* That was all. No apology. No accusation. Just the fact of it, entered into the record like a man pressing his hand against a door he had no intention of opening, so that someone, someday, would know he had stood there.

Felix had written back to Marcus anyway. A short letter. *I heard her voice too. It's not the same as forgiveness but it's adjacent. The door's not locked.* They did not expect an answer. They wrote it because the notebook had taught them that unsent letters are just debts you insist on keeping, and Felix was done with debts.

* * *

The desk had come with the flat, secondhand, scarred with rings from other people's cups. Felix had begun, without quite deciding to, writing letters at it. Not emails. Letters. There was something about the slowness of it, the way a pen made you mean what you said, that had turned out to be the whole inheritance — not the money, not the land, but the discipline of saying a thing all the way down to the bottom of it, where the unsaid things used to live.

They wrote to Ada monthly, and got answers the way you get weather from a force of nature. They wrote to Ruth, though they saw her in person, because Ruth had said, the third Saturday, with a small embarrassed smile, *I like getting your letters. It's the first time anyone's posted me anything in fifty years and I find I'm greedy for it.* They wrote to their mother, sometimes, which had felt unhinged the first time and less unhinged the fourth, and which had produced one letter that Felix had actually burned in the sink — the angry one, the one that began *You let him erase her* — because the notebook had taught them that too: some letters are for sending and some are for the burning, and the trick of a life is knowing which is which, and their mother had never known, and had kept sixty years of letters in a tin rather than choose.

And once — only once, at two in the morning, after a date that had gone well enough that Felix had come home and stood in the kitchen grinning at the kettle like a fool — Felix had written the opening of a letter that began *Dear you, whoever you turn out to be,* addressed to a child that did not exist and might never exist, and had gotten as far as *There are things I want you to know that no one told me, and I'd rather you got them from the source* before stopping, because the letter was true and the child was hypothetical and the combination felt too tender to finish at two in the morning. It lived in the desk drawer now, unfinished, and Felix took it out on the first Saturday of every month and read it and added nothing and put it back, the way you visit a seedling to confirm it hasn't died.

Tonight the light had gone and the radiator was ticking and Felix sat down at the desk with the notebook — the original one, the inventory, the list of everything their mother never said — because it was the last night of the year and it felt like the kind of thing you did at the end of a year, the way you paid off a tab.

The notebook was nearly full. Felix turned back through it, past the entries of the last months, past *Ada laughed today and the laugh was her father's, Ruth says, exactly his*, past *Ruth called Marion by name without flinching — progress*, past *Marcus's letter. He listened. Noted.* Back to the beginning, to the first pages, written three weeks after the funeral in a house that still smelled of other people's lives.

*Item one: She never said why we never went to the coast.* Felix had forgotten that was the first one. It had seemed so small at the time, so petty, and now it read like a man tapping a wall and hearing the hollow place without knowing there was a whole room behind it.

*Item two: She never said what she did before she married.*

*Item three: She never said who taught her to graft.*

On and on, the whole litany, forty pages of it by the end, the inventory of a silence so complete it had its own architecture. And Felix read down through them now with the strange tenderness of someone reading a diagnosis that has since been cured, and came to the last page, the one they had been saving, the way you save the last page of a notebook for something worthy of it.

They had thought, months ago, that they would write *Forgiven* there. That had felt like the ending the story wanted. But forgiveness was Ruth's to give or withhold, and Ada's, and it wasn't Felix's word to spend. They had thought, more recently, of *Understood*, but that was a lie dressed as humility; Felix did not understand why a nineteen-year-old had chosen a wedding over her sister's testimony, and suspected that *understood* was often just the word people used when they'd gotten tired of a question.

The pen hovered. Outside, someone's wind chime was going, and a car passed with its radio on, and the framed letter above the desk caught the streetlight and held it, the way glass holds anything, without opinion.

Felix wrote one word.

*Said.*

They looked at it for a long time. It was such a small word for such a large hole. But the hole had not been large, that was the thing the notebook had taught them in the end; the hole had been small, a biscuit-tin-sized hole, a single unsent letter's worth of hole, and it had only looked enormous from the inside of the house that was built over it. Pull the one page out and the whole structure came down on its own, the way the glasshouse had, the way the house had, quietly, in an afternoon, with Ruth beside them and a single pane of glass in the boot of the car.

Felix closed the notebook and set it on the shelf beside the framed letter, spine out, so that anyone who ever sat at this desk would see it and wonder, and would perhaps ask, and would perhaps be told, and the telling would take less time than it had taken to earn, which was, Felix supposed, how every true thing got passed on: cheap to the receiver, ruinous to the sender, worth it.

They took down a fresh sheet of paper. The unfinished letter to the hypothetical child could wait; it was a perennial, it would come up again. But there was another letter, one that had been forming all evening at the back of Felix's mind the way moss forms on the north side of a thing, patient and inevitable, and Felix picked up the pen and wrote the date at the top and then the salutation, and stopped, because the salutation was the whole question, wasn't it, and it had taken them a year and a house and a sister-in-law-by-silence and a ninety-one-year-old in a victorious hat to learn how to answer it.

*Dear Marion,* Felix wrote. *I'm not going to ask you why. I'm writing to tell you what happened after.*

And downstairs, or perhaps only in the imagination, which at this hour was the same address, the kettle began, very softly, to sing.
