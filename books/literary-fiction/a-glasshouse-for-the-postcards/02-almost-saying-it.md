## 2. Almost Saying It

There is a particular skill to not being asked questions, and Lena Galloway had spent five years perfecting it the way other people perfect scales.

The trick, he'd learned, was never to be interesting. Interesting people got asked things. The man who mopped the recital hall at dawn was not interesting; he was furniture with a schedule. He wore his cap low and his name tag turned backward—*L. GALLOWAY* facing his own chest, a private joke he allowed himself each morning, pressing the letters flat against his sternum like a hand smoothing down a shirtfront. Nobody read a janitor's name tag anyway. But he liked knowing it was there, the way you might keep a photograph in a wallet you never open.

He was on his knees at the edge of the stage when the doors at the back of the hall opened, and that was how he knew something was wrong. Nobody came to the hall at six in the morning except him.

It was the student again. Nadia—the one from last week, the one who had looked at his hands.

She came down the aisle slowly, carrying her violin case like a shield she hadn't decided whether to raise or set down. She stopped three rows back, which he noted with professional respect: close enough to talk, far enough to retreat.

"You're here early," she said.

"So are you."

"I couldn't sleep." She said it lightly, testing whether he would take the bait and ask why. He did not take the bait. He wrung out his mop with the calm of a man who had never once been curious about anything. "I have juries next week," she added, because apparently silence made her nervous enough to fill it herself. "Bach. The Chaconne."

"The Chaconne," he said, and let it sit there, a stone dropped into a well, no splash following.

"It's—" She shifted her weight. "You know it?"

"Everyone knows it." This was true and also a wall, and he leaned against it comfortably. He stood, gathered his bucket, and began rolling it toward the wings, which was a language all its own: *this conversation is over, I am a man with somewhere to be.*

"You play," she said. Not a question this time. She'd learned.

"I clean."

"Your hands—"

"Are chapped," he said. "It's the cleaning solution. You should wear gloves when you work with solvents. Occupational hazard."

And there it was, the small lie, delivered so smoothly it barely cost him anything anymore. That was the thing nobody warned you about lying: it wasn't the big lies that wore you down. It was the small ones, the daily ones, the ones you told so often they stopped feeling like lies and started feeling like your face. *I'm new here. Never played seriously. Wrong Galloway, probably.* He had told so many versions of himself that some mornings, lathering soap in the staff bathroom, he genuinely had to pause and remember which one he was wearing today.

Nadia watched him go with an expression he refused to turn around and look at directly, though he saw it anyway, reflected in the dark glass of the sound booth—a look that was not pity, worse than pity, something hungrier. Recognition, maybe. Or research.

*Later,* he told himself. *Deal with her later.* He had gotten very good at *later*. Later was where he kept everything.

* * *

Five years ago, on the night everything happened, he had been wearing tails.

That detail always surfaced first when the memory came, uninvited, the way it did now as he pushed his bucket through the service corridor. Not Marta's face, not the sound of the door. The tails. How stiff the collar was, how his mother—no. His mother didn't come into it yet. The collar first, then the lights, then the piano under his hands like a living animal he'd ridden since he was seven years old and knew every muscle of.

The Vetrani Hall, sold out. His teacher in the second row with her program rolled into a telescope. Donors in the boxes, Aldous Renner among them, silver-haired and radiant, the patron who had discovered him at eleven and called him *the real thing* in interviews, *a once-in-a-generation hand*. And out front, third row center aisle, Marta, who had promised—had sworn on the tomatoes, which was the most binding oath in their family—that she would be there, that afterward they would talk, that she had something to tell him, something important, and wouldn't he just trust her until then?

He had trusted her. That was the terrible part. Right up until bar sixty-one of the Schubert—the B-flat Sonata, D. 960, the one with the long trill like a held breath—he had trusted her completely.

Bar sixty-one. He could still find it in his hands. If someone put the score in front of him today, blindfolded, his fingers would travel to it like water finding a drain.

Because that was where the door opened.

Not a dramatic door. A side door, stage left, the one the stagehands used. It opened the way it always opened, with a soft hydraulic sigh, and a wedge of corridor light fell across the floor, and a voice said something—two words, maybe three, off-mic, pitched for one person only—and the voice was Marta's, and the tone was wrong. Not angry. Frightened. Marta did not frighten. Marta grew tomatoes in a conservatory glasshouse and taught him to read scores upside down and once told a board member to his face that his endowment smelled like money left too long in the sun. Marta frightened was a category error, a violation of physics, and his hands understood it before his mind did.

His hands simply stopped.

Mid-phrase. Mid-trill, actually, which the critics later found unforgivable in a way they couldn't articulate—to stop anywhere is bad, but to stop inside a trill is to stop inside the instrument's own breathing—and the sound died in the hall with a completeness that he could feel physically, four hundred people inhaling at once, the acoustic equivalent of a held breath, and he sat there with his hands hovering over the keys like a man who has forgotten what tables are for.

He never explained. That was the arrangement, though he didn't know yet that it was an arrangement; he thought it was a failure. They asked—the teacher, the donors, the newspaper, gently at first and then not gently—and he said nothing, and the nothing hardened into a story of its own: the prodigy who broke. Nerves, they decided. Pressure. The price of genius, tut-tutted over coffee. A tidy little tragedy with no loose ends, and isn't it remarkable, said everyone, how the family closed ranks, how dignified they were, how they asked for privacy.

Privacy. That was the word they used for it. He had spent five years thinking of it as his wound, and it took him most of those years to understand it had been a transaction.

But that night, that night he only knew that Marta's voice had come through a door and his hands had believed it, and by the time he walked offstage—escorted, gently, by two stagehands, as if he were the fragile one—she was gone. Not gone-gone. Just gone from the building. Gone from the phone. Gone from the glasshouse, where her tomato plants stood in their rows like an audience waiting for her to come back and explain.

She never explained either.

So the family did what the family did, which was to say nothing, beautifully, in unison, and Lena learned that silence has choreography. There are steps to it. You learn where to put your feet.

* * *

"Lockridge."

His supervisor, Deb, stood at the mouth of the corridor holding a clipboard the way other people hold verdicts. Deb was sixty-ish, built like a fire hydrant, and possessed of the conservatory's most finely calibrated radar for bullshit, which was why Lena had chosen his lie for her with such care.

"Morning, Deb."

"You filled out the personnel form wrong again."

"Did I."

"'Previous musical training: none.'" She looked at him over the top of her reading glasses. "You wrote 'none' on a form for a job at a music school, Lockridge. In this building. Where you work."

"Clerical error."

"You alphabetized the supply closet by manufacturer and viscosity."

"Also clerical."

Deb stared at him for a long moment, and he held the stare with the mild, apologetic blankness he'd practiced in mirrors—not a guilty face, just a face, the face of a man whose inner life was mostly grout. Finally she snorted and made a note on her clipboard.

"One of these days," she said, "you're going to tell me what your deal is."

"My deal is that the east stairwell needs degreasing."

"That's not a deal, that's a Tuesday." She waved him off. "Go. Degrease."

He went, and felt the familiar small exhaustion settle over him, the one that came after every successful evasion. People thought lying was thrilling. Lying was housework. It was never finished; you only stayed ahead of it. Every conversation was a room that needed tidying before the next guest arrived, and he had been tidying for five years, and some mornings he woke up tired in a way sleep had nothing to do with.

The strange part—the part he'd admit to no one, not even himself, not really—was that it worked. Invisibility kept him safe. The prodigy had been a person people wanted things from; the janitor was a person people wanted things *for*, briefly, and then forgot. No one asked a mop for its history. He had traded applause for anonymity and told himself it was a fair exchange, and most days he almost believed it, the way you almost believe a room is warm when you've been in it long enough.

Almost. Everything in his life now came with *almost* attached. Almost saying it. Almost leaving. Almost asking the question that lived under his sternum like a swallowed stone: *Marta, where did you go?*

* * *

The trouble with Nadia was that she didn't respond to walls. Most people, given a wall, apologized and went around. Nadia treated walls as load-bearing structures worth investigating.

She found him Thursday afternoon in the practice wing corridor, where he was replacing a burnt bulb in a sconce, and she stood below him holding sheet music like a summons.

"I looked you up," she announced.

The ladder swayed slightly. He kept his voice level. "Looked me up."

"Lena Galloway. The plaque in the recital hall. 'This hall restored through the generosity of the Galloway Family.' Except it's not generosity, is it? It's a donor plaque, and Galloway isn't a common name here, and the man on the ladder has hands like—" She stopped herself, visibly recalibrating. "I'm not trying to be weird about it."

"You're doing great," he said, screwing in the bulb.

"I just think it's strange that nobody talks about him. Five years and nobody talks about him. My professor changed the subject when I brought up the recording."

Something cold moved through him, slow, like water finding a crack. "What recording?"

"The recital. The one where he—" She gestured vaguely, delicately, the way people gesture at tragedies they weren't present for. "Stopped. There's a copy in the archive. I checked it out last month."

"And?" He heard his own voice from very far away, admirably bored.

"And it's shorter than the program says it should be." She frowned. "The sonata runs thirty-some minutes. The recording cuts off at fourteen. Right after—" she flipped through her notes, and he watched her flip through them with the detached horror of a man watching someone handle his own diary—"bar sixty-one. There's just applause after, like they patched it. Like they cut the rest and stuck an ending on."

The bulb clicked home. The sconce glowed. Lena descended the ladder one rung at a time, because if he hurried, she would see that his knees had opinions.

"Recordings get edited," he said. "Copyright, licensing. Boring reasons."

"Sure," Nadia said. "Probably." And then, with the terrifying gentleness of the young: "Your hands are shaking."

"They're not."

"They are. I watch hands. It's kind of my whole thing." She tucked the sheet music under her arm. "I'm writing my thesis on what happens to prodigies this place decides to forget. So. If you ever want to be forgotten less loudly, you know where to find me."

She left. He stood in the corridor under the fresh bulb, listening to the building hum, and thought about a recording that ended at bar sixty-one, patched with applause, the way you'd patch a hole in a wall before selling the house.

Someone had edited that night. Someone had decided where it ended.

And underneath the fear, underneath the five years of careful choreography, something in him that he'd thought was dead lifted its head—not hope exactly, hope was too generous a word—more like the old performer's instinct, the one that knows when the audience has gone quiet for the wrong reason.

He looked down at his hands. Steady now, or nearly.

"Marta," he said quietly, to the empty corridor, practicing the name the way you practice a difficult passage—alone first, before anyone can hear you miss the notes.

Down the hall, the archive door stood half open, and inside, someone had left the light on.
