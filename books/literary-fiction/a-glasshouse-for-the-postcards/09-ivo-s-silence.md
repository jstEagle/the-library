## 9. Ivo's Silence

Ivo Petran had been tending the conservatory glasshouse for thirty-one years, and in that time he had learned that plants were honest and people were not. A tomato vine did not pretend to be thriving when it was dying. A begonia did not smile at you across a board meeting while quietly arranging for your watering schedule to be cut. Plants told you what they needed. People told you what they wanted you to believe.

This was why he preferred the mornings before anyone else arrived, when the glasshouse belonged only to him and the misting system and the particular green hush of things growing without an audience. He would come in at half past five, put the kettle on the little burner behind the potting bench — technically against regulations, practically a tradition older than most of the board members — and walk the rows with his coffee, saying good morning to each bed like old acquaintances. The tomatoes got the longest greeting. They always had.

The tomatoes were Marta Galloway's.

Not officially, of course. Officially they were part of the educational cultivation program, a heritage variety donated by an anonymous benefactor years ago. Unofficially, they were seeds Ivo had saved from the summer a seventeen-year-old girl with dirt under her fingernails and her brother's sheet music folded in her back pocket had taught him that you could read a score upside down if you understood the logic of it well enough. *The notes don't care which way you're standing,* she'd said. *They just want to be played.* He'd thought at the time she was talking about music. He'd spent five years since suspecting she'd been talking about herself.

He heard the door before he saw it open — the soft hydraulic sigh, then footsteps he knew. Light ones, careful ones, the walk of a man who had trained himself out of being heard.

"You're early," Ivo said, not turning around. "Even for you."

Lena Galloway stood in the doorway with his mop bucket, looking like a man who had been caught somewhere he wasn't supposed to be, which was absurd, because the man was employed here, and also because Ivo had been expecting this visit for two weeks now, ever since the girl — Nadia, the one with the thesis and the hungry eyes — had started asking questions Ivo didn't want to answer.

"I could say the same," Lena said.

"I live here." This was nearly true. There was a cot in the tool room that the fire marshal had never been told about, and Ivo saw no reason to complicate his life with accuracy. "Coffee?"

Lena hesitated. He always hesitated, as though accepting a cup of coffee might constitute a claim on something, might make him visible, might cost him. Then he nodded and came in and set the bucket down by the door with the exaggerated care of a man who had learned to move through other people's spaces without leaving a mark.

Ivo poured him a cup and watched him hold it without drinking. The boy — man, he was twenty-six now, but Ivo's eyes insisted on *boy* — looked at the tomatoes.

"They're hers," Lena said. Not a question.

"Heritage variety," Ivo said. "Anonymous donation."

"Ivo."

"Marta's," Ivo admitted. "From her last season here. I've been saving seed every year since. It's not sentimental," he added, because it was entirely sentimental and honesty required some counterweight. "It's good stock. Resistant to blight. She had a feel for it. Knew which fruit to take seed from and which to leave. Most people your age can't tell a determinate from an indeterminate, but she—" He stopped himself. He was doing the thing he did, filling silence with horticulture, planting words over the hole where the truth went.

Lena was watching him with those eyes. The Galloway eyes, both of them had them, a pale grey-green like weather coming. Marta's had always seemed to be arriving somewhere. Lena's looked like they were leaving.

"The gardener mentioned someone asking about us," Lena said carefully. "About the family. Was that you?"

"No."

"Then who?"

Ivo drank his coffee. Outside, the sky was going from charcoal to pewter, and the first light came through the glass roof the way it always did, laying long pale stripes across the benches like sheet music nobody had written yet.

*Tell him,* said the part of Ivo that had been arguing with itself for five years.

*Don't,* said the part that paid his rent.

* * *

Here is what Ivo Petran knew, and had known for five years, and had carried the way you carry a stone in your boot — not painfully, exactly, not even constantly, but always there, always shifting your weight slightly to one side:

On the night of October the fourteenth, five years ago, he had been working late. This was unremarkable; he worked late often, especially in autumn, getting the tender things bedded down before frost. What was remarkable was who came through the glasshouse door at eleven-forty at night: Marta Galloway, in her good coat, the one she wore for recitals, and behind her, holding the door, a man Ivo recognized instantly and completely, because you do not spend three decades at an institution without learning its hierarchy the way a tree learns which way the light comes from.

Aldous Renner. Board member. Patron. The man whose name appeared on more plaques in the building than any donor except the founding family, and whose charm was famous, and whose generosity was genuine — Ivo wanted to be fair about this, even now, especially now — and whose hand, once, resting briefly on Ivo's shoulder in front of the funding committee, had communicated with perfect clarity that the garden's budget was a living thing and could be made to live or die.

Marta had not seen him. The glasshouse was dim, and Ivo had been in the shadow of the tall bench among the overwintering fuchsias, and she had walked past him close enough that he could have reached out and touched her coat. She was crying. Not loudly. The way you cry when you've decided crying is a private matter regardless of circumstances. Renner was speaking to her in the low, reasonable voice that powerful men use, the voice that makes coercion sound like concern, and Ivo caught fragments — *think about your brother*, and *this doesn't have to end anything*, and *no one needs to know* — and then they moved through into the propagation room, and their voices dropped below the threshold of hearing, and Ivo stood among his fuchsias with his heart going like a trapped bird and did not move.

He told himself he hadn't heard anything definitive. He told himself it was a private conversation between a patron and a student, that young women cried, that board members gave advice, that it was none of his business. He told himself these things while his hands repotted nothing and his mind replayed *no one needs to know* in Renner's smooth baritone until two in the morning.

At midnight, Marta had come back through alone. Renner was no longer with her. She had stopped, just for a moment, near the tomato bed — her tomato bed — and pressed her palm flat against the top of the cold frame, and Ivo, hidden ten feet away, had watched her do it and had understood, in the wordless way one understands certain things, that she was saying goodbye to something. Then she had gone, out the side door toward Corvane Street, and Ivo had stood in the dark listening to the misting system tick, and had done nothing.

She was never seen again. That was the official version. *Ran off*, the story went, *a troubled girl, unstable, probably headed for the coast or farther*. And Lena Galloway had stopped playing mid-phrase at his recital the same week — the dates braided together in everyone's memory like two strands of the same rope — and the conservatory had lost its two brightest young people in a single season, and the board had expressed regret, and the funding had continued, and Ivo Petran had watered his tomatoes and said nothing.

Because here was the arithmetic, the ugly simple arithmetic he could not escape: the garden ran on board money. His wages came from board money. He was sixty-one years old with hands too ruined for any other trade and a cot behind the potting bench and no pension worth the name. If Aldous Renner had done something — and Ivo did not know *what*, that was the maddening thing, he had never known what, only that the shape of it was wrong, that *no one needs to know* is not the sentence of an innocent man — and if Ivo spoke, then Renner would fall or wouldn't, and either way the garden would suffer, and Ivo would be an old man with dirt under his nails and nowhere to grow anything.

Everyone in this story has bought their quiet at a price. Hal Galloway had bought his with whatever arrangement he'd made — Ivo didn't know the details, but he knew the shape of a man carrying a transaction the way Hal carried it, shoulders down, eyes anywhere but forward. The board had bought theirs with donations. Lena had bought his with invisibility, with a mop and a badge and five years of polishing floors around his own engraved name. And Ivo had bought his with thirty-one years of loyalty distilled, in one moment, into cowardice, priced at approximately one salary and one glasshouse.

He had never forgiven himself for it. But he had also never stopped growing her tomatoes, and he had come to understand that these two facts were related — that the seeds were his penance and his confession simultaneously, planted every spring, harvested every summer, an argument he was having with himself in the only language he trusted.

* * *

"You knew her," Lena said now. "Really knew her."

"She used to hide in here when practice got bad." Ivo set his cup down. "Said the glasshouse was the only room in the building where nobody cared how fast you were. She'd sit on that crate—" he pointed "—and eat cherry tomatoes off the vine like they were free, which technically they weren't, and tell me about you."

"Me?"

"All the time. 'My brother's going to be famous,' she'd say. 'Better than famous. He's going to be *good*.' She had this whole theory that famous and good were different things and most people confused them." Ivo smiled despite himself. "She taught me to read her scores upside down. I never told her I already knew how. It made her so happy to teach me something."

Lena laughed — a small, startled sound, like a bird landing — and then his face did something complicated, grief and gratitude fighting over the same ground.

"Ivo." He set the untouched coffee down. "The night she left. Were you—"

And there it was. The question, finally, after five years, walking through the door wearing a janitor's uniform.

Ivo looked at him. At the pale eyes. At the hands wrapped around the mug, those celebrated hands, scarred now at the knuckle from cleaning chemicals, still unmistakably a pianist's hands to anyone who knew what to look for. At the boy who had swept these halls in silence for two years rather than ask anyone anything, because asking meant being seen, and being seen meant risking the wound again.

He thought about the stone in his boot. Five years of walking slightly tilted. He was tired of tilting.

"I was here," Ivo said.

Lena went very still.

"October fourteenth. I was working late, bedding down the fuchsias. She came in at eleven-forty with a man." Ivo's voice came out lower than he intended, roughened, like a tool taken out of storage. "I knew him, Lena. Everyone knows him. He's the director now."

The color left Lena's face in stages, like a tide going out.

"He was telling her things," Ivo went on, because now that the dam had cracked he found he could not stop, five years of held water coming through all at once. "*Think about your brother. No one needs to know.* That's what I heard. Fragments. Then she came back through alone, near midnight, and she touched the cold frame — right there, where your hand is now — and she left through the side door, and I stood behind my bench like a coward and let her go, because the garden runs on board money and I am an old man and I chose my tomatoes over your sister, and I have regretted it every single day since, and I am sorry, I am so sorry—"

"Stop." Lena's voice cracked on it. He was gripping the edge of the bench, and his knuckles had gone white, and he was breathing the way people breathe when the floor has moved under them. "Say his name."

Ivo closed his eyes. Opened them. Said it.

"Aldous Renner."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The misting system ticked. Somewhere in the propagation room, a pipe knocked twice, the way it always did when the temperature changed, a small mechanical heartbeat in the quiet.

Then Lena straightened up, slowly, and Ivo watched something happen in his face — not resolve exactly, not yet, but the raw material of it, ore before smelting. He picked up his coffee and drank it, all of it, cold, in one go, like a man taking medicine or making a decision.

"He requested a meeting with me yesterday," Lena said. "Left a note in my supply closet. He wants to talk about 'the future of the facility.'" A strange, thin smile crossed his face. "I'm still wearing the badge, Ivo. I think I'll wear it to the meeting."

"Lena—"

"He asked about the Galloways," Lena said. "Someone's been asking. You said it wasn't you. Nadia says it isn't her." He looked at the tomatoes, at the cold frame where his sister's palm had rested on the last night anyone saw her, and his voice, when it came, was quiet and terrible and calm. "So it's him. He's asking about us. Which means he's worried."

He turned for the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame.

"Ivo. Thank you. For the seeds. For—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

After he was gone, Ivo stood alone among the rows with the empty mug in his hand, feeling lighter and heavier at once, the way a man feels after setting down a stone he's carried so long his body has grown around it. The tomatoes rustled faintly in the ventilation draft, forty-one days into the season, leaves turning toward the light they couldn't see yet.

"Go on," he told them gruffly. "Grow."

Outside, the morning was fully arrived, and somewhere upstairs, in a carpeted office smelling of polish and power, Aldous Renner was waiting for a janitor to come and explain himself.

Ivo hoped, with his whole stone-free heart, that the boy would keep him waiting.
