## 16. Postcard Forty-Two

Doro had left the postcard with me the way you hand someone a sleeping infant—both hands, slightly away from her body, as if it might wake and cry.

I didn't read it that night. I want to be honest about that, because everything else in this story I have been honest about late, and I'd like at least one thing to arrive on time. I took it home to my apartment above the dry cleaner on Pell Street, set it face-down on the kitchen table, and made tea I did not drink. The card sat there like a small country that had declared independence from the rest of the tin. Forty-one postcards in a floorboard; a forty-second one that had traveled three hundred miles and three years to reach me by way of a stranger's coat pocket and a stranger's courage. It deserved better than to be read standing up in a hallway with my coat still on.

So I sat. And then I sat some more. There is a particular cowardice that looks exactly like reverence from the outside, and I performed it beautifully for the better part of an evening.

What finally made me turn it over was something small and stupid: the smell of the tea going bitter in the pot, which was a smell Marta had hated. She used to say over-steeped tea was what happened when you couldn't decide whether to stay or go, so you did both badly. She said it about people too.

I turned the card over.

The front was a photograph of the harbor at Vellmouth—gray water, gray sky, a row of fishing boats knocked together like teeth. On the back, in Marta's handwriting, slanted leftward the way she always wrote when she was going fast, which was the way she wrote everything:

*Lena.*

*I'm not going to make this long, because I've gotten good at knowing when I'm running out of time, and I don't mean anything dramatic by that. I mean the light is going. I mean my hands are cold. I mean Doro will find you because I asked her to and she's stubborn in all the ways I was and none of the ways I wasn't.*

*First: I forgive you. Not for anything you did—for the thing you didn't do. For staying on that stage. I have thought about that night every day for five years and I want you to know that I never once, not even in the worst hours, thought you chose the piano over me. You were seventeen and a man twice your age had spent four years building a room around you with only one door, and he was standing in it. I knew that. I should have told you that instead of asking you to walk through walls. That's mine to carry, not yours.*

*Second: I know what the silence cost you. I heard the recording—you'd be amazed what reaches a small town if someone loves you enough to mail things. You stopped mid-phrase and never explained why, and everyone decided you were broken, and you let them, because being broken was quieter than being right. I know that arithmetic. I invented it. But listen to me: the silence was supposed to protect us both and it protected him. That's all it ever does. That's the whole trick of it.*

*Third—and this is the part where you're allowed to put the card down and come back later—I name him. Aldous Renner. What he did to me, and what he would have done to you, and how the family took the money and the scholarship and called it love. I named him to Doro. I named him in letters that are waiting with a lawyer in Vellmouth. But names only work out loud, Lena. A name in a drawer is just a word.*

*So here is what I ask, and it's the only thing I'll ever ask you, and you can refuse it and I will still love you, which is the whole point of asking properly:*

*Make the building say my name.*

*Not a plaque. Plaques are for donors. Make the building say it—in its pipes, in its halls, in the room where he taught us both that music was a debt we owed him. I used to grow tomatoes in that glasshouse and pretend they were listening. Let the building be the one thing in our lives that doesn't keep quiet.*

*I'm tired now. Tell Ivo his watering schedule was wrong but his heart was right. Eat something green. You always forget.*

*Your sister,*
*Marta*

*P.S. The last tomato seeds are in an envelope in the tin. They're from the ones I grew the summer we read sheet music upside down. Plant them or don't. But they'll grow either way. That was always the difference between us.*

I put the card down. I picked it up. I put it down again and pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes until the room went orange.

She forgave me.

You would think that sentence would land like warmth. It didn't. It landed like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was locked—the strange, disorienting click of a door opening onto a corridor I now had to walk down. Because forgiveness, real forgiveness, is not a release. Everyone says it is. Everyone pictures it as a weight lifted, a debt erased, a warm bath for the soul. Nobody tells you that forgiveness is a *task*. That she hadn't absolved me so much as deputized me. That the last line of her life was an instruction, and instructions are heavier than apologies, because you can't just feel them. You have to *do* them.

Make the building say her name.

I sat at my kitchen table until the window went from dark to the color of dishwater, and somewhere in those hours I understood two things with total clarity. The first was that I could not do it alone. The second was that I knew exactly who to sit with while I figured out how.

* * *

The glasshouse at six in the morning smells like wet earth and old glass and, faintly, like tomatoes pretending to be summer. Ivo was already there, of course. Ivo has been already there since approximately 1974, and I have a private theory that if the conservatory burned to the ground overnight, Ivo would be found at dawn the next morning standing in the ashes holding a watering can, mildly annoyed about the compost.

He didn't look up when I came in. He never does. He just slid a stool across the flagstones with his foot, the way he'd done since I was nine years old and used to hide in here during theory exams, and kept tying his tomato vines with the little figure-eights he ties, loose enough to grow, snug enough to hold.

"You look," he said, "like a man who slept in his clothes."

"I slept in my clothes."

"Good. Saves time." He snipped a yellowing leaf and let it fall. "Tea's in the blue pot. Don't oversteep it."

The blue pot. Of course he knew. Half the conservatory probably knew by now that Doro existed, that a tin existed, that the past had grown legs and was walking around the grounds asking questions. Ivo's intelligence network is composed entirely of gardeners, caretakers, and bus drivers, and it is faster than the board's, cheaper than the board's, and correct roughly ninety percent more often.

I poured the tea—properly, three minutes, I counted—and sat down among the tomato plants, and told him.

I told him about the forty-second postcard. About Vellmouth. About the illness, the three years, the lawyer with the letters. And then I read it to him. All of it. My voice cracked twice, once at *you were seventeen* and once at *eat something green*, and Ivo pretended very hard to be fascinated by a vine, which is the kindest thing a man can do with his face.

When I finished, the glasshouse was so quiet I could hear the condensation ticking off the panes.

"Well," Ivo said finally. He untied a vine and retied it, though it didn't need it. "She always did give good notes."

That undid me a little. I laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and unglamorous, and Ivo handed me a rag off his belt without comment, the way you'd hand a man a tool.

"She's right, you know," he said. "About the building."

"The building can't talk, Ivo."

"The building talks constantly. You've worked in it five years. You've just been cleaning instead of listening." He pointed the snips at the rafters, at the pipes, at the whole groaning iron skeleton of the place. "Every radiator in that recital hall has a voice. Every door has an opinion. The organ loft whistles in E-flat when the wind comes from the north—it's been doing it since before Renner was director, and it'll do it after, and nobody can fire a whistle." He turned back to me, and behind all the dirt and the squint there was a seriousness I'd only seen from him maybe twice before. "But that's not what she meant, is it."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

"What she meant is that a name gets erased quietly, in filing cabinets and program notes and the memories of people who found it convenient. And it gets restored loudly, or not at all." He snapped a dead leaf clean off its stem. "Loudly costs something. You should decide, before you start, what you're willing to pay. Because he'll offer you a price to stop. He's very good at prices."

"He already has," I said, before I could stop myself.

Ivo's eyebrows went up about a millimeter, which on Ivo's face is the equivalent of another man dropping his coffee.

"Has he," he said. "And what did the great Aldous Renner offer you?"

"A career. An archive. My father's debts." I looked into my tea. "In exchange for the tin."

"And?"

"And I haven't answered yet."

Ivo nodded slowly, the way he nods at weather reports he doesn't believe. Then he did something unexpected. He walked to the far bench, the one under the cracked north pane, and came back with a shoebox, and set it in my lap.

Inside, in labeled envelopes, in Ivo's blocky carpenter's handwriting: *MARTA — CHERRY. MARTA — ROMA. MARTA — THE YELLOW ONES SHE LIKED.* Seed after seed after seed, saved year after year, dried on windowsills and tucked away.

"You've been growing her tomatoes," I said. "All this time."

"Somebody had to say her name," Ivo said. "Even if it was just to the plants." He shrugged, embarrassed, a big man caught mid-tenderness. "They listen better than most boards, anyway."

I held the box and I thought about what she'd written—that the seeds would grow either way, planted or not, and that this was the difference between us. She'd meant it as praise. Sitting there in the damp green morning with forty-one postcards' worth of evidence in my bag and a forty-second instruction in my pocket, I finally understood it as a dare.

* * *

I stayed through the morning. Ivo put me to work, because Ivo expresses sympathy exclusively through labor, and by ten o'clock I had repotted eleven seedlings and learned that my watering technique, according to Ivo, was "enthusiastic," which from him is a diagnosis.

It was Nadia who found us. She came through the glasshouse door at a jog, hair escaping its clip, carrying her laptop like a shield, and stopped dead when she saw the shoebox open in my lap.

"Those are—" She looked at Ivo. "You *have* them. The line seeds. I asked the registrar office about Galloway family records and they said there was nothing, they said—"

"They said nothing because nobody asked the gardener," Ivo said, deeply satisfied. "Story of this whole place."

Nadia crouched down next to me, and I watched her take in the envelopes, the postcard in my hand, my face, in that order, and I watched her decide—visibly, deliberately—not to ask about any of it yet. Instead she said:

"I got into the vault index."

I looked up.

"Not the vault. Just the index. It's digitized—badly, whoever did it in 2011 was clearly punished afterward—but the recital archive is itemized. And there's an entry." She turned the laptop toward me. "March fourteenth, five years ago. Master Recital Series. Running time listed as thirty-one minutes." Her finger tapped the screen. "Lena. Your recital was scheduled for seventy-five."

Thirty-one minutes. I had stopped, by my own count, twenty-two minutes in. Which meant the official recording contained nine minutes of whatever came after I stood up from the piano. Nine minutes of a hall full of people watching a boy not play. Or—my stomach dropped slowly, like a stone settling in pond water—or nine minutes of something else entirely.

"Nadia," I said. "Who controls access to the vault?"

"Director's office. Renner signs off on every request personally." She hesitated. "There's something else. I requested the file last week. Standard thesis request, my advisor countersigned. It came back denied yesterday. No reason given. Just—" she scrolled, and turned the screen again, "*pending review by the director's office.*"

Pending review. In the same week that a stranger from Vellmouth delivered a dead woman's postcard. In the same week that Aldous Renner learned, through channels I didn't yet fully understand, that a janitor with a famous name was holding a tin.

Ivo, who had been following all of it with the expression of a man watching a storm come in over flat water, reached over and closed the laptop gently, like tucking it into bed.

"Then you'd best decide quickly," he said, "what the building is going to say. And who's going to help it say it."

Outside, beyond the fogged glass, the conservatory was waking up—students crossing the courtyard, staff badges bobbing, the great indifferent machine of the place grinding toward another ordinary day with Aldous Renner at the top of it, charming and generous and utterly unafraid.

I looked down at the postcard in my lap, at the last line of my sister's life.

*Make the building say my name.*

"All right," I said, mostly to the tomatoes. "Let's find out what he offers when I say no."
