## 4. Night Shifts and Half-Words

The dictionary came home with me the way most things come home with janitors: in a mop bucket, under legitimate rags, entirely by accident.

That's what I told myself, anyway. The conservatory library — three shelves of donated books nobody had opened since the millennium — sat at the end of the orchid corridor, and among the water-damaged bird guides and a 1987 annual report someone had shelved a *Concise Dictionary of Kessari-English*, its spine cracked, its previous owner's name inked inside the cover: *E. Varga*. I stood there holding it for a full minute while my floor went unwet. Then I put it in the bucket, because that is what thieves do, and I have always been an honest woman about my dishonesty.

Kessari. Even thinking it felt like speaking through a hand over my mouth. At home we'd called it "the kitchen language," which was Marek's joke, and like most of Marek's jokes it was a small cruelty wearing a party hat. English downstairs for the neighbors, Kessari upstairs where the pots clattered and my mother sang off-key. By the time I was seven the singing had stopped. By nine I answered everything in English and my father would say, *good girl*, and mean it as praise, and I let him mean it that way because praise was scarce currency in our house.

Now here I was at two in the morning, perched on an overturned bucket behind the fernery with a stolen dictionary open across my knees, trying to read one sentence like it was a lock and I was feeling for tumblers in the dark.

I knew four words immediately. That sounds impressive until you learn they were *bread*, *salt*, *door*, and *mother* — the entire survival vocabulary of a child who'd been hurriedly de-Kessaried before school each morning. But the sentence had *mother* in it. Third word from the left, unmistakable, and seeing it in her handwriting did something to my chest that no amount of mopping has ever fixed.

Because it *was* her handwriting. That was the second shock of the week, after Ilse Varga herself. The letter wasn't typed or printed; it was written, in the looping leftward slant I knew from recipe cards — the plum cake card still lives in my cutlery drawer, though I've never once made the cake, because making it would require saying the ingredients aloud and half of them only exist in Kessari. My mother had written this sentence. Which meant she had written it knowing it might outlive her, which meant it was testimony, not correspondence. People don't leave recipes in code.

I copied the sentence onto the back of a work order for a broken radiator valve — fitting — and began the slow archaeology.

*Vessel*, maybe. Or *burden*. The first word had too many letters for either and I kept losing my place because the dictionary was organized by some lunatic's idea of usefulness, alphabetical but with the diacritics treated as optional suggestions. I worked until four, until the sky over the glass roof went the color of dishwater, until my eyes ached. I got perhaps a third of it. Enough to know it was addressed to someone. Not to me — the grammar was wrong for family, formal, the way you write to a stranger you owe something to.

Enough to know I couldn't stop.

* * *

The archive on Wexler Street smells of dust and ambition, which is to say it smells of every person who has ever come in certain they'll find the truth and leave having found paperwork instead.

I had been coming for eleven days. The archivist, a patient man named Dobbs who wore cardigans the color of surrender, had stopped asking what I was researching and started simply leaving the relevant boxes on my table. This is either kindness or pity. In my experience they arrive in the same envelope.

My father's name was András Varga. I will not soften it: he was arrested in the spring of '61, along with two other men from the printing collective, and the other two were sentenced and transported and one of them died within the year. My father was released in six weeks. Six weeks. I spent my childhood being told this was luck, and I spent my adulthood learning that luck has co-authors.

The Coldharbour file was thin, deliberately thin, the kind of thin that requires effort. Emigration sponsorship approved. Character references attached. And then a single line in a border official's hand, initialed twice, that read — I translated it three times to be sure — *"cooperation confirmed re: associates."*

Associates. My father died believing himself abandoned by his friends. He wrote letters from the camp that my mother keeps in a biscuit tin, letters asking what he had done, why no one would answer. He died not knowing that the man who sponsored the Coldharbour family's passage — Emil Coldharbour, respectable, generous, mourned last Tuesday by a roomful of people performing grief in polished English — had purchased that passage with my father's name.

And the daughter of that man had stood at his funeral looking like a ghost who'd taken a job as cleaning staff, and when Ilse Varga said the old name aloud — *the* name, the forbidden one — Sebastian Coldharbour had flinched like a struck animal.

I hadn't planned to speak to her yet. I had planned, if I'm honest, to make her feel what I felt: the slow discovery, document by document, that your parent was a stranger with your face. But then I saw her hands around the envelope I gave her, and I saw that she recognized the handwriting, and I understood that she had been robbed too — differently, less bloodily, but robbed. Emil Coldharbour hadn't just bought his safety. He'd bought his children's ignorance, wholesale, and raised them on the proceeds.

Still. Knowing she was also a victim didn't stop me from hating her a little. Grief is not fair-minded. It wants a face, and hers was available.

Dobbs appeared at my elbow with tea I hadn't asked for. "Closing soon, miss."

"Five minutes." I was staring at the manifest of the ship — the *Meridian Star*, out of Vessa, carrying the Coldharbour family east under their new name. Under *my* enemy's new name. There was a passenger list, and I had read it forty times, and today I noticed for the first time that a child's entry had been amended. Inked over. The original name still legible beneath, faint as breath on glass.

A girl. Age six. Traveling under a name that had been crossed out before the ship even sailed.

I sat back. Somewhere in this city, that girl emptied bins at midnight and didn't know her own history, and I had handed her the one document her father had spent thirty years keeping from her, and walked away.

I should have stayed to watch her read it. Instead I found myself wanting — furiously, inconveniently — to know what she'd do now.

* * *

She was at the conservatory.

I want to be clear that I didn't invite this knowledge. I was emptying the fernery bins at 1 a.m. when I saw, through the fogged panes of the reading room, a figure at the long table under a desk lamp — coat still on, hair pinned up with what looked like a pencil, bent over papers. My first thought was *burglar*. My second thought was *she has better posture than a burglar*. My third thought arrived late and unwelcome: *she knows where I work.*

Of course she knew. She'd pressed the letter into my hands outside the crematorium; she'd watched me walk back toward the staff entrance in my uniform. Ilse Varga had known exactly which door I'd disappear through, and she'd waited eleven days, and now she was sitting in my conservatory — my glasshouse, the one where I played Chopin at nine years old to a roomful of people who pronounced my name carefully, like something imported and fragile — reading God knew what.

I should have gone in. Announced myself. *Can I help you, miss, we're closed.* Janitor voice, the flattest dialect I speak.

Instead I stood in the dark of the orchid corridor with a wet rag going cold in my hand and watched her. She read the way my mother used to pray: completely, lips moving slightly, as if the words required supervision. Once she stopped, took out a pen, and wrote something with the force of someone stabbing a bill of fare. Once she looked up, directly at the ceiling, directly at nothing, and I stepped back into the shadow of the lemon tree before my body decided to be seen without consulting me.

What was she building? A case? An accusation? I had the sentence; she had archives. We were two women doing the same jigsaw from opposite ends, and neither of us had shown the other the picture on the box.

Here is what I learned about Ilse Varga that night, watching: she doesn't fidget. Whatever she carried, she carried it the way load-bearing walls carry — silently, structurally, and you only find out how much depends on it when it comes down.

She left at three. I heard the side door, heard her footsteps cross the gravel, heard a car start and fade. Then I went into the reading room and looked at what she'd left behind, because I am a janitor and snooping is technically my job description.

One page, torn from a notebook, folded once and forgotten — or not forgotten. Left. On it, in a small fierce hand, the same sentence I'd been decoding, transcribed in full, with a translation forming beneath it word by word, half-finished, half-right.

And next to her translation, a note in English, underlined twice:

*Ask her what she traded. Ask her what it cost us.*

Not *ask him*. Her father was dead and beyond questions. *Her.* As if the debt had passed down like eye color, like the kitchen language, like grief.

I stood there holding the page, and the heating chose that moment to shudder and die — a great metallic sigh through the pipes, the sound the conservatory makes when it gives up for the night — and in the sudden cold I understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: frost was coming, and the radiators were failing, and tomorrow night I would be working by lamplight among dying plants whether Marek liked it or not.

Second: Ilse Varga wanted a reckoning, and she had decided the reckoning lived in me.

I put her page in my pocket next to my half-translation, next to my mother's sentence, next to the dictionary with E. Varga's name in the cover — and I realized I had been carrying her father's book against my chest all week without knowing whose it was.

The wound had two sides after all. We'd just been licking opposite ones.
