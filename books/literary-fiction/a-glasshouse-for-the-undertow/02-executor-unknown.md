## 2. Executor, Unknown

The county probate office smelled of toner and old rain, and it was there, on a Tuesday in October, that Silas Halloran's careful life began to come apart at the seams.

He had dressed for the occasion the way he dressed for everything now: deliberately unremarkable. A gray sweater his mother would have called "serviceable," which had been her word for anything she intended to quietly replace. He'd rehearsed his errand in the car — *I'm here about the Halloran estate, I'm the son, I just need the paperwork* — and he had not rehearsed the part where the clerk behind the counter looked up at him and said, without any particular malice:

"Oh, you're *the* Halloran? Wren Calloway's been asking after you."

The name went through him like a note played on a string he'd thought was cut years ago.

"She has?" he said, with the composure of a man whose hands had gone cold under the counter.

"Executor," the clerk said, tapping a folder with a fingernail painted the color of a stop sign. "Calloway, Calloway & Marsh. She's handling the whole thing. Sweet girl. Very thorough." A pause, weighted like a rest in a score. "You two know each other?"

"We grew up together," Silas said, which was true, and also the smallest possible container for what they had grown up being.

The clerk gave him directions to the conference room at the end of the hall, and Silas walked them the way he used to walk out onto a stage: one foot in front of the other, trusting muscle memory to carry what nerve could not.

* * *

Wren Calloway stood when he came in, and the first thing Silas noticed — before her charcoal suit, before the reading glasses pushed up into her hair, before the folder squared precisely to the table's edge — was that she looked at him directly.

Nobody did that anymore. The conservatory staff had perfected a kind of courteous blindness; even the ones who remembered him had learned to look slightly left of his face, as though he were a painting hung too high to study comfortably. But Wren looked at him the way she had always looked at everything: fully, without apology, with an attention that made you want to either perform or flee.

"Silas," she said. "Thank you for coming."

"Wren." His voice came out lower than he intended. He cleared his throat. "They told me you were — I didn't know you'd gone into law."

"I know. It surprised everyone, including me." She gestured to the chair across from her. "Please sit. This shouldn't take long, but there are things we need to go over."

He sat. Up close, she was both exactly the same and entirely changed — the same quick dark eyes, the same habit of tucking her pen behind her ear and then forgetting it was there, but older around the eyes in a way that suggested she'd spent five years doing hard things without complaint. There was a faint callus on the inside of her right hand where a violin bow used to sit, and he caught himself staring at it and made himself stop.

"So," she said, opening the folder. "Your mother named me executor three years ago. She was very clear about her wishes. The house goes to auction — that's scheduled for the twenty-third. Contents are to be inventoried and catalogued first, per the will. She wanted—" Wren paused, and for the first time something flickered behind the precision, some small tremor under the professional surface. "She wanted certain items set aside. For family."

"I'm the family," Silas said.

"You're the family," Wren agreed, and wrote nothing down, which somehow made it more solemn than if she had.

He watched her work. She moved through the paperwork the way she'd once moved through a score — no wasted motion, every mark accounted for, but underneath it all a current of feeling she kept strictly leashed. When she slid the auction notice across the table, her fingers were steady. When she said the words *"as-is condition"* and *"sold to the highest bidder,"* her voice didn't change at all.

"The buyer," Silas said. "Do you know who it is?"

"There's an interested party. An offer's already been made contingent on the sale clearing." She hesitated. "There's something else you should hear from me rather than from a flyer."

"That's how I found out about all this," he admitted. "A flyer. On a corkboard. Between a lost cat and a yoga class."

Something almost like a smile touched her mouth and withdrew. "Then I'm sorry. You deserved better than a corkboard." She folded her hands. "The interested party intends to demolish."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when they're waiting to see what you'll do. Somewhere down the hall a fax machine chirped. Outside the window, October light lay flat and silver on the parking lot, and Silas sat with the image of the house — the blue shutters his mother repainted every other summer, the porch where she'd set up chairs for the neighbors during his practice hours so they could listen through the screens, the piano in the front room with its honest, stubborn heart — and tried to imagine it as a vacant lot.

"All right," he said finally.

Wren blinked. "All right?"

"It's her house. Or it was. She can't be hurt by a bulldozer, and I can't afford sentiment." He heard himself say it and recognized the sentence as armor, each clause riveted to the next. "What do you need from me?"

For a moment she just studied him, and he felt that look all the way down, past the gray sweater, past the janitor's lanyard still in his pocket because he'd forgotten to remove it, past everything he'd built to be invisible in.

"What do I need," she repeated softly. Then, briskly, before he could decide whether she'd been amused or grieved: "An inventory. Every item of significance, catalogued and appraised. Normally I'd hire a service, but—" she turned a page, and he saw his mother's handwriting on it, looping and sure, *Wrennie will know what matters* — "she asked that it be done by someone who understood what things meant. Not what they're worth. What they mean."

"She asked for you specifically?"

"She asked for someone who knew the house." Wren's pen hovered. "But if you're volunteering, I can't think of anyone better qualified."

And there it was — the trap and the gift in a single sentence. To walk back into that house was to walk back into every room where his mother had taught him scales, where she'd fallen asleep in the armchair during his practicing and he'd kept playing softer and softer until the music became a lullaby for her instead of an exercise for him. To do it alongside Wren Calloway was to stand in the light of someone who had known him before, who remembered the boy who played Chopin on the porch in bare feet, who might at any moment ask the question everyone else had learned not to ask: *what happened to you?*

"I'll do it," he said, before caution could file an objection.

"Good." She pulled a form toward her, then paused. "Silas."

"Yes?"

"How have you been?"

It was such a simple question. It was the simplest question in the world, and it undid him more thoroughly than any interrogation could have, because it wasn't a formality — she actually wanted to know, and he had no answer prepared that wasn't either a lie or a confession.

"I'm keeping busy," he said.

Her eyes dropped, briefly, to the lanyard peeking from his pocket. *Halloran, S. — Facilities.* She said nothing about it. That was the devastating part, he realized later. Not what she asked, but what she mercifully didn't.

"Well," she said, "you'll be busy again. The house is full. Your mother never threw anything away." A breath of warmth entered her voice. "I found forty years of concert programs in the hallway closet when I was twelve, hiding from my mother. She caught me reading them and made me tea and told me the story behind every single one. It took four hours. I was supposed to be somewhere else entirely and I never regretted missing it."

"She liked telling those stories."

"She liked *you* being in them." Wren said it lightly, already moving to the next page, and perhaps she didn't notice what she'd said, or perhaps she noticed perfectly and chose to let it lie there between them, unexamined, like a coin on a railway track.

* * *

They worked through the logistics for another hour — keys, access codes, the appraisal timeline, a locksmith appointment she'd already scheduled because of course she had, Wren had always been the one who read ahead in the book while the rest of the ensemble was still finding their places. When it was done, she walked him out through the county building's echoing lobby, their footsteps falling into an accidental rhythm, and he was struck by the memory of walking beside her to a hundred rehearsals exactly like this, shoulder to shoulder, the whole day arranged around whatever music waited at the end of the walk.

Outside, the wind came off the harbor carrying salt and cold. She stopped on the steps.

"I'm here Tuesdays and Thursdays," she said. "The house key is with the locksmith until Friday. After that—" she held out a brass key on a plain ring, and he took it, and the metal was warm from her pocket, which felt like information he had no right to have. "Take your time in there. The inventory deadline is generous. She made sure of that."

"Of course she did."

"Silas." She paused on the bottom step, half-turned, the wind pulling at her coat. "Whatever you find in that house — and you'll find things, she kept everything — I hope you'll let it be what it is. Not what you're afraid it is."

He wanted to ask what she meant. He wanted to ask a great many things, among them whether she ever still played, whether she remembered the duet they'd performed at seventeen with the power out and the audience holding phone lights aloft like a field of strange stars, whether she knew that he had written her a letter once and what had become of it. Instead he said, "Thursday, then. Nine o'clock."

"Nine o'clock," she agreed, and walked to her car, and he stood on the courthouse steps watching until the taillights turned the corner, aware that he was smiling at nothing like a man who had forgotten the expression and was testing whether it still fit.

That night he lay awake in his apartment above the laundromat, listening to the dryers turn below like slow metronomes, and understood that something in him had shifted irreversibly. For five years he had arranged his life into a rest — a long held silence, safe and airless. Now the silence had a hole in it shaped like a person, and through it he could hear, absurdly, unmistakably, the first bars of the Schubert they used to play, the one in E-flat, the one that began quietly and refused to stay that way.

He should have felt afraid. What he felt instead was worse and better: the reckless, rising hope of a man who has spent years underwater and has just seen, far above, the bright ripple of the surface.

On Thursday morning, he put the brass key in his pocket, and went home to the house that was waiting to tell him everything.
