## 3. Inventory of Small Mercies

The key to the Halloran house was heavier than it looked. Brass, cold as a coin held too long in a pocket, and it turned in the lock with a sound like a small apology.

Silas stood on the porch a moment before going in. He had told himself the drive over would be the hard part — the town shrinking around him as he passed the conservatory, the bakery that used to give him day-old croissants for free because he was *the boy who played*, the pharmacy where his mother bought her pills and where he now bought rubber gloves and floor wax. But the drive had been easy. It was the door that was difficult, because doors are honest things. They open or they don't. They don't pretend to be anything other than what they are.

He went in.

Dust hung in the light like something the house was still deciding to say. The air smelled of cedar and old paper and, faintly, of lemons — some cleaning product his mother had used until the last year, when she'd stopped having the strength to care whether the house smelled like effort. Silas set down his clipboard, his tape measure, the little price stickers Wren had given him in a neat roll, and stood in the front hall doing nothing at all, which felt, strangely, like a form of prayer.

"Right," he said aloud, to no one. "Small mercies."

That was what he'd decided to call the inventory. Not because the objects were merciful, exactly, but because cataloguing them meant touching each one once, deliberately, instead of being ambushed by them later at random. Grief, he had learned, was mostly an ambush. You could not prevent it but you could sometimes schedule it, the way you scheduled a dentist appointment: Tuesday, ten o'clock, we will now feel everything about the coat rack.

He started in the kitchen, because the kitchen was the least dangerous room.

* * *

The kitchen yielded: one toaster with a crumb tray full of crumbs fossilized into geology; eleven mismatched mugs, three of which said things like WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, gifts from students' parents who had clearly stopped trying; a recipe box containing forty cards in his mother's slanted hand, of which perhaps six were actual recipes and the rest were notes to herself — *call Wren's mother re: recital dress*, *S. practices too fast, slow him down gently*, *buy more lemons.*

He read that third card twice. *Slow him down gently.* She had written it the way she'd said everything — without drama, as if the whole art of loving a child were a matter of tempo.

His mother had taught piano in this house for thirty-one years. The front room had been the studio, its bay window facing the sea, and the schedule had been taped inside the hall closet door: Mondays the beginners, Tuesdays the serious ones, Wednesdays reserved for the duets, which was how a nine-year-old Wren Calloway had come to sit beside him on the bench every week, close enough that their elbows negotiated borders like neighboring countries.

He wrote *recipe box, tin, $4* on his clipboard and then crossed out the four dollars, because it seemed obscene, and left the line blank.

* * *

The studio was next, and he made himself go there second rather than saving it, because he knew himself well enough by now to know that saved-for-last rooms fester.

The piano sat under a sheet like a patient waiting for a diagnosis. He didn't lift the sheet. Not yet. That was scheduled for later, possibly never. Instead he inventoried the sheet music, which lived in a cabinet his father had built before Silas was born — oak, glass doors, organized by composer with a rigor his mother applied to nothing else in her life. Bach. Beethoven. Debussy, whose spines were soft with handling. And at the back, a folder marked in her handwriting: **S. & W. — DUETS**.

He did not open it. He wrote *folder of duet arrangements* on the clipboard and felt his own handwriting come out shakier than usual, as though the pen had opinions.

On top of the cabinet sat the metronome. It was a child's metronome, painted red, shaped vaguely like a bird, bought at a church rummage sale when he was five. His mother had wound it and set it ticking on the first day of his first lesson and said, *There. Now you have a heart that keeps better time than yours.* He picked it up. The winding mechanism still worked; he could feel the spring coiled and eager inside, wanting to be used. He set it down without winding it. Some things you didn't start unless you intended to finish.

* * *

In the afternoon the light moved across the floorboards the way it always had, unhurried, and Silas found himself in his mother's bedroom, which he had been avoiding with the discipline of a man walking past a bar he used to drink in.

Her bed was made. Of course it was. Even in the last months, when getting from the chair to the bed took her ten minutes and cost her visible arithmetic, she had made the bed, because — she'd said — *a made bed is a letter to tomorrow saying you expect it to be decent.*

The nightstand drawer stuck. He worked it loose and found the ordinary archaeology: reading glasses, a paperback romance with a cover featuring a man who appeared to be shirtless on a horse, a hairbrush wrapped in somebody else's long dark hair — Wren's, from some sleepover fifteen years gone, kept the way his mother kept everything, which was completely.

And beneath all of it, a shoebox.

He knew the shoebox. He had known it existed since he was twelve, when he'd gone looking for wrapping paper and found letters instead — dozens of them, envelopes addressed in various hands, none of them stamped, none of them mailed. His mother had caught him holding one and taken it from him gently, the way you take scissors from a toddler, and said, *These are drafts, sweetheart. Some things need more than one try.* He had assumed they were letters to his father, dead eight years by then, and had built a small private shrine of sadness around the idea.

Now he lifted the lid.

They were letters to everyone. To his father. To her sister in Phoenix, whom she hadn't spoken to since 1998 over an inheritance dispute so petty both parties had forgotten the original grievance and were fighting purely on principle. To former students. To Wren's mother. Each one finished, addressed, sealed in some cases — and unsent. A drawer, a box, a lifetime of sentences composed and then withheld. His mother, who had taught him that music was just saying the true thing out loud in time, had spent her whole life writing true things and putting them in boxes.

He laughed, actually laughed, alone in her bedroom, and the laugh came out wet. *Oh, Mum,* he thought. *You absolute hypocrite.* It was the most loving thing he had thought about anyone in years.

Then he reached the bottom of the box, and stopped laughing.

* * *

The envelope was addressed to **Wren Calloway**, in his own handwriting.

He knew it instantly the way you know your own face in a dark window — the way the *W* leaned, the way he'd crossed his sevens. Dated nineteen years ago, the summer after his final recital. He remembered writing it. He remembered the desk lamp, the moths against the screen, the way he'd written *Dear Wren* and then sat for two hours before managing the second sentence, and how the second sentence had turned out to be a confession, love and blame braided together so tightly he couldn't tell which strand was which. *I loved you the whole time we played,* it said somewhere in the middle. *And I think I hated the piano for taking up the space where I should have said so.*

He had sealed it. He had walked it as far as the mailbox at the corner of Shore Road. And then he had stood there in the dark like an idiot, holding it, hearing his mother's metronome in his head, and walked home again, and put it in his desk drawer, and two weeks later his mother had gotten sick, and the letter had become impossible, and then irrelevant, and then simply a thing that had happened to another person, a boy named Silas who used to play.

Except it wasn't in his desk drawer. It was here. In her box. Which meant —

He sat down on the edge of her made bed, the letter unopened in his hands, and did the math he had been refusing to do for five years.

She had intercepted it. She must have found it — mothers find things, it is their single greatest professional skill — and instead of mailing it or confronting him, she had put it in her box of drafts, filed among her own unsent truths, as if to say: *I know. I have one too. We are the same, you and I, and I am keeping yours safe until you're ready, and I am sorry, and I love you, and buy more lemons.*

Or she had taken it to stop it. To protect his future from his own heart, the way she'd slowed him down gently at the keyboard. He couldn't know. She was not available for questions. That was the entire problem with the dead; their motives stayed beautifully ambiguous forever, and you were left to choose the version that let you sleep.

He chose neither version yet. He held the envelope and felt the dread move through him like weather — because the letter existed, because Wren would eventually stand in this room, because Wren was the executor and executors opened drawers, and because a man cannot inventory a house honestly while hiding a letter in his jacket.

Which is what he did. He put it in his jacket, inside pocket, over the heart, which was either sentimental or anatomically accurate, and he went back downstairs and wrote on his clipboard, in a steady hand:

*Nightstand drawer — personal papers. Removed for privacy. Will return to estate.*

It was even technically true.

* * *

At dusk he allowed himself the studio again, and the sheet, and the piano underneath it.

He lifted the cloth. The piano was exactly as out of tune as he remembered — the middle register sour, the high notes thin as winter light — and exactly as beautiful, which is to say it looked like every good thing that had ever happened to him had happened on top of it. He pressed middle C. The note came out flat and wheezy and utterly itself, and something in his chest unlocked one notch, quietly, like a stiff window finally giving.

He didn't play. He just stood there with one finger on the key, letting the sound die out on its own schedule, and thought about the final recital — the one where his hands had stopped mid-phrase in front of two hundred people, the silence afterward longer than any piece of music ever written, the moment the undertow took him. His mother had died believing that silence was temporary. He had arranged his whole life to prove her wrong.

Outside, gravel crunched in the drive.

Through the bay window he saw a car he recognized, and a woman stepping out of it with a briefcase and the particular posture of someone arriving to do a job — and Silas Halloran, janitor, prodigy, coward, son, stood in his dead mother's studio with an unsent love letter over his heart and realized he had about eleven seconds to decide what kind of man was going to answer that door.
