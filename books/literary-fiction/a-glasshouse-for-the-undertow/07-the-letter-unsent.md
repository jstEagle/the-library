## 7. The Letter, Unsent

The drawer stuck the way old drawers do—reluctantly, with a wooden groan like a complaint being lodged—and then gave all at once, spilling its contents into Silas's hands before he was ready to receive them.

He had been cataloguing the study for three days. He'd developed a system: index cards, a pencil worn to a stub, a shorthand of his own devising that he suspected no one but Wren would ever read. *Desk, oak, water-ringed. Lamp, brass, nonfunctional. Globe, terrestrial, missing Australia.* The house was surrendering itself lot by lot, and he had made himself into its clerk, because a clerk could touch anything without it meaning anything.

But this drawer had been locked. He'd noted it on day one—*desk drawer, locked, key unknown*—and moved on, and then last night, unable to sleep in his rented room above the hardware store, he'd remembered that his mother kept spare keys in the flour tin. Of course she did. She kept everything somewhere sensible and secret, and the flour tin had been her favorite vault since he was small enough to stand on a chair to reach it.

The tin was still in the kitchen cabinet, still half full of flour gone hard as plaster, and inside, wrapped in wax paper like something perishable, was a ring of keys.

So now, on the third morning, the drawer opened, and Silas stood in the yellow light of the study holding a bundle of envelopes tied with butcher's twine.

Unsent letters. A dozen of them, maybe more, in various hands and various stages of composition. His mother's handwriting mostly—he recognized the tall, impatient capitals, the way her *e*'s leaned forward as if hurrying somewhere. Letters to doctors. Letters to the conservatory's bursar, arguing about fees. One letter to his father, addressed but never stamped, which told him everything about how those conversations had actually gone.

And at the bottom of the bundle, folded in thirds the way he still folded letters, because some habits outlive their reasons—

His own handwriting.

Nineteen years old. He knew it instantly, the way you know your own face in an unexpected mirror. The ink had faded from blue to a gray like weather. The envelope bore Wren Calloway's name in his younger self's careful, showing-off hand, and no stamp, and no address beyond her name, as if at nineteen he had believed that love alone could route a letter.

Silas sat down in his mother's desk chair, which complained under him exactly as it always had, and unfolded the letter, and read what he had written eleven years ago.

---

*Dear Wren,*

*I have started this letter four times and burned three of them in the sink, which Mother thinks is a chemistry experiment gone wrong, so let's let her keep thinking that.*

*I'm writing because after Saturday I can't pretend anymore. When we played the Schubert—the F minor, the one where the second piano comes in late like someone arriving at a party already forgiven—I looked over at you during the rest before the coda and you were looking at the ceiling and your foot wasn't keeping time for once, and I thought: she feels it too. Whatever this is. This thing that isn't friendship because friendship doesn't make my hands shake before we play, and isn't rivalry because I would hand you every competition I ever won if you asked, and isn't anything I have a word for.*

*Here is the part I'll probably burn: I think I love you. Not the way the boys at school talk about it. The way the Schubert is written—two voices that only make sense together, that keep handing the melody back and forth like it's too precious for either one to hold alone.*

*And here is the part I definitely should burn: I blame you. Not really. But a little. Because you're leaving for London in September and you asked me—do you remember, on the seawall—you asked me why I wouldn't apply anywhere, and I said the money, and you said "the money," in that voice, like you knew I was lying, and you were right. I'm not applying because if I follow you and fail, I'll have failed at the thing that has me. And if I stay and you go, at least the failure will be mine, chosen, like a coat I picked out myself.*

*I don't expect anything. Burn this if you want. I just couldn't let you leave thinking the silence between us was indifference. It was never indifference. It was the opposite of indifference, which apparently looks identical from the outside.*

*Yours, entirely and inconveniently,*
*Silas*

---

Silas set the letter down on the blotter with both hands, the way you set down something breakable, or explosive.

He remembered writing it. He remembered the sink, the matches, the smell of singed paper that his mother had indeed attributed to chemistry. He remembered deciding, finally, at one in the morning, to mail it—to walk it down to the post box on Harbor Street in his slippers like a man committing a small crime—and he remembered nothing after that. That was the strange thing. There was a hole in the memory where the mailing should have been, smooth-edged, like a stone worn by water.

Because he had never mailed it. His mother had taken it.

He understood it now with terrible clarity, the whole mechanism assembling itself in his chest. He must have left it on the hall table for the morning post. She must have found it, read it—of course she read it; she was dying and curious and protective in equal, enormous measures—and she had seen her son about to nail his heart to a girl who was leaving for London, a girl whose own future was already chartered, and she had done what mothers do. She had intercepted. She had filed it away in a locked drawer with the other unspeakable correspondence, and she had said nothing, and in saying nothing she had let him believe that his courage had simply evaporated overnight, that he had chosen cowardice, when in fact his choice had been stolen from him before he could make it.

Five years later, when the unraveling came—the recital, the empty bench, the story everyone told—it had grown from this soil. He had built his whole ruin on the belief that he was a man who couldn't say the true thing. And the true thing had been said, at nineteen, in ink, by his own hand. It had merely never arrived.

The fury came first. It came up through him hot and totalizing, fury at her, at the presumption of it, at the sheer maternal arrogance of deciding the shape of his life from a sickbed—*I know better than you what you can survive*—and he gripped the arms of the chair and let it move through him like weather, because he had learned in five years of mopping floors that feelings are like the tide; you don't argue with them, you just keep your footing.

And then, underneath the fury, arriving not afterward but simultaneously, the way grief and gratitude share a bed: forgiveness. Because he could see her doing it. He could see her exactly—thin in her dressing gown, hands spotted, reading her son's naked heart by lamplight and knowing she would not live to help him through whatever came of sending it. Knowing Wren was leaving. Knowing Silas would follow or shatter or both. She had been a woman running out of time trying to buy her son more of it, and she had spent the only currency she had left, which was secrecy.

*You protected me,* he said aloud to the empty study, and his voice cracked in the middle of it. *You protected me right off the stage.*

The letter lay on the blotter between them—between him and her, though she was dust and the house was sold. He touched the fold. Somewhere in London there was a woman who had crossed an ocean believing herself unloved, or loved badly, or loved in some unnameable gray register, because a dying woman had slipped a letter into a drawer.

He put it in his jacket pocket. Over his heart, which was sentimental, and he allowed it.

* * *

Wren came at four, carrying a legal pad and a thermos, looking like someone who had slept in her car and would deny it under oath.

"Inventory status," she said, by way of good afternoon.

"Study's done." He handed her the index cards. Their fingers didn't touch, and he noticed the width of the gap, and hated that he noticed.

She flipped through the cards, frowning at his shorthand. "*Globe, terrestrial, missing Australia.* Poetic."

"Accurate."

"Silas." She lowered the cards. Something in her face had shifted—the lawyer retreating behind the person. "There's something I need to tell you, and I'd rather say it standing up."

He braced. In his experience, sentences beginning *there's something I need to tell you* ended either in death or demolition, and his mother had already used up the first.

"The buyer's timeline is firm," she said. "Demolition permits are issued. The thirtieth."

"That's nine days."

"I know."

"I wanted to ask—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I wanted to ask you to extend it. Two weeks. Maybe less. There are things in this house that need more than nine days. Not things." He touched his pocket, absurdly. "Time. There are things in this house that need time."

Wren looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her do it—the thing she had always done, the thing he had loved her for across a hundred rehearsal rooms: she listened with her whole body, as though listening were a duet too.

"I can't," she said.

"You're the executor."

"I'm aware of what I am." Her jaw tightened. "The date can't be moved. It's not—there are reasons. Complications."

"What complications? Wren, it's a house. Houses don't have schedules, people impose them—"

"This one has one." She said it flatly, finally, the way you close a door. And then, worse, softer: "Please don't ask me why. Not yet."

*Not yet.* The phrase hung in the dusty air between them, and Silas felt the letter press against his chest like a second heartbeat. *Not yet* was what his mother had said about the will. *Not yet* was what he had said to himself for eleven years about everything that mattered. Everyone in his life, it seemed, was protecting him with postponements, and the protection was indistinguishable from theft.

"Okay," he said, because he was tired, and because he had learned that retreat is sometimes just patience wearing a disguise. "Nine days."

"Eight and a half," Wren said, and almost smiled, and gathered her pad, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame—not looking back, but not quite leaving either.

"Wren."

She waited.

"There's a letter," he said. "In this house. Written a long time ago. Addressed to you."

Her shoulders went very still.

"It never got mailed," he said. "It got intercepted. And I think—" His voice did the cracking thing again, and he steadied it against the doorframe of the sentence. "I think when you hear what's in it, a lot of things are going to make sense. To both of us. Things that maybe explain why you can't move the date. Or don't want to."

She turned then, slowly, and her eyes were bright and frightened and nineteen years old all at once.

"Silas," she said, "what did your mother do?"

And he reached into his pocket, and drew out the letter, and held it in the space between them like a duet about to begin.
