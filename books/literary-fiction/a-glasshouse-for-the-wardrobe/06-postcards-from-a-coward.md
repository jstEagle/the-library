## 6. Postcards from a Coward

The shoebox lived on the top shelf of Coral's closet, behind the winter coats she never wore in a city that had forgotten what real cold was, and for twelve years she had taken it down only twice a year—once in April, around her mother's birthday, and once in October, around the anniversary of the fire, though she would have denied under oath that either date meant anything to her.

Tonight she took it down because Tomas had asked her a question she couldn't answer.

*Why postcards?*

She had given him some answer in the moment—something about the smallness of them, the way a postcard's limited space forgave you for saying little—but walking home through the wet streets with her collar up, she'd known it was a lie dressed as an explanation. The truth was uglier. She'd chosen postcards because they were public objects. Because anything written on their faces could be read by any postal worker, any neighbor, any stranger who happened to glance over her shoulder at a café table. She had spent twelve years writing to her mother in a voice designed to be overheard by everyone except her mother.

Now she sat cross-legged on her bed with the shoebox open in front of her and the lamp turned up as bright as it would go, and she faced the evidence.

Carbon copies. That was the other thing she'd never told anyone. In the first year, back when she was still living in the apartment above the laundromat on Delancey, she had bought a pad of carbon paper from a stationery shop that smelled of glue and dust, and she had begun slipping a sheet beneath each postcard before she wrote, so that every word she mailed went out into the world twice: once toward the house that no longer existed, and once into this box, where it waited for her like a debt.

Four hundred and thirty-seven copies. Postcard number 438 was still out there somewhere, traveling its slow way toward a ruin.

She picked up the first one. February 2012. Her handwriting had been different then—looser, angrier, the letters slanting forward as if trying to outrun themselves.

*Mom — I got your address from Aunt Lucille. I hope you're well. I'm working now, editing for a small press, nothing glamorous. The city is fine. Winter is fine.*

She read it three times. *Fine. Fine. Fine.* Three times in four lines, like a woman knocking on a door and then running away before it opened. Even at twenty-four, even alone in that apartment with the radiator clanking and her heart still raw from the last phone call—the one where she'd said things about her father, about the marriage, about all the years of watching her mother fold herself smaller and smaller until she fit inside the shape other people expected—even then, she had already learned the family language. The language of *fine*. The dialect of almost.

Her mother had been fluent in it her whole life.

That was the thought that arrived now, sitting on the edge of the bed with the lamplight pooling yellow on the cardboard, and it arrived not as a realization but as a verdict. She sounded like her. Not in the handwriting—that was her father's cramped script, inherited through his side—but in the architecture of the sentences. The way she built walls out of weather reports. The way she mentioned *the city* instead of her loneliness, *work* instead of her ambition, *Aunt Lucille* instead of the question that actually lived in her throat, which was: *Why did you let him win? Why did you stay? Why am I angry at you for surviving the only way you knew how?*

Coral set the first card down and pulled another from the stack. June 2013.

*I saw a woman at the market who looked like you. Same coat, green. I almost said something. I didn't. Hope the garden is doing well this year.*

The garden. God. She had known, even then, that the garden was the one true thing between them—the glasshouse her mother had tended since Coral was small, the orchids arranged along the benches like a congregation—and so she had reached for it the way you reach for the only honest word in a dishonest paragraph. But look how she'd done it. *Hope the garden is doing well.* A pleasantry. A closing line, the kind of sentence you write to a colleague. She had taken the most sacred ground she shared with her mother and paved it over with small talk.

And underneath it, unwritten, the actual message: *I saw a woman in your coat and I missed you so badly I had to stand in the produce aisle and breathe until my hands stopped shaking.*

She had never written that. Not once in twelve years. Not in 438 postcards.

Coral pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw constellations. The room felt too warm, too close, the lamp suddenly accusatory. She thought about Tomas sitting in that half-restored house with his careful hands and his archivist's patience, cataloguing her mother's letters while hers sat in a shoebox across town, and she wondered what he would make of the two collections laid side by side. Two women, twelve years apart in time but apparently made of the same material, both writing and both refusing to say. Her mother's letters sealed behind a wardrobe panel, returned unopened. Her own postcards sent faithfully to a dead address, which amounted to the same thing. A conversation conducted entirely in the negative space between two people who had perfected the art of being unreachable.

*You're doing it again,* she told herself. *Even now. Even alone, you're narrating yourself as the tragic daughter instead of asking the simple question: why didn't you ever go?*

Because that was the question, wasn't it. Not why she wrote postcards—she could defend the postcards, they were a ritual, a lifeline, a way of keeping the channel open without risking what might come back through it. But why, in twelve years, had she never once driven the four hours to the house itself? She had the address. She had a car. She had, for most of those years, the money for gas. There had been a hundred weekends when she could have stood on that porch and knocked, and every single time she had chosen the mailbox instead. Chosen the version of her mother that fit on a postcard. Chosen distance and called it devotion.

The fire had made it easy, afterward. The fire had given her a reason that required no courage: *there's nothing there anymore.* But the fire came in 2011, and the estrangement began years before, and in all those living, breathing, knockable years, she had never gone.

She flipped through the stack faster now, letting the cards fan past her thumb like a deck being shuffled, and phrases surfaced and sank: *hope you're well, things are busy here, saw a film you'd have liked, the press is publishing a novel in the fall, weather's turning.* Weather. Weather appeared on sixty-one separate cards; she had counted once, in a bleaker hour than this one. Sixty-one dispatches about precipitation and temperature, mailed across four hundred miles to a woman she loved more than she had ever managed to say.

Here was one from March 2015, the ink slightly smudged where her thumb had sweated:

*Mom — I've been thinking about Dad lately. About everything. Maybe someday we can talk about it properly. Anyway. The magnolias are early this year.*

*Maybe someday we can talk about it properly.* Seven words doing the work of a confession and then immediately buried under magnolias. She remembered writing it. She remembered standing at the counter of the stationery shop—it had closed since, another casualty of the decade—with the carbon paper under the card and her pen hovering over the space where the real sentence should have gone. What she had wanted to write was: *I know about the letters. I found one in your desk when I was sixteen and I burned it and I have hated myself for eleven years and I need you to tell me who Vera is.* 

She had written *maybe someday* instead. And her mother, receiving it, had presumably done what she always did: read the surface, understood the depth, and answered neither.

Except—had she answered? Coral had never received a reply. Not one. For twelve years she had told herself the silence was her mother's choice, her mother's final withholding, the last wall in a house full of them. She had built a whole grief on top of that silence, a grief with rooms and hallways, a grief she furnished and maintained. And now a stranger named Tomas was telling her that behind a wardrobe there was a tin box full of envelopes addressed to *Coral Reyes*, returned unopened, and that her mother had been writing back for years.

Writing back. To postcards about weather.

What must Marisol Reyes have thought, opening her mailbox month after month to find her daughter reporting on magnolias? Had she laughed? Had she wept? Had she recognized the disease in her daughter's prose the way Coral was recognizing it now—instantly, mortifyingly, the way you recognize your own profile in an old photograph? *She writes like me,* her mother might have thought. *She has learned to say nothing beautifully.*

Coral set the stack down on the quilt and stood up, because sitting still had become unbearable. She walked to the window. Below, the street glistened with the day's rain, and a man walked a dog that kept stopping to interrogate every hydrant, and somewhere a siren rose and faded, rose and faded, the city's indifferent lullaby. None of it touched her. She was sixteen again, standing in her mother's bedroom with a letter in her hand, reading words she hadn't been supposed to see—words addressed to a woman named Vera, words so tender they had frightened her—and striking the match.

She had told herself, then, that she was protecting the family. Protecting her father, who worked long hours and trusted his wife completely. Protecting the smooth surface of their lives, which a letter like that could crack straight down the middle. She had fed the page to the sink and watched the edges curl brown and black, and when her mother came home that evening, Coral had looked at her across the dinner table and said nothing, and her mother had looked back, and something had passed between them—some flicker, some recognition, some terrible mutual understanding that a door had closed—and within a year the estrangement had begun, though neither of them ever named the cause.

A coward, then, at sixteen. And a coward at twenty-four, choosing postcards over visits. And a coward at thirty-six, mailing card number 438 to a ruin rather than driving to meet the stranger who held her mother's answers in a tin box.

The word sat in her chest like a stone swallowed whole. *Coward.* It wasn't self-pity; it was worse than that. It was taxonomy. It was finally naming the family inheritance—not the dark hair, not the crooked smile her father used to tease her about, but this. The evasion. The beautiful, literate, endlessly practiced evasion. Her mother had passed it down like a gene, and Coral had expressed it fully, and the tragedy was that they had both been so *good* at it. Two women capable of extraordinary feeling, producing decades of correspondence in which the feeling appeared only as weather.

She went back to the bed and knelt beside the shoebox, and this time she didn't reach for the middle or the end. She reached for the bottom, for the earliest stratum, and lifted out a handful of cards from 2012, and forced herself to read them slowly, one by one, like a judge reviewing evidence.

*April 2012:* *Mom — Spring here at last. Work is work. I miss the orchids.*

There it was. Four sentences, and only the last one true, and even that one armored—*I miss the orchids*, not *I miss you*, not *I miss the woman you were before he wore you down*, not *I miss the mother who taught me the names of flowers and then disappeared into a marriage I never understood*. Just the orchids. Safe. Botanical. Deniable.

*July 2012:* *Mom — Very hot. The press is moving offices. Nothing else new.*

Nothing else new. As if the entire interior life of Coral Reyes could be summarized as a vacancy. As if there weren't nights she lay awake composing arguments with her mother, rehearsing confrontations, drafting the great speech that would fix everything—and then, come morning, condensing all of it down to *nothing else new* and a stamp.

She had been so afraid. That was the plain truth under all the analysis, and she made herself look at it directly, the way you look at the sun through smoked glass. Afraid that if she said the true thing, her mother would deny it. Afraid her mother would confirm it. Afraid of the anger that would come, or worse, the hurt. Afraid that the mother she had—the compromised, diminished, evasive mother—was the only one available, and that pressing too hard on the wound would kill the patient. So she had written to the address and not to the woman, and called it love, and slept reasonably well.

And her mother had done the same thing in reverse. Had written back—apparently for years—and sealed the letters behind a wardrobe, or perhaps mailed them to addresses that didn't exist, or perhaps simply never mailed them at all. Two cowards, mirroring each other across the distance, each waiting for the other to be brave first.

Neither of them had been brave. And then there had been a fire, and now one of them was ash, and the bravery would have to be performed retroactively, by proxy, by a daughter kneeling on a bed with a shoebox of carbon ghosts.

Coral wiped her face with the heel of her hand and noticed, distantly, that she was crying—had probably been crying for several minutes—which annoyed her, because tears felt too easy, too much like another form of almost-saying-it. Grief as a substitute for action. She blew her nose on the sleeve of her cardigan, an act her younger self would have found unforgivable, and gathered the scattered cards back into order.

It was then that she saw it—a card near the bottom of the 2014 section that she didn't remember writing. Or rather, she remembered the writing of it perfectly well; what she didn't remember was this copy existing. The carbon was fainter than the others, the pen pressure lighter, as if she had written it quickly, guiltily, late at night. The date in the corner was November 2014. She read it.

*Mom — I found the name again. Vera. It was in a dream, of all places. Who is she? I'm not angry. I just want to know. Please.*

Please.

She stared at it. She had absolutely no memory of sending this one. No memory of buying the stamp, of dropping it in the blue box on Ninth Street, of any of it. Which meant one of two things: either she had written it and destroyed the original and hidden the carbon even from herself, burying it at the bottom of the box beneath the cards she could bear to reread—or she had written it, and mailed it, and then spent ten years pretending she hadn't.

Either way, the question had been asked. *Who is Vera?* Asked in ink, in her own hand, in 2014—two years before Tomas ever pried loose a wardrobe panel, two years before the name VERA scratched in glass would stop her heart in a ruined conservatory. She had asked. Some part of her, some unguarded midnight part, had broken ranks with the coward and asked the question outright.

And her mother had received it—if it was sent—and had answered it, in a letter that now sat in a tin box in a half-gutted house, in the custody of a young man who owed the dead order and the living patience.

Coral sat very still. The lamp hummed. Outside, the dog and its owner had moved on, and the street was empty, and the rain had started again, soft as static against the glass.

She thought: *I could call him. I could tell him to bring the first one. The earliest letter. The one from weeks after we stopped speaking.*

She thought: *Tomorrow. I'll ask him tomorrow.*

But her hand was already reaching for her phone, because it seemed that some part of her—perhaps the same midnight part that had once written *who is Vera* and hidden the evidence—was finished with tomorrow.

* * *

Tomas answered on the second ring, and before she could compose a greeting, before she could build even one sentence of weather, she heard herself say:

"I want to read the first letter. The one from right after. Can you—have you still got it there?"

A pause. Then, quietly, in that careful archivist's voice that always made her feel like a document being handled with white gloves: "I was hoping you'd ask."
