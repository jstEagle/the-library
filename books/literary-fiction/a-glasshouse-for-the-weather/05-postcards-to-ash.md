## 5. Postcards to Ash

The postcards lived in a shoebox under my bed, and every Sunday I took one out, wrote on it, and mailed it to a house that had been ash for eleven years.

*Dear Mum. The stall's been slow this week. A man wanted a poem for his wife's birthday but couldn't remember her eye color, so I gave him gray, because everyone's eyes go gray if you look long enough.*

I never said the true things on the cards. That was the point of them, or part of it — that they were addressed to someone who could no longer be disappointed by what I withheld. The postal service delivered them to a dead letter office in the county seat, where some clerk with rubber stamps sent them to the dead letter room, and there they accumulated, year after year, forty-nine of them now, a paper sediment at the bottom of the state. Sometimes I imagined the room: fluorescent hum, wire carts, envelopes soft as moth wings. My mother's afterlife was a filing system.

The house had burned on the fourth of November, 2013, and no one had ever explained it.

That sentence sounds simple. It is not simple. It is the load-bearing wall of my family, and we all live inside it without touching it, the way you don't touch a wall you suspect is hollow.

Here is what was said aloud: *electrical fault, old wiring, thank God she wasn't home.* Here is what was done: my father hired a contractor who tore the charred bones down within the month, faster than grief usually moves, faster than insurance required. Here is what was not done: no one asked my mother why, when the fire inspector came, she sat in the car with her hands over her face like a child hiding from a game of hide-and-seek gone wrong. No one asked why she stopped sleeping in bedrooms after that, why she arranged her pillows on the sofa for two years until the dementia took even the arrangement away.

And here is what she said, later, when the disease had already begun its slow demolition — said once, at three in the morning, standing in my kitchen doorway in her nightgown with an expression of terrible clarity, the kind the sick sometimes get, a lucidity like lightning through a ruined roof:

*"You know it wasn't the wiring, Mara."*

Then, before I could speak — before I could do anything but stand there holding a glass of water I'd gotten up to fetch for her — her face closed like a shop shutter, and she said, *"Do we have any of those little oranges?"* and the moment was gone, and I never got it back, and she died fourteen months later not knowing my name but knowing, somehow, still, how to refuse me.

My family arranged itself around that night the way water arranges itself around a stone. Dad moved to Phoenix. My brother Tom called less, then rarely, then only on birthdays, his voice performing a cheerfulness so brittle I could hear it crackle down the line. At the funeral — Mum's funeral — we stood at the graveside and none of us mentioned the fire, though it was the fire that had killed her, in the way that a wound kills you years later by way of infection. The minister said *she is at peace,* and we all nodded, four people nodding at a lie, and afterward at the reception Tom told a story about Mum's garden and Dad laughed too loudly and I excused myself to the bathroom and ran the tap so no one would hear whatever sound I was making.

We never asked. That's the thing I keep circling. We never asked *what happened that night*, and the not-asking became its own architecture, its own family heirloom, passed down intact: here is your silence, Mara, wear it well, it goes with everything.

* * *

Sunday again. Rain coming down in ropes. I sat on the edge of my bed with the shoebox open and chose card number fifty: a photograph of a lighthouse, because Mum had loved lighthouses, or had claimed to, or had bought me a lighthouse snow globe once, which in memory becomes love.

I didn't write the card right away. Instead I did something new. I turned it over and looked at the blank back for a long time, and then I got out the notebook — not the transcription notebook, the other one, the cheap spiral one I kept for lists and half-thoughts — and I wrote at the top of a page:

**Questions I have never asked anyone.**

1. Why did Mum sit in the car during the inspection?
2. Who did Dad call before he called the insurance company?
3. Why did the fire report list the cause as "undetermined" for six weeks and then change to "electrical"?
4. What did Mum mean?

I looked at the four questions. They looked back at me, small and blue-ruled, absurdly modest, like four windows in a vast dark house. Fifty postcards and I had never once written these down. Writing them felt transgressive in a way I couldn't justify — as if the questions themselves might catch, might spread, might burn something down.

Because here is the arithmetic I'd been avoiding: Edele Voss sat in her glasshouse twice a week and handed me her memories like eggs out of a basket, fragile and warm and irreplaceable, and she did it *before the dementia could take them*, deliberately, urgently, while she still had the choosing of it. And I had spent eleven years mailing postcards into a dead letter room instead of asking one living person one living question.

Edele was doing the thing my family never did. She was asking herself out loud.

* * *

Wednesday's session had ended strangely. I hadn't written about it yet; I'd been carrying it around like a stone in my coat pocket.

She had been dictating weather words — her language had, I swear, forty words for rain, each one specific as a fingerprint — and she'd come to a word she pronounced slowly, twice, making me repeat it until my tongue obeyed. Then she'd said, in English, "This one means the rain that falls on a fire. The rain that comes too late to save anything but early enough to make the ash stick to everything. You would say—" and she'd stopped, and her face had changed, the way a landscape changes when cloud crosses the sun, and she'd looked at me and said, "Your turn. Tell me a word like that. From your language."

"I don't have another language," I'd said.

"Everyone has one," she'd said. "It may only be one word long."

And I'd sat there with my pen poised over the notebook, professional, neutral, the transcriptionist, the woman whose whole trade was saying other people's unsayable things for money — and I had nothing. Not a word. Not one. Eleven years of correspondence with a ruin and I had no word for any of it, because I had never let myself look directly at the ruin long enough to name what it was made of.

Edele had watched me fail, and she hadn't pressed. She'd simply moved on to the next rain-word, merciful as weather. But walking home I understood that she had seen it — the stone under my water — the way old women see everything, having spent their lives learning to read faces the way farmers read sky.

* * *

Saturday night I couldn't sleep, and around two I got up and dug out the folder I keep in the closet bottom: the fire documents, such as they were. Insurance settlement. Contractor's invoice. A single page from the county fire marshal's office, dated six weeks after the fire, stamped **CAUSE: ELECTRICAL (AGING WIRING)** — and beneath the stamp, faintly, the ghost of different text that had been whited out and retyped. I'd noticed it years ago and told myself it was a correction, clerical, meaningless. Tonight I held it under the desk lamp and tilted it, and the ghost-text rose up like writing on wet paper:

*Undetermined — pending interview, occupant.*

Pending interview, occupant. They had meant to interview my mother. The interview had never happened — or it had happened, and produced something, and been erased. And my father had torn the house down twenty-four days after the fire, before any second inspection could occur, and everyone had praised him for being so practical, so strong, so good at handling things.

Handling things. That was our family verb. We handled things the way you handle evidence.

I sat on the floor of my apartment with the papers spread around me like tarot cards and I thought about Edele's story — the testimony that vanished from the record, the hearing that left no trace, the daughter taken "for her own protection." How a powerful family can bend an official record until it reflects their face. How the erasure doesn't need to be dramatic; it needs only to be patient, bureaucratic, a white-out strip and a retype and a demolition permit filed quickly enough.

I am not saying the Hallorans burned my mother's house. I am not saying anything. I am saying that tonight, for the first time, the two stories touched. Edele's and mine. Two records altered. Two interviews that never happened. Two families arranged around a silence, keeping the silence the way you keep a load-bearing wall — because you're afraid the whole structure comes down if you touch it.

Maybe that's all coincidence. Maybe old wiring is just old wiring. But I thought of my mother's face at three a.m., that lightning-strike clarity — *you know it wasn't the wiring, Mara* — and I thought: she knew something. She knew something and the disease ate her before she could give it to me, and unlike Edele Voss, she never got her transcriptionist. Nobody sat across from her with a notebook while she still had the words. We were all too busy handling things.

* * *

In the morning I wrote the fiftieth postcard. But this time I didn't write about the stall, or the weather, or the small safe nothings I'd been rationing out for eleven years like a woman eking out a dying ration.

I wrote: *Mum. I'm going to ask the questions now. I should have asked them while you were here. I'm sorry it took me this long, and I'm sorry it took a stranger to teach me how.*

I signed it. Then, below my signature, in careful block letters, I printed my street address — the first return address I had ever put on one of those cards — and I stood at the mailbox on the corner for a full minute, rain dripping off the tin flap, before I let it go.

Walking home, I passed the noticeboard outside the community hall, and a flyer caught my eye, one I must have walked past a dozen times: a county notice about restitution claims, historical agricultural contamination, a public hearing listed for next month. And beneath the boilerplate, in the list of parties named, a surname I now recognized from Edele's fractured Tuesdays.

Halloran Orchards.

I stood in the rain reading it twice, three times, my heart going oddly quiet, the way it does just before it decides something.

There was a phone number on the flyer. There was, I realized, also a phone number in my notebook — Theo Halloran's, scrawled weeks ago when I'd first started pulling threads, unused until now.

I went home and picked up the phone.
