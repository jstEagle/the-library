## 6. The Halloran Reply

The Halloran orchards still had a website, which surprised me more than it should have. I'd expected the family to have dissolved into the past like everything else connected to Edele Voss — a hearing with no records, a daughter with no paper trail, a language with one speaker left. But there they were: *Halloran Family Orchards, Est. 1948*, a photograph of long rows of apple trees in blossom, a phone number, an email address that ended in .com as if the twentieth century had never happened to them at all.

I sat at my kitchen table with the laptop open and three postcards to the burned house stacked beside me, unsent for two days, because I couldn't decide what to say to a house about a woman whose daughter had been taken by people who grew apples.

I did not write to them as myself. That decision came easily, which should have told me something about who I was becoming. I invented a name — Ruth Calloway — and a purpose: a graduate student compiling an oral history of county agriculture, funded by nothing, answerable to no one. It was the kind of lie that works because it is boring. Nobody guards themselves against boredom.

*Dear Mr. Halloran,* I wrote. *I'm researching farming families of the valley for a county heritage project, and your orchard came up repeatedly in older accounts. I'd be grateful for twenty minutes of your time.*

I sent it to the address on the website and then sat very still, listening to the refrigerator hum, feeling the specific shame of a person who has just done something she cannot undo and has not yet decided whether she wanted to.

Edele would have known what I was doing. She read me the way she read weather — some old instinct in her face, a pressure change behind the eyes. *You have the look of someone carrying water in her hands,* she'd said once, apropos of nothing, when I'd come in from checking the mail. I hadn't asked how she knew. In her language, apparently, there was a word for a secret you carried on behalf of someone else, and another word for a secret you carried against them, and the difference mattered enormously, though I could never keep the two straight.

Three days passed. On the fourth morning, an email arrived from an address I didn't recognize: *t.halloran@*. Not the family account. A personal one.

*Ms. Calloway — I'm Theo Halloran. My father handles the business inquiries, but he's not well, so questions about the orchard's history usually come to me. Happy to help if I can. What years are you looking at?*

I wrote back: *1979, mostly. And anything touching the Voss property next door.*

I hit send before I could soften it. Then I made coffee I didn't drink and watched the window over my sink, where the glasshouse would be visible if I lived forty miles east instead of here, among my own ghosts.

The reply came within the hour, and it was short.

*I think you've made a mistake. There was no Voss property next door. Please don't contact this address again.*

I read it four times. It was the phrasing that caught me — not *I don't know what you're talking about*, which is what innocent people say, but *there was no Voss property*, which is what people say who have decided what the record will show. Denial with the architecture of fact. I'd spent eleven years writing other people's apologies; I knew what a sentence looked like when it had been built to bear weight it wasn't meant to hold.

But it was the second email, arriving ninety seconds later, that changed the temperature of the room.

*Wait. Forget what I said. Are you writing to Edele? Is she — do you know her?*

* * *

We met on a Thursday at a diner off the county road, halfway between his orchard and my city, a building with vinyl booths the color of mustard and a pie case lit like an altar. Theo Halloran was younger than I expected — mid-thirties, maybe, with his grandfather's heavy jaw and none of the assurance that jaw presumably once carried. He sat across from me with his hands wrapped around a coffee mug he didn't drink from, and the first thing I noticed was that he kept glancing at the windows.

"You're the youngest," I said. "The website lists three Hallorans."

"Two now. My uncle died in March." He said it flatly, a man practicing at grief. "And my father doesn't talk about before 1990. Not the orchard's problems, not the neighbors, not anything. So whatever you're doing — the heritage project, the oral history — you picked the wrong family."

"I didn't pick you," I said. "You emailed me twice."

He looked down at the mug. Outside, a truck went past, dragging its shadow across us like a hand.

"Tell me exactly what you said to her," he said. "Word for word."

"Why?"

"Because if you used her name wrong, she'll know. And if she knows, she'll—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "She remembers things," he finished, in a different voice. Quieter. "That's the whole problem. Everyone always assumed she wouldn't."

The diner noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. I thought of Edele in the glasshouse, her hands moving through the warm green air, naming things that had no English neighbors. *Everyone assumed she wouldn't.* As if her memory were a well someone had poisoned and spent forty years hoping it would run dry.

"Theo," I said carefully. "What happened in 1979?"

He laughed — one syllable, no humor in it, a sound like a door catching. "You first. Who are you really? Because Ruth Calloway doesn't exist. I checked. There's no grant, no project, no university department." He leaned forward, and up close I could see the fear properly now, sitting behind his eyes like a tenant who paid rent. "I'm not angry. I want you to understand that. I'm thirty-four years old and I have been afraid of my own family history since I was nine, and I don't even know why. Nobody ever told me why. They just made sure I understood the shape of the silence."

"That's not an answer either," I said.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He finally drank his coffee, grimacing at the cold of it. "Here's what I can tell you. When I was a kid, there was a box in my grandmother's attic that we weren't allowed near. Not locked — just forbidden, which is worse, because forbidden means someone might ask questions. After she died my father burned it in the orchard incinerator. I saw him do it. He stood there until it was ash, and then he raked the ash apart and stood there longer." Theo turned the mug a slow quarter-circle on the table. "There was a name on the side of the box. I was too far away to read most of it. But I remember the last word."

I waited. Somewhere behind the counter, the pie case hummed.

"Voss," he said.

* * *

He walked me out to the parking lot, and it was there, under a sky going the color of dishwater, that he stopped being careful.

"Listen to me," he said, low and fast, glancing again at the road. "Whatever you're recording, wherever you're keeping it — don't keep it at her place. And don't go after dark. And if a gray sedan comes down that lane, you leave. Don't answer the door, don't wave, just go."

"A gray sedan," I repeated. "Theo, that sounds—"

"It's been there twice," he said. "In the last month. Parked on the shoulder past her gate, engine running. My father knows about it. I know because I heard him on the phone telling someone *she's just an old woman, let it rest* — and whoever he was talking to didn't agree." His hands had gone white around his keys. "I don't know who it is. That's the truth. It could be lawyers. It could be the county. It could be nobody. But my father is scared, and my father has never been scared of anything in his life except that attic box, so I'm telling you: leave. Take whatever you've recorded and give it back to her and walk away, because you're not from here, and people who aren't from here don't understand what this valley does to—"

He stopped. A car had turned into the lot.

It was nothing — a woman in a minivan collecting a takeout order, parking crooked, going inside. Theo exhaled through his teeth, embarrassed, and I watched him fold the fear back down into himself like a letter into an envelope, practiced, worn soft at the creases.

"I shouldn't have met you," he said. "If anyone asks, tell them I talked about apple varieties and soil pH. Tell them I was boring."

"Theo." I reached out and touched his sleeve, and he flinched and then didn't pull away. "Your father burned the box. But Edele kept things too. Paper keeps, sometimes, in the right conditions." I thought of the glasshouse — the humidity, the drawers, the way Edele filed her life among the plants like a woman who trusted green growing things more than people. "If there's a record anywhere, it's on her side of the fence."

For a moment something moved across his face that was almost hope, and it frightened him visibly, the way hope frightens people who have been trained out of it.

"Don't find anything," he said. "That's what I should say. That's what my father would want me to say." He got into his truck, started the engine, and rolled the window down. The cold came in with the exhaust. "But my uncle died in March, and the night before he died he called me, drunk, and he said, *Theo, we owe that woman a daughter.* Those were his words. *A daughter.* And then he hung up, and two days later he was dead, and I have been sitting with that sentence ever since like a stone in my shoe."

He drove off without saying goodbye. I stood in the emptying lot holding my notebook against my chest, and only then did I notice the ink had bled — I'd been gripping it too hard, the way you grip a railing.

* * *

I drove back east with the windows cracked despite the cold, needing the air. Twice I checked my mirror. Both times the road behind me held nothing but ordinary dusk.

At the turnoff for the glasshouse lane, I nearly drove past. I don't know why. Some animal part of me voting with the brakes. But I turned in, because Edele was in there alone with her nouns and her lullabies and her drawer full of paper, and because a sentence had gotten into me the way sentences do — *we owe that woman a daughter* — and I could feel it working its way toward meaning.

The lane was empty. No gray sedan. Just the frost coming down over the fields, silvering the fence wire, and the glasshouse ahead glowing faintly amber, lit from within, patient as a lantern.

Then I saw the tire tracks.

Not mine. Not Edele's — she hadn't driven in years. Two parallel lines pressed into the frozen mud just past the gate, deep enough to have been sitting there a while, turning in and turning out. Someone had come down this lane recently, slowly, and stopped, and left without going in.

Or without going in yet.

I parked behind the glasshouse where the car couldn't be seen from the road and sat for a moment with my hands on the wheel, listening to the tick of the cooling engine. Through the fogged panes ahead, Edele's silhouette moved among her plants, unhurried, tending, a woman entirely unaware that the world outside her glass had begun to lean in.

I got out. I walked toward the light.
