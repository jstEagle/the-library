## 17. What We Owe the Dead

Ruth had kept the keys for forty years, and in all that time she had never once opened the practice wing after midnight. Not because she couldn't — she could have opened any door in the building blindfolded, by the weight of each ring, by the number of turns each lock demanded — but because there were doors you kept shut the way you kept a wound covered. Not to hide it. To let it close.

Tonight she opened the door anyway.

The corridor exhaled its old air at her: dust and rosin and the ghost of floor polish, the smell of thirty years of nothing happening. Ruth went first with the candle lantern held high, because Hazel carried the box and Tomás carried nothing at all, and Ruth had decided — in the way of old women who have decided things all their lives without announcing them — that the man should walk behind for a while. Let him feel what it was to come last into a room where Ilse Brandt had once been first.

"Mind the third floorboard," she said. "It's been loose since 1989. I told them. Nobody listens to the woman with the keys."

Hazel laughed, a small startled sound, and Tomás said nothing, but Ruth heard him step over the board.

They set up in Studio C, the one with the tall windows facing the river, because if the dead were going to be summoned, Ruth thought they should at least be summoned with a view of water. Hazel had brought candles — real ones, beeswax, from the shop on Delancey — and she lit them one by one along the piano's closed lid until the room filled with a light that moved when you breathed. The demolition notices were still taped to the walls outside, yellow and official, DEMOLITION SCHEDULED — WING CLOSES FRIDAY, and Ruth had peeled one off the door of Studio C and folded it into her apron pocket, which was not vandalism, she told herself, only housekeeping.

Tomás stood by the window with his hands in his pockets, a big weathered man trying to make himself small, and Ruth studied him the way you study a photograph you've seen a hundred times and are finally meeting in the flesh. He had Ilse's posture. That was the strange thing — thirty years of boatyards and salt air, and the man still stood the way she had taught him to stand, spine like a metronome arm, weight forward, ready.

"You knew her," he said to Ruth. It wasn't a question.

"I kept her secrets," Ruth said. "That's a kind of knowing. It's not the best kind."

"And now?"

"Now we're going to stop keeping them. Sit down, Tomás. All of you. This is going to take until the candles burn."

* * *

Hazel read the letters aloud.

That had been argued about — briefly, in the doorway, Tomás saying *they're mine, they were written to me, I should read them* — and Ruth had watched Hazel stand her ground with something close to Ilse's own stubbornness in her face. *You've had thirty years to read them in private,* Hazel had said. *Tonight you read them with witnesses.* And Tomás, who had crossed an ocean of silence to get to this room, had bowed his head and agreed.

He read the first one himself. His voice came out wrecked and rusty, a voice that hadn't been used for reading aloud in decades, and the words were Ilse's — *Dear Tomas, You played the Chopin like a boy apologizing for existing. Stop apologizing. The music is not sorry to be here and neither are you* — and by the second sentence Ruth had to look at the ceiling, at the water stains she knew by heart, because if she looked at the man's face she would come apart and someone had to stay whole. Someone had to keep the keys.

The candles burned. The letters went around the circle: Hazel read some, Tomás read some, and when his voice failed entirely — at the letter from March, the one about his mother's hospital bills, the one where Ilse wrote *whatever you decide, decide it with your head up* — Hazel took the pages from his hands without a word and read on for him, and Ruth thought: there it is. There's the verdict forming already. Not in words. In the passing of pages from one guilty hand to another.

Ruth herself read two. She chose them deliberately, the way she chose which doors to lock last at night. One was from the early summer, light as a feather — *Tomas, I have hidden the good coffee in the Beethoven score again, do not tell the cleaning staff, oh wait* — and it made them laugh, all three of them, a startled, guilty, necessary laughter that cracked the room open like ice going out of a river in spring. The other was the last letter, dated the week everything ended, and Ruth read it slowly because she had never read it before, not once in thirty years, and she found that her hands knew how to hold paper steady even when the rest of her did not.

*Dear Tomas. If you are reading this, then I have made my choice and you have made yours, and we will both have to live inside them. I want you to know that I do not regret it. I want you to know that I am angry, and that the anger is not at you. I want you to practice. I want you to eat something green. I want you to understand that a life can be ruined and still be a life, that I intend to have one, that I intend to have it loudly. Do not come back to explain. Do not write. Live well enough that my lie was worth the price of it. That is the only tuition I will accept now. — I.B.*

The candle flames leaned when she finished, though the windows were shut.

"Thirty years," Tomás said, into the quiet. "I built boats. Good ones. I married, I lost her, I raised a son who thinks his father never touched a piano. And every single day I practiced, Ruth. You know what I mean. I practiced being a man who didn't do what I did. And I never once got good at it."

"No one does," Ruth said. "That's not what the practicing is for."

* * *

Then the ledger.

Hazel laid it open on the piano lid, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, and turned to the photocopied page she'd found misfiled behind the 1987 endowment reports. The withdrawals marched down the page in their tidy columns, and at the bottom, the initials. *R.V.* Not *I.B.* Never *I.B.* Richard Vance, the founder, the man whose name was on the scholarship fund and the bronze plaque in the foyer and the building they were going to demolish this wing to make way for.

Tomás read the initials and then read them again, and Ruth watched something move through his face like weather.

"He signed it," Tomás said. "He took the money himself, and he —"

"He needed somewhere to put it," Hazel said. Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone who has rehearsed not shouting. "The endowment had been bleeding for years. Bad investments, I think, or just him. And Ilse was —" She stopped. Started again. "Ilse was foreign. Difficult. Beloved by students and trusted by no one with power. She was the easiest door in the building."

"The easiest door," Tomás repeated, and Ruth saw his fists close.

And Ruth, who had spent four decades being the woman with the keys, who had watched Richard Vance shake hands at galas while Ilse Brandt died unplayed on any radio in the country, found that she was standing. She didn't remember standing.

"I'll tell you what I know," she said, "since we're doing this. Since we're apparently doing this at midnight with candles like a séance, which Ilse would have found hilarious, by the way, she'd have brought wine." Her voice shook and she let it. "I knew. Not the ledger. Not the initials. But I knew she didn't do it. You know how? Because the week it happened, she came to me at six in the morning, before anyone else was in the building, and she asked me to help her move a box behind that wardrobe. And she was crying, and Ilse Brandt did not cry, and she said, *Ruth, someday a girl with her mother's stubbornness is going to find this, and when she does, tell her I was sorry about the garden.*"

"The garden?" Hazel said.

"There was a garden," Ruth said. "Behind the practice wing, before they paved it for the loading dock. She grew things nobody grows anymore. She said a garden was just a promise you kept on your knees." Ruth looked at Tomás. "She asked me to keep the box, and I asked her what was in it, and she said, *The truth, minus one name.* And I understood. God help me, I understood, and I kept it anyway, because she asked, and because she was right — exposure would have burned him and saved no one, and Lila —"

"Lila," Hazel said softly.

"Lila was twenty-two and about to make a career, and Ilse loved her like a daughter, and Ilse knew that if the truth came out with Tomas's name on it, every musician Lila had ever played beside would be asked what else she'd known, who else she'd covered for. Ilse took the disgrace alone because disgrace alone was survivable and disgrace shared was not. That was her arithmetic. I've checked it for thirty years and I still can't say she was wrong. I can only say what it cost." Ruth sat down, slowly, the way old knees insist. "I've been the door this whole building closes behind itself. Tonight I'm opening it."

* * *

They spoke Ilse's name then.

It sounds like nothing, written down. Three syllables. But Ruth had watched this conservatory perform an erasure so thorough it looked like tidiness — the portrait quietly removed from the corridor, the name dropped from the honors lists, the sonata unperformed, the students who remembered her gently corrected into forgetting. Thirty years, and no one in the building had said *Ilse Brandt* out loud in a full voice, the way you say the name of someone who existed, who was here, who mattered.

Tomás said it first. Then Hazel. Then Ruth, and her voice cracked down the middle of it, and she said it again, louder, the way you call someone in from a garden at dusk, and the candles stood up straight in the still air and the name filled Studio C the way sound fills a room that has been waiting for it.

They rendered their verdict by candlelight, the three of them, and it took no deliberation at all. The verdict was not for a court. Courts could come later, or not; Tomás still hadn't decided what he would do with the truth now that he was holding it in front of witnesses, and Ruth did not press him, because she had learned from the best that a confession forced is a confession half-made. The verdict was for the dead woman. It went like this: *Ilse Brandt did not steal. Ilse Brandt sacrificed. Ilse Brandt loved badly and magnificently and at the full price, and she is owed her name back, in bronze if they'll give it, in chalk if they won't, in the mouths of everyone in this room and everyone they tell.*

Hazel wrote it down. Actually wrote it, in the back of the ledger, on the blank page after the photocopied one, in pencil, dated it, signed it, and passed the pencil to Tomás, who signed, and then to Ruth, who signed with a hand that only shook a little.

"There," Hazel said. "Filed."

"Filed where no one will ever look," Ruth said. "Which is where this building keeps everything true."

"Then we make them look," Hazel said. "The gala is Saturday. The board will be in that hall with the founder's family and the donors and the demolition timeline on a slide. And I have a ledger, and letters, and a witness." She looked at Tomás. "If you'll be one."

The candles had burned down to stubs. Through the tall windows the sky over the river had gone the deep bruised blue that comes an hour before dawn, and Ruth, who had seen ten thousand dawns from inside this building, thought: *the blue hour. Ilse used to teach at this hour. She said it was when the ears were cleanest.*

Tomás looked at the ledger a long time. Then at Hazel, who had her mother's jaw and Ilse's stubbornness and neither of their fears. Then at Ruth, the woman with the keys, who had kept the door shut for thirty years and was now, apparently, holding it open.

"I'll come Saturday," he said. "But I decide what I say when I'm standing in that room. Not before. Not tonight."

"Fair," Hazel said. "But Tomás — they move fast. Boards move faster than conscience. If we wait for the perfect moment —"

"There are no perfect moments," Ruth said. "There are only the ones you're given and the ones you take." She began pinching out the candles, one by one, and the room dimmed toward the gray of early morning. "And one more thing you both should know before Saturday. I checked the schedule this afternoon. They've moved it up. The wing doesn't close Friday."

Hazel went very still. "Then when?"

"Wednesday," Ruth said. "Day after tomorrow. The crews come at seven a.m." She picked up her lantern and looked at the two of them, the thief and the daughter, the last witnesses, standing in the light of one surviving flame. "So whatever you're going to do with the truth, you have one day to do it in."
