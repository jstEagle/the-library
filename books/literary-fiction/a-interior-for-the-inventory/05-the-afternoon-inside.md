## 5. The Afternoon Inside

*Wren*

The gate stuck the way old gates do—half an inch of resistance before it remembered itself—and Wren felt her mother's voice in her ear as clearly as if the woman were standing behind her: *Lift it as you push. It always sticks.*

She lifted. It opened.

Quentin Lockridge stood on the gravel drive with his hands in his pockets and the expression of a man who had agreed to something against every instinct he owned. He looked better without the funeral suit, she thought. In a gray sweater he seemed less like a monument and more like a man who occasionally slept badly.

"One afternoon," he said. "I'll be with you."

"I'd assumed so." She stepped past him onto the path. "You don't have to guard me. I'm not here to take anything."

"Everyone's here to take something. That's what houses are for." He said it lightly, but she heard the ledger underneath—the habit of accounting for loss before it arrived.

The house rose ahead of them, brick gone the color of dried blood at the corners, ivy making its slow argument up the east wall. And Wren stopped, because she knew that wall. Not from photographs. From somewhere older than photographs, in the place where her mother's stories had settled into her like sediment.

"The ivy was cut back when my grandmother lived here," she said. "There was a argument about it. Someone wanted it removed entirely and someone refused."

Quentin's face did something complicated. "My grandfather wanted it removed. My grandmother wouldn't allow it."

"Your grandmother," Wren said carefully, "or his first wife?"

He didn't answer. He unlocked the front door instead, and the sound of the key turning was very loud in the morning quiet.

Inside, the air was cool and still, the particular preserved air of a house that has been mourned in rather than lived in. Wren stood in the entrance hall and let it move through her. The checkerboard floor. The coat hooks too high for children. The mirror with the hairline crack running through the left corner like a river on a map.

"There's a scar on the banister," she said. "Third stair from the bottom, on your left going down. A gouge, about this long." She held her thumb and forefinger apart. "My mother said she made it with a letter opener when she was nine, sliding down the rail, and was punished for a week for lying about how it happened."

Quentin went to the banister. He crouched. She watched him run his fingers along the wood and find it—the groove worn smooth by forty years of palms, but unmistakable, a small dark wound in the grain.

"That's not possible," he said, but softly, the way people say things they already believe.

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

*Quentin*

He led her through the rooms because leading was easier than following, and because if he kept moving he could pretend this was a tour and not an excavation. But she kept stopping, and each stop cost him something.

In the morning room she touched the window seat without looking for it first—walked straight to it, the way you walk to a door in your own house in the dark.

"This faces east," she said. "You can see the light come over the orchard wall. My mother sat here to read because it was the warmest spot in winter, and there's—" she pressed her hand into the cushion near the arm, "—a loose spring, right here, that used to catch her elbow."

Quentin had sat in that window seat a thousand times. He had never once thought about who had worn it soft before him. He had assumed, in the way of heirs, that everything comfortable in the house had been made comfortable by his own line, his own mother, his own childhood. Now the cushion under Wren's hand seemed to hold the shape of another girl entirely, and the room rearranged itself around that absence.

"You could know these things," he said. "From stories. People tell stories about houses."

"My mother told almost no stories about this house. That was the point of her—she was careful. What she did tell came out sideways, in pieces, usually when she was sick or angry or half-asleep." Wren turned to look at him, and her eyes were the color of tea left too long, dark and unhesitating. "A loose spring isn't a story, Mr. Lockridge. A story would say *there was a window seat*. This is memory. There's a difference, and you know there is."

He did know. That was the unbearable part. He knew the difference the way he knew the weight of his father's hand on his shoulder—a knowledge that had never required words.

They climbed to the second floor. On the landing she paused at a door he had never paid attention to, a narrow door to a linen closet, and said, "This one was painted shut for years. My grandmother kept her good linens elsewhere because of the damp."

"It was painted shut until 1998," Quentin heard himself say. "When we redid the roof. My mother had it opened." He stared at the door as though it might explain itself. He had never known why that detail had stayed with him; now it seemed the house had been keeping a record of its own corrections, and he had been walking past them all his life.

*Wren*

Upstairs, the corridor ran long and dim, doors on either side like held breaths. Wren walked it slowly, trailing her fingers along the wallpaper—faded roses, a pattern her mother had once described while dying, apropos of nothing, in a hospital room three hundred miles away: *Roses, but wrong ones. Garden roses gone feral. You'd know them if you saw them.*

She knew them.

At the end of the hall stood a door unlike the others. Darker wood. No keyhole visible, though the escutcheon plate was there, blank and polished from handling. The seal of it was subtle—a seam so true the eye slid off it—but Wren felt it the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

"What's in there?" she asked.

"Nothing. Storage." Quentin said it too quickly, and then, visibly deciding that lies were more expensive than truth today: "I don't know. My father kept it locked. My mother never opened it after he died. I've lived here six weeks and I haven't asked myself why until just now."

The honesty startled her. She had prepared herself for a man made entirely of the family surface—polite, armored, finished. Instead there was this crack running through him, and through the crack she could see something that looked almost like hunger.

"My mother said there was a room at the end of the hall that was always closed," Wren said. "She and her sister used to dare each other to put their ears against it."

"Did she ever hear anything?"

"She said once she heard paper. Like someone turning pages. For years she believed the house had a ghost that read." Wren smiled despite herself. "She was thirty before she understood it must have been wind through a gap. Or a person. She never decided which."

Quentin looked at the door for a long moment. Then he looked away from it, deliberately, the way you look away from something you're not ready to lift.

"The study," he said. "You wanted the desk. You said—"

"I didn't say anything about a desk."

"You will." He almost smiled. It transformed his face briefly into someone younger, someone unguarded. "Come on."

* * *

*Quentin*

His mother's desk sat beneath the study window, walnut, ordered, its blotter still squared to its edges six weeks after her death. Quentin had avoided it. Grief had organized itself neatly around that one piece of furniture the way water organizes around a stone.

Now he unlocked the drawer he had never opened—not locked, exactly, but stiff, resistant, the drawer of a woman who had wanted a moment's pause before anyone got inside.

Inside lay papers. Household accounts. A ration book from decades before his birth, kept out of some sentiment he couldn't guess at. And beneath them, folded twice, a piece of stationery torn raggedly down the middle—torn, not cut, the fibers fraying like something ripped in anger or in haste.

He lifted it. Half a letter. His mother's handwriting, young, slanted, urgent—the handwriting of a woman he had never met, because by the time he knew her, her writing had gone small and round and controlled.

*—cannot forgive what was done in our name. If the money is built on her silence then I want none of it, and I mean to say so to Father tonight. You were right, and I was a coward, and I am sorry beyond—*

The rest was gone. Torn away.

Behind him, Wren made a sound. Small. Involuntary. He turned and found her standing very still with her handbag open and something in her hands—a paper, folded and refolded so many times across forty years that it had gone soft as cloth.

She unfolded it with the care of someone handling a bird. Half a letter. Different stationery, different hand—an older script, formal, Victorian almost—but the tear matched. He could see it from where he stood: the ragged edge, the frayed fibers, the two halves of a single sentence separated by four decades.

She read aloud, her voice not quite steady:

*"—beg you not to confront him alone. He has ruined one family already and he will not hesitate to ruin ours. Come to me first. Whatever you decide, let it be chosen, not forced. Your loving—"*

The sentence ended there, on both halves, incomplete forever: *let it be chosen, not forced.*

Silence filled the study. Outside, a crow crossed the lawn, dragging its shadow.

"They fit," Quentin said. It was all he could manage.

"They fit." Wren set her half on the blotter beside his, aligning the edges, and the torn letter became whole again except for what was missing between them—the middle of the page, the heart of whatever had been written, gone to whoever had torn it apart. "Your mother wrote half. Someone answered. And then someone tore them apart and each of us kept a piece without knowing the other existed."

"My mother kept hers hidden in a drawer she never opened."

"My mother wore hers in a locket until the day she died." Wren touched her collarbone, where a chain would have lain. "She never told me what was inside. She said I'd know when it was time."

Quentin looked down at the joined halves. *Ruined one family already.* His family's name sat inside that phrase like a stone inside a fruit. Everything he had inherited—the house, the smile, the flawless composure—suddenly appeared to him as it must appear from outside: a structure built over a hole.

"Wren." He said her name for the first time since the cemetery, and she looked up. "Who was *her*? In the letter. Who was ruined?"

"I think," Wren said slowly, "you already know. I think you've known since I said the name Ashgrove on your porch and your aunt's teacup shook."

He had known. He admitted it to himself now, in the privacy of his own skull: he had known the way you know a room is sealed—not because you've seen inside it, but because everyone in the house walks around it.

"The first wife," he said. "There was a first wife. Nobody says her name. There's a photograph in the attic stairwell that my father turned to face the wall, and when I was eight I turned it back, and my mother—" He stopped. Started again. "My mother took it down and put it in a box, and told me some pictures are just mistakes."

Wren's breath caught. "What was in the photograph?"

"Two women. Young. Laughing. One of them was my mother." He met Wren's eyes. "I never knew who the other one was. Until now I told myself I didn't care."

"And now?"

"Now I want the box." He was already moving, past her, toward the door, toward the stairs, toward the attic he hadn't entered since he was eight years old. "And I want the sealed room at the end of the hall. And I want to know why my mother tore a letter in half and kept her piece for fifty years."

"Quentin." Wren followed him into the corridor, and the afternoon light fell through the wrong-rose wallpaper, gilding them both. "Whatever's in that room—once you open it, you can't close it again. Not for your aunt. Not for the sale. Not for anything."

He stood with his hand on the newel post, on the scarred banister, third stair from the bottom, and felt the whole house leaning toward him like a witness waiting to speak.

"I know," he said. "That's why I'm going."
