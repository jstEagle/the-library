## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

The pen ran dry on postcard number 3,997, and Lena Yarrow took it as a sign that she should order another coffee.

She shook the pen the way her mother used to shake a thermometer, held it to the diner's fluorescent light as if she could see the ink retreating inside it like a tide going out, and then set it down with the particular gentleness one reserves for things that have died. Outside the window of the Blue Jay Diner, October was doing its annual work on the maple trees along Route 9 — leaves letting go in ones and twos, then in whole reluctant handfuls, the way people leave.

"Refill?" said Dot, who had worked the counter since before Lena was born and had stopped asking questions sometime during the second Bush administration.

"Please."

Dot poured. The coffee was the color of the river in November and tasted like it had been through something. Lena wrapped both hands around the cup and looked at the postcard lying face-up beside her plate: a photograph of the county courthouse, which was absurd, because she had never once thought of the courthouse fondly, but the rack at the gas station had been picked over and this was what remained — civic architecture, blue sky, a flag at half-mast for reasons the caption didn't explain.

*Dear Mom,* she wrote, when Dot brought her a fresh pen from under the register without being asked, because Dot understood rituals even if she didn't understand this one. *The maples are going. I saw a fox on the trail behind the apartment, or possibly a large cat, or possibly nothing — it was dusk and I'd had wine. Work is work. I made the chicken thing you used to make, the one with the prunes, and it came out wrong, too sweet, so I ate it anyway over the sink like an animal. I miss—*

She stopped. She always stopped there. The sentence had eleven years of endings stacked up behind it and none of them would fit through the eye of a postcard.

*I miss you,* she wrote finally, because it was true and because truth, kept small enough, is bearable. *Love, Lena.*

Then the address: 14 Hollow Creek Road. A house that had burned down in March of a year she still measured other years against, the way sailors measure distance in fathoms. She had never looked up whether mail to a destroyed address simply vanished or got returned or went somewhere strange and bureaucratic, a dead-letter office where her mother's name accumulated in drawers like sediment. She didn't want to know. The not-knowing was the engine of the whole arrangement. As long as she didn't know, the postcards were *going* somewhere, and somewhere was a country her mother might conceivably be living in, and as long as that country existed, Lena was not writing to a dead woman or an estranged one but merely to a mother who was temporarily unreachable, the way people are temporarily unreachable on planes, in tunnels, during surgeries.

Eleven years. Four thousand postcards, give or take. She did the arithmetic sometimes — roughly one every single day, more on bad days, fewer only when flu or grief had flattened her entirely — and the number always surprised her, the way a molehill surprises you when you finally stand on it and realize it's a mountain you've been climbing in your sleep.

The bell over the door jangled.

Lena knew before she looked up. She knew by the specific quality of the jangle — apologetic, hesitant, a bell being rung by someone who hoped no one would answer — that it was Marisol from the county assessor's office, where Lena processed deeds and easements and the slow paper erosion of other people's property lines. Marisol was holding two muffins in a napkin and wearing the expression of a woman about to perform an act of kindness against the recipient's will.

"There you are," Marisol said, sliding onto the stool beside her. "I figured. Saturday morning, Blue Jay, corner seat." She set down the muffins. "Banana. The good batch, not the ones that are all stump."

"You're a saint."

"I'm a nuisance is what I am." Marisol laughed, and Lena smiled.

It was a good smile. She'd built it over years, the way a mason builds a wall — course by course, with attention to the load it would have to bear. It began at the corners of her mouth, rose to her eyes, crinkled them just enough to suggest warmth without inviting intimacy. Coworkers called her *the smile*. Not to her face. But she'd heard Doug in the back office say to the new hire, *Don't worry, Lena's fine, that's just how she looks*, and the new hire had nodded with the relief of a man told the dog is friendly right before learning otherwise.

"So," Marisol said, stirring sugar into coffee she hadn't ordered yet, performing the pantomime of casualness. "I'm having people over next Saturday. Nothing fancy. Chili, cornbread, Deb's bringing that dip she makes. You should come."

"I've got plans."

"Plans." Marisol looked at the postcard, the courthouse, the neat block letters of a dead address. "Right."

"It's my standing thing. Saturdays."

"The diner thing."

"The diner thing."

Marisol stirred her coffee another full rotation. Lena watched the spoon go around and felt the familiar tightening behind her sternum, the sensation of a door being gently pushed on from the outside. She could let Marisol in. She knew exactly how it would go — the chili, the dip, the questions she couldn't answer without either lying or detonating, the moment three glasses of wine in when someone kind and tipsy said *but don't you get lonely?* and Lena would deploy the smile and the smile would end the conversation, as it always did, as it was designed to do, and Monday would be awkward for a week and then everything would return to its comfortable temperature.

"Maybe next time," Lena said.

"Yeah," Marisol said. "Next time." They both knew there was no next time. Next time was a room they kept furnished for each other out of politeness.

Marisol stayed twenty more minutes, talking about her sister's divorce and a leak in her ceiling and a documentary about octopuses, and Lena listened the way she was good at listening — fully, warmly, giving nothing back. When Marisol left, she left the muffins, and Lena ate one slowly and finished her coffee and wrote on the back of the courthouse postcard, beneath the mailing address, a single line she hadn't planned:

*Someone offered me chili today. I said no. You'd have gone. You always said yes to everything — that was your whole problem.*

She stared at it. Then she crossed out *problem* and wrote *gift*, and then crossed that out too, and left the word *everything* standing alone in the sentence like a man on a platform after the train has gone.

* * *

Sundays were for the house.

She drove out Route 9 past the feed store and the shuttered drive-in, past the church whose sign read THIS WEEK'S SIN: Gossiping. Come Sunday to learn more, and turned down Hollow Creek Road where the pavement gave out and the gravel took over, rattling under the tires like applause from a distant, unenthusiastic crowd.

The house stood back from the road behind a chain-link fence strung with plastic snow fencing that had faded from safety-orange to the pink of an old scar. Eleven years of weather had done its patient work. The fire had taken the roof and the east wall; rain had taken most of the rest, working floor by floor with the diligence of a slow employee. What remained was a blackened ribcage of joists, a chimney standing like a headstone, and — miraculously, stubbornly — the wardrobe.

That was how Lena thought of it: *the wardrobe*. Her mother's walnut wardrobe, the one piece of furniture that had survived, scorched along one side, its mirror cracked into a spiderweb but still hanging on, reflecting pieces of sky and fence and, on Sundays, a woman in a gray coat standing at the gate with her hands in her pockets.

Lena never went inside. That was the rule she'd made and never broken, though she couldn't have told you why. She walked the perimeter instead, every week, the same circuit: along the fence line, past the lilac hedge that had grown wild and leggy and bloomed anyway each May with a fragrance that hit her like a hand across the face, around to the back where the garden had gone to goldenrod and burdock, and then back to the front, to the spot directly opposite the front door, where she would stand for exactly as long as it took to say, silently, whatever needed saying that week.

This week it was: *I'm sorry about the prunes. I know yours had more lemon.*

Some weeks it was bigger. Some weeks, in the early years, she had stood at the gate and rehearsed the argument — the real one, the unfinished one, the one about her father that had been interrupted by the fire and never resumed, suspended mid-sentence like a phone call dropped between towers. She had delivered her side of it to the empty doorway dozens of times, silently, eloquently, with revisions. Her mother had never once answered. That was the cruelty and the mercy of the arrangement: Lena got the last word forever, and the last word meant nothing.

Today the light was doing something it did in late October, coming in low and amber through the broken east wall so that the interior of the house glowed briefly, treacherously, like a hearth. For a second — one full second — Lena saw it as it had been: the kitchen table with its oilcloth, the radio on the windowsill tuned to the classical station out of Ashford, her mother at the stove with a wooden spoon in her fist conducting some private orchestra. Then the light shifted and it was a ruin again, a mouth of charred timber, and the chimney stood over it all with the indifference of stone.

A truck was parked up the road.

Lena noticed it on her way back to the car — a white flatbed with a company logo she couldn't read at this angle, and a man leaning against its fender, arms crossed, looking at the house with the professional detachment of someone assessing a job. He was maybe thirty, wearing a canvas jacket and a look she recognized, because she wore a version of it herself: the look of a person keeping watch over something.

He straightened when he saw her. She gave him the smile — automatic, well-maintained, the wall going up brick by brick — and got in her car. In the rearview mirror she watched him watch her leave, his head tilted slightly, like a man trying to place a face he'd seen somewhere before.

* * *

At home, the apartment was quiet in the way she had engineered it to be: one lamp, one chair, one mug in the dish rack. She pinned the courthouse postcard to the corkboard above her desk, where four thousand others hung in overlapping shingles, a wall of sky and monuments and beaches she'd never visited, each one carrying its small cargo of unsayable things. From a distance it looked like decoration. Up close it looked like what it was: a diary written in a language with no words longer than a sentence.

She poured two fingers of the wine she kept for evenings and stood at the board, reading random cards the way other people reread old letters.

*Dear Mom. The dentist says I grind my teeth. I said, at what?*

*Dear Mom. Bought a coat like yours. Doesn't fit right. Nothing does.*

*Dear Mom. Today is eleven years.*

She touched that one, the oldest-looking card on the board, its ink gone brown at the edges. And then, because the evening demanded it, she took down a fresh card — a lighthouse this time, storm-gray sea — and wrote tomorrow's date in the corner, because she liked to be ahead of the ritual, liked the feeling of one already written, waiting, like bread set out for someone expected home.

Outside, the wind was getting up, worrying the last leaves loose. Somewhere down the block a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, or at the particular nothing that dogs can see and people can't.

Lena Yarrow raised her glass to the wall of postcards, to the burned house on Hollow Creek Road, to the mother who had been unreachable for eleven years and perfectly addressed the entire time.

"To next time," she said aloud, to no one, and drank.

She did not know — could not, standing there in her one-lamp quiet with the lighthouse card drying on the desk — that the house had been keeping something for her. That behind the wardrobe, in the dark, in a tin box with a rusted lid, words had been accumulating all these years like water behind a dam. That the silence she had treated as a shelter had been, all along, a held breath.

Three days later, a notice would appear stapled to the snow fencing, official and orange, announcing a demolition date.

But that was three days away, and tonight the fox — or the cat, or the nothing — crossed the parking lot below her window, paused, and looked up at her light for a long moment before dissolving into the dark.
