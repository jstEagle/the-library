## 6. A Name in the Margin

The rain had stopped by the time I got back to my apartment, but the car smelled like it — wet wool and old coffee, the letters still warm from my hands even though I'd left them in the glovebox. I carried them up the stairs like something rescued from a flood. Which, I supposed, they were.

My apartment was the third floor of a house that had once been someone's pride and was now four units and a shared laundry. I had lived there nine years and had never hung a picture. The walls were bare the way a held breath is bare. I set the tin box on the kitchen table, under the one working bulb, and sat down across from it like it was a guest I hadn't invited.

Forty-one letters. I'd counted them in the car, twice, because counting was something my hands could do while my mind refused to catch up. Forty-one letters in my mother's handwriting, addressed to me, stamped, sealed, and never sent. The stamps were the worst part. Someone had licked them, pressed them down, walked them to the edge of the mailbox — and then turned around. Forty-one times.

I made tea I didn't drink. I read the fourth letter, then the fifth, and it was in the fifth — dated March of a year I had spent two thousand miles away, a year I could account for almost hour by hour, a year in which I had received exactly nothing from Halloway Street — that I found the name.

*Your sister has the croup again. Ruthie won't sleep unless the radio is on, so we listen to the farm report like it's opera, the two of us on the kitchen floor.*

I read the sentence four times. Then I read it aloud, because sometimes the ear catches what the eye refuses.

*Your sister.*

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below me, a television laughed at something in unison with its audience. I sat very still, the way you do when the floor of a room you've trusted all your life turns out to be a trapdoor.

Ruthie. Born — I did the arithmetic against the letter's date, against the dates of the letters before it, working backward through references to a pregnancy I had skimmed past at two in the morning in a parked car, assuming *the baby* meant a neighbor's, a cousin's, anyone's — born roughly fourteen months after I left home. Born while I was sleeping in a hostel in Spokane, telling a man at a diner counter that I was an only child, that my mother was dead, that both things were easier than the truth.

I had not been the only daughter. I had not even been the only daughter for very long. The ground didn't shift so much as it revealed itself to have been moving the whole time, the way a riverbank does — slowly, and then all at once you're standing somewhere else.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I read all forty-one letters again by lamplight, this time hunting the name the way you hunt a stain in a patterned carpet — once seen, everywhere. Ruthie at two, eating sand at the lake. Ruthie at four, refusing to wear anything but a raincoat. Ruthie learning to write her own name and getting it backward, *eih tuR*, so that my mother framed the page anyway and hung it by the stove, where the grease had softened the paper. My mother wrote about her the way I had always wanted to be written about — with a humor that wasn't armor, with a tenderness that didn't apologize for itself. I catalogued my own jealousy the way you catalogue weather: noted, expected, passing through.

But there were other entries, and these I read slower.

*Ruthie asked today where her big sister lives. I said far away. She asked if far away was past the water tower. I said yes. She said she'd walk there someday, past the water tower, and I said I believed her.*

And later, the letters thinning, the handwriting tightening like a fist:

*Ruthie saw your picture in my drawer. She asked who it was. I said it was her sister. She said she didn't have a sister. I didn't correct her. I'm telling you this so you know what your silence costs, and so you know I'm not innocent of it either. We have both been paying the toll. We just never took the same road.*

I put that letter down and walked to the window. The street below was doing its ordinary midnight business — a cat, a car alarm that gave up halfway through its complaint, the blue flicker of someone's television. All of it continuing as if the world had not just been reorganized around a single word on a page.

She didn't have a sister. Except she did. Except I did. Except we were two halves of the same silence, raised on opposite sides of it, each of us told the other was a locked room.

* * *

By morning I had a plan, and the plan was this: find Ruthie Yarrow.

I started where all my searches started, which is to say in denial of the obvious. I could have called the aunt — Aunt Dora, who signed her Christmas cards with a flourish and had answered my calls over the years with the warm vagueness of a woman guarding a door. I could have asked her directly: *Do I have a sister?* But asking Dora meant admitting I hadn't known, and admitting I hadn't known meant explaining why I hadn't called home in eleven years, and that conversation had a locked door behind it that I wasn't ready to pick.

So: phone books. The library on Delancey still kept a shelf of them, regional directories going back decades, and I sat among them from opening until the librarians began stacking chairs, running my finger down columns of Yarrows like a woman reading tea leaves. Yarrow, A. Yarrow, D. Yarrow, M. — my mother, listed in the old ones, back when the family still appeared in print, back when I was still a daughter in the world's ledger. And then, in a directory from six years ago, in the town of Marrow's Bend, three counties east:

**YARROW, R. — 41 CEDAR LANE.**

R. I sat with that R for a long time. It might have been a Robert, a Ronald, a Ruth — the initial was a door with the name scratched off. But the town was right. Marrow's Bend was where my mother had grown up, where Aunt Dora still lived, where the family gravity pooled. A Yarrow in Marrow's Bend, appearing in the record six years after I'd left, was either a coincidence or a sister, and I had spent my whole life mistaking coincidences for mercy.

I copied the address onto a postcard — of course I did; the habit preceded thought — and then looked at the blank side and understood that I had nothing to say that could survive being read by a stranger who happened to share my blood. I put the card in my pocket instead.

* * *

The parish records were Dora's idea, though she didn't know it. My mother had been baptized at St. Ambrose in Marrow's Bend, had been married there — badly, briefly, to my father, before he became a story instead of a man — and if Ruthie had been born in that county, there would be a record. Baptisms were public enough. I told myself this on the four-hour bus ride east, watching the country go by in its brown early-spring rags, telling myself that what I was doing was research, genealogy, a harmless hobby. Telling myself I was not about to walk into a church and ask a stranger to confirm that my entire life had contained a person I'd been told wasn't there.

St. Ambrose sat on a hill above the town like a held breath in stone. Inside it was cool and smelled of candle wax and old plaster, and a woman in a cardigan the color of oatmeal looked up from a desk by the vestry and asked if she could help me.

"I'm doing family research," I said. The sentence came out smooth. Eleven years of deflection had built me a whole vocabulary of smooth sentences. "The Yarrow family. I believe there was a baptism here — a daughter, born about twenty-six years ago."

"Name?"

Here was the moment I could have said R. and left it open. Here was the moment I could have protected myself with the initial, the way the phone book had. Instead I heard myself say it, my mother's word, the word from the kitchen floor and the radio and the croup:

"Ruthie. Ruth Yarrow."

The woman turned to a cabinet, pulled a drawer, ran her finger down a ledger written in the fading ink of a parish that had seen better funding. And there it was. I watched her finger stop. I watched her lips move slightly, reading to herself, the way people do when the name is ordinary and the moment is not.

"Ruth Anne Yarrow," she said. "Baptized April the ninth. Parents listed as Miriam Yarrow." She looked up. A small, careful pause. "Only the one parent listed."

Only the one. My mother had stood in this church, or some church, with a baby in her arms and a pen in her hand, and had written down one name. Mine would have been a two-name line. My father's, when he existed in records, had always been there, taking up space, taking up air. But Ruthie got one name, one parent — my mother, alone, signing for both of them, the way she'd signed for everything in the end.

"Is there a date of birth?" I asked.

"March second." The woman smiled the small smile of someone who helps strangers with dead relatives all day and has learned to keep her curiosity in a drawer of its own. "Twenty-six years ago. Is that the family you're looking for?"

"Yes," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize. It cracked, just slightly, on the yes, and the woman's smile adjusted — not pity, exactly, but the professional kindness of a person who has decided not to ask.

I thanked her. I walked back down the aisle past the candles, past the plaster saint with his hands open in the gesture of a man forever explaining something, and out into the cold.

March second. I stood on the church steps doing the arithmetic I'd already done, checking it, needing it to be wrong and knowing it wasn't. Ruthie had been born on the second of March. And I knew — with the sick certainty of a person who has finally located the wound — that March second was the week I had left home. The week of the argument about my father. The week of the last conversation, the unfinished one, the one I had been answering with postcards ever since. My mother had been pregnant when she stood in that doorway and watched me go. She had been carrying the sister I didn't know existed while I walked to the bus station with a duffel bag and eleven years of silence in my pocket.

She hadn't told me. Or I hadn't stayed long enough to be told. The two possibilities sat in me like stones in a riverbed, and I could not tell which one had carved the channel.

* * *

I found a diner near the bus depot — of course a diner; my whole emotional life was conducted at diner counters — and ordered coffee I didn't want and sat with the letters spread out like evidence. Which is what they were. I had begun to think of them that way: Exhibit A, the sister. Exhibit B, the toll. Exhibit C, my mother's handwriting getting smaller over the years, the way handwriting does when the person writing has decided no one is listening.

The waitress refilled my cup without being asked. Outside the window, Marrow's Bend went about its business: a hardware store, a funeral home with tasteful lettering, a water tower — *past the water tower* — standing over the town like a lighthouse for a sea that wasn't there.

Ruthie had said she'd walk past the water tower someday. Ruthie had been four. Ruthie was twenty-six now, and somewhere on Cedar Lane or wherever the phone book had lied to me or told the truth, she was living a life that contained, presumably, no sister. A life that had been constructed, brick by careful brick, around the same absence that had constructed mine. We were both buildings built around the same empty room.

I took out the postcard from my pocket and looked at the address I'd copied. 41 Cedar Lane. It might be years out of date. It might be a Robert Yarrow, a Ronald, a man who had never eaten sand at a lake or worn a raincoat for a year or gotten his name backward on a page his mother framed by the stove.

But it might not.

I thought about walking there. I thought about standing on a porch in a town where my mother had grown up, asking a stranger *are you my sister* the way you'd ask *do you have the time.* I thought about the slam of a door, which was the ending my imagination kept producing, because my imagination had been trained on silence and knew no other genre.

The coffee had gone cold. I drank it anyway. Then I took out a pen, and on the back of the postcard — the one addressed to R. Yarrow, 41 Cedar Lane, Marrow's Bend — I wrote nothing. I turned it over. I turned it back. The blank side sat there like a held breath.

Eleven years of postcards, and for the first time in my life, I could not write one. Because this one wouldn't be a ritual. This one would be a door, and doors open from both sides, and I had spent my whole adult life on the side of the door where you don't have to see what walks through.

The waitress came by. "You need anything, hon?"

"No," I said. Then, because the word came out before I could stop it, before the practiced smile could catch it: "Actually. Do you know Cedar Lane?"

She did. Everyone in a town like this knows every street the way you know the veins on the back of your hand. "Sure. Out past the tower, take the left at the feed store. Why, you got family out there?"

The question hung in the air between us, ordinary as salt.

"I'm going to find out," I said.

And I picked up the postcard, and the tin box, and the forty-one letters that my mother had written and never sent, and I walked out into the cold light of Marrow's Bend, toward the water tower, toward the left at the feed store, toward a door that might open onto a sister or a stranger or nothing at all — carrying, in my pocket, the one blank postcard I had ever been unable to fill.
