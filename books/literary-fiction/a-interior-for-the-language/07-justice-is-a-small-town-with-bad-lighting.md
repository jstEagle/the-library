## 7. Justice Is a Small Town with Bad Lighting

The bus took nine hours to get to Harlow Bend, and for eight of them Lena watched the landscape flatten the way grief flattens a person — slowly, then all at once. The hills of the coast gave way to scrubland, the scrubland to corn, the corn to nothing much at all, fields lying fallow under a sky so wide and pale it looked like it had been bleached by decades of looking down on the same small sorrows.

She had brought the letters in a manila envelope wedged inside her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel them shift when the bus hit the seams in the road. Eleven letters. She had read them all now, some of them four and five times, and she had begun to know her mother's late voice the way you know a song you can't stop humming — the dry jokes, the lists of small expenses, the way she circled back to certain grievances the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. *Your uncle Dale says hello, which is a lie, but it's a polite lie, and this town runs on polite lies like a mill runs on water.*

The letters had names in them. Names were how you traveled, her mother had taught her without meaning to — you followed a name the way water follows a slope. And the name that appeared most often, after Lena's own, was *Marion*. Aunt Marion, who had signed the sympathy card eleven years ago with a single line: *She's at peace now. Let her rest.* Aunt Marion, who had answered the phone once, years back, when Lena had worked up the nerve to call the house, and said, in a voice like a door closing, *There's nothing here for you, honey,* and hung up before Lena could ask what *nothing* meant.

The bus let her off at a gas station on the edge of Harlow Bend at 4:40 in the afternoon, and the light was already going bad. That was the thing about the place — she understood it within ten minutes. The light came in low and yellowed, filtered through dust and distance, and it made everything look like a photograph of itself. The main street was four blocks of brick facades with faded painted signs still ghosting across them: *HARDWARE — FEED — NOTARY*. A water tower stood over everything with the town's name peeling off it in long strips, so that from certain angles it read *HAR O BEND*, which felt more accurate. There was a church with a steeple, a diner with a neon sign that buzzed even in daylight, and a courthouse on a small rise at the end of the street, white and columned and lit — she would notice this later, and it would matter — by floodlights that came on before dusk, as if the building were afraid of the dark, or as if the dark were afraid of what the building kept.

She checked into the Starlite Motel because it was the only one, and because its name was a joke the town didn't know it was telling. The clerk, a boy of maybe nineteen with a jaw full of gum, looked at her a beat too long. Small towns read strangers like weather. She signed the register in a handwriting that wasn't quite hers and carried her bag to a room that smelled of cigarettes that had been apologized for but not removed.

She stood at the window a long time, holding the curtain aside two inches, watching the street. Learning it. Her mother had grown up four blocks from here. Had walked to school under that water tower, had sat in that diner, had — if the letters were true — come back to this town after the fire with nothing but a suitcase and a grudge and had lived eleven years writing to a daughter who never wrote back, in a house that stood until it burned, on a street Lena had never once visited.

*You could have come,* her mother had written in letter six. *You could have come any time in eleven years. I looked for your face in every bus that stopped.*

Lena had read that sentence in her car in the rain and had to pull over. Now, standing in a motel room in the town itself, she felt the sentence rearrange itself around her like weather closing in. She had come. Eleven years late, but she had come, and the only person left to receive her was the woman who had helped keep the letters in a tin box behind a wardrobe.

* * *

Marion Voss lived on Cypress Street, in a house that was the tidy twin of every other house on the street except for the hydrangeas, which were ruthless — blue and purple mounds crowding the porch steps like they were trying to get inside. Lena stood at the gate for a full minute before she opened it. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic. She had rehearsed this on the bus, in the diner, in the shower: *I'm Lena. I'm her daughter. I have the letters.* Three sentences. She had faced down editors, she had read her work to rooms full of strangers, she had smiled at a thousand people she wanted to walk away from. She could say three sentences.

She rang the bell. Through the door she heard a television go quiet, the particular hush of someone deciding whether to exist for visitors.

The door opened on a chain. One eye, one wedge of a face. Marion Voss was seventy-something, small-boned, with her sister's jaw and none of her sister's warmth — or rather, Lena thought, with a warmth that had been packed away somewhere, like china used only for funerals.

"We're not buying anything," the eye said.

"I'm not selling." Lena lifted the manila envelope so it showed in the gap. "I'm Lena. Lena Yarrow. Vera's daughter."

The chain did not come off. The eye moved — down to the envelope, back up to Lena's face — and something crossed it that was not surprise. That was the thing Lena would keep turning over later, at 2 a.m. in the Starlite's lumpy bed: it was not surprise. It was more like the look of someone finding, on their doorstep, a bill they had known was coming.

"Vera's been dead eleven years," Marion said.

"I know what she's been."

The chain slid off. The door opened the rest of the way, and Marion stepped back with a formality that was almost hospitality, almost ceremony, and said, "Well. You'd better come in before the neighbors get a look at you."

The house inside was a museum of a certain kind of life: plastic runners on the carpet, doilies under everything that would sit still, a console television, photographs on the wall in matching frames. Lena scanned them with a hunger she couldn't disguise — school portraits, wedding portraits, and there, third from the left, a woman with her own face aged forward, standing stiffly beside a man Lena recognized from the courthouse lawn, from the newspaper clipping she'd found online at 3 a.m. in a motel room: *Judge Dale Voss, 30 years on the bench.* He had his arm around Marion. Everyone was smiling the smile Lena knew from mirrors — the smile that ends conversations.

"Sit," Marion said, and Lena sat, on a plastic-slipcovered couch that exhaled coldly under her. Marion did not sit beside her. She sat opposite, in an armchair angled like a witness box, and folded her hands. "You look like her," she said, and it came out like an accusation.

"So people say."

"People say a lot of things in this town." Marion's eyes went to the envelope on Lena's knee. "Most of them aren't worth repeating."

"I have letters," Lena said. "Eleven of them. My mother wrote them to me. She never sent them because someone was taking them out of the mail." She let that sit in the doily-silenced room. "They were behind her wardrobe. In a tin. A demolition crew found them."

For the first time Marion moved — a small thing, a recrossing of ankles, a hand smoothing a skirt that didn't need smoothing. "Wardrobe was mine," she said. "I bought it at the estate sale. Everything had to go somewhere."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting today."

Lena felt the anger arrive the way she always did — not hot but structural, load-bearing, a beam swinging into place. It had been her engine for eleven years, though she had mostly run it in idle, aimed at herself. Now it had somewhere to go. "You knew," she said. "You knew she wrote to me. You knew the letters weren't going through. And you let her sit in that house for eleven years writing to a daughter who never answered — let her think I'd chosen—" Her voice caught on the shape of it. "You let her die believing I didn't care."

Marion's face did something complicated and then settled into a flatness that was clearly practiced, worn smooth as a river stone. "Vera was not well, after the fire," she said. "She said things. She believed things. A person can write anything down. That doesn't make it true, and it doesn't make it mail."

"Eleven letters."

"Eleven years of a sick woman talking to herself." Marion's voice stayed level, and that was the most frightening thing in the room. "You want me to say I'm sorry the mail was unreliable. Fine. The mail in this town has always been unreliable. But don't you come into my house and tell me what my sister believed. You weren't here. You weren't *anywhere*."

"I was eleven hundred miles away because—" Lena stopped. Because of what she'd seen. Because of the gasoline cans. Because she was sixteen and had run instead of spoken, and had spent her whole adult life converting that silence into postcards. She heard herself about to say it — felt the whole confession lean forward in her chest — and then looked at Marion's face, at the flat watchfulness there, and understood with a cold clarity that this woman would use it. Whatever Lena said in this room would be carried to the courthouse on the hill by nightfall, weighed, filed, and answered.

She closed her mouth.

"Cat got your tongue?" Marion said softly. "That runs in the family too."

Lena stood. The room seemed to tilt slightly, the photographs watching. "Did she ask about me? At the end?"

Marion looked at her for a long moment, and for one unguarded second — Lena would swear to this later, to Ruthie, who wasn't yet in her life, to anyone who'd listen — the flatness cracked, and underneath it was something old and tired and drowning. "She asked about you every day of her life," Marion said. "And every day I told her the same thing. That you were better off."

"Better off than what?"

But the crack had sealed. Marion rose too, smoothing her skirt, and walked her to the door with the brisk courtesy of a woman seeing off a salesman. On the porch, under the ruthless hydrangeas, she paused with her hand on the screen door.

"You should go home," she said. "Whatever you think you found in that box — this town has a way with people who dig. Ask anyone. Ask the ones who are still allowed to talk." She almost smiled. "And Lena. Your mother's letters. You should burn them. It's what she'd have wanted."

"She wanted them *mailed*," Lena said. "For eleven years. That's the one thing I know for certain now."

The screen door shut. Lena stood on the walk a moment, and from inside she heard the television come back on, too loud, a wall of sound built in a hurry, the way you'd build a wall.

* * *

She walked, because she couldn't stand the motel yet. The bad light had thickened into true dusk, and the floodlights on the courthouse came on all at once, white and total, erasing the shadows the building had been growing all day. She stopped across the street and looked up at it. A man was coming down the steps — she recognized him from the photograph, older, heavier, but unmistakable: the jaw, the smile, the arm that had once rested on Marion's shoulder in a matching frame. Judge Dale Voss paused on the sidewalk, checked his watch, and looked — casually, the way a man looks at traffic — directly at her.

He held the look for two seconds. Three. Then he nodded, one small courteous dip of the head, the nod a judge gives a stranger, and walked to his car.

Lena stood in the floodlit street with the letters against her ribs and understood, with a certainty that felt less like discovery than like confirmation, that she had been seen. That she had been expected. That somewhere in this town of polite lies, a phone had already rung, and a voice like a closing door had already said: *She's here. She has the box.*

Her mother's last unsent letter was still folded in the envelope, the one she hadn't been able to finish reading, the one that stopped mid-sentence. She had assumed the sentence was unfinished because her mother had died.

Standing in the courthouse light, she suddenly wasn't sure her mother had died before she could finish it.

She was sure, now, that someone else had.
