## 1. The Tuning Fork

The dead are punctual in their way. Vivian Wetherell had kept every appointment of her ninety-one years—dentist, solicitor, the annual flu jab—and so it seemed only proper that she should die on schedule too, quietly, between the last frost and the first crocuses, with her affairs in order and her son already rehearsing his grief for the reception line.

I arrived at the house four days after the funeral, which is to say I arrived when the piano needed me.

Let me explain how a person comes to be let into a dead woman's house by a housekeeper who doesn't quite remember her name. My name is Quentin Eastwick. I have tuned the Wetherell piano for thirty-eight years, and in all that time I have been introduced perhaps six times, always as "our tuner," as though I were an appliance that walked. This did not wound me. I want to be clear about that, because people assume invisibility is a wound, and mostly it is a trade. You give up being seen and in exchange you get to hear everything. It is not a bad rate. I have known worse ones—marriages, for instance, where the exchange runs the other direction.

I let myself in with the key Mrs. Okafor handed me without looking up from her list of casseroles. The hall smelled the way it always smelled—beeswax, cold radiators, and under it something older, the smell of a house holding its breath—but there was a new note in it now, the particular hush of a place whose center of gravity has been removed. A house knows when its owner dies before anyone tells it. The pipes go quiet differently. The stairs creak with less conviction, as if they've stopped performing.

The parlor piano was a Bechstein Model V, 1911, ivory gone the color of weak tea, and it was out of tune in the specific way it went out of tune every February: the bass strings sagging a full eight cents flat, the middle register fighting the dry heat of the radiators like a sibling. I set my case down on the parquet—I knew exactly which boards announced themselves and stepped over them out of habit, though there was no one left upstairs to disturb—and opened the lid.

Before I touched a single pin, I did what I always do. I listened to the house.

People think tuning is about the piano. It isn't. The piano is just the instrument you can see. The house is the instrument you can't, and it has been playing continuously since the day it was built—a long, slow piece with movements in steam and contraction, in the tick of joists warming at dawn and settling at dusk. Every house has a voice. Most people only hear it when it complains. I have spent my life learning the complaints by heart.

The radiators were the first section of the orchestra this morning. The cast-iron radiator in the parlor—the one beneath the window, Victorian, older than the plumbing that fed it—was making its familiar sound, a faint rhythmic knocking I had catalogued years ago as *the metronome*. It knocked at roughly sixty beats per minute whenever the heat came up, and for thirty-eight years I had privately used it to calibrate my pitch before ever opening my fork. A-440, more or less, if you counted the knock's decay. The radiator in the front hall was a different animal: a high thin whistle on the inhale of each heating cycle, which I had long ago named *the widow*, because it sounded like a woman drawing breath to say something she would then decide against saying. The kitchen radiator groaned—*the porter*, I called him, an old man complaining about stairs he'd climbed ten thousand times. And the third-floor radiator—

Well. We will come to the third floor. We always come to the third floor eventually, whether we mean to or not.

I tuned. Middle C first, then fifths outward, the way my father taught me, stretching the octaves just slightly because human ears prefer a piano that sounds bigger than it is. My hands knew this instrument the way you know a face. There was a scratch on the fallboard above middle C, shaped like a comma, that Marcus Wetherell had put there with a compass at age eleven while I watched and said nothing. There was a water ring on the right cheek of the case from a glass of sherry Vivian had set down in 1994 during her husband's last illness, listening to me play Chopin's Opus 28 No. 6 because she'd asked for "something short and sad" and I'd obliged without comment. She had listened with her eyes closed and her hands folded, and when it ended she'd said, "You're wasted on pianos, Miss Eastwick," which was the closest thing to intimacy she ever offered me, and I had treasured it the way you treasure a coin from a country that no longer exists.

Grief, if you're wondering, feels like this: you tune a dead woman's piano and your eyes fill at the water ring, and you wipe them on your sleeve and keep turning the pin, because the C is still four cents sharp and grief does not excuse you from the work. I have never believed in stopping the craft for the feeling. The feeling can wait in the car. The C cannot.

* * *

By eleven o'clock the parlor was done and I had moved to the inventory.

This was another part of the trade nobody knew about. Every visit, I made notes—not just on the piano but on the house entire. Which radiators knocked, which valves wept rust onto the floorboards, which windows had swollen in their frames. I kept it in a series of notebooks, plain black, bought in bulk from the stationer on Collier Street, and I told myself for decades that this was professionalism, thoroughness, the mark of a craftsman who took the whole acoustic environment seriously.

It was only later—much later, embarrassingly late—that I understood the notebooks were something else entirely. But that morning, four days after the funeral, they were still just notebooks, and I was still just a tuner, and I wrote:

*Parlor rad.: metronome steady, 60 bpm, bearing fine. Hall rad.: widow louder than usual—valve seat likely worn through; recommend replacement, will not be replaced. Kitchen: porter unchanged. Dining room rad. silent—airlock? Or simply sulking. House generally quieter than any previous Feb. Grief acoustics.*

That last phrase I underlined. *Grief acoustics.* A house in mourning holds its resonance differently. Sound dies faster in it. I noticed it most in the stairwell, where Marcus's footsteps—he was up there somewhere, on the phone with solicitors, his voice doing that thing where it got louder to prove he was calm—fell into the well of the stairs and vanished without echo, as if the house were swallowing them whole rather than passing them along.

Mrs. Okafor came in twice. Once to dust around me, which she did with the delicacy of a woman defusing something, and once to say, "You'll want to lock up when you go, Miss—" and then pause, and I supplied "Eastwick," and she said, "Yes, of course," in the tone of someone who has just been told a fact she will lose again by Thursday. Thirty-eight years. Six introductions. I was furniture that invoiced quarterly.

I don't say this bitterly. I want that on the record too, because everything I'm going to tell you will sound like an accusation eventually, and I would hate for you to think I didn't understand my own part in it. Nobody made me invisible. I applied for the position. I interviewed excellently. Vivian Wetherell hired me on the spot in the spring of 1987, and I have often thought since that the interview ran both ways, and that she saw in me precisely what I was offering: a woman who could stand in a room and become its wallpaper, who could listen without appearing to, who could be trusted to hear everything and repeat nothing. I thought I was selling discretion. She was buying silence. The difference between those two things is the size of a human life, but it took me thirty-five years to measure it.

That morning, though, I was innocent. That morning I was just a woman with a tuning hammer and a black notebook, doing her work in a dead woman's parlor, and the worst thing I knew about the Wetherell family was that they paid promptly and tipped at Christmas.

* * *

The first wrongness came at half past twelve.

I had finished the music room—the upright there, a Broadwood, was the least-loved instrument in the house and accordingly the most honest, out of tune in ways that told the truth about neglect—and I was packing my felt mutes when I heard the floorboard above me.

Not the third floor. The second. The landing outside the master bedroom, the board I privately called *the announcer*, because it spoke so clearly that for years I had used it to track Vivian's nightly progress to bed: eleven-forty, the announcer, three steps, the bedroom door, quiet until morning. I could have set clocks by her. I had, in fact, once done so mentally, and been right within two minutes.

But Vivian was at Halloway & Sons, in a drawer, awaiting cremation. Marcus was downstairs—I could hear him now in the study, his phone voice gone hoarse. Mrs. Okafor had left at noon. And yet the announcer spoke, one clear syllable of pine, followed by nothing.

I stood very still in the music room with a mute wedge in my hand and did the arithmetic of the house. Front door: locked behind me, key in my pocket. Back door: bolted, Mrs. Okafor's habit. Windows: painted shut on the second floor since the eighties; I knew because I'd heard Vivian try one during a heat wave in '89 and curse with a vocabulary that would have surprised the parish council. So whoever had crossed the landing had either been in the house all along or had entered it in some way I couldn't reconstruct, and both possibilities sat in my chest like swallowed stones.

I told myself: pipes. Old houses make noises; boards flex; thermal expansion is a documented phenomenon. I am a professional. Professionals do not run through clients' houses chasing ghosts. I finished packing my kit with hands that were almost steady, carried it down to the parlor, and stood there deciding.

Here is the thing about useful invisibility: it cuts both ways. The same quality that lets you hear everything makes you the last person anyone tells anything. If there was someone in this house, no one would think to warn me, because no one remembered I was in it.

I went back up. Not bravely—I want to be honest about the fact that my heart was going like the hall radiator's whistle—but because the alternative was to pack my case and leave, and some stubborn guild instinct in me refused to abandon an instrument mid-service. Also, I will admit, thirty-eight years of listening had bred in me a terrible curiosity. I had spent decades hearing the shape of other people's lives through walls and never once seeing the thing itself. If the house was finally going to show me something, I found I lacked the character to look away.

The landing was empty. The announcer board bore no weight. The master bedroom door stood open on a room already stripped for the estate sale—bed bare, wardrobe gaping, Vivian's forty years of ordered life reduced to lot numbers on little white stickers. The light came through the curtains in dusty bars. Nothing moved.

Except.

On the dressing table—Vivian's dressing table, the one object in the room not yet stickered, as if even the appraisers had flinched—someone had placed a photograph. Face down.

I did not touch it. I want you to know that. Whatever else I did in the weeks that followed, whatever trespasses I committed and however thoroughly I repaid the trust this family never knew they'd placed in me, I did not turn that photograph over that day. I stood in the doorway of the dead woman's stripped bedroom, listening to the radiators begin their afternoon cycle—the metronome starting up below me, the widow whistling her unsaid sentence in the hall—and I looked at the brown paper back of a photograph I had never seen in thirty-eight years of visiting this house, and I understood with complete certainty that it had not been there yesterday.

Because I would have known. I knew this house the way I knew the Bechstein's pin block. A new object on that table would have sounded different, somehow, even through a floor. Call it superstition. Call it thirty-eight years of attention finally paying out.

From downstairs, Marcus's voice rose through the stairwell and died without echo: "—no, the sale proceeds Friday, tell them the title's clean—"

Clean. He said it twice, the way men do when they're persuading themselves.

I left the photograph where it lay, gathered my coat and case, and locked the front door behind me with the key Mrs. Okafor had given me without looking up. On the front step I paused, as I always did, for the ritual goodbye I never performed aloud: a moment of attention given to the house entire, top to bottom, pipe to slate.

And there—at the edge of hearing, at the very threshold of what thirty-eight years had trained into me—I caught something I had never caught before. Not from inside the house.

From across the street. Someone standing in the cold by the low wall of the empty corner property, motionless, watching the windows. Watching, I would have sworn, the exact window behind which a photograph lay face-down on a dead woman's dressing table.

They didn't move, and neither did I, and the distance between us filled up with the sound of the hall radiator whistling its long unfinished sentence through the brickwork, out through the walls, into the street, to her.

Then a bus went past, and the pavement was empty, and I told myself I had imagined it.

I have never once, in thirty-eight years of professional listening, imagined anything.
