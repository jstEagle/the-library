## 5. What the Executor Knows

The email address was printed at the bottom of a probate notice Theo found by accident, which is to say he had been searching for it for eleven days and needed the accident to make it feel like fate rather than obsession.

*Okafor & Associates, Estate Administration. In re: Vega, M.*

He drafted the first message four times. The first draft was too long — six paragraphs of explanation that read, on rereading, like a hostage letter. The second was too short: *I knew Marisol.* The third tried to be charming and came out sounding like a man selling timeshares in his own grief. The fourth he deleted entirely and sat with his hands flat on the desk, looking at the postcards pinned above his monitor, forty-one of them now, each one a half-sentence addressed to a lot that no longer contained a house.

He wrote the fifth draft in under a minute.

*Ms. Okafor — My name is Theo Sandoval. I was a friend of Marisol Vega's. I understand you're administering the sale of her estate, including her papers. I'm not writing to contest anything or to claim anything. I'm writing because I heard one of her songs on the radio last month, credited to someone I've never met, and I've realized I don't know as much about her last year as I thought I did. If there's any way to see what's in those boxes, I'd be grateful. I can come to you. I can sign whatever needs signing. — T.S.*

He sent it at 11:47 p.m., then lay awake doing the math on how long a reply might reasonably take. Two days, if she was efficient. A week, if she was careful. Never, if the estate's lawyer read it first and flagged it as the kind of message executors are trained to route straight to the shredder.

The reply came in nine hours.

---

Dana Okafor answered her email at 8:32 a.m. from the kitchen table, coffee going cold beside her laptop, because her daughter had finally slept through the night for the first time in three weeks and Dana had decided to spend the gift on the Vega file instead of sleep.

She'd been an executor's assistant once, twenty years ago, before law school, and she remembered the shape of these letters. They arrived in categories. There were creditors, who wrote in the language of invoices. There were relatives, who wrote in the language of grievance. And there were *the others* — the ones who never appeared in any document but had clearly loved the deceased, or believed they had, and who surfaced during probate like fish rising to a hole in the ice. Her mentor had called them the weather system. Every estate had one. You couldn't control it; you could only decide whether to stand out in it.

This one wrote well. That was the first thing she noticed. No demands, no claims, no wounded capital letters. Just a man saying, in effect, *I have been standing outside this story for a long time and I would like to see through the window.*

She checked the file. Vega, Marisol — died 2004, fire, no will located until 2019, when a cousin in Sacramento produced one from a safe-deposit box nobody had known about. The rebuilt house on the cove, held in trust since construction, now instructed to be sold. Personal effects — including, per the will's own language, "all notebooks, recordings, and papers" — to be catalogued and either sold with the house or consigned to storage. Dana had been on the file eight months. She had inventoried two hundred and eleven items herself, wearing white cotton gloves in a house that smelled of new lumber and old salt, and she had cried exactly once, alone, over a grocery list written on the back of a setlist.

She typed:

*Mr. Sandoval — Thank you for writing. Before I answer your question, I need to ask you one: what is your relationship to the estate? Not legally — I can read the file for that. I mean actually. Were you family, friend, collaborator? It matters for how I handle this, and I'd rather know now than find out later. Also: which song?*

She hesitated over the last question, then left it in. She wasn't sure why. Instinct, maybe. Or the sense that this man had spent years circling something and deserved to be asked a direct question for once.

---

*Which song,* Theo read, and laughed out loud in his empty kitchen, a sound that startled him.

He typed the answer with both hands, fast, the way you'd say something if you were afraid of stopping:

*"Saltglass." She wrote it in my kitchen in March 2003. Yellow legal pad, blue pen, the second verse crossed out twice. As far as I knew, it had never been recorded. Last month a station in Santa Cruz played it. The credit said E. Brandt. I need to know if there are more songs I've never heard. I think that's the truest sentence I've written in fifteen years.*

He hit send before he could revise it into something safer.

---

Dana read it twice.

*Saltglass.* She knew the title. It was on the spine label of Box 4, the one she hadn't finished cataloguing because every time she opened it she found another notebook and another notebook, yellow legal pads mostly, dozens of them, filled edge to edge in handwriting that slanted hard to the right like it was leaning into wind. And interleaved among them — this was the part she hadn't put in any report yet — pages in a second hand. Smaller, rounder, patient. Annotations. Arguments. Little marginal notes that read like conversation: *no — try it slower here*, and *this line is better than you think*, and once, simply, *yes.*

She had assumed the second handwriting belonged to whoever Brandt was. The radio station's website had been helpful there; the interview was still up, and she'd listened to it while driving back from the county recorder's office, one hand on the wheel, listening to a man with a warm voice call the song "ours" and decline, three separate times, to say whose kitchen it was written in.

Now here was a man claiming it was written in *his* kitchen.

Two kitchens. One song. Dana had administered fourteen estates and she had learned that the dead leave behind not one story but a weather system, and her job was not to decide which version was true. Her job was to keep the papers dry until the truth, whatever it was, could stand in front of them.

But she was also, she admitted, a person. And the person in her wanted to see these two men in the same room.

She wrote back:

*Mr. Sandoval — There are boxes. More than you're imagining. Forty-one notebooks, roughly sixty loose pages of lyrics, three reel-to-reel tapes, one shoebox of photographs, and assorted correspondence. Most of it has never been published, performed, or catalogued publicly. The will directs me to sell or store it all by the end of next month.*

*I'm going to offer you something I don't offer everyone, so listen carefully: I'll let you into the house to see the papers. Conditions: you sign a confidentiality agreement, you touch nothing without gloves, and you tell me honestly, in person, who you were to her. Not for the file. For me. Executors get lied to professionally and I'm tired of it.*

*I'm at the house Thursdays. Come Thursday. Bring the gloves yourself if you want; I only have one pair and they're mine.*

*Dana Okafor*

---

Theo read the email standing up, because sitting felt insufficient. He read it three times. *Forty-one notebooks.* He did the arithmetic involuntarily — fifteen years of his own silence against forty-one notebooks of hers, and the ratio made him dizzy. All that time he'd been mailing postcards to a burned lot, mourning a body of work that had, apparently, survived the fire. Survived it in boxes. Sat in a rebuilt house on the same footprint while he drove past the old address every Sunday like a pilgrim visiting an empty church.

*Who you were to her.*

That was the question, wasn't it. He'd been rehearsing versions of the answer for fifteen years — *friend, neighbor, almost, nearly, the man who — * — and none of the drafts had ever survived contact with daylight. Now a stranger with a law license wanted the real one, in person, and he found that the prospect terrified him more than the boxes did.

He typed: *Thursday works. What time?*

Her reply was one line: *Nine a.m. The gate sticks. Lift, don't pull.*

---

Wednesday night, Dana drove out to the cove alone, because she wanted to see the house the way he would see it — cold, unprepared, at the hour when the light went strange.

She stood in the kitchen where the grocery list had undone her, and she looked at the counter, and she thought about the two men. The one on the radio, who said *ours* like a man holding a door open. The one in the emails, who wrote about a yellow legal pad like it was a relic. She had spent eight months as the only living person who knew everything in those boxes, and she understood suddenly, standing there in the blue hour with the harbor lights coming on below, that she was about to stop being the only one — and that whatever happened between the two of them, the papers would never again belong only to the dead.

She took Box 4 down from the shelf, set it on the kitchen table, and looked at the top notebook for a long time. Then she closed the box and put it back, higher, behind the others.

Some things you want witnessed properly.

---

Theo didn't sleep Thursday-eve. He laid out clothes like a kid before a recital, then mocked himself for it, then did it anyway. At 6 a.m. he bought a thermos of coffee and a second cup, because his landlady — who had extracted the whole story from him over the fence two nights ago, wringing it out of him with the patience of a woman who'd buried two husbands — had said, "Bring the woman something. Executors are people. People run on caffeine and being seen."

So at 9:04 a.m. he stood at a sticking gate on the coast with two cups of coffee and a pair of white cotton gloves he'd bought at an archival supply shop, which the clerk had assumed were for a museum and which Theo had not corrected.

The gate stuck. He lifted. It gave.

Dana Okafor was smaller than her emails, quicker than her voice on the phone, and she looked at the second coffee cup for exactly one second before taking it, which told him everything about how the morning would go.

"You brought gloves," she said.

"I brought gloves."

"And coffee."

"I'm told executors are people."

"We're the worst kind," she said, and led him inside. "We're people with keys to other people's whole lives. Sit down. Before I show you anything, you're going to tell me who you were to Marisol Vega, and you're going to take as long as you need, and I'm going to sit right here and know whether you're lying, because I've gotten very good at it."

Theo set down his cup. Above the sink, through the window, the harbor was doing its morning silver thing, indifferent and beautiful.

"She lived upstairs from me for two years," he began, and then stopped, because the true beginning was further back than that, and Dana waited with the patience of a woman who had all day and all the boxes.

* * *

It took him forty minutes. He told it badly and then, somewhere around the part about the yellow legal pad and the crossed-out second verse, he told it well. Dana didn't interrupt once. When he got to the fire she closed her eyes briefly, the way you do at a familiar prayer, and when he got to the radio she opened them again and watched him with an expression he couldn't file.

When he finished, she was quiet for a moment. Then she stood, went to the shelf, and brought down Box 4.

"There's something you should see before you see anything else," she said. "And I want you to understand I haven't shown this to anyone. Not the cousin, not the lawyers, not the buyer who's sniffing around the property. Because I couldn't decide whose business it was." She set the box on the table between them. "I think maybe it's yours."

She lifted the lid. Notebooks, edge to edge, yellow covers gone soft at the corners. And on top, loose, a single page in a second handwriting — small, round, patient — with a note in the margin that Theo read upside down before Dana turned it toward him.

*This one's about him. Don't change a word of it. — M.*

Theo's hands, when he reached for the page, were already shaking, and Dana slid the box closer and said, gently, the way you'd say it across a hospital bed:

"Mr. Sandoval. There are forty-one of these. And I don't think the second handwriting belongs to Elias Brandt."
