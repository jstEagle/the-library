## 1. The Weight of Unplayed Keys

The conservatory's concert hall had four hundred and twelve seats, and every one of them knew how to hold its breath.

Emrys Brightwater had counted them twice—once out of professional obligation, when the seating survey came through and someone had to verify the numbers against the cleaning rotation, and once for no reason at all, standing in the aisle with her mop bucket dripping, remembering what it felt like to make all those seats lean forward at once. She'd been seventeen then. The portrait said so. It hung in the east corridor, third from the tall window, in a heavy gilt frame that the maintenance budget had never quite gotten around to rehanging straight, so that the girl in it appeared to be listening to something slightly below eye level—some music only she could hear, pitched low, near the floorboards.

Emrys cleaned around that portrait every Tuesday and Friday. She did not look at it. This was a discipline she had refined over three years, the way other people refined their scales: daily, methodically, until the not-looking became its own kind of technique. You didn't look at the portrait the way you didn't look down between the rails of a high bridge. Not because you feared you'd fall, exactly. Because some part of you wanted to know what falling was like again, and that part could not be trusted on Tuesdays or Fridays or any day ending in *y*.

"Morning, Emrys."

It was Dev, the security man, doing his rounds with his thermos and his radio murmuring traffic reports. He was the only one who used her name, and he used it the way you'd use the name of any colleague—flatly, kindly, without freight. She had chosen well, she often thought. Of all the buildings in this city where a woman might disappear into a uniform and a lanyard, she had chosen the one place where nobody looked up. Musicians looked at their hands. Composers looked at the middle distance. Conductors looked at everything except the person pushing a mop cart through the back of the hall during rehearsal, because conductors were busy conducting, and besides, everyone knew that cleaning staff were furniture that moved.

"Morning, Dev."

"Cold one."

"It's October," she said. "It's contractually obligated to be cold."

He laughed his small laugh, the one he kept for her, and moved on, and Emrys wheeled her cart into the hall proper and began the work.

* * *

There was an order to it, and she loved the order even as she refused to admit she loved anything about this building. The hall first, before the students arrived, because the acoustics were honestest in the early dark—she'd learned that much without meaning to. You could hear a chair scrape from forty rows away. You could hear your own breathing come back to you off the balcony, softened, like a stranger's. In the first months she had found this unbearable and had requested, in writing, to be reassigned to the practice rooms. Her supervisor, a harried woman named Colleen who signed off on requests the way other people swatted flies, had denied it on grounds of seniority and shift coverage, and Emrys had gone home that night and sat in her rented kitchen and laughed until she cried, which she supposed was one way to feel something.

Now it was simply the shape of her mornings. Stage left first, because the stage left door stuck and needed working loose before anyone official noticed. Then the aisles, row by row, the mop whispering over wood that had been laid the year before she was born. Then the stage itself, which required a different cloth and a different care, because the piano lived there—a nine-foot Steinway, concert black, maintained by a technician named Osgood who treated it with more tenderness than he had ever been observed to treat a human being—and because the stage collected things. Rosin dust. The occasional lost earring. Once, memorably, half a sandwich, which she chose not to investigate too closely.

She worked with her headphones in but nothing playing. That was another discipline. Music in her ears while she touched these surfaces would have been like drinking alone in the morning: technically possible, spiritually inadvisable.

By seven-fifteen the light came through the clerestory windows in long amber bars, and by seven-thirty the first students began to drift in, scarves and instrument cases and the particular walk of people who had been awake since five arguing with a passage of Debussy. Emrys stepped back into the shadow of the proscenium arch and became, officially, no one.

This was the part outsiders would never understand, she thought—not that there were outsiders, since no one outside knew enough about her life to be outside of it, but still, hypothetically, if there had been someone to explain it to. They would assume the hard part was being unrecognized. They would imagine the drama of it: the double-take, the whispered *isn't that*, the moment of exposure trembling just offstage. But recognition wasn't the danger. Recognition had a shelf life of about eighteen months even under the best circumstances; the classical world chewed through its prodigies faster than it replaced its strings, and the girl in the gilt frame had been news in a decade when news still traveled by newspaper. No—the hard part was the opposite. The hard part was how completely it worked. How thoroughly the world agreed to forget you, once you gave it permission. How a person could stand in the room where she had once made four hundred people forget to breathe, holding a mop, and be looked at directly, in the eyes, by a violinist asking if the hall would be open till ten, and receive from that exchange not one flicker of anything.

"Yes," Emrys had said. "Till ten. Sign out with security."

"Thanks." And gone, already turning back to her companion, already saying something about fingerings.

Emrys stood in the amber light a moment longer than the task required. The ache of it was not sharp. That was the trick of deliberate invisibility—it didn't cut, it *pressed*. A weight distributed so evenly across the whole of you that some days you mistook it for your own body. Some days you mistook it for calm.

* * *

At eight o'clock the hall filled for masterclass—Thursdays were Aldous Wren's, and Wren's classes drew an audience the way weather drew umbrellas—and Emrys retreated to the corridors, where her real territory waited: scuffed baseboards, the eternal war against the men's restroom floor, the water fountain on level two that spat like a cat being bathed.

She saved the east corridor for last. She always did. Not because of the portrait—she told herself it was because the corridor got the morning sun and dried slowly after mopping, so it was efficient to do it last, and this was even true, in the way that most good lies are built on load-bearing truths. But the truth underneath the true thing was that the corridor held the photographs, all of them, the whole gilded procession of the conservatory's self-regard, and walking it was like walking through a family album of a family she had been expelled from.

There was Wren himself, young, hair still obeying gravity. There was the cellist Marguerite Iles, mid-bow, eyes closed. There was the founding benefactor, some shipping magnate whose fortune had evidently been laundered through generosity. And third from the tall window, tilted two degrees toward the music below the floorboards: a girl of seventeen in a green dress, hands resting in her lap as though the piano had just been carried away from under them, with an expression the photographer had probably intended as poised and which Emrys now read, every Friday, as terrified.

She remembered sitting for it. She remembered the photographer saying, *Look just past me, like you're hearing something beautiful,* and she remembered thinking: I don't have to pretend. And she remembered that this had been true, and that it was the last time, for a very long stretch of years, that it would be.

A pair of students passed her while she wrung out the mop. One of them, a lanky boy with a case covered in enamel pins, glanced at the portrait, then at her, then back at the portrait, and Emrys felt the old electric jolt—the flinch before the fall—and braced herself.

"That's weird," the boy said to his friend. "They should really hang that straight."

"They should really take it down," said the friend. "Who even is that?"

"No idea. Someone from, like, forever ago."

They passed on, arguing amiably about whether the recital hall or the main hall had better heating, and Emrys stood with her mop wrung dry in her hands and discovered that she was laughing. Quietly. Into her collar. Because there it was—the thing she had spent three years engineering, delivered to her free of charge by a boy with a pin-covered cello case: *who even is that?*

It should have felt like victory. She examined the feeling carefully, the way you'd examine a coin found on the street, checking both faces. On one side: safety. On the other: a hollowness shaped exactly like a concert grand, and her hands ached suddenly, ferociously, with the memory of filling it.

* * *

Lunch she took in the loading bay, because the loading bay had a radiator and no portraits. She ate her sandwiches—cheese pickle, the same as every day, variation being a luxury she associated with people who had answers about themselves—and read the same page of the same paperback she'd been reading for a week, a mystery novel in which a detective was uncovering secrets in a seaside town, which she had chosen from the charity shop shelf precisely because it seemed safely unlike anything, and which turned out to be set, according to a throwaway line on page forty-one, forty miles from Tresillian Cove.

She had put the book down then. She had not picked it up since. It sat on her kitchen table acting as a coaster, which felt like a fair arrangement.

Her phone buzzed against her knee. A message from Colleen: *Can you take Hall 2 tonight? Kowalczyk's rehearsal ran over and they've wrecked the green room. Double time.*

Emrys typed back: *Fine.* Then, because Colleen was human, roughly: *What did they do to the green room?*

*Don't ask questions you don't want answered before eating.*

She smiled, pocketed the phone, and sat a while longer in the radiator's dry warmth, watching dust turn in the light from the bay doors. Somewhere above her, muffled through three floors of concrete and ambition, someone was playing Chopin—the B minor scherzo, attacked with more courage than control. The building hummed with it. It always hummed. Ten hours a day this place exhaled music through its vents and ducts and door seams, and Emrys had learned to let it wash over her the way you learn to live downstream of a factory: you tell yourself you don't notice the smell anymore, and most days you almost believe it.

But some days—Thursdays, say, when Wren's masterclass ran long and the Chopin came down through the bones of the building—her fingers moved. Just slightly. Against her thigh, against the spine of the coaster-novel, a small involuntary grammar of muscle memory, the left hand reaching for a bass note that wasn't there. She watched it happen the way you'd watch a dog dream. Poor animal. It couldn't help it. It was only running in its sleep.

* * *

The afternoon passed in its usual litany: Hall 2 reset, the practice-room corridor cleared of the archaeological strata of coffee cups, a negotiation with the photocopier, which respected no one. At four she paused at the tall window in the east corridor—her window, though she'd have denied the possessive—to watch the light go thin over the city, and found herself, without any decision having been made, looking at the portrait.

The girl in the green dress looked past her, hearing something beautiful.

*I know,* Emrys told her silently, the way you'd speak to a photograph of the dead, which in every way that mattered this was. *I know what you're hearing. I'm sorry about what happens next.*

And then, because the corridor was empty and the ache had pressed a little harder today than usual, she allowed herself thirty seconds—one full tide of breath in, one out—with her hand lifted, not touching the frame, hovering near the edge of it as if warming her fingers at a fire. The gilt was cool. She didn't touch it. She never touched it. There were rules, and the rules were the only reason the arrangement worked.

Then she went back to work.

* * *

It was nearly six, and the corridors had emptied into practice rooms and evening classes, when she started on the hall itself for the second pass—the deep clean, the one that happened after the day's traffic, done in the amber-and-grey hour when the building felt least like a workplace and most like a ship becalmed. She liked this hour best, which she also declined to admit. The seats rose around her in their patient rows. The stage waited under its ghost light. Somewhere in the walls the heating ticked and settled, and the whole vast room breathed the way big old rooms breathe, in slow tidal exchanges of warm and cool air.

She was halfway up the center aisle when she saw it.

The water stain.

It was on the ceiling, or rather blooming down through the plaster near the junction of wall and roofline, stage right—a soft grey continent that hadn't been there on Monday. She knew the building's stains the way she knew her route sheets; this one was new, and new water meant trouble, and trouble meant a report to facilities, and facilities meant forms. She climbed the stage steps, fetched the ladder from the wings, and stood at the top of it with her head craned back, examining the damage with the flat professional attention she brought to all surfaces.

It was a strange shape, even for water. Water usually spread in rings, in continents with reasonable coastlines. This had edges. A long irregular curve sweeping down from the upper left, then fracturing into smaller inlets, a scatter of islands, a suggestion—she tilted her head—of a peninsula hooking round a sheltered bay.

She raised her hand without thinking and traced it in the air, the way you'd trace a scar on someone else's arm. Down the long curve. Round the hook of the peninsula. Into the bay.

Her finger stopped.

Because she knew this coastline. Not the way you know a map. The way you know a face. It was the shore beneath the house at Tresillian Cove—the sweep of Porthmerra beach, the black hook of Carrick Point, the little sheltered notch where the boats moored at high tide and lay gasping on the sand at low, and where two children had once spent entire summers learning what the sea takes and what it gives back.

Emrys stood on the ladder in the emptying hall with her arm still raised, tracing water damage shaped like home, and did not move for a long time.

Above her, the pipes ticked. Below her, the unplayed keys waited in their long white silence. And somewhere in the walls of the building, faint through concrete and steel, the evening's practicing had begun—dozens of rooms, dozens of hands, the whole conservatory humming like a shell held to the ear—and inside that hum, unmistakable, a phrase she had not heard in ten years rose and fell and rose again, as if the building itself had finally decided to ask her the question her family had arranged its whole life around never asking.

She climbed down the ladder. She got her phone. She photographed the stain for the facilities report, and her hands, she noticed, were not steady at all.
