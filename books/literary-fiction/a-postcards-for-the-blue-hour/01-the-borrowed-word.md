## 1. The Borrowed Word

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a translation office at four in the afternoon, and I have spent five years learning to inhabit it the way other people inhabit their apartments. It's not quiet, exactly. The building hums. Somewhere below us a pneumatic drill argues with the pavement, and in the kitchenette the kettle ticks as it cools, a small domestic clock nobody winds. But between the desks there is a hush that feels negotiated, contractual — the silence of people who spend all day putting words into other people's mouths and have none left over for themselves.

My desk faces the window because I asked for it and lied about why. I said I liked natural light. What I actually like is being able to see the city do its business without me: the delivery vans, the pigeons conducting their petty diplomacy on the ledge, the woman across the street who waters her office plants at exactly this hour with the devotion of someone who has never had to choose between two versions of anything. From here, language looks simple. People open their mouths and out comes one language, theirs, whole, unexamined, like a birthmark.

I translate for a living. That sentence always gets a certain look at parties, so let me be precise about what it means. It means I am fluent in six languages and native in none of them, or native in two of them depending on how you count, or native in zero if you count the way I count, which is to say honestly. This morning I rendered a shipping contract from German into English — forty pages of liability clauses in which the word *Haftung* appeared so many times it stopped meaning anything and became a sound, a stone dropped repeatedly into a bucket. This afternoon I am translating love letters. Actual love letters. A man named — well, his name is none of your business, but he signs himself with an initial and a flourish — has written eleven letters to a woman in Lisbon, and my job is to make him sound like himself in Portuguese.

He writes badly, which is a gift. Bad writers are easy to translate because they mean exactly what they say. He writes things like *I think about you when the tram goes past my window* and *I have started buying two of everything by accident*, and my work is simply to carry these small confessions across the water without getting them wet. I have become, over eleven letters, something of an expert in this man's heart. I know he is afraid of her forgetting him. I know he measures time in trams. I know that when he writes *I am not a man of many words* he means it as an apology, and I render it into Portuguese as an apology, and somewhere in Lisbon a woman reads it and presumably smiles, never knowing that the apology passed through a third person first, through me, through this desk, through this window with its pigeons and its drill.

That is the part of the job nobody warns you about. You don't just move words. You move the weight in them. And some days the weight transfers. Some days I finish a letter and sit there holding feelings that aren't mine, the way you might hold someone else's umbrella, unsure whether to keep it or chase them down the street.

"Montrose."

Petra. She appears at the edge of my desk the way she appears everywhere — already mid-thought, coffee in hand, lanyard swinging like a pendulum marking time in an office where nobody has time. Petra runs intake. She decides which jobs land on which desks, and she does it with the serene authority of a woman who believes deeply in spreadsheets.

"You free Thursday?" she says. "Big job came in. Patent portfolio, Finnish to English, sixty thousand words, rush."

"Finnish," I say. "I can do Finnish."

"I know you can do Finnish. You can do everything, it's very annoying." She consults her tablet. "Oh — also, we got a request this morning. Literary, actually, for once. Someone wants a short story collection translated, and the source language is—"

She pauses. Not dramatically. Petra doesn't do drama; she does data. But she pauses, and I feel the pause before she even speaks, the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

"—it's your language," she says. "The island one. The one you're from."

Here is what I do in moments like this. I reach for the nearest professional instrument and play it. "It's not really translatable," I say. "The market's tiny. There's no dictionary worth trusting. Whoever submitted it should try Halvorsen — he does dialect work."

"Halvorsen retired."

"Then they'll have to wait for the next Halvorsen."

"Jonah." She says my name the way she says *invoice*: patiently, with the implication that this will need resolving eventually. "You've turned down every job in that language for five years. Every single one. I've stopped flagging them for you, but this one came addressed. To you. By name. Somebody asked for you specifically."

"Somebody remembers a name from a conference panel," I say. "It happens."

"It doesn't happen," Petra says, "five years running." She looks at me over the rim of her cup, and I give her the look back, the one I've polished like a stone — pleasant, closed, faintly amused, the face of a man with nothing hidden because nothing interesting is happening behind it. It works on clients. It works on Petra, mostly. It worked on my ex-wife for almost three years, which tells you either how good the face is or how little she was looking.

"Fine," she says finally. "I'll decline. But for the record—" and here she taps the tablet twice, a little staccato of finality, "—for the record, you are the best translator in this building, possibly in this city, and you won't touch the one language you dream in. That's not professionalism, Jonah. That's whatever the opposite is."

She leaves. The kettle ticks. Across the street, the plant-watering woman moves on to a fern.

I want to say she's wrong. I want to say I don't dream in that language, that it's a dead thing I carried out of childhood like a shell you find on a beach and keep on a shelf until one day you notice you've stopped seeing it. But the truth is less tidy. The truth is that I dream in it occasionally — rarely, and always in the same setting, a kitchen with a blue door, water boiling, someone's back turned to me — and I wake from those dreams with words in my mouth I couldn't write down if you paid me. Which, notably, no one has been able to do for five years.

* * *

Under the bottom drawer of my desk, taped where the laminate meets the metal, there is a folded piece of paper. I put it there four years ago, when keeping it in my wallet started to feel too much like carrying a wound against my skin, and under the drawer felt more like filing it. This is the kind of distinction translators make. We believe location changes meaning. We are wrong, but we believe it.

The paper holds a single sentence, handwritten, in my grandmother's hand — the hand going loose now, the letters leaning like fence posts after a storm. Ilse Montrose wrote it to me two years ago, in a letter that was mostly about her garden and the unreliability of the care home's heating, and then, near the end, without preamble, this sentence, in the old tongue, followed by nothing. No explanation. No translation offered, though God knows she could have — she raised me bilingually, she taught me my first words in both languages, she knows perfectly well that I understand it.

That's the cruelty of it, or the mercy, I've never decided which. She wrote a sentence she knew I could read and knew I would refuse to finish carrying. Because translating it — really translating it, the way I'd translate a contract or a letter from a tram-obsessed man in Lisbon — would require me to decide what it means. And I know what it means. That's the problem. I've known since the day the letter came. I read it standing in my hallway, coat still on, and understood it completely, instantly, the way you understand a door opening onto a long drop.

Translation is decision. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone believes. Every rendering is a choice among ghosts: this word and not that one, this rhythm, this register, this degree of mercy. For sixty thousand words of Finnish patent law I will happily make ten thousand decisions and sleep fine afterward, because the stakes belong to someone else. But this sentence — this one sentence, thirty-one words in a language with maybe two hundred living speakers, most of them older than the ferry that serves their island — this sentence asks me to decide what my family means. What happened. Who loved whom. Whether the word she chose for *betrayal* is the same word the island uses for *leaving*, and whether that's a flaw in the language or a truth in it.

So it sits under the drawer. Untranslated. The only sentence in six languages I have ever refused to finish.

Some afternoons I take it out. I'm doing it now, actually — peeling back the tape, unfolding the paper along its softening creases, smoothing it flat beside my keyboard like a document awaiting processing. The letters lean. The ink has gone brown at the edges of each stroke, blood drying in the veins of the handwriting. I read it the way I read everything: completely, involuntarily, understanding arriving before I can stop it.

And then I fold it up again. Tape it down. Return to the love letters, where a man who buys two of everything by accident waits for me to make him legible in another country.

"Everything okay?" asks Dev from the next desk, without looking up. Dev translates video game dialogue into Japanese and has the calmest eyes of anyone I've ever met. Nothing unsettles Dev except misplaced semicolons.

"Fine," I say. "Client query."

Dev nods and returns to his dragons.

* * *

I stay late. I often stay late; the office empties around six and what's left is mine — the hum, the ticking kettle, the city lighting itself floor by floor across the street. Tonight I finish the eleventh letter. He has written something new at the end, something that made me stop with my hands off the keyboard for a full minute:

*I know you have someone who carries these words for you. Tell them they do it well. Tell them I know the difference between a letter and its shadow.*

He doesn't know me. He has never thought about me at all, and yet he has written, accidentally, the most accurate description of my life anyone has produced. I am a carrier of shadows. I take the shape of a thing and move it carefully across a border and hand it to someone else, and the border guard never sees me, and the original stays home.

I render his line into Portuguese — *diga-lhes que fazem bem o seu trabalho* — and hate it immediately, revise it, hate the revision, settle on something adequate, and close the file. Adequate. My whole career in one word.

Then, because it's late and the office is empty and shame keeps different hours than courage, I take the sentence out again.

This time I open a blank document. This time I type the first word of a translation, just to see what happens. *I* — no. The sentence doesn't begin with *I* in any honest rendering; it begins with a word that means both *before* and *without*, a word that only exists in a language shaped by an island where things are measured by what the sea takes first. I delete it. I try again, approaching from the middle, the way you'd approach a horse that spooks. Three lines in, I have produced something grammatically flawless and emotionally false — a tax return of a confession — and I understand, sitting alone in the humming dark with the pigeons gone to roost, that I cannot do this the way I do everything else. I cannot stand in the doorway of this sentence. To translate it I would have to go where it was written. I would have to stand in the kitchen with the blue door. I would have to ask the woman leaning against the counter, in her own dissolving language, what she meant — and she is losing that language now, word by word, the tide going out of her, and one day soon there will be no one left to ask.

I close the document without saving. Some habits are load-bearing.

On the drive home the radio plays low, the way I keep it, background noise against the noise in my head. The late-night program comes on at eleven — the one that plays field recordings and obscure folk music, the host's voice like smoke settling. I'm half listening, thinking about trams, thinking about a blue door, when the melody starts.

I know it.

That is not a sentence I use lightly. In five years of professional translation I have claimed certainty perhaps a dozen times. But I know this melody the way you know a scar — not because you studied it, but because it was made on you. A slow rising phrase, a fall, a held note at the top like a hand pausing on a latch. My father hummed it once, in a kitchen with a blue door, standing at the stove with his back to me, and said, *this is for your mother, and no one else, ever,* and then he left, and then he died, and I have spent twenty-five years being the only person alive who ever heard it.

Except whoever is playing it right now, on the radio, at eleven o'clock on an ordinary Tuesday, in a language with two hundred speakers left in the world.

I pull the car onto the shoulder. The hazard lights tick. On the radio, the melody ends and a voice begins — an old man's voice, weathered, careful, singing words I haven't heard spoken aloud since childhood, and beneath the song the host murmurs a station ID, a phone number, a name.

An archive, he says. A community archive. Someone recorded this. Someone has the tape.

I sit on the shoulder of the road with my hazards blinking and my heart going like the drill under the office, and I understand that the sentence under my drawer has just found company.
