## 1. The Inventory Begins

The key still turned the way it always had—half a rotation, then a small resistance, then the give, like my mother's hand at the end of her life deciding to let go of something. I stood in the doorway with the funeral program folded in my coat pocket and the estate agent's clipboard under my arm and I did not cry. Crying was not on the schedule.

The will was specific. My mother had been specific about everything, which is what people mistook for coldness, and what I had inherited instead of her hazel eyes. *The contents of the property at 14 Alder Row shall be itemized, valued, and prepared for sale by the executor.* Executor: Tamsin Sterling. Of course. Marcus got the car and the freedom from detail; I got the detail, all of it, every teaspoon and dust sheet, because I was the one who made lists, who alphabetized, who once reorganized a hospital bedside table while our mother slept so that the water glass sat on the left where she liked it and the pills marched in order of consumption. Competence was the family's currency and I had always been rich.

I set the clipboard on the hall table. Item one.

The house smelled the way it had smelled my entire childhood—beeswax and old radiators and something faintly green from the back room where plants went to be forgotten rather than die. But underneath it now there was another smell, or rather an absence, the way a room smells different when no one is expected home. Forty-six years my mother had lived here. Thirty-one of them with my father, fifteen without. And now the house belonged to no one, which meant it belonged to me, at least until the inventory was done and the sign went up and the developers came with their clipboards of their own.

I took out a pen. I took out the printed schedules. I began at the front door and worked clockwise, because that was the method, and method was the only language I spoke fluently that week.

*Item 1: Entrance door, hardwood, original 1962 fittings, brass letterplate (tarnished).*

My father had polished that letterplate every Sunday morning of my childhood, a cloth and a tin of Brasso, humming something tuneless. He said a house should look like it was expecting good news. For thirty years the only post had been bills and the parish newsletter, and he polished it anyway, right up until the stroke, and after the stroke my mother polished it, though she never hummed. She did everything he had done but stripped of music, like a translation into a flatter language.

*Item 2: Hall table, oak, three drawers.*

Three drawers. I opened none of them. Not yet. The schedule required me to note condition and provenance, and condition could wait until I'd walked the whole perimeter, the way you circle a thing before you touch it.

The coat hooks by the door held nothing now—the coats had gone to the charity shop two days before the funeral, Marcus and I folding them into bags in a silence so complete I could hear the zip teeth. But the wall behind them showed the ghost of them, four paler rectangles where the paint had been shaded from light. Four hooks. Father, mother, me, Marcus. The house had kept their outlines even after the bodies left. That was the thing about houses nobody warned you: they were archives. They filed everything. They never threw anything away, and neither, apparently, did I.

I moved into the sitting room.

*Item 3: Three-piece suite, teal corduroy, worn at armrests.*

Worn at the left armrest only, actually, because my mother had sat in the same corner of the same sofa for decades, angled toward the window, a book face-down on her knee. I used to think she watched the street. Later I understood she watched nothing; she was somewhere else entirely, some interior country with its own weather, and we had all been raised in the borderlands of it without visas. When I was nine I asked her what she thought about when she looked out the window and she said, *Rain mostly,* and smiled as if this were an answer, and perhaps it was, and perhaps I'd spent forty years failing to translate it.

I wrote *teal corduroy* and my handwriting, usually so level, wobbled on the descenders.

This was the trap of the inventory, I realized by item six. It was not a list. It was an interrogation, and I was both interrogator and witness, and every object deposed against us. The mantel clock (*Item 7*) that my father wound on Fridays and that stopped the week after his funeral and was never restarted, because my mother said she preferred the quiet, as if time itself had been his habit and not hers. The piano (*Item 8*, upright, Berry of London, needs tuning) that nobody played, that had arrived with my mother as a bride and had never once, in fifty years, been opened for anything but photographs—a framed school picture of me aged seven propped on its lid like a hostage. The photograph would need its own number. Everything needed its own number. Grief, apparently, was a filing exercise.

I worked through the morning. By noon the schedule held sixty-one entries and I had eaten nothing and drunk tea gone cold twice, and my knees ached from crouching, and I felt—this is the shameful part—I felt *good*. Not happy. Nothing near happy. But useful, structured, occupied in the way that lets you pass through a burning building carrying things, one at a time, in order. If I was cataloguing then I was not standing in the middle of the sitting room understanding that I would never again hear my mother call my name from the kitchen in that particular flat tone that meant both *lunch* and *come here* and *I have something I will not say directly*. If I was numbering teacups I was not numbering the years.

Marcus rang at one o'clock.

"How's it going?" he said, in the voice he'd used since the funeral, a voice pitched halfway between solicitude and efficiency, grief performed through logistics.

"Sixty-one items," I said. "Sitting room done. Starting the kitchen."

"Good. Listen, the agent thinks we should get it listed inside two months. There's interest already, apparently. Developers."

"Two months," I said. "For a whole house."

"You've done half a room in a day, Tam." A pause, in which I heard him decide to be gentle, which was worse than if he hadn't. "Look, I know this is how you cope. The lists. Mum was the same. Just—don't disappear into it, all right? Don't make it a project."

"It's the will," I said. "It's literally the will."

"I know. I know it is." Another pause. Through the phone I could hear his office behind him, the keyboard sounds, the life continuing. "She'd have wanted it done properly."

"She'd have wanted it done *her* way," I said, and heard the edge in it, and didn't take it back, and he let it go the way we let everything go, smoothly, without friction, the way objects slide past each other in a well-organized drawer.

Afterwards I stood in the kitchen with the schedule and looked at the table where we'd eaten twenty thousand meals and understood that Marcus was right about one thing: I could disappear into this. The inventory could become the whole of me, item by item, until there was no Tamsin left outside the numbering system. And some part of me wanted exactly that. Some part of me had been wanting it for years—to be absorbed into a task so completely that feeling became optional.

So I gave myself a rule, because rules were how I survived: I would catalogue everything. I would open every drawer, every cupboard, every box. No exceptions. Whatever the house held, I would hold it too, briefly, and write it down, and let it go. That was the deal I struck with myself at two o'clock on the first afternoon, standing in my dead mother's kitchen, and I want it noted that I kept the deal almost perfectly, right up until the study.

* * *

The kitchen yielded ninety-four items, including a drawer of string and rubber bands that I entered as a single lot (*Item 112: miscellaneous household fastenings*) because entering each rubber band separately would have been the beginning of madness, and I knew where that road went, I had seen the signposts all my life. The pantry gave up jars with dates on masking tape in my mother's hand—*plums '09, plums '11, plums '13*—three harvests of a tree my father planted, preserved year after year by a woman who never served them to anyone. I stood holding a jar of plums older than my marriage and understood that I was looking at a form of speech. She had kept them the way other people keep diaries. She had put up fruit instead of saying whatever the fruit stood for, and now the fruit was mine to interpret, and I wrote *Item 118: preserved goods, various vintages, dispose* in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

The dining room was quick. The bedrooms less so.

In my mother's room the wardrobe still held her clothes, because we had run out of time before the funeral and I had quietly postponed this particular excavation, and postponement, in my experience, is just grief with a calendar. I catalogued the furniture first—bed, mattress, two bedside tables—and then, because the rule was the rule, I opened the wardrobe. Cedar and lavender and, beneath them, the warm ghost-smell of her skin that hit me like a hand flat against the sternum. Her coats. Her good dress from my graduation, the navy one. Behind them, on the high shelf, hatboxes, and in the hatboxes photographs, and in the photographs—

A young woman I didn't recognize, laughing, head thrown back, on a beach I didn't recognize. My mother, but younger than I had ever known her, younger than any version of her I'd been permitted to meet. Beside her in the frame another woman, dark-skinned, radiant, arm looped through my mother's arm with the easy ownership of intimacy. In another photograph the two of them again, at some gathering, and the other woman's face had been cut out with scissors—cut cleanly, carefully, along the outline of her profile, leaving my mother smiling beside a white absence shaped like a person.

I sat down on the edge of my mother's bed, in the room where she had died four months ago, and held a photograph of a woman-shaped hole.

There were eleven photographs. In eight of them, the same woman. In five of those, cropped or cut away. Whoever she was, she had been removed from the record with the same thoroughness my mother brought to everything—no tearing, no rage-marks, just patient editorial surgery, repeated over years, the way you might trim a hedge or correct a manuscript. Someone had been written out. Someone had been numbered out of existence before I ever started numbering.

I photographed the photographs with my phone, for the record, and then I did not enter them on the schedule. I told myself I'd log them later, under personal papers, under effects. I told myself the schedule was for the sale and these were not for sale. Both things were true. Neither was the reason.

* * *

By evening the light in the study had gone the colour of weak tea, and I was still working, because stopping meant going home to my flat where the silence had no items attached and therefore no handles to hold it by. The study had been my father's room, then, after he died, my mother's—she'd moved in there within a month, abandoning the bedroom's twin desks, and none of us had remarked on it, because in our family a change that isn't explained is a change that doesn't exist.

Desk, rolltop, oak. *Item 203.* Chair. *204.* Bookshelves, four. *205–208.* Filing cabinet, two-drawer, contents: household accounts, warranties, my school reports kept in date order like a prosecution file. Then the desk itself, and the desk had drawers, and the drawers opened the way drawers do—pens, stamps, a tin of paperclips, spare keys unlabelled because everyone in this house had always known where everything was except me.

Except the bottom drawer. The bottom drawer stuck.

Not jammed—locked. A small brass keyhole, no key on the ring in the top drawer, no key in the tin, no key anywhere obvious. I knelt on the floorboards and pulled, gently, then less gently, and the drawer held with the stubbornness of a thing kept. I could feel weight shift inside it when I tilted the desk. Paper weight. Something sliding.

I want to say I considered my options carefully. What I actually did was fetch the flathead screwdriver from the kitchen toolbox, kneel down again, and apply it to the gap between drawer and runner with the patience of a woman who has taken apart a great many things that did not wish to come apart. The lock was old and the wood was old and grief makes either a lever or a crowbar of us all. The drawer surrendered with a crack that sounded, in the empty house, like a small bone breaking.

Inside: postcards.

Forty-one of them, I counted twice, fanned in a shoebox that had once held my father's church shoes. Postcards of harbours, of hillsides, of a city skyline I didn't recognize, of gardens, of a single lighthouse. Unsent—all of them unsent, no stamps, no postmarks, addressed on their faces in a small upright hand to a single initial: *M.*

Margaret? My mother's name was Margaret. M. And the writing on the backs—not English. Not the alphabet I knew. An alphabet of loops and strokes I had never seen, dense and deliberate, covering every card edge to edge, the handwriting of someone who had a great deal to say and a private place to say it.

I sat back on my heels. Downstairs a radiator ticked. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and stopped, as if corrected.

Forty-one postcards, written to my mother, in a language I couldn't read, never sent—or sent and returned, or never meant for the post at all, meant only for a locked drawer in a locked room in a house full of people who didn't know they were characters in someone else's sentence. My mother had kept them the way she'd kept the plums. Preserved. Labelled by year, I saw now—a pencil notation on the shoebox lid, tiny numerals running from what looked like *1971* to a date only a few years old. Fifty years of correspondence with somebody, conducted entirely in secret, in a script designed not to be read.

I should have logged them. Item 209: correspondence, foreign-language, forty-one pieces, provenance unknown. That was the rule. That was the deal I'd made with myself at two o'clock in the kitchen, and I had kept it all day, through wardrobes and hatboxes and jars of plums, and I had photographed the cut-out woman and promised myself I'd file her later.

I picked up the top card—a harbour, grey water, gulls rendered in three colours—and turned it over, and the loops of that unknown alphabet rose off the card like something alive, like handwriting that had been waiting in the dark for a pair of eyes.

And I put it in my coat pocket.

Not the box. One card. The first dishonest act of a careful life, committed in under a second, with a fluency that frightened me—because it turns out I spoke my mother's language better than I'd ever admitted, the language of keeping things, of *instead*, of the drawer that locks, and my mouth had never been taught it, and here it was anyway, native on my tongue.

I closed the empty space where the card had been, pushed the drawer shut on the remaining forty, and stood in the darkening study holding my coat pocket closed with one hand, as if the card might fly out, as if anyone were watching, as if the house itself—the great silent archive, the keeper of outlines—had not already recorded the theft under its own numbering system, and entered it, and filed it away.
