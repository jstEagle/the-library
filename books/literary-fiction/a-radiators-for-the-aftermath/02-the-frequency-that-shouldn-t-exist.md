## 2. The Frequency That Shouldn't Exist

I did what any reasonable engineer would do with an impossible signal: I measured it.

The song ended at 11:47 p.m. The DJ — a voice like warm gravel poured over honey, unhurried, unbothered by the hour or the fact that no one was presumably listening — said only, "That was for the ones who kept the receipts," and put on something with horns that I couldn't have identified under torture. Then I sat in my kitchen with the radio's dial glowing and my hands flat on the table, waiting for my pulse to return to its documented range.

It didn't. So I got out the calipers. Not for the pulse — for nothing, really, except that holding them steadies me the way some people are steadied by prayer. Teo used to say I calibrated my feelings. He said it like an accusation. He said most things like accusations, toward the end.

Here is what I know about songs: they have authors, and authors die or don't, but songs don't come back from anywhere. A melody is not a migratory bird. It does not arrive nine years after its writer stopped existing, on a frequency at the far left edge of the FM band, in a voice — his voice, humming, then singing, then laughing at himself mid-verse because he always laughed when he hit the high part — that has been silent since before the city repaved Halvorsen Avenue.

Except it had arrived. And it had done so wearing Teo's handwriting.

People talk about recognizing a voice. What I recognized was structure. Teo wrote melodies the way he wrote everything — starting confident, losing the thread in the second phrase on purpose, then finding it again somewhere unexpected, like a man who drops his keys on purpose to see where they roll. The bridge of the song modulated up a fourth and hung there, refusing to resolve, and I sat in my kitchen with my throat doing something I refused to measure, thinking: *that's the suspended joint. That's the expansion gap you leave so the whole structure doesn't shear apart when the temperature changes.*

He'd learned that from me. Or I'd said it once within earshot and he'd stolen it, the way siblings steal, which is to say permanently and without acknowledgment.

* * *

The next night I recorded it. The night after that, again. Three nights running, same time slot, 11:31 to 11:47, give or take. The song was identical each time — not a live performance, then. A recording. Which meant it existed somewhere as a physical object: tape or file, something with mass and provenance, something that had been made, stored, forgotten or hidden, and then played.

Which meant someone had it.

I ran the numbers the way I run everything. Signal strength fading in and out suggested low power — under a hundred watts, maybe less. The station ID, when the gravel-voice finally gave one at the top of the hour, was two words delivered with the casual pride of someone reading their own name off a birth certificate: "Lighthouse 104."

I looked it up. Lighthouse 104 did not exist. Not on the FCC database, not in the radio-station directories, not in the local press archives. A pirate, then — a low-power unlicensed broadcaster, the kind that operates out of garages and church basements and gets fined into silence every eighteen months and reappears six months later under a new call sign like a weed with a lawyer.

Pirates broadcast from somewhere. Somewhere near, given how cleanly the signal cut through at my apartment — maybe five, eight miles out, accounting for terrain.

There was only one neighborhood in that radius that mattered, and I had spent nine years arranging my life so that I never had to drive through it. I took the long way to work every morning. I had opinions about which grocery store was superior that were, if I'm honest, entirely a function of which one didn't require crossing the old rail spur into Marsh Creek. Nine years is a long time to route around a place. It becomes a kind of architecture. You build your days with the missing room load-bearing, and eventually you forget the room was ever there, except you don't, because you built around it, and the building leans.

At 10:40 p.m. on Thursday, I got in my car with a directional antenna zip-tied to a yardstick, a portable receiver, and the specific dread of a man returning to a house fire to look for his keys.

* * *

Marsh Creek at night looked exactly like itself, which was the worst possible outcome. The laundromat on Delancey still had its hand-painted OPEN sign, the O faded to a C, so it read CPEN, which Teo had once declared was more honest anyway. The bodega. The mural of the heron that three separate artists had been commissioned to restore and each had simply added another heron, so there were now four, standing in water that had never been there. My hands did the thing on the wheel where they gripped at ten and two with inspector precision, and I noticed I was doing it, and did it harder.

The signal strengthened as I crossed the spur. I watched the meter on the receiver climb — 71, 78, 84 — and felt something I can only describe as being *read*. As though the frequency were a finger running down a list of everyone who'd ever left, checking me off.

The bearing pointed northeast. I followed it past the shuttered cannery, past the lot where the community garden had won its fight against the parking structure and lost its fight against gravity, until the road ended at a chain-link gate and I was looking up at the old municipal water tower.

Everyone in Marsh Creek knew the tower. Hundred feet of riveted steel legs holding up a bulbous tank that hadn't held water since the nineties, rusted the color of dried blood, visible from half the neighborhood like a lighthouse that guided you toward nothing. Kids dared each other to touch the legs. There had been a rumor about a body in it my entire childhood, unverified, immortal.

A light was on inside the tank.

Not a work light. Colored gels, amber and blue, glowing through the tank's access windows so the whole upper structure looked like a lantern with the shade on crooked. A cable ran down one leg, zip-tied at intervals with the fastidiousness of someone who cared about cable management, disappearing into a shed at the base that had once, judging by the signage ghosting through its peeling paint, sold bait.

From the tower, faintly, through the night air itself and not through any speaker: music. Live. Someone playing guitar badly and joyfully, and other voices laughing, and the gravel-honey voice saying something I couldn't make out, followed by more laughter.

I parked two blocks away, because I am a coward with excellent technique, and sat with the engine ticking and the receiver in my lap showing 91 decibels of a dead man's song coming out of a water tower.

Let me be precise about what I felt, since feeling is not my native language and I want to get the translation right. It was not grief, exactly. Grief is what I'd done at the funeral, which had been efficient and brief and mostly concerned with not making eye contact with our mother. This was older than grief. This was the feeling from inspections — the one I get when I put my palm flat against a girder and feel, through the steel, traffic moving on a bridge I cannot see. Something enormous passing through a structure. Something already underway, whether or not I stood there touching it.

Teo had been inside this neighborhood the whole time I was gone. His song had been sitting in a wall or a drawer or a shoebox the whole time I was calibrating my mornings to avoid it. And now it was being broadcast — *given away*, freely, illegally, generously — by strangers, from a tower I'd climbed exactly once, at fourteen, on a dare, while Teo stood at the bottom yelling that I was going to die and also that he was going to kill me if I didn't.

The song came around again. 11:31. Right on schedule. Through the car's bad speakers this time, and it sounded worse and better for it — compressed, slightly warped, the way things sound when they've been copied and loved and carried around. In the second verse, Teo's voice cracked on the high part and he laughed, and the laugh was caught on the recording, kept, broadcast, and I understood suddenly and completely that whoever ran this station had listened to that laugh enough times to decide it belonged in the mix. They had curated my brother's imperfection. Strangers had done the thing I had not done, which was stay in the room.

I turned the receiver off. The song kept going without me, which felt correct.

Then I did the thing I'd driven here pretending I wouldn't do. I got out. I walked the two blocks back with my badge case in my pocket — habit, useless, nobody asks bridge inspectors for credentials at midnight — and stood at the chain-link gate looking up at the lit tank, at the cable descending the leg like a rope ladder for someone escaping a ship, at the shed with the bait sign and a new padlock and, taped beside the door, a piece of cardboard with marker letters:

**LIGHTHOUSE 104 — IF YOU CAN HEAR US, YOU'RE ALREADY CREW.**

Underneath, smaller: *Knock loud. We're usually up.*

I stood there long enough that my breath started to fog, though it wasn't cold enough to justify it. Somewhere above me the gravel voice said my brother's song title — I hadn't known it had a title until that moment, and the title was four syllables in a language only two people had ever spoken, one of whom was me, and hearing it aloud in a stranger's mouth was like watching someone else turn a key I'd swallowed years ago.

The title translated to: *the space that holds.*

I knocked. Loudly, as instructed. The padlock rattled, a bolt slid, and the shed door opened onto warmth and lamplight and a woman about my age with paint-flecked forearms and the expression of someone who had been expecting me for nine years and was trying not to show it.

"You're the Kavanagh face," she said. Not a question.
