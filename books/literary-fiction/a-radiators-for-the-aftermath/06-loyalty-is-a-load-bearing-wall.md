## 6. Loyalty Is a Load-Bearing Wall

The thing about cracks is that they don't announce themselves. They start as hairline fractures in a weld nobody looks at, in a gusset plate buried under forty years of paint, and they grow in the dark the way anything grows when no one is watching. My whole career is watching. That's the joke, if you want it: I spent nine years learning to find the flaw in every structure I stood on, and it took one song on a pirate frequency to show me where mine was.

It was Tuesday when Marisol called the meeting, which meant it was serious, because Lighthouse 104 did not believe in Tuesdays. Tuesdays were for the FCC, she liked to say, and for people who alphabetize their spice racks. The station's calendar ran on a logic of its own — Threnody Thursdays, Static Sundays, a Wednesday night slot called Confessions of the Recently Deceased that turned out to be, thank God, a poetry show. But she'd pinned a note to the corkboard by the transmitter: ALL HANDS. TUESDAY. 7 PM. BRING CHAIRS.

Bring chairs. As if the crisis could be solved with adequate seating.

* * *

The water tower smelled of rust and rain and, faintly, of solder — Dez had been re-flowing connections on the mixing board all afternoon and the whole room carried the sweet chemical ghost of it. Seven of us gathered on folding chairs arranged in a lopsided crescent: Dez, who communicated primarily through equipment; Priya from the archive, who kept her fingernails short for handling tapes; old Hollis, who had allegedly been at the first broadcast in 2011 and mentioned it the way other men mention war; a teenager named Kit whose entire personality was headphones worn around the neck like a talisman; two others whose names I knew but hadn't yet earned; and Marisol, standing in front of the whiteboard with a marker she wasn't using.

On the whiteboard, someone had written: PRUITT. Underlined twice.

"He came back," Marisol said. No preamble. She'd learned, I think, that I flinched at preambles. "Yesterday. With papers this time. A court order — well, the beginning of one. A demand letter, anyway. He says the station is broadcasting copyrighted material without license. He named the song."

She didn't have to say which song. Everyone in the room knew which song. It hung over us the way weather hangs over a coastline.

"He gave us fourteen days," she went on. "Hand over the masters, sign an affidavit about provenance, or he escalates. Fines. He's talking about fines that would buy this tower three times over and melt it down for scrap."

"Let him try," Hollis said, in the tone of a man who has tried letting people try and found them wanting.

"They don't have to try, Hollis. That's the point. They just have to be right." Marisol turned to the whiteboard and wrote nothing under PRUITT, which was somehow more eloquent than writing something. "The law doesn't care that he's a vulture. Vultures are legal. The question is whether what he's holding is his, and right now, on paper, it might be."

Priya spoke up from the archive corner, quiet and precise. "The provenance is the weak link. If we can't document how Teo's recordings left his possession, Pruitt's chain of title looks clean. Death, estate, sale. Courts love chains. Chains are easy."

"So we break the chain," said Kit, who was seventeen and believed most problems were locks.

"With what?" Priya asked gently. "We don't have anything. We have a broadcast and a belief."

They all looked at me then. I want to be honest about that moment, because honesty is the currency I'm trying to learn to counterfeit less often. They looked at me — the brother, the inspector, the man with the surname that made Marisol go still the first time she heard it — as if I were not a crack in the structure but a load-bearing wall. As if my presence alone could hold something up.

"Wren," Marisol said. "You knew him better than any of us. You'd know if there were papers. Letters. Anything that shows what Teo intended for those recordings."

And there it was. The exact millimeter of drift where everything I'd built these past weeks began to shear.

Because I did know. I'd known since the rehearsal space above the laundromat, since I'd knelt in the insulation with dust in my throat and found the envelope tucked behind the reel-to-reel cases — the one addressed in Teo's slanted, impatient hand to Dale Pruitt, care of Meridian Sound Group, postmarked eleven days before he died. I'd read it twice, kneeling there in the dark, and then I'd folded it back into its hiding place like a man returning a body to a grave he'd dug himself.

*Teo mailed them,* the letter said. *Deliberately. On purpose.* He'd catalogued every tape, listed the tracks, written instructions — *air them, sell them, I don't care, just don't let them die in a closet like I did.* He wanted to be discovered after death. He'd chosen Pruitt the way you choose a doctor you don't like: because the alternative was no doctor at all.

Which meant the station's whole theology was wrong. The song hadn't escaped. It hadn't been stolen. It had been sent — sent away from the neighborhood, away from the water tower, away from the people who loved him, toward a man in a clean suit, because my brother believed no one here would ever come looking. Because of me. Because the one person who should have come looking had spent nine years perfecting the art of not looking.

I could have said all of it. Fourteen words would have ended the siege: *He mailed them. It's legal. Pruitt wins, but Teo chose him.*

Instead I said: "I haven't seen anything like that. Teo wasn't... organized. He kept things in his head."

The lie went out of me the way a breath goes out — necessary, invisible, immediately regretted. Marisol nodded. Priya wrote something down. And somewhere in the wall behind my sternum, I felt the hairline fracture extend another centimeter.

* * *

After the meeting, Dez caught me on the catwalk outside the transmitter shed. The city lay below us in its usual arrangement of lit and unlit windows, and somewhere down there was the laundromat, and above the laundromat the letter, patient as a landmine.

"Marisol wants to move the masters," Dez said. Dez spoke maybe forty words a day and spent them like a miser. "Tonight. Before Pruitt's people get a court order with teeth. There's a storage unit in Saxon Heights. Dry. Locked. Off the books."

"You're asking me to help hide evidence."

"I'm asking you to help hide music." Dez looked out at the city. "Evidence is what Pruitt calls it. Music is what it is. You've heard it. You know the difference."

I did know the difference. That was the problem. I'd spent my career being paid to not know the difference — to look at a corroded bearing and see only a bearing, to write *within tolerance* in reports where *within tolerance* meant *nobody died today, probably*. The whole profession is a conspiracy of acceptable risk. And now here I was, being invited into a smaller conspiracy with warmer lighting, and finding that complicity, it turns out, feels almost exactly like belonging.

"When?" I said.

"Midnight. Bring gloves."

* * *

We moved the tapes in two trips in Hollis's van, which smelled of diesel and decades. I carried the cases myself — refused help, actually, which Priya noticed and said nothing about, because Priya noticed everything and said nothing about most of it. Forty-one reels. Each one labeled in Teo's handwriting, dates and titles and little marginal notes: *fix the bridge in bar 12* — and I stopped breathing for a second at that one, until I realized he meant the musical bridge, the passage between verse and chorus, the part of a song that exists to carry you across.

Everything was bridges with him. Even now. Especially now.

At the storage unit, while Dez worked the lock, I held the last case against my chest and understood, with the clarity I usually reserved for structural failure reports, exactly what I had become. I was the inspector who signs off on the span he knows is compromised. I was the stamp that says SAFE on a document that means *we decided not to look closer.* Every load-bearing wall in every building I'd ever condemned had been, at some point, a person deciding that the truth could wait until after the inspection.

"You okay?" Priya asked. She'd ridden along on the second trip.

"Fine," I said, which was becoming my signature forgery.

She looked at me for a long moment in the sodium light of the storage corridor. "You know what's strange? Teo talked about you. Not much. But when he did—" She stopped herself, adjusted the strap of her bag. "He never said your name angry. That's all I'll say."

Then she walked back to the van, and I stood alone in the corridor with forty-one reels of my brother's voice locked behind a door I had helped choose, keeping a secret that was eating through me at a rate I could measure, and could not report.

* * *

Marisol found me Thursday, at the tower, doing nothing in particular — which is to say, standing near the transmitter with the particular stillness of a man listening for a sound he dreads. She had two coffees and handed me one without asking, which was her way of establishing that whatever came next, we were on the same side of it.

"Priya told me you carried every case yourself," she said. "Wouldn't let anyone touch them."

"They were heavy."

"They were forty pounds each and you're built like a bridge cable. You carried them because you needed to." She sipped her coffee, watching me over the rim. "That's a guilt carry, Wren. I've seen it before. Hollis does it with the original transmitter parts. People hold what they think they failed."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You don't seem to." Then, softer: "Whatever you're not telling me — and you are not telling me something, I've run a radio station for thirteen years, I can hear dead air even when music's playing — whatever it is, it will be safer here than anywhere else. That's the whole point of this place. We keep lights on so things can be seen."

I looked at the coffee. I looked at her. And I said, "There's nothing to tell," and watched her face do the small, terrible arithmetic of a woman realizing the load-bearing wall has a crack running through it — and deciding, for now, to trust the math anyway.

She patted my arm and went back inside, and through the window I could see her stop at the whiteboard, marker uncapped, staring at the word PRUITT for a long time before writing, beneath it, in letters I could read from the catwalk:

ASK WREN AGAIN.
