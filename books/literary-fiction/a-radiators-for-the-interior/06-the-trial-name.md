## 6. The Trial Name

The Marrow Bay Public Library occupied a Carnegie-era building that had been "modernized" in 1974 and had regretted it ever since. The carpet was the color of burnt orange dreams, and the microfilm machines—two of them, hulking gray dinosaurs with hand-cranks and viewing screens like portholes—sat in a back corner behind the reference desk, under a sign that read GENEALOGY & LOCAL HISTORY: PLEASE BE PATIENT WITH THE PAST.

Kestrel appreciated the sign. She felt she'd been being patient with the past for three days straight, and it had not once returned the courtesy.

The librarian on duty was a woman of about seventy named Mrs. Okonkwo, whom Kestrel remembered vaguely from childhood—the same cardigan, possibly the same glasses, both apparently immortal. Mrs. Okonkwo took one look at Kestrel's request slip and said, "You're Ruth Ishikawa's daughter."

It wasn't a question. Kestrel was learning that in Marrow Bay it never was.

"I am."

"Your mother used to come in every Thursday. Same chair." Mrs. Okonkwo pointed to a reading chair by the window as if pointing out a historical monument. "Mysteries. She liked the ones where everybody turns out to be lying."

Kestrel laughed before she could stop herself—a short, startled sound—and then something in her chest folded over. *Everybody turns out to be lying.* Her mother had read forty years of those books and never once recognized herself in them, or maybe she'd recognized herself in every single one.

"I need the newspaper archives," Kestrel said. "1981. The *Marrow Bay Sentinel*."

"Microfilm," said Mrs. Okonkwo, with the reverence other people reserved for first editions. "Machine two works if you talk nicely to it. Machine one hasn't been right since the Nixon administration, but then neither have I."

* * *

Machine two accepted Kestrel's niceties and rewarded her with a screen full of grainy newsprint. She started with January 1981 and cranked forward, week by week, watching the town scroll past in reverse-faded ink: the harvest festival, a school board dispute about the gymnasium roof, an advertisement for the Sea Breeze Motel promising COLOR TV IN EVERY ROOM, which explained, Kestrel thought, why Theo had been so unimpressed by everything since.

She didn't know exactly what she was looking for. That was the honest version. The dishonest version—the version she'd told herself over coffee that morning—was that she was doing due diligence, verifying the name Odile Vasseur against the public record, a reasonable archival step in any estate inventory. But inventories were lists. This was a hunt. She could feel the difference in her hands, which wanted to crank faster than the film allowed.

The letters were in the trunk of her car. Forty-one of them, wrapped now in a clean pillowcase because the shoebox had begun shedding cardboard flakes onto everything she owned. She had not read past the fourth letter. She'd meant to read them all last night, alone in the house, and instead she'd sat at the kitchen table until two in the morning holding letter number five without opening it, the way you might hold a door you weren't sure you wanted to walk through.

So: the library. Public record. A place with fluorescent lights and other people, where the past had to behave itself, or at least be catalogued.

March 1981. The crank turned. The weeks went by.

And then there it was—not on page one, which somehow made it worse. Page three. Below a story about a church bake sale. Above a classified ad for a used outboard motor.

**CANNERY WORKER CONVICTED IN PIER FIRE**

The photograph was small and blurry, the way photographs were when they'd been printed from a negative that had already lived a hard life. A young man, mid-twenties, dark hair, high cheekbones. He was not smiling. He was not scowling either; his face held the particular blankness of someone who has been told to stand still by people who own the camera and everything else.

Émile Vasseur.

Kestrel read the article twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way you reread a sentence in a foreign language hoping fluency will arrive if you just give it another chance.

The fire had been in March 1981, a night fire at the Pacific Crest cannery pier—the cannery. *Her mother's* cannery, the place Ruth had worked from the time she was nineteen, packing salmon in the summers, then bookkeeping in the off-seasons. The place where, according to family legend worn smooth by retelling, Ruth had met Henri Ishikawa, who ran the loading schedules. Family legend said Henri had charmed her by fixing her broken umbrella. Family legend said a lot of things.

No one had been hurt, the article said. The fire had consumed the north pier and the cold-storage annex, causing damages estimated at $180,000—"a devastating figure," the paper editorialized, "for a facility already struggling." Arson was suspected almost immediately. Émile Vasseur, a cannery worker employed seasonally for three years, was arrested within nine days.

Convicted in October 1981. Eleven years.

Kestrel's eyes went down the list of prosecution witnesses the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

*Ishikawa, R.*

Not even her whole name. Just the surname, married into, compressed into eight characters between two others. *Ishikawa, R.* had testified. *Ishikawa, R.* had stood up in a courtroom in front of a jury and said words that helped put this young man—this brother of Odile's, this face in the blurry photograph—in prison for eleven years.

Kestrel sat back from the machine. The viewing screen glowed green-white in the dim corner, patient as a held breath.

There were explanations. Of course there were. There were always explanations; she was beginning to understand that her entire inheritance was a stack of explanations, each one load-bearing, none of them inspected. Maybe Ruth had simply seen what she saw. Maybe Émile had done it. Maybe a witness is just a witness, and testifying to what you believe is not betrayal, it's citizenship. Maybe forty-one unsent letters to a woman whose brother you helped convict were the letters of a woman with nothing to hide and a lot of unsent mail.

But the letters hadn't read like nothing-to-hide. They'd read like weather before a storm that never breaks—pressure, pressure, pressure, and then a change of subject. Letter four, the last one Kestrel had permitted herself, dated June 1979, had ended mid-sentence about a trip to the tide pools, and then, in a different, smaller handwriting, as if written later in the margin of her own life: *I saw E. at the cannery today. He asked after you. I didn't know what my face did.*

He asked after you. Odile's brother. Working at the cannery. Two years before the fire.

Kestrel wrote the date on the back of a library request slip: October 14, 1981. Conviction date. Underneath it she wrote, in her careful inventory hand—the hand that had catalogued every spoon and doily in the house on Larch Street—*Ruth testified.*

The silence had acquired a shape. It was no longer a fog, no longer the general atmospheric condition of her childhood, the thing she'd assumed was simply climate. It was a shape with a date. It began, or at least it thickened, on a specific October day thirty-nine years ago, in a courtroom she'd never known existed, involving a name she'd learned only this week.

She thought about her mother's Thursdays in this library. Mysteries where everybody turns out to be lying. Had Ruth sat in that window chair reading about fictional crimes while the real one sat in her own history like a stone in a shoe? Had she ever walked past machine two, this machine, and wondered what Kestrel would find if Kestrel ever came looking?

Had she known Kestrel would come looking? Was that what the letters were—not a secret kept, but a message left, the way you leave a light on?

"Find what you needed?" Mrs. Okonkwo appeared beside her with a cart of returns, moving with the unhurried authority of someone who has reshelved ten thousand books and buried several husbands' worth of gossip.

"I'm not sure," Kestrel said honestly.

Mrs. Okonkwo glanced at the screen. Kestrel watched her read the headline, watched something move behind the old woman's eyes—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone confirming a fact they've carried so long it's become furniture.

"The Vasseur girl," Mrs. Okonkwo said quietly. "Odile. She worked the line with your mother, summers. They were inseparable for a while there. People used to say you couldn't return one's library book without the other's turning up in the bag." She paused. "After the trial, nobody much spoke of either of them. That's how it goes here. We don't forget things in Marrow Bay. We just agree not to say them, and eventually the agreement does the forgetting for us."

"Do you know what happened to Odile?"

"She left. Early eighties. Portland, I heard, though who knows." Mrs. Okonkwo squared a stack of returns against her hip. "Her brother got out around '92. Came back for a year or two. Kept to himself. Died some years back." She looked at Kestrel then, directly, and her voice gentled. "Your mother was a good woman, Kestrel. I want to say that before you go digging whatever you're digging. Good women are still women, though. And this town has always been better at burying things than at burning them—which, given our history with fires, may be the one sensible habit we've got."

She wheeled the cart away. Kestrel stared at the screen until the film burned its ghost-image into her vision: Émile Vasseur's blank young face, floating.

* * *

She should have gone home. It was nearly four o'clock; the light through the library windows had gone the color of weak tea, and she had half a casserole in the refrigerator that Theo's wife had sent down with him, and a house full of boxes that expected to be inventoried. Instead she found herself asking Mrs. Okonkwo for the county records, and Mrs. Okonkwo, who had clearly been waiting for exactly this request, led her to a shelf of bound volumes and left her alone with them like a nurse leaving a patient with the good drugs.

The trial transcript wasn't there—that would take a records request, a fee, probably a form in triplicate—but the *Sentinel*'s coverage of the trial itself was bound in the annual volume for 1981, and Kestrel read every word of it standing up, which felt appropriate. Standing, in a corner, in bad light, reading about a man whose life had been decided while everyone else sat down.

The coverage was thin and dutiful. The prosecution's case rested on a witness who placed Émile near the pier that night, on a set of keys found in his truck that opened the annex door, and on the testimony of cannery employees regarding his "recent disputes with management." The defense called it circumstantial. The jury took six hours.

And Ruth Ishikawa—Ishikawa, R.—had testified to seeing Émile's truck at the pier gate at eleven-forty that night, a detail the article called "pivotal."

Eleven-forty. Kestrel closed her eyes and tried to imagine her mother at twenty-eight, sitting in a witness box, saying *eleven-forty* in her precise, careful voice. Her mother, who alphabetized spices. Who apologized to furniture when she bumped into it. Who had once driven forty minutes home because she thought she might have left the iron on and hadn't been able to enjoy the movie either way.

What had her face done, in that box, saying those words?

And here was the thing that made Kestrel's hands go cold on the bound spine: the fire had happened at the cannery where Henri worked. Where Henri was, by all accounts, indispensable—the man who knew the loading schedules, the insurance paperwork, the value of everything on that struggling pier. The article mentioned, in passing, that the facility collected on its fire policy the following spring, and that the rebuilt annex was "finer than the original." Nobody in 1981 seemed to find that interesting.

Kestrel found it interesting. Kestrel found it so interesting she had to put the volume down and press her palms flat against the shelf until the room stopped tilting.

Because there was a letter—letter three, the one she'd read on the second night, the one she'd filed mentally under *strange* and moved past—where Ruth wrote: *Henri says we'll be fine now. He says the worst is behind us. He keeps saying it, Odile, the way you'd repeat a password until you believed it.*

She had thought, reading it, that Ruth was writing about money troubles. About the lean years. Everyone knew the Ishikawas had struggled early on; it was part of the family legend, the humble chapter before the modest prosperity.

Now the sentence sat differently. Now it sat like a confession wearing a raincoat.

"No," Kestrel said aloud, to the shelf, to the dust, to nobody. Because she was not going to think that. Not yet. Not on the strength of one ambiguous sentence and a newspaper's passing mention of an insurance payout. Accusing her dead father of arson based on vibes and a bound volume was not inventory. It was not even investigation. It was grief wearing a detective's hat.

But her heart kept going anyway, the way hearts do, down the corridor she'd just told it not to enter, knocking on doors. *Henri says we'll be fine now.* The fire in March. The conviction in October. The letters beginning in '79 and running for forty years, addressed to a woman whose brother was serving the sentence. Ruth's silence, which Kestrel had spent her whole life mistaking for temperament—her mother was private, her mother was reserved, her mother was a locked room, ha ha, isn't that just how she was—

It wasn't how she was. It was what had been done to her. Or what she'd done. Or both. Those were not always different things, Kestrel was beginning to suspect. Sometimes the lock and the prisoner are installed on the same day, by the same hand.

* * *

She drove home along the water road, the one that passed the cannery district, because the alternative was the highway and she wasn't ready for the highway's efficiency. The cannery itself had been closed since the nineties; its long shed roofs sagged like tired shoulders, and the pier stubs stuck out of the gray water like the teeth of something that had died mid-sentence. In the low evening light the rusted corrugated walls glowed a dull copper, almost beautiful, the way ruins are permitted to be beautiful precisely because they're finished.

Her mother had parked in that lot for eleven years. Had punched a clock, packed fish, fixed her lipstick in a rearview mirror. Had worked alongside Émile Vasseur, who asked after Odile. Had watched the north pier burn—or hadn't watched it, had maybe been home, had maybe been asleep next to Henri, who said *we'll be fine now* the way you'd repeat a password.

Kestrel pulled over at the gravel turnout across from the old gate and sat with the engine ticking.

Forty-one letters in the trunk. She'd read four. She had thirty-seven left, and every one of them now had a new weight class. Before today they'd been evidence of a hidden friendship, poignant, strange, sad. Now they were potentially something else: forty years of correspondence between her mother and the sister of the man her mother helped convict—and possibly, her mind whispered, the man her mother's husband had framed.

*Don't,* she told herself again. But the word had lost its authority somewhere around mile marker four.

A gull landed on the guardrail and regarded her with the frank appraisal of a creature that has seen everything and been impressed by none of it.

"All right," Kestrel told it. "Yes. I know."

She drove the rest of the way home in the dark, and when she got to the house on Larch Street she did not go inside right away. She stood in the driveway looking up at her mother's bedroom window, the dark rectangle of it, and behind that window, somewhere in the wall, there was a cavity in the plaster, and in the cavity there had been a shoebox, and in the shoebox there were thirty-seven letters she hadn't read yet, and one of them—one of the ones from the eighties, she was suddenly certain of it, certain the way you're certain about weather—was going to be about the fire.

She went in. She hung up her coat. She fed herself standing at the counter, and then she carried the pillowcase-wrapped bundle to the kitchen table, and turned on every light in the room, because some rooms you read in the dark and some rooms you don't.

Letter five was dated September 1981. Six weeks before the verdict.

Kestrel smoothed it flat beneath her palms, the way her mother used to smooth pie dough, and began to read.
