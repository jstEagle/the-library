## 17. Odile's Door

The apartment above the bait shop smelled of diesel and old coffee, which was to say it smelled of Marrow Bay, and Odile Vasseur had made her peace with that the way you make peace with a limp—by forgetting, most hours, that it was a choice.

She had put out two cups anyway. She didn't know why. Habit, maybe. Or hope wearing habit's coat. When Kestrel Ishikawa came up the stairs—the girl moved carefully, like someone walking on a floor she suspected of being rotten—Odile gestured at the kitchen table without getting up from her chair by the window.

"Sit," she said. "I only have decaf. At my age caffeine is just a way of borrowing trouble from tomorrow."

Kestrel sat. She had the box on her lap. Forty-one letters in a shoebox gone soft at the corners, and Odile looked at it the way you look at an X-ray of your own hand: familiar bones, wrong context.

"You found them behind the wardrobe," Odile said. Not a question. She'd known where Ruth would hide them before Kestrel said a word about finding anything. There were only so many places in that house with room for a silence that size.

"The plaster cavity. It took me three days to even notice the wall sounded different."

"She was good at walls." Odile poured the decaf, her hand steady enough, thank God, though her knees had opinions these days and her left eye saw halos around bright things, which the doctor called a cataract and Odile called a filter, finally, on a world that had always been too much. "You want to know about the night she came here."

Kestrel's hands went still around her cup. "I want to know everything. But yes. That night."

Odile looked out the window. The harbor was doing its gray morning business, gulls arguing over something dead, a crab boat idling at the fuel dock with its name half flaked off the hull—the *Marta B*, and she thought, not for the first time, that boats should be required to keep their names legible out of respect for everyone who'd ever loved one.

"It was March," she said. "The ninth or the tenth. I know because Émile's sentencing had been the week before, and I had stopped keeping a calendar after that. What was the use? Every day was going to be the same day."

* * *

She told it plainly, because she had told it to herself so many times over forty years that it had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket.

Émile had been convicted in February 1981. Arson at the Pacific Crest cannery. Eleven years. He was twenty-six years old. Their mother had died the following autumn—not of grief, the doctors said, of her heart, but Odile knew what a heart dies of when it has been fed nothing but injustice for eight months. And Odile had stayed in Marrow Bay through all of it, working the fish counter at Jensen's, writing appeals nobody answered, being the sister of the man who burned down half the waterfront. People were not cruel to her face. They were worse. They were careful.

"I had learned to read the town like weather," she told Kestrel. "Averted eyes meant rain. Kindness meant a storm coming. Your father—" she paused, and made herself go on, because the girl deserved the whole weight of it, "—your father used to nod at me across the street. Just a nod. Small, polite, nothing anyone could object to. But every time he did it I felt like a dog being shown who held the leash."

"He set the fire." Kestrel said it quietly, not as a question either. "I found the ledgers. The insurance. The timing."

"I know what you found. I've known since 1981." Odile turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, a small ritual, aligning the handle toward the window. "Knowing and proving are different animals. Knowing lives in your chest. Proving needs paper, witnesses, money, luck. I had none of those. I had a brother in Walla Walla and a town that wanted the story closed."

"And Ruth?"

"Ruth." Odile laughed then, a short dry sound with no humor in it, more cough than laugh. "Ruth was the one thing I could never read. You understand—I knew her better than I knew my own handwriting. We met at the cannery in '74, sorting line, and inside a month we were finishing each other's sentences. She was the other interior of my life. Whatever I thought, I thought it twice, once alone and once for her. When Émile was arrested I went to her house and sat in her kitchen and she held my hands and said, *Odile, I will do whatever is in my power.* And I believed her. That's the part I want you to carry. I believed her completely. I wasn't a fool. I was certain."

The gulls outside had found whatever they were fighting over and dispersed, victorious or defeated, impossible to say.

"So. March. The ninth or tenth. Evening. I was living in the apartment over the hardware store then, and I heard footsteps on the stairs and I knew them before I heard them—you know how that is, when your body knows a person's rhythm better than your ears do. I opened the door and there she was."

Odile stopped. Even now, even smoothed by four decades of retelling-to-herself, that image arrived intact: Ruth Ishikawa on the landing, coat buttoned wrong—one button off, so the hem hung crooked—hair uncombed, face pale as a plate. Standing. Not knocking. Not speaking.

"She stood there," Odile said. "That's the whole of it. That's the story. She stood on my landing for—I don't know. A minute. Two. Long enough for me to say her name three times. *Ruth. Ruth. Ruthie.* Nothing. Her mouth opened once, like a fish, and closed. And then she turned around and went back down the stairs, and I stood in my doorway listening to her steps get smaller until the street took them."

Kestrel's eyes were wet. Odile watched her cry with a kind of clinical tenderness, the way she watched the tide come in. Grief was grief; it didn't matter whose.

"Why?" Kestrel asked. "Why would she come and say nothing?"

"Because saying it would have made it real, and she couldn't afford real." Odile said it without cruelty, or trying to. "Your mother came to confess and couldn't. Came to warn me and couldn't. Came to beg forgiveness and couldn't. She came because the pressure of the secret had built up past what a body can hold, and she thought if she could just stand near me, some of it might leak out through the floorboards. And when it didn't—when she found she was still sealed—she left. And I was left standing there with a confession that had no words in it, which is worse than any words. Words you can argue with. Words you can hate. Silence you just have to live inside."

She got up, slowly, one hand on the table, and went to the dresser under the window. From the bottom drawer she took a photograph, creased white at one corner, and laid it on the table between them. Two young women on the cannery dock, arms slung around each other's shoulders, squinting into sun. One dark-haired, laughing mid-word. One smaller, watchful, half-smiling like someone who'd just been talked into joy and wasn't sure she'd stay.

"That's us in '75," Odile said. "I keep it in the drawer because if I keep it on the wall I'll talk to it, and I promised myself I was done talking to people who don't answer."

* * *

They drank the decaf. Outside, the crab boat finished fueling and chugged out past the breakwater, and Odile tracked it until it was a smudge.

"You wrote to her," Kestrel said finally. "Forty-one times. You never sent them."

"I wrote to her the way you'd write to the sea. It doesn't answer, but it holds what you give it." Odile folded her hands. "And I never sent them because sending them would have been asking her for something, and I had decided—around 1983, I remember deciding it deliberately, sitting right in this chair—that I would rather die owing nothing to Ruth Ishikawa than live one more day waiting on her."

"But you came back. Two years ago. To Marrow Bay."

"I came back because I read in the county paper that they were tearing down Cannery Row for condos, and I thought, good, let it fall, and then I couldn't sleep, and then I was on a bus." She shrugged. "Old women are allowed to be unreasonable. It's one of the few benefits."

"And you tended Émile's grave."

"Somebody had to. The funeral home does a mowing, but mowing isn't tending." She looked at Kestrel directly then, and let her see everything, because the girl had earned it, carrying that box up the stairs. "I waited two years, Kestrel. Do you know what I was waiting for? I told myself I was waiting for the demolition, that I wanted to see the cannery district fall. But that's not true. I was waiting for the house on Alder Street to give up its ghost. I knew Ruth kept something. Women like Ruth always keep something—it's where they store the part of themselves they traded away. And I thought, someday that house will come down, and whatever she hid will come out, and someone will bring it to me. Because that's how it works. The truth is patient. It's the only thing in this world more patient than I am."

Kestrel set down her cup. Her hands were trembling slightly, and she pressed them flat against her thighs like a woman steadying a small boat.

"I brought the letters," she said. "All of them. I've read every one. I'm not—I don't know what I am. I'm not here to apologize for her. I don't think I'm allowed to."

"Good," Odile said sharply. "Because if you apologized to me on her behalf I would have to throw you down these stairs, and at my age I'd pull something."

The laugh escaped Kestrel sideways, wet and startled, and Odile permitted herself half a smile. Gentle humor was the last tool in her kit and she'd earned the right to use it.

"What do you want, then?" Kestrel asked. "Everyone keeps asking what I want. Theo wants it buried. The county wants it demolished. I thought I wanted the truth, but the truth turns out to be a house fire—it spreads. So tell me. What do you want?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. She looked at the photograph on the table, at the two girls on the dock with their whole lives ahead of them, one of whom had spent hers inside a locked room of her own building.

"I'll tell you what I don't want," she said. "I don't want an apology. Apologies are currency, and I'm too old to shop. I don't want her shame, and I don't want her regret—they're hers, and she can keep them wherever she is, if anywhere is." She leaned forward. "Here is what I want. I want those letters read aloud. Out loud, in the open air, at that house, where she wrote them and hid them and lived around them for forty years. I want the walls to hear them before the walls come down. Because a letter that's never been read aloud is just paper with wishes on it. Read aloud, it becomes testimony. It becomes the record. It becomes the thing she owed me, paid in the only coin she ever had, which was her voice."

"The house comes down Thursday," Kestrel said. "Demolition morning. Seven o'clock."

"Then you know what to do." Odile rose, joints announcing themselves, and took the photograph back. Before sliding it into the drawer she looked at it one more time—at the laughing girl, at the watchful one—and her thumb passed over the watchful one's face, briefly, the way you touch a scar to check it's still yours.

"And Kestrel," she added, as the girl gathered the box and stood. "When you read them—read the hard ones too. Don't curate her. She curated herself her whole life. Give her the dignity of being fully known, even posthumously. Especially posthumously. The dead can't defend themselves, which means the living owe them honesty instead of mercy."

Kestrel nodded. At the top of the stairs she paused, box against her chest.

"Will you be there? Thursday?"

Odile looked out at the water, at the gray light coming apart into morning.

"I've been there for forty years," she said. "Thursday won't be any different. Except louder."

* * *

After the girl left, Odile washed the two cups and dried them and put them away, and then she sat by the window with her knitting she didn't knit and thought about the ninth or tenth of March, 1981, and about a woman standing on a landing with her coat buttoned wrong.

For years she had hated that image. Then for years she had pitied it. Now, holding it up in the light of this particular morning, she found she felt neither. What she felt was recognition—the awful, ordinary recognition of one coward meeting another across a wound. Because Odile had known. Had known since the insurance adjuster's face changed in 1981, since the numbers in the trial record refused to sit straight, since Émile himself stopped writing *they've got the wrong man, Odile, tell them* and started writing *don't ruin your life over mine*. She had known, and she had done nothing but write unsent letters and tend a grave.

Two women, she thought. Both knowing. Both silent. One hiding letters behind a wardrobe, one hiding a photograph in a drawer. Ruth had gotten a daughter out of the arrangement, and a life, and a death in a bed. Odile had gotten patience, which she had mistaken for virtue for so long she'd nearly believed it.

Thursday, then. She would stand at the property line and hear forty years of Ruth Ishikawa's voice pour out of her daughter's mouth, and the walls would hear it, and then the walls would fall, and that would have to be enough. It wouldn't be justice. Justice had sailed years ago, if it had ever been in port at all. But it would be true, and true was the only currency Odile had ever respected.

She got out the county paper and checked the notice again, running her finger under the print: *Demolition of structures at 1140 Alder Street scheduled to commence Thursday, 7:00 a.m.*

Seven o'clock. She would need to be up early. She hadn't dressed for a confession in forty years, and she intended to look like what she was: the last witness.

Outside, the tide was turning, and somewhere over the water the gulls had started up again, arguing, always arguing, about who owned what the sea gave up.
