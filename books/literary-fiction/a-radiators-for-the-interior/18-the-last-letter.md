## 18. The Last Letter

I had been treating the box like a loaf of bread I was afraid to finish.

Forty-one letters, and I had rationed them—one in the morning with coffee, one at night with wine—because some part of me believed that as long as unread letters remained, my mother was still capable of surprising me. Still alive in the only way that mattered now, which was the way of the unfinished sentence. Theo would have called this morbid. Theo called most of what I'd been doing morbid, when he called at all, which lately he didn't.

It was a Tuesday, eleven days before the demolition, and I had run out of reasons to slow down. The morning letter had been number forty-one, dated March of Ruth's last spring, and it had ended with a line that kept snagging on something in me all day: *I think I am almost done being a coward, but I have thought that before.* It snagged because it sounded less like a letter and more like a door left open on purpose. A door with light behind it.

So that evening I went back up to the bedroom—the one that had been hers for fifty years, then nobody's for three days, then mine by accident—and I took the shoebox down from the wardrobe cavity where I'd returned it, because returning it had felt correct in a way I couldn't defend, and I turned the box over and shook it gently, the way you shake a purse looking for a coin you're sure is there.

Something slid against the cardboard. Not paper-on-paper. Something heavier, wrapped.

I tipped the box over the bed and out came a flat parcel, folded in waxed paper gone brittle as moth wing, tied with kitchen twine in a knot so old it had fused into itself. Underneath it, pressed flat against the bottom of the box where no one would ever look unless they were looking for nothing, was an envelope. No stamp. No address except mine.

*Kestrel Ishikawa,* it said, in her handwriting, and beneath it: *to be opened last or never.*

My hands did not shake. I want to be honest about that, because everything after this people assume my hands shook, and they didn't. They went very still and very cold, the way they used to go when I was a child and touched a stove element to see if it was on. My body has always known danger before my mind agrees to it.

The twine knot defeated me for four full minutes. I tried fingernails, then scissors, then finally just worried it apart like gristle, and the waxed paper crackled open along its creases, and inside was a photograph I had never seen.

Two young women on the cannery loading dock, arms slung around each other's shoulders, squinting into a sun that fell on Marrow Bay sometime around 1980. On the left, my mother—young in a way that rearranged my whole understanding of the word, her hair loose, her head tipped toward the other woman as though mid-laugh, as though the laugh belonged half to each of them. On the right, a woman I knew instantly even though I had never seen her face: Odile Vasseur, dark-eyed, sharp-chinned, holding a cigarette she wasn't smoking, wearing the expression of someone who finds the world funny but intends to keep that opinion to herself.

On the back, in pencil: *R & O, dock, June. She says we look like sisters. I say better.*

I sat with the photograph in my lap for a long time. Downstairs the refrigerator cycled on, the house ticked through its evening small talk, and outside a gull made its usual complaint about the state of the world. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a house that had been keeping a secret so long the secret had become part of its structure, load-bearing, and now here I was pulling it out to see if the walls would hold.

Then I opened the envelope.

* * *

*My Kestrel,*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you found the box, or you are dead and this letter will be read by strangers sorting through our things, in which case, hello strangers, please be gentle with her memory, she deserves it more than I do.*

*I have started this letter eleven times. I know because I count things when I am frightened. Eleven beginnings, all in the fire or the drawer. This is the twelfth, and I am writing it in pen so that I cannot erase myself again. That is the whole trick of a coward's life, Kestrel. There is always an eraser within reach.*

*You deserve to hear this from my mouth, and I waited too long for my mouth to be brave, and now my mouth is mostly occupied with hospitals and apologies to nurses, so you get the pen instead. Forgive the handwriting. It was always bad. You used to say my grocery lists looked like seismographs.*

*Here is what happened. I will tell it plainly because plainness is the only gift I have left to give you.*

*In February of 1981, your father set fire to the Marrow Bay Cannery. He did it for the insurance. The company was failing—he had been hiding it from me for a year, borrowing against everything, and there was a plan, and the plan required a fire, and the plan required someone to burn for it. He chose Émile Vasseur. He chose Émile because Émile was loud and foreign and had once publicly called him a thief, which was accurate, and because Henri understood that a jury forgives insurance companies long before it forgives arsonists, and forgives them fastest when the arsonist has an accent.*

*I knew. I want to be exact about this, because vagueness is how I hid from myself for forty years: I knew before the fire. He told me. He stood in our kitchen—I can still show you the spot, third tile from the sink, the cracked one—and he told me what he intended, and he told me that if I loved him I would stand beside him, and he told me that you were coming, Kestrel, I was six weeks pregnant and sick every morning, and he said: a child needs a father with a name that isn't ruined.*

*And I stood beside him. And when the police came, and later when the lawyers came, I gave them my testimony, and my testimony was true in every particular and false in its whole. Every fact I swore to was accurate. I saw Émile near the cannery that night; he was there, arguing with your father about wages. What I withheld was the argument's content, and the plan, and the third tile from the sink. They asked me if I knew of any reason Émile Vasseur might have wanted to harm the cannery, and I said no, and that "no" is the single worst thing I have ever done, and I did it while carrying you, which means you are woven into the lie whether you consented or not. You weren't. Consent requires knowledge. I made sure you never had the knowledge. That was my crime too, in the end—not the silence itself but the maintenance of it, the daily sweeping of it, forty years of dusting around a body in the room.*

*Émile got nine years. He served six. His sister Odile—my Odile, my other interior, the person who knew me better than God does—never spoke to me again, and she was right not to, and I have missed her every day of my life with a specificity that would embarrass us both if she knew. Your father knew I knew. We never discussed it. We built a marriage on top of it the way you build a deck over a sinkhole, and the deck held, and we had good years on that deck, Kestrel, I need you to know that too, because I will not let him be only a monster and I will not let myself be only a victim. He was generous and funny and cruel in exactly one place, and I married the generosity and lived with the cruelty and called the arrangement love, and some days it was.*

*Theo knows a piece of this. He found a letter when he was seventeen, and I made him promise, and I told him it was for your sake, and that was partly true and partly the oldest coward's trick of all: using love as a lock. If you are angry at anyone, be angry at me. He was a boy doing what his mother begged. I was a grown woman doing what I pleased and calling it protection.*

*Now the instructions, because you will want instructions, you are my daughter and you alphabetize your spice rack.*

*The letters in the box are for Odile. All of them. Even the ugly ones, especially the ugly ones. Do not read them first and decide which she can bear. That was my sin, deciding what others could bear. Deliver them whole.*

*Do not apologize for me. If you find her—if she can be found, and I believe she can, because Odile is a person who returns, she returns like weather—do not say "my mother was sorry." Say the letters. Let her hear my actual voice, forty years of it, unsent. She spent her life listening to what I wouldn't say. Give her the saying.*

*And do this before the house comes down. I know about the demolition order; the county writes to me still, out of habit. I could have contested it. I signed the papers instead, and I want you to understand why, because it is the closest I ever came to courage: I wanted the silence to die somewhere. A house that holds a secret that long shouldn't outlive the people who kept it. Let the walls take it with them. But the words should leave first. Words are the only part of us that survives being carried.*

*I am not asking for forgiveness. I checked, while writing this, whether I was, and I am not. Forgiveness is Odile's to consider and yours, and neither of you owes it to a woman who couldn't manage a sentence for four decades. What I ask is smaller and harder: tell the truth where the lying happened. That's all. Stand in the place and say the thing. I have learned, late, that truth doesn't heal anything. It just stops the wound from pretending to be closed.*

*You have my hands, Kestrel. I mean that literally—look at them, the long fingers, the crooked left index—but I also mean it in every other way. You have my talent for noticing and my disease of not-saying. Break the second one. Keep the first. Notice everything, and then, for the love of whatever you love, say it out loud.*

*There is a radiator in the front hall that clanks at 3 a.m. every night of its life. I never fixed it. Your father offered to fix it a hundred times and I always said no, and I never told anyone why, so I'll say it here where it's safe: it was the only honest voice in the house. Everything else in that hall was polished and quiet and lying. The radiator alone told the truth about itself, all night, every night, and I loved it for that, and now you know, and now somebody knows, and that is what a confession is, I discover—a radiator for the interior. Clanking away in the dark so the house stays honest.*

*I love you past the end of what language handles. I have loved you since before the lie, during the lie, and after it, and my love was never the false part. Remember that when you hate me. Hate me accurately.*

*Your mother,
*Ruth*

*P.S. The key to the storage unit, if you ever need it, is in the flour tin. Not the flour tin in the kitchen. The one in the garage that has never once contained flour.*

*P.P.S. Tell Theo he is released. He'll know what I mean.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the light through the window went from gold to gray to the blue-black of a coastal evening, and I never turned on the lamp, because some animal part of me felt that if I moved, if I made noise, the letter would stop being true.

But it stayed true. That was the terrible, gorgeous thing about it. It sat on my knees radiating its plainness, itemized horror delivered in my mother's wry, careful, seismic hand, and it did not dissolve, and neither did I, though both felt possible.

I laughed once—actually laughed, a single startled bark—at the flour tin, because that was so completely her, burying the evidence of a lifetime's cowardice behind a joke about baking, and the laugh broke something loose in my chest and then I was crying, hard, the ugly kind, the kind with no control in it at all, sitting on my dead mother's bed in a house with eleven days left to live, holding a photograph of two girls on a dock who looked like they believed the future was going to be kind to them.

When it passed, I did what she told me to. I noticed.

I noticed that my hands had stopped shaking and were steady now, steadier than they'd been since the funeral. I noticed that the grief had changed shape—it hadn't shrunk, but it had acquired edges, a geography, and grief with a map is a different country than grief without one. I noticed that I was not destroyed. I had braced for destruction the way you brace for a wave, and instead the wave had turned out to be information.

And I noticed, standing at the window with the photograph, looking down the dark street toward the water, that I was not finished. The letter had instructions, and instructions require a recipient, and somewhere out there—she believed out there, *returns like weather*, she wrote—was a woman named Odile Vasseur who had spent forty years listening to a silence.

The flour tin in the garage had never contained flour. Of course it hadn't. My mother had hidden a key inside a lie about bread for thirty years, and I had walked past it ten thousand times.

I went downstairs in the dark, past the front hall radiator, silent now, the house cold enough that it had stopped telling the truth about itself, and out through the kitchen door into the garage, and I found the tin on the shelf where it had always been, dented green steel, and inside, under a fold of newspaper from a year I wasn't born, a small brass key on a loop of twine, and a label in her handwriting:

*Unit 114. Marrow Bay Self-Storage. Émile's things. I promised Odile I'd keep them safe. It is the only promise I ever kept, and I kept it in the dark, which is not the same as keeping it.*

I stood in the cold garage holding the key, and the house ticked and settled around me like an old man falling asleep, and eleven days out from the wrecking ball, my mother's voice was still reaching through—clearer now than it had ever been in life, clear as a radiator clanking at three in the morning—and it was pointing me, of all places, toward the last person on earth with reason to help me.

Toward the sister of the man my father burned.

Toward the woman whose brother's things my mother had guarded like a reliquary, in secret, for forty years.

I closed my hand around the key until the teeth of it printed themselves into my palm, and I went inside to pack a bag.
