## 3. First Words

The kitchen table had been cleared for war. A dictionary the size of a paving stone sat at its center, bristling with paper flags in three colors. Beside it: a notebook, two sharpened pencils, a tin of butter cookies whose contents had been replaced by index cards, and a small glass of something amber that Marta Ilves called, without further explanation, "medicine."

"Sit," Marta said, and Gideon sat, because it seemed less dangerous than the alternatives.

The radiator in the corner of the kitchen chose that moment to deliver its verdict — three clanks, a shudder, and a long metallic sigh, like a man settling into a chair he had no intention of getting up from. Gideon had been hearing it for two days now, ever since she'd arrived to assess the piano and found herself, through some failure of the word *no*, agreeing to this. Twice a week. Ten weeks. Until the closing.

She had tuned pianos for thirty-one years and had never once been asked to learn anything in return. Usually the transaction was clean: she made the instrument honest, they paid her, everyone went back to not talking. This arrangement had the texture of something else entirely.

"We begin with greetings," Marta said. She was a small woman built like a bird that had survived several winters on pure stubbornness, with white hair cut short and practical and eyes that did not blink as often as eyes should. "Then seasons. Then we see if you have any aptitude at all."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you will develop some. I am eighty-six years old. I do not have time for your lack of aptitude."

Gideon opened the ledger she'd brought — her working ledger, the one with the radiator notes in the margins — and Marta looked at it with open suspicion, the way one looks at a rival's flag planted on one's own soil.

"You write everything down," Marta said. It was not a question.

"It's how I work. Houses tell you things if you keep records."

"Houses." Marta snorted, a sound of pure Baltic contempt, and Gideon felt an odd, unwelcome jolt of recognition — her grandmother had made exactly that noise, at exactly that volume, whenever Gideon's father said something she considered soft-headed. "Houses don't talk. People don't talk. That is the whole tragedy of the species, and you are keeping a *record* of it."

Gideon looked down at the ledger. The page was full of her neat, cramped handwriting: *front hall radiator — knock at 6:14 a.m., daily, like a creditor. Parlor — hums in E-flat when the wind comes off the lake. Kitchen — the one that sighs.*

"The piano talks," she said. "When it's out of true. That's how you find the problem — you listen for the note that's lying."

For the first time, something in Marta's face moved. Not softened, exactly. *Unlocked*, maybe, one tumbler of it. "Good," she said. "Then you already know the hardest part. Now we do the words."

* * *

The greetings took an hour, which was, Marta announced, "slow, but not hopeless."

*Hello* was *tere* — a word Gideon's mouth knew before her mind did, the *r* rolling up out of some locked room in her throat. She said it and the kitchen went briefly, strangely bright, the way a room looks when a car's headlights sweep across the ceiling outside.

*Good morning* was *tere hommikust*. *Good evening*, *tere õhtust*. The word for *thank you* — *aitäh* — came out of her sounding like her grandmother, and Gideon put her pencil down.

"Water," she said. "Could I have some water?"

Marta fetched it without comment, but her eyes stayed on Gideon the way a person watches a bird that has flown into a window and might fly into another.

They moved to the seasons. *Spring* was *kevad*, a word like something opening. *Summer* was *suvi*, soft as a lake at dusk. *Autumn* was *sügis*, which sounded like a door closing in another room. And *winter* was *talv*, flat and white and final.

"And the one between winter and spring," Marta said. "The ice going out. The word for thaw."

She said it: *sulamine*. And then she said it again, slower, and made Gideon repeat it, and Gideon did, and the sound went through her like a current through a pipe.

Because her grandmother had said that word. Every March, standing at the kitchen window of the house on the lake, coffee mug in hand, watching the ice darken and rot at the edges, saying *sulamine* to no one — to Gideon, to Toomas, to the window, to God — in that language Gideon had spent her childhood pretending was just the sound of an old woman being old. *Sulamine.* The ice going out. The year letting go of its grip.

"Gideon." Marta's voice, careful now. "Where did you go just now?"

"Nowhere."

"You went *somewhere*. You went the way people go when they hear a name called in a crowd." Marta folded her hands on the dictionary. "Your surname is Montrose. Your grandmother's was not."

"No. It was Ilves."

The word landed in the kitchen and sat there. Marta did not look surprised. That was the thing Gideon noticed, the thing that would keep her up that night: Marta did not look surprised at all.

"Then you have been carrying this language around in you for fifty years," Marta said, "like a stone in a coat pocket, wondering why you list to one side." She tapped the dictionary. "We will take it out. Slowly. It will hurt in places. This is normal."

"I'm not sure I agreed to the hurting part."

"You agreed to the lessons. The hurting comes bundled. Like the pipes in this house — you wanted heat, you got the clanking." She poured herself a thimble of the amber medicine and did not offer Gideon any, which Gideon chose to interpret as mercy.

* * *

They broke at four, and Marta made tea, and Gideon did what she always did in a new house: she listened.

The kitchen radiator — the sigher, the one she'd named in her ledger *the old man* — had been quiet since she arrived, but as the afternoon light went long and orange across the floorboards, it began up again. Clank. Pause. Clank-clank. Pause. Clank. A rhythm. Gideon had spent decades learning the rhythms of pipes the way other people learned the rhythms of speech, and this one had been nagging at her since day one, familiar the way a face is familiar across a crowded street.

Clank. Pause. Clank-clank.

Air in the line. Expansion, contraction. That's what she'd have told any client. That's what it always was.

But sitting in her grandmother's language for three hours had loosened something, and the rhythm came at her sideways, the way a melody does when you stop trying to place it. Two beats and one. Two beats and one.

*Too-mas.*

She set down her teacup harder than she meant to. Tea jumped the rim and made a small dark map on Marta's tablecloth.

"That radiator," Gideon said. "The pipes. When were they put in?"

Marta looked at the radiator, then at Gideon, and her bird-bright eyes were unreadable and very old. "Nineteen seventy-one," she said. "The year before I bought the house. Why?"

Nineteen seventy-one. The summer before the summer. The summer the pipes went into the walls of this house, carrying water and air through rooms she had never seen, knocking out a name in a rhythm she would not decode for fifty years.

"No reason," Gideon said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and neither of them said anything about it, which was — she was beginning to suspect — the most fluent either of them had been all day.

* * *

She drove home with the windows down even though it was October, because the car felt full of sounds. *Tere. Sulamine. Too-mas.*

At a red light she caught herself saying the greeting out loud, just to hear it, and an old man in the next lane looked over, and she pretended to be on the phone.

At home, her apartment was quiet in the way it was always quiet — the quiet of a place where one person keeps an inventory of everything unsaid and says none of it. She took out the ledger and, beneath the radiator notes, wrote in her smallest hand:

*Lesson one. Greetings, seasons, thaw. Grandmother's kitchen. The word for ice going out is sulamine and I have been carrying it since 1972 without knowing its weight.*

*The kitchen radiator knocks two-and-one. I am not going to write what it sounds like.*

She stared at that last line for a long time. Then she closed the book, and lay awake until two in the morning, and at two in the morning she got up and opened the book again and wrote, beneath everything, one more line:

*It sounds like a name. I don't know yet whose.*

The next lesson was Thursday. On Wednesday, a letter came — not email, an actual envelope, cream-colored, with handwriting like a seismograph of a small determined earthquake. Inside, one sentence:

*You did not ask me the right question about the pipes. Come Thursday and ask it. — M.I.*
