## 4. Postcards from a Dead Man

My mother's attic smelled the way all attics smell when they've been waiting for you: dust, cedar, and accusation. The ladder groaned under me like an old woman asked to stand one more time, and I climbed into a space lit by a single bulb on a pull chain, the kind of light that makes everything look like evidence.

I had told Daniel I'd handle the attic. He'd handled the solicitor, the flowers, the small and correct logistics of a small and correct funeral, and he'd done it the way he did everything — efficiently, and with the air of a man closing accounts. The attic was mine because I was the one who still lived close, and because Daniel believed, with a conviction I'd never tested, that our mother's house contained nothing but furniture and grief. He was wrong about the furniture, too. Half of it was our grandfather's, brought over in 1957 in crates that had crossed a war's worth of borders to end up holding doilies in a semi in Croydon.

The bulb swung when I moved, and shadows swung with it, patient as tides.

I wasn't looking for anything in particular. That's what I'd tell myself later, anyway, though I suppose I was looking for exactly one thing: the postcard Ilse had slipped into the condolence book, which I had carried home in my coat pocket like a stolen organ. It sat on my kitchen table now. I had not turned it over. Turning it over felt like a decision, and I had spent forty-six years perfecting the art of not deciding.

So instead I did what I always did with grief, which was tidy.

* * *

My mother's attic was organized the way her life had been organized: by suppression. Boxes labeled in her narrow hand — WINTER, TAXES, YOUR FATHER, and then a gap where a label should have been, a box with no marking at all, just a lid gone soft at the edges and a fine gray fur of dust that told me no one had touched it since before I was born. The labeled boxes I carried down without opening. The unlabeled one I carried to the window, where the light came through the vent in the gable in a single dusty blade, and I set it on my knees, and I sat there for a long time doing nothing, which is a skill my family has refined over generations.

The name Vaszary had been sitting in my chest since the funeral. Not a word — a splinter. Ilse had said it the way you'd say a diagnosis, looking at Daniel and then at me, and Daniel had said *she's confused, poor woman*, and our aunt had agreed, and the whole small correct funeral had folded itself back up around the moment like a fan snapping shut. But I had felt the name land in me and stay, the way a piano string keeps ringing after the damper felt comes down, a ghost of vibration in the wood that only the player knows is there.

I opened the box.

Postcards. Hundreds of them. Bundled in packets of ten with string gone brittle, each packet wrapped — and this was the part that made my hands go cold, before I even understood why — each packet wrapped in paper, addressed, stamped, and sealed. Ready to post. Forty years of postcards, all of them mailed to no one.

The topmost packet was addressed in a hand I recognized before I could name it. My mother's kitchen had a drawer of pens and a tin of recipes, and on the inside cover of the recipe tin, in faded ink, was a name: *G. Vaszary*. I had seen that handwriting my whole childhood and never once asked who G. Vaszary was, because asking was against the rules, though nobody had ever said the rules out loud. The rules were: no Hungarian at the table. No questions about Budapest. No mention of the name that came before Pemberton, which we children didn't know existed until we were old enough to be told not to say it.

Every card was addressed to the same person. *Erzsi*. Just that. No surname, no street, no city. As if the name alone were an address, as if somewhere in the world there was a postal system that delivered to grief.

* * *

I cut the string on the earliest packet with my pocketknife, carefully, like a surgeon, like a thief.

The card showed the pier at Brighton, gulls wheeling over a gray sea. Dated 1959. The message, in that same careful hand, was in Hungarian, which I cannot read, and I sat in the attic dust feeling the full absurdity of my inheritance: a box of letters in a language my family had amputated, addressed to a woman I'd never heard of, written by a man whose name I'd learned only a week ago from a stranger at a funeral.

I could read the dates, though. Dates are the Esperanto of the dead. I laid the packets out along the attic floor in order, and the order itself was a sentence: 1959, 1961, 1963, one every year or two at first, then more often, then — in the seventies — two, three a year, the handwriting loosening as the decades went on, the careful émigré script of the early cards degrading into something hurried, cramped, the writing of a man running out of time and knowing it.

Some cards were pictureless, plain stock, as if the sights of England had stopped being worth the ink. Some were of Budapest — Buda Castle, the Chain Bridge, the Danube at dusk — and those were the strangest, because where had he bought them? Had he stood in a shop in a city he'd fled, holding a picture of the house he'd lost, and paid for it, and brought it home, and written on it, and not sent it? The stamp side of those cards had been left blank. He'd sealed them anyway. Sealed them and put them in a box in his daughter's attic, which is either the most private act in the world or the least, and I still haven't decided which.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only inheritance I've ever trusted: my first feeling was not wonder. It was resentment. Forty years of my childhood had been governed by silence about exactly this man, this country, this name, and here was the silence made physical — a box of it, string-tied, dust-furred, complete. My grandfather had not been silent. My grandfather had been writing constantly, yearly, obsessively, to a woman named Erzsi, and the silence in our house had not been the absence of words but their redirection. All that language, flowing year after year into a box instead of into the air. We hadn't been raised without a past. We'd been raised inside its mailroom, sorting nothing.

My mother had known. That was the thing I kept circling. She had kept this box in her attic for fifty years, through two moves, through my father's death, through every spring cleaning that had thrown out my school reports and Daniel's rugby trophies. She had thrown away everything except this. You do not keep what you don't love, and you do not keep, sealed, what you don't fear.

* * *

By the time I reached the last packet the light through the vent had gone from blade to ember, and my knees had gone from ache to numbness, and I had stopped feeling like a son clearing his mother's house and started feeling like an archaeologist of a country that existed only in one box.

The last card was different. The others were picture postcards, mass-produced views, the world reduced to souvenir. This one was plain, cheap stock, yellowed, the kind sold in packs of a hundred. No image. And it wasn't sealed with the others. It lay loose at the bottom of the box, under everything, face down, as if it had been dropped there last, or hidden, or left for whoever dug deepest.

The date on it was three weeks before my grandfather died. I was nine that year. I have a memory — or I have a story I've told so often it has the texture of memory — of him sitting in the garden that summer, humming something, a five-note phrase that rose and fell like someone climbing stairs and thinking better of it. I hummed it to myself in the attic without deciding to. My hands, resting on the box, shaped the phrase in the air, small unconscious chords. Forty years since I'd played seriously, and my hands still thought in Bartók.

I turned the card over.

The handwriting was different from the rest. Not looser — *slower*. Each letter placed like a stone in a wall. And it was a single sentence, one line running the width of the card, and it was not English, and it was not — I knew this even without knowing Hungarian, the way you know a face isn't quite the face you remember — it was not quite Hungarian either. The words had Hungarian bones but the grammar bent somewhere in the middle, the endings not where a Hungarian would have put them, as if the sentence had been written by a man translating himself in real time, caught between two languages and choosing neither.

I could read one word in it. One.

*Ház*. House.

I sat in the failing light with that word in my lap and the splinter in my chest turned and turned. A house. Of course a house. Every exile carries one address in the place they left, and my grandfather had carried his for forty years and written it down at the end in a language that belonged to neither of his countries, addressed to a woman who might already have been dead, and hidden it under a box of unsent love letters in his daughter's attic in a house in Croydon.

The condolence postcard from Ilse was in my coat, downstairs, still face down, still unturned. Two cards. One from the dead and one from the living, and I understood, sitting there in the dust, that they were the same card. That Ilse had not appeared at my mother's funeral by accident. That the name Vaszary had not been spoken into our family's silence by a stranger, but by a messenger, and that messengers only come when the message has somewhere to land.

The bulb's pull chain swayed in a draft from the vent. The five-note phrase was still going in my head. Somewhere below me the house ticked and settled, full of radiators my mother had never let anyone service because she said the noise was company.

I put the last card in my shirt pocket, over the heart, which is where you carry things you haven't decided to believe yet. Then I re-tied the packets — I couldn't bring myself to break the strings of the rest — and I carried the box down the ladder one-handed, which was stupid, and which I did anyway, because some things you do not leave in attics, and some things you do not leave alone with yourself.

In the kitchen I set the box on the table next to Ilse's postcard. Two envelopes' worth of a man's whole life. I made tea I didn't drink. I turned on the radio for the noise and turned it off because the noise was wrong.

Then I picked up Ilse's card, and before I could stop myself — before the forty-six years of training could kick in, before the rules could clear their throats — I turned it over.

There was no message. Just an address, in ink gone brown: a tea shop, eleven streets from the house I grew up in, on the route of the 47 bus.

And beneath the address, in handwriting I did not know but recognized instantly, because it bent the same way my grandfather's had bent, caught between two languages, choosing neither — a single word.

*Erzsi?*

With a question mark. As if she were asking whether the name still worked. As if she were asking whether anyone was left to answer to it.

I put on my coat.
