## 19. What Rafael Played

The tuxedo still fit. That was the first insult of the evening.

I'd found it in the attic two days after Budapest, hanging in its garment bag like a witness in protective custody, and I had stood there holding it at arm's length the way you hold a snake you're not sure is dead. Forty years since I'd worn it — I was seventeen the last time, playing Rachmaninoff for a benefit dinner where a duchess or someone adjacent to a duchess had called my hands "a national treasure," and my father had beamed from the third row with the particular pride of a man watching his investment appreciate. The bag smelled of cedar and dust and, faintly, of 1983.

It fit. Not well — the shoulders were a lie told by memory — but it closed, and the mirror in my mother's hallway confirmed that a man stood there who could plausibly be mistaken for someone who had once mattered.

"You could just say no," Daniel said on the phone. "You know that, right? Saying no is a thing people do."

"I already said yes."

"To the man who fired you."

"He didn't fire me. He never knew he employed me. That's different."

"Rafael." My brother has a way of saying my name that makes it sound like evidence in an ongoing trial. "The director of the conservatory invited 'the famous Rafael Pemberton' to play at the gala. You are currently the night janitor. When he figures out those are the same person, one of two things happens: either it's a beautiful story about redemption, or it's the most humiliating evening of your life, and given your track record, I'd bet on door number two."

"Thank you for your support."

"I'm not supporting you. I'm narrating you. There's a difference." A pause, and then, quieter, the way he gets when he's about to say something true and hates himself for it: "What are you going to play?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question.

Because the program said *Rafael Pemberton*, and the program meant the showpiece — the Prokofiev Toccata, the one I'd made famous at nineteen, the one with the fourth-octave repeated notes that audiences counted like gunshots, the piece that had been printed on the poster of me that used to hang in Hall B until they took it down, presumably when I stopped being famous and started being furniture. The director would expect the Toccata. The audience would expect the Toccata. The ghost of the boy I'd been was standing in Hall B right now, probably, leaning against the Steinway with his arms crossed, expecting the Toccata.

"I don't know yet," I said.

"Liar."

"I'm considering my options."

"You're going to play something small," Daniel said. "I can hear it in your voice. You're going to play something small and strange and nobody will clap properly, and you'll call it integrity, and I'll have to hear about it for a decade."

My brother knows me the way you know a house you grew up in — badly, completely, and without ever being able to leave.

* * *

The gala was everything galas are: too much light, too little music, champagne in glasses designed by someone who hated thumbs. The conservatory had rented out the atrium, hung banners celebrating fifty years of excellence, and arranged the seating so that donors faced the stage and students faced the donors, which told you everything about what the institution believed it was producing.

I arrived early because I wanted to see Hall B before the crowd did. Old habit. Before every performance I used to walk the hall alone, touch the back of the piano lid, say nothing to anyone. Superstition dressed up as preparation.

The hall was empty except for a custodial cart parked by the fire exit — my cart, actually, left over from Tuesday, which meant somewhere in this building my mop bucket and I were having parallel careers. The Steinway sat under the stage lights with its lid open like a mouth mid-sentence. I walked down the aisle and put my hand on the lid, the way I used to, and waited for the old feeling to arrive.

It didn't. What arrived instead was Budapest.

The upright piano in the young mother's front room, the one her daughter practiced scales on, slightly out of tune in the upper register, the C above middle C ringing a half-beat long like a question mark. The girl had played her scales with the grim competence of a child doing homework, and then, thinking no one was listening, she'd drifted into something else — four bars of a folk melody, wrong notes and all, played the way you hum when you think you're alone. Her mother had shushed her. I had almost cried into my coffee, which would have been difficult to explain to a stranger whose house my grandfather may or may not have owned in some grammatical sense.

That was the last time I touched a piano before tonight. Nine days ago. In a stolen living room in Buda, on an instrument that belonged to someone else's childhood.

"You're on in twenty minutes," said a student volunteer, mistaking me for a guest, which was technically accurate and existentially hilarious. "There's green room coffee, but honestly it's terrible."

"I know," I said. "I made it for three years."

She laughed, assuming wit, and moved on, and I stood in the empty hall understanding that I had just told the truth to someone who couldn't hear it, which is more or less the story of my entire life compressed into a single exchange.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: it doesn't feel like fear anymore once you're old enough. It feels like logistics. At nine, before my debut in this same hall, I threw up twice and cried in the coat room. At nineteen, before the Toccata, I paced and drank water and imagined the audience naked, all the standard equipment. At fifty-eight, standing backstage at a gala where the director had introduced me — warmly, generously, with a hand on my shoulder and a look in his eyes that said he still hadn't quite placed me, or perhaps had placed me and decided the fiction was more useful than the fact — at fifty-eight, I felt only a strange administrative calm, as if fear had been handed off to a junior associate and I was merely cc'd.

He introduced me as "a pianist whose recordings many of us grew up with," which was true, and as "someone who understands what this institution means," which was also true, though not in the way he meant it. I understood exactly what the institution meant. I had mopped its meaning off the floor every night for six years.

I walked out. The applause was polite, the applause of people who will clap for anything between courses. I sat down at the Steinway. The bench was the right height. Some things the body keeps even when the soul has moved out.

And here is the moment I want to get exactly right, so bear with me.

My hands went to the opening position of the Toccata. Automatically. The way a dog goes to its bowl. The fourth-octave repeated notes, the machine-gun figure, the piece that had been my name for twenty years — my fingers found it before I decided anything, hovering there, ready, and the audience leaned in with the pleasant anticipation of people about to receive what they ordered.

And I thought: *this is the door.*

Not metaphorically. Well — metaphorically too, obviously, I'm a literary man, everything is a door if you squint. But I mean it concretely. The sentence. *I left you the door open because I could not walk through it myself.* Three translators, three readings, and in the end it wasn't any of them; it was the whole sentence, all at once, apology and accusation and deed braided together like the three strands of a rope, each one useless alone. My grandfather had stood outside a door his whole life — the house on Rózsadomb, yes, but also every other door: England, the new name, the table where Hungarian was forbidden, the postcards addressed but never mailed, forty years of letters to a woman named Erzsi that lived in an attic like birds that wouldn't fly. He left doors open everywhere he went. He just never went through them.

And I was sitting at a piano with my hands on a door I hadn't walked through in forty years, and the door was open — it had always been open, that was the terrible joke, the Toccata was still in my hands, the fame was one performance away, the boy in the poster was still waiting — and I understood, sitting there in the tuxedo that still fit, that walking through it would change nothing. That the door was open onto a room I had already lived in. That going through it again would not be courage. It would be tourism.

So I moved my hands.

Down an octave. Left. To a key signature with no sharps and no ambitions. And I began to play Bartók.

* * *

The lullaby. You won't find it in any catalogue under the name I know it by, because the name I know it by is my grandfather's humming. It's from the *For Children* pieces — the ones Bartók built out of folk songs, little machines for teaching small hands to grieve — and my grandfather used to hum it while shaving, while fixing the radiator, while standing at the window of the house on Marlowe Road watching weather come in off the common. He hummed it in a minor key that Bartók may or may not have intended, with a rhythmic lilt that belonged to no notation system on earth, and when I was small I asked him what it was called and he said, in English, carefully, "An old song," and then, so quietly I might have invented it, "*Népdal*," and my mother cleared her throat from the kitchen and the word died in the air between us.

One word. One Hungarian word, spoken aloud in that house in my entire childhood, and it was the name of a song.

I played it imperfectly. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only inheritance I've got that isn't disputed. My hands shook. Not the romantic tremor of the aging artist — the actual shake, adrenaline and age and the specific vibration of a man doing something he cannot take back. I fumbled the second phrase. I simplified the left hand where the writing got dense, dropped a voice entirely in the middle section, and in the final bars I slowed down past any tempo a teacher would sanction, dragging the melody out the way my grandfather dragged his hum out on Sunday evenings, reluctant to finish, finishing anyway.

Somewhere around bar twelve I stopped being afraid. I can't tell you why. Maybe because the piece was too small to fail at — you cannot butcher a lullaby, you can only sing it badly, and singing badly is still singing. Maybe because the shaking hands felt familiar, felt inherited, felt like the hands of a man who stood outside a house in 1974 and couldn't knock. Maybe because for the first time in my life I was not playing *at* an audience but *for* the dead — for my grandfather, who never heard me perform, who died with forty unsent postcards and one unfinished sentence; for Erzsi, whoever she fully was, who kept a drawer full of silence; for Ilse, who was sitting — I learned later — in the fourth row, having flown back from Budapest without telling me, because of course she did, we are the same animal, she and I, two people who show up unannounced at doors.

The audience listened the way audiences listen to small pieces: patiently, kindly, with their attention already drafting its exit. Nobody recognized me. I could see it in their faces — the polite search for a name, the flicker toward their programs, the shrug. *Rafael Pemberton.* A name attached to no memory. The man who made that Toccata famous was somebody they'd read about, maybe, or somebody their teachers mentioned, and this quiet, stumbling lullaby clearly wasn't him, so whoever this was, he was nobody, and they clapped for nobody the way you clap for nobody: briefly, warmly, and without consequence.

It was the best reception of my life.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor, the director found me. He was holding two glasses of champagne, which in my experience is the universal sign that a man wants to discuss something delicate.

"That was — unexpected," he said.

"Yes."

"Not the Prokofiev."

"No."

"A bold choice." He said it the way you'd say *an unusual medical result*. "The Toccata, of course, is what everyone remembers you for. The recording. The—" he gestured vaguely at the air where my career used to be. "But there's real maturity in programming against expectation. Real maturity." He handed me the champagne. "We'd love to have you back. Formally. A recital, perhaps. Next season. We could build an evening around—"

"Thank you," I said, "but I'm busy."

"Busy?" He looked at my tuxedo, which fit, and my hands, which were steady now, and whatever he saw confused him enough that he didn't pursue it. People rarely do. The truth is too administratively inconvenient.

I found Ilse by the coat check. She was wearing the same grey coat she'd worn to my mother's funeral, and she was holding the condolence book — no, not the condolence book, a postcard, one of mine, the one I'd mailed her from Budapest three days ago, the first sentence I've ever written in Hungarian, misspelled, I'm sure, my tutor wincing sympathetically as I composed it.

"You played the song," she said.

"You came."

"I always come. That's my entire personality." She turned the postcard over in her hands. "Your Hungarian is terrible."

"I know."

"It says almost what you want it to say."

"Almost is the family language," I said. "We're fluent."

She smiled, and it was the smile of someone who has spent a year translating a dead man's sentence and has finally, grudgingly, accepted that translation is not arrival — it's travel, permanent, and the best you can do is keep moving and pack lightly.

Daniel was waiting by the doors. He'd come. He didn't clap during the lullaby — I checked, of course I checked, brothers audit each other — but he stayed for the whole thing, arms crossed, jaw set, and afterward he didn't say *that was beautiful* or *what was that* or any of the things a normal brother might say. He said:

"The 47 runs for the last time Saturday. Final service, end of the line, 11:40 p.m."

I looked at him.

"They're giving the old buses to a museum," he said. "There's a farewell ride. Full route. Both of us, or neither." He shrugged, aggressively casual, a man performing indifference at a level of effort that betrayed him completely. "No pressure."

Saturday. The last bus to the neighborhood where our father pointed out windows and said *that was ours, that was never ours*, where Hungarian went to die in a kitchen, where two boys were raised on the strict diet of an erased name. Daniel, who burned nothing and threw away nothing, who kept his father's naturalization papers in a shoebox like relics of a saint he was furious at. Offering to ride the route one last time. With me. Saying the words aloud, maybe, the ones nobody used — Vaszary, népdal, Rózsadomb — in public, on a bus, where anyone could hear.

"Both of us," I said.

He nodded once, turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the lullaby was better than the Toccata ever was."

"That's a lie."

"Obviously," he said, and pushed open the door, and held it — held it open, I noticed, the way you hold a door for someone coming behind you — and walked out into the cold.
