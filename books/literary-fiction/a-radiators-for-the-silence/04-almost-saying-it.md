## 4. Almost Saying It

The auction house had been a bank once, and it still wore its old dignity like a coat that no longer fit. Marble steps worn to a soft gloss in the middle, columns flanking the entrance, brass doors that someone had polished faithfully for eighty years and then stopped caring about sometime in the nineties. A banner hung above the door now, vinyl and sun-faded: **ESTATE & SURPLUS AUCTION — MARIGOLD HOUSE — PREVIEW HOURS 10–4.**

Jonah stood across the street with his mop bucket still in the trunk of his car, as if he'd driven here straight from work without deciding to. Maybe he had. The morning had gone strange on him — he'd cleaned practice room four twice, unable to leave it, listening to some kid butcher a Chopin étude through the door while his hands rested on the wet handle and his mind was already three neighborhoods east, at a boarded-up house with a piano in the parlor.

He crossed against the light because waiting felt impossible.

Inside, the preview room smelled of dust and lemon polish and other people's lives laid out on folding tables. A dollhouse with its roof pried open. Kitchen chairs stacked like kindling. A wall of framed photographs, most of them face-down because the auctioneer's assistant hadn't bothered to turn them over — but Jonah could see the backs of them, the curl of old tape, and he knew that somewhere in that stack there were pictures of him at nine years old, gap-toothed, holding a trophy he didn't remember winning.

He kept his hands in his pockets. That was the rule he'd made for himself in places like this: hands in pockets, eyes moving, nothing touched. A man who touched things wanted things, and wanting things was how you got hurt.

The piano wasn't here yet. It would be moved to the block tomorrow morning, hauled out of the parlor where it had sat for forty years and put under lights so strangers could walk around it and tap its rim and say *solid bones on this one* in voices that carried no memory at all. Tonight it was still in the house, alone in the dark, holding whatever it held.

"Small world," said a voice behind him, "or maybe just a small town pretending to be a big one."

Jonah turned, and there was Dev.

Twenty years should have made him a stranger. Instead it made him a stranger wearing a familiar face — broader through the shoulders, gray coming in early at the temples, a scar over one eyebrow Jonah didn't remember giving him time to earn. But the way he stood, weight on his heels, arms loose like a man perpetually about to catch something falling — that was unchanged. That was eleven years old on the back porch, teaching Jonah chords by pressing his fingers down onto the keys one at a time, patient as water.

"You look good," Dev said, which was a lie delivered kindly, or maybe not a lie, maybe just the cheapest available sentence between two men who had nothing else ready.

"So do you." Jonah's voice came out rusted. He cleared his throat. "You're— you're here for the preview?"

"That's generally what people do at preview hours." Dev smiled, and it was the old smile, crooked, guarded, doing more work than it showed. He nodded at the tables. "They've got Mrs. Okonkwo's rolling pin. Can you believe that? Somebody's going to buy her rolling pin for two dollars."

"Somebody'll get a good pin."

"She'd have charged them five."

And just like that they were laughing — short, startled, guilty laughter, the kind that dies fast when both parties remember simultaneously why they haven't spoken in twenty years. The silence that followed had weight and texture. Jonah studied a lampshade on the nearest table as if it might contain instructions.

"You want coffee?" Dev asked. "There's a machine. It's terrible."

"I'm okay."

"Yeah." Dev put his hands in his pockets too, mirroring him, and for a moment they stood like reflections of each other across twenty years of nothing. "I heard you were back in town. Somebody mentioned it."

*Somebody.* Jonah felt the word go through him like a splinter working toward the bone. "Oh yeah? Who?"

"You know how it is. People talk." Dev picked up a brass candlestick, weighed it, set it down with exaggerated care, as if handling evidence. "I hear you're at the conservatory these days."

The floor did not open up. The lights did not flicker. But something in Jonah's chest folded closed, quietly, like a hand smoothing a page.

"I do maintenance there," he said. "Custodial. Nights mostly."

"I know."

Two words. Flat, unadorned, and absolutely devastating in their calm. Jonah watched Dev set down another object — a picture frame this time, face-up, showing strangers at a picnic neither of them attended — and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, that *I know* meant more than it said.

"How long have you known?" he asked.

Dev considered the frame. "Awhile."

"Awhile."

"Four years. Maybe five." He shrugged, and the shrug was the cruelest thing yet, casual as weather. "You used to take your lunch on the loading dock behind the rehearsal hall. Thursdays you'd get the soup from the cart on Ninth. You keep your car in the structure on Delancey, third level, because it's cheaper and you don't mind the walk." He finally looked up, and his eyes were steady and terrible. "You wear your wedding ring on a chain now instead of your finger. I noticed that about two years ago."

Jonah's mouth was dry. "That's—"

"Stalking?" Dev offered. "It's not stalking. Stalking's when you follow somebody. I never followed you. I just... knew where you were. There's a difference. I checked."

"There isn't a difference."

"No," Dev agreed softly. "Maybe there isn't."

Around them the preview room went on with its small commerce. An elderly couple argued gently over a sewing machine. The auctioneer's assistant dragged a table six inches left. Somewhere a phone rang and rang and gave up. Jonah became aware of his own heartbeat, loud and stupid, and of the fact that his hands had come out of his pockets without his permission and were hanging at his sides like they belonged to a younger man, a boy, someone who once stood in a parlor doorway watching his foster brother lift the lid of an upright piano and say, *you press these, that's all it is, anybody can do it.*

"Why?" Jonah managed. "Why watch me?"

Dev set the frame down. For a moment his composure slipped and Jonah saw underneath it — not anger, exactly, though anger was in there, but something older and more tired, something that had been carried so long it had worn grooves into the man carrying it.

"Because you left," Dev said, "and I spent about ten years trying to figure out if you left *me*, specifically, or if you just left. And I figured the only way to know was to know where you were. What you did. Whether you ever—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Whether you ever thought about coming back. And you never came back, Jonah. Twenty years. You lived forty minutes away and you never once came back to the house, never called, never wrote, and I needed to understand how a person does that. So I watched. And what I learned—" he laughed, short and airless, "—what I learned is that you clean floors at the place that made you famous, and you eat soup on Thursdays, and you don't play. Not even in the practice rooms when nobody's there. Especially not then. And I still don't understand it."

The terror of being known was not what Jonah had expected. He had expected shame; shame he could carry, shame was an old coat. What he felt instead was exposure — the vertigo of realizing that his careful, invisible life had been read like large print by someone standing patiently in the margins for years. Every Thursday soup. Every quiet night. Dev had assembled him from fragments, the way you assemble a bird from feathers found one at a time on different lawns, and the bird he'd built was accurate enough to break your heart.

"I had my reasons," Jonah said, and hated the sentence even as it left him, because it was the sentence of every coward in every century.

"I'm sure you did." Dev looked at the face-down photographs on the wall. "You know what's funny? I practiced this. Coming here today. Had a whole speech in the car. And now I'm standing here talking about rolling pins."

"The speech any good?"

"It had a part where you apologized."

Jonah almost laughed. Almost. "Mine had a part like that too."

They regarded each other. Between them lay everything unsaid, arranged like the furniture on the folding tables — catalogued, priced, waiting for someone to carry it out.

"Tomorrow," Dev said finally. "They sell the piano tomorrow. Lot forty-one. I checked." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone to his jacket pocket, and Jonah saw the motion and pretended not to. Something in that pocket mattered. Something Dev had come all this way for.

"You want it," Jonah said. Not a question.

"I want what's fair." Dev's jaw worked. "That's all I ever wanted. Fair."

The words landed strangely, and Jonah felt again the shape of something hidden — the same feeling he'd had at the house last week, standing in the parlor, sensing that the piano was not merely furniture but a vessel, that it had been keeping something all these years the way it kept sound, in its ribs, under its lid.

"Look," Dev said, and now his voice dropped, went practical, went flat and quick like a man crossing dangerous ground at a run. "I'm staying at the Starlight Motel out past the freight yards. Room twelve. If you—if anything comes up, if you remember anything about the house, or the piano, or—" he faltered, the first crack in him, "—or if you just want terrible coffee from a worse machine, that's where I am."

He handed over a card. Plain white, typed, a name and number. No title, no business. Just *D. Vasquez-Reyes* — and Jonah stared at the hyphenated name for a beat too long, because he remembered when it was just D., just Dev, just a kid with no surname anyone bothered to use correctly.

"I'll think about it," Jonah said.

"Sure." Dev backed away a step, then another, the way you leave a room where someone is sleeping. At the door he paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the brass and glass. "Thursday soup," he said. "I always liked knowing that about you. Don't know why."

Then he was gone, and the preview room resumed its murmur, and Jonah stood among the dead household of Marigold House with a card in his hand and his heart going like a metronome set far too fast.

* * *

He didn't go home. He drove toward the old neighborhood without deciding to, the way he'd come to the auction without deciding to, and parked two blocks from Marigold House with the engine ticking and the card lying on the passenger seat like a live thing.

Room twelve. The Starlight Motel. Four years of being watched, and Dev had given him an address inside of five minutes, handed it over like it cost nothing — which meant it cost everything, which meant the man was either desperate or done hiding, and Jonah suspected both.

Through the windshield, Marigold House sat in the late afternoon light with its boards nailed across the windows, and Jonah thought about lot forty-one, about the piano alone in the dark parlor, and about what Dev's hand had done when it went to his jacket pocket — the quick, protective gesture of a man guarding something small and important.

Letters, he thought suddenly. Or money. Or both. Mrs. Okonkwo had hidden things her whole life — cash in flour canisters, documents taped under drawers — and she had loved that piano more than she loved any object in the house except, perhaps, the boys themselves.

Whatever was inside it, Dev knew. And tomorrow it would belong to whoever paid the most.

Jonah picked up the card, put it down, picked it up again. Then he started the car and drove, not home, not to the motel, but toward Ninth Street, toward the soup cart, toward the small rituals of a life that had just stopped being invisible — and realized only at the red light that he was rehearsing, under his breath, the first four bars of a piece he hadn't played since he was nineteen.
