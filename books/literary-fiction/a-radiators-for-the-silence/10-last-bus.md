## 10. Last Bus

The bus came at 5:47, and it came empty.

Jonah saw it first from the bench outside the diner, its headlights swimming up through the blue-gray fog that the river left behind every morning, and he felt something in his chest loosen and tighten at once — the way a fist does around a coin it has carried too long. The 14. The last run. In twelve hours the route would be struck from the city's maps, folded into some consolidated line that would never again turn down Marigold Street, and this morning was all that remained of it: one driver, one bus, one final pass through a neighborhood that had already forgotten it needed saving.

Dev stood beside him with his hands in his jacket pockets, his breath making small ghosts in the cold. He hadn't slept either. Jonah could see it in the bruised skin under his eyes, in the way he kept shifting his weight like a man standing on a dock that might pull away.

Neither of them spoke about the night before. There was no need. The words were still in the air between them, spent, settled into the pavement like rain — *I left to survive. I left you. Both things are true.* Dev had listened without interrupting, which was more than Jonah had dared hope for, and when it was done he had not forgiven, exactly. He had nodded. He had said, *Okay,* in a voice that meant the opposite of okay and also, somehow, the beginning of it.

The bus sighed to a stop in front of them. Its destination sign read MARIGOLD / TERMINUS in letters that flickered, the G dying and reviving, dying and reviving.

"After you," Dev said.

"No," Jonah said. "Together."

They climbed the steps side by side, two grown men paying their fares in exact change because neither of them wanted to break the silence with conversation, even the transactional kind. The driver — a heavyset woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray curls — glanced at them with the mild curiosity of someone who had driven this route for thirty years and seen every species of early-morning passenger: nurses coming off shift, bakers going on, drunks going home, lovers going somewhere else. She didn't ask why two men in rumpled clothes were boarding at dawn after an auction that had emptied them both.

She just said, "Back's free," and pulled out into the empty street.

* * *

They sat in the back seats because that was where boys sat. That was the law of buses, older than any written rule: the front belonged to adults with errands, but the back belonged to anyone young enough or reckless enough to believe the ride itself was the destination. Jonah slid into the window seat and felt the vinyl, cracked and re-taped, give beneath him, and twenty years fell off his body like a coat.

Dev dropped in beside him. Their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away.

Outside the glass, the city unspooled in reverse. They passed the shuttered laundromat where Mrs. Okonkwo had sent them every Saturday with quarters rolled in paper sleeves, Dev's always short a few coins because he bought candy with the laundry money and lied about it badly. They passed the church whose basement had hosted the Christmas recital where Jonah, nine years old, had played "Ode to Joy" with both hands and received a plastic trophy that Mrs. Okonkwo kept on the kitchen windowsill for eleven years, dusting it weekly, as if it were made of something holier than injection-molded gold.

"You remember the trophy," Dev said. It wasn't a question.

"I remember you knocked it off the sill playing indoor soccer."

"And you cried."

"I did not cry."

"Jonah." Dev turned to look at him, and there — for the first time since the auction house, since the accusation, since the dealer's men had wheeled the piano out the loading door while strangers wrote numbers on clipboards — there was the ghost of a smile. It was a tired thing, crooked, missing nothing of the years between them. "You cried like the world ended. I gave you my pudding cup to make it stop."

"It was butterscotch."

"It was butterscotch," Dev agreed solemnly. "Top-shelf stuff."

Jonah laughed. It came out of him rough and startled, a sound he hadn't made in this register in years, and it echoed in the empty bus and seemed to surprise even the driver, who glanced up into her mirror and smiled briefly at whatever she saw there.

The bus turned onto Marigold Street.

* * *

It slowed, as if the route itself remembered. The house stood where it had always stood, but wrong now — the porch boards gone gray, plywood nailed over the windows, the auction sticker peeling from the door like a scab half-healed. Someone had spray-painted a tag across the garage door, bright and meaningless. The maple tree in the yard, the one they'd climbed until Mrs. Okonkwo threatened them with the wooden spoon, had been cut down; only the stump remained, wearing a collar of sawdust.

But the kitchen light — no. There was no kitchen light. Jonah had imagined it, that was all. Twenty years of imagining could do that. It could put light in windows that had been dark for decades.

"This is where I got off," Dev said quietly. "Every day. Right here. And you'd be at the window."

"The parlor window."

"Watching for me."

"I wasn't watching for you."

"You were absolutely watching for me." Dev said it without heat, without the old accusation. Just the fact of it, laid down gently, like a card turned face-up on a table. "You always watched for me. Even after. Even when we weren't talking. You'd be at that window, and I'd see you, and I'd walk slower so you'd have to keep looking."

Jonah stared at the stump of the maple. His throat worked. "Why?"

"Because being looked for is almost the same as being loved." Dev shrugged, eyes still on the house. "Nobody ever told me that. I figured it out myself. Took me about fifteen years and a lot of bad apartments."

The bus pulled away from the curb. Marigold House slid backward out of the frame, taking its boarded windows and its empty parlor and its sold piano with it, and Jonah did not watch it go. He watched Dev instead, the profile of him against the passing streetlights, and understood that some things are not lost but converted — house into memory, memory into word, word into the man sitting next to you who stayed on the bus.

* * *

The terminus was a loop of cracked asphalt behind a strip mall, ringed by a chain-link fence and a single sodium lamp that buzzed like a trapped insect. The bus idled there for eight minutes before turning around — everyone who rode the 14 knew this, the way they knew weather. Eight minutes of nowhere, at the end of the line.

There was a musician there.

He was maybe sixty, set up under the overhang of the shuttered pharmacy with a portable keyboard on a folding stand, powered by a car battery, playing to no one. He wore fingerless gloves and a wool cap, and what he played was slow and bluesy and slightly out of tune in the upper octave, and he played it the way men play when the playing is the whole point — not for coins, not for audience. For the hour. For the fact of being awake when the world isn't.

Dev saw him first. Nudged Jonah with an elbow, the same elbow that had jabbed him through a thousand childhood arguments, and said nothing, because he didn't have to.

Jonah's heart was doing something complicated. He stood. His legs felt borrowed.

"Hey," he said to the musician. "Mind if I —"

The old man looked up, took in the rumpled suit, the sleepless face, the other man lingering by the bus, and simply lifted his hands off the keys. "All yours," he said. "Battery's got maybe ten minutes left in her."

Ten minutes. Jonah almost laughed. Twenty years of silence, and the universe was offering him ten minutes of dying battery on a keyboard with three dead keys in the middle register.

He sat down. His hands hovered.

And then — because Priya had begged him, because Dev had taught him chords on a parlor upright with a missing ivory, because Mrs. Okonkwo had said *music is the only furniture you get to take with you*, because there was no reason left in the world to keep it locked — he began to play.

The unfinished piece. The one he'd started at eleven and abandoned at nineteen along with everything else. It came out of his hands whole, or whole enough — the opening figure rising like something climbing out of water, the middle section that Dev had always said sounded like rain on a tin roof, the place where it stopped, where it had always stopped, the cliff edge where boyhood ended.

Except this time he didn't stop.

He didn't know the notes that came next. He had never known them. But his hands found something anyway — a resolution, simple and plain, four bars that a conservatory examiner would have called naive, that Priya would have called honest, that Dev, standing three feet away with his fists in his pockets and his jaw tight and his eyes shining in the sodium light, would have called, later, on the phone, from whatever city he landed in, *the ending*.

The battery died mid-sustain. The note faded upward into the buzzing lamp and the gray morning, and was gone.

Silence. Then the old musician clapped twice, softly, the way you applaud in a church. "Been waiting twenty years for somebody to play that thing like it mattered," he said, and began packing up his stand.

* * *

The bus driver leaned on her horn once — the eight minutes were up, the loop complete, the last run of the 14 beginning its final return to the depot. Dev and Jonah walked toward it slowly, unhurried, two men who had learned overnight that deadlines are mostly fiction.

"So," Dev said at the bus door. "I fly back Thursday."

"Yeah."

"But I'm thinking —" He stopped. Started over, the way he used to when the words wouldn't come, when he was ten and angry and couldn't say *stay*. "I'm thinking I'll call. Not text. Call. Like a person."

"Like a person," Jonah agreed.

"And next time there's an auction, or a house, or a — anything — you tell me before the flyer does. Deal?"

Jonah looked at him. His foster brother. His witness. The boy at the window, the man on the bus. Found family, he thought, isn't the family you find. It's the family you refuse to lose twice.

"Deal," he said.

They boarded together. Back seats. The bus pulled out of the terminus into the full arrival of morning, the sun finally clearing the rooftops and laying a long gold stripe down the aisle between them, and Jonah closed his eyes and let his head rest against the vibrating window, and somewhere behind his ribs, faint but unmistakable, the piece went on playing — past the ending now, into bars no one had written yet.

The driver glanced in her mirror at the two men in the back, sleeping or pretending to, shoulder to shoulder like brothers, and turned the bus toward home.
