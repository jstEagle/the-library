## 1. The Pitch of Rooms

My mother's funeral was the first time in five years that everyone in my family was in the same room, and I spent most of it with my hands inside a piano.

I'd like to tell you this was an accident. That the vicar asked, or that grief drove me to it like a fever. But the truth is I volunteered before anyone thought to offer me a chair. The church piano at St. Enodoc's had been complaining for months, according to my mother, who complained about it in the way she complained about most things: briefly, precisely, and without ever once saying what she actually wanted done about it. So there I was, an hour before the service, lifting the lid and going through it key by key, because grief is unbearable and middle C is not.

People think piano tuning is about music. It isn't, mostly. It's about listening to the space between two notes and deciding which one to trust. Strike the key, listen to the beat — that slow wobble where two strings disagree — and nudge one of them until the wobble dies. Every room has its own opinion about pitch. A piano tuned in a warm drawing room will go sour in a cold church, because the wood drinks the damp and swells, and everything you thought was settled comes loose. I have spent twenty years learning that nothing stays in tune. You don't fix a piano. You negotiate with it, repeatedly, until you both die.

My family, you will be surprised to learn, is not a piano. My family is a room where nobody has struck a key in five years.

I tuned through the prelude of the service, which is to say I tuned through the arrival of everyone I had spent half a lifetime avoiding. I heard the door open and close behind me, heard the particular hush of relatives deciding whether to approach a woman kneeling inside an instrument. My father arrived first — I knew his tread, the deliberate, careful walk of a man who has spent decades not startling anyone — and he stood beside the piano for a moment without speaking, which is how we have always spoken to each other. Then he went to sit in the front row, in the seat that had been my mother's, which was either a gesture of devotion or of territorial claim, and with my father it was never possible to be sure.

My aunt Margaret came next, smelling of lilies and disapproval in equal measure. Then cousins, then the neighbors from the village, the ones who had known my mother for forty years and would tell me afterward, each of them individually, that she was *a wonderful woman, so private, you know, kept herself to herself*, as though privacy were a virtue rather than a load-bearing wall.

I kept my head down. I struck A above middle C and listened to it fight with itself.

Here is what I want you to understand about my mother, Vera Pemberton, née — well. That's rather the point of all this, isn't it. Here is what I understood about my mother, for fifty-one of my forty-three years: that she was from Bristol originally, that she met my father at a dance in 1971, that she was a careful woman with excellent posture and a horror of waste, that she could make a chicken last three meals and a grievance last a decade. That she had one sister, Margaret, who was actually my father's sister, which meant my mother had no siblings at all, a fact I never once thought to question because why would you? Why would anyone interrogate the shape of a family that had never given them cause to?

I'll tell you why. Because of the radiators.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and a funeral is no place to get ahead of yourself. A funeral is a place to perform. And I was excellent at performing. I had been rehearsing the role of Quiet Competent Daughter since Daniel left — since the night in 1989 that we did not discuss, in the house we did not sell, in the family that had learned to walk around one room of itself the way you learn to walk around a sagging floorboard, automatically, without looking down, until the avoidance becomes so practiced it looks like grace.

Daniel. My brother. Who was not dead, as far as I knew, and not alive, as far as I knew, and whose absence had a texture my mother and father and I had spent thirty-four years polishing. He was the silence at the center of us. He was the note we'd all agreed, without ever agreeing, to stop striking.

I tuned the piano through the whole service. I tuned it during the vicar's remarks, during the reading of Psalm 121, during my father's eulogy, which he delivered in the voice of a man reading someone else's shopping list — *she was fond of her garden, she made a good lemon cake, she never complained* — and I want to note, for the record, that *she never complained* was the most dishonest sentence spoken in that church, because my mother complained constantly, beautifully, about radiators and vicars and the price of coal, and never once about the thing that mattered, and I learned that trick from her the way you learn a language: without noticing, until one day you open your mouth and it comes out fluent.

I tuned it during the hymn, too, though I played nothing. The organist handled the hymns. I was there as a courtesy, as a prop, as the daughter who fixes things. When the coffin went past I set down my tuning lever and stood, and my hands were steady, and I remember being quietly proud of that. Steady hands. That was the family inheritance. Whatever else we'd lost or hidden or failed to say, we could all be counted on to keep our hands still.

* * *

The wake was at the house in Cornwall, which is a sentence that requires more unpacking than I gave it at the time. The house was mine now, technically, along with my father, who had moved into a bungalow in Bodmin two years earlier when the stairs started winning. The house stood empty, unheated, its pipes drained for winter, and my aunt had declared it *the natural place* for people to gather, in the tone she used for declarations that were actually ambushes. So forty mourners stood in my mother's kitchen and her sitting room, holding tea in her cups, and I moved among them doing the thing I do: refilling, nodding, catching coats before they slid off chairs. Invisible competence. If you do it well enough, no one remembers you were there.

The radiators were cold. I noticed this the way you'd notice a missing tooth in a familiar smile. Cast iron, most of them, Victorian originals in the older rooms, and my mother had kept them — kept them *serviced*, kept them painted, kept a man in Bodmin on retainer for decades to bleed and balance and tend them as though they were livestock. In a house where she let the roof leak for ten years rather than pay a roofer, the radiators were immaculate. I had always filed this under *eccentricity*. I would come to file it elsewhere.

It was in the sitting room, beside the cold radiator with its flaking green paint, that the grey-eyed woman found me.

I saw her before she spoke, and I remember thinking: *I don't know you, and you don't belong here.* She was perhaps sixty, tall, with the kind of stillness that reads as either confidence or long practice at not being noticed — I recognized the second one, being fluent in it myself. Grey eyes, the pale grey of weathered zinc, and hair going silver at the temples in a way that suggested she had decided to let it. She held her tea like someone who had learned manners in a different country and kept them out of stubbornness.

"You must be Emrys," she said.

"And you must be lost," I said, and then, because grief makes you polite in the strangest places, "I'm sorry. Are you a friend of my mother's?"

Something crossed her face then — a flicker, quick as the beat between two mistuned strings — and she said, "I came to pay my respects. She was a remarkable woman." And then, extending her hand: "Ilsa Voss."

"Voss," I repeated. I shook her hand. It was warm. Everyone else's hands had been cold; the heating was off; the whole house was cold. Her hand was warm as a radiator.

"Voss," she agreed, and watched me.

I ran through the family registry the way you run through a hymnal looking for a page number. Pemberton, obviously. My mother's maiden name, which I had seen on her birth certificate exactly once, when settling her affairs — I'd had to dig the certificate out of a biscuit tin, of all places, and I remember being faintly annoyed that I had to look at all. *Vera Constance—* and here my memory did the thing it had apparently been trained to do for decades: it softened, blurred, slid off. I could not retrieve the name. I could not retrieve it, and the failure felt less like forgetting and more like a door that had been painted shut.

"I don't know that name," I said. "Voss. Are you — connected to the Cornish side? My father's people are from Falmouth."

"No," said Ilsa Voss, in the gentle voice you'd use with a bird you didn't want to startle. "I knew your mother before she was Mrs. Pemberton. Before she was Vera at all, perhaps." She set down her tea untasted. "I'm sorry to intrude on a day like this. I only wanted to say — she was loved. Whatever else. She was very much loved, and she loved very much more than she was ever allowed to show."

"Allowed," I said. "That's an odd word."

"Is it?" She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, kind and knowing and full of thirty years of something I had no word for yet. She moved toward the door, and then paused, and looked back at me with those zinc-grey eyes, and said:

"She kept our name too, you know. Alongside yours. All those years. Ask yourself what a woman has to be hiding, that she needs two names to carry it."

And she was gone, out into the churchyard grey, before I had assembled a single syllable of reply.

I stood there beside the cold radiator with my steady hands, and I felt it — the first hairline crack, fine as a fracture in a tuning pin, the kind you can't see but can hear, a new faint beat in every note. The room was full of people who had known my mother for forty years. The tea was in her cups. The cake was her recipe. And somewhere in Bristol, or wherever Bristol had actually been, there was apparently a name — *our name*, she'd said, *our* — that my mother had carried alongside mine like a second suitcase she never unpacked.

My aunt Margaret came in then, saying something about the sandwiches, and I turned to her with the question already forming, the whole sentence, *Aunt Margaret, who is Ilsa Voss and what did she mean* —

"Margaret," I said, "about that woman, the tall one, she said she knew Mum from—"

"You should see to the urns," my aunt said, not unkindly, in the voice of a woman closing a door that had been painted shut long before I was born.

And I did. Of course I did. I went to see to the urns, because that is what I was for, and because I had been trained too well, and because the sentence died in my mouth the way five years of sentences had died, halfway, always halfway.

But that night, alone in the cold house, with the mourners gone and my steady hands finally shaking, I stood in the dark hallway with my palm flat against a cold cast-iron radiator, and I listened to the silence of the pipes the way I'd listen to a piano before tuning it — and for the first time in my life, I heard the silence as a sound someone had made on purpose.

I wanted to know who had tuned my family, and why, and what they had been so afraid of hearing.

I would find out. God help me, I would find out.
