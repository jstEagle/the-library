## 6. Ilsa's Version

My mother lied the way other people hummed—constantly, tunelessly, without any apparent awareness that anyone could hear her.

I want to be clear about that before I tell you anything else, because I've noticed that when people talk about the dead they tend to sand off the inconvenient grain. Nell—my mother, Eleanor, Ellie, Nel, a woman who collected names the way other people collected spoons—was not a tragic figure. She was not a saint with a secret. She was a liar, a cheerful one, a liar with excellent handwriting and a gift for the specific detail, and I loved her the way you love weather, which is to say helplessly and without any expectation that it will explain itself.

She died in March, two years before Emrys Pemberton's mother died in October, though of course I didn't know then that the two deaths were connected, or that the woman I'd spent my childhood calling Aunt Vera would turn out to be the loose thread that, once pulled, would unravel the entire sweater of my life. I'm mixing metaphors. My mother would have hated that. She was a seamstress by trade and a perfectionist by temperament, and she believed that a sloppy metaphor was a moral failing. She also believed my father was a man named Gerald who worked in insurance in Bristol, and that I was born in 1978, and that she had one sister, dead of scarlet fever in childhood, and none of those things were true either. So perhaps we can forgive each other our metaphors.

* * *

Aunt Vera came every autumn.

Not in summer, when visitors are easy and expected. Not at Christmas, when even liars make appearances. Autumn—late September, usually, when the light in Bristol goes thin and gold and the chestnut trees on our street started dropping their conkers onto the pavement like small brown bombs. I could set my school calendar by her. Half-term was coming when Aunt Vera came.

She arrived by train, always, never by car, and she always brought gifts that were slightly wrong in a way I couldn't articulate as a child and can articulate all too well now. A music box that played a tune none of us recognized. A tin of shortbread from a bakery in a town I'd never heard of, in a county I'd never visited. Once, a child's wool coat, navy, beautifully made, in a size I would not grow into for another two years. "She'll grow," Aunt Vera said, and my mother laughed in a strange bright way and said, "She will, won't she," as though the coat had said something funny.

Aunt Vera was tall. That's the first thing. Tall and straight-backed, with dark hair going silver at the temples in a way she made look deliberate, like she'd chosen it from a catalogue. Her hands were remarkable—long, strong, with the calluses of someone who worked with them—and she had a habit, when she thought no one was looking, of pressing her palm flat against the radiators in our house. Any radiator. She'd walk past one and lay her hand on it like it was a horse's neck, checking its temperature, its pulse. My mother caught her doing it once and said, "Vera," in a voice I had never heard her use before or since—a warning voice, a voice with a fence in it—and Aunt Vera withdrew her hand and smiled and said, "Force of habit," and the two of them looked at each other for a moment too long, the way people do when an entire conversation has happened in the pause and neither of them wants to admit it.

I was seven the first time I noticed. I was twenty-three the last time I saw her. In between, I built a whole religion out of Aunt Vera, the way lonely children do. She was exotic. She was from Somewhere Else—Cornwall, my mother said, vaguely, the way you'd say *the sea* or *the moon*. She had a house with a name, not a number. She played the piano, though never when asked, only when she thought the room was empty; I caught her once, aged nine, sitting at our neighbour's upright through an open window, playing something slow and sad that I hummed for weeks afterward without knowing its name. She smelled of lavender and engine oil, which even at seven I understood to be a contradiction, and contradictions, in my experience, are where the truth keeps its coat.

She asked about me the way no other adult did. Not *how is school* but *what do you hear when the house is quiet*. Not *what do you want to be* but *what do you notice that other people don't*. I told her I could tell what mood my mother was in by which floorboards she avoided. Aunt Vera looked at me for a long time with her grey eyes—grey, like weather coming—and said, "You're hers, all right," and my mother, in the doorway, dropped an entire tray of biscuits.

I thought, for years, that the tray was an accident.

* * *

Here is what I believed, for most of my life, in the order I believed it:

One: Aunt Vera was my mother's cousin, or family friend, some loose category adults use when the true category is unspeakable or, more often, simply tedious to explain.

Two: Aunt Vera was my mother's sister—the real one, not the scarlet-fever ghost—estranged for reasons never given, reconciling in annual autumn increments.

Three, arriving later, in adolescence, with the terrible X-ray vision of a teenager: Aunt Vera was not my aunt at all. Aunt Vera was my mother.

I want to defend that third theory, because I held it for eleven years and it cost me two relationships and one very expensive course of therapy, and I'd like it to have been worth something. The evidence was good. We had the same hands. We had the same grey eyes, whereas my mother's were brown and my father's—Gerald's—were brown. We had the same habit of humming when we lied. When I was fifteen I found a photograph in my mother's sewing box, hidden under the pin cushions: a woman on a beach, tall, dark-haired, holding a baby, and the woman was Aunt Vera and the baby was wearing the coat, the navy coat, the one I'd grow into, and when I confronted my mother with it she went white as starch and said, "That's a cousin," and I said, "Which cousin," and she said, "A dead one," and I said, "Dead people don't hold babies," and she said, "Give it here," and I never saw the photograph again.

You can live a long time on evidence like that. You can build a cathedral.

The truth, when it finally arrived, was so much stranger than the cathedral that I almost felt cheated. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

Aunt Vera stopped coming the autumn I was twenty-three.

No warning. No letter. October came, the conkers dropped, and no tall woman with grey eyes arrived at our door smelling of lavender and engine oil. I asked my mother where she was. My mother said, "Who?" — actually said *who*, with a little frown, as if I'd asked after a character from a book—and I stood in our kitchen with the radiator ticking beside me and felt the floor of my life tilt by two degrees, which is all it takes.

"She kept our name too."

That's what the note said. The note that came with the brass valve, left at the shop of a piano tuner in a city I'd never been to, addressed to a woman I'd never heard of—Emrys Pemberton, a name like a spell from a language that died young. I found Emrys the way you find anything now, by typing fragments into a screen at three in the morning: *Pemberton piano tuner*. There she was. A photograph of a woman with her mother's—*our* mother's—brown eyes and an expression of someone listening to a frequency no one else can hear.

I should explain how I got the valve, and the note, and the name. My mother died in March, as I said, and I inherited her house and her sewing box and, in the sewing box, where the photograph used to be, a small brass radiator valve wrapped in a scrap of navy wool. The same navy wool as the coat. Under it, a card in her handwriting: *If anything happens to me, find the tuner's daughter. She'll know. She always knew more than she said.* Which is the sort of sentence that makes a person sell a flat in Bristol and drive to Cornwall, and I am not a person who does that sort of thing, I am a person with a mortgage and a sensible job in archives, and I did it anyway.

The tuner's daughter turned out to be the tuner. Emrys. I stood in her shop and watched her hold the valve like it was a bird with a broken wing, and I watched her not finish three separate sentences in the space of ten minutes, and I thought: *oh, you poor woman, you've been living in the same house I grew up in, the house where every sentence has a fence in it.* I left the valve and the note because I lost my nerve, which is a Pemberton family trait I apparently inherited along with the hands.

* * *

Here is what I know now, or think I know, and I offer it the way my mother offered her lies—specifically, confidently, and with no guarantee of accuracy.

My mother was not born Nell. My mother was born with another name, in another house, in Cornwall, in a family that had a piano that needed tuning and radiators that needed bleeding and a daughter who learned to read a room by its heat. My mother left that family—fled it, was sent from it, I don't yet know which—and built a new self in Bristol the way you'd build a wall: carefully, load-bearing brick by load-bearing brick, with a person named Gerald mortared in for stability. And once a year she went back. Not to the family. To the house, or the town, or the woman—Vera, if that was ever even her name, though I suspect Vera was as constructed as Nell, two women built by the same architect for different sites.

And there was a child in the other house. A child my mother wrote to for twenty years, letters I haven't read yet but know exist, because Emrys told me on the phone in a voice like someone walking on a frozen lake. Letters to someone called Nell. My mother signing letters to someone else with the name she'd stolen for herself, or been given, or grown into like a coat two sizes too big.

I don't know yet if Emrys's mother was my mother's sister. I don't know yet if she was more than that. I know that when I finally met Emrys—properly, in the kitchen of that cold house in Cornwall, with the boiler ticking like a clock in a room where a clock has no business being—she looked at me and I looked at her and we had the same hands.

Not similar hands. The same.

* * *

People ask me—well, no one asks me, I've told no one but Emrys and a therapist who charges £90 an hour to say "and how did that make you feel"—but if they asked, they'd want to know what it's like, hearing your mother described as a stranger. It's like this: it's like finding out the house you grew up in was a film set, that the kitchen was plywood, that behind the fridge there was scaffolding and a man with a clipboard. Everything still looks the same. You can still make tea in it. But you will never again lean against the counter without testing it first.

And yet.

Here is the part I didn't expect. When Emrys told me about her mother—Vera, or whatever her name was, the woman with the callused hands and the grey eyes and the habit of touching radiators like they were horses—when Emrys described her trailing off mid-sentence for five years, keeping the family's silences like a tuner keeps a pitch, I felt grief, yes, but underneath the grief, shamefully, I felt recognition. Not of the mother. Of the mourner. Emrys and I were standing on either side of the same locked door, and we had both spent our lives pressing our ears to it, and neither of us had ever heard the other one listening.

My mother lied the way other people hummed. And now I catch myself doing it. Last week my supervisor asked why I'd taken leave and I said "family things" and hummed, actually hummed, a small tuneless note, and heard it come out of my own mouth in my mother's cadence, and had to sit down in the archive stairwell for ten minutes.

The letters are in a tin behind the boiler. Emrys told me. She said she hasn't read the last one yet—the last envelope has an address on it, an address two counties away, in a hand that isn't my mother's, a hand that shakes.

She asked me if I wanted to be there when she opened it.

I said yes before she finished the sentence. Which, for a woman raised on fences, felt like the first whole thing I've ever said.
