## 1. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

There were eleven chairs occupied at the funeral of Marion Crane, and Gideon counted them the way they counted everything: twice, then once more for certainty. Eleven mourners, nine of whom had come for the free coffee in the parish hall, one of whom was their brother Deshawn, and one—a woman in a coat the color of wet slate—whom Gideon did not know at all.

The chapel smelled of lilies and old varnish, that particular sweetness churches cultivate like a second congregation. Rain worked at the windows in long, patient strokes. Gideon stood at the front with eleven index cards fanned in their left hand like a losing hand of poker, and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at funerals and 3 a.m., that they had been rehearsing this moment their whole life without knowing it.

They had always been the keeper of records. Not the archivist—that title implied authority, a salary, a door with their name on it—but the keeper, the one who remembered which cousin owed which apology, which aunt had died owing money to whom, which stories the family had agreed to stop telling and when exactly they had stopped telling them. Their mother had trained them for it early, though she would never have called it training. She'd simply made silences the way other mothers made dinners, and Gideon had learned to catalogue each one: *the silence about Grandma's maiden name, begun circa 1989; the silence about why we don't visit Aunt Ruthie anymore, ongoing; the silence about the photograph in the hall closet, face-down since before I could read.*

The eulogy was four minutes long. Gideon knew because they had timed it three times in the library stacks the night before, standing between the 900s and the oversized atlases where no one ever went after midnight. Four minutes was generous. Marion Crane had lived seventy-one years and had requested, in the note she left taped inside her medicine cabinet—Gideon found it Tuesday, along with everything else a mother leaves behind when she decides to stop—"keep it short, sweetheart, and don't cry where people can see."

So Gideon didn't. They recited.

"Marion Crane believed in clean windows," they said, reading from card three. "She believed a person could be measured by what they kept in order. She raised two children alone and never once told us it was hard."

All true. All insufficient. That was the craft of it, Gideon thought—the same craft they practiced nightly among the returned books and the quiet patrons who came in at odd hours to not be home. You told the truth in such small increments that no one noticed the shape of what you'd left out. Their mother had been a cathedral of things unsaid, and Gideon had spent forty years as its docent, pointing visitors gently toward the stained glass.

Deshawn sat in the second row with his jaw set, his good suit straining at the shoulders, staring at the casket as if it might open and apologize. He had hugged Gideon once outside, briefly, the way you handle something that might be broken. "You'll handle the paperwork," he'd said, which was not grief but assignment, and Gideon had accepted it because accepting assignments was what they were for.

Card seven. "She loved this city, even when it didn't love her back."

Outside, the rain kept its own eulogy going.

* * *

It was during card nine—the one about the garden, the tomatoes she grew too proudly every August despite the shade—that Gideon saw the woman in the slate-colored coat lean forward.

She sat at the end of the third row, apart, her hands folded on her knee. She was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples, with the kind of stillness Gideon associated with certain patrons: the ones who came to the library not for books but for the permission a public building grants to simply exist somewhere. But there was nothing passive in her attention. She watched Gideon the way an editor reads—not kindly, but closely.

And when Gideon reached the final card, the one that ended, *"We will remember her as she asked to be remembered,"* the woman rose.

Not dramatically. She simply stood, unhurried, while the organist reached for the first hymn, and she said—in a voice pitched not for the room but for the room's edges, for whoever was listening—

"Goodbye, Halloway."

The word moved through the chapel like a draft under a door.

Halloway.

Gideon felt it land somewhere below the ribs, in the place where they kept the catalogues. Because the name was wrong, and the name was right. It belonged to no one present—Crane was the family name, had been for as long as Gideon had been alive—and yet it fit the mourners like a key fits a lock it was cut for. Deshawn's head came up sharply. The widow Petersen from next door frowned at her program. And Gideon stood frozen at the lectern with their cards, feeling the entire archive inside them shudder on its shelves.

Because there was a silence about that word. Gideon had catalogued it decades ago and filed it deep: *the silence about the name we had before this one.* Their mother had gone white as paper once, years back, when a telemarketer misread the surname off some ancient form—*Halloway? Is this the Halloway residence?*—and hung up on him mid-sentence. Gideon had been fourteen. They had written it down that night in a notebook they kept under the mattress, spelling it phonetically, unsure even then whether they'd heard it correctly.

They had heard it correctly.

The woman gathered her coat, nodded once—to the casket, or to Gideon, impossible to say—and walked up the aisle and out into the rain without another word. The doors sighed shut behind her. The organist, mercifully oblivious, began the hymn.

* * *

Afterward, in the parish hall, Gideon performed the other role: receiver of casseroles, thanker of the half-hearted. Deshawn cornered them by the coffee urn.

"Who was that," he said. Not a question. Deshawn's questions never were.

"I don't know."

"You know everything." He said it like an accusation, which it was, and a compliment, which he didn't mean it to be. "You keep track of everything. Don't tell me you don't know."

Gideon opened their mouth to produce the usual soothing inventory—I can find out, I'll ask the parish office, I'll check the guest book—and discovered, with genuine alarm, that nothing came out. The system had failed. Somewhere between the lectern and the coffee urn, between *Halloway* spoken aloud and *Halloway* echoing in their own skull, the great catalogue had developed its first hole. They searched for the woman's face and found it perfectly preserved; searched for a category to put her in and found none. *Unknown relation. Uninvited. Unexplained.* The cards in their pocket suddenly felt absurd, eleven small rectangles of managed truth, and Gideon wanted, violently, to burn them in the parish hall sink.

"The house goes to auction in nine days," Deshawn said instead, because Deshawn processed grief through logistics the way Gideon processed it through records. "Sign whatever they put in front of you. We're done with that place, and we're done with whatever she was hiding in it." He squeezed Gideon's shoulder—his version of tenderness—and left.

* * *

That night the library was nearly empty, rain against the tall windows, the radiators ticking their slow metallic heartbeat. Gideon shelved returns they didn't see. At the desk they took out the old notebook—the one from under the mattress, rescued through six moves and thirty years—and turned to the page with the phonetic spelling, fourteen-year-old handwriting, pencil gone soft with time.

*Hall-o-way,* it said. *Mom went white. Ask someday.*

Below it, in the space where a younger Gideon had always intended to write the answer, there was only blankness. Decades of blankness. An entire shelf reserved for a book that had never come in.

At 2:47 a.m., the entrance chime sounded.

Gideon looked up expecting the insomniac professor, the kid who slept in the periodicals room. Instead, dripping and unmistakable, the woman in the slate-colored coat crossed the lobby toward the desk, carrying something flat wrapped in oilcloth, held against her chest the way you carry a thing that matters more than staying dry.

She stopped at the desk. Water pooled around her shoes. Up close she had eyes the gray of the chapel rain, and she looked at Gideon with an expression that was not quite pity and not quite apology.

"You're the one who gave the eulogy," she said. "You have her way of saying a lot by saying almost nothing." She set the oilcloth bundle on the desk between them. "My name is Vera. And I think your mother and I have been keeping the same secret—one of us just ran out of time to keep it."

She began to unwrap the bundle, and Gideon, keeper of records, invisible for forty years, found they could not look away.
