## 2. What the Weather Station Kept

The house on Vetch Street had been wearing a plywood mask for eleven years, and like most masks, it had begun to fit better than the face beneath.

I went on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays at the library were slow enough that Marisol could cover the desk without asking questions, and because I had learned long ago that grief keeps business hours if you let it. The city that never sleeps still manages to be quietest at four in the afternoon, in the neighborhoods it has already decided to forget. Vetch Street was one of those. The houses leaned together like old men sharing a secret they'd rather not have confirmed.

I stood at the chain-link gate for a while, doing the thing I do best, which is nothing. Thirty years of night shifts teach you the value of a well-timed pause. Patrons think a librarian who waits is a librarian who cares. Mostly he's just buying time to decide whether the question deserves an answer or a smile.

The catalog had called this place a "decommissioned municipal weather observation station, parcel 4471-V." Which was true, in the way that official language is always true — technically, expensively, and with its back turned. My mother had kept weather logs in that house for thirty-one years. Barometric pressure, wind direction, the particular gray of a particular sky, recorded in her small slanted hand every morning at seven-fifteen, before my father left for the mill and before everything else happened too. She called it her correspondence with the sky. She said the sky was the only correspondent who never asked her to explain herself.

The plywood over the front door had been pried loose at one corner by some enterprising teenager years ago, and the gap was exactly the width of a man who had spent three decades making himself narrow out of professional habit. Inside, the dark smelled the way all abandoned houses smell — dust and mouse and the ghost of some cleaning product applied too hopefully decades ago — but underneath it, faint as a signature under erasure, there was lily of the valley. Her soap. Eleven years gone and the house still carried it, the way a radiator carries heat after the boiler shuts down.

I don't know what I was looking for. That's not quite honest. I knew what I was looking for; I just hadn't admitted it yet. I was looking for the sentence.

Aldous

My brother has a tell. He's had it since we were boys: when Wendell is about to do something reckless in the quietest possible way, he becomes punctual. He answers emails within the hour. He returns calls. For a man whose entire career is built on deferral, sudden responsiveness is the equivalent of a fire alarm.

So when he replied to my birthday text in nine minutes flat — *Thank you, Aldous. Lovely to hear from you.* — I knew.

I'd known since the auction catalog went out anyway. I wrote the parcel description myself, all of it except the final line, which came up from Records already typeset, and which I told myself was a clerical artifact, a scanning error, a fragment of some old inventory note. *She chose the barometer over the boy.* In her shorthand. Our mother's shorthand, the one she invented for her weather logs, the one she taught us both one summer on the porch, promising it would keep our secrets from the sky.

I should have shredded the catalog draft. Instead I approved it. I've been trying to understand that decision for two weeks, and the closest I can come is this: some part of me wanted it found. Some part of me wanted Wendell to find it, so that I wouldn't have to say it aloud, so that the sentence could convict me without my testimony.

That part of me is a fool. The rest of me got to work.

* * *

Wendell

The radiators were still in the house. Everything else portable had been stripped — copper pipe, fixtures, the mantel clock my father won at a raffle the year I turned nine — but the radiators remained, six cast-iron monsters squatting against the walls like furniture from a heavier century. The city had apparently decided they were structural. Or had simply forgotten them, which in my experience amounts to the same thing.

I started in the parlor because that was where her desk had been. The radiator there was the largest, its fins furred with dust, its valve capped with a wooden plug someone had whittled to fit. I recognized the whittling. My father did that — capped every valve in the house so the children wouldn't burn their hands. It was the only tenderness I ever caught him performing without an audience.

The plug came out with a sound like a held breath released. Inside the valve housing, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with kitchen twine, was a bundle of notebooks.

Not letters. Notebooks — the weather logs themselves, forty-odd of them, going back further than I'd guessed. I sat down on the floor of the parlor with my back against the cold iron and opened the earliest one, and there was her hand, young and sure, recording the sky of 1963 as though the sky needed a witness.

Between the meteorological entries, in the margins, in the shorthand, she had written other things. Small things at first. *The mill whistle is flat today.* Then less small. *H. drank the grocery money again.* Then, over years, in entries growing denser as the margins narrowed, the whole private climate of our house, condensed into a code the sky alone could read.

And then, near the end of the last notebook, the sentence. The one from the catalog. Written once, dated the night everything fractured, and never repeated.

I didn't decode it. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of my own skull: I sat in the dust with my mother's confession in my lap and I closed the notebook, because I am a librarian, and librarians know that some books you are not ready to read are better left shelved until you are. I wrapped the bundle back in its oilcloth, put it inside my coat, and walked out through the plywood gap into the ordinary afternoon, carrying forty years of weather against my chest.

It was only later, walking home, that I understood the notebooks shouldn't have been in the radiator at all. She hid them there the way she hid everything — in plain sight, in machinery, in the thing the house ran on. And she hid them *before* the demolition order, before the auction, before any of it. Which meant she had expected the house to be opened someday. By someone. Perhaps even by me.

Which meant someone else had known they were there, and had failed to remove them.

* * *

Aldous

The shredder in my office is a cross-cut model, city issue, rated for confidential documents. It has a modest appetite. I have been feeding it slowly, a few pages a day, the way you're supposed to feed anything you don't want to choke on.

Mother's letters — the ones she wrote to the city, petitioning, appealing, begging them to reconsider the condemnation of the house — those went first. Then the internal memos about the parcel designation. Then the appraisal notes that mentioned, in passing, "occupant's personal effects, unclaimed." Each page into the teeth, each page a small act of mercy or betrayal depending on which brother you ask, and I no longer trust myself to say which.

Because here is what I know that Wendell doesn't, or didn't: Mother sent the logs to me. Two years before she died, a box arrived at my office with a note in the shorthand — *You always were the one who could carry weight.* I read exactly one entry. One. Enough to learn that the sentence in the catalog wasn't a fragment, wasn't an error, wasn't a clerical artifact. It was addressed to one of us. It named one of us. And I burned the box rather than learn which.

I told myself I was protecting him. I have been telling myself that for twenty years, and I am very good at telling myself things; it runs in the family, along with the shorthand and the silence.

Then this morning, a colleague mentioned seeing a tall thin man letting himself into the Vetch Street property through the plywood. Tall, thin, moving "like he worked nights."

I fed the shredder another letter, and my hands were steady, and that frightened me more than anything else — how steady they were. How practiced. Somewhere across town my brother was holding pages I had failed to destroy, reading our mother's weather, circling the same wound from the other side.

By tomorrow he'll have decoded enough to come for me. And I will have to decide, again, whether to bury him with the truth or finally, at fifty-eight years old, dig myself out.

I looked at the shredder. It hummed, patient, ready.

I unplugged it.
