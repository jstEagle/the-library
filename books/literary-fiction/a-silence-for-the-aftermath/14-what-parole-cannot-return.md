## 14. What Parole Cannot Return

The bus to Evergreen Cemetery is the 38, which I have never ridden before, and I ride it like a tourist in my own grief, gripping the pole and studying the route map as if the dead might relocate without notice. Twenty-five years. My mother died in year nine of my sentence. I know this because the chaplain told me, standing at my bunk with his hat in his hands like a man delivering weather. He said it gently, that she'd gone in her sleep, and I thanked him, because what else does a person do with a thing like that. Thank you, I said. Thank you for telling me my mother is dead.

I had not been to her grave before today. There was no one to take me when I got out—no car, no occasion—and so she has waited eleven months in the ground while I learned how to use a debit card and flinched at doorbells. It occurs to me, riding past the auto shops and the check-cashing places, that I have been paroled from a prison but not yet from the sentence. The sentence was twenty-five to life, and life, as far as I can tell, is still in session.

The cemetery office gives me a map with a photocopied X on it. Section 14, row 6. Of course it's section 14. The universe has a sense of humor; it just never shares the joke.

Her stone is small, gray, modest—the kind you buy when the insurance doesn't cover ambition. MARISOL REYES SR., it says, BELOVED MOTHER, and then the dates, and nothing else, because there wasn't money for an epitaph and nobody in the family had the gift for compression anyway. I stand there in the July heat with my good shoes getting dusty and I wait for something to happen in me. That's the thing nobody tells you about grief after prison: it arrives pre-delayed. You've already done your crying in a concrete room with a pay phone, and by the time you reach the actual grave, all that's left is a kind of administrative sorrow. Paperwork of the heart. I'm here to sign for my feelings, ma'am. Sign here, initial there.

I talk to her anyway. What else.

"Mami," I say. "I got out."

A sparrow lands on the neighboring stone and regards me with the flat professional interest of a bird that has heard every eulogy ever delivered and found them all derivative.

"I work at a diner now. Eggs mostly. I'm writing a book." I stop. Even to the dead I can't say it plainly. "I'm writing some things down."

The sparrow leaves. My mother, who once chased a landlord out of our kitchen with a cast-iron skillet, offers no comment, which is unlike her, and I understand suddenly that this is the real cost—not the missing of her death, not the eleven months, but this: that I will never again hear her tell me what to do. For twenty-five years I survived on the memory of her voice giving orders. Get up, mija. Eat. Don't let them see you cry. And now the voice is a rock with her name on it, and the orders have stopped coming, and I am a woman of forty-one standing in a field of stones with no instructions whatsoever.

I put my hand on the granite. It's hot. Everything here is hot, baking, patient.

"They say I should forgive myself," I tell her. "There's a counselor. She has a certificate on the wall."

My mother's silence sounds exactly like disagreement.

* * *

The advance is sixty thousand dollars against royalties, which the agent, Dana, explained to me over lunch at a restaurant where the waiter described the provenance of the pork as if introducing a child. Sixty thousand dollars. In prison I earned nineteen cents an hour sewing sheets for state hospitals, so by my old mathematics, this lunch represented approximately four hundred years of labor, and the pork itself was beyond calculation. Dana kept saying words like *platform* and *compelling* and *the moment*, and I kept looking at the number on the contract and thinking: that's a house down payment. That's my daughter's student loans gone. That's a car that starts in winter.

That's also the price of a human being, apparently, retail. Twenty-five years, wholesale, went for nothing—no compensation, no apology from the state, just a bus ticket and a pamphlet about reentry resources. But sell the story of those years to people who watch true crime documentaries with their dinner, and suddenly everyone's got a checkbook out. I did the arithmetic in the restaurant bathroom afterward, sitting on a toilet in a stall nicer than any room I lived in for two decades, and I could not make the numbers come out clean. Sixty thousand divided by nine thousand one hundred twenty-five days. Six dollars and fifty-seven cents a day. Less than the diner pays me. Less than the eggs cost.

Blood money, my mother would have called it, and she'd have taken it anyway, because my mother was not a fool, only proud, and pride is a luxury item she could never afford. But she'd have made me say out loud what it was first. Name the thing, mija, she used to say, or the thing will name you.

So: it is blood money. Mine, mostly. Some of Danny Vasquez's, though he didn't live long enough to bleed for it. And some of it—I'm still deciding how much—Coral Vasquez's, who sat across from me at table six last month and ordered her eggs scrambled soft and looked at me like a person looks at a fire they remember starting.

* * *

My daughter lives in Van Buren Courts, third floor, walk-up, with a man named Terrence who answered the door in socks and looked at me the way you look at a salesperson. "She's home," he said, and shouted it into the apartment without turning around, and left me in the doorway holding a grocery bag of plantains I'd bought because I didn't know what else to bring. You can't bring twenty-five years. You can't wrap them. Plantains were what I had.

Lucia came to the door wiping her hands on a dish towel, and I want to be accurate here, because accuracy is the whole discipline of my new life: she did not run to me. She did not cry. She looked at me the way you look at a photograph of someone you knew—a resemblance, a record, a fact that requires verification. She was four when I went in. She is twenty-nine now. She has her father's forehead and my mother's way of standing with her weight on one hip, arms folded, and she stood like that in her own doorway and said:

"Hey, 4-1-9-2-0."

Not Marisol. Not Mom. Not even *you*. The number. Her number for me, worn smooth by use, the way a prayer bead goes smooth. She called me that through eight years of letters and visiting-room glass, and somewhere along the way the number stopped being a placeholder and became my name to her, the truest one, because numbers don't lie and mothers do—or so the child's logic ran, and who was I to argue with it from inside the glass.

"Hey, baby," I said, and held up the plantains like a peace offering from a country that no longer exists.

She stepped aside to let me in. That was the whole ceremony. Stepping aside.

The apartment is small and clean and smells like fabric softener and frying oil, and there is a boy on the couch—her son, my grandson, seven years old, playing a game on a tablet with the sound off. He looked up at me with Lucia's exact eyes and asked, "Who's that?"

"That's—" Lucia started, and stopped, and I watched her arrive at the same wall I keep arriving at. Grandmother is a word for a person with a history. Prisoner is a word for a person with a record. Neither fits. She settled on, "That's my mom. From before."

From before. I am a period of history now. Pre-war. Before the incident. The boy nodded with the magnificent indifference of seven-year-olds and went back to his game, and I put the plantains on the counter and stood in my daughter's kitchen like a ghost doing an inspection, checking whether the living are managing alright.

We drank coffee at her table. She has good cups—thick white ones, diner style, and I recognized them because we sell the same ones at the Paloma, and I didn't say so, because everything I almost say these days comes back to that diner, to that review, to that woman eating tasteless eggs and watching me with her careful face.

"You look good," Lucia said, in the tone of a person reporting a finding.

"I eat regular," I said. "You get thin in there. They feed you starch and despair."

Almost-smile. Progress, by the standards we're working with. Then she turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit I recognize because it was mine first, and said, "Dana called me. Your agent. She wanted family background. Dates, details." A pause with weight in it. "She asked me if I remembered the fire."

The kitchen got very quiet. The boy's game ticked away on the couch, soundless explosions.

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her I was four." Another quarter-turn. "I told her I don't remember anything except the smoke smell stayed in our coats for a year."

"That's true?"

"That's true." She looked at me then, straight on, and her eyes did a thing I couldn't read—my daughter, whom I cannot read, whom I missed learning to read. "Is there something I *should* remember? Because the lady on the phone sounded like there was more story than the newspaper had."

And there it is. The memoir, the advance, the agent with her platform and her moment—all of it circling back to this kitchen, this table, this girl who lost her mother to a number and grew up on the official version. I thought about what the truth costs. I've had twenty-five years to price it. It costs everything going in and everything coming out, and the difference between the two directions is that coming out, at least, you get to choose the day.

"Not yet," I said. "When the book's done, you'll read it first. Before anyone. I promise you that."

"That's not an answer, 4-1-9-2-0."

"No," I said. "It's not."

She walked me to the door because that was the polite thing, and at the threshold she did something that undid me more thoroughly than tears would have: she hugged me, quick and hard, the way you hug someone you're afraid will be taken back, and then she pulled away and said, "Don't be a stranger. Again." And closed the door, and through it I heard her say to the boy, in a voice scrubbed bright for his benefit, "That was your grandma, baby. From before."

* * *

On the 38 back, the sun going orange behind the water towers, I took out my order tickets—the draft, the pages, the whole ragged archive of my nine thousand days—and I read the note I'd tucked into the middle of it three weeks ago, addressed to nobody, addressed to whoever you are:

*If you're reading this, you already know what you did. The question is whether you know what it cost. Those aren't the same thing. I've met a lot of people in my life who confused the bill with the damage.*

I wrote it the week after Coral Vasquez left her anonymous review, generous and glowing, five stars for a diner she ate at with a dead tongue. I understood it immediately for what it was—an installment, a down payment, guilt wearing kindness like a borrowed coat. She knows. She has known since the eggs. And she is circling, the way guilty people circle, paying for things, buying houses I hear about from customers, leaving tips too big.

The bus shuddered over the tracks. An old man across from me slept sitting up with his mouth open, trusting the world completely, and I envied him with my whole heart.

Because here is what parole cannot return, and I have the list memorized: it cannot return my twenties, which I served instead of living. It cannot return my mother's funeral or my daughter's first day of school or the sound of my own name said without a number attached. It cannot return the version of Lucia who might have loved me easily, if love hadn't been routed through glass and guards for her whole childhood. It cannot give me back the girl I was at sixteen, before the fire, before the trial, before I learned that the truth is a currency and I was broke.

But it also cannot take certain things back, and this is the part I hold onto, the part I press flat and keep in my shirt pocket next to my heart like a photo: it cannot un-teach me what I know. And what I know is this—somebody in that house set that fire, and somebody else in that house watched him do it, and neither of them was me. And one of them sat at my table last month and couldn't taste her food.

Tomorrow I go back to the Paloma. Tomorrow she'll come in, or she won't, and either way I'll pour the coffee and keep my head down and write my pages on the backs of order tickets. But tonight, riding the 38 through the neighborhood they're tearing down street by street, I made a decision, and it felt like setting down something heavy and picking up something heavier.

The book gets written. All of it. The Vasquezes included.

Let her read it when it's done. Let her read it first, if she wants—before the publishers, before the cameras, before Lucia. Let her sit with it the way I sat with twenty-five years.

Fair is fair. I read the official version of my life for a quarter century.

She can spend one summer reading mine.
