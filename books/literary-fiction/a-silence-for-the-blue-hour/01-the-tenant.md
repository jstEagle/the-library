## 1. The Tenant

The house did not recognize me, and I could not blame it.

I had been back four days and the house had made its position clear in small ways: the front door swelled against its frame so that I had to lift and shove it like a stubborn relative; the ceiling fan in my mother's room clicked on a rhythm that was not quite a rhythm; and every surface seemed to hold a faint residue of Chinyere Okonkwo, as if she had rubbed herself into the walls over forty years and left me only the polish to maintain. I moved through the rooms with the careful politeness of a guest who has arrived too early for her hosts. I knocked on doorframes before entering. I apologized to furniture.

Ngozi, the housekeeper, had no such difficulties. She had been with my mother for thirty years, and she grieved the way she cooked — steadily, generously, without asking anyone's permission. On my second morning I came downstairs at six to find her already in the kitchen, frying plantain for no one in particular, tears running freely down her face while her hands turned the slices with perfect calm.

"Ndo," she said when she saw me. Sorry. Condolence. The word that in Igbo does double duty for grief and greeting, so that mourning a person and meeting one are conducted in the same breath.

"Ndo," I answered, and heard immediately how wrong it sounded — the vowels too flat, the tone falling where it should have risen. It is a strange thing to be corrected by your own voice. Ngozi's face did something complicated: sympathy first, then a flicker of embarrassment on my behalf, then the swift professional smoothing-over of both. She held out a plate of plantain.

"You will eat," she said. Not a question. In this kitchen, English arrived only when necessary, and necessity was rare.

I ate. The plantain was exactly as my mother's had been, which meant Ngozi had learned it from her, which meant I was eating grief prepared by someone else and finding it delicious, and there was no way to sit with that thought gracefully, so I sat with it ungracefully, chewing, while the compound woke up around us — the neighbor's generator coughing awake, a rooster with an inflated sense of its own importance, the muezzin from the mosque two streets over finishing his dawn call as though reluctant to let go of the last note.

"Madam used to eat standing," Ngozi said, watching me. "Right here. By the window. She said sitting made the food think it was winning."

I laughed — a real laugh, surprising us both — and then felt the laugh curdle, because I hadn't known that about my mother. I had known her phone number, her blood pressure medication, her opinions on my career and my childlessness and my accent. I had not known how she ate her breakfast. Thirty years of Ngozi had bought her that knowledge. Forty years of being her daughter had bought me a forwarding address.

* * *

By nine each morning I converted myself into a working translator, because grief does not excuse you from invoices. I set up at the dining table — my mother's table, mahogany, imported by my father in 1983 and treated ever since with the reverence owed to a dowry — and opened my laptop among the lace doilies that survived from another decade. My London clients believed I was "handling family matters." Family matters, in their imagination, presumably involved casseroles and solicitors. In practice it involved me translating a forty-page custody agreement between a shipping company and a former employee while a gecko watched me from the wall with what I chose to interpret as judgment.

The work itself was easy, which was the problem. Contracts are the most honest dishonest prose in the world: they say everything twice and mean nothing once. "The party of the first part hereby undertakes..." I could render such sentences in my sleep, and some mornings I suspected I did. Legal English has no threshold, no blue hour, no word for the specific silence between a mother and a daughter on a telephone line. It asks nothing of you but accuracy, and accuracy, after all these years, was the one language I spoke fluently.

At noon on the third day, Mrs. Adeyemi from next door came to the gate with a covered bowl. She was eighty if she was a day, dressed in Ankara print bright enough to require sunglasses, and she had known my mother since the two of them had argued over a fence line in 1979 and become inseparable friends because of it.

"Marguerite!" she called through the gate, before I even reached it. "Come! You look like your mother when she was pretending to be angry."

I unlatched the gate and received the bowl and attempted the greeting. "De-de-e, Ma. Kedu ka ọ dị?"

What I produced was, I am told by my own ears in retrospect, roughly the Igbo of a parrot that has studied hard. Mrs. Adeyemi's face performed the same sequence Ngozi's had — kindness, wince, recovery — and then, mercifully, she switched to Yoruba-inflected English and saved us both.

"Eh, don't worry yourself. Your mother's tongue was too heavy for you people of these days. Come, sit. How is the house?"

"The house is fine."

"The house is not fine. The house is grieving. Houses do that, you know, when the owner dies and strangers start touching things." She said this without malice, the way you'd remark on weather, and handed me a spoon. "Eat. Pepper is good for mourning. It gives the eyes something else to do."

We sat on her veranda, which smelled of eucalyptus and old radio, and she told me stories about my mother that I had never heard, each one landing like a letter addressed to someone else. Chinyere dancing at a naming ceremony until her wrapper came loose. Chinyere feeding a beggar every Friday for eleven years and threatening him cheerfully if he ever thanked her. Chinyere, during the last harmattan, standing at her own gate at dusk singing — singing! — some song half in Igbo, half in something older, while the streetlights came on one by one as if taking attendance.

"She loved the evening," Mrs. Adeyemi said. "That hour when it's not day and not night. She said it was the only honest time. Everything else is performing."

I wrote that down later, in the notebook I kept for phrases I didn't want to lose. I told myself it was professional habit. A translator collects. But I knew, even then, that I was collecting evidence of a woman I had lived with for eighteen years and somehow missed entirely.

* * *

That evening I attempted the neighbors' greetings again, on my own side of the fence this time. Mr. Okafor across the street, watering his tomatoes at dusk, returned my "kedu" with such elaborate enthusiasm that I understood he was congratulating me the way one congratulates a toddler who has just walked into a table. His wife emerged, took one listen, and gently completed my sentences for me, the way you'd finish a child's homework.

"It's the tones," Mr. Okafor said kindly. "Igbo is music. You have the notes but not the tune."

"I grew up speaking it," I said, and heard how defensive it sounded, how much like an alibi.

"Ah, but London," his wife said, with a shrug that contained entire histories of her own children lost to Houston and Toronto. "London takes the tune out of everybody."

They waved me off warmly. I walked back across the street carrying their kindness like a package too large for my hands. Behind me I heard them resume in rapid Igbo, the language closing ranks behind me like water over a stone.

Here is what nobody tells you about losing a language you were born into: it doesn't leave all at once. It leaves the way a tenant leaves — gradually, politely, taking things you don't notice missing until you reach for them. First the proverbs go, then the jokes, then the insults (and oh, how I missed the insults; English scolds, but Igbo *wounds*, beautifully). What remains is a skeleton of vocabulary that clanks when you walk. I could order food. I could offer condolences. I could say *ndewo* and *jisie ike* and *ka ọ dị* with the confidence of a woman reading cue cards. What I could not do was think in it, dream in it, or stand in my dead mother's kitchen and ask her housekeeper a single question that mattered.

And the terrible thing — the thing I circled for days without landing on — was that I couldn't remember whether I had ever been able to. Whether the fluency had been taken from me or whether I had, with great diligence, set it down somewhere and forgotten where, the way my mother had apparently set down an entire self.

* * *

On the fourth night, Ngozi found me in my mother's room.

I hadn't meant to go in. I had been avoiding it with the discipline of a professional — I'd slept in the guest room, worked at the dining table, routed my days around that closed door the way water routes around a rock. But the fan in the guest room had developed a new click, a mocking one, and past midnight I gave up and crossed the corridor and stood outside her door like a teenager outside a headmaster's office.

Inside, the room was exactly as she'd left it, which is to say it was a museum curated by a woman who intended to come back. Her glasses folded on the nightstand. Her Bible — worn soft as cloth, King James, with Igbo hymns penciled in the margins in handwriting that looped like birdsong. Her wrapper cloths stacked in the wardrobe in strict chromatic order, blues through greens through golds, a spectrum of decades. The air still held her: camphor and shea butter and something underneath, something mineral and unnameable that I recognized with a jolt as *home* and resented for being recognizable.

I sat on the edge of her bed, which creaked a warning, and picked up the Bible, and read a margin note beside the Twenty-third Psalm: *"He maketh me to lie down"* followed by, in smaller pencil, *"even when we do not want to."*

I was still holding it when Ngozi appeared in the doorway with a candle, though the electricity was on.

"I did not hear you come up," I said.

"I move quietly. Madam trained me." She set the candle down anyway, lit it, and stood looking at the wardrobe with an expression I couldn't read. "You should sleep. Grief is heavy and you are carrying it in your face."

"I'm fine," I said, in the fluent English of liars everywhere. "Just looking."

She nodded slowly. Then, in Igbo — deliberately, I realized later; she chose the language the way you choose a key for a lock — she said, "Ụlọ a maara onye nwe ya." This house knows its owner.

"And I'm not it," I answered in English, because answering in Igbo would have required me to find words I didn't trust myself to carry.

Ngozi looked at me for a long moment. Thirty years of dusting around other people's silences had given her a face that gave away nothing except tiredness, and even the tiredness might have been decorative. "Your mother also said things in English," she said finally, "when the Igbo was too true to say." She gathered her candle. "Sleep well, madam. Tomorrow I will show you where things are."

"Where things are?"

But she was already moving down the corridor, quiet as her training, and the house settled around her departure with a sigh of wood and heat. Outside, the light was doing the thing Mrs. Adeyemi had described — that blue, undecided hour — and I sat on my mother's bed holding her Bible and listening to the fan click and thinking: *the house knows its owner, and the housekeeper knows the house, and I know contracts.*

It was then, lying back on sheets that smelled of camphor, staring at the ceiling where the wardrobe threw a long shadow like a door standing open, that I noticed the floorboard.

It was near the wardrobe's right foot — a board slightly paler than its neighbors, its grain running the wrong way, as if it had been lifted and laid back down by someone in a hurry, or by someone who was not a carpenter. I filed it away the way I filed everything: accurately, uselessly, in English.

In the morning, I would remember it. In the morning, the wardrobe would have to move.

But that is getting ahead of the story. That night I slept in my mother's bed for the first time, badly, dreaming in two languages and understanding neither, while downstairs the house went on grieving for a woman who had apparently spent forty years telling it secrets — and keeping one, perhaps, under a warped board, within arm's reach of where she slept.
