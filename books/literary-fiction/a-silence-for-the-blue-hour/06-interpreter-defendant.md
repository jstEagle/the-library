## 6. Interpreter, Defendant

The work arrived on a Tuesday, in a courier envelope soft with handling, as if it had been passed from desk to desk across two continents looking for someone willing to hold it. Barrister Folake's office in Victoria Island had sent it over with a note in her looping hand: *London-returned files, urgent-ish, no drama please.* The "no drama" was aimed at me personally; we had history, Folake and I, most of it affectionate, some of it involving me refusing to soften a deposition and her calling me "the conscience that bills by the hour."

I cleared my mother's dining table — the one I now worked at because the study still smelled of camphor and closed years — and spread the files out under the ceiling fan. Custody hearing. A Nigerian mother, a British father, a seven-year-old girl caught between them like a coin spun and never allowed to land. My job was to translate the mother's testimony from Igbo into English for the court record, cleanly, completely, without embellishment. It was work I was good at. It was work I had done for eleven years. And it was work that, on this particular Tuesday, felt less like a profession than like a mirror held up at an unflattering angle.

The audio files were recorded in a solicitor's office in Peckham. The mother's voice came through first: young, careful, the Igbo of someone who has lived abroad long enough to reach for English nouns and plant them in Igbo sentences like cut flowers in foreign soil. *"Biko, I want to say it properly,"* she said to the recording solicitor, before she began. *"Tell the interpreter I want to say it properly."*

I liked her immediately, which should have warned me.

* * *

For the first hour, it was ordinary work. She described the flat in Peckham, the school run, the daughter's asthma, the father's new partner who kept parrots. Her Igbo was imperfect but honest, the way a person's voice is honest when they are too tired to perform. I typed steadily, pausing the recording only to make tea I didn't drink, and the house around me did its afternoon business — Ngozi sweeping the veranda with that particular rhythm she had, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause, like a woman keeping time with a song only she remembered.

Then, forty minutes into the second session, the mother's voice changed.

She was asked about an incident — a night the child had been left alone, briefly, while the mother worked a double shift at the care home. The question came through the court interpreter in English, then again in Igbo, and the mother went quiet for so long that I could hear the solicitor's clock ticking on the recording.

*"O naghị m achọ ịgha ụgha,"* she said finally. *I don't want to lie.*

And then she lied.

Not crudely. That's what I want you to understand, whoever you are reading this transcript someday, if anyone ever does. She lied beautifully. She constructed a version of that night in which a neighbor — a Mrs. Adaeze, whose name she stumbled slightly on, the way you stumble on a word you invented four seconds ago — had been present in the flat the whole time, watching television, helping with the inhaler. She gave Mrs. Adaeze a personality. She gave her a laugh. She said Mrs. Adaeze had made the child jollof rice, which was a detail of such specific tenderness that I actually stopped typing, because I understood in my chest, before my head caught up, exactly what that detail was for. It was not for the court. It was for the daughter, who might one day read this record, who must never be allowed to believe she had been alone.

The court interpreter rendered it faithfully. *The witness states that a neighbor was present throughout the evening.*

And I sat there in my dead mother's dining room with my hands hovering over the keyboard, and I thought: I know this woman. I have been this woman. I have spent my entire adult life sitting in the space between what Chinyere Okonkwo said and what the world was permitted to hear, smoothing the seams, choosing the words, deciding — always deciding — how much truth a sentence could carry without breaking.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about translation, or at least nobody told me before I made it my living: you cannot be neutral. People imagine the translator as a pipe, water goes in one language, comes out the other, unchanged. But a sentence is not water. A sentence is a living thing with a temperature, and when you carry it across, it warms in your hands or cools, it trembles or goes still, and all of that is you. All of that is your judgment, your loyalty, your fear. Every translation is a small custody hearing where you decide who gets the child.

I learned this at my mother's knee, though neither of us called it that. When the neighbors asked after the family, Chinyere would say things in Igbo that arrived in my ears as one country and left my mouth in English as another. *"Anyị dị mma"* — we are fine — became, in my nine-year-old rendering for the British missionary's wife, "Mother says thank you for asking." What Chinyere had actually said, in full, was: *We are fine, tell her we are fine, and do not tell her about your father, do not tell her anything, a child's mouth is a door and I am teaching you which way it locks.* She never said those words aloud. She didn't need to. I absorbed the grammar of silence the way I absorbed verb conjugations — early, permanently, without knowing I was learning.

So there I sat, thirty-some years later, listening to a stranger in Peckham build a shelter of lies around her daughter, and instead of judging her I recognized her the way you recognize your own handwriting on a form you don't remember filling out.

* * *

The problem — and I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only integrity I have left some days — the problem was not the lying. Courts are full of lies; interpreters hear them the way doctors hear coughs. The problem was what I did next.

There was a moment, near the end of the third session, when the mother broke. The solicitor pressed her — gently, but pressed — on whether Mrs. Adaeze could be contacted to confirm her account. And the mother's voice did something I have heard perhaps five times in my career: it split. One layer kept performing, kept giving addresses and approximate times, and underneath it another layer said, in pure village Igbo, unadorned, the register you fall back into when the performance budget runs out:

*"M na-arọ ụra n'anya mgbe ọ na-ehi ụra. Ọ dịghị onye nọ ebe ahụ. Abụ m onye nne na-eche naanị ya."*

*I was asleep on my feet while she slept. There was no one there. I am a mother alone.*

It lasted maybe six seconds. Then the performance resumed, seamless as a hem. But those six seconds were the truest thing in fourteen hours of recording, and they were also, legally speaking, catastrophic. A confession wrapped in the exact words a family court would use to take her daughter away.

My cursor blinked in the empty transcript line.

The faithful rendering sat there waiting to be typed: *The witness admits no one was present and describes herself as a single mother overwhelmed by exhaustion.* Two sentences. Eleven years of training. A code of ethics thinner than the paper it was printed on, and heavier than the wardrobe upstairs that I still hadn't moved.

I thought about my mother. I thought about the biscuit tin behind the wardrobe — because yes, I had found it by then, I had been carrying it around the house like a hot coal for weeks, opening it at night, closing it before dawn — and I thought about how every letter in it was a Mrs. Adaeze. A constructed presence. A tender, invented detail standing where the unbearable truth should have been. *The child was given to God,* Chinyere had written in 1971, when what she meant was: my husband refused to look at our daughter and I let him win. Somewhere in Enugu there was presumably a real story with a real midwife and a real handoff, and Chinyere had translated it into something survivable, and I — I was her interpreter. I had spent forty years rendering her silences into acceptable English for the world, and worse, rendering them into acceptable memory for myself.

If I typed those two sentences, I would be doing to this stranger in Peckham what nobody had ever done to my mother: refusing the translation she needed in order to live.

If I didn't, I would be complicit. Again. Professionally this time, which almost made it worse, because at least with my mother I'd had the excuse of being nine.

* * *

What I did, in the end, was neither faithful nor false. This is the translator's purgatory, the place we go when we can't be saints and won't be traitors. I rendered the six seconds, but I blurred them. I wrote: *The witness becomes distressed and states that she was alone and exhausted that evening, before regaining composure.* Which is true. Which is also not the whole truth. Which lets a careful reader see the crack in the wall without handing them the crowbar. I told myself it was mercy. I told myself it was strategy — a good solicitor could work with distress; a confession would end her. I told myself several things, and the fan turned overhead, and Ngozi swept her three strokes and a pause, and somewhere in the transcript a woman's six seconds of honesty dissolved into professional language, the way everything true in my family had always dissolved into professional language, into *we are fine*, into *given to God*, into *no drama please*.

Folake would have caught it. Folake reads transcripts the way priests read faces. But Folake wasn't there, and the file went back in its envelope, and the envelope went back across the ocean carrying my small treachery inside it like a stone in a bird's crop.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep.

This will not surprise you. It barely surprised me, and I am the one who did it. I lay in my childhood bed — I had moved into it after the funeral, unable to keep sleeping in the guest room like a visitor, unable to admit by taking the master bedroom that the house was mine now — and the ceiling fan clicked through its rotations, and the gecko on the wall performed its one-word vocabulary, and my mind replayed the mother's voice on a loop: *Abụ m onye nne na-eche naanị ya.* I am a mother alone. Six words in Igbo, and I had buried them in English like a body in a shallow grave, and now they wouldn't stay buried, they kept pushing up through the soil of the transcript, through the mattress, through my ribs.

At some point past two I got up and stood in the doorway of the dining room, where the files were stacked, and beyond them, visible down the corridor, the door of my mother's room, and beyond that, in the dark, the wardrobe. I hadn't opened the tin in four days. I had told myself this was discipline — finish the contract work first, approach the letters in daylight, be a professional about your own life. But standing there at two in the morning, I admitted what it actually was. Fear. The same fear that had made me blur six seconds of truth. Because the tin contained sentences in my mother's own hand, in her own Igbo, unperformed, unsponsored, addressed to a dead woman, and reading them meant serving as interpreter for a client who could never review my work, correct my errors, or forgive my choices. Every letter was a custody hearing where the child on trial was the truth itself, and I was the only translator available, and my record — as of tonight — included one deliberate misrendering.

I went back to bed. I did not sleep. Around four, the blue hour began its slow work on the windows, and I heard myself, without deciding to, whisper the mother's sentence into the dark, complete, unblurred, in the original: *Abụ m onye nne na-eche naanị ya.* As if returning stolen property. As if the words belonged to her and I had merely been holding them wrong.

In the morning there was a message from Folake: *Client says the transcript reads well. You have a gift for tone.* I looked at that sentence for a long time. A gift for tone. In my family, we had a different phrase for the same talent, and my mother had used it about me exactly once, when I was nineteen and had smoothed over some disaster at a family gathering so seamlessly that even the casualties thanked me afterward. She had squeezed my arm and said, in Igbo, *Ị maara otu esi ekpuchi ihe* — you know how to cover a thing.

I had taken it as praise for thirty years.

Now, standing in the kitchen with my phone in my hand and the biscuit tin waiting behind the wardrobe like a defendant awaiting trial, I understood it had been an instruction. A job description. An inheritance passed from a woman who knew how to cover a thing to a daughter who did it better, in two languages, for money.

I put the phone down. I walked to my mother's room. It was time to stop being the interpreter and start being, whatever the cost, the reader.

The wardrobe was heavier than I remembered — but then, I had never tried to move it before. Nobody ever had.
