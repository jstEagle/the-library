## 2. The Woman Who Counts Words

The county records office occupied the old Carnegie building on Delmore Street, the one with the sandstone facade going soft as sugar at the corners, and Theo had been in it perhaps four times in her life—twice for property disputes she was only nominally part of, once to renew a certification, once, years ago, to look up something about her mother's house that turned out not to matter. The building smelled the way all such buildings smelled: paper, dust, floor wax, the faint mineral tang of radiators that had outlived their warranties. It was the smell of things kept past their usefulness, which was not the same as things kept past their value. Theo understood the difference. She had built a career on it.

She came on a Tuesday because Tuesdays were the day the county engineer's office received the as-built drawings, and she needed the original 1953 plans for the Kestrel River Bridge. Not the digitized copies—those were on her laptop, flattened and lossy, the way everything became when it was converted for convenience—but the linen originals, the ones with the draftsman's hand still visible in the lettering, the ones where you could see where a man named H. Kowalczyk had second-guessed himself on the pier spacing and erased it, and erased it again, and committed. Theo liked to read the hesitations in old drawings. They told you what the bridge had been afraid of before anyone poured a yard of concrete.

The clerk at the front desk was a young man with a name tag that said DYLAN and a manner that suggested he had been interrupted mid-thought by the twentieth century and never quite resumed. He looked up her request, made a small sound of surprise, and said, "Oh—the Kestrel materials. Those are with Mrs. Nwosu. She's got the whole annex. Has for, I don't know, years? You can go back, I guess. Just—" he gestured vaguely toward a door marked ARCHIVES in letters that had once been gold, "—she doesn't really like to be rushed."

"Who is she?" Theo asked, because the name meant nothing to her, which was itself a small failure she registered without comment. She knew this town. She had grown up forty minutes from it and inspected its infrastructure for eleven years. She knew the names of every culvert commissioner and every gravel quarry operator within three counties. She did not know Adaora Nwosu.

Dylan shrugged in a way that was either deference or ignorance. "She's, like, the archive lady? She used to teach, I think. She's doing some project. She's got all the cards."

"All the cards," Theo repeated.

"Yeah." He said it the way you'd say *all the snakes*, with a faint, unexamined unease. "Just knock. Or don't knock. She hears you anyway."

* * *

The annex had been the building's reading room once, and then its overflow storage, and now it was something that had no name on the door. The tall windows on the east side let in a light that was already going amber with the lateness of the season, and the light fell across long tables, and across the tables, in rows of perfect, obsessive order, lay cards.

Thousands of them. Five-by-seven index cards, most of them, though some were cut-down file folders, some were the backs of calendars, some were pieces of heavy paper that had clearly been salvaged from somewhere decades ago. Each card held a single handwritten entry in ink that shifted color across the decades—blue-black, then black, then, in the later years, a careful, tremulous ballpoint. Each entry had a word in a script Theo didn't recognize, and beneath it a phonetic rendering, and beneath that a definition in English, and beneath that, on some cards, a sentence. An example. A small life for the word to live in.

And at the far table, beneath the best of the light, sat a woman so old that her age seemed less like a number than like a geological fact.

She was small. She sat very straight, which surprised Theo—she had expected the collapse of extreme age, the forward fold toward the table—but Adaora Nwosu sat the way Theo imagined queens sat in countries that no longer existed: spine vertical, chin level, hands moving over the cards with a precision that had nothing to do with haste. Her hair was white and wrapped in a printed cloth the color of persimmons. She wore a cardigan over a dress that had been fashionable in a decade Theo could not name, and on the table beside her, within reach of her right hand, sat a teacup of cold tea, a magnifying glass, and a tin of mints.

She did not look up. She was writing. Her pen moved in small, certain strokes, and the only sound in the room was that sound, and the tick of the radiator, and somewhere far off the traffic on Delmore Street, which included, Theo noted with a reflexive tightening in her chest, a gravel truck taking the corner too fast, probably overloaded, probably bound for the bridge.

"You're the bridge woman."

Theo stopped. She hadn't made a sound that she knew of. Her boots were the quiet kind; she'd bought them for site work, where you wanted to hear the structure and not yourself.

"News travels," Theo said.

"News sits still and you walk past it." Adaora finished her line, capped the pen with a small click, and looked up. Her eyes were the thing that undid Theo's preparation. She had prepared for old—watery, vague, the eyes of a woman who had begun the long withdrawal from the visible world. These eyes were not withdrawn. They were dark, and quick, and they took Theo in the way a customs officer takes in a traveler: thoroughly, without hostility, and with an authority that made the traveler aware of everything she was carrying.

"You're from the county," Adaora said. "For the bridge."

"The as-built drawings. 1953. I filed the request last week."

"I know. Dylan told me Tuesday." A pause, in which Theo understood that today was Thursday, and that the delay had been deliberate, and that she was not going to be told why. "They're behind you. Third shelf, the gray boxes. Kowalczyk's drawings. He was a better draftsman than an engineer. He worried about the piers."

Theo felt something in her sternum, a small displacement, like a bearing shifting under load. "The pier spacing," she said. "I've seen the erasures."

"Have you." It wasn't a question, and it wasn't approval either, exactly. It was a door held open a few inches, and Adaora Nwosu was watching to see whether Theo would push it or stand outside in the weather. "You can work at the table by the window. The light is better there and I won't have to listen to you breathing in the stacks."

It was, Theo would understand later, a welcome. At the time it sounded like a dismissal, and she was grateful for it. She retrieved the gray boxes—three of them, heavier than they looked, linen drawings sleeved in mylar with a care that told her someone had loved these documents long before the county ever thought to—and she carried them to the window table, and for two hours the room held the two of them in a silence that was, Theo realized with something close to unease, comfortable. That was the strange part. She had spent her life in rooms where silence was a thing she maintained, a structure she was responsible for, load-bearing, hers to inspect and shore up. This silence was not hers. It belonged to the room, or to the woman at the far table, and Theo was merely permitted to stand inside it.

She worked. She photographed the drawings with the county tablet, noted the pier spacing Kowalczyk had finally committed to, the rebar callouts, the concrete spec—3,000 psi, which had been optimistic for 1953 and which explained, in a single line of faded ink, a great deal about what she'd been finding in the girders. She took measurements she didn't need. She was aware, the whole time, of the pen moving at the far table. Scratch. Pause. Scratch. The rhythm of it got into her the way a river gets into a valley.

It was when she stopped for water—there was a cooler in the corner, and she'd been under the bridge since dawn and hadn't drunk anything—that she made the mistake of looking at the cards.

The nearest stack sat on a cart by the cooler, ready to be filed. The top card read, in that careful late-period ballpoint:

*ìhè — [ee-heh] — n. a thing; also, a matter that concerns someone. Lit. "what it is." Ex.: Ìhè a dị m — "It is my matter." Said when claiming a burden as one's own.*

Theo read it twice. There was nothing remarkable in it. A word. A definition. And yet the example sentence sat in her chest with a weight out of all proportion to its fourteen letters, and she stood there holding her paper cup, water forgotten, looking at the small life of that word.

"You read English," Adaora said, without looking up. "But you read that card like it was a bridge report. Looking for the crack."

Theo set the cup down. "What language is it?"

"Igbo." Adaora said the word the way you'd say the name of a child. "But not only. The way it was spoken here. In this county. In this house." She turned a page of her notebook. "There were forty-one of us on Delmore Street in 1961. Did you know that? Forty-one speakers, counting the children. I have been counting them down ever since. I am the last card in the file now." She said it without self-pity, in the tone of a woman reporting a measurement. Theo recognized the tone. It was her own.

"Forty-one," Theo said.

"Thirty-eight by 1970." The pen paused. Adaora looked up again, and this time she looked for a long moment, and Theo had the distinct and disorienting sensation of being *inspected*—not the way men at job sites inspected her, a quick inventory and dismissal, but the way she herself inspected a structure: with patience, with attention to what was underneath the surface coat. "You stand like someone listening to a wall," Adaora said. "Most people stand like someone waiting to talk. You've been standing there four minutes and you haven't asked me a single question about myself, which means you're the first person this month who came into this room to hear something instead of to be heard. Or you're very good at pretending." Her eyes narrowed, not unkindly. "You have your grandmother's way of listening."

The room went very still. The radiator ticked. Somewhere outside, the gravel truck had come and gone, and Theo had missed it, and was obscurely glad.

"I don't know what you mean," Theo said. "My grandmother died before I was born. Ruth's mother. I never—there's no one who could have told you what she was like."

"I didn't say anyone told me." Adaora set down her pen and folded her hands, and Theo saw for the first time how the age lived in them, the knuckles swollen like river stones, the skin fine as tissue over all that architecture. "I knew her. Edith. She came to the Saturday classes. 1958, 1959. She sat where you're standing and she wrote her words on the backs of seed packets because she was too proud to ask for proper cards." A small movement at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile and was much more than one. "She had that same stillness. Like the words had to be let in through a door you kept almost shut. I used to think she was afraid of them. Later I thought, no. She was afraid of what she'd do if she opened the door all the way."

Theo's hand had found the edge of the table without her permission. She stood there, a woman who measured everything, and could not have said what her pulse was doing, only that it had changed key.

"You're mistaken," she said, and heard how flat it came out, how exactly like her mother's voice, which was exactly like the voice the whole family used for anything that mattered. "I think you've confused me with someone. I should take the drawings and go."

"Take the drawings," Adaora agreed mildly, and turned back to her cards, and that was somehow worse than an argument. "The gray boxes go back on the third shelf, spine out. Kowalczyk would want his hesitations kept in order."

* * *

Theo did not leave.

She told herself she was staying to finish the rebar schedule, and she did finish it, and then she told herself she was staying to cross-check the concrete pour dates against the county's maintenance ledger, which was a real task and took a real hour, and then it was past four and the light in the annex had gone from amber to the color of weak tea, and Adaora rose from her table with the unhurried verticality of a woman for whom rising was a decision rather than an ordeal, and began, card by card, to put her day's work away in the wooden drawers of a cabinet that had once held something else entirely—Theo could see the ghost of an old label, *VITAL STATISTICS*, beneath a strip of masking tape that now read, in Adaora's hand, *THE WORDS*.

Theo watched her file them. Each card slid into its place with a sound like a breath being held and released. And Theo understood, with the suddenness of a load finding a new path, that this was not a hobby. This was a woman building a structure. This was a woman who had looked at the fact of her own dying and responded the only way a certain kind of person responds: by measuring, by recording, by making sure that when the thing failed, the drawings would survive it.

Theo knew that response from the inside. She had just never seen it aimed at anything so large.

"Mrs. Nwosu." The name came out of her before she had decided to say it. Adaora turned. In the last light her face was half gold, half shadow, and Theo thought: *ninety-one years old, and she is the only person in this county doing work that cannot be redone.* "Why cards? Why not a tape recorder, a computer, something—"

"Something that dies when the power goes out?" Adaora said. "Something a child in fifty years can't hold in her hand?" She closed the drawer. "A card is a sentence you can carry. My mother taught me with cards. Her mother before her. The palm oil traders' wives carried words on scraps of cloth from village to village." She picked up her magnifying glass, her cold tea, her tin of mints, and looked at Theo with that customs-officer gaze one last time. "You'll come back."

"I don't—"

"You will. Not for the drawings. Those are done. You'll come back because you stood at that cart for four minutes over a word that wasn't yours, and people who do that are always hungry, and they always think they're the only one." She moved toward the door, small and straight and certain. "Tuesday. I'm here at nine. Don't bring flowers. Bring a notebook. And Theo—"

Theo had not told her her name. Theo was certain of it, certain the way she was certain of load paths, certain down to the bolts. She stood in the amber room with her tablet and her gray boxes and the word *ìhè* still sitting in her chest like a stone placed deliberately, a stone with a purpose, and waited.

"Your grandmother kept her door almost shut her whole life," Adaora said, "and it cost her. I watched it cost her. Don't make me watch it cost you."

Then she was gone, and the annex was just a room again, and Theo stood there in the settling dust and the ticking radiator, a woman who measured everything and said almost nothing, unable for the life of her to write down a single number.

* * *

That night Marcus made chicken and rice, the Thursday meal, and asked about her day in the way he always asked—gently, briefly, a question offered like a coat held out, expected to be declined. "Records office," Theo said. "Old drawings. They're in good shape." And he nodded, and the silence came down over the table the way it had come down for fifteen years, comfortable, maintained, hers.

But she had brought the notebook home. She hadn't meant to; she'd stopped at the drugstore on Delmore on the way back and bought it without letting herself examine the impulse, the way she did most things, and now it lay on the counter by her keys, and she could feel it the way you feel a crack you haven't reported yet.

On the first page, before she could stop herself, she wrote the only entry the day had left her:

*ìhè — a thing; a matter that concerns someone. "It is my matter." Said when claiming a burden as one's own.*

She looked at it a long time. In the living room, Marcus turned the television down low, considerate even in his solitude, and the house held its breath around them both, and Theo closed the notebook on the word like a door kept almost shut.

Tuesday, then. She would go back Tuesday. Just to see the cards.
