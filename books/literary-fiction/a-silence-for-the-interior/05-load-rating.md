## 5. Load Rating

The report was due on a Tuesday, which Theo considered an insult. Reports of consequence should come due on Fridays, when there was at least the mercy of a weekend between the writing and the living with it. Instead she had Tuesday, gray and ordinary, the kind of day that asked nothing of anyone, and she had to ask everything of herself.

She had been drafting it for eleven days. The software on her county laptop was a decade out of date, the interface the color of old dishwater, and she moved through its forms the way she moved through most things: completely, without hurry, leaving no field blank. Girder G-7. Weld toe, west face, 340 millimeters from bearing. Hairline, longitudinal, propagating laterally. Measured length at inspection: 41 millimeters. She had the photographs open in a second window, blown up until the crack looked like a river delta seen from orbit, branching, patient, alive in the way that steel fatigue is alive—not moving, exactly, but *going* somewhere.

Forty-one millimeters. She had measured it three times with three different gauges because that was who she was. Forty-one, forty-one point five, forty-one. The software wanted a number. The software always wanted a number, and the number would become a rating, and the rating would become a recommendation, and the recommendation would become, somewhere up the chain in a conference room she would never see, a decision about whether eleven thousand vehicles a day kept crossing the Kestrel River Bridge or whether, one morning in late May, they didn't.

She typed 41 into the field. Then she sat with her hands in her lap and looked at it.

* * *

The problem was the model. Not her model—the model, the load rating analysis she'd run twice, the finite element simulation that chewed on the bridge's geometry and spat out a factor of 1.34 against the AASHTO legal loads. Anything above 1.0 was, in the language of her profession, *adequate*. The bridge could carry what the law said it must carry, with a third again to spare. On paper, the Kestrel was fine.

But the model didn't know about the crack. Or rather, she hadn't told it. A hairline at a weld toe was a fatigue indicator, not a capacity reduction—not yet, not at 41 millimeters, not in a girder with that much redundancy around it. She could defend 1.34. She could defend it in a hearing, in front of a state engineer with a gray beard and a legal pad, and she would win, because the numbers were the numbers and she had never in fifteen years fudged a number in her life.

That was the thing she kept circling, the way her thumb circled the rim of her coffee mug without her noticing: she hadn't fudged anything. Not yet. The crack was real and the rating was real and the two of them coexisted honestly in the report she was building, field by field, like stones in a wall.

What she was deciding, in the small hours of that Tuesday morning, was what to do with the *recommendation*.

* * *

The festival was in six weeks. Kestrel Days, three days in June, the whole town turned out along the riverfront—craft stalls and a pie contest and the volunteer fire department's fish fry and, on Saturday night, fireworks launched from the old rail spur, the crowd packed shoulder to shoulder on the bridge itself because it was the only vantage point, and everyone agreed it was the best seat in the county. The bridge wasn't just a crossing. It was the town's front porch. It was where the teenagers went to smoke and the old men went to argue and the church group went to watch the water rise in spring. When the state had floated a pedestrian-only restriction during her first year on the job, the county commissioner had called her office personally, a man she'd never met, his voice warm and reasonable and absolutely immovable, and the restriction had quietly died in a drawer.

She thought about the crowd. Eleven thousand vehicles a day was the weekday count; festival Saturday would be different, a static load of human bodies, dense as gravel, plus the fireworks barge traffic on the river below and the food trucks idling at the approaches. She had run that scenario too, at home, after Marcus went to bed, the laptop light blue on her face in the dark kitchen. The number had come back 1.19. Still adequate. Still above one. But the margin had narrowed, and the crack would not have narrowed with it. The crack would be doing what cracks do, which is work in the dark, invisibly, at the molecular scale where metal gives up one bond at a time.

She could write the recommendation three ways.

*Recommend no restrictions.* The honest-to-the-model option. The bridge rated 1.34; the festival was within capacity; end of report. If the crack grew—and it might, hairline fatigue cracks at old welds were notoriously unpredictable, some propagating a few millimeters a year, some sitting dormant for decades, some running overnight—it would grow under a rating she had certified as adequate with full knowledge that a fatigue indicator existed and went unmonitored.

*Recommend closure.* The honest-to-the-crack option. Restrict the bridge to emergency vehicles pending a fracture-critical reinspection with ultrasonic testing, which meant the state, which meant months, which meant no festival, which meant the fish fry in the church basement and the fireworks from the levee and the town's summer, its one good summer, its identity, spent in the flat disappointment of *not this year*. And it meant her name on the closure order. It meant the commissioner's phone call, warmer this time, and then less warm. It meant the word *alarmist* traveling through the county offices the way water travels through limestone—slowly, invisibly, dissolving things.

Or the third way. The way she was already writing before she admitted she was writing it.

*Recommend monitoring.* Quarterly visual inspection of G-7, documented, dated, signed. Note the crack. Describe it precisely—she was good at describing things precisely, it was the one fluency she had—characterize it as *non-structural at present, subject to continued observation*. Let the words do what words do when you arrange them carefully, which is tell the truth and still arrive somewhere else.

*Non-structural at present.* Every word of it true. The crack was non-structural at present. She would know, each quarter, exactly how non-structural it remained. She would be watching it more closely than anyone had ever watched anything on that bridge. If it grew past a threshold—and she set the threshold in her own head, 100 millimeters, no, 75, she would write 75 on a card and put the card in her wallet—she would escalate. She would close it herself, personally, consequences be damned.

It was, she told herself, the engineering judgment call she was paid to make. Redundant girder system. Adequate load rating. Fatigue indicator under active monitoring. There were textbooks that would back her up. There were bridges all over the country carrying heavier traffic with worse cracks and no one watching at all.

She typed it. Her fingers moved on the keyboard with the same unhurried completeness she brought to everything, and that was the worst part, that her hands did not know the difference.

* * *

At lunch she drove out to the bridge and walked it, hardhat on, clipboard under her arm, in the flat white light of early afternoon. Traffic passed her in a steady wind of displaced air, each truck a small earthquake in the deck plates, and she felt the vibrations through her boots and catalogued them without meaning to. Expansion joint three, sounding dull. Joint four, fine. The river below was high and brown with spring rain, carrying last year's fields toward the sea.

She stood over G-7. You couldn't see the crack from the deck; you had to climb down the ladder to the inspection catwalk, into the girder's shadow, where the steel smelled of rust and pigeon droppings and old paint. Her headlamp found the weld toe. She put the gauge to it, out of ritual, out of a need to touch the thing with an instrument rather than only her eyes.

Forty-one millimeters.

She had known it would be. It had been eleven days. But she had needed to know, the way Marcus needed to hear her say things she'd already shown him a hundred times, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome and she stood with it in the girder's shadow, breathing rust-smell, until a truck crossed overhead and the whole bridge hummed like a struck tuning fork.

*Non-structural at present.*

She thought about her mother, briefly, the way she'd been thinking about her a lot lately—Ruth Oyelaran, dead four years, a woman who had answered every direct question of Theo's childhood with a precise and total non-answer, a woman who could discuss the weather for twenty minutes and never once say what she meant. Theo had inherited the weather. She had built a career, a marriage, a whole load-bearing life out of the weather.

The bridge hummed and settled. She climbed back up into the light.

* * *

Marcus asked about it at dinner, which was unusual, and that was how she knew the county gossip had already started. He'd heard something at the hardware store—Dale Kowalczyk's cousin worked in the commissioner's office—and the something was that the Kestrel inspection had come back and there was talk.

"Talk of what?" Marcus asked. He was pushing peas around his plate in the small circular motions he used when he was assembling a sentence.

"Routine stuff," Theo said. "Wear items. Deck sealing."

"Talk of closing it?"

"No."

It wasn't even a lie. No one was talking of closing it. That was the point of the report she'd written, the point of the careful words, the point of the whole architecture of the thing: that no one would talk of closing it, and the not-talking would hold, and under the not-talking the crack would do its work in the dark, and she would watch it, quarterly, with her gauge, and the watching would be her penance and her alibi at once.

Marcus nodded and ate his peas and asked nothing further, because asking nothing further was the accommodation they had built together over fifteen years, the expansion joint in their marriage, the engineered gap that let two structures move without grinding each other apart. She had always thought of it as a strength. Sitting there with the report finished and submitted and sitting in some server in the county basement, she thought of it for the first time as what it also was: a gap. Something that let things through. Something that, if you never maintained it, never cleaned the debris out of it, would seize up solid, and then the structure would have nowhere to go when the temperature changed, and the temperature always changed, and something would have to give.

"The festival committee wants a banner across the deck this year," Marcus said. "Big one. I told them you'd know the load limits for that kind of thing."

"A banner's nothing," Theo said. "A few pounds per square foot."

"Good." He smiled at her, and it was a real smile, warm, the smile of a man who had no idea he was eating dinner with the person who had just decided his town's summer, and she smiled back, and the smile cost her something she could not name and did not measure.

* * *

She did not sleep well, which was also new. She had always been a good sleeper; her body treated unconsciousness the way it treated everything, as a task to be completed efficiently. But that night she lay in the dark running the numbers again, 1.34 and 1.19 and 75 millimeters on a card in her wallet, and somewhere past two in the morning she got up and went to the kitchen and opened the laptop and pulled up the report she'd submitted, read it end to end, and found it flawless.

That was the thing. It was flawless. Every statement in it was true. The crack was characterized accurately. The rating was correct. The monitoring program was, if anything, more rigorous than the county standard. A reviewer would find nothing. A hearing would find nothing. She had told the truth in every particular and arranged the truths so that they would produce a lie, the way a load path produces a deflection, and no one would ever be able to point at any single member and say *there, that's the failure*.

Except her.

She sat in the dark kitchen with the laptop light on her face and understood that she had done something she could not undo, not because it was irreversible but because undoing it would require the one thing she had spent fifteen years and a whole career avoiding: saying, out loud, to another person, *I made a mistake, and here is why, and the why is not flattering.* She could amend the report. She could call the state office in the morning and request a fracture-critical review and let the machinery grind forward and take what came. But the amendment would raise the question of the original, and the question of the original would raise her, and she would have to stand in some room and explain the gap between what she knew and what she wrote, and she did not know how to stand in rooms and explain gaps. She knew how to measure them.

She thought of Adaora Nwosu, absurdly, there in the dark—she hadn't thought of the woman in weeks, not since the records office, not since *you have your grandmother's way of listening*, a sentence she had turned over maybe four hundred times and never once mentioned to anyone. The old woman cataloguing her cards, a language with no other speakers, a whole vocabulary of things that could only be said one way, to no one, forever. What did a person do, Theo wondered, when she was the last one who knew a thing? Did the knowing get lighter or heavier, at the end, when there was no one left to check it against?

She closed the laptop. The kitchen went dark.

In the wallet in her purse by the door, on an index card in her small square handwriting, the number 75 sat in the dark like a pilot light. She would check the crack in June, after the festival, before the festival, both. She would check it in her sleep if she had to. The bridge would hold. It was rated to hold. She had made sure of it, in the only way she knew how, which was to make the numbers say so.

She went back to bed and lay beside Marcus, listening to him breathe, and told herself the story she would be telling herself for a long time after: *it's monitoring, it's prudent, it's fine.* The words were true the way the report was true, every particular of them, arranged.

Three days later, a letter arrived at the county office, and then a phone call, and Theo's name was in both.
