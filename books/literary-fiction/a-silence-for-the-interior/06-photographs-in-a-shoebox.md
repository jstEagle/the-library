## 6. Photographs in a Shoebox

The house on Delmore Street smelled the way it always had—cedar and old carpet and, beneath it, something faintly mineral, like water left standing in a kettle. Ruth Oyelaran had been dead eleven weeks, and the smell had not changed. Theo noticed this every time she came through the door: that a house could hold its dead so completely, could keep her in the walls and the baseboards and the particular way the second stair creaked, without any of it meaning anything.

Marcus had offered to come. He'd stood in their kitchen that morning with his keys in his hand, turning them over the way he did when he wanted to be useful but didn't know how to say so, and he'd said, "I could drive you out there. Two hands are better than one for boxes."

"I work faster alone," Theo said, which was true, and also not the reason.

He nodded and put his keys down and made her coffee in the travel mug instead, and pressed it into her hands at the door, and said, "Call me if—" and then didn't finish the sentence, because neither of them had ever been good at finishing sentences like that. She kissed his cheek, which was their version of *yes*, and drove the forty minutes to Delmore Street with the mug steaming in the cup holder and the radio off.

Her mother had lived in the same house for fifty-one years. She had moved into it as a bride, at twenty-three, with a man named Gerald Oyelaran whom Theo had loved the way you love a wall—reliably, without thinking much about it—and she had died in it at seventy-eight, in the downstairs bedroom, with the hospice nurse holding one hand and Theo holding the other, and her last coherent words had been about the garden hose. *Turn off the spigot before it freezes.* Not I love you. Not forgive me. The spigot. Theo had turned it off. It was the last thing she ever did for her mother, and she thought about it more often than she wanted to.

The estate lawyer had given her until the end of the month. The house would go to a young couple from the county seat who wanted the oak trees, and everything inside it had to be sorted, kept, sold, or hauled away. Theo had broken the job into tasks, because that was how she survived everything: the attic first, then the bedroom closets, then the kitchen, then the terrible arithmetic of the photographs.

She started in the attic at nine in the morning, in work gloves, with a legal pad on which she wrote inventory lines in her small square handwriting. *Box 1: Christmas ornaments, glass, 14 intact, 3 broken. Box 2: Gerald's military records, medals (2), discharge papers. Box 3: linens, moth damage.*

It was good work. It was the kind of work that asked nothing of her except accuracy. She measured the boxes, stacked them by weight, noted which floorboards flexed under load. Somewhere below her, the house ticked and settled in the October cold, and Theo felt the familiar peace of being inside a structure she understood.

The shoebox was under the eaves, behind a trunk she'd catalogued an hour earlier, wedged where the roofline dipped low enough that a person had to kneel and reach into the dark to get it. It was a woman's shoebox, pale blue, the cardboard gone soft at the corners, and across the lid someone had written—in fountain pen, in a hand Theo did not recognize, a hand too loose and looping to be her mother's careful print—the word *Keep*.

She knelt there a long moment with the box in her lap before she opened it. Later she would try to reconstruct why. There was no reason not to open it; it was her mother's attic, her mother's things, her inheritance entire. But some part of her, the part that read cracks in steel, registered the word *Keep* the way she registered a hairline fracture—not as failure, not yet, but as information. Something here had been set apart. Set apart meant it mattered. Things that mattered in her family were usually heavy.

She lifted the lid.

Photographs. Maybe sixty of them, loose and sleeved, mixed decades, mixed sizes. On top, a funeral card for a woman named Beatrice Nwosu, 1911–1989, with a line of text beneath the name in a script Theo couldn't read—characters that looked like they'd been designed to carry sound the way trusses carried weight, economical and certain.

Theo sat back on her heels.

*Nwosu.* She knew the name now, though three months ago she wouldn't have. Adaora Nwosu. The woman from the records office, ninety-one years old, cataloguing vocabulary cards for a language that lived nowhere else. *You have your grandmother's way of listening,* Adaora had said to her, and Theo had walked out of that building unsettled in a way she hadn't been able to file anywhere, because her grandmother was a woman named Edith Hale from two counties over, a woman of flour sacks and hymnals, and no one had ever accused Edith Hale of listening.

She turned the funeral card over. On the back, in pencil, faded almost to nothing: *B. Nwosu — Emeka's mother — Ruth's second mother, she said.*

*Ruth.* Her mother's name, sitting there in a stranger's sentence like a key fitted to a lock Theo hadn't known her house contained.

She began going through the photographs properly then, gloved fingers suddenly clumsy, laying them out on the plywood decking in rows the way she laid out inspection findings. And the past came apart in her hands.

* * *

Here is what Theo knew of her mother's youth, the official version, the version served at holidays and funerals: Ruth Hale had grown up on a farm outside Harlow Bend, married Gerald Oyelaran at twenty-three, worked thirty years as a bookkeeper at the feed co-op, raised one daughter, gardened ferociously, and never once, in all that time, done anything unaccounted for. Her life had the structural integrity of a well-built culvert. Water went through it. Nothing shifted.

Here is what the photographs said:

Ruth at seventeen, laughing—actually laughing, head thrown back, caught mid-motion in a way none of the family albums ever captured her, because in the family albums Ruth posed, Ruth performed composure, Ruth held her chin at the angle that said *all is well*. In this photograph she was standing on the rail bridge over the Kestrel—that same bridge, Theo realized, her stomach doing something complicated—the same green steel, the same riveted girder line, forty years younger. And beside her, arm hooked through hers, was a young Black man in a white shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, grinning at whatever had made Ruth laugh, his other hand shading his eyes against a sun that fell, in the photograph, across both their faces equally.

He was beautiful. That was the first thing, and Theo hated herself a little for it being the first thing, but it was true—he had the kind of face that made a photograph feel underlit even in full sun. Tall, long-limbed, one shoulder higher than the other where he leaned into Ruth. On the back, in that same looping hand: *E & R, June '71. He says the river is older than the town. I believe him.*

*E.* Theo's mouth was dry. She reached for the travel mug Marcus had filled and drank coffee gone lukewarm and bitter, and kept sorting.

Ruth and E at a church picnic, 1972, sitting on a tailgate, sharing something from a paper bag, both of them mid-laugh again. Ruth and E at what was unmistakably a dance—a gymnasium, crepe paper, other kids blurred at the edges of the frame—his hand at her waist, her hand on his shoulder, both of them looking at each other instead of the camera with an expression Theo recognized from the inside of her own marriage and had never once seen on her mother's face. It was the look of two people who are speaking fluently in a language no one around them knows.

There were photographs without dates. Ruth on a porch steps that might have been the Nwosu place—Theo had seen the house once, driving past, the wide veranda, the pecan tree. A woman Theo guessed was Beatrice Nwosu, stern and splendid, arms folded, with young Ruth beside her learning something from her hands—kneading dough, or shaping clay, or signing, Theo couldn't tell. A boy's bicycle against a fence post. A riverbank in autumn.

And then, near the bottom of the box, a photograph that stopped Theo's hands entirely.

Ruth, maybe nineteen, seated on the running board of a pickup truck, and she was crying—not elegantly, not for a camera, but with her whole face ruined and open, and the young man—E, it had to be E, though he was thinner here, harder, the beauty gone taut—crouched in front of her, holding both her hands, looking up at her with an expression that Theo, who had spent fifteen years reading faces for what they withheld, read instantly and completely.

He was saying goodbye. Or he was saying *wait*. One of the two, and the photograph knew which, even if Theo didn't.

On the back, in pencil, pressed hard enough to groove the paper: *They can't stop a river. They can only make it go underground.*

* * *

She sat in the attic until the light through the gable window went from white to amber, and the temperature dropped, and her knees ached against the decking, and she did not move. Below her, the house ticked. Somewhere a truck passed on Delmore Street, and the windows shivered in their frames, and Theo ran load calculations on her own history and found them not adding up.

Emeka. The name surfaced from the records office the way bodies surface in rivers—slowly, unwillingly, after everyone has stopped watching the water. Adaora Nwosu had spoken of her son exactly once in their two conversations, and briefly, the way you step over a gap in a floorboard you've learned the location of by heart. *My son Emeka was the better ear. He heard the language in his sleep.* Past tense. No elaboration. Theo had filed it, the way she filed everything, under *not mine to ask about*.

But she remembered now—remembered the town's own sediment, the stories that settled at the bottom of Harlow Bend and got stirred up only at bars and funerals. A Nwosu boy, the brilliant one, the one who'd gone away to school and come back talking about things. Gone again in '74. Nobody agreed on where. Nobody agreed on why. The versions diverged like a crack propagating through steel: he'd gotten a girl pregnant; he'd gotten in trouble with the law; he'd been run off by men whose names everyone knew and no one said. What everyone agreed on was the shape of the leaving—sudden, total, no forwarding address, a family diminished overnight and a silence laid over the whole business thick enough to build on.

1974. Theo did the arithmetic the way she did all arithmetic, automatically, helplessly. Her mother had been twenty-six. Married four years. Theo herself would arrive in 1976.

Which meant that Ruth Hale had spent her girlhood with her arm through the arm of Adaora Nwosu's son, and had then married Gerald Oyelaran, and had then raised a daughter in a house where none of it—not the laughter, not the river, not the ruined-cry photograph, not the woman called *second mother*—was ever mentioned, not once, not in fifty-one years, not even at the end, when the dying loosen people's tongues and the spigot is the last thing on your mind.

*They can't stop a river. They can only make it go underground.*

Theo thought about underground water. She thought about artesian pressure, about springs that surfaced miles from their source, about how a hydrologist could read a landscape and know what moved beneath it by what grew on top—the willows, the rushes, the green that had no business being green. She thought about herself, kneeling in her dead mother's attic, fluent in measurement and mute in everything else, and felt the ground shift under the whole edifice of who she'd believed she was.

She had thought her silence was hers. Built by her, maintained by her, hers to manage like any other asset. Fifteen years of almost with Marcus, and she'd assumed the design flaw was local—her defect, her responsibility, her private fatigue crack to monitor and understress. But here was the original drawing. Here was the as-built plan, dated decades before her birth: a woman who loved loudly and was taught, thoroughly, by everyone with power over her life, to pour concrete over the sound. Ruth hadn't passed down her mother's laugh. She'd passed down the silencing of it. Theo was not the engineer of her own reticence. She was the inheritor of a retrofit.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. Marcus. She looked at his name on the screen for a long time—the letters steady, familiar, load-bearing—and did not answer, and did not know whether the not-answering was habit or symptom, anymore.

* * *

Near the bottom of the box, beneath the crying photograph, was an envelope. Unsealed. Empty, except for a slip of paper folded in quarters, and when Theo unfolded it she found not a letter but a list—words written in that looping hand, paired with words in the strange carved-looking script she'd seen on the funeral card. A vocabulary list. *Water. Road. Stay. Go.* English on the left, the other language on the right, in a child's careful study-hand.

In the margin, small, half-erased, as if written and then rubbed at with a thumb: *His language. Learn it so I can—*

The sentence stopped. Fifty years ago it had stopped, and it was still stopping, and Theo sat in the failing light holding her mother's unfinished sentence and understood that she was holding the oldest crack in the structure—the one everything else had settled over, the one every floorboard creak above it had been warning about.

Adaora Nwosu had looked at her across a county records desk and said, *You have your grandmother's way of listening*, and Theo had thought it a mistake, an old woman's confusion. But Adaora hadn't meant Edith Hale. Adaora had looked at Theo's face—the face Theo now saw, in photograph after photograph, mirrored in a laughing girl on a rail bridge—and Adaora had known exactly whose daughter was standing in front of her. Had known, perhaps, the moment Theo walked in. Had said the one sentence calculated to make a woman like Theo stay in the room.

Theo gathered the photographs back into the box with hands that were not quite steady, squared the edges, replaced the lid with the word *Keep* facing up. She carried it down the attic stairs, past the ticking rooms, out to the car, and set it on the passenger seat and buckled the seatbelt around it, which she had never done with a box of anything in her life.

Then she sat in the driveway of her childhood home in the dark, engine idling, and took out her phone, and found the number the county had given her—the outreach contact for the Nwosu language documentation project, the number she had entered three months ago and never dialed.

Her thumb hovered over it. Underground water, she thought. You can't stop it. You can only decide where you'll let it surface.

She pressed call.
