## 7. The Grammar of Leaving

The girl comes back on a Tuesday, which is how I know she was always going to come back. People who refuse on a Monday and return on a Tuesday have already lost the argument with themselves; they simply need you to pretend the argument never happened so they can keep their dignity. I am ninety-one years old. I have had a great deal of practice pretending.

She stands in my doorway holding her clipboard against her chest like a shield, like a book of psalms, like a thing that has never once protected anyone.

"I'm not here for lessons," Theo Oyelaran says.

"Of course not," I say, and step aside to let her in.

* * *

I learned English at a mission school where the sisters believed God spoke only in the future tense—everything promised, nothing delivered—and so I have always known that every language is a theology, whether it admits it or not. The language of my mother's people, the one now living nowhere but inside my skull and in three shoeboxes of index cards, has a different theology. It does not promise. It does not even, strictly speaking, describe. It *points*. Every sentence is a hand extended toward a thing, and the listener must decide whether to take it.

This is why it is dying, probably. A language that requires you to reach will starve in a country full of folded arms.

Theo sits in the chair by the window—the good chair, the one Emeka sat in when he was small enough to fit in it whole—and she looks at my vocabulary cards spread across the table like a patient waiting for diagnosis. She does not touch them. Her hands stay flat on her knees. This is a woman who has trained herself out of reaching, I think. Someone taught her early that hands extended get cut.

"Water," I say, and hold up the card. On it, in my own handwriting, which has gone from copperplate to something like bird tracks over ninety years: *mmí*.

"That's it? Just—water?"

"Just water. Say it."

"Mi."

"*Mmí.* The m swallows itself. Like this—" and I make the sound, lips closed, a hum in the nose, water held behind a dam. "Water held. Then released." I open my lips on the vowel and let it spill.

She watches my mouth the way she must watch girders. Total attention. No expression. I would bet my last good year that no one has ever watched her mouth like that either—not her husband, not her mother, not God himself—and that some starving part of her has been waiting for it without knowing its own name.

"Mmí," she says.

Better. Not perfect. Perfect does not matter; perfect is a mission-school disease. What matters is that she said it as if the word were a place she could be.

"Again."

"Mmí."

"Good. Now: road."

* * *

We do not agree to anything, she and I. That is important. If we agreed, she would have to break the agreement or honor it, and either way there would be a document between us, and this woman has spent her whole life filing documents instead of feelings. So there is no agreement. There is only a Tuesday, and then a Thursday, and then a Saturday, and then I stop keeping track because the days have begun to arrive on their own, carrying her.

I teach her the way rain teaches a roof—one drop at a time, no hurry, total confidence that the roof will eventually give.

Road: *ụzọ*. Stay: *nọ*. Go: *gaa*.

It takes her four visits to ask the question I have been waiting for since the first card.

"Why are these the first words?" she says. "Most language courses start with hello. Please. Thank you."

"Do you say please and thank you to your husband?"

Her face does something very small and very expensive. "Yes."

"And does he hear it?"

She looks at the window. The river is visible from my house—I chose this house fifty years ago for exactly that reason, though everyone assumed it was for the light—and the Kestrel moves under its bridge the way it has moved since before any of us were a rumor.

"In my language," I tell her, "you do not begin with greetings. Greetings are what you say when you intend to leave soon. We begin with what stays. Water. Road. Stay. Go. Everything else is built on those four. You cannot greet someone until you know whether you are staying or going, and you cannot know that until you know where the road is, and you cannot find the road without water to follow. That is the order of things. Hello comes last, if it comes at all."

"That's backwards," she says, but softly, the way people say *that's impossible* when they mean *I think it might be true*.

"It is not backwards. It is only older than the habit of leaving."

* * *

Here is what I have decided about grief, after nine decades of close study: grief is grammatical. It has tenses. There is the grief that happened, which fades into the past like everything else. There is the grief happening now, which is merely pain and can be endured. But the dangerous grief, the one that eats the load-bearing walls of a person, is the grief in the future tense—the loss you rehearse daily, the goodbye you keep saying in advance so that when it comes it will find you already practiced.

I have been saying goodbye to my language for thirty years. One word at a time, the way I am teaching her. Except in reverse. She gains *mmí*, *ụzọ*, *nọ*, *gaa*. I lose. Last month I lost the word for *threshing floor*. Two weeks ago, the word for a specific kind of laughter—the kind that starts as anger and gives up halfway through and becomes something else entirely. I knew that word for eighty-eight years. I woke one morning and it was gone, and what remained was the shape of it, the feeling of it, an empty room in the mouth.

Some mornings I open my eyes and conduct an inventory, the way she inspects her bridge. Load-bearing words first. Mother. River. Yes. No. My own name—still there, thank whatever remains to thank. Then the smaller structures. Yesterday I found that *umbrella* had collapsed in the night. I remember umbrellas. I remember the word existing. But when I reach for it, my hand closes on air.

So understand me: when I began teaching Theo Oyelaran, I was not being generous. Generosity implies surplus. I was dividing a diminishing estate while the heir still stood in the room.

And I chose her not because she asked—she never asked—but because of what happened on her third visit.

* * *

On her third visit I gave her the word for *stay*, *nọ*, and told her it was also the root of the word for *sit*, and *dwell*, and *remain*, and—to her visible surprise—*belong*. One sound, many houses.

"Nọ," she repeated. And then, without deciding to, I could see, without her permission being granted by any part of her conscious mind, she said: "My husband asks me every morning if I slept well. Fifteen years. And I have never once answered him."

The clock ticked. The river moved. Somewhere a truck crossed the bridge and I felt it in the floorboards, faint as a pulse through a wall.

"Why not?" I asked. Not gently. Gentleness is sometimes just cowardice wearing a shawl.

"Because he doesn't actually want to know. He wants the answer that keeps the morning moving." She said it flatly, the way she would read a measurement off a gauge. "So I give him that answer. 'Fine.' 'Slept fine.' And then we both go to work."

"And what is the true answer?"

She looked at me then—really looked, the first time—and I saw the crack. Not in her face. In the thing behind her face, the structure underneath, the girder that has been carrying more than it was rated for.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the terrible part. I've given the false answer so long I'd have to inspect myself to find the true one. And I don't—" Her voice did something. Almost nothing. A millimeter of movement. "I don't know how to do that."

"Yes you do," I said. "You're doing it right now. It's called speaking."

* * *

Now I will tell you about Emeka, because she has earned it, and because telling it is the other half of why I summoned her into my life—though *summoned* is too strong. *Permitted* is better. I permitted her to come near, and she came, and now the story is owed.

Emeka was born in this house. In the front room, in 1948, on a night the river flooded its lower banks and my husband Chukwuemeka Senior—dead eleven years now, and I still set two cups out some mornings, old habit, old grammar—carried the midwife through the water on his back. Our son came into the world during a flood, and I should have understood then that water would be the medium of all his comings and goings.

He was a boy who talked the way other boys ran—constantly, joyfully, falling down and getting up talking. By six he had the whole language in his mouth like a songbird. By ten he was correcting my tones, laughing, giving me that laughter whose word I have now lost. He learned English at the mission school and wore it lightly, bilingually, a man with two rooms in his mouth and lights on in both.

And then Ruth.

Ruth Calloway. The banker's daughter. I knew her before Theo ever existed, which is a strange sentence to be able to write, and stranger to have lived. She used to come to the library where Emeka worked summers, and I would see them through the window—two young heads bent over the same book, and the space between their shoulders getting smaller week by week, the way it does, the way it always does, gravity being older than propriety.

They were going to leave together. I want that written somewhere besides my dissolving memory: they had a plan. California, he said. He had a scholarship letter. He showed it to me at this table, right here, and his voice shook, and I touched his face and said the word for *go* the way you say it to a child on the first day of school—proud and terrified in equal measure, *gaa*, go, the road is yours.

Then Ruth's father found out. And what happened after that—what was said in which office, which threats were made, which futures were priced and purchased—I have my suspicions, and my suspicions have kept me alive with anger for fifty years, but suspicions are not testimony, and I am too old to perjure myself even privately.

What I know is this: in September of 1974, Emeka left alone. He left at night. He left without saying goodbye in our language, because our language has no true goodbye—it has only *ka ọ dị*, *until it is so*, *until we meet again*, and he could not bring himself to lie to me even in the grammar. He said the English word. *Bye, Ma.* Bye. As if he were going to the store.

He wrote for two years. Letters from Los Angeles, then Oakland, then a town I could never find on any map. Then the letters stopped, and I have spent fifty years not knowing whether he stopped writing because he died, or prospered, or simply discovered—as Ruth's father must have promised him—that some doors, once walked through, are easier to leave closed.

I never explained this to Theo. Not yet. I gave her the shape of it: a son, a departure, a silence. She listened the way she listens—with her whole body and none of her face—and at the end she said, quietly:

"You don't have to tell me why he left."

"No," I agreed. "I don't."

"But someday you might."

"Someday," I said, "you might already know more than you think you do."

She frowned at that, and I let her frown. A student must be allowed her unsolved equations. Besides, the photograph will surface on its own; photographs always do, they are the most patient things in a house.

* * *

Today she stayed three hours. Today she brought her own cards—she has begun making her own cards, in her precise inspector's handwriting, each word ruled and numbered, and I pretend not to be moved and am moved past all measuring.

We worked on sentences. Simple ones. *Mmị na-aga n'ụzọ*—the water goes along the road. She built it slowly, laying each word like stone, checking the joints.

"It's strange," she said, setting down her pencil. "When I think in English, my thoughts are mostly questions I'm not asking. But when I think in—in your language—it's like the thoughts are just... statements. Things that are true. The water goes along the road. The road stays. I keep thinking things like that, and they feel like—" she searched, this woman who measures everything, for a unit of measure— "like load paths. Like weight finding its way to ground."

I laughed—that ordinary laughter, the kind I still have the word for—and covered her hand with mine.

Her hand flinched. Then turned over. Then held on.

That is the whole lesson, child. That is the entire curriculum. Everything else is vocabulary.

* * *

After she left, I sat by the window until dark, doing my inventory. River: present. Daughter—no, I never had a daughter; the word surfaced anyway, unbidden, testing a door in a house I thought I knew. Student: present, and newly load-bearing.

And then, in the blue hour, another word arrived, one I had not reached for in decades, and it arrived complete, intact, shining like a coin pulled from a riverbed:

*Ruth.*

Not the word—the name. Her name, in my mouth again after fifty years, and behind it, rising with it like silt disturbed, the memory I have kept longest and shared least: the last time I saw Ruth Calloway, three days after Emeka fled, standing at my gate in the rain without an umbrella, her father's car idling at the curb, saying my name in my own language—saying it perfectly, tones and all, though I had never taught her a single word—

And what she said to me at that gate. Which I have carried like a stone in the load path all these years.

Which Theo Oyelaran will need to hear, sooner than either of us is ready—because tomorrow, I have decided, I will show her the shoebox, and the photograph, and the face of the boy her mother loved, and watch the ground shift under everything she believes she inherited.

The water goes along the road. The road stays. Nothing else does.
