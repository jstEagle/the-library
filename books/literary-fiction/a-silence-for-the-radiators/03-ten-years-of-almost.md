## 3. Ten Years of Almost

Daniel Okafor—no, that wasn't right either, he was Daniel Reyes, and the fact that he had to correct himself in his own head, ten years into this marriage, told him something about how carefully he'd learned to hold his wife's name without ever quite gripping it—stood at the kitchen counter with a lemon and a knife and rehearsed.

*Hazel. I've been thinking about us. About whether we ever actually decided not to have kids, or whether we just... let it happen by not happening.*

No. Too much like an audit. She'd hear the ledger in it, the columns being balanced, and she'd do what she always did when he brought anything heavier than salt to the table: she'd go quiet in a way that wasn't absence but presence, a full-body listening that made him feel like a specimen under glass, and then she'd say something precise and deflecting like *the roast needs ten more minutes,* and the moment would close over like water.

He tried again, slicing the lemon into wheels with more force than the task required.

*Do you ever think about the room we don't use?*

Better. Smaller. The second bedroom, which they called the study, which held a desk neither of them sat at and boxes neither of them opened, and which Daniel privately, shamefully, thought of as *the nursery that never got its chance to be wrong.* A crib would have fit against the north wall. He knew this because he had measured it once, three years ago, with a tape measure he'd found in Hazel's inspection kit, standing in that room at eleven at night feeling like a burglar in his own marriage.

He hadn't told her. That was the thing about them—they were both archivists of things unsaid, and the archive had gotten so large it had its own weather.

The chicken needed turning. He turned it. Outside the kitchen window, October was doing its annual demolition of the maple on the corner, leaves coming down like slow confetti for an event no one wanted to admit was an ending. Daniel watched a woman across the street wrestle a stroller up her steps and felt the old ache arrive on schedule, punctual as a train, and he let it sit there in his chest the way you let a note sustain past comfort—not because it sounded good anymore, but because releasing it would mean admitting how long he'd been holding it.

They had never fought about it. That was the strange, terrible grace of their childlessness: it had never once been argued, only *survived*, the way you survive a mild chronic condition by never naming it at dinner parties. Early on, in the first years, people asked—their mothers asked, friends with strollers asked—and Hazel had developed a single sentence she deployed like a well-machined part: *We're focused on our work.* And Daniel would nod, because it was even true, and because contradicting her in public was a door he didn't know what was behind.

But lately—he couldn't say when it started, maybe after the Hendersons' funeral last spring, maybe earlier, maybe the whole time—the silence around it had started to feel less like peace and more like a load-bearing wall nobody had inspected since construction. You could live in a house with a cracked beam for decades. Right up until you couldn't.

Tonight, he'd decided. Not a confrontation. God, no. Just a question, set gently on the table between the plates, the way you'd set down something breakable. *Do you ever think about the room we don't use?* And then whatever came, came.

He heard her key in the lock at 6:47—she was always home within four minutes of 6:45 unless there was an emergency closure, and he could read her day from the sound of the door alone. Tonight: steady, unhurried, one beat of hesitation before the turn. A normal day. A day where nothing fell.

"Something smells good," Hazel said, from the hallway.

She appeared in the kitchen doorway in her work clothes, hi-vis vest still on over her flannel, which meant she'd come straight through without stopping, which meant her mind was still on a bridge somewhere. Her hair was pulled back in the same practical knot she'd worn every day of the decade he'd known her. There was chalk dust, faintly, on her left forearm. Even now, even at forty-one, she looked like someone who trusted numbers more than air.

"Lemon chicken," he said. "Ten minutes."

She nodded and went to wash her hands, and Daniel stood there holding the rehearsal in his mouth like a stone, already feeling it dissolve.

* * *

Dinner happened the way their dinners happened, which is to say it was genuinely pleasant and completely beside the point.

He almost said it twice. The first time was over the chicken, when she mentioned—casually, in the way she reported facts, like readings off a gauge—that the Delmore Street span had passed inspection and wouldn't need closure. He heard himself begin, "Hazel, I—" and then a car alarm went off outside, a long brassy shriek, and by the time it died the sentence had decomposed into "—I think this is the best chicken I've made."

"It is," she said, and meant it, and smiled at him, and the smile undid him a little, the way it always did, because her smiles were rare enough to be structural events and he was never sure whether he deserved the load they carried.

The second time was during dishes. She washed; he dried. This division of labor was older than their marriage and had the permanence of geology. He had the towel moving over a plate, and the words were right there—*the room we don't use, the room we don't use*—and instead he heard himself say:

"The hospice got a new patient today. Retired music teacher. Ninety-one. She requested Chopin."

"Which one?"

"Prelude in E minor."

"Play that one," Hazel said. "She'll know if you fake it."

"She always knows." He set the plate in the cupboard. "That's the thing about dying people. They've run out of patience for approximation."

Hazel looked at him then—a look that lasted half a second too long, that had something in it he couldn't parse, some pressure behind the eyes like water finding a crack—and then she dried her hands and said, "I'm going to check the radiators before it gets cold," and left the kitchen, and the conversation closed itself behind her like a door with a soft pneumatic hinge.

Daniel stood in the kitchen with a dry plate in his hands and understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the moment has passed, that she had been about to say something too. That they had both been standing at the edge of the same pool, and both stepped back, and both pretended the other hadn't noticed the ripple.

* * *

Later, he passed the doorway of the bedroom and stopped.

She was already asleep—or asleep enough—curled on her side of the bed with the covers pulled to her shoulder, one hand open on the mattress like something that had let go mid-flight. The lamp was off. The window was cracked the two inches she always kept it cracked, even in winter, even when he complained about the heating bill, because she said she liked to hear the street.

And there, beside the bed, exactly where they always were: her boots.

Laced. Upright. Toes pointing toward the door.

Everyone else in the world kicked their shoes off wherever they landed—under coffee tables, in hallways, flung toward closets in the general direction of tomorrow. Hazel placed her boots by the door each night like a soldier staging equipment for a withdrawal. Laced so she could step into them running. Pointed out, so the exit required no adjustment, no wasted motion, no looking back.

He had noticed it years ago and filed it under *quirk*, under *she's practical, she's a field inspector, she's ready for callouts.* But somewhere in the last year the filing system had failed him, and now he saw it plainly, every night, the way you finally see a crack in a foundation once it's been pointed out: those boots were not readiness for work. Those boots were a sentence. *I can leave. I can leave at any time. Do not mistake my staying for my being unable to go.*

He stood in the doorway longer than a man should stand in his own doorway. In the dark, the boots seemed almost luminous, two small black assertions.

Once—only once, early, in the first year—he'd asked her about it. *Why do you lace them?* And she'd said, half-asleep, muffled into the pillow, "Habit. Bridge collapses don't schedule themselves." And he'd laughed, and she'd smiled, and the answer had been just plausible enough to close the file.

But he'd been to her office. He'd met Ruth, her crew chief, who wore sneakers to job sites and kept a spare pair of steel-toes in the truck. Nobody else laced their boots for bed. Nobody else slept coiled.

He climbed into his side of the bed carefully, the way you board a vessel you're afraid of waking. She stirred but didn't surface. He lay on his back looking at the ceiling, listening to the street through her two inches of open window, and did the arithmetic he always did: ten years. Ten years of dinners, of dishes, of her cold feet finding his calves under the blankets like a docking maneuver, of Sunday mornings and her hand on the back of his neck at funerals and the way she said his name—which was rarely, which was why each instance rang like struck metal. Ten years, and he could count on one hand the number of times she'd said what she wanted. Not from a menu. From herself.

And he could count on zero fingers the number of times he'd asked.

Because here was his own confession, the one he kept in the locked drawer behind his sternum: he was afraid of the answer. Not afraid she'd say yes or no to children specifically—afraid that any real question, any question with weight, would be the one that showed him what those boots already told him every night. That asking *do you want to stay* might teach her she didn't know. That the marriage was a bridge he'd never dared inspect, because he suspected what the report would say, and he was not strong enough to close it.

So he played piano for the dying instead, which was its own kind of cowardice dressed as vocation. At the hospice he was brave all day long. He sat with people in the last rooms of their lives and played whatever they asked and let them say the unsayable—*I resented her, I loved him more than I ever told him, I wish I'd gone, I wish I'd stayed*—because the dying had nothing left to lose by saying it, and he absorbed their courage like a sponge and brought none of it home. Every afternoon he witnessed plain speech as a sacrament, and every evening he came home and served his wife small talk like it was a meal.

*Do you ever think about the room we don't use?*

He'd get it next time. Next time he'd—

He was asleep before he finished the promise, which was how he always finished it.

* * *

In the morning she was gone before he woke, which was usual. But on the kitchen table, under his coffee mug, she'd left the crossword folded to the page, one clue circled in her precise engineer's hand. Seven letters. *Unspoken understanding.* He stared at it for a full minute before he saw that she hadn't filled it in.

She'd left it for him. Or she'd left it for herself and forgotten. With Hazel he could never tell the difference, and the not-knowing sat in him all day at the hospice, through two sets of Chopin and one long request for hymns from a man who'd been a choir director, until the music teacher, Mrs. Okonkwo, ninety-one, gripped his wrist with surprising strength during the E minor prelude and said:

"Young man. You play like someone apologizing."

He laughed. "Is that bad?"

"It's honest," she said, closing her eyes. "Whoever you're apologizing to—stop practicing and go tell them."

He drove home in the blue hour with her voice in the car, and the city sliding past, and somewhere across town, he would learn soon enough, a yellowed piece of paper was waiting in his wife's inspection kit with a house number on it that would change everything: 14 Marigold Row.

But tonight he only knew the boots by the door, and the circled clue, and the sentence he'd carried for ten years, still unrehearsed enough to be true: *Hazel, what are we waiting for?*

He parked the car. He looked up at their lit kitchen window, where her silhouette moved, measuring something.

Tomorrow, he thought. And reached for the door handle, already rehearsing.
