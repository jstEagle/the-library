## 13. Open House

The notice said viewing from ten to two, and Hazel arrived at 9:41 because she could not bear to be the first and could not bear to be late. She stood across the street under the dead elm — the city had marked it with an orange X months ago, a diagnosis in paint — and watched the first cars pull up along Marigold Row. A silver SUV. A hatchback with a realtor's magnetic sign. A man on foot carrying a clipboard the way she carried her inspection kit: like a weapon he hoped not to use.

She had dressed for it without deciding to. Steel-toed boots, work trousers, the canvas jacket with the chalk dust ground so deep into the seams that no laundering would ever get it out. Armor, Daniel might have called it, if they were still speaking in metaphors instead of logistics. She had left before he woke, or before he pretended to wake; lately the difference was academic.

At 9:58 she crossed the street.

The porch rail stopped her at the top of the steps, as it always did. The chalk lines were still there — faded to ghosts, but there, if you knew where to look. Nine-year-old arithmetic pressed into weathered wood: *4 ft 2 in*, *rail height*, *H.O., age 9*. She had measured everything that summer. Her mother had let her, had even held the other end of the tape sometimes, humming, saying *numbers don't lie, Nkechi's daughter* — which wasn't quite her name but was what Adaeze called her when she was pleased, some private joke between mother and daughter that Hazel had never decoded and now never would.

A woman's voice behind her: "Excuse me, hon."

Hazel stepped aside. The woman was maybe sixty, wearing a quilted vest and an expression of professional optimism, and she ran her hand along the same rail Hazel had been touching and said, "Original millwork. Could be stripped and stained. Or replaced — vinyl holds up better these days."

Vinyl. Hazel moved into the house before she could say something she'd regret, which was most things.

Inside smelled the way abandoned houses smell when they are being prepared for strangers: bleach and fresh paint over old dust, the ghost of Adaeze's cooking buried beneath all of it like a body under snow. The estate company had staged nothing. There was nothing left to stage. Emeka had cleared it out in three days after the probate ruling went against them — against *her*, really, since Emeka had never contested anything except her right to contest — and now the rooms stood empty and echoing, their dimensions suddenly legible in a way furniture had always hidden. The living room was smaller than her memory of it by half. The ceiling lower. The window over the kitchen sink still framed the neighbor's fence, the same crooked slat she'd stared through while washing dishes at fifteen, and that at least was true to scale.

"Load-bearing wall," a man was saying near the hallway. He had a tablet and a hard hat tucked under one arm, though no hard hat was required here, and he knocked his knuckles twice against the plaster as if the house might answer. "Probably original balloon framing. We'd gut it anyway."

*We'd gut it anyway.* Four words, tossed off like a receipt. Hazel stood very still and let the sentence pass through her the way she'd been trained to let traffic pass through a closed lane: acknowledge, absorb, proceed.

She walked through her childhood room. Someone had written something on the wall in pencil, low near the baseboard where a child would write — not her handwriting, not Emeka's. Some earlier tenant? No. She crouched. It was hers. Age eleven, maybe: *HZL WAS HERE*, the E missing because the pencil had broken and she hadn't bothered. She put her thumb over it as if she could take it with her. The plaster was cold and gave up nothing.

By eleven the house was full. That was the word for it, absurdly — full — thirty strangers moving through the rooms with the unhurried entitlement of people at a museum, except museums ask you not to touch. Here everyone touched. A young couple ran their palms along the doorframes checking for plumb. An older man opened every closet and shone his phone flashlight into the dark corners as though looking for something specific, something hidden, and Hazel wanted to tell him: whatever you're looking for, my mother already looked for it for forty years and didn't find it either. A woman in expensive sneakers photographed the kitchen from four angles and said "good bones" to no one, and Hazel flinched at the phrase the way you flinch at a stranger using your private nickname.

Good bones. The house had good bones. She thought of Ruth saying, in her flat way, *every structure fails at the joint, never the beam,* and thought: this house failed at its joints too. At the marriage that made it. At the family that was supposed to hold it together. The bones were fine. The bones were always fine.

The realtor — a brisk man named Okafor, which had made Hazel's chest seize when she read it on the sign, some cousin or no relation at all, the name scattered across Lagos and London and now this lawn — worked the crowd with practiced warmth. "Period details," she heard him say. "Solid lot, forty by one-twenty. And the location —" he gestured out the front window toward the street, toward the orange-Xed elm, toward the notice stapled to the bus shelter at the corner announcing the end of the 47 line in five weeks — "the whole corridor's slated for redevelopment. You're not buying a house, folks. You're buying the future."

You're not buying a house. No, Hazel thought. You're buying the future. We lived in the past, and look how that turned out.

*

She found Emeka in the kitchen at noon.

He stood where the stove used to be, arms crossed, watching the foot traffic with an expression she recognized from funerals: the face of a man performing composure for an audience that kept changing. He'd shaved. He wore a blazer over a T-shirt, his compromise between grief and commerce, and when he saw her he didn't startle, which meant he'd known she'd come, which meant he'd probably counted on it.

"You're blocking the sightline," he said.

"To what?"

"The kitchen. People want to imagine themselves in the kitchen."

"People want to imagine themselves anywhere," Hazel said. "That's the whole business."

He almost smiled. She saw it start and die. Ten years of silence between them had not erased the fluency; it had only made each word cost more. They stood in the kitchen where their mother had stood for forty years, where the drawer had been — the drawer was gone now, sold or trashed, Hazel didn't know which, and the letters inside it she had rescued one night last week, carried out in a grocery bag like contraband — and around them strangers drifted through the doorway saying things like *south-facing* and *could open this up* and *what's the school district.*

A man came in while they stood there. Hazel registered him in pieces, the way she'd been trained to register structures: mid-fifties, good coat, watch that cost more than the auction estimate, the particular restlessness of someone who sees rooms as square footage awaiting improvement. He had a rolled set of drawings under his arm. He didn't browse like the others. He moved through the house the way water moves through a channel that has already decided its course.

Emeka straightened. Hazel saw him straighten, saw the shift in his weight, and understood one second before it happened.

"Holden," the man said, extending a hand. "Marcus Holden. Corridor Partners." He shook Emeka's hand and then, after a glance at her, offered his own. Her grip was dry and brief. His palm was warm and soft, a hand that had never gripped anything harder than a pen.

"We've met," Emeka said. "At the planning office."

"We have." Holden smiled like a man who remembered everyone because forgetting was inefficient. He turned a slow circle in the kitchen, taking in the bare walls, the patched plaster, the window over the sink. "You know what I like about these old houses? They don't waste space. Everything's where it needs to be. Somebody built this who understood proportion."

Somebody built this, Hazel thought. Somebody. Not a man named anything. Not a house where a woman hummed while her daughter measured the porch rail in chalk. Somebody built this.

Holden unrolled his drawings on the counter — the counter that was no longer there, so that the paper sagged in the middle over empty air, and he smoothed it flat against nothing. Elevations. A rendering. Hazel looked despite herself. The new building rose four stories where the house stood one, glass and pale brick, balconies like afterthoughts, and at the corner where the bus shelter was, a café with umbrellas rendered in cheerful digital green. The drawing was beautiful the way renderings are beautiful: lit from an angle the sun never actually takes, populated by figures caught mid-laugh, a dog frozen forever at the edge of frame.

"Fourteen units," Holden said. "Market rate on the top floors, workforce housing below. I'm not the villain here, Ms. —" he glanced at Emeka, then at her, and Hazel realized he didn't know their name, didn't care to learn it, that to him they were *the sellers* — "I keep the neighborhood alive. These houses are dying. Look at the street. Half of them are rentals rotting from the inside. Somebody's going to build here. Better it's somebody who builds well."

"I'm sure that's what everybody says," Hazel said.

"It is what everybody says," Holden agreed pleasantly. "Doesn't make it wrong."

He turned back to Emeka and lowered his voice, but the kitchen was small and Hazel had spent twenty years listening to buildings, and buildings teach you to hear what people try to muffle. "My number stands. Forty above appraisal, cash, close in thirty days. No financing contingency, no inspection period. Your sister can fight the auction if she wants —" a nod toward her, courteous, terrible — "and drag this out six months, a year, and at the end of it the price comes down and you both walk away with less and more lawyers' bills. Or you take my offer today and we're done. I'll even let her keep whatever she wants from the house. Sentimental value." He said the phrase the way you'd say *scrap metal*: something with weight but no worth.

Forty above appraisal. Hazel did the arithmetic without wanting to, because numbers were the only language she trusted and they betrayed her anyway: her childhood, itemized, came to a figure that would buy maybe four years of Daniel's salary, or one unit in the building that would replace it, or nothing at all, depending on how you measured. Depending on what you thought a porch rail was worth with a nine-year-old's chalk still on it. Depending on whether *sentimental value* was a category or a joke.

Emeka was quiet for a long time. Hazel watched his jaw work, watched the muscle jump under the skin, and understood that he was not deciding whether to sell. He had decided that long ago. He was deciding whether to do it in front of her.

"I need to think about it," Emeka said.

"Of course." Holden rolled up his drawings. "Offer's open through Friday. After that I bid at auction like everyone else, and everyone else includes people who won't preserve the façade." He paused at the doorway, and for one moment the developer's polish slipped and something almost human looked out — tiredness, maybe, or the memory of a house of his own somewhere. "For what it's worth, I grew up in a place like this. In Newark. They tore it down in '89 and I've driven past the parking lot maybe five hundred times." He shrugged. "The lot doesn't hurt. That's the thing nobody tells you. The lot doesn't hurt at all."

Then he was gone, and his absence filled the kitchen worse than his presence had.

*

The viewing ended at two. The crowd drained out the way crowds do, all at once and then in stragglers, and the realtor locked nothing because there was nothing to lock, and the house settled into its own silence like a body relaxing after holding a pose.

Hazel stayed. Emeka stayed. Neither acknowledged the other staying.

They ended up in the living room, on opposite sides of the empty space where the couch had been, standing because there was nowhere to sit, and the afternoon light came through the front window at the angle it had always come at — she knew this angle, had done homework in it, had watched dust move through it while her mother napped — and lay in a long warm stripe across the floorboards.

"He offered you forty over," Hazel said. Not a question.

"He offered *us* forty over. It's half mine. That's what the lawyer says, anyway, if I stop fighting it." Emeka looked at the window, not at her. "You should take it, Hazel. You should stop. This house is a hole in the ground with a roof on it. Every month it stands there it costs us money and it costs you—" he waved a hand at her, at all of her, the boots and the jacket and the rigid spine— "*this*. Whatever this is."

"You slept here your whole childhood."

"I *endured* here my whole childhood. There's a difference." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, she could see him hating it, hating that his body kept betraying him into feeling in front of her. "You got the humming. You got the measuring on the porch and the — the *Nkechi's daughter*. I got the silence. I got the closed door. You think I'm selling a house? I'm selling the evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

He didn't answer. Outside, a bus went by — the 47, still running, five weeks left — and its shadow crossed the window and the floor and was gone, and both of them watched it go the way you watch a departure you're not on.

"Take the offer," Emeka said finally, quietly. "Or don't. Bid at the auction. Lose. Either way, Friday I'm calling Holden, because if I don't, some fund is going to buy this place at estimate and strip it to the studs, and at least Holden will keep the walls." He pushed off the wall he'd been leaning against. "Keep your letters. Keep your chalk. Keep whatever it is you think you're saving, because I promise you, Hazel — nobody else in that room today saw it. Nobody priced it. It isn't on any drawing."

He left through the front door, and the hinge — the same hinge that had squeaked her entire life, that she could have identified blind, that she had once oiled at age twelve and been scolded for, because her mother said the sound told her who was coming home — the hinge didn't squeak anymore. Someone had oiled it for the sale.

Hazel stood alone in the stripe of light. Below her, faint through the floorboards, the basement breathed its cold mineral breath, and she thought about the drawer, the grocery bag of unfinished letters waiting in her car, addressed to a father whose name she still didn't know, sentences that broke off mid-word like bridges that end at the river.

Her phone buzzed. A text from the realtor, sent to all interested parties: *Reminder — sealed bids due Thursday, public auction Saturday, 10 a.m., county steps.*

Thursday. Two days.

Hazel took out her phone, opened the banking app she almost never opened, and looked at the number there — the number that was supposed to be a roof, a buffer, a decade of careful silent provision — and began, for the first time in her life, to calculate what she could lose.
