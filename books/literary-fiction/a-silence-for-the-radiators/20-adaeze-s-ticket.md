## 20. Adaeze's Ticket

Emeka had always been the one who found things.

It was a small talent, unremarkable, the kind of thing nobody thanked you for. He found his mother's reading glasses under the couch cushions, found Hazel's lost hair ribbons in the hedge, found coins in vending machine slots and returned them to their owners with a shrug that said *it was nothing*, because it was nothing, because being the finder meant you were never the one who was lost. He was eleven when he understood this about himself, and thirty-four now, standing in the gray light of the kitchen at 14 Marigold Row, watching his sister sit on the floor where she used to sit, and understanding it still.

She hadn't called the night before. She hadn't told him she was coming. He'd driven over at six a.m. to load the last of the boxes into the truck — the movers came Thursday, the auction was Saturday, and there was nothing left in this house but dust and argument — and he'd found the back door unlocked and a flashlight lying on the hallway floor like something dropped in flight.

He'd known before he reached her room. Some part of him had known since yesterday, since the open house, since he watched Hazel stand in the doorway of the parlor with her arms crossed tight as rebar while strangers ran their thumbs along the doorframes checking for rot. She had looked like a woman doing an inspection. Of course she'd come back. Of course she'd come back the only way she knew how: without asking, without announcing, without giving anyone the chance to say no.

Now she sat on the floor of what had been their mother's room, the letters spread around her in drifts, her eyes red-rimmed and dry, which Emeka knew from long experience was worse than crying. Hazel didn't cry. Hazel measured. And right now she was measuring him across the width of a room that had raised them both, and he could feel the question assembling itself behind her face like weather.

"You slept here," he said. Not a question. He set the box he was carrying down on the bed — their mother's bed, still made, still holding its shape after two years the way certain things hold their shape out of pure stubbornness — and lowered himself onto the floor across from her, his back against the radiator, because his knees were forty-one years old and the floor was where she was.

"I needed to read them," Hazel said. "All of them."

"They're not all there."

Her head came up. "What?"

Emeka looked at the radiator beside his shoulder. It was cold, of course; the gas had been shut off in March. But he remembered the sound of it, the clank and tick of winter mornings, Adaeze's voice rising through the floorboards singing hymns off-key while the pipes knocked their accompaniment. A silence for the radiators, he thought, absurdly, and didn't know where the phrase had come from or why it felt true.

"There's one missing," he said. "From the drawer. You noticed the drawer sticks?"

"It sticks."

"It doesn't stick. I bent the runner. Years ago." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it went flat when he talked about money or the house or anything that mattered, and he hated that flatness, had been hating it for decades, this voice like a locked door that everyone assumed was his personality instead of his protection. "I put things in there I didn't want you to find."

Hazel went very still. His sister had a way of going still that most people mistook for calm. Emeka had spent a childhood learning the difference. Stillness in Hazel wasn't peace. It was load transfer — the moment when some weight inside her got redistributed to structures that could bear it, and God help whatever structure failed next.

"What things," she said.

And here it was. The thing he had carried for twenty-two years, folded into himself like a document kept past its expiration date, useless now except as evidence. He had rehearsed this conversation maybe a thousand times, usually in the shower, usually angrily, usually with Hazel cast as the one who finally listened and Adaeze cast as the one who finally explained herself and Emeka cast as the one who was finally, *finally* understood. In none of those rehearsals did they sit on a cold floor at dawn with the house already sold.

He reached into his jacket pocket.

* * *

He had been twelve. That was the arithmetic that never stopped hurting — that he'd been twelve, old enough to understand, young enough to be broken by it, exactly the age when a boy is building the skeleton he'll live inside forever.

It was a Sunday in October. Adaeze had gone to the early service alone, which was itself unusual — she always took them, both of them, marched them down Marigold Row in their good clothes like a woman leading troops — but that morning she'd said her head hurt and kissed the tops of their heads and gone, and Hazel had immediately buried herself in homework because Hazel was fifteen and already perfect, and Emeka had been left with four hours and a house and the specific restless energy of a boy whose mother has lied to him for the first time in a way he can't yet name.

He hadn't known she was lying. That came later. That Sunday he'd just been looking for the good scissors, because he was cutting pictures out of a magazine for a school project, and the good scissors lived in the drawer of the dresser in Adaeze's room, the bottom drawer, the one with the sewing kit and the spare buttons and the important papers.

The ticket was under the sewing kit.

Greyhound. He remembered the color of it even now, that institutional blue-grey, the font like a bus station announcement. One way. Departing Friday, 9:40 p.m., from the downtown terminal. Destination: a city three states north that meant nothing to him then and meant everything to him now, because it was the city on the return address of the letters, the city where a man named — but no. Not yet. He wasn't ready to give Hazel the name yet, and besides, the name wasn't the point. The name was just ink. The point was the date.

The date was two weeks away.

Two weeks. Which meant that on that Sunday, when their mother said her head hurt and went to church alone, she was not going to church. Or she was going to church and also carrying, somewhere in the pocket of her good coat, a ticket out. Purchased. Paid for. Dated. A door already built, waiting only to be walked through.

Emeka had stood in her bedroom holding the ticket and felt the floor of his life tilt.

He was twelve. He did what a twelve-year-old does with an earthquake: he put it back. Under the sewing kit, exactly where it had been, squared to the edges of the drawer the way she kept everything squared, and he closed the drawer and took the good scissors and went downstairs and finished his project and said nothing.

Friday came. He remembered Friday. He remembered sitting at the kitchen table doing homework he wasn't reading, watching the clock, watching his mother move through the evening — dishes, laundry, the ironing board set up in front of the television — ordinary as bread. At nine o'clock she sent them to bed. At nine-fifteen he lay in the dark listening to the house. At nine-forty he watched the luminous hands of his clock reach the minute printed on a piece of blue-grey paper hidden twenty feet above his head, and nothing happened. No suitcase. No car. No door.

At ten o'clock she came up to say goodnight and kissed his forehead and her lips were warm and smelled of the cocoa butter she used, and Emeka lay there beneath that kiss like a man under a passing train, alive by inches.

Saturday morning she made pancakes. She hummed. She burned the first batch the way she always did and blamed the pan the way she always did, and Hazel laughed, and Emeka ate his pancakes and understood, with the terrible clarity available only to children and the dying, that his mother had chosen them.

Chosen. That was the word he'd built his whole architecture on. She had a ticket and she stayed. Whatever was wrong — and things were wrong, Emeka had known things were wrong the way you know a wall is load-bearing, by the sounds it makes — whatever grief or hunger or unfinished business lived in her, she had held a door out of it in her hand and let it close.

He never told Hazel. That was the part that curdled over the years, the part that turned devotion into something that tasted, from the outside, like cruelty. Because telling Hazel would have meant sharing it, and sharing it would have meant it became theirs instead of his, and Emeka — twelve, thirteen, twenty, thirty — could not survive it becoming theirs. It was the one thing he had of her that Hazel didn't have. Hazel had the humming, the hymns, the chalk measurements on the porch rail, the easy fluent love between mother and firstborn that Emeka had watched from the outside his whole childhood like a boy with his face pressed to a lit window. Hazel had been favored. Everyone knew it; Hazel knew it least of all, which was its own kind of insult. But Emeka had the ticket. Emeka had the knowledge that underneath all that light, their mother had once stood at the edge of leaving, and turned back.

He'd told himself it was mercy, keeping it. Later, older, he'd told himself it was strategy. Neither story survived contact with the truth, which was simpler and uglier: he kept it because it was his, and because if Hazel knew, she would have tried to fix it, and Hazel fixing things was its own violence. Hazel would have asked their mother questions. Hazel would have wanted the whole story, footnoted, sourced, complete. And Adaeze Okafor did not give complete stories. Adaeze gave you a hymn and a burnt pancake and a kiss that smelled of cocoa butter, and if you pushed, she gave you silence — a silence so total, so structural, that the whole family had learned to build around it, the way trees grow around fence wire.

So Emeka carried it alone, and the carrying changed him in ways he couldn't see until much later. It made him careful with money, because a woman with a bus ticket teaches you that security is fiction. It made him hard in negotiations, because he'd learned at twelve that love keeps ledgers whether you admit it or not. It made him furious, later, when Hazel left — when she married Daniel and moved across town and visited monthly, punctual as a utility bill — because how dare she leave lightly? How dare she walk out of this house not knowing what it cost to stay?

That was the sentence he'd thrown at her, three years ago, the last real fight, the one that had ended Thanksgiving forever: *You don't know what it cost to stay.* He'd meant the mortgage, the repairs, the years of driving in every weekend while she built her life elsewhere. He'd said it like an accusation about labor.

He'd meant the ticket. He'd meant: *I know something you don't know, and it is killing me, and I cannot say it.*

* * *

"Now," Emeka said, and took his hand out of his pocket, and opened it.

The ticket sat in his palm.

It should have crumbled. Twenty-two years in a bent drawer, through damp summers and the year the roof leaked and the two years the house sat half-empty — paper doesn't last, he knew that better than anyone, he'd watched their mother's letters yellow and soften like leaves. But the ticket had been folded in half, and then in half again, small enough to hide in a fist, and folding preserves what flatness destroys, the way a river stone survives because it keeps moving. It was soft as cloth now, blue-grey gone the color of old sky, and the print had faded almost to ghost. But the date was legible. The destination was legible. He'd checked, last night, in the truck, under the dome light, before he drove over here knowing — knowing — what he was going to do.

Hazel didn't take it. Her hands stayed in her lap, curled loose, and Emeka watched her look at the ticket the way she must have looked at cracks in steel: reading depth, direction, propagation. Reading whether it was fatal.

"Where did you—" she started, and stopped, and started again. "When."

"I was twelve." He set the ticket down on the floorboards between them, gently, the way you set down something that might go off. "October. She bought it two weeks out. One way." He swallowed. "I put it back. She never used it. I waited my whole life to find out why, and she died before I could ask, and now the auction's Saturday and the answer is a pile of drafts addressed to a man neither of us ever heard of, and I am forty-one years old and I am so tired, Hazel. I'm tired of being the one who knows things."

The radiator ticked. It couldn't have — the gas was off, the pipes were cold, there was nothing in the system to expand or contract. But Emeka heard it anyway, the old winter music, and saw by the flicker in Hazel's face that she heard it too, and understood that they were both hearing the same house at different volumes, and had been their entire lives.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Hazel asked. Her voice was level. He knew that level. It was the voice she used on bridges, on reports, on Daniel, on him — the voice of a woman who has decided that feeling is a defect to be engineered out. It made him want to shake her and it made him want to weep, because he recognized it. It was their mother's voice. It was the voice of people who learned early that the hard thing, said aloud, breaks things.

"Because she chose us," he said. "That's why. I thought — I built my whole —" He stopped. Started over, plainer. "She had a way out. A real one, dated, paid for. And she stayed. And I decided that meant we were worth more to her than whatever she was running toward, and I decided somebody in this family had to act like it. Somebody had to stay like it. So I stayed. I stayed so hard I forgot how to do anything else." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And you — you left like it was nothing. Like the door was just a door. You married your pianist and you came home once a month and you never once asked why I was angry, because to you the anger was just Emeka being Emeka, difficult, obsessed with money, obsessed with the house. And the whole time I was sitting on the biggest secret in this family because I thought it was a gift. I thought it proved she loved us." He looked at the letters scattered around his sister, dozens of them, begun and abandoned, all of them addressed to a stranger. "Turns out it proved nothing. Turns out she just couldn't finish a sentence. Any sentence. To anybody. Turns out I spent my life guarding a locked door in a house where nobody ever finished anything."

Hazel picked up the ticket.

She held it the way she held everything — carefully, at the edges, like evidence — and Emeka watched her thumb trace the faded route number, and watched something move through her face that he had no word for, some structural event deep in the superstructure, invisible from outside, the kind of thing that doesn't show until it shows everywhere at once.

"She nearly left us," Hazel said quietly. "And we both grew up thinking we were the reason she stayed."

"We were the reason she stayed," Emeka said. "Or the excuse. I don't know anymore. Maybe those are the same thing." He dragged a hand down his face. Dawn was coming up properly now, grey light filling the window, laying itself across the floorboards they'd chalked and scuffed and slid down on sock feet. Outside, a bus went by — the 47, early run, nearly empty, its lights sweeping the ceiling like a search beam saying goodbye. Five weeks, the notices said. Then the route was gone and the street was just a street.

"Hazel." He waited until she looked at him. "I need you to hear this, because I've said it badly my whole life and I'm going to try to say it right once." His chest hurt. It occurred to him distantly that this was what people meant by *it hurts to say it*, and that he'd assumed they were being dramatic. "I stayed so you wouldn't have to feel it. The abandonment. I thought — if I kept the house, kept the accounts, kept everything standing, then even if she'd gone, even someday if it all came out, you'd come home and it would still be here. The porch. The rail. Your chalk numbers. I thought I was building you a place to land." He shook his head. "And instead I just built a fight. Every time you came home I met you at the door with a bill. Because the bill was easier than the truth, and the truth was that I was scared. Twelve years old and scared, and I never stopped being twelve about it. I never told anyone. Not Ruthie, not my pastor, not —" His voice caught on their mother's name and he let it. "Not her. I never asked her. I had twenty years of Sundays. I never once asked her why."

Hazel was crying. Silently, the way she did everything, tears running straight down and off her jaw without a single change in her expression, and Emeka realized he had never seen his sister cry in his adult life, and that this was what it cost her, and that he had spent decades calling her cold.

"She couldn't finish," Hazel said. "Look at them. Look at all of them. She got as far as the hard part and stopped. Every time." She looked up at him, wet-eyed and fierce. "Except one."

Emeka went cold. "What?"

Hazel reached into the pocket of her jacket — the jacket she'd slept in, the jacket that smelled of the old neighborhood and of Daniel's apartment and of bridges — and drew out a small book. Navy cloth, gold lettering worn to shadow. Their mother's hymnal. He knew it instantly, the way you know a voice on the phone; it had lived on the piano-side shelf of the parlor for fifty years.

"It was tucked inside," Hazel said. "Behind the back cover. One letter she actually finished. Addressed to both of us." She held it out across the space between them, across the ticket and the drafts and the twenty-two years. "Emeka. There's a name in it. His name. And there's a reason she stayed, and it isn't the one either of us built our lives on."

Outside, the 47 sighed away toward the depot, and the light came up full on Marigold Row, and Emeka Okafor reached for the book his mother had hidden in the one place she knew her children would never stop looking — the songs.
