## 7. The Archivist of Small Things

Teodora Vass had a rule about men who called radio stations at one in the morning to ask about songs: they were either cranks, or they were carrying something. The cranks she could handle in four minutes. The carrying ones took longer, and they were worth the coffee.

This one—Kavanagh, he'd said, Ivo Kavanagh—had called twice. The first time he'd hung up when she answered, which told her he hadn't expected a human being. The second time he'd spoken so carefully, so evenly, that she'd known immediately he was the second kind. Cranks don't modulate. Cranks don't apologize for the hour. Men who apologize for the hour are men who have been rehearsing the call for days.

She'd played him the archive recording over the phone, the anonymous caller singing the lullaby into a studio line, voice thin and close, the way voices are when a mouth is pressed to a receiver in a cold room. She had watched, in the way you watch a phone call you can't see, the long silence afterward. Not the silence of recognition—she'd have bet money on that—but the silence of a man trying to decide how much of himself to spend.

"Where did you get that?" he'd finally asked.

"A man phoned it in. Live, during my slot. Three weeks ago." She'd let that sit. "You said you know the song."

"I know *a* song," he'd said. "It may be the same one."

Which was, she thought, the most interesting sentence anyone had said to her all year. People who merely know a song say *yes, that's it.* People who know a song the way you know a wound say *it may be the same one.*

So now there was coffee, at a café near the station with bad lighting and good chairs, because she'd learned that if you wanted people to talk you put them somewhere soft and let the room do the work. He arrived four minutes early, which she noted, and chose the chair facing the wall, which she noted harder. Men who sit facing the wall are men who don't want to be watched watching other people. Or men who've spent their lives being looked through and have stopped performing for the window.

He was smaller than his voice. Grey at the temples, precise hands, a cardigan that had been expensive once and was now simply faithful. He looked, she thought, like a man made entirely of margins.

"Teodora Vass," she said, though he knew. "I produce and present the night slot. Eleven to two. Mostly music, some oral history when I can sneak it past management. They think nobody listens at one a.m., which is exactly why I do it. The people who listen at one a.m. are the people who matter."

"You say that like a creed."

"It is a creed." She stirred her coffee without drinking it. "So. Ivo Kavanagh. What do you do, when you're not calling radio stations in the dark?"

"I'm a translator."

"Of what?"

"Whatever pays. Contracts, mostly. Legal, technical." A small shrug, practiced. "Occasionally literature, when someone's feeling generous."

"Languages?"

He named four, and she raised her eyebrows, and he named a fifth so quietly she almost missed it. "And Velskan," he said.

There it was. She'd known it was coming—had known since the call, since the way he'd said *traditional air of Velska Row* with a bitterness that didn't belong to a neutral phrase—but knowing didn't blunt it. Velskan did that to people. It was a small word that opened into a large room.

"Velskan," she repeated. "That's not a language you pick up for contracts."

"No."

"Family?"

He looked at his coffee. "It's not widely spoken."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know it isn't."

She laughed, and she meant it, and she saw the flicker of surprise on his face—the surprise of a man who had expected to be pushed and had instead been enjoyed. Good. Enjoyment loosened people faster than pressure. She'd learned that from her grandmother, who had extracted the entire history of their street from the butcher across three decades of buying meat, and had never once asked a direct question.

"Let me tell you what I do," Teodora said, "and then you can decide how much of yourself you're willing to trade for it. Fair?"

He nodded, warily, the way men nod when they suspect a contract is about to be read aloud.

"I collect small things," she said. "That's the honest version. The grand version is that I document endangered languages and the oral traditions attached to them, and I've been doing it for eleven years, unpaid, in the margins of a radio job that management tolerates because my ratings hold at one a.m. The small version is truer. I collect small things. A word for the space under a staircase. A song sung at shearing. A way of saying *goodbye* that only exists in one village and means, literally, *until the road remembers us.*" She paused. "Velskan has that one, incidentally. *Do veyna.* Or it had. I've found two speakers who still use it. Both over eighty. Both in care homes."

Ivo Kavanagh had gone very still. She watched the stillness and understood it wasn't the stillness of a man hearing trivia. It was the stillness of a man hearing his own house described by a stranger.

"Go on," he said. His voice had dropped half a tone.

"There's a box." She said it plainly, because plainly was how you delivered the things that mattered. "It came to me four years ago. A woman named Halina Dorn—she died before I could thank her properly—had spent the seventies and eighties doing field recordings around Velska Row. Door-to-door, tape recorder under her arm, asking old people to sing, to talk, to say the names of tools and herbs and insults. She got maybe ninety hours of tape. Nobody wanted them. The university archive rejected the collection—*insufficient scholarly framework,* their words. Her nephew was going to bin them. He called the station instead because he'd heard me do a segment on disappearing dialects and figured I was the kind of person who'd take a box off his hands." She smiled without much humor. "He was right. I took the box. It's in my flat. It's been in my flat for four years because cataloguing ninety hours of a dying language alone, evenings and weekends, is the work of a decade, and I am one woman with a day job."

"And the lullaby," Ivo said. "Is it in the box?"

"No."

The word landed. She watched it land.

"I've been through maybe a third of the tapes," she said. "There are songs. Fragments, mostly—people remember refrains better than verses, the way they remember choruses of prayers but not the psalms. There's a wedding song, half of it. A working song about the tanneries that I have four verses of. Children's counting rhymes. But not your lullaby. Not the one your caller sang." She leaned forward. "Which is the interesting part. Because I've had the box four years, Mr. Kavanagh, and I know what's in it, and that song isn't in it. And a song that isn't in the only substantial field collection of Velskan song—that's a song with a history. Songs with histories are my favorite kind."

He was turning his cup slowly on its saucer, a quarter-turn at a time, like a man working a lock.

"You said a caller phoned it in," he said.

"A man. Older voice—I'd guess sixty or seventy. He wouldn't give a name. He called the studio line, and my screener put him through because he said he had a song nobody else had, and I'm a sucker for that sentence. He sang it live, down the line. I recorded it, obviously—I record everything; that's not a habit, it's a pathology. When he finished I asked him where he'd learned it. He said, *from the woman who wrote it.* Then he hung up." She watched him. "The number was a payphone. I checked. I check everything; that's the other half of the pathology. A payphone at the community center on Row Street."

Something moved through Ivo Kavanagh's face—fast, buried immediately, but she caught it, the way you catch a fish's shadow under grey water. She was good at faces. You had to be, in her line of work. Half of what people recorded was what they didn't say.

"Here's my offer," she said. "I'll play you everything I have. The caller's recording, the tapes from the box as I catalog them. I'll tell you what I find as I find it. I'll even let you listen to the uncatalogued material, which I do for no one, because cataloguing is half the value and I'm possessive of my half." She sat back. "In exchange, you tell me why it matters. Why a translator of contracts and technical manuals called a radio station at one in the morning about a lullaby, and then agreed to meet a stranger in a café with bad lighting. That's the trade. Help for honesty. I don't take payment in any other currency."

The silence went on long enough that the waitress came and went, refilling cups neither of them had emptied.

"I've told you," he said finally. "It's a song from where I grew up. I was curious whether—"

"Mr. Kavanagh." She said it gently. "I interview people for a living. Liars, mostly—politicians, developers, councilmen explaining demolitions. And I'm going to tell you what I tell the young producers: a lie is easy to spot not because of what it contains but because of what it's *shaped* like. A true answer has edges. It has things in it the speaker didn't plan. Your answer is smooth. It's been sanded. You've been giving it, or one like it, for years, to people who didn't listen closely enough to notice." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "I'm listening closely. It's the only skill I have."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the calculation run—the whole ledger of a careful life being audited in real time. She'd seen that look before, in the holdouts and the archivists and the last speakers, the people who had one thing left and were deciding whether to spend it. It was the most human expression there was, and it never got easier to watch.

"It's not a song from where I grew up," he said.

She waited.

"It's *the* song." His voice had gone quiet and slightly rough, like a page turned too fast. "My mother wrote it. She wrote it at our kitchen table on Row Street, before the row was even called Velska Row officially—that came later, the heritage signage, the plaques. She wrote it for—she wrote it for me. And it was never recorded. It was never written down. She wouldn't allow it. She said—" He stopped. Started again. "She said some things only live if they're passed mouth to mouth. That writing it down was a kind of killing it. I was a child. I took it as law." He turned the cup a quarter-turn more. "She died thirty years ago. I have spent thirty years not thinking about that song, which is a full-time occupation, and I am apparently not as good at it as I believed."

Teodora did not move. There was a discipline to this—she'd learned it from the tapes, from Halina Dorn's fieldwork, where the recorder's stillness was what made people sing. When a person finally opens a door, you do not walk through it loudly. You stand in the frame and let them come to you.

"Three weeks ago," he said, "I heard it on your program. Sung by a stranger, from a payphone, announced as a *traditional air.* Traditional." The word came out with a fine contempt. "It isn't traditional. It's thirty-one years old and it has two owners and one of them is dead, and the other one—" He closed his mouth. Opened it. "The other one is me. And I don't know who the man on the phone was. I don't know how he has it. I don't know why he called your station at one in the morning, though I notice he and I have that in common." He looked up, and his eyes were dry and very tired. "That's the truth. It has edges. I hope you can hear them."

"I can hear them," Teodora said softly.

And she could. She could also hear what he hadn't said, the rooms behind the rooms: *the woman who wrote it*—not *my mother*, not to the caller; *two owners*—which meant the song had been given, shared, split; and underneath all of it, the way his whole body had angled toward the word *Row Street* like a compass needle. She was an archivist. She knew what an archive was: a place where what people couldn't bear to keep, and couldn't bear to lose, got stored. Ivo Kavanagh was an archive. He was carrying ninety hours of tape behind his sternum and he had never once pressed play.

"Then we understand each other," she said. "Here's what happens next. I'll copy the caller's recording for you today. The box—come to my flat Sunday. Bring gloves if you're squeamish about other people's fingerprints; Halina's are on half of it and I consider them part of the collection."

"Teodora." He said her name carefully, testing its weight, and she noticed he had a translator's mouth even in his own language, the habit of handling words like borrowed goods. "Why do you do this? The box, the one a.m. slot, the—" he gestured at the café, at the whole apparatus of her attention. "Ninety hours of a dead language. No funding, no credit. Why?"

It was a fair question and she'd answered it a hundred times with the sanded version—preservation, heritage, the record of a community—and she found, sitting across from a man who had just been caught lying by his own smoothness, that she couldn't bring herself to give it. That was the thing about honesty trades. They were contagious.

"My grandmother spoke Velskan," she said. "Fluently. Natively. It was her first language and her only language until she was nine, and then the schools happened, and then the city happened, and by the time I was born she spoke it never. Not rarely. *Never.* I asked her once—once, when I was maybe ten—to teach me a song she used to hum. Just one. She went white. She said, *why would you want that? It's a door to nowhere.*" Teodora turned her cup, and realized she was turning it a quarter at a time, and stopped. "She was the last speaker in our family and she locked the door behind her and threw away the key, and I have spent my entire adult life collecting other people's keys. That's why. Not preservation. Not scholarship. I'm trying to pick a lock."

Ivo Kavanagh was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—less sanded, more grain.

"A door to nowhere," he repeated. "She said that."

"She said it like a woman describing a wound."

"My mother said the opposite." He looked out at the street, at the traffic moving over the bridge toward the old quarter, the bridge that was scheduled—everyone knew, it was in the papers—to close within the year. "She said the language was the only place some things could live. That if it went, they'd have nowhere to be." He turned back. "Two women, one language. One called it a door to nowhere. One built a house in it and hid a song there."

"And now the song has walked out of the house," Teodora said, "and phoned a radio station, and we're both sitting here."

"Yes."

She finished her coffee, finally, and set down the cup, and took out a pen—the actual pen, the one she used for the catalog, because some transactions deserved the good pen—and wrote her address on a napkin, and slid it across the table.

"Sunday," she said. "Ten o'clock. Bring your edges, Mr. Kavanagh. I'll bring the box."

He took the napkin. He read the address twice, and folded it along a precise crease, and put it in his breast pocket, and she thought: he'll keep that. Whatever else he does with his careful, sanded, margin-shaped life, he'll keep that napkin.

"One more thing," she said, as he stood. "The man on the phone. When you listen to the recording—and you will, a hundred times, I know the breed—you'll hear what I heard and couldn't place until this minute." She looked up at him. "He sings it like someone who learned it from a woman singing to a child. Not a child learning it. A *woman singing to a child.* There's a difference. You'll hear it."

Ivo Kavanagh stood in the bad light of the café with his precise hands and his folded napkin, and for a moment he looked less like a man made of margins and more like a man made of a locked door that someone had just said the right word to.

"Sunday," he said, and left.

Teodora sat alone with two cold cups and thought about the box in her flat, ninety hours of voices, a third catalogued, and about the quarter-turn of his cup on the saucer, and about the fish-shadow that had moved under his face when she said *Row Street*—not grief, she'd decided. Recognition. The look of a man who has just learned that a place he buried is still, somewhere, breathing.

She paid for both coffees, out of the petty cash she kept for sources, and walked back toward the station through the grey morning, already composing the Sunday in her head: the tapes, the order she'd play them in, and the one she'd save for last—the tape she hadn't told him about, the one from the box she'd catalogued in her first month, the one labeled in Halina Dorn's handwriting with nothing but a date and a single word.

The word was *Ana.*
