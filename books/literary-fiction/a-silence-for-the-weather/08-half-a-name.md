## 8. Half a Name

The boxes had been in the storage unit for twenty-nine years.

Ivo knew this because the date was written on the top box in his own handwriting, in the ballpoint he'd been holding the day the ambulance came back empty: *Ana Kavanagh — effects — do not open.* He had written *do not open* like a man sealing a tomb, and then he had paid rent on the tomb every month since, a small monthly fee for the privilege of not grieving. Four hundred and eleven pounds a year, multiplied by twenty-nine. He had never done the arithmetic before. He did it now, standing in the cold corridor of the storage facility with the roller door rattling up behind him, and the number that arrived was obscene. He could have bought a piano. He could have paid for a headstone. He had paid instead for silence, and silence, it turned out, was not even free.

The song on the radio had done this. Six nights of it now, humming under everything he did, the way a splinter hums under skin. He had told himself he would wait until the weekend, but it was Tuesday and he was here, and the man at the desk had asked no questions, which Ivo appreciated. Questions were things he answered for a living. He resented them in his private hours.

*Do not open* had faded to a ghost of ink. He put his thumb over it anyway, as if covering a mouth, and lifted the lid.

The smell came first. Camphor and old paper and something sweeter underneath — dried lavender, maybe, or the ghost of the soap she used, the grey bars from the shop on Velska Row that sold everything by weight. It went through him like a current through wire. Thirty years of professional composure, and one bar of soap nearly finished him.

He stood very still until the corridor light buzzed and steadied him. Then he began.

His mother had been an orderly woman in the way of people who own almost nothing: everything folded, everything squared, everything in its place because there were so few places. The boxes preserved her taxonomy. One for linens. One for kitchen things — the heavy iron pan he'd learned eggs in, wrapped in newspaper gone the colour of weak tea. One for books, mostly hymnals and a battered dictionary of Velskan that made his chest tighten; he'd have to come back for that one. One marked, in her careful schoolroom hand, *IVO*, which he set aside without opening, because he could feel through the cardboard that it contained his childhood — drawings, report cards, the plaster cast of his hand at seven — and he wasn't ready to be seven years old again in a fluorescent corridor.

And then there was a box with no label at all.

It sat at the bottom of the stack, smaller than the others, and it was heavier than it looked. He carried it out into the daylight near the roller door, where the November air came in sharp off the yard, and knelt over it like a man kneeling over a well.

Inside: letters.

Not many. A dozen, perhaps fifteen, bound with the same grey wool thread she'd used for darning his socks. His name was on none of them. He saw this immediately, the way you see a missing tooth in a familiar face. Every envelope was addressed in her hand — that upright, unornamented script, each letter standing at attention — and every envelope said the same word.

*Mira.*

He sat down properly on the concrete. The cold came up through his trousers and he let it, because the alternative was feeling something else.

Mira. He turned the name over. It wasn't Velskan, quite — or it was, but the old form, the river-form, the dialect his mother had spoken when she forgot herself, when she was tired or happy or singing. *Mira* meant peace in half the languages of the world and probably in the other half too, and it was also, he realized with a slow cold certainty that started in his stomach and climbed, a person. A person his mother had written to. A person whose letters she had kept, tied with thread, hidden at the bottom of a box she had apparently expected someone to store for three decades without opening.

She had known him better than he'd given her credit for.

His hands were steady — they were always steady, that was the whole architecture of him — but there was a tremor somewhere deeper, a frequency only he could hear, like the radio's night signal coming through walls. He untied the thread. It resisted, then gave, the way old knots do, all at once, forgiving.

The earliest letter was dated fourteen years after he left home. He did the arithmetic without wanting to. He had been gone fourteen years. Elias still played cello in their flat; the duty-call home was still four years away; his mother was alive in a way he had stopped picturing, alive as a woman with a pen and an afternoon and someone to write to.

*Dear Mira,* it began. *You asked me why I don't tell him. Here is why, though you will think it is a poor reason, because you are nineteen and reasons should be tall as trees at nineteen—*

Ivo stopped reading. He looked at the roller door, at the grey rectangle of yard beyond it, at a pigeon walking the fence rail with the self-importance of a foreman. He understood, in the way you understand a language you're translating — syntax first, meaning arriving after, slightly delayed, slightly deniable — that the woman on the page was not writing to a friend named Mira. Friends are addressed by surname sometimes, by endearment usually. This letter assumed a history. This letter answered questions. This letter was one side of a conversation that had been going on for years, conducted in his absence, about him.

*—because if I tell him he will come back,* the sentence finished, *and he must not come back for me. He is building a life. You can't ask a bird to return to the nest to keep the nest warm. And if he came back hating me for the asking, I would rather have the distance and the love than the presence and the resentment. Do you see? You are my peace, mira moja. That is what your name means. I didn't choose it. It chose itself.*

Ivo read the paragraph three times. On the third reading, the words rearranged themselves into the thing they actually said, and the floor of the last thirty years tilted.

A sister.

Not a friend. Not a confidante from the church sewing circle. A daughter. Born after he left — born, God, born while he was learning to make himself small between languages, while he was perfecting the smile that ended conversations, while he was becoming the man who translated other people's lives because his own required too much courage — a girl had been born into the flat above the shop on Velska Row, and his mother had raised her, and had told him nothing, and had written to her about him the way you write about weather in a country you used to live in.

He should have felt betrayed. He located the place where betrayal would go and found it occupied by something else, something more complicated and less comfortable: recognition. Because he had done the same thing. He had spent thirty years keeping people out of rooms they might have wanted to enter, telling himself it was protection — theirs, always theirs, never admitting it was his. His mother had kept a whole child secret and called it love, and he believed her, and that was the worst part. He believed her instantly, completely, the way you believe a voice you've heard singing.

He read on.

The letters spanned eighteen years. They stopped — he checked this twice, with the dread of a man checking a pulse — two years before she died. The last one was short, and its shortness frightened him more than anything else in the box.

*Dear Mira,*

*I am tired tonight and the pen is heavy. Come Sunday if you can. Bring nothing. Sing the second verse for me, the way we practised. My voice is going and I want to hear it in yours while I still know it's mine.*

*Your mother,*

*A.*

The second verse.

The radio had played one verse. He had lain in the dark six nights ago and counted the phrases like a translator counting clauses, and the melody had closed itself neatly where he remembered it closing, at the line about the river keeping what it's given — and he had thought, with the dull satisfaction of correctness, *yes, that's all of it, that's the whole song.* But songs have verses the way houses have rooms, and he had only ever been shown one room. She had sung him the first verse at the kitchen table and made him promise, and he had kept the promise so long he'd forgotten it was a promise, and all that time there had been another verse, a second verse, taught to someone else. To *Mira*. Sung to her while he was away being invisible. Practised, the letter said. *The way we practised.*

His mother had split the song. Had given each child half. Had made of a lullaby a kind of custody arrangement, and neither child had known the other existed.

He laughed — one syllable, dry, appalled — and it echoed off the corrugated walls and came back sounding like someone else.

He took the letters home. All of them. He told himself he would photocopy them and return the originals, that archives mattered, that he respected provenance — the professional reflexes of a man whose entire life was moving other people's words carefully from one place to another — but he knew, wrapping the bundle in his scarf against the wind, that he was stealing. That some part of him had been waiting twenty-nine years to steal something of hers, something that was his by blood if not by disclosure, and that the theft felt less like taking and more like reclaiming a deposit.

In the flat, he laid them out on the floor in chronological order, the way he'd lay out parallel texts before a difficult translation. Fifteen envelopes. He read them all, slowly, twice, and by midnight he had assembled, from the accumulated marginalia of a hidden correspondence, a portrait of a stranger who shared his nose.

Mira had been born in March. There had been complications; his mother wrote of a fever, of a neighbour who helped, of money worries described in the oblique grammar of the proud. Mira had been clever — *she asks what things mean, all the time, like someone else I knew* — and musical, and stubborn, and she had wanted, at seventeen, to leave Velska Row, and his mother had helped her pack and had written afterward: *I watched her go from the window and I thought, good, fly, and then I thought, this is how it was with him, and I am the kind of woman who teaches her children to leave.* There was grief in that sentence, and pride, braided together so tightly you couldn't separate them without breaking both.

And there was this, recurring, in letter after letter, a refrain his mother perhaps didn't know she was repeating:

*Don't look for him. When you're older you'll understand. Some doors you keep shut not because of what's behind them but because of what opening them costs the hinge.*

Ivo lay on the floor among the letters and stared at the ceiling, where the streetlight made its nightly appearance, the orange ghost that had replaced the view of actual sky years ago. *What opening them costs the hinge.* She had thought of herself as a hinge. She had held two doors — two children — and chosen to swing shut between them rather than let either door scrape the other. And he had spent thirty years believing himself the abandoned one, the son who fled and was not pursued, and here was the correction, delivered in her upright schoolroom hand: she hadn't failed to pursue him. She had stationed herself, deliberately, painfully, at the exact midpoint of her two absences, and stayed there until she died.

Betrayal, he thought. Then: no. Protection. Then: those were the same word, in her dialect, and possibly in everyone's.

He got up at two in the morning and made tea he didn't drink, and stood at the window, and admitted the thing he had been circling since the corridor: he was not angry. He had been robbed of his anger, the way you're robbed of a weapon you'd planned to use. Anger was the currency he'd budgeted for this moment — anger at her secrecy, at the wasted decades, at the birthday dinners of a sibling he'd never known existed — and the letters had devalued it entirely. What remained was worse. What remained was tenderness with nowhere to go, and a question with an address attached.

Because there was an address. Of course there was. His mother had posted these letters; the envelopes bore stamps and postmarks and, in the later ones, a street name in a district across the river that he knew only as a shape on maps, a place where trams went to be retired. The last envelope, the short tired one, the *sing the second verse* one, was addressed in full, number and street and postcode, in handwriting that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, as if she'd needed the address to survive even if the letter didn't.

Number 14, Alder Terrace.

He stood at the window until the orange light went grey, holding the last envelope the way you hold a ticket you haven't decided to use. Across the city, in a flat he'd never seen, a woman in her forties was asleep or waking or making coffee, a woman with his mother's voice and possibly his mother's hands, a woman who had been told *don't look for him* and had evidently obeyed, because otherwise he'd have known, wouldn't he? Someone looking for you leaves traces. Someone looking for you eventually finds you, and then you are found, and everything changes.

Nobody had ever looked for Ivo Kavanagh. He had arranged his life specifically to ensure it. And now the search had reversed direction: he was the one standing at the edge of someone else's quiet, holding letters that proved her quiet had a name, an age, and an address, and that the same woman who built his silence had built hers, brick by identical brick, and had left the blueprint in a box marked *do not open*.

He put on his coat. It was five in the morning and dark and the first buses wouldn't run for an hour, and he stood in his hallway with his hand on the door for a long time, longer than the decision required, because the decision wasn't the hard part. The hard part was knowing that whatever he did next, he would be doing it as a brother — a word he had never once applied to himself, a word that had been waiting in the language all along, patient as vocabulary, for the sentence that finally needed it.

The 47 didn't cross the river. He would have to change lines, change routes, build the journey out of connections the way he built everything, piece by borrowed piece.

He opened the door and went down into the cold.
