## 1. The Tenant

The word came to her on a Tuesday, the way lost things do—unannounced, at an hour when she should have been thinking about something else.

Coral Whitlock was translating a claim file from a warehouse fire in Rotterdam, forty pages of Dutch that had already been rendered into serviceable English by a junior associate who thought *verzekeringsmaatschappij* was a mouthful. Her job was to make it sing, or at least to make it stop limping. Insurance language was its own dialect: passive constructions stacked like sandbags, responsibility diffused until it evaporated. *The premises were found to be compromised.* By whom? By what? The fire had simply happened, the way weather happens. Nobody burned anything; damage occurred.

She was good at this. Thirty years of practice at making blame grammatically invisible.

It was while she was rewriting a sentence—*the insured party's negligence could not be excluded*—that the word surfaced, or rather the place where the word used to be. She was reaching for it the way you reach for a light switch in a room you grew up in, hand already knowing the wall, and found nothing there.

The word for the space between tide and shore.

Not the beach. Not the tideline. The in-between—the wet margin where the water has just left and hasn't decided to come back, where the sand holds the memory of the sea in every ripple. Her mother had a word for it. Of course she had; her mother had words for everything the mapmakers never bothered naming. The smell of rain before it arrives. The particular silence of a house whose people are all asleep except one. The way gulls argue. And this one—the margin, the neither-nor, the ground that belongs to the water only part-time.

She set down her pen.

The apartment was quiet in the way she had engineered it to be quiet: third floor of a building on a street with no character, double-glazed windows, a neighbor above her who walked like a man carrying something fragile he didn't want to drop. No photographs on the walls except one, and even that was a landscape—a stretch of gray water she'd bought at a charity sale because it looked like nothing anywhere. A person could live here for years and leave no more trace than breath on glass.

She said the English words aloud, testing them against the absence. *Foreshore.* Too official. *Intertidal zone.* That was a textbook talking. *The flats.* Closer—that was the local word, the plain one—but the flats were the whole exposed breadth at low tide, acres of them, while the word her mother used was narrower than that, more intimate. It named not the place but the condition. The being-between.

She closed her eyes and tried to hear her mother say it.

Nothing came. Not the word, and worse—not the voice. She could remember facts about the voice: low, a little rough at the ends of sentences, prone to dropping into the old dialect when she was tired or moved or wanted to say something the new language couldn't hold. But the sound itself, the actual grain of it, had gone the way of the word. Eroded. You don't notice a shoreline retreating until one day the porch steps end in air.

*That's how it works,* she thought. *You lose the word first. Then you lose the voice that said it. Then you lose the woman.*

She got up and made tea she didn't want, because her hands needed occupation, and stood at the window watching the street below, where a man was arguing with a parking meter and losing. Thirty years in this city and she knew no one well enough to call when the word wouldn't come. She knew people, technically. Colleagues who admired her precision. A woman at the gym who saved her a locker sometimes. A dentist who remembered her birthday because it was near his own. All of it held together with the same passive constructions she earned her living smoothing over: meetings were attended, pleasantries were exchanged, a life was had.

Renting. That was the honest word for it. She rented the apartment, rented the furniture—she'd never quite committed to owning any—and if she was truthful, which she made a discipline of never being, she rented the self too. Some other girl had lived the first eleven years of her life, a girl with a dialect and a house and a name that meant something in a town called Marrow Cove, and then that girl had been sublet to strangers' memories, and Coral had moved into the vacancy and kept the utilities on ever since.

She went back to the desk. The Rotterdam file waited. *Negligence could not be excluded.* She deleted the sentence entirely and wrote: *Someone left a heater running beside a stack of solvent-soaked rags, and everyone involved hoped no one would ask why.* It would never survive review. She kept it anyway, for one private second, the way you keep a stone in your pocket from a beach no one else remembers.

Then she translated it properly—flatly, safely, professionally—and sent the file off into the corporate dark, and fixed herself the dinner she always fixed on Tuesdays, and washed the single plate and single fork, and sat with the dictionary.

Not a real dictionary. There wasn't one. That was the grief inside the grief: the dialect her mother spoke had never been written down, or hardly ever—a few hymn transcriptions, a linguist's thesis from 1962 that treated it as a curiosity, dying on arrival. It lived in maybe two hundred mouths along thirty miles of coast, and most of those mouths belonged to people older than her mother would be now, if—

If.

Her mother had died twelve years ago, in a hospital two hundred miles from the coast, and Coral had sat by the bed holding a hand that had once pointed out the word for the between-place, and neither of them had said anything that mattered. There hadn't been time, or there hadn't been courage, or—Coral suspected now, in the long forensic light of middle age—there hadn't been permission. Some silences are so old they've become load-bearing. Remove them and the whole structure comes down.

So she did what she could. She kept a notebook, cloth-bound, unmarked on the spine, and into it she copied every dialect word she could still retrieve, with phonetic spelling and whatever definition she could reconstruct. It was a salvage operation. Bailing out a sinking boat with a thimble. She had forty-one words as of last spring. She had been afraid to count lately.

Tonight she counted.

Forty.

One gone since spring, and she couldn't even identify which one had drowned until she went down the list, checking each like names on a manifest. *Skerrick*—a small amount, almost nothing. Still there. *Hagger*—to stumble on flat ground out of pure weariness. Still there, thank God; that one had carried her through her twenties. *Meldrow*—the hour when the light goes blue and everyone in a house becomes honest. Still there.

She read down to the bottom of the page and understood.

The between-word had never been in the notebook. She had assumed it was safe—assumed, the way you assume the sea will stay behind its line. And somewhere in the years, without ceremony, without even the courtesy of a final use, it had walked out of her.

She wrote the entry anyway, in the space where it should have been:

*[ — ] n. the ground between tide and shore; the margin the water keeps returning to claim. Mother's usage. Example lost.*

Then she crossed out *Example lost*, because it was too much like an epitaph, and left the rest.

* * *

Wednesday she worked a deposition summary, Thursday a policy dispute involving a flooded vineyard, Friday nothing arrived and she sat in the apartment listening to the building breathe around her. On Saturday morning she did the thing she did every Saturday, which was to walk the same route through the same streets at the same hour, buying the same bread, passing the same dog tied outside the same bakery, a routine so stable it functioned as architecture. Invisibility, she had learned, was not achieved in a single dramatic act. It was maintained. It required upkeep, like a garden, like a lie.

The girl at the bakery counter said, "The usual?" and Coral said yes, and it pleased her and depressed her in equal measure—that her life had become compressible to a single syllable.

She was halfway home, bread warm against her ribs, when she saw the envelope.

It was in her mailbox, wedged among the pizza flyers and a utility statement, and it was wrong in every particular. It was heavy cream stock. It was hand-addressed—or typed on an actual typewriter, the letters unevenly struck, the kind of machine that required someone to sit down and commit. And the return address read:

*Est. of H. Voss, c/o Pelt & Marsh, Attorneys-at-Law, Marrow Cove.*

Voss.

The name landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She stood in the lobby holding it, hearing the well echo, and around her the ordinary morning went on—someone's radio, someone's keys, the elevator's patient groan—and none of it touched her, because she was thirty years away and eleven years old again, standing in a courtroom while a man named Voss was led past her in shackles, and his daughter Nell was somewhere in the gallery, or maybe she wasn't, maybe Coral had invented the daughter's face afterward to have someone to apologize to across the years.

She took the stairs. Three flights, the bread going cold against her ribs, the envelope held out from her body like something venomous.

At her table she opened it with a knife, though it wasn't sealed like anything dangerous. Just paper. Paper was never dangerous. She had built a career on that principle.

*NOTICE OF SALE BY PUBLIC AUCTION,* the letterhead said, and beneath it, in prose as flat and careful as anything she herself produced: the estate of Harold Voss, deceased, having failed to dispose of certain real property by private treaty, would offer the same at public auction on the twenty-sixth day of next month at the Grange Hall, Marrow Cove. The property: parcel 114, Auction Row, known locally as the former Whitlock residence, rebuilt subsequent to the fire of [date], four bedrooms, water frontage, sold as-is.

Sold as-is. As-is meaning: with everything still in it. With the walls her mother had painted and the floors her father had laid and whatever the fire hadn't taken and the rebuilders hadn't bothered to replace. As-is meaning: with her childhood still stored in it like furniture under sheets.

Coral read the notice three times. Her hands, which could steady themselves over any document, any hostile clause, any lawyer's ambush buried in subsection (b)—her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of clinical interest, as if they belonged to the girl from the courtroom and had simply been waiting thirty years for instructions.

Water finds the crack. That was another of her mother's words—one she still had, *seep*, though her mother had used it for more than water. You can seal a floor, her mother used to say, but the water knows where you didn't. It waits. It has all the patience in the world, because it isn't going anywhere. It's already home.

She put the notice face-down on the table, then turned it face-up again, because hiding it from herself was a habit she could see the shape of now, and she was tired, in a bone-deep way she rarely permitted herself to notice, of habits shaped like hiding.

Twenty-sixth of next month. The Grange Hall. Parcel 114.

She should throw it away. She knew exactly how the throwing-away would go: the envelope in the bin under the coffee grounds, the evening spent translating something anodyne, the word still missing, the life still rented, the past still holding its position offshore like weather deciding whether to come in.

Instead she reached for the cloth-bound notebook, opened it to the page with the gap in it, and wrote beneath the orphaned entry, in her smallest, most controlled hand:

*Auction. They're selling the house.*

And below that, after a long moment, the thing she had not let herself think until the pen made it true:

*I could go back.*

Outside, the city went on making its indifferent noise. Somewhere east, beyond the rooftops and the rail lines and three hundred miles of everything she'd built her invisibility out of, the tide was doing what tides do—leaving, hesitating, returning—and there was a word for that margin, her mother's word, and she had lost it, and the only place she might ever find it again was the one place on earth she had sworn, at eleven years old, never to stand in daylight again.

The notice lay on the table like a summons.

In a way, she supposed, that was exactly what it was.
