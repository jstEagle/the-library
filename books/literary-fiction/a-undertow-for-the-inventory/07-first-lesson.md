## 7. First Lesson

Maren Oduya had learned, over the long years of auditioning strangers, to read a person's hands before she read their face.

Faces lied. Faces said *I have always wanted to learn* and meant *I saw a documentary once.* Faces said *this is so important to me* and meant *I am collecting you, the way people collect a dying breed of cattle, for the satisfaction of having touched one.* But hands told the truth. The tourists' hands hovered, camera-ready. The academics' hands twitched toward notebooks before they'd earned a single syllable. And the girl who filed people — Crane, Ansel Crane, though it had taken three visits and a trick with a library card to get the name — stood in Maren's doorway on the fourth morning with her hands hanging at her sides like tools someone had forgotten to put away, and did not knock.

She just stood there. On the porch of the little house on Sabine Row, where the paint peeled in long gray ribbons and the wind chime made of spoon-handles turned without sound because there was no wind, only the harbor's slow breathing two streets away.

Maren let her stand. She was seventy-nine years old and she had buried a husband, a sister, a language, and most of her patience, and she could out-wait anyone. She went on shelling peas at the kitchen table, the radio murmuring weather in English, and listened to the girl's silence the way other people listened to music.

At last: "You said come back if I changed my mind."

"I didn't say that." Maren did not look up. A pea slipped its skin under her thumbnail. "I said come back when you changed your mind. There's a difference. One is an invitation. The other is a forecast." She looked up then, and let herself study the girl properly, because studying was free and she was old enough to be rude. Thin as a filing cabinet. Hair pulled back so tight it looked like punishment. Eyes that went everywhere except at what they wanted. "You haven't changed your mind. You've just run out of reasons not to be here."

"I have work," Ansel said. It came out automatic, a card played from a deck worn soft at the edges.

"You always have work. That's not a reason, that's a hiding place." Maren swept the peas into a bowl and stood, joints announcing themselves one by one, hip, knee, ankle, the liturgy of age. "Well. You're here. Come in or don't. But decide on the threshold, because my mother used to say a person who stands half-in is letting the cold argue both sides."

And that was how it began — not with agreement, not with the girl saying yes or even almost-yes, but with her stepping over the sill as if the floor might be electrified, and sitting down at the kitchen table across from a bowl of peas, and looking around the room the way people look around a church they've entered by mistake.

Maren put the kettle on. She had learned this too: never begin with the language. Begin with tea, with bread, with the ordinary machinery of hospitality, because a tongue opens easier through the stomach than through the will. Her own teacher — her grandmother, Naleni, who had crossed the water in 1911 with a contract and a bundle and eleven words sewn into the hem of her skirt against forgetting — had fed her first. Salt fish and cassava, and only after, the words.

The kitchen was small and crowded with the archaeology of one woman's life: photographs going silver with age, a clock that ran four minutes fast because Maren refused to correct it, shelves of jars labeled in two scripts, one fading, one gone entirely from every mouth but hers. The girl's eyes snagged on the jars. On the labels. Maren watched her lips move slightly, trying the shapes silently, and felt the old ache turn over in her chest like a stone in a tide pool.

*Careful,* she told herself. *You've been fooled before.*

* * *

"Say hello," Maren said, when the tea was poured and the girl's hands were finally occupied, wrapped around a cup too hot to drink quickly.

"In Kivunji?"

"There's no other kind in this house."

Ansel set down the cup. Straightened, very slightly, the way people do when they are about to be tested. "I don't know any."

"That's why we're here." Maren folded her hands. "Listen first. Don't try yet. *Mwende.*" She said it slowly, giving each part its weight. "*Mwen-de.* It means hello, but it means more than hello. It means *I see you standing there.* Literally. In Kivunji you cannot greet someone without admitting you have seen them. There is no greeting for pretending not to notice a person. Your language has that one. Mine doesn't."

Something moved behind the girl's eyes — quick, submerged, gone. Maren had spent a decade learning to see those movements. People flinched at strange moments, and the flinch always had an address.

"Now you."

"*Mwende.*"

"Too careful. You're handling it like glass. It isn't glass. It's a stone. Throw it." She demonstrated again, harder, the *w* rounding full, the final vowel open as a doorway. "*Mwende!*"

"*Mwende.*"

"Better. Again."

"*Mwende.*"

"Again."

The girl said it seven times. By the fifth, something loosened in her jaw. By the seventh, Maren heard it — faint, far off, like a boat heard before it's seen — the ghost of the island in the sound. Not accuracy. Accuracy would take months. But kinship. The mouth remembering something the mind had never known.

Her grandmother had called that *the salt in the blood.* Maren had thought it superstition until she'd watched enough students fail to know that some mouths opened and some mouths locked, and there was no predicting which, and no teaching past a lock.

This mouth, so far, was opening.

*Don't hope,* she warned herself, and hoped anyway. Hope was the one habit she'd never managed to break, and she was too old to start housebreaking herself now.

* * *

They worked through the morning. Greetings first — *mwende* for meeting, *saveli* for parting, which meant not goodbye but *until the road brings us level again*, because in Kivunji you did not speak of roads ending, only of them diverging and converging like tides. Then numbers, because numbers were the bones of any language and Maren had begun her own counting-backward ritual thirty years ago, reciting downward from a hundred each night so the numerals would not slip away like everything else had slipped.

Ansel took to the numbers with a frightening, hungry precision. Of course she did. Numbers were order, were filing, were safe. Maren watched her master *tano-sita-nane* in minutes and understood that the girl would use the counting to hide, the way she used the archive to hide, and resolved to starve her of it soon.

But first — the tide.

"The sea," Maren said, rising, and went to the window that faced the harbor, gray and glittering beyond the rooftops. "Every language has a word for the sea. Most languages have a word for the tide. Kivunji has—" she held up one finger, "—one word, but it does double work. Listen. *Nduma.*"

"*Nduma.*"

"No. The *d* is softer. Your tongue touches light, like this—" she showed her, the tongue tip barely grazing, "—as if the tooth were something holy you didn't want to disturb."

Ansel tried. Failed. Tried again. Her mouth resisted — Maren could see it, the small civil war happening behind her lips, the English jaw fighting the island tongue for territory. The *d* came out hard as a hammer. Came out hard again. The girl's neck went pink with frustration, and her hands flattened on the table, and Maren recognized the gesture: the archivist confronting a document that would not obey.

"Stop," Maren said. "You're fighting it."

"It won't come out right."

"Of course it won't. It's not supposed to. Your mouth has been speaking one language for—" she calculated, "—thirty years? Thirty-five? A mouth is a country with borders. I'm asking you to smuggle something across." She sat back down. "Again. Slower. And stop apologizing with your eyebrows."

That startled a breath of laughter out of the girl — brief, rusty, as if the laugh itself hadn't been spoken aloud in years and needed oiling. Maren pretended not to notice, and noticed everything.

On the ninth attempt, the *d* softened. Barely. Enough.

"*Nduma,*" Ansel said, and Maren closed her eyes for a moment, because for one syllable the kitchen filled with another kitchen, sixty years gone, and her grandmother's voice saying the same word over a pot of fish broth while rain came off the harbor in ropes.

"The tide," Maren said, when she trusted her voice. "But also: what returns. What goes out and insists on coming back. When we say *nduma* about a person — *nduma ya nia* — we mean someone who keeps returning. To a place. To a grief. To a door." She opened her eyes and looked directly at the girl. "Your people would call it stubbornness. We call it tide."

The color rose in Ansel's face again, but differently now. Caught, Maren thought. Caught at something. The girl who files people, who corrects strangers' names in the dark of the records office and signs nothing of her own — caught by a single word, as if the word had been waiting for her specifically, patient as water.

Maren did not ask. Asking was a door slammed in your face. She poured more tea instead, and waited, the way the tide waits, which is to say without impatience, because impatience is for things that doubt they will arrive.

* * *

In the afternoon, when the light through the window had gone the color of weak honey, Maren taught her the hard word.

She had been saving it. Every student got it eventually, and every student failed it, and she had built a small private museum of their failures: the seminarian who wept and left; the linguistics doctoral candidate who transcribed it phonetically and thanked her and never returned; the middle-aged man from the islanders' association who said it perfectly and then asked, fatally, whether it could be put on a T-shirt.

"This one," Maren said, "you will not get today. Maybe not this month. I teach it to you now so it can begin working in you, the way a seed works in ground that looks like nothing is happening. Are you listening?"

Ansel nodded. Her pen — she had produced a notebook at some point, of course she had, a narrow black one — hovered.

"Put the pen down."

The girl hesitated, then set it down, and Maren felt a small victory pass through her like warmth.

"*Kolima,*" she said.

She said it only once. Some words deserved the dignity of solitude.

"It means *left behind.*" She looked out the window at the harbor while she spoke, because it was easier to say these things to water than to faces. "Not abandoned — we have a different word for abandoned, and it's an ugly word, it deserves to be. *Kolima* is gentler. It means the thing that stays when the rest has gone. The shell when the animal has moved. The name when the person has died. The word when everyone who spoke it—" she made a small gesture that took in the kitchen, the jars, the whole shrinking territory of her life, "—hasn't."

Silence. The clock ran its four fast minutes. Somewhere down the street a dog was barking at gulls.

"When I die," Maren said, still to the window, "this word will mean me. For a while. Until someone takes it up." She turned back. "That's all a language is, girl. A relay. Each speaker holds the word a while, runs with it, passes it. My grandmother held it. I have held it longer than I was meant to. The question was never whether I would drop it. The question is whether anyone's hand is under mine when I do."

Ansel's face had gone very still. Not blank — still, the way deep water is still, with currents underneath. Her hands were flat on the table again, and Maren saw that the knuckles were white.

"I should go," the girl said. "It's late. I have—"

"Work. Yes. The famous work." Maren waved a hand, dismissive, generous. "Go. Come Thursday. Bring nothing. Especially not the notebook."

At the threshold, Ansel stopped. Half-in, half-out, the cold arguing both sides. Maren waited.

"Why did you choose me?" The question came out rough, unpolished, as if it had been carried a long way. "You said you audition people. You've been watching me at the library for weeks. Why me? I'm nobody. I file things. Nobody knows I exist."

Maren considered lying, or being kind, or being wise. She had all three available, worn soft from use.

"Because you write postcards to a burned-down house," she said.

She watched the blow land. Watched the girl go white, then blotchy, then rigid — the whole weather system of a private grief passing over her face in the space of two seconds. The postcards. Maren had seen her, once, at the sorting window of the general post office, mailing one; had seen the address written in that cramped, controlled hand, and had asked a postal clerk, idly, about the lot on Verrine Street, and been told the story in the bored tone clerks reserve for local tragedies: fire, years ago, a woman dead, a daughter who wrote anyway.

Everyone in this city was haunted. Maren had simply learned to tell the difference between the ones who fled their ghosts and the ones who kept feeding them.

"A person who writes letters to ash," Maren said quietly, "already knows the hardest thing there is to know about language. That you can speak to what cannot answer, and survive it. That the speaking is not for them. It's for you." She stepped closer. "I am not going to be able to answer you much longer either, Ansel Crane. So think about which of us is writing to whom."

Then she shut the door — gently, because cruelty done gently lasts longer — and stood with her ear near the wood, listening to the girl breathe on the other side. Ten seconds. Twenty. Then footsteps, retreating, uneven, down the walk and away toward the harbor.

* * *

That night Maren could not sleep.

She sat up in bed with the lamp on and the photograph album open to the page she always opened to: the wharf at home, 1953, her father's generation lined up along the pilings, squinting into a sun that had since set on all of them. Behind the men, half-cut-off by the frame, the meeting hall on Delacroix Street as it was when it was new — the bright paint, the sign in Kivunji script that half this city could once read and no one alive could now, except her.

Except her, and perhaps — *perhaps*, said the dangerous voice — the girl with the locked jaw and the opening mouth.

Maren had taught her nine words today. Nine. At this rate, if the girl came twice a week, if she did not flee, if her mouth continued its slow smuggling — Maren did arithmetic she hated doing and arrived at a number that was not a number at all but a shape of absence. There was not time. There was never going to be time. She had known it for a year, since the doctor used the word *progressive* in his gentle bureaucratic voice, since the nights she reached for a word and found the shelf empty where it had stood that morning. The language was leaving her the way the tide leaves, gradually and then all at once, and she was racing it downhill with cupped hands.

Nine words. She laughed aloud in the dark room, a dry sound with no humor in it. Nine words, and the hall coming down — she'd seen the notices stapled to the door last month, the city's clean bureaucratic squares, DEVELOPMENT PARCEL 14-C, comments due by such-and-such a date, as if a building could be commented out of existence, as if paper ever lost an argument with paper.

Unless.

Maren turned the thought over. The girl worked in the records office. The girl knew where documents lived and how they breathed and what they feared. The girl had spent ten years making herself invisible inside the city's paperwork — and invisible people, Maren knew from long experience, saw everything and were seen by nothing.

She did not finish the thought. Finishing thoughts was a young woman's sport. She closed the album, turned off the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to the harbor breathe, and practiced, silently, in the privacy of her failing skull, the word for *left behind* — *kolima, kolima* — holding it the way you hold a coal passed hand to hand, feeling it burn, refusing to drop it.

Thursday, she thought. Come Thursday. Bring nothing.

Bring your stubborn tide of a heart, girl. Bring whatever it is you're carrying in those flat white-knuckled hands.

And bring, if you have it in you — and Maren was beginning to suspect, with a hope that frightened her more than dying did — bring the keys to the filing room.
