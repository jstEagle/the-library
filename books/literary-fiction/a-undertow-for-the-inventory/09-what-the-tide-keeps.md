## 9. What the Tide Keeps

The girl who files people has started coming on Tuesdays.

Maren notices this the way she notices weather—not with surprise but with a kind of professional respect for patterns. Three Tuesdays now. The first time, Ansel Crane stood in Maren's doorway holding a canvas bag of groceries she claimed was a delivery error, though no delivery service had ever confused this street with anywhere else. The second time, the excuse was a library book that had to be returned to the harbor branch anyway, and wasn't this practically on the way. The third time there was no excuse at all, only Ansel standing on the step with her hands empty and her face arranged into something like indifference, which Maren recognized immediately because it was her own face from sixty years ago, worn by every Oduya before her.

Today is Tuesday. Maren has put out two cups without comment. She will not offer; offering is begging, and Oduyas do not beg. But the cups are out, and one of them is the blue one with the chip on the rim, and Ansel will take it because it is the one closest to her hand, and this small choreography is as close to affection as either of them will admit to in daylight.

They are working on numbers when the memory comes. Not the counting itself—Ansel has the numbers now, all the way to a hundred, her accent still thick as harbor mud but her ear true, which is more than Maren can say for most of the auditioners, the failures, the polite ghosts she has tried to teach over the years. No, it is the way Ansel counts backward that does it. *Kumi, tisa, nane, saba*—ten, nine, eight, seven—counting down the way Maren taught her, the way you count down a wave before it breaks, and suddenly Maren is eight years old and standing on the seawall at Point Ferren with her father's hand closed around her wrist, counting down.

"Stop," she says.

Ansel stops. Her mouth stays open around the next number, *sita*, six, and Maren looks at that open mouth and thinks: there it is. There is the door.

"You count well," Maren says, "but you don't know what you're counting."

"The tide," Ansel says. "You said the old speakers counted the tide."

"I said we counted *with* the tide. There's a difference." Maren turns her cup in its saucer. The tea has gone cold; she drinks it anyway, cold tea being another inheritance, from a mother who believed hot water was for the sick and the wealthy. "Sit properly. I'm going to tell you something today, and I'm only going to tell it once, so your ears had better be worth the trouble."

Ansel sits. She has the particular stillness of people who have spent their lives trying not to be noticed—the shoulders drawn in, the hands folded, the whole body a closed envelope. Maren has watched her for weeks now, filing away observations the way the girl herself files paper, and she has decided something she has not yet said aloud: this one might last. This one might actually stay until the end. Which means it is time to give her the weight. A language carried without its grief is just vocabulary. Anyone can learn words. What Maren needs to pass on is what the words cost.

"In 1954," Maren begins, "the water came up Delacroix Street to the second-story windows."

* * *

She was eight. The house on Delacroix was her grandmother's then, built by men whose names were not on any deed because the deeds were held by the fruit company, and the fruit company did not write island names on its papers—it wrote numbers. Maren's grandfather had been number forty-one. Her father had refused a number and so had been assigned a letter instead, B-7, stamped on his work permit like a brand on a steer. When Maren was born, her father stood in the registry office and gave her a name in Kivunji, and the clerk wrote it down wrong, and her father looked at the misspelling for a long moment and then said, "Leave it. Let them practice."

That was the humor of those days. It was thin humor, stretched over fear like skin over water, but it was theirs.

The storm came in October, on a night when the moon was wrong—too low, too fat, sitting on the horizon like something drowning. Maren remembers her grandmother standing in the yard looking at that moon and saying one word, *maji-kuja*, water-coming, an old compound word from the island that had no use in this flat coastal city except on perhaps three nights in a hundred years. Her grandmother said it calmly, the way she said everything, and then went inside and began moving the furniture to the high shelves.

By midnight the water was in the streets. By one it was at the doors. And here is the thing Maren needs Ansel to understand, the thing she has carried for seventy years like a stone in the shoe: the Kivunji families lived lowest. Delacroix Street, the flats behind the cannery, the lanes by the harbor—all of it had been sold or rented to the island laborers because no one else wanted land that smelled of fish meal and flooded when the moon sulked. The white families, the city families, they lived uphill, on the terraces, in houses with foundations. But water does not read property lines. Water came up the hill that night. It came up the terraces and through the foundations and into the parlors of people who had never once spoken to their downhill neighbors except to complain about the smell of drying fish.

"And who knew the water?" Maren asks, and answers herself before Ansel can. "We did. We knew it the way you know a relative. My grandmother could read that moon. My father could feel the pressure change in his bad knee and tell you within the hour when the surge would come. The old people stood on their porches and counted the waves coming in—*moja, mbili, tatu*—and they could tell by the seventh wave whether the sea meant business or theater. That counting you learned today? That is not arithmetic, girl. That is survival arithmetic. That is how you know how long you have."

The Kivunji families went out into the flood. In rowboats, in washtubs lashed to planks, in some cases swimming, hauling neighbors off roofs and out of upper windows. Maren's father made eleven trips that night, and on the last one his boat was crushed against the church fence and he swam the final stretch with a child under each arm, and the child under his left arm was the daughter of the man who would later sign the schoolyard ordinance. Maren has never forgotten this. She doubts God has either.

By dawn, forty-one people who did not speak Kivunji owed their lives to people who did. The newspaper called them heroes. There was a ceremony at the city hall, photographs, a plaque—Maren's father kept the clipping his whole life, yellowed soft as cloth, folded and refolded until the ink wore away in the creases. For about a year, the city was grateful. White women brought casseroles to Delacroix Street. A councilman shook her father's hand so hard Maren thought the arm might come off.

Then the gratitude curdled, the way gratitude always does when it has been sitting out too long in the heat of other people's discomfort. Because here was the problem, seen clearly from the terraces: if the islanders could read the water, what else could they read? They had saved the city, yes—but they had done it with knowledge the city didn't have, in a language the city couldn't understand, organized among themselves without asking anyone's permission. The casserole ladies began to notice that when the Kivunji families talked among themselves on their own porches, the white neighbors couldn't follow. And a people you cannot overhear is a people you cannot trust. That was the logic. It was never stated so plainly, of course. It arrived dressed as concern. It arrived as petitions about school standards, about children being confused, about American values, about fairness to the children themselves—always the children, always fairness—and in 1957 the school board passed the rule that no language but English would be spoken in the schoolyards at recess.

Maren was eleven. She remembers the first day of the rule. A teacher—a woman named Mrs. Calloway, whose life Maren's father had arguably saved, whose roof his shoulder had held while her sons climbed down—stood in the yard and slapped a ruler against her palm whenever she heard island speech, and the children learned, within a week, to go silent. Not to switch languages. To go silent. Because the punishment fell on the tongue itself, and a child protects her tongue by hiding it, and a hidden tongue, unused, unexercised, begins to die in the mouth like a muscle in a cast.

"That is how you kill a language," Maren tells Ansel, and her voice comes out flatter than she intends, flatter than the feeling underneath it. "You do not burn books. You do not need soldiers. You wait for a flood, you let us save you, and then you forbid our children to speak at recess. Three generations. That's all it takes. My mother's generation spoke it in the home. My generation spoke it in whispers. My daughter—" and here Maren pauses, because Ruthie is a wound she keeps wrapped, "my daughter understood it and answered in English, and my granddaughter knows four words, and the four are food words, because a people can always be permitted its cooking."

Ansel has not moved. She has the listening posture of someone who understands silence professionally, and Maren finds, to her irritation, that this makes her want to say more, not less. Dangerous. She pours more cold tea to occupy her hands.

"People ask me—when they ask at all—why I stayed in this city. Why I didn't move somewhere warm, somewhere the language might have cousins, why I kept the house on Delacroix through everything." Maren looks toward the window, where the light is doing its late-afternoon trick of making even the rusted gutters across the street look gilded. "I stayed because of the water. Do you understand? The water took things from us, but it also proved something. For one night, the city needed us exactly as we were. Not improved. Not assimilated. Needed. And I have never decided whether that night was a gift or an insult. Some mornings it is one and some mornings the other, and I am eighty-one years old and I still have not decided."

* * *

Afterward they sit in the quiet that follows a heavy telling, and Maren watches Ansel turn the blue cup in her hands and knows the girl wants to say something consoling and is wisely failing to find it. Good. Consolation is cheap. What Maren wants is witness.

"Bitterness," Maren says finally. "You're wondering whether I'm bitter."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"No. You file things. You'd have filed the question away for later." Maren allows herself the smallest smile. "Yes, I'm bitter. I want that understood, and I want it understood correctly. Bitterness is not a failure of character. It is an accurate record. My bitterness is the most honest document I own. Every sweet old woman you meet who survived something and says she holds no grudges—I promise you, she has simply misplaced the paperwork." She taps her own temple. "Mine is all filed here. Cross-referenced. Alphabetical."

Ansel almost laughs. Maren counts it a victory.

"But hear the rest of it," Maren continues, leaning forward, because this part matters, this part is the actual lesson, more than any vocabulary. "Bitterness kept the language alive when nothing else could. You think we spoke Kivunji in our homes after 1957 out of love? Partly. But partly out of spite. Every sentence my mother spoke in that kitchen was a sentence Mrs. Calloway's ruler could not reach. Spite is a preservative, girl. Salt keeps fish. Anger keeps tongues. The problem—the great, stupid problem—is that salt keeps the fish but it also keeps you tasting salt in everything, forever, until you cannot tell whether you love the food or only the preserving of it."

She stops. She hears what she has said and lets it lie there between them, because Ansel is clever enough under all that gray quiet to catch it, and because Maren is not certain she wants to explain it even to herself.

Outside, a gull lands on the fence rail and screams once, twice, with the outrage of the permanently shortchanged. Maren watches it. On nights like the flood night, her grandmother used to say, the gulls left the coast entirely days in advance—they knew, they always knew, and no one thought to ask them anything.

"There's a hearing coming," Maren says, changing course abruptly, because she has decided, in the way of old women who have run out of patience for gradual approaches. "For the hall. The city posted the notices. Redevelopment office." She watches Ansel's face very carefully as she says it, and sees—there, quick as a fish under a boat—the flicker. The recognition. So the girl already knows. The girl works in the very office that is killing the building where Maren's father's photograph hangs crooked on the wall, where the flood-year minutes are kept in a biscuit tin, where every plank smells of sixty years of incense and fish stew and argument.

Maren says nothing about the flicker. She files it.

"They'll take comments from the public," she goes on. "A comment period. Two minutes at a microphone, if anyone bothers." She lifts her chin at Ansel. "Nobody will bother. Nobody who speaks will speak. And I will tell you what I told the others I tried to teach, the ones who failed me: a language that is never spoken aloud in the rooms where decisions are made is already dead. It is just waiting for someone to file the paperwork."

Ansel sets down the blue cup. Her hands are not quite steady, and Maren pretends not to see, and both of them let the pretense stand, because between these two women pretense is a form of mercy.

"It's getting late," Ansel says.

"Yes," Maren agrees. "It has been getting late since 1954."

At the door, as Ansel steps out into the bruised evening light, Maren catches her sleeve—lightly, barely, the touch of a woman who has learned not to grasp at people—and says the thing she has been circling all afternoon:

"Next Tuesday, I'll teach you the word for *left behind*. It's a beautiful word. It's also, I'm sorry to say, the most commonly used word in the entire language."

Ansel nods once and walks down the steps into the dark, and Maren stands in her doorway watching her go, thinking about floods and rulers and the taste of salt, and wondering—not for the first time, but with new urgency—whether this silent, careful, envelope-of-a-girl will prove to be the seventh wave. The one that means the sea means business.
