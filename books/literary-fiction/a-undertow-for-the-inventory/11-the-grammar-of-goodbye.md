## 11. The Grammar of Goodbye

The girl who files people has begun to arrive before I do.

I notice it on the fourth morning, coming up Delacroix Street with my cane ticking against the pavement like a slow clock, and there she is already at my door, standing in the salt wind with her coat buttoned to the throat and her hands folded as though she is waiting for a train that will never come. She does not knock. She would not dare. She stands outside my house the way she must stand outside other people's lives — patient, invisible, rehearsing her apology for existing.

"Ansel," I say, because I have decided she will hear her name spoken aloud every day whether she likes it or not. A name is a door. You keep knocking until someone opens it from the inside.

"Maren." She says it carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "I brought—" She lifts a paper bag. "Bread. From the bakery on Corvus. The woman said it was good yesterday."

Yesterday. Everything this girl says arrives one day late, wrapped in cotton, secondhand. But bread is bread. I take the bag and hold the door open and do not thank her, because thanks are a wall, and we have no time for walls anymore. Though I did not know yet how little time we had. That knowledge was still sitting quietly inside me like an egg, unhatched, warm, waiting for its morning.

* * *

Inside, the light comes through the east windows the color of weak tea. My mother's table — brought across an ocean, across two deaths, its legs shimmed with folded newspaper from 1961 — holds everything I own of Kivunji now: three notebooks gone soft at the corners, a tin of pencils, a reel-to-reel recorder heavier than a sleeping cat, and a jar of sea glass I keep for no reason except that my grandmother kept one, and somewhere behind all reasons there is a woman counting shells on a beach that no longer exists under a language that lives now only in my mouth.

Ansel sits where she always sits, spine straight, hands in her lap, a student made of held breath.

"Today," I say, "we kill the future."

She blinks. "I'm sorry?"

"In your language — in English, in French, in all the languages they let us learn in school — the future is a room down the hall. You walk into it. *I will go. I will be. It will rain.*" I tap the table. "Kivunji has no such room. We tore it down centuries ago, or maybe we never built it. Maybe we looked at the ocean every day of our lives and understood that nothing arrives by being promised."

"What replaces it?"

"The arriving form." I write it for her, the long looping construction, the verb wearing its suffix like a boat wearing water. "*Nkola-vu.* Literally: it-is-on-its-way-of-being. Not *it will rain.* Rather: *the rain is arriving.* Not *I will die.* Rather: *my dying is on the water.*"

"That seems—" She stops herself. Ansel stops herself constantly; she is a woman made almost entirely of stopped sentences. "That seems sad."

"Sad!" I laugh, and the laugh costs me something in my chest, a small tearing like wet paper. "Child. It is the opposite of sad. Your future tense is a lie you tell yourselves so you can sleep. *It will be fine. We will always have time. I will say it tomorrow.* Kivunji refuses to lie. In Kivunji, tomorrow is not a possession. Tomorrow is weather. It may come; it may turn aside. So we speak of things as *arriving*, and if they arrive, we are grateful, and if they don't—" I shrug, and my shoulder aches doing it, everything aches now, my body has become a list of small complaints — "then the sentence simply never finishes itself. The language knows how to leave a door open without pretending anyone will walk through it."

Ansel is writing this down. Her handwriting is small and exact, archivist's script, each letter filed in its proper drawer. I watch her copy *nkola-vu* three times and I think: she writes the way her mouth won't speak. Everything arrives on paper. Nothing arrives in air.

"Say it," I tell her.

"*Nkola-vu.*"

"Again. The *vu* is not a word, it is breath leaving a sail. Let it go soft."

"*Nkola... vu.*"

"Better. Again."

And she says it again, and again, and on the fifth time something loosens in her jaw — I see it happen, the small surrender of muscle — and the word comes out of her whole, round, alive, and for one moment the kitchen holds two speakers of Kivunji instead of one, and I have to look out the window at the gray harbor so she will not see what my face does.

Two. For one minute, two.

* * *

We work through the morning. Greetings first, always greetings — *mweni ka,* the hello that literally means *I see your day* — then numbers, then the tide words, which are many, because an island makes a hundred names for the one thing that rules it. There is the tide that comes to feed and the tide that comes to take. There is the tide that lies. There is the word for the moment the water turns, that held breath between coming and going — *sulima* — and I make her say it twenty times because it is the most beautiful word we have and beauty should be drilled like arithmetic.

At noon I tire. This is new, this tiredness that arrives not gradually but all at once, like a wave that has been traveling toward me since last winter and only now makes landfall. I sit down heavily in my chair by the window and pretend it is a choice.

"Tea," I say. "Second shelf. The dark tin, not the one with the rust."

She moves through my kitchen competently, silently, and while her back is turned I study her the way I used to study the harbor from my father's boat — reading the water for what it hides. Ten years I have watched this city misplace its people. The islanders came in 1911 and 1923, contract labor, sugar and salt fish, and they carried Kivunji in their mouths like coins sewn into a coat lining, and the city took the labor and left the language to starve. Schoolteachers rapped knuckles. Landlords frowned. Children learned quickly which words made them strange, and strangeness is a debt children pay faster than any other. By the time I was grown, the language lived in kitchens and funerals and nowhere else. Now it lives here. In this tea-colored light. In me.

One speaker. One. I am a country whose entire population is a single old woman who cannot climb her own stairs without resting twice.

Ansel sets the tea before me and sits, and I decide — the way you decide to step off a ledge you've been circling for weeks — that today is the day.

"Put down your pencil," I say.

She puts it down instantly, obediently, and something in her readiness twists in me, tender and terrible. This girl has spent ten years waiting to be told what to do by someone who sees her.

"I'm going to tell you why the lessons can't be slow," I say. "And I want you to listen without filing anything."

Her hands go still in her lap.

"In March I went to the clinic on Halden Street. Chest pains. Tiredness. They did their tests, their photographs of my insides, their machines that beep like patient birds." I wrap my hands around the cup. The heat hurts and I hold on anyway; pain is proof of present tense, and I have become a great collector of the present. "There is a growth. They gave it a Latin name, which I have already forgotten, because Latin is just another colonizer's language and I refuse to give it houseroom. What matters is the shape of it, and the shape of it is bad. They offered me procedures. I declined. I am seventy-nine years old. I have buried a husband, two sisters, and every person alive who spoke my mother's tongue besides myself. I am not interested in spending my last season in a hospital arguing with machines."

The kitchen is very quiet. Outside, a gull drags its cry across the rooftops.

"A season," Ansel says. Her voice is thin. "You mean—"

"I mean autumn, possibly. Possibly less. The doctor said *months* in English, but English exaggerates comfort and minimizes grief, so let me put it properly." And I say it in Kivunji, slowly, watching her write even though I told her not to — some habits are deeper than instruction: "*Nkufwa nkola-vu.* My dying is on the water. It may be a fast boat. It may be a slow one. But it launched in March, and I felt it leave the shore."

Ansel's face does something complicated. Grief and panic fighting for the same doorway. She looks down at her notebook, at the word *sulima* written twenty times, the held breath between tides, and I watch her understand, maybe for the first time, what she has been drilling.

"Why teach me at all, then?" she asks, and her voice cracks on *me*, as if she cannot believe she is the answer to anything. "If there isn't time. If I'm — Maren, I'm a bad student. My mouth doesn't work right. You said yourself the sounds resist me. Why waste—"

"Waste?" I lean forward, and my bones protest, and I lean anyway. "Listen to me, girl who files people. Do you know what I have been doing for thirty years? Auditioning. Every Kivunji descendant in this city, I tried. The Oduya cousins — gone to Toronto, too busy. Old Etienne's grandchildren — embarrassed, every one, they smile at me like I'm a photograph. I stood up at that church on Verrone Street in 1998 and asked, in front of two hundred people, *who will learn?* Two hundred people looked at their shoes. Their shoes were very interesting, apparently. Thirty years of shoes." I drink the tea. It has gone lukewarm. Everything goes lukewarm eventually; that is the whole of mortality in a teacup. "Then you walked into the harbor library looking like a woman who had misplaced her own funeral, and you stopped to listen to an old woman counting backward in a dead language, and you didn't look away. Do you know what that is? That is not interest. Interest looks away after a polite minute. That is recognition. Something in you knew what it was watching — a person keeping count so that something isn't lost — because you do it too. Postcards to a burned house. Don't think I haven't figured that out. You've been keeping count of your mother for ten years."

Her breath catches. I pretend not to hear it. Kindness sometimes means aiming slightly wide.

"So no," I say, gentler now, because the fierceness has done its work and what remains is only the truth, "there is no time for you to be a bad student slowly. We will make you a bad student quickly. We will fill whatever season I have with as much Kivunji as your stubborn mouth can hold, and when my boat comes in, you will be standing on the shore with the words still ringing in you, and you will pass them on broken, the way everything true gets passed on. That is the whole grammar of goodbye, child. Nothing survives intact. Things survive by being handed off mid-fall."

* * *

She stays past dark. I teach her the arriving form applied to farewell — there is a construction, *kwenda-vu*, leaving-that-is-arriving, which our elders used at docks and deathbeds, the goodbye that acknowledges the return it may never witness — and she copies it until her pencil wears flat, and I let her, because rhythm is a kind of prayer and repetition is how the body learns what the mind resists.

When she finally gathers her coat, she pauses at the door. The wind comes in around her, smelling of low tide and diesel and the cold coming season.

"Maren," she says. "The tea. Next time, I'll bring the honey from my side of town. It's better."

It is the longest sentence she has ever volunteered about the future. Small as a coin, but she minted it herself. I nod, and she goes, and I stand in the doorway watching her figure shrink down Delacroix Street under the broken streetlight, walking not like a woman fleeing but like a woman carrying something heavy and precious that she has only just agreed to hold.

Inside, I lower myself into the window chair and let the tiredness take me fully, wave over wave. On the table her notebook lies open where she forgot it — or did not forget it; I am beginning to suspect the girl forgets strategically, leaves pieces of herself in rooms so she has a reason to return — and I reach for it meaning only to close it.

But it falls open to earlier pages. Weeks of pages. And there, among the vocabulary, in her small exact hand, I find sentences that are not vocabulary at all. Fragments. *Mother. The lot on Verrone. The letter I never answered.* Written in English, half-hidden between tide words, the way a child hides a note inside a schoolbook.

I read one line twice. Then I close the book softly, the way you close a door on a sleeping person, and I sit in the dark with my hand flat on the cover, and I understand that next lesson, whether she is ready or not, we are going to stop hiding notes inside schoolbooks — hers and mine both.

The dying is on the water. There is no time left for armor.
