## 2. What the Postcard Didn't Say

My mother kept her papers the way she kept her secrets: alphabetized, boxed, and labeled in a handwriting so tidy it looked printed. I found the postcard on the third evening of sorting her estate, wedged between *Insurance—Auto* and *Invoices—Paid (1987–1991)*, which was exactly where she would have hidden something she wanted no one to find.

It was a picture of Gullvik harbor. Boats like toys, water the color of hammered tin, a church steeple leaning into the wind as if eavesdropping. On the back, in my mother's hand, half a sentence:

*He chose the sea because it never asked him to*—

And then nothing. The pen had lifted mid-word, or mid-thought, and never come down again. No signature. No date. The postcard had never been stamped, never addressed, never sent. It was a letter to nobody, from nobody, about a man who had made himself both.

I sat on the floor of her study with the card in my lap for a long time. Outside, the city went on being a city—buses sighing, someone's television laughing at its own jokes—and inside, the house did what it always did when I visited, which was smell faintly of cardamom and dust, the two scents my mother had never managed to separate.

*He chose the sea because it never asked him to.*

To what? Stay? Explain? Be a father? The sentence was a door with the handle missing. I turned it over in my hands until midnight, and then I did what I do with everything I cannot digest: I wrote around it. Three hundred words on grief and harbors that would have earned me a polite nod from my editor and nothing else, because they said everything and meant nothing, which is the house style I have practiced for ten years now, the way other people practice scales.

The song was still in my head. That was the trouble. Songs are supposed to leave. This one had moved in, hung its coat by the door, and started rearranging the furniture.

---

My mother's last months were spent mostly in the kitchen, though she could no longer cook. She would sit at the table with her tea going cold and direct me like a conductor whose orchestra had wandered off years ago.

"More lemon," she'd say. "No. More than that. Freya, lemon is not a rumor."

"I'm a restaurant critic, Mother. I know about lemon."

"You write about restaurants. Knowing is different." She'd tap the table twice, her signal for *try again*, and I would zest another lemon while she watched me with an expression I mistook, then, for criticism. It was only later—much later, standing in her kitchen after the funeral—that I understood it had been longing. She was looking at food the way you look at a country you've been exiled from.

She talked more, near the end. Not confessions, exactly. Fragments. She told me about a dance hall in Gullvik where the floor bounced. She told me my father could whistle two notes at once, which I refused to believe until she demonstrated, badly, coughing, laughing at herself. She told me the sea in Gullvik was "honest," which I took for one of her aphorisms until I realized she meant it literally—the tide there came all the way in and went all the way out, hiding nothing, unlike certain men and certain mothers.

Once, she said, "If you ever hear the song—" and stopped, and drank her cold tea, and asked me to pass the lemon.

I should have asked. That is the sentence I return to. *I should have asked.* But asking felt like trespassing, and we were two women who had built our whole relationship out of not trespassing, each of us keeping to our own garden, waving politely over the fence for thirty years.

---

The radio station took me four days to find.

I had recorded the broadcast on my phone, badly, the way you record a bird—you get the song and also your own breathing, a neighbor's vacuum, the ghost of a bus. I played it for a friend who works in music licensing, and she narrowed her eyes and said, "That's not a recording, that's a live studio take. Someone played that on purpose. Small station, probably regional. Why?"

"It's a long story."

"Is it about a man?"

"It's about a postcard."

She looked at me the way people look at me when I say things like that, which is to say, kindly, and from a slight distance.

The station was KRVS-Gullvik, broadcasting at a wattage so modest the engineer I finally reached described their signal as "basically a strong suggestion." The DJ who hosted the Sunday night program—a show called, with the confidence of a man who has never worried about ratings, *Whatever the Tide Brings In*—was named Halvard Brandt. I called the station three times. The first two times, a machine told me, in a voice suspiciously like Halvard's, that he was unavailable to persons who would not leave their names. The third time, I left my name, my number, and, in a moment of weakness, the sentence: *You played a song on Sunday that my father wrote before I was born.*

He called back within the hour.

"How do you know that?" His voice was gravel poured slowly. Old gravel. Sea-tumbled.

"I don't. I know it's his. I've known the melody since I was a child. He used to hum it."

A silence on the line, long enough that I checked whether the call had dropped.

"What was your name again?"

"Freya Nystrom."

Another silence, longer, and this one had weather in it.

"Nystrom," he said. "Yes. Yes, I suppose you would be." And then, before I could ask anything at all: "I can't talk about this on the phone."

"Then talk about it off the phone. I'll come to Gullvik."

"That's a long way to come for a man who'll tell you nothing."

"Sir, I once flew nine hours to review a tasting menu that consisted entirely of things served on rocks. I am very comfortable with disappointment."

He laughed—one dry bark, quickly suppressed, as if laughter were a habit he'd given up along with other vices. "Sunday," he said. "After the show. There's a café by the harbor. The one with the green door. You'll know it."

"How will I know it?"

"Because it's the only café in Gullvik with a green door, and because your father painted it that color, and everyone here has been too stubborn to repaint it for thirty years."

He hung up before I could ask the obvious question, which was *why*, which was *what happened*, which was *who are you to him*. Instead I sat holding the phone, listening to the dial tone, thinking: *a green door.* My father had left a color behind. A small, permanent mark on a town I'd never seen, made by a man who hadn't managed to leave so much as a forwarding address for his daughter.

---

I packed that night. Or rather, I performed the ritual I call packing, which involves laying out clothes, reconsidering, laying out fewer clothes, and then bringing a book I won't read and a notebook I will fill with sentences I'll later delete. At the bottom of my bag, wrapped in a scarf, I put the postcard.

Before I left, I stood in my mother's kitchen one last time. Her recipe cards were still in the drawer where they had always been, soft-cornered, annotated in her hand—*less salt than you think*, *the oven lies, check early*. On the back of one card, for fish soup, she had written a single line I'd never noticed: *Gullvik, 1963. Ask Halvard about the boat.*

Ask Halvard about the boat.

I stood there with the card shaking slightly in my hand, feeling the particular vertigo of discovering that your mother, whom you knew completely and understood not at all, had been leaving you instructions all along—buried in the one place she knew you'd eventually have to look, because grief, sooner or later, sends everyone to the recipe drawer.

The train north left at six. I watched the city dissolve into fields, the fields into forest, the forest into that gray-green scrub that means *coast soon*, and all the while the song hummed itself in my chest, patient as tide. Somewhere ahead was a green door, a dying man—I heard it in his voice, that careful breathlessness; I know the sound of someone rationing himself—and a sentence my mother had abandoned mid-word thirty years ago.

*He chose the sea because it never asked him to.*

By morning I would know what came next. I only hoped it wasn't the word *stay*, because I had already spent a decade learning how much a person can survive without that one.
