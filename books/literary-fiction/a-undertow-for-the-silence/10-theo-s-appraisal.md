## 10. Theo's Appraisal

The man from Halloway Development Group wore a tie the color of wet sand, and Theo Marchetti distrusted him immediately, which was a problem, because Theo distrusted everyone who wanted something and this man wanted something very badly. You could see it in the way he sat — forward, elbows on knees, hands open like a man showing he carried no weapon, when in fact the weapon was the folder on the table between them, thick with renderings and figures and a number that had been underlined twice in blue ink.

"Mr. Marchetti." The man's name was Voss. He said it like a handshake you'd already agreed to. "I appreciate you meeting me here. I know it's an unusual ask."

They were in the back booth of the Copper Kettle, which at eleven on a Tuesday morning smelled of burnt coffee and bacon grease and the particular loneliness of diners between rushes. Theo had chosen it because it was neutral ground, equidistant from his office and the house on Kestrel Lane, and because he'd wanted to watch Voss eat eggs first. A man who salted before tasting would overpay for land. A man who tasted first knew what he was buying. Voss had ordered black coffee and hadn't touched it.

"I'm not the one to talk to," Theo said. "There are four of us with a claim. Five, if you count Bram, and nobody counts Bram."

"I'm not sure that's true." Voss smiled. It was a good smile, dental-plan quality, deployed without warmth but also without malice. "From what I understand, you're the one who does the math for the family. The practical one."

Theo turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on its saucer. *Practical.* His whole life had been summarized by strangers in that word, usually right before they asked him for something his siblings wouldn't give. Practical Theo, who kept the spreadsheets of Gran's medical bills after she died. Practical Theo, who had driven two hundred miles for the funeral and sat in the last row because the family rows filled up fast with people who had actually visited her, which he had not, not in the final year, not since the argument about the roof, which had really been about everything else.

"The house is going to auction," he said. "That's public record. Whatever happens happens at auction."

"Auction's a lottery." Voss finally lifted his cup, tasted, set it down. Good sign. Bad sign. "You know what sells at auction? Stories. Somebody falls in love with the porch light and bids past sense. Or nobody shows, and a flipper takes it for scrap value and strips the copper out of the walls before the bulldozers come. Either way, your family gets whatever the room decides. I'm offering certainty."

He slid the folder across the formica. Theo didn't touch it. Touching it would mean something, and he wasn't ready to be someone yet.

"There's a demolition permit already filed," Voss continued, unhurried, a man laying track. "The parcel's worth more flat than standing — that's just arithmetic, nothing personal against the house. But permits take time, auctions take time, families take time. Time is the only thing I can't buy outright." He paused, let that sit. "So I buy it in pieces."

"And I'm a piece."

"You're the hinge piece." Voss opened the folder himself, turned it so the numbers faced Theo, and tapped the page once. "If the heirs present a unified front — if they petition to pull the property from auction and sell directly — the process compresses by months. Months of taxes, insurance on a vacant structure, liability if some kid breaks a window. I've seen families bleed five figures just holding a house hostage to their own disagreements." He looked up. "I'm offering you forty thousand dollars as a finder's consideration. Not a bribe — a fee, documented, taxable, boring. For facilitating consensus. And if the direct sale closes, there's a buyer's premium that flows through the same channel."

Forty thousand. Theo did the math the way he always did, involuntarily, the way other people flinched. Forty thousand was the balance left on his ex-wife's car, which he still paid for, because the car was also technically his daughter's car, and Priya needed it for community college in the fall. Forty thousand was the hole in the roof of his own rental duplex that he'd patched himself with a tarp and denial. Forty thousand was roughly what he imagined it would cost to stop feeling, for one full year, like a man who had let his grandmother die alone in a house full of working radiators while he lived ninety minutes away and told himself the drive was too long.

He had not visited enough. That was the ledger underneath every other ledger. Gran had died in February, and Theo's last visit had been the previous June, and he could still see her on the porch, smaller than memory had made her, waving him off with that hand that had kneaded bread and pulled splinters and once, when he was nine, cracked him across the mouth for lying about a broken window. She'd waved until he turned the corner. He'd found out later from Marisol — in one of her letters that were half update, half indictment — that Gran had stood at the window a long time after. Marisol wrote things like that the way other people threw stones.

"I'll need to think," Theo said, which was what he said instead of yes, and both of them knew it.

"Of course." Voss closed the folder but left it on the table, which was its own kind of message. "One thing I'll say, Mr. Marchetti, and then I'll let you eat your lunch in peace." He leaned back, and for a moment the salesman's polish thinned and something older looked out — not kindness, exactly, but recognition. "I've done this thirty years. Every family has one person who kept score and one person who kept vigil, and they hate each other for it, and both of them are grieving wrong. The ones who kept score always think the money will settle it. It doesn't. But it does make the settling quieter." He stood, dropped a twenty by his untouched coffee. "The offer stands through Friday. After that I bid at auction like anyone else, and you get the lottery."

* * *

Theo drove back toward his office the long way, which put him on Kestrel Road, which he told himself was efficient, which was a lie with good posture.

The house looked worse than he remembered and better than he feared. Shutters hanging at the angle of a shrugged shoulder. The fence posts orange with new notices — he slowed, read DEMOLITION in block letters, felt it in his sternum like a door closing. Someone had mowed the strip along the walk. That would be Bram. Bram, who had shown up out of nowhere after three years of silence and started touching things, bleeding radiators, sitting in Gran's sewing room like they owned the grief. Marisol had called him Tuesday night, voice tight: *Bram's been in the house. Nobody gave them a key, Theo. They just walked in like the house was waiting.*

It probably had been waiting. That was the thing about Bram — houses liked them, doors opened for them, and Theo had spent forty-one years being the one who knocked.

He parked across the street without meaning to and sat with the engine ticking down. Through the front windows, dust sheets made pale ghosts of the furniture. Somewhere inside, faintly, a piano note — single, patient, repeated. Bram tuning something, or listening to something, or doing whatever it was Bram did that had made Gran's eyes go soft in a way they never went soft for Theo's report cards, Theo's salary, Theo's sensible life built at sensible distance from the woman who raised everyone and thanked no one.

*Favorite,* Marisol had written once, and Theo had never argued, because arguing would have required admitting how much he'd wanted it to be true.

His phone buzzed. Voss's email, already, subject line: *Follow-up and term sheet.* Attached, no doubt, the number again, underlined twice. Theo looked at it for a long moment, thumb hovering, and then did the thing he would spend the rest of this story trying to earn back: he downloaded it. Saved it to a folder he named *Estate — Misc.* Boring name. Contraband wants a boring name.

Forty thousand. Certainty. An end to the bleeding, Voss had said, and Theo thought about the tarp on his roof, and Priya's tuition deposit due in three weeks, and the way Marisol would look at him when she found out — because she would find out, Marisol found out everything, she was the family's weather system — and he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *I don't have to tell them. I just have to make the sale happen. Everyone gets clean money faster. Nobody gets hurt. This is just efficiency.*

It was amazing, the architecture a man could build around a crack, if he had a weekend and a calculator.

Inside the house, the piano stopped. Then, after a silence, it began again — a chord now, low and warm, the kind of sound that got into radiators and pipes and stayed for decades. Theo gripped the wheel. He had grown up in that sound. He had fallen asleep to it through the floor vents, the music coming up through the heat like the house itself was humming, and he had spent thirty years telling himself he didn't miss it.

Friday, Voss had said.

Theo put the car in gear and drove away from the house, and the chord followed him the whole way, and he did not delete the file.
