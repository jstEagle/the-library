## 21. Gran's Upright

The demolition notices went up on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday I had run out of reasons not to open the piano.

I'd been avoiding the parlor the way you avoid a room where someone died — walking wide around its doorway, letting my eyes slide off the dust sheet that covered the upright like a shroud too small for the body beneath. Three weeks in this house and I had bled every radiator, swept two floors, catalogued what the cousins would want salvaged, and still I hadn't touched the piano. A piano tuner who won't tune a piano. Odile would have found the irony gentle and unbearable at once.

But the house was quieter now. Theo had hauled off the dining table on Saturday, Marisol had boxed the china with a violence that cracked two saucers, and without their voices bouncing down the stairwell the silence had settled into the walls like water into plaster. And in that silence, every time I passed the parlor, I could hear it.

Not sound exactly. More the memory of sound, held in the strings the way heat is held in cast iron long after the boiler shuts down. An instrument left untuned for years doesn't go silent. It goes sour — slowly, note by note, each string relaxing out of true at its own pace, so that the piano becomes a record of its own neglect. I have walked into hundreds of rooms and heard hundreds of pianos telling me how long they've been alone. Gran's upright was singing a chord of abandonment I knew by heart.

So on Wednesday morning I folded back the dust sheet.

Dust came up in a slow gray bloom, motes turning in the light from the window, and there she was — the Heintzman upright, walnut case gone dull as old honey, the fallboard scarred where I'd once, age nine, dragged a metal toy truck across it and been made to polish out the scratch with walnut oil while Gran sat beside me saying nothing, which was worse than any scolding. The ivory had yellowed unevenly, darker where her hands had rested, lighter everywhere else, so that even the keys remembered her.

I lifted the lid over the keys. The smell rose out of the innards — felt and glue and old copper and something faintly sweet, the smell of my entire childhood compressed into a cubic foot of air.

"Okay," I said aloud, to no one, to the house. "Okay."

* * *

Tuning is listening before it is touching. Any tuner will tell you that, though most of us learned it the humiliating way, by turning pins before we understood what we were hearing. You strike a note and you wait inside the sound. You listen for the beats — those slow, sick throb-throbs when two frequencies are close but not married, the wave interference that says *this string has drifted from its partner*. A pure interval is silent in a way that isn't absence; it's fullness. A mistuned one trembles. Grief, if you want a word for it, sounds like trembling between two things that should be one.

I struck middle C with the heel of my hand, soft, and braced myself for the wobble of three years' neglect.

It rang clean.

I struck it again, harder, disbelieving, and put my ear down among the strings the way I used to put my ear to radiators. Clean. Not just close-to-pitch, not just *survived-the-winters* — clean, dead center, the C sitting in its octave like a stone set in a ring. I played the fifth above it. Pure. No beats. I played the third, which is the interval that always betrays an untended piano first, the third that goes sour within months of abandonment —

Pure.

I sat down on the bench properly then, and played through the whole instrument, note after note, chord after chord, and every single string was where it belonged. The unison strings of each note agreed with each other perfectly. The temperament was intact, even, warm, tuned slightly narrow in the thirds in a way no machine would choose but every good human ear does — *her* tuning. Gran's tuning. My grandmother, who had been dead three years, whose hands had last touched these pins before her hands stopped being hers, had left behind a piano that should by now have sagged half a semitone flat in the bass and gone wild-eyed in the treble.

Instead it held pitch like a held breath.

I checked the pinblock. Solid. I checked for signs someone had tuned it recently — fresh tool marks on the pins, a coil of new wire, the disturbed dust that any tuner's work leaves behind. Nothing. The dust inside the action lay undisturbed in a gray fleece thick enough to write your name in. Nobody had touched this piano since the last time Gran did.

And yet.

I am not a person who uses the word impossible lightly. Houses do strange things; pipes carry more than water; I have built a whole life on the premise that objects remember. But objects remember passively. They hold what was given them. This was different. This was the piano doing something — actively, continuously, against physics — holding itself true the way a person holds themselves together at a funeral, and for whom? For me? As if it had known I was coming. As if it had spent three years keeping itself ready, the way Gran kept the porch light burning on nights I came home late, not because she expected anything but because leaving it on was a form of faith.

I pressed middle C again and held the damper up and listened to the string sing itself out, and somewhere under the tone, under the decay, I heard — or the house let me hear — the ghost of her humming. That tune again. The one the radiators sang, the one the boiler knocked in its sleep. Four notes, rising, then falling away unresolved, a phrase that never lands anywhere, that just lifts and lets go. I had been hearing it all my life and had never once identified it. It wasn't a hymn. It wasn't a lullaby I knew. It was just — hers. The sound she made instead of explaining herself.

My eyes were stinging. I wiped them with the back of my wrist, smearing dust across my cheekbone, and laughed once, a short ugly sound, alone in the parlor of a condemned house.

"You couldn't have just written it down," I said to her. "You had to make the *piano* keep the secret too."

The piano, naturally, said nothing. It only held its pitch.

* * *

I worked anyway. Habit is a kind of prayer. Even though nothing needed doing, I went through the motions — tested each unison, eased the sticky F#2 damper, cleaned the dust from the soundboard with the soft brush I keep for instruments I love. It's a form of intimacy, tuning. You learn a piano's body the way you learn a lover's: the loose pin that wants gentleness, the bridge notch that buzzes at one specific frequency, the key that sticks in damp weather. Every piano is a biography of everyone who ever played it. I read biographies for a living.

When I finished the top section I reached under the keybed to check the pedal lyre bolts, and my knuckles knocked wood that sounded hollow.

I crouched. The bench.

Gran's bench. The lift-top kind, storage under the seat, where sheet music lived — hymnals, the songbook from her church years, my childhood attempt at "Für Elise" with finger numbers penciled in her handwriting above every note because I kept losing my place. I hadn't thought about the bench in years. I hadn't let myself.

I lifted the seat.

On top lay the music, exactly as expected, exactly as remembered, and the familiarity of it undid me a little — her pencil marks, her habit of dog-earing pages she loved. Beneath the music, a flat parcel wrapped in a dish towel, the blue one with the faded cornflowers, the towel that had lived on the oven handle my whole childhood.

My hands knew before my head did. Hands always know first. They went cold the way they go cold before bad news, before a phone rings at the wrong hour, and I sat there on the floor of the parlor with the towel in my lap for a long time, listening to the house tick and settle around me, listening to my own pulse.

I unwrapped it.

Photographs. Maybe thirty of them, loose, mixed sizes, some creased white at the corners from having lived in an album once and been removed. Photographs of my parents.

I knew them instantly, the way you know your own face in a stranger's features. My mother's laugh caught mid-motion in a kitchen I recognized as this kitchen, forty years younger. My father on the porch steps, young, in a shirt I had somehow inherited a version of without knowing its history. The two of them together at the lake, squinting into sun, her hand flat on a belly that would become me. Photographs I had never seen. Photographs I had been told, in the vague passive grammar adults use for disappearances, did not exist — *there weren't many pictures from those years, sweetheart.*

There were many pictures from those years. They had been here the whole time, four feet from where I practiced scales, wrapped in the cornflower towel.

And every face was scratched out.

Not torn — scratched. Deliberately, thoroughly, with something sharp enough to bite into the emulsion. Faces reduced to gray storms of gouged silver, eyes and mouths obliterated, identities erased with a fury or a desperation that showed in the depth of the strokes. Some scratches were tight and controlled, almost careful. Some had torn clean through the paper. On the lake photograph, my mother's belly — the belly that was me — had been left untouched. Everything above her chin was storm. Everything below was preserved.

I sat very still. The radiator in the corner ticked its small clock-tick, the sewing-room heart of the house beating through the wall, and the piano behind me held its impossible pitch, and I understood that I was looking at two contradictory acts performed by the same pair of hands. Someone had saved these photographs. Saved them carefully, wrapped them in a beloved towel, hidden them where only a musician would find them, in the bench, in the instrument, in the sanctuary. And someone had destroyed them first — or destroyed them after, or destroyed them *while* saving them, unable to do either thing completely.

You don't need fingerprints to know whose hands. There was only one pair of hands in this house that could hold tenderness and violence in the same grip. Only one person who loved hard enough to hurt. I had felt those hands on my hair, on my shoulders, guiding my fingers on these very keys. I had kissed those knuckles.

Gran had done this. Gran had scratched out her own daughter's face, and my father's, and kept the photographs anyway, and hidden them in the one place in the world she trusted — inside music, inside the thing we shared, inside the instrument she had somehow kept in tune for three years past her death as if holding a note for me to come home to.

Why keep what you've destroyed? Why destroy what you've kept?

Because the faces were dangerous. Because the faces were precious. Both at once, the way a sentence can be both unsayable and the only thing worth saying. She couldn't throw them away and couldn't look at them, so she made them unlookable-at and kept them close, the way she'd kept the letter under the floorboard — sealed, addressed to no one, dated the winter everything happened. *Because I promised.* Three words through an envelope, and now thirty ruined faces in a bench. My parents existed in this house only as damage. Their images survived only as wounds.

I spread them out on the parlor floor with the care you use for broken glass, and I looked at what was left. Bodies. Postures. The shape of a life around the hole where the life was. In photograph after photograph, my parents stood in this house, sat in this kitchen, leaned on this porch rail — present, laughing, alive — and rendered faceless by the woman who raised their child. Who raised *me*. Who taught me to hear what walls remember and never said what she remembered herself.

The violation of it moved through me slowly, colder than the draft under the door. This was sanctuary — the piano, the bench, the towel, the pencil-marked hymnals — and the violation was inside it, had always been inside it, had been here all along under the seat while I played "Für Elise" badly at her knee. She had sat on this bench with the ruined faces beneath her and taught me scales. What was it like, I wondered, to rest your weight on a secret like that every day for twenty years? Did it press up through the wood? Could she feel it under her, the way I could feel grief in a string?

Outside, a car slowed on Kestrel Lane — the auctioneer's people, maybe, come to measure the rooms for the listing, strangers with clipboards who would stand in this parlor next week and call this piano "vintage, good bones." Let them. They'd never know it was the most faithful thing in the house.

I gathered the photographs back into the towel, gently, all except one — the lake picture, the one where my mother's hand rested on the belly that was me. I looked at the scratched storm where her face had been and I made myself say it out loud, quietly, to the empty parlor:

"I'm going to find out whose face that was. I'm going to find out what you promised, and to whom, and why it cost this much."

The piano held its pitch. The radiator ticked. And from the front of the house, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the gate hinge — the one that always announced a visitor whether they knocked or not — swinging open.

Footsteps on the porch. A knock, polite and unhurried, from the direction of Mr. Okonkwo's fence line.

Through the dusty window I saw him standing there, hat in his hands, looking older than he had last week, looking like a man who had finally decided to carry something heavy across the yard — and I thought of the photographs in my lap, of the faces only he, of everyone still living, might have seen whole.
