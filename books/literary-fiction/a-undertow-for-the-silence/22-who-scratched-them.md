## 22. Who Scratched Them

Emmanuel Okonkwo had lived at 12 Kestrel Lane for forty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one thing about the house next door: it kept its appointments. The gutters wept on schedule every November. The porch boards swelled and sang in July. And on Sunday mornings, if you sat where he was sitting now — in the green metal chair by his hydrangeas, coffee going cold in his hands — you could hear the piano through the walls, faint as a pulse under a wrist.

Except there was no piano playing this morning. There hadn't been for three years, not since they carried Odile's grandmother's body out through the front door she'd painted the color of ripe plums, and even before that, the music had grown sparse, like a heart learning to skip beats.

What he heard instead was Bram Marchetti's voice, low and questioning, coming from inside 14 Kestrel Lane, and beneath it the small percussion of a tuning hammer on pins. They were tuning the old upright. He'd seen them carry their kit up the walk two days ago, moving like a person entering a church they'd been excommunicated from.

Emmanuel drank his cold coffee and thought about photographs.

He thought about them the way you think about a debt you've been carrying so long it has become part of your skeleton. Because he knew something. He had known it for twenty-six years, and he had kept it the way Odile's grandmother — Ruth, though everyone called her Gran, including him, eventually, after enough shared fence-line summers — had kept her own silences: carefully, in a place where the damp couldn't reach it.

And now the house was going to be sold, and then knocked down, and whatever truths lived in its bones would be buried under a parking structure or a duplex or whatever the developers dreamed up, and Emmanuel Okonkwo, seventy-three years old, retired mail carrier, widower of nineteen years, would take the last secret of Kestrel Lane into his own grave with him.

He set down the cup. His knees announced their objections. He crossed the strip of dead grass between the properties anyway.

* * *

The front door of 14 Kestrel Lane stood open to the morning, which felt wrong the way an open casket feels wrong — necessary, but an invitation no one should have to extend. Inside, the air was cool and mineral-smelling, dust and old plaster and the iron tang of unbled radiators. Emmanuel stood in the parlor doorway and watched Bram work.

They had the upright's fallboard open and were striking intervals with one hand while the other adjusted something inside, their head cocked at the angle of a person listening to a conversation happening just below human hearing. The light came through the lace curtains in gray coins. Dust hung in it like a held breath.

"Your E-flat is lying to you," Emmanuel said.

Bram didn't startle — or rather, they startled inwardly, a flinch he saw only in the stillness of their shoulders, because they'd grown up in a house full of quiet arrivals and had learned to absorb shock without broadcasting it. They turned. Up close they looked thinner than the child he remembered, sharper at the jaw, with their grandmother's exact way of holding their chin level when they wanted to seem calmer than they were.

"Mr. Okonkwo." A pause while they recalibrated. "You're the first visitor who hasn't come to measure anything."

"I've measured everything worth measuring on this street already." He nodded at the piano. "Ruth tuned that herself, you know. Every spring. Bought a kit from the hardware store on Delancey and did it by ear, badly, and refused all offers of help."

"She told me once it wasn't about the pitch." Bram's hand rested on the keyboard without pressing. "It was about checking whether the house was still listening."

"That sounds like her." He came further into the room, drawn despite himself toward the instrument, the way you're drawn toward a photograph of someone gone. "I need to tell you something, Bram. And I'd ask you to let me get all the way to the end before you say anything, because I've been rehearsing the beginning for three days and I don't trust myself past the first verse."

Bram closed the fallboard softly, the way you close a door on a sleeping person. "Okay."

"The photographs," Emmanuel said. "In the bench. I imagine you found them, since you're tuning that thing instead of avoiding it, and anyone who opens that bench finds them. Her parents. Your parents. Faces scratched out."

Bram went very still. It was the stillness of a struck string, he thought — vibration held inside the body, nowhere to go. "You know about those."

"I know who scratched them." He looked at his own hands, brown and spotted and steady, steadier than he deserved. "It was Ruth. She did it herself. With a kitchen knife, at this table" — he touched the piano's lid — "on a night in February, twenty-six years ago now, give or take. I know because I was sitting where you're sitting. And I know why. And I have wished for twenty-six years that I didn't."

* * *

He asked for tea, not because he wanted any but because Ruth had always said a hard story goes down easier with something warm in your hands, and her kitchen was still her kitchen — the yellowed wallpaper, the tin on the counter, the window over the sink that framed nothing but the neighbor's fence, which she'd claimed was her favorite view in the world because it required no upkeep.

Bram put the kettle on with the automatic grace of someone raised in this room, and Emmanuel watched them move through their grandmother's choreography and understood that grief has a geography, and Bram was standing in the middle of theirs.

"There was a man," he began. "February, I want to say the fourteenth, because Ruth mentioned later that he'd come on Valentine's Day, and she laughed when she said it, that awful dry laugh she had, like gravel poured slowly. He knocked on my door first, by mistake. Said he was family. Said his name was—" Emmanuel stopped. Frowned. This was the strange part, the part that had bothered him for decades the way a loose tooth bothers a tongue. "I'll be honest with you, Bram. I cannot remember his name. I remember everything else about that evening — what I was cooking, that the radiator in my hall was knocking because it needed bleeding, the color of his coat. But the name slides off. Like it was never properly given. Like he handed me a word-shaped thing that wasn't actually a word."

Bram had gone pale above the stove. "What did he look like?"

"Tall. Well-dressed in a way that was trying too hard — good coat, wrong shoes. Smooth. You know the kind of smooth I mean? The kind that makes you feel like you're being handled without ever being touched." Emmanuel wrapped his hands around the mug Bram gave him. Tea he wouldn't drink. Warmth he needed. "He apologized for the wrong door, very charming, and went next door, and I thought nothing of it. People claimed kinship with Ruth all the time, back then. Half the neighborhood borrowed sugar off her and called themselves cousins."

"But he wasn't."

"No. Or yes. That's the devil of it." He stared into the tea. "I heard most of it through the wall, you understand. My kitchen and hers share a wall, and in winter, when the radiators are talking, voices carry along the pipes like they're being delivered. I've heard half my life through these walls. Your grandmother used to joke that she never had to write a diary because I had one installed in my ears."

"And what did you hear?"

"A conversation that started polite and ended quiet." He set the mug down untouched. "That's the part people don't understand about violence, Bram. Everyone waits for shouting. But the worst conversations end quiet. Shouting means someone still believes they can win. Quiet means somebody has lost. I heard his voice go low and pleasant, and I heard Ruth's voice do something I'd never heard it do before or since. She got smaller. This woman who argued with the city over sidewalk cracks, who once made a census taker apologize — she got *small*, in her own kitchen, in her own house. And then the front door closed, and the house stayed quiet in a way houses don't. You know how a house breathes? Even empty ones. Furnace ticking, pipes settling. That night it held its breath until morning."

* * *

The kettle clicked off. Neither of them moved to pour.

"He came back," Emmanuel said. "Twice more that winter. I know because I started watching after that first night — watching my neighbor's windows the way you watch a sick friend's lights. Third time, I didn't wait for the wall to report. I walked over and knocked, and Ruth answered before I got to the step, like she'd been standing behind the door, and she smiled at me — that smile, the one that was a locked gate — and said everything was fine, family business, thank you, Emmanuel. And behind her, in the parlor, the man stood up from the sofa and shook my hand and called me sir, and his palm was dry and cool, and he said, 'You have a lovely neighborhood, Mr.—'"

Emmanuel stopped.

"'Mr.—,'" Bram repeated quietly. "He didn't know your name either."

"Twenty-six years I thought I was being forgetful." Emmanuel's laugh came out cracked. "A man lives somewhere forty years, everyone knows his name, the mailman knows his name, and this stranger stands in Ruth's parlor and can't finish my name. I filled it in for him — Okonkwo — and he repeated it perfectly, like an actor taking a line. And I went home, and I lay awake, and I told myself I was an old man getting suspicious of strangers. Because that's what we do, isn't it? We talk ourselves out of what we know."

"What did he want?" Bram's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "From her."

"I don't know. Not money — she had none to speak of. Not the house; there was no reason anyone would want it then, before the neighborhood turned. He talked, through the wall, in that low pleasant register, and mostly what I caught were questions. Always questions. About the past. About people who'd been gone a long time." Emmanuel met Bram's eyes. "About your mother and father, Bram. By name. I heard their names come out of that stranger's mouth in my neighbor's kitchen, spoken soft as a prayer, and I heard Ruth answer him the way stone answers water. Nothing. Nothing, nothing, nothing. And then the last visit — the fourth, in late February — I heard the door slam, and I heard her cross the kitchen, and I heard drawers opening, and I heard her sit down at the piano bench right there in the parlor, and I heard—"

His voice failed him. Not dramatically. Just a small mechanical failure, like a key sticking.

"You heard the knife," Bram said.

"I heard a sound I didn't understand until later. A rhythmic scraping. I thought she was sharpening something, and God forgive me, I was afraid, and I did not go over. An old man afraid of his own neighbor's grief. The next morning she was on her porch like always, drinking her coffee, and she waved at me like always, and I waved back like always, and it was weeks before I noticed — she'd moved the photographs. They used to sit on the mantel, all of them, the whole gallery of the dead and the departed, and after that February they were gone, and I assumed she'd packed them away. Put them in the bench, maybe. Out of sight. Grief does that."

"But she didn't pack them away."

"No." He finally drank the tea, cold as it was, because his throat needed something. "Years later — five, six years, you'd have been a teenager by then, off at your music camps — she and I were splitting a bottle of terrible sherry on her porch, the way we did, and she was three-quarters asleep in the chair, and out of nowhere she said, 'Emmanuel, do you think a face can be dangerous?' And I said something wise and useless, and she said, 'Because I took care of it. I took care of the faces.' And she looked at me — fully looked, the locked gate wide open for one second — and she said, 'Don't ask me. If you ask me, I'll have to lie to you, and I don't want to lie to you.' And I didn't ask. Forty-one years on this street and that is the single hardest thing I have ever done: I did not ask."

* * *

Silence filled the kitchen, the particular silence of that house, which was never really silence at all — the boiler ticked in the basement, the pipes murmured their iron liturgy, the refrigerator hummed its one note. Emmanuel had spent a lifetime listening to this house breathe, and he heard it breathing now around the two of them like a third presence.

Bram stood at the counter with both hands flat on the surface, the posture of someone keeping themselves moored.

"The faces in the bench," they said at last. "My parents. And her parents too?"

"All of them. That's what she meant by taking care of the faces. She didn't discriminate. Whatever that man wanted — whatever knowing those faces might have given him — she burned the map. Scratched it blind." Emmanuel shook his head slowly. "I used to think it was rage. Twenty years I thought: she was furious at someone, at your parents maybe, for leaving her with a child and no explanation, and she defaced them in anger. It's a tidy theory. People love a tidy theory."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was armor." He said it carefully, laying each word down like a plank over deep water. "Think about it the way she would. A stranger comes asking questions about people who are gone. Comes four times. Won't leave a name in your mouth afterward, like smoke. What does a woman like Ruth Ishikawa do? She can't call the police — about what, a polite man asking questions? She can't run; she has a child in the house, a house, a life. So she does the one thing within her power. She makes sure that if he comes back — if he ever gets inside again, gets his hands on anything — he finds nothing. No faces. No trail. She blinded the photographs the way you'd burn a letter." He paused. "She disarmed the house, Bram. She turned her own family album into a weapon that couldn't be used against anyone she loved."

Bram's hands had curled against the countertop. When they spoke, their voice was stripped down to studs. "You think he might still—"

"I'm an old man, and I don't believe in monsters, and I believe every word I'm about to say." Emmanuel pushed back from the table and stood, knees complaining, and laid one dark hand briefly over Bram's white knuckles. "That man knew things about your parents that your own grandmother wouldn't say aloud. He came looking for something, and she convinced him — at cost, at terrible cost, you can hear it in the scratches, how hard she pressed — that it wasn't here. And now the house is being emptied, and auctioned, and strangers are walking through it touching her things, and the photographs are sitting in that bench like a wound reopened. If he was the patient sort — and men like that are always the patient sort —"

The sound came from the front of the house. The front door, which Bram had left open to the mild morning, swung wider against its stop, and footsteps crossed the parlor floor — unhurried, confident, the tread of a man who considered every door an invitation already accepted.

A voice preceded its owner into the kitchen, warm and modulated and smooth as poured cream:

"Hello? I hope I'm not intruding — the door was open. My name is—" a pause, brief as a skipped beat, "—well. I'm family. I'm looking for the Marchetti family. I understand there's been a death in the old house, and I've only just heard."

Emmanuel Okonkwo did not turn around. He watched Bram's face instead, and on it he saw recognition arrive like weather — the blood draining, the eyes going fixed, the lips parting around a name that had traveled through forty years of pipe and plaster to find them.

Because Bram had heard that voice before. Somewhere in the oldest memory of the house, in the deepest register of the radiators, that voice had been speaking all along.

And now it was standing in the kitchen, smiling, waiting to be introduced.
