## 3. The Estate's Courtesy

The telephone at Quill & Marrow had a habit of ringing exactly when Marguerite had settled into the rhythm of a sentence, as though the instrument could smell a comma forming and resented it. On Tuesday morning it rang while he was deep in clause fourteen of a shipping contract concerning liability for damp cargo, and he let it go to the machine, because clause fourteen was finally behaving.

The machine clicked. A voice said: "Mr. Eastwick? This is Prudence Okafor, from Halloway & Sons, Auctioneers and Estate Valuers. We received your letter regarding lot 114, the Saltmere Row property. If you'd like to discuss the catalogue arrangements, do ring back."

Marguerite read clause fourteen four times without absorbing a word of it. Then he set down his pen, which was the professional equivalent of dropping everything, and dialed.

"Ms. Okafor. Thank you for calling back. I'm—well." He had rehearsed this in the shower, on the bus, into the bathroom mirror while brushing his teeth, and every rehearsal had collapsed at precisely this syllable. "I'm a translator by trade. Literary and commercial. But I grew up in that house."

There was a pause on the line with the particular texture of someone deciding how much of you to believe. "The notice did say the property had been in the Eastwick family," she allowed.

"It was. For sixty years, before—" He stopped. The *before* was a country he didn't want to enter over the phone, or possibly ever. "I saw the auction listing. I understand you'll need the contents catalogued before the sale. Furniture, books, household effects. I'd like to offer my services. Pro bono, if it helps. I know the house. I know what things are."

Another pause, longer. In the background he heard paper shuffling, the small bureaucracy of an auction house waking up. "You'd be willing to catalogue your own family's effects?"

"I'm told it's therapeutic," Marguerite said, and was surprised to hear himself joke about it, the way one is surprised to find one's own hand reaching into a fire.

"Well," said Ms. Okafor, and he could hear her warming, the way people warm when they realize a job that should cost them two thousand pounds might cost them nothing but courtesy. "It would be a considerable help. The contents are extensive and Mr. Halloway's knees are not what they were. Could you start next week?"

"I could start tomorrow."

"Enthusiastic," she said approvingly, and wrote something down that Marguerite suspected was his name spelled wrong.

He hung up and looked at clause fourteen. It concerned the question of who bore responsibility when goods were damaged by water in transit, and Marguerite thought, with a clarity that felt less like insight than like vertigo, that he had spent ten years translating other people's losses into polite language so that no one ever had to look at them directly.

He was going home to catalogue the wreckage. Officially.

* * *

His editor, Renata, occupied the corner office at the far end of the Quill & Marrow floor, an office she had earned through twenty years of rescuing bad manuscripts and terrorizing worse authors. She was eating a yoghurt when he knocked, which meant she was in a good mood; she only ate yoghurt when she felt the world could still be improved.

"Renata. Do you have five minutes?"

"Four," she said, tapping the spoon against the pot. "Talk."

He told her. He gave her the tidy version—the auction, the catalogue, the professional courtesy of a native son offering his expertise—and watched her face perform its slow descent from pleasant attention to the expression she reserved for manuscripts that began with the protagonist waking up.

"You're going back," she said. Not a question.

"For a fortnight. Maybe three. The house needs cataloguing and I'm the only person alive who knows that the thing in the attic is a loom and not a torture device."

"Mm." She put down the yoghurt with the deliberation of a judge setting aside a gavel. "Marguerite. How long has it been since you've been back?"

"Ten years."

"And in those ten years, how many times have you taken a commission that involved going back?"

"None."

"How many times have you been *offered* one?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. There had been three. A dialect dictionary project in year two. A regional anthology in year six. A court transcription last spring that would have paid obscenely well. He had turned down all three with reasons so elegant he had almost believed them himself.

"That's what I thought," Renata said. "So here's my concern, offered with love and also with the awareness that you are the best translator I have and I would rather not lose you to a nervous breakdown in a seaside town." She leaned forward. "You are dressing this up as work. And you are very good at dressing things up as work. You once translated a suicide note into iambic pentameter because the client asked for 'something dignified,' and you billed it as a stylistic challenge."

"It *was* a stylistic challenge."

"It was avoidance with a deadline." She pointed the spoon at him. "What's actually in that house, Marguerite?"

"Furniture," he said. "Books. My grandmother's things. Some papers, probably. Legal papers, receipts, the ordinary sediment of an ordinary family."

"Ordinary families don't produce sons who flinch when you say the word *home*."

"I don't flinch."

"You just did. Your left eyebrow went up like a drawbridge." She sighed, and the sigh softened her, the way it always did; Renata's ferocity was load-bearing but it was never cruel. "Look. I'm not your therapist. God knows you pay someone else to hear this. But I am the person who reads your translations, and I will tell you something I've noticed in ten years of reading them. You're brilliant with other people's grief. You can take the most wrecked, waterlogged testimony and make it sing. And in all that time, I have never once seen you translate anything of your own. Not a letter. Not a memory. Nothing. It's like watching a man who can swim anywhere in the world except the pool in his own garden."

Marguerite looked out the window at the traffic below, the orderly river of it, everyone going somewhere they had chosen.

"The house is being demolished," he said. "In six weeks. Whatever is in it stops existing. Someone has to decide what gets saved."

"*Someone* does," Renata agreed. "And it's touching that you've volunteered. Just promise me one thing."

"What?"

"When you find whatever it is you're actually looking for—and you will find it, because you always find what you're looking for, it's your single most inconvenient talent—promise me you won't pretend it's a filing task."

He promised. They both knew the exchange rate of promises made in that office.

* * *

That night he packed, which took forty minutes, and then sat on the edge of his bed for two hours doing the arithmetic of self-deception, which took considerably longer.

Here is what he told himself, item by item, the way he would later catalogue the house:

Item: a translator's skills include organization, description, and the rendering of difficult material into manageable form. A house full of unsorted effects is, in essence, an untranslated text. His offer was therefore professional. (This was true in the way that a map is true—it accurately described the terrain while omitting everything that mattered.)

Item: closure. Everyone spoke of closure as though it were a room you could visit once, dust, and lock behind you. He had read enough psychology, in the course of translating other people's memoirs, to know the word was suspect, but he deployed it anyway, to himself, in the mirror, and it fit his mouth the way a borrowed coat fits—close enough, if nobody looked too hard at the shoulders.

Item: the letters. Here his internal auditor cleared its throat. Because there *were* letters—he half-remembered them, the way you half-remember a dream that woke you crying. Tomas had written from prison. Marguerite had been fifteen and his mother had read the letters aloud at the kitchen table in her careful school English, flattening them, sanding off every rough grain of the old dialect until they sounded like they'd been written by a stranger, a polite stranger, a stranger serving four years for something—

For something. The sentence had never finished itself, even inside his own head. Ten years of professional precision, and the one sentence he could not complete was the one about his brother.

He was not going for the letters. He told himself this firmly. He was going because the house needed cataloguing and he happened to be uniquely qualified, and if, in the course of cataloguing, certain papers came to light, well. An archivist does not choose what the archive contains.

An archivist, said a smaller voice, the voice that sounded uncomfortably like Renata, also does not travel eight hundred miles to volunteer for free.

He silenced it with the efficient brutality of long practice and went to sleep.

* * *

The train north took most of a day, which was convenient, because a day is exactly long enough to talk yourself into and out of a decision eleven times. Marguerite passed the hours translating a sample chapter of a Danish novel about a lighthouse keeper's divorce—not because anyone had commissioned it, but because his hands wanted occupation—and noticed, somewhere past the third chapter, that he had rendered the keeper's wife's silence in a dialect of English that was not standard at all, that leaned toward the sea-words of his grandmother, words like *haffle* for the wind that comes before rain and *mirk-light*, the blue hour when the water gives back what it took.

He stared at the page. He had not used those words in ten years. They had come up out of him like fish rising to feed, unbidden, and now they were on the page looking at him.

He closed the notebook.

At the station, the town met him the way towns do—smaller than memory, smelling of salt and diesel, the station clock still four minutes fast as though even time here couldn't quite commit. He bought a coffee from the kiosk where a boy who could not have been born when Marguerite left sold him a flat white without comment, and the anonymity of it was almost restful. Almost.

Then he walked the length of the high street to the offices of Halloway & Sons, above the ironmonger's, and Ms. Prudence Okafor turned out to be a compact woman of about fifty with reading glasses on a chain and the brisk kindness of someone who handles other people's dead possessions for a living and has made her peace with it.

"Mr. Eastwick." She shook his hand firmly. "Thank goodness. Mr. Halloway was going to do it himself and he catalogs everything as 'miscellaneous.' Last month we sold a Regency writing desk listed as 'old table, some marks.'"

"I can do better than that."

"I'm counting on it." She pulled out a folder. "Now. The practicalities. You'll have keys, access any daylight hours. The utilities are off, so bring a torch. And—" she hesitated, the first hesitation of an otherwise fluent performance, "—there's no easy way to say this, so I'll just say it. The property has attracted some local interest. The Harrow Charitable Trust has registered as a bidder. They're keen on the site for a car park extension to the maritime museum."

Something moved through Marguerite, cold and quick, a fish under ice. "Of course they have," he said, and was pleased to hear his voice stay level, professional, the voice of a man discussing parking.

Ms. Okafor looked at him over her glasses for a moment longer than strictly necessary. "You knew them, perhaps. The Harrows."

"Everyone knew everyone," Marguerite said. "It's that kind of town."

"It is that kind of town," she agreed, and there was something in her tone—a small, dry current beneath the briskness—that suggested Ms. Okafor had catalogued more in this town than furniture. "Well. Keys." She slid them across the desk, three of them, heavy old brass, colder than the desk they'd lain on. "Number 14 Saltmere Row. Take your time with it. Good cataloguing wants patience."

"Thank you," Marguerite said, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it, how much the simple weight of brass in his palm undid him.

"One more thing." She was already turning back to her files, but she paused. "When you're in there. If you find letters. Family correspondence, that sort of thing." She adjusted her glasses. "Legally, personal papers aren't part of the sale lot. They revert to next of kin. People forget that. Auction houses forget that. It saves everyone bother when somebody remembers."

She said it lightly, the way one remarks on the weather, and returned to her files, and Marguerite stood there holding three cold keys and understanding that he had just been given something—not permission exactly, nothing so explicit—but a door left visibly unlocked, and that Ms. Okafor knew precisely what she had done.

"Next of kin," he repeated.

"If there is any," she said, not looking up.

* * *

He walked to Saltmere Row that evening instead of taking the bus, telling himself it was to save the fare, which was a lie so small it barely qualified, and arrived at the top of the lane as the light was going the color of weak tea.

The house stood where it had always stood, at the crook where the row bent away from the sea, as if it had spent ten years listening for footsteps and had finally heard them. Boards over the windows. Weeds in the gutter line, green hair growing from a stone skull. The gate hung open—one hinge gone, the other resigned—and the front door wore a Halloway & Sons padlock like a brooch pinned slightly askew.

Marguerite stood at the gate for a long time. Long enough that a neighbor's cat crossed the road, assessed him, and decided he was furniture.

He had prepared speeches for this moment. In the shower, on the train, into the dark of the sleeping carriage: *hello, old house; hello, ghost; I've come to count you.* None of them came. What came instead was the smell—damp stone and salt and, underneath, impossibly, after ten years, the ghost of his grandmother's woodsmoke, which was scientifically impossible and emotionally undeniable.

"You're here to catalogue," he said aloud, to the house, to himself, to the cat, who flicked an ear. "Furniture. Books. Effects. That's all."

The house said nothing, in the way of houses, which is to say it said everything, in creaks and settling and the sound of the sea moving somewhere beneath the floorboards like a sleeper turning over.

He fitted the key. The lock resisted, then remembered, then gave with a click that sounded, in the empty street, like a decision being made.

Inside, the hall smelled of dust and stopped time. His torch beam found the staircase, the newel post, the coat hooks, the door under the stairs. And at the end of the hall, tall and dark under its sheet like a mourner who had never left the funeral, stood the wardrobe.

Marguerite's torch held steady. His hands did not.

"Tomorrow," he told it, in the old dialect, before he could stop himself—the sea-word for *later*, the one his grandmother had used for everything deferred, everything promised, everything postponed past the point of safety.

Then he turned around, locked the door behind him, and walked back down the lane without looking at the wardrobe again, which was, he would reflect later, the first lie the house caught him in.
