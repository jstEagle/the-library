## 9. The Widow's Ledger

Edith Croy had kept her husband's ledger in the same drawer for eleven years, beneath the good tablecloth she never used and the funeral cards of everyone she'd ever outlived, which was nearly everyone. She did not open the drawer often. She opened it the way you visit a grave: on schedule, briefly, with your hands folded.

The ledger itself was nothing to look at. A fishmonger's book, salt-swollen, its cover gone soft as old skin, filled with her husband Bram's crabbed columns—herring landed, herring sold, herring owed by men who paid in gossip instead of coin. She had read it a hundred times. The fish were not what kept it in the drawer.

What kept it in the drawer was the last page.

She had shown it to no one. Not the constable who came asking polite questions the week after the trial. Not the priest, though he'd fished for it gently enough over two Easters. Not even herself, some nights, when the house creaked and she lay awake doing sums she didn't want to finish. For eleven years she had performed the small daily miracle of not knowing what she knew, and she had gotten so good at it that most mornings she almost believed her own performance.

Then the Eastwick boy had come back.

She'd seen him at the harbor his first morning, standing where the water could see him, looking at 14 Saltmere Row like a man reading his own obituary. He had his mother's way of holding his shoulders, as if apologizing to the air in advance. And he had Tomas's chin. That was the thing nobody in this town ever said aloud—that the convicted boy and the acquitted town had shared a face, more or less, and the town had chosen to forget it.

Edith had looked through him at the harbor, deliberately, the way you look through a window when you don't want the person outside to know you're home. It was cowardice. She recognized it professionally; she'd been practicing the discipline for a decade.

But then he'd started going into the house every day, cataloguing, and the auction signs had gone up, and the demolition notice after that, and Edith had begun to lie awake doing her sums again. Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't avoid: if the house came down, the last page came down with it. Not literally—the page lived in her drawer, not in the wardrobe—but everything around it would be gone. The context. The witness of walls. There were things a paper needed a house for, the way a word needed a mouth.

And there was the other sum, the one she hated: Bram had died before he could say what he saw, and part of her had always been grateful. Grateful, because saying it would have cost them everything, and she had been a young widow with a boat payment and no appetite for martyrdom. She had let the silence keep them both. It had fed her like a bad tenant feeds a landlord—regularly, just enough, and with contempt.

On Thursday she put on her good coat, wrapped the ledger in the unused tablecloth, and walked up Saltmere Row in weather that couldn't decide between fog and rain, which was the town's favorite kind because it excused everyone from seeing anyone.

* * *

Marguerite heard the knock twice before he answered, because the first time he assumed it was the boards settling. Nobody knocked on doors in this town anymore unless they wanted something or were delivering something, and he had ordered nothing and wanted, officially, nothing.

Edith Croy stood on the step with a parcel held against her chest like a shield held backward, protecting whatever was inside from the world rather than the reverse.

"Mrs. Croy," he said, and heard his own voice do the thing it did here—drop half an octave, pick up sand.

"You've got the fire lit." It wasn't a question. She was already looking past him into the hall, at the grate where he'd burned driftwood scraps against the damp. "Your mother used to say this house ate heat like a gull eats chips."

"She did." He stepped aside. It felt less like hospitality than surrender.

She came in, wiped her boots twice on the mat with a thoroughness that was clearly inherited from someone, and stood in the parlor taking inventory of him taking inventory. Boxes everywhere. His neat little tags. The wardrobe under its sheet in the corner like furniture in mourning.

"You're selling my childhood by weight," she said. "That's what the tags are, isn't it? Lot numbers."

"That's the job I told people I was doing."

"And what's the job you're actually doing?"

He should have lied. He was good at lying; he'd had ten years of practice translating himself into someone the city found unremarkable. But something about her directness—the flat harbor light of it—made the lie feel like showing up to church in the wrong clothes.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I think I'm looking for letters."

Something moved behind her face—not surprise, exactly. More like a door opening onto a room she'd already furnished.

"Hm," said Edith Croy, which in the local dialect was a complete sentence containing *I know*, *I'm afraid*, and *sit down*.

He sat. She did not. She unwrapped the parcel on the table between them with the care of a surgeon and the expression of someone defusing something.

"Bram kept the tide-book," she said. "Forty years. Every landing, every sale, every hour the water did anything worth writing down. He said the sea kept better books than God and he liked to check its work." Her hands flattened on the cover. "This is the year your brother went away."

Marguerite's mouth had gone dry in the specific way of a man hearing a language he thought dead spoken fluently in his kitchen. "Mrs. Croy—"

"Edith. You're past Mrs.-ing me; I watched you wet yourself at the harvest fair when you were four and I've never mentioned it, which makes us practically kin." She opened the ledger to the marked page and turned it toward him. "Read the entry for the ninth of October."

He read it. Then he read it again, because the first time his eyes had slid off it the way eyes slide off a price you can't afford.

*Bram Croy, out late mending pots off Gull Point. Fog thick. Saw lantern on the point road at 11:40, two figures, one tall carrying something heavy over the shoulder going DOWN toward the water, not up. Came ashore 12:15. Wrote it down because Bram writes everything down.*

"The trial record says the body went into the water before eleven," Marguerite said slowly. "That was the whole case against Tomas. That he was seen near the point at half ten, alone."

"Aye."

"If the— if someone was still carrying something down to the water at quarter to midnight—"

"Then your brother wasn't done being elsewhere yet," Edith said. "Or wasn't there at all when it mattered. Either way, the timeline the court swallowed whole has a hole in it you could moor a trawler through." She sat down finally, across from him, and her hands, which had been steady through all of it, began their trembling now, belatedly, like thunder arriving after the lightning. "Bram meant to go to the constable. He wrote that too—he was going to go Monday. He died Sunday night. His heart, they said. He was fifty-one and strong as kelp rope, but hearts are convenient."

The room was very quiet. Under the floorboards, faint and patient, Marguerite could hear the sea working at the pilings, the sound he'd been dreaming in all week.

"Why are you telling me now?" he asked. "Eleven years, Edith. Why today?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide to tell the truth, watched it cost her visibly, like watching someone count out coins they'd been saving.

"Because I'm a coward," she said, "and cowards can't carry things alone forever. The weight doesn't lessen. It just gets familiar, and familiarity is worse." She tapped the ledger once. "Bram saw something and died with it in his mouth, and I let it stay there because speaking would have cost me the shop, the pension, the roof. I told myself keeping quiet was keeping safe, and safe for whom? Not for Tomas Eastwick, rotting in a cell while the town patted itself on the back for having such tidy justice." Her voice cracked on the name and she let it. "I looked through you at the harbor because you're the spitting image of a boy we all helped bury alive, and I couldn't stand to be seen knowing it."

Marguerite found he couldn't speak. Ten years of words for hire—contracts, manuals, other people's confessions—and the plain English of *me too* was lodged in his throat like a bone.

"I spent a decade translating myself out of this place," he managed finally. "Every letter home, I made it smaller. Every memory, I made it neater. I thought if I could just get the grammar right, the guilt wouldn't conjugate." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "My brother took the blame for something. I've always known it the way you know weather coming—in the joints. I just never had the instrument to prove it."

"Now you have half of one." Edith pushed the ledger across the table. "Take it."

"I can't—"

"You can and you will, because if I keep it, I'll find a reason to put it back in the drawer, and I've had eleven years of reasons and they multiply like mice." Her jaw set. "There's more, isn't there. In that house. You've found some of it already—I can see it sitting on you like wet wool. Letters?"

He hesitated, then nodded. "One. Maybe more. My brother wrote that the truth lived 'in the wardrobe of a house that drowns.'"

Edith closed her eyes briefly. "Of course it does. This town buries everything in furniture and calls it inheritance." She stood, buttoned her coat, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch—an old woman silhouetted against fog, suddenly looking every one of her years and none of her apologies.

"One condition," she said. "Whatever you do with it—read it, burn it, nail it to the church door—you do it before they knock this house down. A testimony needs a floor under it, Marguerite Eastwick. Even the truth needs somewhere to stand."

The door closed behind her. Outside, the fog was lifting off the row in rags, and for the first time since he'd arrived, the house didn't feel empty. It felt occupied—by a ledger, by letters, by the sea talking under the floorboards in his grandmother's dialect, saying the one word it had always known how to say:

*Return. Return. Return.*

Marguerite looked at the sheeted wardrobe in the corner for a long time.

Then he reached for the corner of the cloth.
