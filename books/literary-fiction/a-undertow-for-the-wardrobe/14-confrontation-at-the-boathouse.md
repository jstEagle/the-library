## 14. Confrontation at the Boathouse

I rehearsed the confrontation four times on the walk down to the water, and in three of the rehearsals I was magnificent.

In the first, I arrived unannounced and said nothing at all, simply held up the child's confession like a lantern, letting Aldous Harrow read his own nine-year-old handwriting in the salt air while gulls commented overhead. In the second, I quoted Tomas's letters back to him line by line until he sat down on an oar bench and wept, which was satisfying but, I suspected, unlikely. In the third, I threw the letters into the sea and told him he could dive for them if he wanted them so badly.

The fourth rehearsal was the honest one: I walked down the shingle with my hands shaking so badly I had to put them in my pockets, and when I finally spoke, it would probably come out wrong, in the wrong order, with the wrong words, the way everything important I had ever tried to say had come out wrong since the night the police car took my brother away.

The boathouse stood at the end of the town's one respectable promenade, past the new memorial benches—*Donated by the Harrow Family Trust*, said the little brass plaques, all six of them—and out where the harbor gave up pretending to be a harbor and became estuary. It had been a working building once, tarred black, smelling of rope and diesel. Now it had been restored into something called a *boathouse*, which is what wealthy people call a room they keep boats out of. Glass doors faced the water. A small white launch knocked gently at its mooring outside. There was a wood stove visible through the glass, lit, though it was June.

Aldous Harrow was inside, alone, reading. Of course he was alone. Men like Aldous were never caught in crowds doing things that required witnesses; crowds were for endowing school roofs.

I stood outside the glass for a full minute watching him, this man who had been a boy beside me in Mrs. Tallow's classroom, who had once traded me half a jam sandwich for the privilege of looking at my tin soldiers, whose hand had written—I had seen it now, in the wardrobe, in pencil gone soft as breath—*I did not mean for him to go in the water. I did not tell anyone because Papa said the family would be ruined. I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry.* Three times. The way children write sorrows, as if repetition were penance.

He looked smaller than a magistrate should look. That was the thing nobody tells you about the powerful: up close, through glass, holding a paperback with a cracked spine, they are just bodies. Bodies that flinch.

I knocked on the glass.

He looked up, and I watched something move across his face—not guilt exactly, not yet, more like a man hearing a floorboard creak above him and knowing which room it came from. Then he composed himself, which took perhaps two seconds, and gestured me in with the gracious weariness of a host who has been expecting worse news than this.

"Marguerite Eastwick," he said. "They told me you were back. Cataloguing, I heard."

"That's right."

"For the estate." He set the paperback face-down on the arm of his chair—a thriller, I noticed, which pleased me obscurely; even magistrates want someone else to be guilty for a few hundred pages. "Sit, please. Can I—there's coffee, or—" He stopped himself, because we both knew there was no version of this conversation that included refreshments, and offering them was its own kind of confession. A man innocent of a thing offers you tea. A man guilty of a thing gets straight to the money.

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He folded his hands. They were good hands, magistrate's hands, kept soft by other people's labor. "You've been in the house."

"Yes."

"And you've found things."

"You know I have."

"I imagine I do." He looked out at the water, and for a moment—the first moment—he let the mask slip enough that I saw underneath it not a monster but a tired man of fifty-odd who had been carrying a boy's crime in a grown man's briefcase for forty years. "My father burned most of what there was to burn, you know. The week after the trial. In a biscuit tin, on the beach, at low tide. I stood next to him. I was fourteen. He made me watch so I'd understand what families owe each other." He smiled without any pleasure in it. "It's the only lesson of his I ever learned properly."

"Tomas went to prison for you."

"Tomas went to prison *instead of* me," Aldous said quietly. "Which is not the same sentence, though I grant it rhymes."

The rage arrived then, right on schedule, the one I'd been rehearsing. It came up through me like cold water through a wetsuit, and I heard myself say, "He was nineteen years old. He served six years. He came out and couldn't get work and drank himself dead by thirty-five, and your family paid for a *memorial wing*, Aldous. Brass plaques. Six of them between here and the church. Your name on brass while his name isn't even on a stone, because we couldn't afford a stone, because the fine took everything, because—"

"I know what it cost," he said.

"You don't. You can't. You were never—"

"I know what it cost," he repeated, and there was something in his voice now that stopped me, something that wasn't denial. "Not the way you know it. Not the way he knew it. But I've known it every day since I was nine years old, Mr. Eastwick, and I will know it on the day I die, and no amount of brass changes the arithmetic." He unfolded his hands, refolded them. "So. What do you want?"

And there it was. The question, laid on the table between us like a fish on a slab, still moving.

What did I want? I had walked down the promenade rehearsing justice, and justice had felt very clear from the high ground of the shingle. But standing in the warm boathouse, smelling the stove's applewood smoke, watching a guilty man age in real time, I found that rage, which had seemed bottomless from a distance, had a floor after all, and I could feel it under my feet, and it was closer than I'd thought.

"I want the truth on the record," I said. "The confession. The ledger. Edith Prynne's husband's tide-times. All of it, filed, public, permanent."

"The statute expired eleven years ago," he said. Not arguing. Informing. There is a particular tone men of law use when they are telling you the weather rather than the law. "No court can touch me. You must know that. Whatever you file, whatever you read aloud—and I'm told you're planning to read aloud, at the auction, which is theatrical, I'll give you—it changes nothing legal. It changes my life, yes. My wife's. My daughter's, who is twenty-two and believes her father is a decent man, and who is, on the whole evidence, correct, apart from one afternoon in 1979." He paused. "But it changes nothing legally. So again: what do you *want*?"

"You could say it yourself," I said. "Publicly. You could stand up and say it."

Something crossed his face then that I will be thinking about for the rest of my life. It was longing. Brief, fierce, immediately strangled—but unmistakable. For one half-second, Aldous Harrow wanted nothing in the world so much as to say it.

Then the magistrate reassembled himself, piece by piece, like a man putting on a uniform.

"No," he said. "I can't do that."

"Why not?"

"Because the town runs on the version of events it agreed to in 1979, and if I pull that thread, the school roof comes down with it. The harbor trust. The clinic. Half the endowments trace back to my father's indemnity arrangements—you think the Harrow money was always clean? It was bought with silence, and it has been paying the town's bills ever since. You'd be burning down the village to warm one grave." He leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not even asking you to believe I'm sorry, though I am, and have been, in the useless way that helps no one. I'm asking you to price it."

"Price it."

"Name a figure. For the letters, the ledger, the child's confession—all of it. And separately, a figure for the house. I'll buy the house at auction through a proxy, quietly, and it comes down, and the ground goes into the trust, and in ten years no one remembers there was a house there at all. You walk away with enough to live on for—" he did a small calculation, the way he might assess damages in chambers, "—ten years. Fifteen, if you're careful. You could stop translating contracts, Marguerite. You could write your own book. Isn't that what you always wanted? You used to say so. Under the pier, summer of '78. You said you'd write a book about the sea, and I said books about the sea were boring, and you pushed me in." Almost, almost, he smiled. "You were strong for a small boy. I remember being surprised."

I remembered it too. I had forgotten remembering it. That is the trick memory plays, keeping the harmless things safe while it lets the important ones rot.

"I'm not selling," I said.

"Everyone sells," Aldous said, without cruelty. That was the worst part. He said it the way a fishmonger says *the tide comes in*. "I don't mean you're weak. I mean everyone has a number, and the number is usually lower than they think, and higher than they can admit. Mine was my father's approval. Yours—" he gestured gently at me, at my coat, at the rented room I hadn't mentioned, at the whole shape of my returned and threadbare life, "—yours is probably rest. You look like a man who hasn't slept indoors properly in some time. No judgment. I've read your situation in the probate filings; the estate owes debts, the sale proceeds are spoken for, and you, personally, will leave this town with less than you came with unless something changes. I'm offering to be the something."

The stove ticked. Outside, the little white launch knocked at its mooring, patient as a dog.

And here is what I am ashamed of, and will write down anyway, because a man who translates testimony for a living ought to be able to translate his own: I considered it. Not for a moment—for minutes. I stood in the warm room and let him show me the life: the flat with a door that locked, the desk facing water, the mornings that belonged to me, the word *no* finally affordable. I let myself feel how much I wanted it, and I watched Aldous watch me wanting it, and neither of us pretended otherwise, and there was a horrible honesty in that, a courtesy almost, between two men who understood each other perfectly across the wreckage.

"I need to think," I said.

"Of course." He rose, and shook my hand, and his grip was dry and firm and human. "Take the week. The auction is Saturday, yes? I'll have my solicitor draw up papers either way. If you decide against, burn them, and no harm done, and I will never trouble you again." At the door he stopped. "Marguerite. One thing, off the record."

"What?"

"The night it happened. I've never told anyone this, not even—" he glanced toward the town, toward his wife, his daughter, his brass plaques. "Tomas knew. Before the trial. He came to me, the morning after, down by the sluice gate, and he looked at me—you were eight, you wouldn't remember, you were asleep—and he said, *I know it was you, and I'll carry it, but you'll owe the sea, not me.* Those were his words. *You'll owe the sea.*" Aldous looked out at the gray water. "I've spent forty years paying the town instead, because the town sends receipts and the sea doesn't. But I've never once swum past the second sandbar. Forty years, and I can't make myself go past the second sandbar." He opened the glass door; the salt wind came in and moved the papers on his desk. "So when you price it, price that too. What it costs, living owed."

I walked back along the promenade past the six brass plaques, and the evening light came low across the water and turned them briefly gold, each one, in sequence, like a row of small dishonest suns, and I thought: he didn't deny it. He didn't confess. He offered money, which is what remains when a man has run out of both innocence and courage.

And I thought: I considered it. God help me, I considered it, and I would consider it again tonight, and tomorrow, right up until the moment I chose—and the terror was not that I might choose wrongly, but that I no longer knew, walking home in the dark past the dark house on Saltmere Row, which choice was the wrong one.

At the corner by the fishmonger's, Edith was waiting under the awning with her collar up, and she took one look at my face and said, "He offered you money."

"How did you—"

"Because he offered my Harold money, forty years ago, and Harold took it, and it bought the plot we're buried in, and it's been sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook ever since." She fell into step beside me. "Come inside, love. Put the kettle on. And then you're going to tell me your number—because everyone has one, and I'd rather hear yours while you're still ashamed of it than after."
