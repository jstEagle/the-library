## 16. The Dying Man's Request

The hospice nurse had a name like a weather report—Dana—and a way of delivering bad news that made it sound like a forecast you'd already half expected.

"He's sleeping more," she said, in the corridor of a facility that smelled of lemon disinfectant and someone else's flowers. "Eating less. You know how this goes."

I did know how this went. I had read about it, dreaded it, rehearsed it in the shower the way other people rehearsed arguments they'd never have. My father was dying at the speed of tide going out—not dramatically, not all at once, but steadily, so that every time I looked at him there was slightly less of him than the time before, and no single moment I could point to and say *there, that's when it happened.*

"Is he lucid?" I asked.

"This morning, yes." Dana smiled the professional smile of someone who has learned that hope is a dosage issue. "He asked for his razor. Wanted to shave. Said—" she checked nothing, recited from memory, which told me she'd heard it more than once— "*said if a Nystrom is going to see me, I'm not doing it looking like this.*"

"A Nystrom," I repeated.

"That's what he said."

My father had not said the name *Nystrom* aloud in my presence since the funeral, since the graveside, since the day I drove home with salt drying on my cheeks and a stranger's grief in my passenger seat where a person should have been. He said *Vesterholt* the way other men said their own names into hotel reservations—flatly, out of habit, without believing anyone was listening. But *Nystrom* he kept behind glass, like something in a case, like evidence.

And now he wanted to shave for one.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being the child of a dying man who is also an unreliable narrator: you become the editor of a manuscript whose author can no longer answer your queries. Every afternoon I sat by his bed with my marginalia—*who pushed whom? what did Grandma Vesterholt's will actually say? why did Aunt Ingrid let you take the blame?*—and every afternoon the author drifted off mid-sentence, or answered a question I hadn't asked, or looked at me with eyes gone suddenly clear and said things like, "You have her chin," meaning my mother, dead eleven years, meaning *you are made of everyone I've lost,* and then closed his eyes as though the sentence had cost him everything he had left.

That morning he had been clearer than usual. The nurse had opened the blinds because he asked, and the light came in off the parking lot, gray and flat, the color of the sea on days it couldn't be bothered.

"Wren." He said my name like a man testing whether his voice still worked. It did, barely. A voice like a door hinge that needed oiling but would open. "Did you go back?"

"To the house? Yes. Twice."

"And?" And. One syllable containing forty years of exile. I told him about the study, the drawers, the photograph turned face-down on the parlor table—the way Ingrid Nystrom had arranged her whole house like a woman hiding a body in plain sight. I told him about Dominic, careful not to say too much, because my father's face did something complicated whenever I mentioned him, some muscle memory of love and blame fighting over the same territory.

"He wrote to me," Elias said.

"What?"

"Ingrid. She wrote. Every year." His hand moved on the blanket, a small gesture, like smoothing a page. "Never sent them. I know because Marta Voss told me, years ago, and because—" he almost laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and I held the cup of water to his mouth and hated how naturally my hands knew this work now, how quickly tenderness becomes logistics— "because if she'd sent even one, I'd have come home. She knew that. That's why she never sent any. They weren't letters. They were anchors. She was making sure I stayed sunk."

"Dad—"

"I don't want an apology." He said it fast, before the water could soften him. This was the speech. I recognized it; he'd been drafting it for weeks, revising in his sleep. "I want you to understand that. If the boy comes here and starts in with *my mother meant well*—"

"He won't."

"—or *she was protecting us*—"

"Dad."

"—then I'll ask him to leave, and it will be ugly, and I will die ugly, and neither of us needs that." He lay back against the pillows, exhausted by three sentences, furious about being exhausted by three sentences. "I want him to sit down. I want him to listen. I want—" and here his voice did the thing, dropped through the floor of itself, "—I want to look at his face and see if there's anything of Hal in it. Of my brother. Before I go. That's all. That's the whole request. It isn't much. It's enormous. I know."

It wasn't much. It was everything. It was a man asking to be acknowledged by the family that had deleted him, and asking it the only way pride permitted—as a demand, wrapped in conditions, armored against the possibility of refusal.

"I'll get him there," I said.

"You'll try."

"I'll get him there."

"Wren." He caught my wrist. His grip was still strong; the dying hadn't gotten to his hands yet, and I was grateful and horrified in equal measure. "If he says no—if he's his mother's son down to the marrow—then you leave it. You hear me? You leave it and you come home and you don't you-dare carry this for me. I've carried it sixty years. It fits me. It won't fit you."

I promised. We both knew I was lying. It's a family trait, promising things while planning the opposite. At least I came by it honestly—from him.

* * *

Three days later, Dana called me at seven in the morning, which is a hour hospice nurses reserve for news that can't wait for business hours but isn't quite the middle-of-the-night kind either.

"He had a bad night. Confusion, some distress. He's settled now, but—" the pause, the professional recalibration, "—he's asking for you. And he keeps saying something about the house. About the tide coming in. Wren, I don't want to alarm you, but these windows get shorter. When he's clear, he's clear for an hour, maybe two. If there's something that needs doing—"

There was something that needed doing. There had been something that needed doing for fifteen years, and everyone involved had been very busy not doing it.

I hung up and stood in my kitchen holding my phone like a compass that had stopped pointing north. The problem was not getting Dominic to the hospital. The problem was that Dominic had said no—had said it kindly, which was worse, had said it the way his mother must have said everything, with that terrible gentle finality, *I can't, not yet, I need time*—and I had driven home from that corridor wanting to put my fist through his beautiful, cowardly, grieving face, and instead had said the true cruel thing because it was the only weapon I had: *You're still practicing almost.*

Fifteen years of almost. That's what he'd confessed to, in the candlelight, in the storm-dark house, reading Ingrid's last unfinished letter aloud while the rain tried the windows like a creditor. Fifteen years of composing a sentence to a drowned man and never finishing it. And I understood—I did, God help me, I understood better than I ever admitted to him—because I had spent my whole childhood practicing my own version. Practicing *someday we'll go back.* Practicing *someday Dad will tell me the whole story.* Practicing loving people who kept the important rooms locked.

But understanding is not the same as patience, and my father's supply of someday had run measurable now. Days, maybe. Dana hadn't said it in words but she'd said it in the length of her pauses.

So: force him, or respect him?

Force him, and I became the thing I accused the Nystroms of—a person who decided for someone else what they could bear. Respect him, and I became my father—proud, principled, and alone at the end, having honored everyone's silence including my own.

I thought about Ingrid's letters. Anchors, Elias called them. But sitting there in my kitchen with the kettle ticking toward boiling and refusing to be hurried, I thought: no. Wrong word. Letters you write every year for fifteen years to a man you refuse to contact aren't anchors. They're a rope thrown over the side of a boat, year after year, hoping the drowning man has the dignity not to grab it. She wanted to be forgiven without having to ask. She wanted him saved without having to save him. Almost. Everyone in this family was fluent in almost.

I was done being fluent in almost.

* * *

I drove north along the coast road with the windows down because the house, when I got to it, always smelled of someone else's history, and today I needed my own air. The sky was doing that pale washed-out thing it does in late October, light without warmth, the sun a coin behind wax paper. Offshore, the water lay in long gray swells, patient as paperwork.

I had a plan. I want to be honest about the plan, because the plan was not a good plan, and honesty is supposed to be the whole point of this family I was trying to build out of wreckage.

The plan was: I would drive to the house. I would not go in. I would leave the envelope on the porch—inside it, a copy of the letter Elias had written, the one he'd dictated to me two nights ago at three in the morning when the confusion lifted and he surfaced, blinking, and said, *Write this down before I lose it.* Four sentences. I had copied them out in my best handwriting because it felt like a document, because it was one:

*I am not asking you to forgive her. I am asking you to bring me your face before mine stops working. Whatever happened on those rocks, boy, I was reaching for you. I have been reaching for you for fifty years. Come be reached.*

Four sentences, and the fourth one had undone me completely, and I had sat in the hospice parking lot afterward with my forehead on the steering wheel, honking the horn once, accidentally, into the empty morning, like a fool, like a daughter, like both.

So the plan was: envelope on the porch. No confrontation. No scene. I would respect his no by leaving the yes where he couldn't help finding it. Cowardly? Perhaps. Strategic? Certainly. It was, I realized somewhere around the second mile, exactly the kind of thing Ingrid Nystrom would have done—arranging truth on a table and turning it face-down, trusting the right person to eventually turn it over.

God. Even my rebellion was inherited.

The cove appeared the way it always did, too fast, the road dropping away and the house rising up out of the dune grass like something the tide had decided to give back. Shuttered. Waiting. Gulls standing around the roofline like a committee that had convened and forgotten its agenda.

His car was there.

I pulled in behind it and sat with my hands on the wheel and the engine ticking down and felt the whole plan begin quietly to die. Because the car meant he was home, and home meant he might come out, and if he came out I would have to do the thing face-to-face that I had designed the envelope specifically to avoid: ask a man to walk into a room where his entire childhood was waiting to be revised.

I got out anyway. Love refuses to wait for permission—that was the lesson of the week, the lesson of the letters, the lesson of a woman who wrote to her erased brother-in-law every anniversary for fifteen years and never once licked a stamp. Ingrid loved Elias. I believed that now, finally, the way you believe hard things: reluctantly, and with evidence. She just loved him in the passive voice. And passive-voice love is how families drown—one person waiting for another to act, everyone treading water, the shore receding politely in every direction.

I climbed the porch steps. The boards gave their complaint. Through the window I could see movement inside—him, a shadow crossing the parlor, pausing where the photograph used to lie face-down. It lay face-up now. I'd turned it over myself, weeks ago, and watched his face when he saw his mother young and laughing under my father's arm, and I had thought: *there, that's when it started, that's the moment the ground tilted.*

I set the envelope on the table by the door. Weighted it with a stone from the garden, because the wind off the cove had opinions about paper. Then I stood there like an idiot for a full minute, rehearsing a note I hadn't written, a speech I wouldn't give, and finally turned to go—

And the door opened.

Not dramatically. Not with music. Just the latch, and the hinge, and Dominic Nystrom standing in the doorway in a sweater with a paint-stained cuff, holding a mug of something, looking at me with the expression of a man who has been standing on the other side of a door for some time, listening to someone decide whether to knock.

"The stone was unnecessary," he said. "I've been watching you argue with yourself for ten minutes. You lost, by the way. You picked the envelope up twice and put it down twice."

"I was deciding on placement."

"You were deciding whether to run." He stepped back from the doorway—an invitation, or a surrender, I genuinely could not tell. "Come in. Because I think you're here to ask me something, and I've been practicing my answer, and I'd rather do it sitting down."

I looked past him, into the hall of that sealed and catalogued house, at the boxes and the face-up photograph and the long corridor of a family's managed silence—and I thought of my father upstairs in a narrow bed, shaving that morning with a shaking hand, asking a nurse if his collar was straight, preparing to be seen by the boy he'd pulled from the water fifty years ago.

"All right," I said, and stepped inside. "But I'm only going to ask once. And Dominic—it isn't really a question."
