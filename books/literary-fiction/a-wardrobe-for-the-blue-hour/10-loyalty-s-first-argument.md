## 10. Loyalty's First Argument

The knock came at seven in the morning, which was how Odile knew it was her brother before she opened the door. Anders had never in his life knocked at a civilized hour; he arrived the way weather arrived, on his own schedule, with his own pressure systems, and expected the world to adjust. She had been awake anyway, sitting at the kitchen table with the letters fanned out in front of her like a losing hand of cards, and when the knock came her first instinct — absurd, shameful — was to sweep them out of sight.

She did not. She left them where they were and went to answer the door.

Anders stood on the step in his good coat, the charcoal one he wore to closings and funerals, and behind him, parked crookedly at the gate, sat the rental car he'd driven up from the city overnight. He looked like he hadn't slept, which softened her for exactly the length of time it took him to speak.

"Who is the man who's been standing at our gate," he said, "for the last three days?"

"Come in."

"Odile. Who is he?"

"Come in, Anders. It's cold, and you look terrible, and I've made coffee."

He came in the way he always came into the house — too fast, too loud, filling the hallway with the smell of the outside world, petrol and cold air and the particular synthetic sweetness of hotel soap. He hung his coat on the hook their mother had painted blue forty years ago and never repainted, and he stopped in the kitchen doorway when he saw the table.

The letters. The locked drawer, opened. The notebook with its lists. Odile watched him take it in the way she'd once watched him take in a broken window — the quick calculation of cost, of blame, of how much of this was going to land on him.

"You went through her things," he said.

"I'm cataloging her things. That's what I came here to do. That's what we agreed."

"We agreed you'd sort the house for sale. I don't recall agreeing you'd open her private drawers and read her private letters."

"They're addressed to a V.," Odile said. "Forty years of them. None of them sent. Anders, she never posted a single one."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he did the thing he always did when he was frightened, which was to move — to the kettle, to the window, to the task at hand — as if motion could outrun whatever was chasing him. He poured himself coffee she hadn't offered and drank it standing, looking out at the garden, where the rain had finally stopped but everything still dripped.

"Tell me about the man," he said again.

So she told him. The graveside. The name — *Vesterholt* — spoken over the coffin like a password to a room neither of them had known existed. The lawyer who claimed ignorance of the codicil. The hour Tomas had stood at the gate in the rain, asking not for money but for an answer, and how she had turned him away, and how she had watched him from the upstairs window until the dark took him, and how she had not slept properly since.

Anders listened without interrupting, which was worse than interrupting. When she finished he set his cup down with a click that sounded, in the quiet kitchen, like a small bone breaking.

"Vesterholt," he said, tasting it. "That's a Danish name."

"That's what I thought too."

"Mother was from Jutland. That's not a crime, Odile. Half the families in this country changed their names somewhere in the last two centuries. Wars, borders, bad marriages. People reinvent themselves. It doesn't mean —" He stopped. He looked at the letters again, at the topmost one, at the faded ink of *Kære V.* across its face, and something moved behind his eyes that she couldn't name. "It doesn't mean anything."

"It means something to him. He stood in the rain for an hour."

"Or he's mad," Anders said. "Or he's after the estate. Grief makes people strange, Odile, and estates make them stranger. You've been alone in this house for two weeks reading a dead woman's mail and you're ready to hand our mother's name to the first gaunt stranger who murmurs something dramatic over a coffin."

"He wasn't dramatic. He was —" She searched for the word and found it, and hated it. "He was grieving. That's what I keep coming back to. You grieve for someone you loved, Anders. He wasn't performing it. You can't fake that particular kind of tired."

"You don't know him."

"No," she said. "And I didn't know her. That's rather the point I've been arriving at."

* * *

They fought properly for the first time since their father's funeral, eleven years ago, and it had the same shape — the old choreography of it, the way they fell into their childhood positions without meaning to, Odile the one who wanted to look at things, Anders the one who wanted to put them away.

He wanted the house sealed. That was the word he used — *sealed* — as though the house were evidence, or a tomb, or a letter. Lock it, list it with an agent, let the estate lawyers handle the codicil however codicils were handled, and go home. He had a life in the city. He had a daughter who needed him. He had, he said, no appetite for excavating their mother's past on the strength of a stranger's grief and a drawer full of letters that their mother had very clearly chosen not to send.

"Chosen not to send," Odile repeated. "You hear yourself. She wrote to this person for forty years and chose, every year, not to send it. What does that tell you?"

"That she was private. That she was —" he gestured vaguely at the kitchen, the house, the whole fabric of the life they'd grown up in — "that she was *Mama*. Odile, she alphabetized the spice rack. She ironed tea towels. She went to church every Sunday of her life and put flowers on Mrs. Brandt's step every spring. That woman did not have a secret that required a stranger standing at our gate in the rain."

"Then the letters will say so. Read one. That's all I'm asking. Read one and tell me I'm wrong."

"I'm not reading them."

"Why not?"

"Because they're *hers*." His voice cracked on the word and he hated that she'd heard it, and she watched him hate it, watched the flush climb his neck. "Because whatever is in that drawer belongs to her, and she kept it locked for a reason, and the decent thing — the loyal thing — is to let it stay locked. She's not here to explain herself. She can't defend herself. And you want to drag her out and put her on trial in her own kitchen on the word of a man we've spoken to for four minutes total."

"The codicil, Anders."

"The codicil is a lawyer's problem."

"*The Vesterholt matter is closed.* She wrote that. Her own hand. She tied the house — tied *us* — to something she called a matter. Matters close, Anders. This one never closed. That's why the house can't be sold. That's why she wrote it into her will instead of telling us at the dinner table like a human being."

He was silent. The rain started again against the window, soft and indifferent. And Odile saw it then — saw the thing underneath his anger, the thing his anger had been built on top of like scaffolding over a crack — and she understood, with a lurch, that he was not afraid of what the letters would say about their mother.

He was afraid of what they would say about him. About what he might have known, or half-known, or chosen not to know. About the shape of the silence he had lived inside all these years without ever once putting his ear to it.

"Anders," she said, gently now. "What aren't you telling me?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "Nothing. I'm telling you we sell the house and go home. That's all. That's the whole of what I'm telling you."

* * *

He spent the morning on the phone in the sitting room, and she let him. She heard the low professional murmur of it — the estate agent, the lawyer, someone at the bank — and she understood that he was building a wall out of appointments, that every call was another brick. By noon he had an appraiser coming Thursday and a viewing arranged for the following week, and he came into the kitchen with the particular brightness of a man who has solved a problem by refusing to look at it.

"I've booked you a room at the hotel in town," he said. "For after the appraiser's been through. There's no reason for you to keep sleeping here."

"I like sleeping here."

"You like *punishing* yourself here. There's a difference." He sat down across from her, and for a moment the anger drained out of him and left something younger, something she recognized from a hospital waiting room eleven years ago, from a corridor outside an intensive care unit, from every bad night of their shared childhood that had ended with the two of them sitting somewhere quiet, waiting for news. "Odile. Listen to me. I loved her too."

"I know."

"And whatever this is — this man, this name, this drawer — it's going to hurt. You can feel it already, can't you. You can feel it in your chest. And you keep reading anyway, because you think the truth is going to be a door and on the other side of it you'll finally understand her. But it isn't a door. It's a well. You don't get to the bottom of a thing like this and climb out clean. You just get wet."

"That's the most honest thing you've said all morning."

"I mean it." He leaned forward. "Some things are better carried than opened. She carried this — whatever it is — for forty years, alone, and she still made us dinner every night. She still came to every one of my school things. She still —" his voice went, briefly, "— she still braided your hair every Sunday until you were twelve and told her to stop. Whatever she was carrying, it didn't stop her from being our mother. Maybe that's the answer. Maybe the letters don't need reading. Maybe the whole of what she was is already in the house, in the forty years we actually had her, and the rest of it belongs to the dead."

Odile looked at her brother — at the charcoal coat over the back of his chair, at the shadows under his eyes, at the wedding ring he still wore a year after the divorce — and she felt the argument in her go quiet, not because he had won but because she could see, finally, how much it was costing him to lose so badly and keep pretending he hadn't.

"Loyal," she said. "You keep using that word."

"Because it's the right word."

"Loyal to what, though? That's what I can't get past. Loyal to her — or loyal to the version of her we invented because the real one frightened us?" She turned one of the letters over on the table, so the address faced up, so the faded ink faced the ceiling like a small grey sky. "Because I've been thinking about loyalty, Anders. I think loyalty and silence get confused an awful lot in families. I think we call it love when really it's just fear with better manners."

He stood. He took his coat off the blue hook and put it on, and for a moment, in the doorway, he looked exactly like their father — the same set of the shoulders, the same way of leaving a room as though the room had disappointed him.

"Thursday the appraiser comes," he said. "After that, we seal it. I mean it, Odile. I'll get a court order if I have to. Don't make me be that brother."

She didn't answer. He let himself out, and the rental car pulled away through the wet lane, and she stood at the window until it was gone.

* * *

The house was very quiet after he left — that specific post-argument quiet, in which every object in the room seems to be listening. Odile washed his cup and put it back in the cupboard where their mother had always kept it, third hook from the left, and her hands were steady, and she was surprised by that. She had expected to shake. Instead she felt a strange, cold clarity, the clarity that comes after a storm has passed and shown you the shape of the coastline.

He knew something. That was the day's harvest, and it was a bitter one. Not everything — but something. The way he had looked at the letters. The way he had said *hers* with that crack in his voice. The way he had reached for *sealed* so quickly, so fluently, as though he'd had the word ready for years, waiting in a pocket.

And underneath her anger at him, worse, was the question his anger had planted in her: *how much of this is my fault too?* She had lived in this house eighteen years. She had eaten at this table. She had watched her mother stand at this window on winter evenings with a look Odile had always filed away as homesickness — homesickness for Jutland, for the sea, for some lost Danish childhood — and she had never once asked. Not once in eighteen years had she said, *Mama, what are you looking at?*

She had respected the silence. That was what she'd called it, all these years. Respecting it. And now a gaunt stranger stood at her gate and called the silence by its true name, and the name was *Vesterholt*, and it turned out that respect and cowardice wore the same coat.

She went back to the kitchen table. The letters waited, forty years deep, and she had read only the first. She had been rationing them — one a day, she'd told herself, like medicine — but Anders's visit had changed the arithmetic. He wanted them sealed. He would come back with lawyers and court orders and the full weight of his frightened love, and every day she delayed was a day he grew more entrenched.

She squared the bundle on the table. Forty letters, give or take, tied with the same faded ribbon her mother had used for the Christmas cake boxes. She untied it again, and her fingers were steady, and she was not sure whether that was courage or numbness, and she suspected the two looked identical from the outside.

The second letter was dated the year Odile was born. The third the year after. The fourth skipped a decade — a gap she noted in the margin of her notebook, a gap like a held breath — and the fifth, the sixth, and then she came to the seventh, and the seventh was different. The handwriting was shakier. The paper was a different stock, bought later, and there was a small brown stain in one corner, old tea or old tears.

She unfolded it, and read the opening line, and stopped breathing.

*Dear V. I saw the boy again today. The one who watches the house burn in his sleep. He was at the fence, and he looked at me, and I could not look away.*

Odile sat very still in the blue hour light, her brother's coffee cup drying on the rack behind her, and understood that whatever she had been reading until now had only been the approach.

She turned the page.
