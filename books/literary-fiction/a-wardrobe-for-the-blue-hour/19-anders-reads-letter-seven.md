## 19. Anders Reads Letter Seven

Anders arrived on a Thursday, which was how Odile knew he had been rehearsing.

Her brother had never in his life come to the house on a weekday without an appointment, without a reason that could be written on a calendar and defended in front of witnesses. Thursday meant he had planned the visit in advance, had sat somewhere — his office, his car, the kitchen of the house he shared with a wife who kept the curtains open in every room — turning over what he intended to say. Odile had learned this about him long ago, back when they were still children and he was already a small man in a boy's body, arranging his grievances like cutlery before a meal.

He stood in the doorway with his coat still buttoned and his keys in his fist, and he looked past her into the hall, at the boxes she had not finished, at the wardrobe standing open at the end of the corridor like a mouth that had not yet said what it came to say.

"You're still here," he said.

"I live here now. Temporarily."

"That's not what I meant." He stepped inside without being invited, which was also new, or perhaps not new — perhaps it was only the old habit of a brother who had never quite learned that his sister's thresholds were real. He hung his coat on the hook their mother had screwed into the doorframe thirty years ago, and the gesture was so automatic, so domestic, that it hurt her to watch. He hung the coat as if the house were still his mother's. As if the dead had merely stepped out for an hour and would come back wanting their hallway tidy.

"I spoke to the lawyer," Anders said. "He told me you've been going through her papers."

"Someone has to."

"And he told me about the codicil." He turned to face her then, and she saw that he had not slept well, that the skin beneath his eyes had gone the color of weak tea. "The Vesterholt matter. Odile, what have you found?"

It was the question she had been waiting for, and she had imagined answering it a dozen ways — coldly, gently, with the letters already in his hands, with the letters hidden behind her back. She had imagined him defensive, dismissive, laughing. What she had not imagined was the thing she saw now, which was fear. Not the fear of a man about to be accused. The fear of a man who already knew the shape of the room he was standing in and had known it for years.

"You've seen them," she said. "Haven't you."

"Seen what."

"Don't." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Don't do the thing where you make me say everything first. You came here on a Thursday. You spoke to the lawyer. You're standing in this house like it's a courtroom you've already lost. You know what I'm talking about, Anders."

He looked at the coat hook. He looked at the floorboards, at the pale rectangle on the wall where their mother's only landscape had hung and where Odile had taken it down to catalog it. He did not look at her.

"There's a locked drawer," he said finally. "In the desk. In the study."

"Yes."

"I found it when I was nineteen."

* * *

He would not sit in the kitchen. He said the kitchen was too much her, which Odile took a moment to understand and then understood completely — he meant it was too much the room where their mother had stood every morning of their childhood with her back to them, stirring something, silent in the particular way of a person who has decided that silence is the safest country and has taken up permanent residence. He wanted the study, with the door closed, as if the walls of this house had ever kept anything in.

So they sat in the study, on either side of the desk that held the drawer, and Odile put the letters on the blotter between them. Forty of them, tied with the same green string their mother had used for the garden beans, addressed in her small upright hand to a single initial. *V.* Forty years of them. The earliest yellowed and soft at the corners; the last one written on paper so recent it still smelled faintly of the stationery shop in town, which meant their mother had bought it within the last two years, which meant that until nearly the end of her life she had been writing to a name she never used, a name she had buried so deep that her own children had never heard it spoken aloud until a gaunt stranger murmured it over her coffin.

Anders did not touch them.

"I was nineteen," he said again, as if the number explained everything, and maybe it did. "It was the summer I came home from my first year at university. You were away — that camp, the one with the lake. She went to visit Aunt Margit for a week and I stayed to look after the house. I was looking for — God, I don't even remember. A corkscrew. I was nineteen, I wanted to drink her wine and feel grown up." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and then stopped being one. "The drawer was locked. It had always been locked. And I was nineteen, and it never occurred to me that a locked drawer might be locked for a reason. I found the key in the tin box on top of the wardrobe. She kept everything in that tin — spare buttons, the key to the shed, the little brass elephant. I don't think she ever imagined one of us would think to look there. Or maybe she did. Maybe that was the point. Maybe she kept the key somewhere findable because some part of her wanted to be caught."

Odile's hands were flat on the blotter, on either side of the bundle, as if she were bracketing it, holding it in place. "You read them."

"I read the first one. I read the first one standing up, and then I sat down on the floor of her bedroom and read all of them, every one, back to back, and it was dark when I finished and I hadn't turned on a light." He was speaking quickly now, the words coming up out of him like water from a well that had been capped for decades. "I put them back. I locked the drawer. I put the key in the tin. And when she came home on Sunday I ate dinner with her and asked her to pass the salt, and I never said a word, not once, not in thirty years."

"Why?" The question came out of Odile almost gently, which surprised her. She had expected it to come out like a blade. "Why not tell me? Why not tell anyone?"

"Tell you what?" And there it was — the crack in him, the first real one. His voice went up and broke on the word. "Tell you what, Odile? That the woman who made our lunches was — that she had done — " He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "I was nineteen. She was my mother. You don't understand what it was like, reading those letters. It wasn't like reading about a stranger. It was her. Her handwriting. Her little way of crossing her sevens. I could hear her voice in every line. And the things she — " He lowered his hands. "Letter seven. You've read letter seven."

"I've read them all."

"Then you know."

"I know what's in it. I want to know what it did to you."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the light was doing what the light did in this house at this hour — the blue hour, their mother's hour, the hour she had always claimed for herself, standing at the window with her tea going cold. The room filled with that color, that bruised, tender blue that made everything look like a memory of itself, and in that light Odile watched her brother's face and understood that she had never actually known him, that they had grown up in the same house on either side of the same wall of silence and had each believed they were alone behind it.

"I went back to the house the next summer," he said. "And the summer after that. Every year I told myself I'd say something. Every year I opened the drawer and read letter seven again, as if it might have changed, as if I'd dreamed it. And every year I put it back." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "You want to know the worst part? The worst part is that I got good at it. I got so good at not knowing. I built a whole life on top of it — career, marriage, the house with the open curtains — and the whole thing stood on this one locked drawer and I never told a soul, because telling would have meant it was true, and as long as it was a secret it was almost like it hadn't happened."

"And her name," Odile said. "You protected her name."

"I protected *her*." He said it fiercely, and then the fierceness collapsed. "I protected her, and I protected myself, and I told myself those were the same thing. That's what shame is, I think. It's the story you tell so you don't have to tell the true one."

* * *

She made him read it again. Not because she was cruel — or not only because of that, though she was honest enough with herself to know there was some of that in it, some need for him to feel what she had felt, alone, at midnight, with the house ticking around her. But mostly because she had learned something in the past weeks, something the letters had taught her: that a thing read silently stays a secret, and a thing read aloud becomes a fact, and facts, however terrible, could be carried by two people where one would be crushed.

"Out loud," she said. "Please."

He took the page in both hands, the way you take something that might be alive. Their mother's voice filled the study, in his mouth, in her words, in the blue light:

*Dear V. — The boy came back today. He stood at the fence the way he always stands, not close, not far, just at the exact place where the ground drops away, and he watched the house the way children watch things they cannot look away from. He is growing. He has his father's shoulders already. I wanted to go out to him. I stood in the doorway with my hand on the latch and I could not open it. What would I say? What can a person say who stood in the light of what burned and said nothing? I have written this letter eleven times. I will write it eleven more. I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking you to know that I saw him. That I see him still. That I will see him every day for the rest of my life, standing at the fence, and that this is not a punishment I accept but one I have chosen, and that even chosen it is not enough, and that I know it is not enough, and that I am a coward, and that the boy deserves someone braver than I will ever be.*

Anders's voice failed on the last line. He set the page down on the blotter with exaggerated care, the way you set down something breakable, and then he put his face in his hands and wept.

Odile had never seen her brother cry. Not as a child, not at their father's funeral, not at their mother's — at the graveside he had been dry-eyed and correct, arranging the flowers, thanking the mourners, and she had thought, in her numbness, that he simply grieved differently, more privately, more like their mother. Now she understood that he had been carrying this for thirty years and that the graveside tears had been spent long ago, in a locked study, at nineteen, over a letter about a boy at a fence.

She came around the desk. She did not know what she intended until she did it — put her hand on the back of his neck, the way their mother never had, the way their father never had, the way no one in their family had ever touched anyone, and felt him lean into it like a man leaning into a door he had been holding shut for decades.

"She watched him," Odile said. "All those years. She knew where he was. She knew what he looked like growing up."

"I know."

"She could have gone to him. One word. One visit. She had forty years, Anders. Forty years of letters to a name, and she never once — "

"I know." He lifted his head. His eyes were swollen, his face wrecked, and beneath the wreckage there was something new, something that looked almost like relief, terrible and clean. "I used to think I was the coward. All these years, I thought the shame was mine — that I'd found the letters and said nothing, that I'd chosen her name over the truth. And I did. I'm not taking that back. But sitting here, hearing it again — she chose it too. She chose it every single day for forty years. And she was the one who'd done it." He breathed out. "I don't know if that makes it better or worse."

"Both," Odile said. "It makes it both."

* * *

They stayed in the study until the blue hour gave itself up to dark. At some point Odile got up and turned on the lamp, and the small yellow light made the room ordinary again, made it a room where two middle-aged siblings sat among their mother's papers with red eyes and damp sleeves, and there was something almost funny in it, almost warm.

"I used to think you were just like her," Anders said. "Cold. Careful. Everything in its place."

"I am just like her."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "She never would have shown me the letters. She never would have opened the drawer. Whatever else you are — whatever we are — you did the thing she couldn't do. You turned the key."

Odile looked at the bundle on the desk, at the green string, at the small upright handwriting on the topmost envelope. *V.* Thirty years her brother had kept this. Thirty years of Sundays and birthdays and Christmases, passing the salt across the table from their mother, saying nothing. She should be furious with him. Part of her was, and would be for a long time. But underneath the fury was the other thing, the thing that had been growing in her since the graveside, since Tomas and the rain and the name nobody used: the understanding that silence is not one silence but many, that everyone in this story had stood at their own fence unable to open the latch, and that the only way out was the one her mother had never taken — through, and out loud, and together.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I haven't told you. Tomas has a sister. Lene. She came to the house on Tuesday."

Anders went still.

"She's given me a week. Truth, publicly, or they take the story to the press — the story as they know it, which is wrong in the details and right in the shape, which is almost worse. And there's a document, Anders. In the wardrobe. I haven't opened the back panel yet, but I know it's there, I can feel it, the way you felt that drawer when you were nineteen." She met his eyes. "I'm going to open it. Tomorrow. And when I do, I'd like you to be in the house."

Anders looked at the drawer, then at his sister, and something in his face settled — not peace, nothing like peace, but the particular steadiness of a man who has finally stopped carrying a thing alone.

"Then I'll stay," he said. "I'll sleep here tonight. In my old room." He almost smiled. "If the bed's still there."

"It's still there. It's in the inventory. Page three."

And it was only after he left the study, after she heard his feet on the stairs, the familiar creak of the third step, that Odile let herself look at the wardrobe at the end of the corridor — dark now, patient, its false back holding whatever her mother had written and never sent — and understood that tomorrow she would not be opening it alone.
