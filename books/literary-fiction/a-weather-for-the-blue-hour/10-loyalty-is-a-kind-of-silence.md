## 10. Loyalty Is a Kind of Silence

The cheque came in an envelope with no letter, only the company crest embossed in the corner and Maren's name typed on a label that had been pasted slightly crooked. Four months' salary. She stood in the payroll office doorway holding it for a long time before Mr. Zhou's secretary — a small grey woman named Fru Vik who had worked at Kestrel since before the fog and would work there until it closed — said, "You're meant to sign for it, dear."

Maren signed. Her hand did it without her.

That was the thing she would remember later, in the years when remembering was all she had: how much of it happened without her. The signature. The smile she gave Fru Vik. The walk back down the corridor past the notice board where the safety posters had been replaced overnight with new ones about *teamwork* and *shared prosperity*, as if prosperity were something you could hang between pins.

She was twenty-four years old. She had been promoted to assistant records supervisor, which meant a desk by the window on the second floor, which meant she could see the harbour from where she sat, and every morning the boats went out and every evening they came back, and she counted them sometimes without meaning to. Eleven boats. Twelve. On bad days ten. The fog had taken the herring runs east of the point and everyone knew it and no one said it, because saying it would mean saying why, and saying why would mean saying what she had seen in the disposal logs the week the white fog rolled off the cooling ponds and lay over the low streets like a tide that had forgotten to go out.

Her new office smelled of toner and cold coffee. Her old desk in the clerk's pool had smelled of salt off the coats of men coming in from the pier. She told herself this was progress. Everyone told her it was progress. Her mother wept with pride at Sunday dinner and asked twice whether the pay was really what people said, and Maren said yes, and it was, and that was somehow the worst part — that the number was real, that the lie had a market rate, and the rate was generous.

*You're protecting them,* she told herself, the way you tell yourself anything often enough until it stands up on its own like a scarecrow. *Half this town eats because the plant runs. You saw what happens when they start asking questions. Inquiries. Closures. Halvard dies either way — but one way it dies fed.*

It was almost true. That was the craftsmanship of it. Mr. Zhou — the elder, Arne, though everyone called him Mr. Zhou behind his back and sir to his face — had not threatened her. He had done something far more effective. He had been kind. He had come to the little interview room himself, not a lawyer, not security, just a broad soft-spoken man with reading glasses pushed up into white hair, and he had sat across from her with his hands folded and said, "Maren. You're Signe Vassvik's girl, aren't you. Your mother taught my daughter piano for two years." A pause, warm as a stove. "I have daughters too. I know what it is to want to keep them safe."

He had never once said *be quiet*. He had talked about jobs, about the inquiry being "an emotional process," about how a town survives on trust and how trust is a fragile thing, and he had looked at her with such gentle, patient sorrow that she had understood, in the wordless way you understand weather, that if she signed the statement already prepared on the table between them, she would be doing a good thing. And if she didn't —

He hadn't finished the thought. He hadn't needed to. The unfinished thought filled the room like the fog had filled the streets.

She signed. Nine days later the inquiry closed. *No evidence of irregular discharge practices attributable to operational negligence.* The words had a smoothness to them, a lacquered quality, and she learned something in those nine days about language that she would spend the rest of her life trying to unlearn: that English could be made to do this. That it could be polished into a surface nothing stuck to. Vessic couldn't. Vessic was too old and too wet and too close to the bone; in Vessic a lie came out of your mouth tasting like a lie, and everyone at the table tasted it. That was why they'd written the statement in English. That was why everything important was written in English now.

She kept the logs. Not copies — she wasn't brave enough for copies, and anyway the numbers were burned into her the way a hymn is burned into you, irretrievable, singable at three in the morning against her will. Barrel counts that didn't match intake. Disposal routes that ended at a quarry with no permit. A week of readings from the east ponds, torn out, the binding re-stitched with new thread so carefully she had to admire it even as her stomach turned over. Someone had done that stitching by hand, unhurried, in daylight, in this building, while children in Neshavn coughed blood-flecked sputum into pillowcases their mothers boiled every night.

And she filed papers. And she drank her coffee. And she counted the boats.

* * *

Signe knew before Christmas.

Of course she did. Signe had known her since they were six years old, since the winter the two of them had dug a hole in the snowbank behind the fish sheds and declared it a nation, population two, official language Vessic, and had spent an entire summer inventing laws for it. Signe read her the way she read weather off the water — no instruments, no charts, just a look at the surface and a narrowing of the eyes.

They were walking home along the breakwater in the blue hour, the hour Maren loved best, when the sky went the colour of a bruise healing and the first lamps came on along the quay, and Signe said, without turning her head:

"Say something."

"What?"

"In our language. Say anything. It's been weeks."

"I talk to you all the time."

"In English, you do." Signe stopped walking. The wind came off the water and pushed her hair sideways and she let it. "You used to swear at the gulls in Vessic. You used to tell me your dreams in Vessic, half asleep, before you even knew you were awake. Now I say something to you in it and you answer in English like a tourist. Like one of the summer people."

"It's just — work. I'm tired. You get out of the habit—"

"You don't get out of this habit." Signe's voice was quiet, which was worse than loud. Signe loud was a squall; it passed. Signe quiet was ice forming. "This is the language your grandmother sang the netsong in. This is the language we buried my father in. You don't get out of it like a coat."

Maren looked at the water. There was a boat coming in, the *Elsbeth*, Odd Halvardsen at the tiller, running dark and slow the way they all ran now, and she watched it and said nothing, and heard herself say nothing, and understood that her silence had become fluent. That she had practiced it so many hours a day at her window-desk that it had spread, the way damp spreads through a wall, into every room of her.

"Something's happened to you," Signe said. "Since the fog. Since the inquiry. You flinch when the plant comes up. You changed the subject at Petra's birthday so fast I got whiplash. And now you won't speak to me in the only language that's ours." She stepped closer. Out on the water the *Elsbeth*'s lamp swung as the boat turned in. "Maren. What did you see?"

And here was the terrible thing, the thing she turned over at night like a stone looking for the cool side: she wanted to tell her. It rose in her throat like a tide — the barrel counts, the quarry, the stitched binding, the gentle folded hands of Arne Zhou — and she opened her mouth, and what came out was:

"Nothing. It's nothing. Work is just work."

In English. Smooth as lacquer. Nothing stuck to it.

Signe looked at her for a long moment, and then she nodded slowly, once, the way you nod at a diagnosis, and walked on, and Maren followed, and neither of them spoke again the whole length of the breakwater, in any language at all.

* * *

The money changed things in ways she hadn't predicted. She had expected guilt to be a single wave that broke over her and receded. Instead it settled in like a lodger. It took the chair by the window. It ate first at meals.

The raise helped — that was the obscene part. Her mother got new glasses and stopped wincing at the price of flour. She bought Signe a proper wool coat for Christmas, navy, expensive, and Signe held it across her knees at the café table for a long time before she said, "Is this a gift or a payment?" and Maren laughed and said a gift, obviously, don't be dramatic, and Signe put it on and wore it and thanked her, and never once mentioned it again, and Maren understood that she had been forgiven nothing and lent something instead, and that the debt was accruing interest in a currency she couldn't see.

At the plant, they liked her. That was the strangest punishment of all. She was good at the work — precise, quick, discreet — and discretion, she learned, was the whole curriculum. Fru Vik brought her cinnamon buns on Fridays. The men in shipping called her *the professor* affectionately because she caught their errors before the manifests went out. Once, in February, Arne Zhou passed her in the corridor and stopped and shook her hand and said, "Good people make hard times bearable, Maren. This company is built on good people," and she smiled her new smile — she had a new smile now, she'd noticed it in shop windows, smaller, more administrative — and said thank you, sir, and walked on with his warmth sitting in her palm like something dead she was carrying home to bury.

She tried to make it true. That was the discipline of it, the daily labour no one ever saw. She made lists in her head at night, lying awake under the eaves of her mother's house: *the workers are fed. My mother has glasses. The inquiry would have closed the plant and then eleven boats becomes zero boats and Neshavn empties inside two winters, that's arithmetic, that's mercy with hard edges.* Some nights the list held. Some nights it dissolved the moment she touched it, and she lay there hearing the stitched binding, seeing the thread, neat and patient, and thinking: *someone sat in daylight and sewed a book shut, and I file papers forty feet away, and the boats still go out.*

In March the east ponds were drained and refilled, quietly, over a long weekend, and a memo went around thanking staff for their flexibility during "scheduled maintenance." She read the memo four times. Then she filed it. Her hands did it without her. She was beginning to suspect that her hands belonged to the company now, or to some version of herself that had been promoted along with the desk, a Maren Ostrand who was punctual and pleasant and knew how to sign things, while the other Maren — the one who had dug a nation in a snowbank, population two — stood somewhere behind glass, mouthing words no one could hear.

* * *

April. The blue hour again, earlier now, the light stretching longer, and Signe waiting for her outside the plant gate, which she never did, which drew looks, which was the point. Signe wanted the looks. Signe had decided, apparently, that shame was a tool and she was done being polite with it.

They walked up past the church without speaking. At the top of the hill, where the whole town lay below them — the roofs, the masts, the plant with its steam standing up straight in the still air like a pillar of smoke by day — Signe finally turned to her.

"My cousin in Neshavn," she said. "Little Torbjørn, you remember him, he used to follow us with his wooden boat. His lungs are scarred. The doctor says he'll have them his whole life. He's nine." A pause. The steam pillar rose. "His mother asked me yesterday if I knew anything. Because I work at the cannery and I know everyone, and she thought maybe I'd heard something true, because everything official is in English and everything official says nothing." Signe's eyes were dry and very bright. "And I said no. I said I didn't know anything. Do you understand what I did tonight, Maren? I lied to a woman whose son can't run to the gate without stopping to breathe. I lied in our language, and it tasted exactly like you said it would taste, and I did it because I trusted that whatever you're carrying, you're carrying it for a reason. For a good reason. So tell me the reason. Tell me and I'll carry it with you. Two can carry what one can't."

The tide rose in her throat again. The whole truth, entire, in the old language, on a hilltop in the blue hour — it would take four minutes. She timed it afterward, in imagination, over and over: four minutes, maybe five, and then she wouldn't be alone inside the glass anymore, and Signe would hate her or hold her, but either way the words would be out in the air where words could breathe.

Instead she heard herself say: "There's nothing to tell. The inquiry found nothing because there was nothing to find."

And Signe stepped back as if struck — actually stepped back, one heel grinding on the gravel — and looked at her for a long, long moment, and Maren watched something happen in her friend's face that she would see again in dreams for thirty-five years: not anger. Grief. The specific grief of watching someone choose, in front of you, in real time, to become smaller.

"All right," Signe said at last. Quietly. Ice, fully formed. "Then there's nothing to tell."

She walked down the hill toward the town. Maren stayed at the top. Below her the lamps were coming on along the quay, one, then another, then another, the blue hour deepening toward dark, and the plant's steam stood straight up into the last of the light, and out on the water the boats were coming home, and she counted them without meaning to.

Eleven. Twelve.

She stood there until she was cold through, telling herself the story one more time — *protecting them, protecting the jobs, protecting the town* — and for the first time the story didn't stand up on its own. It leaned. It swayed. Somewhere inside it a seam had split, and through the seam she could see the other story, the true one, waiting with its hat in its hands.

That night she took the cassette recorder her mother had won at the co-op raffle, and a fresh ninety-minute tape, and set it on her kitchen table beside the salt fish and the bread, and wrote on the cassette's paper label a single word in careful letters, and looked at it for a long time before she switched off the lamp.

The word was *Signe*.
