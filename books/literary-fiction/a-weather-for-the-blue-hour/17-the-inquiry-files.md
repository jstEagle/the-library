## 17. The Inquiry Files

The Halvard municipal record office occupied a flat-roofed building between the shuttered fire hall and the sea, and it was, by the look of it, the least loved structure in a town that had already stopped loving its structures. Salt blooms climbed the brick. A plywood sheet covered one window where a storm window used to be. The notice taped to the door was newer than everything else on it: *BY APPOINTMENT ONLY. ACCESS LIMITED. ALL MATERIALS SUBJECT TO SCHEDULED DISPOSAL.*

Quentin had read that last phrase four times on the walk over, and each time it landed a little heavier. *Subject to scheduled disposal.* Not archived. Not transferred. Disposed of, the way you dispose of a solvent drum or a diseased animal. Somewhere in a planning office, a date had been assigned to the contents of this building the way a date had been assigned to the town itself, and the date was coming.

He pressed the buzzer. Somewhere inside, a bell with the tone of a dying doorbell made its argument.

The man who answered was seventy if he was a day, cardigan under a municipal lanyard, glasses pushed up into white hair. His name tag said *H. VOLLAN, RECORDS CLERK (ACTING)*, and the parenthetical carried the weight of a career: the actual records clerk had presumably taken the buyout like everyone else, and Vollan had been acting ever since, acting alone, in a building full of paper no one wanted.

"Mr. Zhou," Vollan said, and Quentin watched the name go through him — not recognition, exactly, not yet, but a flicker, the way a fisherman's face flickers at a familiar tide line. "You called about the family history."

"I did. Thank you for fitting me in." The smile, the practiced one, the one that ended conversations — he deployed it and then, deliberately, dialed it down to something humbler. A family historian didn't need charm. A family historian needed patience and a pencil.

"Most people are trying to get *out* of this town's paperwork," Vollan said, holding the door. "You're the first appointment I've had since March who wasn't a demolition surveyor."

"I'm told my family had some history here."

"Your family had some *something* here." Vollan said it mildly, already turning, and Quentin couldn't tell if it was an old man's bluntness or a trap being baited. He decided it was probably just an old man's bluntness. Halvard seemed to have run out of energy for traps.

Inside, the reading room was a single table under a fluorescent panel that buzzed at the frequency of a trapped insect. The shelves along the walls were metal, municipal, gray, and labeled in a hand that had clearly once cared: *COUNCIL MINUTES 1961–74*, *HARBOUR BOARD*, *SCHOOL DISTRICT*, and then a run of boxes with no labels at all, which Quentin understood, with a small cold feeling, to be the important ones. The uncatalogued things. The things that would go into a dumpster without ever having been missed.

"Rules," Vollan said, laying them out with the relish of a man who had not been able to enforce rules on anyone in years. "Pencils only. Nothing leaves the table. You can photograph documents but not with flash. I stay in the room. And—" he paused, adjusting his glasses, "—if you find anything that mentions the Kestrel works, I'd ask you to be careful with it. Those pages are old and they've been handled angry."

"Handled angry?"

Vollan looked at him over the glasses. "People come looking. They always have. Some of them want to burn it, some of them want to publish it. Neither kind handles paper gently." He pulled a chair out for Quentin with a courtesy that contradicted everything else about him. "So. The family name?"

"Zhou," Quentin said. "My grandfather was Wen Zhou. My father grew up here before they moved south. I'm trying to trace the years in Halvard — the plant years, mostly. For a family history." The lie sat in his mouth with a strange taste, because it was almost true. He *was* trying to trace the plant years. He simply hadn't told himself yet what he'd do with what he found.

"Wen Zhou." Vollan said the name the way you'd handle a shell you suspected was live. "He'd have been the one who testified."

Quentin kept his face still. It was one of the few skills his mother had given him, though she'd never known she was teaching it: the ability to receive information that detonates and appear to be merely listening. "Testified?"

"1988." Vollan was already moving toward the unlabeled boxes, and his voice had gone into a register Quentin recognized from archivists and priests — the flat, protective tone of a man about to handle something sacred or radioactive. "There was an inquiry. After the fog. You'd have been — what, not born. Your father would have been a boy." He set a box on the table and looked at Quentin for a long moment. "You didn't know there was an inquiry."

"I knew there was a plant," Quentin said. "I knew it closed. That's more than my father ever told me, and I only got that from a shipping ledger I found last month."

Vollan nodded slowly, and something in his face softened — not trust, Quentin thought, but the particular pity of a man watching someone approach a thing he himself had looked at too long. "Then I'll save you some digging. It's all in here anyway. Inquiry file, 1988. Opened the fourteenth of March, closed the twenty-second." He let the dates sit. "Nine days, Mr. Zhou. They opened it and closed it in nine days. I've been the acting records clerk of this town for eleven years, and I have read that file more times than I can count, and every time I get to the last page I feel like I've been handed a receipt for something I paid for and never received."

He opened the box.

* * *

The file was thin. That was the first obscenity. Quentin had expected — he didn't know what he'd expected. Cartons. A paper trail commensurate with a disaster that had killed livestock along four miles of coastline and put eleven children in the regional hospital with lungs the doctors had described, in the one newspaper clipping Signe had shown him, as "chemically scoured." What he got instead was a folder barely an inch thick, its cover stamped *KESTREL CHEMICAL WORKS — INCIDENT INQUIRY — CLOSED*, and beneath the stamp, in the same tidy bureaucratic hand, the dates. March 14. March 22. Nine days. A human gestation is two hundred and sixty-six. A corporation's was nine.

"Take your time," Vollan said, settling into his chair with a thermos. "I mean that. Nobody's ever taken their time with it."

Quentin opened the cover.

The first documents were procedural: the order establishing the inquiry, signed by a regional administrator whose name meant nothing; a list of persons summoned; a schedule of hearings that read like a joke told in deadpan — two days of testimony, one day of site inspection, one day for "technical submissions," and then a week of deliberation compressed, apparently, into a single afternoon. He photographed each page with his phone, no flash, hands steadier than he felt. The camera shutter sound was off; he'd turned it off in the café that morning, a small premeditation he chose not to examine.

The site inspection report was four paragraphs. Four paragraphs for a chemical works that had, according to everything the town remembered, operated a disposal program for two decades. The inspector had visited on a clear day in late March, eleven months after the fog, and had found the facility "in material compliance with applicable discharge standards." There was a photograph attached — grainy, photocopied twice — of a man in a hard hat gesturing at a treatment tank. The tank was clean. The tank, Quentin thought, would have been clean. He found himself thinking of Signe's kitchen, of the salt fish and the bread, of her voice when she'd said the founding family's name — *Zhou* — and watched him the way you watch a fuse.

Then he reached the testimony.

His grandfather's statement ran eleven pages. Quentin had seen photographs of Wen Zhou — a severe, handsome man in the few surviving family pictures, always slightly apart from the group, always in a better coat than everyone else — but he had never heard his grandfather's voice; the man had died before Quentin was four. Reading the testimony was the closest he would ever come, and it was a strange intimacy, because the voice that emerged from the typewritten lines was fluent, assured, and utterly hollow. Wen Zhou answered questions that had been asked to be answered. He described the plant's disposal practices in terms so bland they achieved a kind of poetry: *materials were handled in accordance with established procedure.* *No irregularities were observed at any level of operations.* *The events of the night in question are best understood as an unusual confluence of meteorological conditions.*

"Unusual confluence of meteorological conditions," Quentin read aloud, because the room was empty except for Vollan and the words needed air. "They killed a coastline and called it weather."

"The chair of the inquiry was a man named Brakstad," Vollan said, not looking up from his thermos. "He went to work for Kestrel's parent company fourteen months later. Director of regulatory affairs. There's a photograph of him at a ribbon-cutting in the *Regional Herald*, if you ever want it. I clipped it." He nodded toward the bottom of the box. "I clip things. It's not much, but it's what a records clerk can do."

At the bottom of the box, beneath the inquiry file, was a manila folder thick with clippings, annotated in a careful pencil hand — Vollan's, Quentin assumed, though the hand seemed too fine, too patient, for a man who drank from a dented thermos. And beneath the clippings, loose, unattached to anything, was a single typed page with a handwritten signature line left blank.

Quentin almost skipped it. It looked like another form — a witness statement template, the kind of bureaucratic residue that accumulates in any file. But the template had been filled in. Someone had sat down, in 1987 or 1988, and answered every prompt in a hand so careful it looked typeset: even strokes, no slant, the letters of someone who had learned to write by copying and had never stopped being careful about it. The statement described the disposal logs. It gave dates — three dates in the autumn of 1987 — and figures, tonnage figures that did not match the official discharge reports, and it said, in a sentence that seemed to have been written very slowly and perhaps rewritten several times: *The records I was responsible for keeping were altered to reflect quantities substantially less than those actually received for disposal. I did not alter them. I was instructed to enter the lower figures and I did so, and I have regretted it every day since.*

The signature line was blank.

Below it, where the form asked for the witness's name and position, the careful hand had written: *A clerk of the works.* Nothing else.

Quentin sat very still. The fluorescent panel buzzed. Somewhere outside, a gull made its one argument with the world. He was aware of Vollan watching him, and aware, more distantly, of his own heartbeat doing something arrhythmic and stupid, because there was something in the handwriting — something in the *a*, in the way the descenders were disciplined like children, in the total absence of flourish — that he knew.

He knew it the way you know a voice on a tape.

He had spent three weeks in Signe's kitchen learning a language that had no alphabet, and in all that time the only writing he'd seen from the era of the fog was the newspaper clipping and the demolition notices. But he'd spent thirty-one years, before that, receiving letters. Birthday cards. Notes in lunchboxes through a boarding school's mail room, because his mother had considered calls insufficient and visits impossible and so had written — weekly, unfailingly, in a hand so careful it looked typeset. Even strokes. No slant. Descenders disciplined like children. A woman who had learned to write by copying — by copying, perhaps, a mother's hand, or a father's expectations — and had never once allowed herself a flourish.

*The records I was responsible for keeping were altered.*

His mother had never worked at the plant. He knew that. He knew the shape of her life the way you know a house you grew up in even after it's demolished — she'd gone to school in Halvard, left at eighteen for the university in the south, married his father, and never come back except for funerals. She'd been a girl in 1987. Sixteen, seventeen.

But a clerk of the works. A clerk who kept the logs, who was instructed to enter the lower figures, who did so and regretted it every day — and who wrote it all down, in her careful hand, for an inquiry that closed in nine days, and then withdrew it. Unsigned. Unfiled. Left loose in a box, or misfiled, or hidden in the one place in the municipality where paper went to be forgotten.

"Mr. Zhou." Vollan's voice was gentle. "You've gone the color of the wall."

Quentin realized he was holding the page and that his hand was not steady, and that steadying it would take everything he had, and that he did not want to spend everything he had on steadiness, not here, not with this. He set the page down on the table, face up, and looked at it.

"I need to ask you something," he said, "and I need you to answer me straight, because I think you've been waiting eleven years for someone to ask you straight."

Vollan took his glasses off. It seemed to be a formal gesture, a man preparing to see without the official instrument.

"Who filed this," Quentin said. "The unsigned statement. Was it ever submitted? Was it part of the record, or—"

"It was never submitted." Vollan said it immediately, without checking anything, and Quentin understood that the man knew this page the way Signe knew her three words — by heart, by weight, by long and solitary acquaintance. "It came in with the inquiry materials. It's referenced — there's a line in the deliberation summary, 'a further statement was received but could not be attributed and was given no weight.' That's all. No name. The chair didn't ask for one. Nine days, Mr. Zhou. You don't ask questions on day eight that you've spent seven days avoiding."

"Could it be traced? The paper, the typewriter, the—"

"I've had eleven years and no budget." Vollan's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "But I'll tell you what I can tell you, since you asked me straight. The statement came in by post. The postmark was Halvard. And the date—" he leaned forward and tapped the folder, not the page, careful even now, "—the date was the nineteenth of March, 1988. Day six of nine. Someone in this town wrote that statement while the inquiry was still sitting, mailed it to the inquiry, and then — nothing. No follow-up. No name ever offered. Whoever it was watched the door close from the inside and let it close."

Quentin looked down at the page. *I did not alter them. I was instructed to enter the lower figures and I did so.*

His mother had been seventeen in March of 1988.

But the hand. The hand was the hand. He had eleven of her letters in a box in his room at the boarding house, and he had read them so many times in the dark of the last month that he could have reproduced them from memory, and the *a* was the same, and the disciplined descenders were the same, and the terrible careful absence of any self at all in the strokes was the same.

Unless there had been two people in Halvard who wrote like that. Unless the careful hand was common, was regional, was nothing. Unless grief was making him see her face in every window, the way the old man in the café had looked at him — *you have a familiar face* — and found his mother in it.

He photographed the statement. Then he photographed it again, closer, the signature line, the words *a clerk of the works*. Then he sat back, and the chair creaked, and Vollan poured a second cup from the thermos and set it in front of him without being asked, and Quentin drank it. It was terrible coffee. It was the best thing he'd had in weeks.

"Can I get copies?" he asked. "Officially, I mean. Family history."

"Everything here is scheduled for disposal," Vollan said. "Officially, you can have whatever you can photograph before the bulldozers come, and I will stand in this room and watch you do it, and I will sign whatever certification you need saying you photographed it in my presence, in accordance with regulation, and I will do it gladly." He put his glasses back on, and the official instrument returned, but his eyes over it were the eyes of a man who had clipped a ribbon-cutting photo and kept it for thirty years. "Unofficially, Mr. Zhou — and you can take this or leave it — I've been waiting a long time for someone from your family to sit at this table. I'd begun to think no one ever would."

* * *

He walked back through the town with the copies — Vollan had a photocopier, ancient, wheezing, and had made them himself, unasked, sliding them into a manila folder stamped *DISPOSAL PENDING* with a kind of grim ceremony — held against his chest like a schoolbook. The light was going long and coppery off the water. The demolition numbers on the houses caught the sun: 14, 22, 31, each one a small orange verdict. He passed the café, where Petra was hauling in the chairs, and she lifted a hand to him, and he lifted his back, and the gesture was easy now, unperformed, and he noticed that it was easy and filed the noticing away for later, for a time when he could afford to feel things in sequence.

At the boarding house he went straight up to his room and took out the letters. Eleven of them, spanning his fourteenth to his seventeenth year, the years when his mother's weekly pages had been the only unguarded thing between them. He spread them on the bed under the window's failing light and set the photocopy of the witness statement in the middle, and compared.

The *a*. The same. The *y* with its disciplined tail. The same. The way she broke her words at line's end — never hyphenating, always leaving the syllable whole and starting fresh, a habit he'd once thought was hers alone. The statement broke its words the same way. *Substan-tially.* *Regret-ted.* He counted. Six breaks. Six identical breaks in habit.

It was her. It was her hand, her seventeen-year-old hand, writing to an inquiry that would not listen, describing the crime her father's plant had committed, and then — nothing. She had written the truth at seventeen, and mailed it, and then spent the rest of her life never signing it, never repeating it, never telling her son that Halvard existed except as a word on a shipping ledger. She had written *I have regretted it every day since* and then she had folded the regret away so completely that it had taken a cassette tape and a dead language and a dying town to find the seam.

Quentin sat on the edge of the bed for a long time. The blue hour came up the walls.

Then he gathered the letters, and the photocopies, and the phone with its photographs, and went downstairs, and out, and up the hill toward Signe's house, because there was a question he could no longer carry alone, and only one person in the world had known his mother at seventeen — only one person had sat across from her in a language with no alphabet, in a town with nine days of inquiry, and watched her decide.

The kitchen light was on. Through the window he could see Signe at her table, and across from her, laid out flat, an old photograph he hadn't seen before — two girls, arms linked, squinting into a sun that had not yet learned what was coming.

She looked up as his shadow crossed the glass, and he saw her read his face in one pass, the way she read everything, and he saw her understand — before he said a word, before he held up the folder stamped *DISPOSAL PENDING* — exactly what he had found, and whose hand it was in.

"You went to the archive," she said, opening the door before he knocked.

"I found the statement," Quentin said. "Signe — it's her handwriting. It's my *mother's* handwriting. She wrote it. She wrote the truth and she never signed it." His voice cracked on the last word, and he let it. "Why didn't you tell me she'd tried?"

Signe looked at him for a long moment, and the look was not surprise. It was the look of a woman setting down something she had carried for thirty-five years, and finding her hands still shaped to its weight.

"Come inside," she said. "There's a part of this you don't have yet. And Quentin—" she held his eye, and her voice dropped into Vessic for one word, one word he knew, the word for *listen*, "—you're not going to like the part where I come in."
