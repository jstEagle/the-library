## 4. The Conservatory Remembers Differently

Ruth Okafor had worked at the Halvard Conservatory for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one thing about institutions: they do not forget, and they do not remember. They file.

Memory was her job, technically. Every transcript, every disciplinary record, every recital program going back to the founding charter — it all passed through her office on the second floor of Ashworth Hall, through her hands, into the climate-controlled archive annex across the courtyard where paper went to be official forever. People assumed a registrar remembered students. She didn't, not really. She remembered *files*. A girl was not a girl; she was a folder with a color-coded tab, a GPA, a scholarship notation, an exit interview. When the folder left the building — withdrawn, expelled, graduated — the girl stopped existing in any way Ruth could be held accountable for.

This had once struck her as a kind of mercy. Lately, approaching retirement the way you approach a doctor's appointment you've been dreading for a year, it struck her as something else.

It was the janitor who started it. The new one, the quiet woman with the careful smile who cleaned the rehearsal wing on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Ruth had seen her mopping the corridor outside Practice Room 9 last week, and something about the set of her shoulders — the particular way she'd squared herself before the old Bösendorfer that nobody was allowed to touch anymore — had snagged in Ruth's mind like a splinter. She'd thought: *I know that posture.* And then she'd thought: *No you don't,* and gone back to her enrollment reports.

But the thought kept working at her, the way thoughts did when they were true.

* * *

The scandal — they still called it that, though officially there was no word for it at all; officially there was a gap in the records like a pulled tooth — had happened twenty-two years ago, in the spring. Ruth had been fifteen years into her career then, young enough to follow instructions without questioning them and old enough to know exactly what she was doing.

She remembered the weather first, the way she always remembered things. Rain for nine straight days, the courtyard flagstones dark and slick, students running between buildings with their instrument cases held over their heads like shields. And in the middle of that rain, a girl named Mara Vane walked out of a disciplinary hearing on the third floor of Ashworth Hall, came down past the registrar's window, and stood for a moment in the corridor below Ruth's office, just standing, her back to the wall, while the whole building hummed around her with the particular frequency of a secret being managed.

Ruth had watched her from above. Seventeen years old. Hands hanging at her sides like they belonged to someone else. Then she'd pushed off the wall, straightened, and walked out into the rain without hurrying, and Ruth had thought at the time: *that child has been taught how to leave a room.*

She hadn't known yet what the hearing had decided. She knew by Friday.

Here is what the administration's version looked like, the version Ruth helped assemble, page by page, because assembling versions was her function: A student at the Halvard Conservatory, following allegations of academic misconduct and inappropriate conduct during examinations, has voluntarily withdrawn. The matter is considered closed. The family has requested privacy. No further comment.

Every clause of it had been drafted upstairs, in Director Merrick's office, or rather by the people who drafted things for Director Merrick, and Ruth had typed the exit paperwork, and stamped the withdrawal effective date, and pulled the Vane file from the active drawer and moved it to the restricted cabinet, and that was that. Three days of work. The tidiness of it had impressed her even then — how fast an institution could close over a person like water closing over a stone. By the following semester, when a trustee asked casually why the founding charter no longer listed a Vane among the original signatories' descendants, Ruth had already prepared the answer: *the family requested removal of their name from public materials.* Which was true, in the sense that a request had been made, in the sense that everything in those weeks was technically true the way a skeleton is technically a body.

What Ruth had never put into any file was the rest of it.

She remembered Elias Thorn coming to the office — this was later, months later, autumn — a cellist, gaunt, with the ruined-hands look of a man who played too much and slept too little. He had wanted to see the complaint he and Edmund Vane had filed. He'd filed it himself; he knew it existed; he had the carbon copy in his coat pocket. But the conservatory's copy, he said, seemed to have gone missing, and the board secretary told him no such document had ever been received, and he wanted to check with the registrar directly, woman to institution, whether paper could simply vanish inside these walls.

Ruth had checked. She'd known where it was. It was in the restricted cabinet, in a folder marked with a code she'd been instructed to use, and she had looked at Thorn's carbon copy and at her own memory of the original and said, "I'm afraid I have no record of that filing," and watched a man understand, in real time, that the building itself was lying to him.

He hadn't raised his voice. That was the part that stayed. He'd thanked her. He'd folded his carbon copy back into his coat with great care, the way you'd handle a letter from someone dead, and he'd said, "You'll want to remember that you said that," and left, and Ruth had sat at her desk for a long time listening to the radiator tick.

She had remembered. That was the problem. Institutions didn't remember, but she did, helplessly, in the body, the way pianists remembered fingering.

* * *

There were other pieces. There always were, once you let yourself count them.

The teacher. Ilsa Brandt, whose studio had produced more competition winners than any other in the conservatory's history, whose name was on the marquee every gala season. In the spring of the scandal, Brandt had taken a sudden sabbatical — health, the memo said — and returned in the fall thinner and quieter, and her student roster had been quietly redistributed, and no one had explained anything because no explanation had been requested. Ruth had processed the sabbatical paperwork herself. She had also processed, two years earlier, a different piece of paper: a brief incident memorandum concerning an off-campus masterclass, signed by a chaperone, routed to the director's office, and never seen again. She had been twenty-nine and had assumed it was being handled. Handling was what the offices upstairs did. It was practically their job description.

And there was Edmund Vane. The father. Ruth had liked him, in the limited way registrars liked anyone — he was punctual, courteous, always exact with forms. He'd taught theory in the prep division, a gentle man with ink-stained fingers and a habit of addressing Ruth by name, which almost no one did. After the withdrawal, after the name came off the charter, he had come back exactly once, to settle a library fine of four dollars and sixty cents, and he'd paid in cash and counted it out carefully and said, "Thank you, Miss Okafor. I know you're only the hands. I don't blame the hands."

She had thought about that sentence for twenty-two years. *I don't blame the hands.* At the time it had felt like absolution. Now it felt like an indictment with better manners.

Because here was the thing Ruth had come to understand late, in the long amber light of a career's final stretch: the Vanes had not been made villains by accident, or even by malice, exactly. They had been made villains by *process*. Someone upstairs — she had never let herself think the name, but the name was Aldous Merrick, it had always been Aldous Merrick — someone had understood that the fastest way to bury a complaint was to bury the complainants, and that an institution's memory was not a passive thing but an instrument, playable, and that Ruth Okafor's office was where the keys were kept.

The story had been tidied. That was the phrase she used privately, in the privacy of her own skull, where no minutes were kept. Tidied, like a room before company. The complaint vanished. The name vanished. The teacher's roster was redistributed. The girl became a rumor, then a cautionary tale, then nothing — and Ruth knew how thoroughly nothing, because just last year a doctoral candidate researching the conservatory's history of women pedagogues had asked her, brightly, whether the Halvard archives held anything on "that prodigy from the nineties, the one who imploded? Vane? Or was it Vaughn?" And Ruth had said, "I'd have to check," and hadn't checked, and the candidate had written around the absence, the way everyone wrote around the absence, the gap in the tooth, the weather where a person used to be.

* * *

On Thursday morning, Ruth arrived early, as she always did now, to enjoy the building empty. This was her private ritual, her last one: thirty minutes in the corridors before the conservatory woke up, when the light came low through the east windows and the practice rooms were silent as held breath. She loved the building. That was the trap of it, the thing she couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't given three decades to an institution — that complicity and love could live in the same chest, share the same blood supply, and neither one killed the other. She had spent her life keeping this place's memory, and its memory was a crime scene, and she had dusted it daily and called the dusting devotion.

She was halfway down the rehearsal wing when she saw the janitor again.

The woman was wringing out a mop at the utility sink, methodical, unhurried, and the early light caught her face in profile, and Ruth stopped walking. Not because of the posture this time. Because of the profile itself — the line of the jaw, the set of the mouth — which she had last seen from above, through a registrar's window, on a girl standing in a rainy corridor deciding how to leave a room.

Twenty-two years. Older, harder, wearing a staff polo with a name tag that said M. COLE. But Ruth had spent her life recognizing people from their files, from fragments, from the shape of a record rather than the substance of a face. Recognition was her trade.

The woman looked up. Their eyes met. And Ruth watched her do it — watched her produce the smile, warm and modest and perfectly calibrated, the smile of a person who had practiced being unremarkable until she could perform it in her sleep — and the smile was so good, so professionally good, that it confirmed everything.

"Morning," the woman said pleasantly.

"Good morning," Ruth said.

Neither of them moved. The mop dripped. Somewhere in the walls, the heating system knocked twice and settled, the old building clearing its throat. Ruth understood that she was standing at a door she had kept closed for twenty-two years, and that the door had just swung open on its own, and that whatever she did in the next five seconds would go into no file anywhere except the one she would die holding.

"You work Tuesdays and Thursdays," Ruth said. It came out as small talk. It wasn't.

"That's right." The smile held. The eyes didn't quite. There was something behind them, watchful and tired and very young under the age, assessing Ruth the way you assess a surface before you decide whether it will bear weight.

"I've been the registrar here a long time," Ruth said. "I keep the records. All of them. Even the ones people think are gone."

Something shifted in the woman's face — barely, a millimeter, a tuning peg turned a quarter-turn — and then smoothed over again, and she said, "That must be interesting work," and wrung the mop, and turned back to her bucket, conversation ended, door politely closed from the other side.

Ruth walked on toward her office. Her hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed her.

At her desk she did something she had not done in thirty-one years of scrupulous procedure. She opened the restricted cabinet — her key, her sole responsibility, the trust of four directors sitting in her palm like a coin worn smooth — and she took out the folder coded in a language only she and two dead men had ever understood, and she carried it to the copier, and she copied it. All of it. The missing complaint. The incident memorandum. The withdrawal paperwork with its too-tidy clauses. She fed each page through with the calm of a woman defusing a bomb she had built herself, and when she finished she locked the originals away and slid the copies into a manila envelope, and the envelope she did not file anywhere at all. She put it in her handbag, under her reading glasses, against her heart, which was hammering like a student's before juries.

Then she sat down and opened her email, and the first message at the top of the queue was from the director's office, cc'd to facilities, subject line: **Annex Demolition — Board Vote — Inventory Certification Required.**

Three weeks. The archive annex — the vault, the sealed records, the original ledger she had heard whispered about for two decades and never once been permitted to see — scheduled to come down, and Ruth Okafor's signature required certifying that everything inside had been properly inventoried and disposed of.

She read the message twice. She thought of a gaunt man folding a carbon copy into his coat. She thought of a father counting out four dollars and sixty cents. She thought: *I am the hands.*

Ruth Okafor picked up her pen, and for the first time in thirty years, she began drafting a refusal.
