## 11. Love Against Duty

Tomas called at seven in the morning, which was how I knew it was serious. My brother did not believe in mornings as a habitat; he believed in them the way a soldier believes in a border crossing — something you get through, efficiently, on your way to somewhere defensible.

"Mara." Not Renata. He'd stopped using my assumed name the moment he'd learned of it, the way you'd stop calling someone by a nickname after their diagnosis. "We need to talk. Not on the phone."

"I'm working."

"At six-thirty tomorrow, then. Before your shift. The diner on Calloway. I'll be there at six-fifteen and I'll order for both of us because you'll be late."

I wasn't late. He was already in a corner booth with two coffees going cold in front of him, which meant he'd been early, which meant he was frightened. Tomas Vane — Tomas Reyes now, on his letterhead, his mortgage, his bar association card — was a man who arrived early only to funerals and negotiations, and he had decided this was both.

He looked good. That was the first thing I noticed and resented. Twenty years since the conservatory had chewed up our family and spat us into different cities, and my brother had come out the other side polished: gray at the temples like it had been applied by a stylist, a coat that cost more than my year of rent, hands quiet on the table because he'd trained them to be quiet. A lawyer's hands. A man whose body had been taught that stillness reads as authority.

"You look tired," he said.

"You look expensive."

The waitress came and he ordered eggs he wouldn't eat. When she left, he leaned forward and lowered his voice, though the diner was nearly empty, though no one in it would know either of our names.

"Cade came to see me."

The words landed in my chest like a dropped key. "Cade. Merrick's Cade."

"He's not Merrick's anything, formally. He's counsel to the board's governance committee. Which is why he came to me and not to some hired fixer. He wanted it done properly." Tomas turned his cup a quarter-turn, a tic from childhood, from the days when we sat at our father's kitchen table while Dad explained fingering and Tomas explained why he'd never practice. "They know who you are, Mara. They've known since spring. They let you mop their floors because they were waiting to find out what you wanted."

"What I wanted," I said, "was to be left alone."

"And now?"

* * *

Here is what I had not told him. Here is what I had told almost no one.

That Elias Thorn had put a brass key in my hand at our father's funeral and said the word *Vane* out loud like setting down something heavy he'd carried too far. That the storage unit off Delmore Road held twenty years of my father's nights — teaching journals interleaved with photocopied ledger pages, Aldous Merrick's initials marching down the margins like ants. That my father, the gentle man who taught me scales with a metronome and never raised his voice, had spent two decades quietly assembling a case against the institution that destroyed us, and had died for it, probably, or at least partly, and the police report said *slippery steps, cardiac event, unfortunate*.

That last week a man named Dev, who cleaned the rehearsal wing beside me and used to be a law clerk, had looked at the photocopied pages over gas-station coffee and said, "These are copies. Copies are opinions. You need the original or you need authentication, and either way you need a lawyer who isn't scared."

And here is what Tomas knew, because Cade had told him, because Cade wanted him to tell me:

The board would restore our father's pension, posthumously cleared of any implication in the endowment discrepancies. They would expunge whatever remained of the record from twenty years ago — the findings, the letters, the quiet blacklisting that had followed me through three cities and two name changes. They would do all of this discreetly, generously, with the warm machinery of an institution that had never done anything wrong except trust people too much.

In exchange: the journal pages. All of them. Signed receipt, binding confidentiality, the whole apparatus of silence dressed in silk.

"They'll also consider," Tomas said, and here his voice did something careful, "a visiting lectureship. Non-performance track. Pedagogy. Your name would have to come back, obviously — Vane, restored, on the faculty page. Dad's name next to it in the emeritus roll. The charter thing. What Thorn told you about the founding families — they'd acknowledge it. A plaque, even."

"A plaque."

"Mara—"

"My father is dead because of these people, and they're offering me a plaque."

"We don't know that he's dead because of them." He said it fast, and I watched him hear himself say it, and I watched him flinch. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, that came out wrong. What I mean is we can't prove it, and the medical examiner didn't flag it, and if we go to war with the Halvard Conservatory with photocopies and a dead man's journals, we will lose, Mara. We will lose the way we lost before. Except this time I'm forty-six years old and I have a wife and two kids and a practice I built from nothing, and you—" he gestured at me, at the diner, at the whole gray morning, "—you have nothing left to lose, which is exactly why they're afraid of you, and exactly why you should take the deal before they decide fear isn't enough."

* * *

I made myself drink the coffee. It had gone bitter and I drank it anyway, because my hands wanted something to do besides reach across the table and shake him.

"You rebuilt your life on it," I said. "On the silence."

"Don't."

"Think about it. Think about what actually happened, Tomas. Dad lost his position. We lost the apartment, the school, the name. You changed yours so fast I barely got to say goodbye to it. And every year after that, when anyone asked about the family, you gave them the tidy version. The respectable version. And it worked. Look at you. It worked so well." I kept my voice low and even, the janitor's voice, the voice that ends conversations. "You're not begging me to take the deal because you're afraid for me. You're begging me because if I burn the conservatory down, someone's going to start asking how the Vane boy became Reyes so quickly, and who helped him, and what it cost."

His jaw tightened. For a second I saw our father in his face — the same long line from cheekbone to chin, the same way anger moved through him like weather rather than fire.

"You think I don't know that?" he said. "You think I sleep that well? I was fourteen, Mara. Fourteen years old when they took everything, and I learned in one winter that a name is a liability, and I have spent thirty years being careful, and yes — yes — I built a life on the silence, because the alternative was what happened to Dad, which was dying poor and obsessed in a storage unit full of paper nobody would ever read." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, I could see him hating it, the lawyer in him reaching for composure and missing. "I love him. I loved him. And I am telling you, as his son and as a lawyer and as the only person alive who remembers what we lost — those pages will not give you justice. They will give you a longer, crueler version of what already happened to us."

"And the pension," I said. "Dad's pension. Thirty years of him being treated like a criminal, and they'll fix it, quietly, if I hand over the only proof."

"Yes."

"That's not restitution, Tomas. That's a purchase."

"It's a life!" He said it louder than he meant to; the waitress glanced over. He dropped back down. "It's a life. You're forty-one years old and you clean toilets in the building where they broke you, and you smile at people with a face you practiced in a mirror, and you call yourself Renata, and you haven't played — have you? Have you played once? Not for me, not for anyone. Because playing is the name, and the name is the wound, and you've amputated both." He was leaning across the table now, eyes wet and furious. "They are offering to give you back your hands, Mara. Your actual hands. And you're going to say no because of principle? Principle doesn't keep you warm. Principle is what Dad had, and look where principle put him."

* * *

I let the silence sit. In the window behind him, the city went about its morning — buses, umbrellas, a woman running for a door that closed anyway.

"Dad didn't die of principle," I said finally. "Dad died of them. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, and the fact that you've spent thirty years pretending there isn't is the sacrifice you made. Not the name. The difference."

Tomas sat back. Something moved through his face — not agreement, but the place where agreement might someday live, if it survived the winter.

"Their offer expires Friday," he said quietly. "After that, Cade says, the board 'pursues other remedies.' That means they pin the endowment shortfall on Dad's estate. They have documents, Mara. Forged ones, probably, but real-looking. They'll make him the embezzler. Posthumously. They'll make our father the thief, and it will hold up, because institutions always win the paperwork war." He pulled an envelope from inside his coat and set it on the table between us. Heavy cream stock. The conservatory's seal embossed blind, no ink — even their stationery whispered. "Read it. Don't answer me. Read it, and then decide who you want to be for the rest of your life: the woman who got her name back, or the woman who was right."

He stood, put money on the table for eggs neither of us had touched, and paused with one hand on the booth's edge.

"For what it's worth," he said, not looking at me, "I hope you burn it down. I just hope you survive it. Those were never the same thing in our family."

Then he walked out into the ordinary weather, and I sat alone with the envelope, turning it over, the seal catching the light like a small closed eye.

Inside, I knew, was everything I'd lost, priced and itemized.

Outside, my brother's cab pulled away, and my phone buzzed once against the table. A text from Dev, four words:

*Found something. Tonight. Trust me.*
