## 10. Ruth Comes Down the Hill

I have driven down from Ashford to the coast perhaps forty times in my life, and I could do it blind, which on this particular morning felt like a genuine possibility because I had not slept. I had spent the night composing speeches. That is what I do instead of sleeping; I compose speeches the way other people count sheep, each one more eloquent than the last, each one destined to die unspoken in the light of day.

The speech for this morning went roughly as follows: *Lior Anne Kavanagh, you are fifty-one years old, your mother has been in the ground eleven days, and you have decamped to a condemned building on a cliff like some kind of literary hermit. You will get in this car. We will go home. We will eat something with butter in it and you will stop this nonsense.*

I had rehearsed it through three rounds of the M25 and most of the A-road, and I want it noted that when I pulled into the inn's gravel lot and saw the kitchen door standing open — *open*, propped with a brick, in a building slated for demolition, in November — every word of it evaporated like spit on a hot pan.

The inn looked worse than grief. Grief at least has a shape. The Bellamy — I still could not say the name without my sister's voice rising in my throat like bile, so let us call it the inn — sat on its headland with its paint gone the grey of old dishwater, its sign hanging by one chain, turning slowly in the wind like a man deciding whether to jump. Plywood over the downstairs windows. A council notice nailed to the door, already curling at the corners. Six weeks, the notice said. As if a building could be given six weeks the way a patient is given six months, as if anyone believed either number.

And there, in the doorway of the kitchen, stood my niece, wearing a cardigan I recognized as her mother's and an expression I recognized as her own: the look of a woman who has been caught doing exactly what she intended to do and sees no reason to apologize for it.

"Ruth," she said.

"Lior."

"You found me."

"It wasn't difficult. You're the only Kavanagh alive who would choose a condemned building over a hotel with heating." I got out of the car and my knees announced their opinions, as they do now, all the time, about everything. "Your phone goes to voicemail. Your flat's dark. Your editor called me, Lior. Your *editor*. Do you understand the indignity of that? I had to pretend to be a responsible adult with information."

"I'm fine."

"You're living in a derelict pub."

"I'm living in my childhood home."

"It is neither derelict nor a home, and it certainly isn't both." I came up the path, stepping around a gull feather the size of my hand. "Your mother just died. You should be with family. With people. In a house where the doors lock and the boiler works."

"This is where the family is," she said, and stepped aside to let me in, which was so like her — offering hospitality as a form of argument — that I nearly turned around and left on principle.

I did not leave. God help me, I did not leave. I stepped through that kitchen door and thirty-eight years fell on me like a dropped ceiling.

* * *

You have to understand what that kitchen was to me before you judge what happened in it.

I was nineteen when my sister married Tom Bellamy, and I spent a summer here as an unpaid kitchen drudge, peeling potatoes and listening to him sing off-key while he made stock. He was the only man I ever met who seemed genuinely happy, not performing happy, not managing happy, but *inhabiting* it, the way you inhabit a coat that fits. My sister loved him the way you love weather — constantly, and with complaints. And then Danny drowned, and Tom left, and my sister renamed herself and her children with the efficiency of a woman scrubbing a stain, and I helped her do it, and I have carried that help like a stone in my shoe for twenty-seven years.

The kitchen was smaller than memory insisted. Of course it was. Memory always inflates the rooms where we were young, gives them cathedral ceilings and golden light. This one had a sagging shelf, a rusted stove, a table with one leg shimmed by a folded beer mat I swear was the same beer mat from 1989. Dust lay over everything like a second, better-behaved family.

But someone had been cooking.

There was a saucepan on the stove, rinsed. Two bowls drying on the rack. A bag of onions, a knob of ginger going papery on the sill, a lemon cut and wrapped in film with the care of a surgical dressing. Someone had been making soup in this tomb. Someone had lit the old burner — I could smell the gas ghost of it — and fed themselves, here, in the mouth of the building that ate our whole family.

"Who's been here with you?" I asked, and heard my own voice go sharp, because sharpness is the only tool I've never misplaced.

Lior did one of her pauses. She gets them from her mother, these pauses, these little drawn curtains. "A friend."

"What friend?"

"A friend from the funeral."

"The woman who called us Bellamys." I said it flatly, and watched my niece flinch, and thought: *there it is.* "Yes. I saw your face at the graveside, Lior. You've been chewing on that word for two weeks like a loose tooth. So let's have it out. Who is she?"

"Why does everyone in this family treat a name like a crime scene?" She said it lightly, but her hands were busy — wiping the clean counter, straightening the film on the lemon. Her mother's hands. My mother's hands. We are a lineage of women who tidy when terrified.

"Because in our family," I said, "it usually is one."

The wind came off the sea and pushed at the plywood over the window, and somewhere upstairs a door moved on its hinges — Danny's room, I thought, and hated myself for thinking it, and thought it again anyway.

"Sit down, Ruth," Lior said. "I'll make tea. The kettle takes forever; the element's half dead."

"Everything in this building is half dead."

"Yes," she said, setting the kettle on. "It's why I feel at home."

* * *

We sat at the shimmed table with tea that tasted of iron and dust and, faintly, of being nine years old. She had brought cups from the flat — good ones, her good ones, the ones with the blue rim — and I understood that she had come here intending to stay long enough to need good cups, and my speech about hotels and boilers rose up in me again and died again, because what do you say to a woman who has packed her best crockery for a ruin?

"Your editor says you missed a deadline," I tried.

"My editor can wait."

"She says you've missed three."

"Ruth." She set her cup down with a small precise click. "Why don't you tell me why the name bothers you? Not why it bothered Mum. Why it bothers *you*. You flinched too, at the funeral. You flinched before Mum ever did."

"I did not flinch."

"You flinched like a woman touching a hot handle twice."

I looked into my tea. There is a particular cowardice available to people of my generation, which consists of believing that silence is protection rather than complicity. I had practiced it for decades. I was very good at it. And sitting in that kitchen, in the light coming grey through the boarded window, with my niece watching me with her mother's patience and her father's directness — because she has his directness, God knows where else she'd have gotten it — I felt the stone in my shoe finally work its way somewhere I couldn't ignore.

"Her name was Margaret," I said.

Lior went still. "Whose?"

"The woman Ada mentioned. At the funeral. When she said *Bellamy* and no one corrected her — you all assumed she was confused, or mad, or cruel. She wasn't any of those things. She said it because it's true. It was our name. It was your name. And Margaret—" My voice did something humiliating, wobbled, and I pressed on through it because if I stopped I would never start again. "Margaret was a woman in Portsmouth. Tom wrote to her. For years. Before you were born, after you were born. Letters."

The kettle behind us ticked as it cooled. Outside, the gulls were conducting their eternal grievance.

"How do you know this?" Lior asked, very quietly.

"Because your mother found the letters, and your mother handed them to me, and told me to burn them. And I read them instead." I made myself meet her eyes. "Every one. I was the one who knew. I was the one who helped her pick the new name out of a phone directory — Kavanagh, because it sounded like nothing, because it attached to nothing, because that was the entire point. I drove her to the solicitor. I held her hand. I told myself I was protecting two grieving children, and maybe I was, partly, but mostly I was doing what my sister asked, and what my sister asked was to erase a man."

Lior didn't move. I have seen her sit through tasting menus described by liars, through eulogies that lied worse, and she has this stillness, this professional stillness, and I understood suddenly that she used it the same way her mother had used silence: as a wall she built between herself and the thing that would otherwise knock her down.

"He didn't abandon us," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a woman testing the weight-bearing capacity of a sentence.

"No," I said. "He didn't."

"Where did he go?"

"I don't know. Your mother arranged it. She gave him money and a condition — that he never contact the children, never use the name, never come back. And he kept it, Lior. For twenty-seven years, he kept it. Whatever else he was, whatever happened that night—" I stopped. Careful, Ruth. "He kept his side of it."

"That night." Her voice changed. Something moved under the stillness, tectonic. "What happened that night, Ruth? Because I've been inventorying that night for nine years. I have notebooks. Pages of it. Everything we never said about Danny, everything we never said about Dad, catalogued and cross-referenced like a stockroom of things too heavy to lift. And nobody — not you, not Mum, not one person in this family — would ever give me a single entry."

The wind shoved the door against its brick. Down on the rocks below, the sea was working at the foundations the way it always had, patiently, like a creditor.

"I don't know what happened that night," I said, and it was almost true. "I know what your mother believed. She believed Tom waited too long to call for help. That Danny was in the water longer than he needed to be because Tom—" My hands were shaking. I put them flat on the table, on the old wood, on the ring-stains from Tom Bellamy's beer mats. "She believed he froze. Or worse. She never said *worse*. But she packed us out of here within the month, and she changed the names, and she built a life on top of it the way you build on landfill — fast, and shallow, and hoping nothing shifts."

"And you think she was wrong."

"I think," I said, "that your mother chose a story she could survive inside, and she made the rest of us live in it too, and I let her, because the alternative was asking questions in a house where every question was a load-bearing wall." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet. "You write about restaurants, Lior. You know what a menu is? It's a story a kitchen tells about itself. Your mother wrote us all a menu. And I was the one who laminated it."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, in a voice stripped of everything except the question itself:

"Do you know where he is? Is he alive?"

"I don't know." And then, because I owed her more than I had ever paid anyone: "But I know someone who might. Your mother kept things, Lior. Even after the burning. There was a box — I helped her seal it, after the funeral arrangements were made, weeks ago. She told me where it was and made me promise not to tell until she was gone, and I have hated that promise every single day since, and I am telling you now because I cannot carry it into another winter."

"Where."

"Under the floorboards of the pantry. Here. In this kitchen." I nodded at the wall behind her, at the low cupboard door with its paint blistered like burned skin. "She never let go of this place, whatever she pretended. She kept the deed in her name until the year she died. And she kept the box."

Lior rose. She crossed to the pantry cupboard the way people approach altars they're angry at, and knelt, and worked her fingers into the gap in the floorboard, and I sat at the shimmed table with my cold tea and understood that whatever came up through those boards was going to rearrange both of us, and that I had driven four hours to drag her home and was instead about to hand her a crowbar.

The board came up. She reached in. And what she lifted out, wrapped in a tea towel gone soft with age, was not the box I remembered — the box was there too, tin, rusted shut — but beneath it, wedged deeper, a book. A fat, oil-stained, spine-cracked notebook that I knew at once, the way you know a face across a crowded station, though I hadn't seen it in nearly thirty years.

Tom's recipe book. His hand on the cover in grease-pencil: *THE BELLAMY — KITCHEN ONLY.*

Lior opened it right there on the pantry floor, and I watched her face as she turned the pages, and I watched the face change — because pages were missing. Torn out. Not lost: torn, deliberately, close along the spine, a whole run of them gone like teeth pulled.

"Ruth," she said. "Someone took pages out of this."

And I confess I was so busy watching her that I forgot, for one full moment, the other thing in the box — the letters, the bundle tied in butcher's twine, addressed in a hand I had hoped never to see again and addressed, I now saw with a lurch, not to my sister at all.

They were addressed to Danny.
