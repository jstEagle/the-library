## 20. Aftertaste

Morning after a storm has a particular smell, and for the first time in nine years I could tell you what it was.

I stood in the doorway of the kitchen with a broom I had no intention of using and let the smell come at me in layers: wet ash, brine, the green rot of the collapsed wing bleeding in through the broken wall, and underneath all of it, improbably, bread. Ada had found a bag of flour in the pantry that had somehow survived both the flood and the decades, and she'd decided, at six in the morning, in a kitchen with no roof over half of it, that what the situation called for was baking. She was at the camp stove with a skillet, humming something tuneless, her hair still damp from the night, and the smell coming off that skillet was doing something to my chest that I didn't have a word for yet.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only religion I've ever practiced, and because for nine years precision was all I had. I licked my lips. It was a habit, not a decision — I do it when I'm thinking, which my ex once called disgusting and which I have since decided is charming. And there it was. Salt.

Not the idea of salt. Not the memory of salt, which I had become an expert manufacturer of, the way a blind man becomes an expert on light. Salt itself, arriving on my tongue like a stranger at a funeral, and me knowing — the way you know a face you haven't seen since childhood — exactly whose it was.

I said, "Ada."

She didn't turn around. "If you're going to tell me the flour's gone off, I already know. It's got a flavor. I'm calling it *rustic*."

"Ada. I can taste the salt."

The skillet stopped moving. She turned, and her face did a thing I recognized from the night before, from the moment the east wing groaned and slid and we stood on the lawn holding hands like two children at a fireworks display, except this face was the opposite of that one. This face was the face of a woman being told the sea had given something back.

"Say it again," she said.

"Salt." I licked my lips like an idiot, like a scientist, like both. "Just salt. Just — the storm got in my mouth, I guess. But it's *there*. It's —" My voice did something embarrassing and I let it, because I'd spent the previous night saying every unspeakable thing in my possession to an empty dining room, and it turns out that after you've done that, your dignity doesn't grow back right away. Maybe it doesn't grow back at all. Maybe that's the point.

Ada crossed the kitchen and put her floury hands on either side of my face, which should have been absurd and was instead the most serious thing that had happened to me in a decade, and she looked at me the way you look at a stove to check if it's still hot.

"Open," she said.

"Ada—"

"*Open.*"

I opened my mouth like a patient and she fed me a piece of bread off the skillet, still too hot, and I chewed, and the world went quiet the way it does in the second before a verdict.

It wasn't much. That's the honest review. The bread was dense and slightly burnt on one side and the flour had, as advertised, a flavor of rust, and my tongue reported all of it the way a damaged radio reports a symphony: staticky, partial, dropping whole frequencies. But it was a report. For nine years my tongue had been a dead line, and now there was a voice on it, tinny and unreliable, saying *bread, warmth, burnt, salt, bread*.

I sat down on an upturned crate and cried in a way that was not elegant, and Ada sat down next to me and ate the rest of the bread, because she is a practical woman, and we let the kitchen drip around us.

* * *

Here is what the storm had done, in the daylight's accounting.

The east wing — Danny's wing, the rooms above the dock, the corridor where he'd kept his room untouched and where I'd found his torn recipe pages lining a drawer like a message he'd mailed twenty-five years in advance — was gone. Not damaged. Gone. The sea had taken it the way it takes things, without malice and without apology, and left a gap in the inn's profile like a missing tooth. The kitchen survived because the kitchen had always been the stubborn part of the building, the heart that refused to stop beating even after the rest of the body gave up, and I choose to believe this was my father's construction and not sentiment, though I am no longer as allergic to sentiment as I once was. The dining room survived, its windows boarded where the demolition crew had gotten halfway through their work before the storm interrupted them, so that the room where I'd spoken my inventory aloud sat in a strange striped light, half-sealed, half-open, like a mouth deciding whether to speak.

The demolition notice was still nailed to the front door. It had gone soft with rain, the ink bleeding, the date illegible. I looked at it for a long time and felt nothing I needed to write down.

Ada found me there. "They'll be back," she said. "Crews like this, after a storm, they come back early. They'll say the building's compromised."

"It is compromised."

"It's *ours*," she said, and then, because we are still learning to say that word to each other and it still lands strangely in both our mouths, "It's the inn's. Whatever we are to it."

I had spent the night before defending this building with my body, more or less — standing in the rain with a two-by-four while Ada called the one number in town she trusted and Ruth, of all people, arrived at midnight in a raincoat over her nightgown to stand on the lawn and shout at the crew foreman with a fluency that suggested a misspent youth. We had held the line. The sea had taken the line anyway, the part of it that mattered least and most. And now, in the gray morning, with salt on my tongue and my sister's flour on my face, I understood that I had been defending the wrong thing, or rather that the building had been doing what buildings do — standing in for something else — and the something else was still here, dripping, smelling of wet ash and bread.

* * *

I went upstairs, because there was something I had to do before the crew came back, and it could not wait, and I have learned — at fifty-three, at last, the slowest student in every class — that when the thing cannot wait, you do the thing.

My room at the inn was the one I'd slept in as a child, the one with the window over the garden that no longer existed. My notebooks had traveled with me from the city in a waterproof bag, because I am the kind of person who waterproofs her grief before her passport, and I took them out now and stacked them on the windowsill in the order of their years. Nine of them. Nine years of inventory. *Things Not Said*, the first one said on its cover, in my handwriting from before, when my handwriting still had confidence in it.

I had written in them every day. Not diary — I want to be clear that I never once wrote what happened to me. I wrote what didn't happen. What went unsaid. *Danny, I never told you I was scared of the dock at night. Mother, I never asked why we changed the name. Father, wherever you are, I never said I understood, because I didn't, and I still don't.* Thousands of entries. A museum of the unspoken, curated nightly, dusted, defended. I had thought it was devotion. I had thought I was keeping them alive, the dead and the vanished, by keeping their silences alive.

Standing there in the wet light with salt still faintly on my tongue, I saw it for what it was, and I am not going to be gentle with myself about it, because gentleness is what got me nine years of notebooks. It was a way of never living. It was a house I built out of the things I couldn't say, and then I moved into it, and then I nailed the doors shut, and then I called the nailings devotion. The inventory had been a beautiful, meticulous, nine-year-long act of not speaking.

But here is the thing I also saw, and this is the part I want to get right, because I almost got it wrong in the night: the inventory was also the only reason I could do what I did last night. You cannot speak aloud, to an empty room, every unsaid thing, unless you have spent nine years writing them down. The list was the rehearsal. The notebooks were the score. I had thought I was building a tomb and I had been building a libretto.

So I did not burn them. I had considered burning them — I'd stood in the ruined kitchen at some black hour with a box of matches and a genuine appetite for the gesture — and I'm glad now that Ada had taken the matches away to light the camp stove and never given them back. Burning them would have been another silence, a louder one. Instead I sat on the floor of my childhood room with my back against the bed and I opened the ninth notebook to the last written page, and beneath the final entry — *Father: I never said come home* — I wrote a new heading.

*Things Now Said.*

And then I made the list. It took most of the morning. I will not reproduce all of it here — some of it is between me and an empty dining room and a storm — but I will give you the entries I can bear to hand over, because a list of things said should be allowed to travel.

*Danny: I said I loved you more than the inn, which was a lie, because I loved you as the inn, and I said that too, and it's true, and you knew, you always knew, you hid the pages where I'd find them.*

*Mother: I said I forgave you, and that I understood why you couldn't, and that you were wrong about what happened on the dock and also not wrong, because grief doesn't take facts, it takes hostages, and you were its hostage and I was yours.*

*Father: I said come home, and he heard it, in a room two towns over, with his mind going soft as sea glass, and he said "Bellamy" back to me like a password, like a hand reaching up out of the water, and I held it.*

*Ruth: I said you were a coward, and I was right, and I said you were a child too when they made you carry the secret, and I was righter, and we stood in the rain and neither of us apologized, which is its own kind of apology, the Kavanagh kind, the Bellamy kind.*

*Ada: I said you're my sister, and I said it into the sea, up to my knees in the cold, and the sea didn't give it back, which I'm taking as acceptance of the terms.*

When I finished, my hand ached and the light had gone from gray to actual daylight, and I heard the sound every person who has ever loved a building dreads: a truck, and then voices, and then the particular cheerfulness of men with a schedule.

* * *

They were already at the front door when I came down, three of them, with the foreman from before — the one Ruth had shouted at, the one who had, I'll say this for him, looked genuinely sorry when the storm rolled in and he'd had to call off the boarding-up mid-wall. He saw me and did the thing men with schedules do when confronted with a woman who has clearly been crying and clearly does not intend to discuss it: he looked at his clipboard.

"Ms. — Kavanagh, is it?" he said. "We're resuming. The east wing's gone, which — I'm sorry, for what it's worth — but the rest of the structure took water damage, and honestly, after last night, my insurance people are saying sooner rather than later."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, "that we've retained counsel."

"We haven't," said Ada, appearing at my shoulder with the timing of a woman who grew up on stories about this inn and has decided to out-dramatize all of them.

"Ruth's driving down," I said. "She has papers. She's been on the phone since six with a lawyer in Port Ellery and the historical society and, I believe, a man who owes her money from 1987. The inn is being reclassified. Community use. There's a process and she is *enjoying* it."

The foreman looked at his clipboard, then at Ada, then at the bleeding demolition notice on the door, then back at me, and I watched him do the arithmetic of a man deciding how much trouble one storm-ruined inn is worth. Behind him, his crew had gone still in that way crews go still when they sense a story — and this town, I had learned, runs on stories the way it used to run on fishing.

"The Bellamy place," one of them said quietly. Not a question. The name had come back to the town the way the taste had come back to me: not all at once, and not clean, but back.

"The Bellamy place," I said. "And we're going to need your crew to board up the *west* windows properly this time, because we open in three weeks, and I am not serving the first dinner of my life with a draft."

The foreman looked at me for a long moment. Then he turned the clipboard over, found a blank page, and clicked his pen, and I understood that I had just watched a demolition order die of natural causes.

"Three weeks," he said. "For what?"

"A dinner," said Ada. "For the whole town. Everyone."

I looked at her. She looked at me. Neither of us had discussed this. Both of us, I think, had been thinking about nothing else since the moment the east wing slid into the sea and we understood that what was left was not a ruin but a room — one room, one kitchen, one table's worth of future — and that a future that size doesn't need a plan. It needs a menu.

The foreman wrote something down. The crew moved toward the west windows. The sea, beyond the gap where Danny's wing had been, lay flat and glittering and unrepentant, holding everything it had taken and, this once, giving some of it back.

I went into the kitchen, where the camp stove was still warm and the skillet still smelled of bread and rust, and I picked up the ninth notebook, and at the bottom of the list of things now said I added one more line, the last line, the one I'd been circling since the funeral, since the photograph, since the first salt on my lips this morning:

*Lior: I said I'm staying.*

Then I closed the notebook, and opened the pantry, and started counting what we had.
