## 3. Iluva, or the Sound of Rain on Tin

The recorder was older than Noor's shop, and it smelled like it. Elias Okonkwo set it on the table between them with both hands, the way a man sets down something that has been carried a long way, and then he sat very still, as if waiting for permission from the machine itself.

It was a Tuesday afternoon, and the light in the back room of Brightwater & Sons Stationery came through the one window at an angle that made everything look like it was already being remembered. Dust moved in it. The shelf of discontinued inks glowed amber and violet. Elias had chosen the chair by the window without being asked, and Noor had decided not to mind.

She had told herself, all week, that this was just another commission. A job. An old man with an unusual request and presumably an unusual checkbook. She had written for stranger purposes: apology letters to mistresses, eulogies for dogs, a farewell note from a man to his own liver. One afternoon of transcription. She would record his words, type them up neat, invoice him, and be done.

But she had spent Sunday evening preparing anyway — laying out fresh cassettes she didn't need yet, testing the microphone twice, choosing a notebook with paper soft enough to take pencil fast. She had caught herself doing this at eleven o'clock at night, standing in her kitchen in her coat, and had stood there a moment longer than necessary, holding the notebook like a question she hadn't asked yet.

Now Elias opened a leather satchel and produced, with ceremony, three items: a tin of lemon drops, a photograph of a woman Noor did not know, and a folded page of handwritten notes so worn its creases had gone to fur.

"My wife," he said, setting the photograph facing himself, where he could see it but Noor mostly couldn't. "Adaeze. She is why we are here."

Noor waited. She was good at waiting; it was most of her trade.

"Iluva is not my first language," he said. "I want that written down first, before anything else. Write: *Elias Okonkwo learned Iluva when he was nineteen years old, from a girl.*"

"From a girl," Noor repeated, pencil hovering.

"From Adaeze." He touched the edge of the photograph without looking at it, the way you touch a banister in the dark. "I came here in 1954 with nothing but English and a suitcase with a broken latch. I could not say what I meant in English. English kept sliding off me. And Adaeze spoke Iluva, and Iluva had — " he searched, tapping the table once, twice — "Ilúvà had *rooms* in it. Rooms English doesn't have. I walked into those rooms and I never came out."

He said the word — *Ilúvà* — and Noor felt it in her sternum before she understood it in her ears. It had a weight that dropped at the end, like a stone set down gently.

"Say it again," she said, before she could decide whether to.

"*Ilúvà.*"

She wrote it down. Her handwriting, usually so controlled — customers complimented it, commissioned it, paid four dollars a line for it — went slightly loose around the letters.

* * *

They worked until the light changed. Elias spoke slowly, deliberately, the way a man lays bricks he knows no one will build on after him. He gave her numbers first, because numbers are how languages begin to be counted. Then colors, though he paused long over color, because Iluva, he said, did not separate a thing from what the light was doing to it. There was no word for simply *red*. There was a word for red at dawn, another for red seen through leaves, another for red that meant someone had been crying near a fire.

"That last one has a word?" Noor said. "Crying near a fire?"

"*Ìjà rẹ́dọ̀*," Elias said. "It is used also for a lie that everyone agrees to believe."

Noor wrote it down and did not say anything for a while.

The recorder turned and turned. Outside, the delivery trucks made their afternoon noise on Delancey, and once a siren passed and Elias stopped speaking until it was fully gone, out of respect, he said, for anyone who might be inside it.

Around four o'clock he asked for water, and while Noor poured it he studied her shelves of pens through the doorway with the expression of a man auditing a life.

"You write letters for people," he said. Not quite a question.

"For people who can't find the words."

"And do they pay you to find them, or to keep them?"

Noor set the glass down harder than she meant to. "To find them," she said. "What happens after is theirs."

Elias nodded slowly, as if this were an answer to a different, better question. "In Iluva," he said, "there is a word for a person who carries words for other people. *Ònìṣòwò ọ̀rọ̀*. Literally: trader of speech. But it is not a compliment and it is not an insult. It is a warning. It means: this one knows what everyone means and says nothing of their own. We say such a person's mouth is full of other people's bread."

Noor laughed — a short, surprised sound — and then didn't laugh, because it had landed somewhere true.

"And your own bread?" Elias asked mildly. "What does Noor Marchetti say in Noor Marchetti's language?"

"I write postcards," she heard herself say, and was appalled at how much of her that sentence carried, and how little of it he could possibly understand.

"To whom?"

"A friend." The lie came out smooth as a receipt. Nine years of practice will do that.

Elias looked at her for a long moment with eyes that had done a great deal of looking in ninety-one years, and then, mercifully, he let it go. He picked up his lemon drops instead and offered her one, and she took it, and the lemon was so sharp it made her eyes sting, which she decided to blame entirely on the lemon.

* * *

It was near five, the light gone copper, when he taught her the phrase that undid her.

He had been giving her farewells — or trying to. He'd start, stop, tap the table. Finally he pushed the recorder aside, which she understood was significant, because everything else he had wanted recorded.

"You are waiting for the word for goodbye," he said. "You have been waiting since two o'clock. Every language has one, you think. So ask me."

"All right," Noor said. "What is the word for goodbye?"

"There isn't one."

She waited for the joke. It didn't come.

"Iluva has no word for goodbye," Elias said. "None. In sixty years of speaking it I have never said goodbye in Iluva, because it cannot be said. What we have instead are phrases. Many phrases. Each one describes a particular kind of leaving."

"Like what?"

He held up fingers, counting, an old teacher's habit surfacing through the old man.

"*Káàbọ̀ lójú òpópó̀* — until our eyes meet again on this road. That is for leaving someone you will see soon, a day, a week. Casual. You use it for the market."

"*Dìde kí ẹjìká* — rise before the wind rises. For leaving a place you love but must leave anyway. My wife said it to me every morning for forty years, even though I wasn't going anywhere. It was a joke between us and then it was not a joke."

"*Tí ọ̀nà yóò kùnkùn* — " he slowed here, and his voice changed key, dropped into something lower, more careful, the way you lower your voice near sleeping things — "*until the road bends.*"

"Until the road bends," Noor repeated.

"When you leave someone and you do not know if you will see them again. When the parting is real. You do not say goodbye, because goodbye pretends the separation is finished, complete, a closed door. Instead you say: until the road bends. Because a road always bends. And what is past the bend is hidden. You may come around it and find each other again. Or you may not. The phrase holds both. It refuses to choose."

Noor looked down at her notebook. She had written the phrase phonetically, and beneath it, automatically, the translation, and beneath that — without deciding to — nothing. Just blank space, three lines of it, as if the pencil itself needed room.

Her brother Teo had left on a Thursday. He had hugged her in the doorway of the shop, smelled of cigarettes and the cheap cologne Rosa bought him every Christmas, and said, "See you Sunday, Nor." He had died the following Wednesday night, in the fire, nine years ago now, and the last thing she had ever said to him was *see you Sunday*, and she had never found a word big enough to hold what that sentence became afterward. English had offered her *goodbye*, which was obscene, and *farewell*, which was for strangers and ships, and *rest in peace*, which was for gravestones and other people's griefs. She had settled, in the end, for silence, and silence was a country she knew too well.

And here was an old man saying that his language had looked at that exact hole in the world — the leaving that might be forever, the parting that refuses to close — and had built a phrase to live in it. *Until the road bends.* Both truths held at once. The seeing-again and the not. Held so gently you could carry it in your mouth.

"What's wrong?" Elias said quietly.

"Nothing."

"You have stopped writing. A writer who stops writing is either thinking or drowning. Which is it?"

"Thinking," Noor said, and wrote something, anything, to prove it.

But the afternoon had shifted under her, the way floorboards shift. All through the session it had been happening in small doses — the word for red-near-a-fire, the trader of speech whose mouth is full of others' bread — and each time she had filed it away as curiosity, as color, as material. Now the filing cabinet wouldn't close. This language kept naming rooms she had lived in alone for years without knowing the rooms had names. It was like walking through her own house with a stranger who kept opening doors she'd painted shut, saying, *oh, you have a library,* and *oh, you've been sleeping in the hallway.*

She should have found it wonderful. Part of her did — a large part, a hungry part she hadn't fed in years. But underneath the wonder ran something colder, and it took her until she was locking up the shop that night to name it:

Fear.

Because if a language could name these things — if these feelings had words, had always had words, sitting in some grammar somewhere waiting — then the excuse of unnameability was gone. She could no longer tell herself that what she felt about Teo was beyond language, that silence was the only honest response, that some griefs were simply unsayable. Iluva said them. Iluva had been saying them, apparently, for centuries, with perfect composure, to anyone willing to learn.

A language that names your secrets makes keeping them harder.

* * *

Elias packed his satchel with the same ceremony he'd unpacked it. Photograph, lemon drops, folded page. He wound the recorder's cord in slow loops.

"Same time next week?" Noor said, professional, invoicing voice.

"Thursday," Elias said. "Thursdays are better. On Thursdays the street is quiet enough that you can hear the rain coming before it comes." He shrugged into his coat, one arm at a time, unhurried, a man who had decided long ago that rushing was for people with less time, not more. At the door he stopped, hand on the frame, and turned half around.

"One more thing, for your notes. Today you asked me nothing about myself. Six hours, and you know my language better than my biography. Most people it is the reverse." He smiled, and the smile rearranged his whole face into someone younger. "So I will give you one fact free. The first full sentence Adaeze ever taught me in Iluva — before numbers, before colors — was this: *Ọ̀rọ̀ tí a fi ọmọ kọ́ ni ó ń gbé ilé*."

"Which means?"

"It means: the word you teach a child is the word that will live in your house." He tapped the doorframe twice, softly, the way he'd tapped the table. "Think about what that means for a woman who writes everyone's words except her own."

Then he was gone, into the copper evening, an old man with a broken-latch suitcase's worth of a language in his head, walking home down a street that — though neither of them knew it yet — had less than a year left to stand.

Noor stood in the empty shop a long time. Then she took out her notebook and read back what she'd written, all those borrowed rooms, and in the three blank lines beneath *until the road bends* she finally wrote something of her own, small, in pencil, in English:

*Teo. See you Sunday.*

She looked at it until the shop went dark around her, and then she closed the book, and did not sleep, and began — though she would not admit it for weeks — to wait for Thursday.
