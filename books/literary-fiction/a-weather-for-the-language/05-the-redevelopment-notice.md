## 5. The Redevelopment Notice

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which Noor would remember later because Tuesdays were the days she restocked the envelopes, and she was standing in the doorway of the shop with a box of A7s balanced on her hip when she saw the man in the orange vest stapling paper to the lamppost outside the bus depot.

She had seen city notices before. They came like weather — predictable, gray, arriving without anyone's permission. But this one had a color to it, a municipal yellow that seemed designed to be ignored, and it flapped once in the wind before the staple caught, and even from across the street Noor felt her stomach perform a small, cold revolution.

The man worked his way down the block. Lamppost. Telephone pole. The chain-link fence around the depot's fuel yard, where the staples wouldn't hold and he had to use zip ties, cursing softly at the wind. He did not look at the buildings he was condemning. That was what struck her most, afterward — how he never once looked up. He treated the whole street as a surface, something to be fastened to, the way you'd treat a corkboard. As if the block were already gone and he was just decorating its absence.

Noor set down the box of envelopes. She crossed the street without deciding to, the way you cross toward an accident.

*NOTICE OF DEMOLITION AND REDEVELOPMENT*, the header said, in a font that aspired to authority and achieved only bureaucracy. *Pursuant to Ordinance 44-C, the following parcels are scheduled for clearance effective—*

She read the date twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings had produced different numbers in her head, which was impossible, which meant her mind was doing what it always did under pressure: refusing.

Six weeks. Forty-two days, if she counted the way she counted everything, obsessively, the way she'd counted nine years of Sundays. In forty-two days the bulldozers would come for the whole eastern face of Halloway Street — the depot, the shuttered laundromat with its ghost-sign still faintly legible (*STEAM PRESSED WHILE YOU WAIT*), the three-story walk-up where the Ferreiras had lived until they moved to the suburbs, the corner lot where the bodega used to be before the fire took it and half the row beside it.

And Elias Okonkwo's house.

The notice fluttered against its staple. Noor stood in front of it long enough that the man in the orange vest, packing up his stapler two posts down, gave her a look — not unkind, just the look of a man who had seen a hundred people stand where she was standing, reading the end of something, and who had learned there was nothing useful to say about it.

"It's posted," he offered, finally, as though confirming a train time.

"I see that," Noor said.

"There's an appeals process." He said it the way people say *there's a prayer process* — technically true, functionally decorative. "Form 11-B. You'd need the property owner to file."

"The property owner is ninety-one years old."

The man shrugged with his whole equipment belt. "Then I guess form 11-B goes in a drawer." And he moved on down the street, stapling the future to whatever would hold it.

* * *

She didn't go straight to Elias's house. She went back to the shop, because that was what Noor Marchetti did when the world tilted — she returned to the counter, to the small republic of pens and paper where every problem could at least be *addressed*, literally, in her careful hand, on someone else's behalf.

But the postcards wouldn't come that morning. She sat with a customer's commission open in front of her — Mrs. Adeyemi wanted a note to her sister in Lagos, something warm but not gushing, mentioning the garden, omitting the hip — and Noor wrote *Dear Adaeze* and then stopped, her pen hovering, because all she could hear was the sound of the zip ties going around the fence posts. The little ratchet-click of them tightening. The sound of something being secured for its own destruction.

At noon she closed the shop. She put up the hand-lettered sign that said BACK IN AN HOUR, which was a lie she told roughly four times a month, and walked the six blocks to Elias's house carrying the demolition notice folded into quarters in her coat pocket like contraband.

He was on the porch. He was almost always on the porch now, weather permitting, sitting in the green metal chair that had belonged to his wife, with a blanket over his knees even in mild air, as if his body had begun pre-mourning itself. The reel-to-reel recorder sat on the table beside him under an oilcloth, protected from dew, the way other men protected tools or photographs.

He saw her coming from halfway down the walk. His eyes were still good; it was his hands that had begun to negotiate separate treaties with the world.

"You have the face," he said.

"What face?"

"The face of a woman carrying bad news in her pocket instead of wearing it." He gestured at the chair opposite him, the one with the wobbly leg that Noor had learned to brace her heel against. "Sit. Tell me."

She sat. She took out the folded paper and unfolded it and smoothed it flat on the small table between them, next to a cup of tea gone cold and a dish of peppermints that had fused into a single geological formation.

Elias read it the way he read everything — slowly, his lips moving slightly, though Noor knew he'd been literate in English for seventy years. It was Iluva he'd never learned to read, only speak; the missionaries who had made the alphabet had also, he liked to say, made the cage around it. He read the ordinance number, the parcel numbers, the date. When he reached the date he did not read it aloud. His lips stopped moving.

"Six weeks," Noor said, because someone had to.

"Yes." He folded the notice along its original creases, precisely, and set it under the fused peppermints as a paperweight, as though it were merely another piece of correspondence that had arrived at his door. People brought him things all the time now, Noor had noticed. Bills, church bulletins, flyers. He was the kind of old man the neighborhood delivered itself to.

"They can't do this," Noor said, and heard immediately how young she sounded, how much like a customer asking her to make an injustice sound better on paper. "There's an appeal. Form 11-B. If the owner files—"

"I own the house." He said it mildly. "I have owned it since 1974. Do you know what I paid? Eleven thousand dollars. The realtor told my wife the neighborhood was 'transitional.' We thought that meant people were moving in." He smiled at some private joke between himself and 1974. "It meant we were moving out. Eventually. All of us. This is just the eventually arriving."

"But your house isn't condemned. Structurally it's fine, it's—"

"Noor." He said her name the way he pronounced many words, with a slight musical delay before the second syllable, *no-OR*, as if checking the word for ripeness. "Look at the map on the back."

She turned the notice over. There it was: the redevelopment parcel outlined in red, the whole eastern face of the block, and inside the red line, drawn with the indifferent precision of a computer, the little rectangle that was his house. The little rectangle that was the depot. The little rectangle that was the burned gap where Teo had died, now a weed lot the city had mowed exactly twice a year for nine years, a grave maintained by neglect.

"They're not demolishing buildings," Elias said. "They're demolishing an address. Buildings they could condemn one at a time, fight one at a time. An address is easier. You erase the street name off the bus route and within ten years nobody remembers there was ever anything here to miss." He looked down the block, toward the depot, its windows boarded since the route had been cut back in the spring. "That is how you kill a place. Not with fire. Fire leaves ash, and ash means something happened. You kill a place with paperwork."

Noor's hands had gone cold in her lap. "The recordings," she said. "The sessions. How far—"

"We have done thirty-one hours." He said it without drama, a fact among facts. "Thirty-one hours of a language that took—" he paused, calculating, and Noor realized he was counting in decades, not years, "—took four thousand years to become what it was when I was a boy. Thirty-one hours. You could fit it in a suitcase. You could lose it in a suitcase."

"Then we work faster. Every day. Twice a day if you're able—"

"I am able." Something moved through his face then, and Noor recognized it, because she had seen it in mirrors and in her mother's kitchen and in the eyes of every customer who ever asked her to write what they couldn't: it was fear, dressed carefully as practicality. "But listen to me. Listen, because this part matters more than the hours." He leaned forward, and the blanket slid, and he let it. "A language is not words. I have told you this. Words are what a language wears. Underneath, a language is *agreement*. It is a contract between people who promise to understand each other. Iluva does not live in these tapes. The tapes are a photograph of a voice. Beautiful. Dead. What lives—" he touched his own throat, briefly, "—is here, and in one other place, which is whoever agrees to keep speaking it. And I will tell you something I have not said plainly, because I am an old coward in many ways and brave in only one."

He waited. Down the street, a bus that no longer stopped here anyway ground past on the cross avenue, and neither of them turned to watch it.

"When the bulldozers take this house," Elias said, "I will be moved somewhere with rails on the bed and a schedule for soup. Perhaps I live another year. Perhaps a week. Either way, the last throat that knows Iluva will be behind glass. And if by then there is not a second throat—" he looked at her, directly, with a steadiness that made her want to stand up and leave and also never move again, "—then six weeks is not a deadline for a project, Noor. It is a funeral with a date printed on it. Which is more than most funerals get."

The wind came down Halloway Street the way it always did, funneling between the buildings, carrying the smell of the rail yard and, faintly, someone frying onions three houses down. Life continuing, oblivious, delicious. Noor held onto the arm of the wobbling chair.

"You're saying you want to teach me," she said. "Not record me recording you. Teach."

"I am saying that a language needs a throat, not a shelf." He settled back, retrieving the blanket, tucking it with the fussy dignity of a man reasserting control over at least one square yard of the world. "I am saying we have forty-one days now, because today is spent. And I am saying that I have watched you write other people's hearts for"—he tilted his head—"how long have you had the shop?"

"Nine years."

"Mm." Something flickered there, too quick to catch, gone before she could name it. "Nine years of everyone's words but your own. Iluva has a phrase for that condition. Most languages don't bother. Ours needed it often enough to make a word."

"What phrase?"

"*Kavu ilen.*" He pronounced it slowly, giving her each piece. "*Kavu* — the mouth. *Ilen* — full, overfull, brimming. A mouth too full of unsaid things to speak." He shrugged one thin shoulder. "We were a people who lost a great deal. We got good at naming the losses."

Noor looked at the demolition notice under the peppermints. Six weeks. Forty-one days. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the ribs, in the place where she kept the postcards and the lie about the neighborhood fund and the Sunday ritual she had never once described aloud to any living person, something began — quietly, without permission — to count.

"All right," she said. "Tomorrow. Both sessions. Morning and evening."

"And Sundays," Elias said. "You strike me as a woman who keeps her Sundays busy."

The sentence landed harder than he could know. She kept her face still. "Sundays too," she managed.

"Good." He nodded once, satisfied, and then, as she rose to go, he added — lightly, almost as an afterthought, which is how the heaviest things are always handed over — "Bring the tapes home with you tonight. All of them. The reels from the cabinet, the ones I recorded before you. If the house is coming down, the archive should not sleep here. A wise archivist keeps copies apart."

"I'll bring boxes," Noor said.

"Bring strong ones." He closed his eyes against the afternoon light, conversation clearly concluded, an old man returning to the country behind his eyelids. Then, just before she reached the steps, without opening his eyes: "Tape forty-seven is mislabeled. It says *rain sounds* on the sleeve. It is not rain sounds. Set it aside. We will deal with it another day."

* * *

That night the shop was cold and the radiator knocked its familiar arrhythmic knock, and Noor carried the reel boxes in from the taxi one at a time, stacking them behind the counter beside the ledgers, forty-some reels in their cardboard sleeves, each labeled in Elias's careful missionary-schoolhand: dates, places, names of speakers, most of them crossed out now with single lines — dead, dead, dead — the tape equivalent of those little flags cemeteries plant.

She should have gone to bed. She had a six-week deadline and a mother to call and a stack of commissions due Thursday and a Sunday postcard to write in three days' time, addressed as always to Apartment 4B, 118 Halloway Street — an address that, she understood now with a clarity that felt like falling, would not exist in six weeks. Not as a deliverable fiction. Not even as a fiction.

Instead she sat down behind the counter, under the lamp, and began reading sleeves. *Session 12, Elias alone, proverbs. Session 19, Elias and Mireille Baptiste (deceased), children's songs. Session 30, rain on the tin roof of the shed, two hours, ambient.*

And near the bottom of the third box, a sleeve whose label had been written in a different hand — younger, looser, the letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line:

*Tape 47 — rain sounds.*

It was not rain sounds. She knew that the way you know a lie in your own family's handwriting.

Noor sat for a long time with the reel in both hands, feeling its weight, which was almost nothing, which was everything. Outside, the streetlight buzzed. Somewhere a few blocks over, the orange-vested man was probably asleep, his stapler racked and holstered, tomorrow's notices lined up in his truck like bullets in a bandolier.

Forty-one days.

She threaded the reel onto the machine she kept for transcribing customers' dictated letters, put on the headphones, and pressed play — and out of the small speakers, into the empty shop, into the nine-year silence she had built her whole life around, came a voice she knew better than God's.

Her brother's voice.

*"Testing, testing — okay, Elias, it's rolling? Okay. Okay. So there's this letter I've been meaning to send my sister. Nine drafts. I'll send it when I'm brave. Whenever that is..."*

Noor's hand found the stop button and hovered above it and did not press it.
