## 17. Funeral in Two Tongues

*Adaeze*

The funeral home on Delancey Street had never hosted anyone like my aunt before, and it showed. The director, a kind man named Mr. Pell with the mournful competence of someone who had said *I'm sorry for your loss* eleven thousand times, kept asking me questions he didn't know how to ask.

"You said she'd want... music?"

"Not music," I said. "A recording. Of a lullaby."

"And the flowers—"

"White ones. But not lilies." I had spent three days learning what Maren Oduya would have wanted, and most of it was a list of things she would have found ridiculous. Lilies were ridiculous. Open caskets were ridiculous—"Why do you want to look at me when I am not in there?" she'd have said, and laughed her dry laugh that sounded like paper turning pages.

What she would have wanted was people. That was the whole of it. She had spent the last years of her life watching a language empty itself out of the world one speaker at a time, and she had developed a horror of rooms with too few voices in them.

So we filled the room. Neighbors from her building. The women from the Saturday market who used to save her the good okra. A man named Emeka who had driven her to dialysis every Tuesday for two years and claimed, correctly, that this made him family. And near the back, half-hidden behind a pillar as if he still wasn't sure he was allowed to be seen: the piano tuner.

Silas Ravensworth wore a suit that fit him like an apology. He stood the way I imagined he tuned pianos—present, careful, trying not to disturb anything. Three weeks ago I had told him he was the reason there was no one left to speak to my aunt. I had watched the sentence land on him like weather.

I hadn't apologized yet. I wasn't ready. But I had saved him a seat in the second row, and when he saw it, something moved through his face too fast for me to name.

The service began at ten. Mr. Pell spoke first, gently wrong about everything. Then Emeka told the story of the time Maren argued with a parking meter for twenty minutes because it had issued her a ticket in "a language with no manners," and the room laughed, and I felt her in the laughing, which is the only afterlife I believe in anymore.

Then Mr. Pell leaned toward me and murmured, "And did the deceased leave any final remarks?"

"She left a language," I said. "That's going to take longer than remarks."

Because there was one more thing. One thing I had found in her papers, folded inside her lesson plans, in her handwriting, addressed to no one and therefore, I understood now, to everyone:

*If someone stands up who shouldn't have to, let them.*

I looked at the second row. Silas Ravensworth was staring at his hands.

"The family will now hear a eulogy," I said, standing, my voice doing something braver than I felt. "From a student of my aunt's. Her last one."

He didn't move at first. The whole room turned, a slow tide of faces, and I watched a man decide—in real time, in front of eighty strangers—whether to remain invisible or not.

Then he stood up.

* * *

*Theo*

Mr. Ravensworth walked to the front like a guy walking into the ocean in November. I knew that walk. It's the walk you do when your body goes somewhere before your brain signs off on it.

I'd only known him since June, since the night I broke into his house and made him eat soup, which sounds insane when I say it out loud but was actually the most normal thing that happened that year. He never asked where I came from. I never asked why he lived in a house that looked like it was holding its breath. We just sort of... got each other. Two people renting space in their own lives.

But this was different. This was him choosing to be looked at.

He unfolded a piece of paper, looked at it, and then put it away. Bad sign, I thought. Then he took a breath—the kind of breath you can hear from the back row—and started talking without it.

"Maren Oduya taught me my first word in her language eight months ago," he said. His voice cracked on *eight*, and he just let it crack, which honestly took more guts than anything else he did that day. "It was the word for rain on a tin roof. *Nkosa.*"

Somewhere in the third row, an older woman made a small sound, like the word had reached into her chest and adjusted something.

"I'm a piano tuner," he went on. "People think my job is about making things sound right. It isn't. It's about listening to what a thing used to sound like, and helping it get back there. For most of my life I thought that was all listening was—archaeology. Digging up old sounds. I didn't know a language could be alive until Maren put her hand on my arm in a diner and said, *You will be my ears when mine close.*"

He paused. The room was so quiet I could hear the radiator in the corner doing its low groan, and I swear even the radiator sounded embarrassed to interrupt.

"Her language has a word I want to try to say," he said. "*Kweli-mara.*" He said it slowly, carefully, the way you carry something breakable across a room. "It doesn't translate exactly. It means... the grief you carry for someone that you refuse to set down, because setting it down would feel like losing them twice."

An old man near the aisle closed his eyes. A woman beside him reached over and held his hand without looking, the way people do when they've been holding hands at funerals for fifty years.

"I don't speak this language," Silas said. "I want to be honest about that. I know maybe two hundred words. I pronounce half of them wrong. If Maren could hear me right now she would stop me three times and make me start over." A ripple of laughter went through the room, warm and wet at the same time. "But she also told me that a language doesn't die when its last speaker dies. It dies when its last speaker is *unwilling to be heard*. So this is me, being heard. Badly. On purpose."

And then he did the thing.

He stopped speaking English.

I don't know how long it lasted—a minute, maybe two. He spoke in Maren's language, halting, accented, sometimes stopping mid-sentence to find a word with his eyes shut like a man groping for a light switch in a house he was still learning the rooms of. I couldn't understand any of it. Neither could most of the room. But here's what I learned watching those eighty faces: understanding isn't the only way to receive something. The old woman in the third row was mouthing along. Emeka had his hand over his mouth. Somewhere around the middle, Silas's voice steadied, stopped being a performance and became a person talking to someone he loved, and the whole room leaned toward him like plants toward a window.

When he finished, he stood there for a second, exposed, like a man who'd taken off his coat in winter.

Then the woman in the third row rose to her feet and answered him.

In the same language.

One sentence. Her voice old and rough and certain. And Silas—I saw this clearly, I'll see it forever—Silas's face did the thing a piano does when you finally find the pitch it's been searching for. He nodded and answered her back, four words, clumsy, true.

She sat down. He came back to his seat. Nobody clapped, because it was a funeral and you don't clap, but the silence afterward wasn't an empty silence. It was a full one.

Later, outside, I asked him what they'd said to each other.

"She said, *The tongue is rusty but the well is not dry.*" He was looking at the funeral home door like it might open again. "I told her I'd come learn from her too. All of them. Anyone who remembers anything."

"That's gonna be a lot of lessons," I said.

"Yeah," he said. And then he smiled—not the polite tuner smile I'd seen him give customers, but the real one, the crooked one. "Good."

* * *

*Silas*

Here is what nobody tells you about courage: it feels identical to nausea.

I had written the eulogy four times. The fourth version was the one in my pocket, the one I abandoned, because when I stood up and faced that room I understood that Maren would have hated it. It was polished. It was safe. It was English wearing a suit. And she had spent her whole life telling me that some things cannot cross over into English without arriving smaller than they left.

So I put the paper away and I opened my mouth and let the words come out in the order she'd taught them to me, which was the order of weather, then grief, then beginning again.

I will not pretend I was fluent. My grammar collapsed twice. At one point I said a word that made Emeka's eyebrows climb—he told me afterward, kindly, that I had called Maren a cooking pot—and the correction, the laughter, the small human stumble of it, did more than polish ever could have. Because that's what she'd done every lesson: laughed, corrected, continued. The mistakes were the method. She used to say fluency was just errors moving at the speed of trust.

When the old woman—her name was Ngozi, I learned later; she had been Maren's friend for sixty years—rose and answered me, I felt something I had no word for in English and, at last, a word for in hers. There is a term in her language, *mbele-osa*, that means roughly *the moment a stranger becomes a witness*. Not a friend. Not yet. Just someone who has seen you and stayed.

Eighty people witnessed me. I stayed.

Afterward, in the receiving line, people shook my hand. Some of them gripped hard. One woman held on a beat too long and said, "You knew her?" and I said, "Barely. Eight months. Two hundred words." And she said, "That's two hundred more than yesterday," and walked on, and I stood there feeling the truth of it land in my chest like a tuning fork struck against bone.

Adaeze found me at the end, when the room was emptying and Mr. Pell was folding chairs with the tenderness of a man putting a day to bed.

"My aunt wrote something," she said. "In her papers. About you, I think. Or about whoever. She wrote: *If someone stands up who shouldn't have to, let them.*" She looked at me. "You shouldn't have had to. I'm sorry I made it harder."

"You were grieving," I said.

"I was angry," she corrected. "Grief was underneath it. But I was angry, and I aimed it at the nearest available target, and you stood very still, which made it easy." She exhaled. "Maren would have said anger is a loan, not a gift. You're supposed to pay it back by letting it go."

"Is that really one of her sayings, or are you inventing wisdom posthumously?"

A flicker of a smile—the family resemblance, sudden and startling. "Posthumous invention is also inheritance," she said. Then, briskly, before either of us could get sentimental: "Her apartment has to be emptied by the end of the month. There are boxes of papers. Lesson plans. Recordings. Things I don't understand and you might. I'm not ready to sort through them alone."

"I could help," I said.

"You could help," she agreed. And that was the whole negotiation, and it was enough.

Walking out, I passed Ngozi on the steps. She took my arm the way Maren used to, firm, proprietary, as if arms were public transportation and she had a right to board.

"Come Thursday," she said. "I remember songs. My memory is bad for names now, but the songs—" she tapped her sternum— "they live down here. They don't leave with the mind. Come Thursday and I will teach you what I still have."

"What do you still have?" I asked.

Her eyes went bright and fierce. "Enough," she said. "For now. Enough."

* * *

That night I drove back to Ravensworth House, and for the first time since I inherited it, the house did not feel like a question I was avoiding.

I stood in the kitchen with the lights on—all the lights, wastefully, gloriously—and I said the final word out loud. The one Maren gave me at the end, her voice a thread, her hand tight on mine: *sava-lume.* Beginning again after ruin. Not recovery. Not return. Something rougher and better than both. The green thing that comes up through the burned field, not because the fire never happened, but because the fire happened and the seed was deeper than fire reaches.

I said it once for her. I said it once for my grandmother, wherever she was in the architecture of that house. And then—because the word means nothing hoarded—I said it a third time, quietly, to the empty chairs around my table, rehearsing.

Thursday, Ngozi's songs. Next week, Adaeze and the boxes. Somewhere in Maren's papers, I was almost sure, there would be more recordings, more lessons, maybe even the second verse of the lullaby in her own handwriting. And somewhere in the sealed room upstairs, my grandmother's letters still waited, unanswered for forty years, for someone to write back.

I put the kettle on. I got out a pen.

It was time to answer her.
