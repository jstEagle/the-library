## 4. Theo's Voice

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, except that Tuesdays were the day Nell did the accounts and the accounts were the thing she used to keep her hands busy when her mind wanted to go somewhere it shouldn't. She was at the kitchen table with a shoebox of receipts and the good pen she only used for numbers when Bram came in from his run holding the mail like it might be wet.

"Mostly junk," he said. "A flyer for gutter cleaning. Something from the city about water rates." He set the stack down beside her elbow, squared its edges against the table's edge out of habit—her husband, who could not leave an object unaligned any more than he could leave a sentence unfinished—and went upstairs to shower.

She almost didn't see it. That was the thing she would remember later: how ordinary it looked. A plain white envelope, hand-addressed, the ink slightly feathered as if the pen had been pressed too hard by a hand that no longer trusted itself. Her name on it—*Nell*—not Eleanor, not Mrs. Marchetti, just Nell, spelled the way only one person had ever spelled it, with the double *l* dragged long, like a note held past its measure.

Marrow Falls Post Office. The postmark sat there like a bruise.

She knew before she opened it. Some part of her had known since Friday night, since the college station and the voice coming through the dashboard speakers singing words she had written at nineteen in a notebook she'd burned the pages of but never the melody, because you can't burn a melody, it lives in the body like a scar lives in skin. She'd pulled over on Route 9 and sat with her hazards ticking and her heart doing something arrhythmic and terrible, and then she'd driven home and said nothing, and Bram had asked how the gig went and she'd said *fine*, and that word had cost her more than any word she'd ever sung.

Now she sat with the envelope in both hands and listened to the house. The shower running upstairs. The refrigerator's low hum. The tick of the wall clock in the hall that Bram kept wound because he believed time should be maintained like anything else.

She opened it.

The handwriting had changed. It had been slanted and quick once, a boy's handwriting, all forward motion. Now it leaned backward, tired, each letter placed rather than written. There were only four lines. She read them standing up, because sitting felt suddenly unsafe, the way a bridge feels unsafe when you know too much about bridges.

*Nell.*

*They played it. I asked them to.*

*I'm at St. Ambrose Hospice, Marrow Falls. They give me until the leaves come down, maybe less.*

*Come before I can't explain.*

No signature. He didn't need one. Thirty years of silence and still no signature, as if signing it would make it a letter between strangers instead of what it was: a summons.

She read it three times. On the third reading she noticed her own breathing had gone shallow and controlled, the way she breathed into a held note, and she thought, absurdly, *you're using technique on your own grief*, and that thought broke something loose and she gripped the counter's edge and stood very still until the shaking passed or mostly passed.

Upstairs the shower shut off. She folded the letter along its original creases, put it back in the envelope, and slid it under the receipts in the shoebox, where it lay face-down among the gas station slips and the hardware store charges like something buried alive.

"Nell?" Bram called down. "You want coffee?"

"I already had some," she called back, which was a lie, and heard herself add it to whatever ledger he kept, because she knew he kept one. She'd found it years ago—not a literal book, though sometimes she suspected a literal book—but a way of accounting, of noting every gap and filing it somewhere behind his eyes. She loved him for it and resented him for it and had built half her life on the assumption that his precision would never be pointed directly at the one load-bearing lie.

"Cup's fresh if you want it," he said, and came down with damp hair and his inspection vest over his arm, and kissed her temple, and asked nothing, because asking nothing was their native language.

* * *

She lasted four days.

Four days of gigs and groceries and Bram's reports spread across the dining table at night, his calipers and his little steel ruler, him murmuring measurements to himself like prayers. Four days of the shoebox in the pantry, second shelf, behind the spare coffee filters, where she'd moved the envelope because the kitchen table felt too exposed. Four days of sleeping next to her husband and lying awake listening to him breathe and thinking: *Theo is dying in a town I swore off at nineteen, and he is dying with my song in his mouth, and he asked the radio to play it, which means he wanted me to hear it, which means this was always going to end with a letter.*

On the fifth morning Bram left early for the Corliss Street deck, some emergency call about a bearing, and Nell took the envelope out and carried it to the backyard and stood at the fire pit Bram had built two summers ago, the one they used exactly twice a year, and she told herself she was going to burn it because burning things was what she did. She'd burned the notebook at nineteen. She'd burned photographs after the trial. Burning was her sacrament; it was how the Marchetti women handled evidence.

She lit the match herself, from the box by the grill, and watched the flame stand up straight in the windless morning, and she held the corner of the envelope to it and watched the paper catch, that beautiful terrible bloom of orange eating the postmark first, Marrow Falls going up in a curl of black lace, and she thought *good*, she thought *there*, she thought *it's done, it's ash, nobody summoned me, I am a woman with a career and a marriage and a fire pit and no brother*—

And then she smelled it. Not the smoke. Under the smoke. His handwriting burning, and some animal part of her brain—the part that had been nineteen once, the part that had struck the match the first time—screamed.

She dropped the envelope into the pit and watched it go and made herself stand there for a full minute, arms crossed, watching the flames do their thorough work, and then she went inside and ran the kitchen tap and filled a glass of water and drank it standing at the sink with the window over the basin looking out at the yard, at the fire pit, at the thin gray ribbon still rising.

That was when she saw it. In the basin. A single corner of paper, curled and black-edged, caught against the strainer of the drain—a scrap she must have torn loose without knowing, a fragment that had ridden in on her sleeve or her hair or simply chosen her, the way certain things choose you. She picked it up with two fingers, expecting nothing.

Three words survived. *before I can't*

She stood at the sink holding the fragment while the tap dripped, and the drip sounded like a metronome, and she thought about tempo, about how every song has a tempo whether you choose it or not, and hers had been set thirty years ago by a sixteen-year-old boy who said *I'll say I did it* in a kitchen that smelled like solder and coffee, and she had let him, because she was nineteen and terrified and her father had looked at her across that kitchen with an expression she had spent three decades failing to describe even to herself.

*Come before I can't explain.*

He hadn't written *come before I die*. He'd written *explain*. As if dying were the lesser problem. As if what he feared most was leaving the record uncorrected.

She turned on the disposal without thinking, some reflex toward destruction, and the machine roared and shuddered and the fragment was gone, ground to pulp, and she stood there in the sudden quiet feeling worse than before, because now the only copy of those words lived in her memory, and memory, she knew better than anyone, was a structure that failed under load.

* * *

By evening she had done something she couldn't undo, which was dig through the fire pit.

Bram wasn't home yet. The light was going amber and long across the yard, that late-September light that made everything look like a photograph of itself, and Nell knelt at the cold pit in her good jeans with a garden trowel and a pair of kitchen tongs, sifting ash like an archaeologist of her own cowardice. Most of it was powder. But paper doesn't always surrender completely; it curls into charred shells of itself, and if you're gentle—if you handle it the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing—you can sometimes read what's left.

She laid the fragments on a paper towel in order of recovery, which is to say randomly, and then began trying to fit them together on the patio stones, kneeling there in the failing light like a woman doing a jigsaw puzzle of her own execution warrant.

*I'm at St. Ambrose* — whole, legible, the address intact, which felt like a joke someone cruel was telling.

*They give me until the* — and then nothing, the rest eaten.

*Come* — alone, orphaned, the imperative surviving everything else.

And one piece she turned over four times before she understood it: a strip containing the bottom halves of letters, and when she held it at an angle to the last of the sun she could make out, faintly, ghosted in the pressure of pen on paper, the indentation of words from the page beneath—the letter had been written on a pad, and the pad had left its impression, and the impression read:

*you don't have to forgive me. I just need it said right, once, while I'm here to say it.*

Nell sat back on her heels in the ashes and the cooling yard and laughed—one short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman recognizing a family trait in the wild. *I just need it said right.* Theo. Thirty years gone and still the same boy who alphabetized his cassette tapes and corrected the liner notes in pen. Their father's son in the worst way: believing the record could be fixed if only someone got the facts down clean.

But she had it wrong, she realized. He wasn't their father's son in that. Their father wanted the record *rewritten*. Theo wanted it *written*. There was a difference, and the difference was the width of a fire, and she had spent her whole adult life on the wrong side of it.

Inside, headlights swept the driveway. Bram's truck. She had perhaps ninety seconds.

She gathered the fragments, folded them into the paper towel, and then—because she had learned something in four days and one burned letter—she did not hide them in the shoebox. She took an envelope from the drawer in Bram's study, one of his, manila and sturdy, the kind he used for sending reports, and she sealed the fragments inside it and wrote on the front, in her own handwriting, plainly, where anyone might read it:

*Marrow Falls.*

Then she put it in her guitar case, in the accessory compartment, under the capo and the spare strings, because a guitar case travels with her everywhere and Bram never opened it. Some sanctuaries are structural.

The screen door opened. "There you are," Bram said, coming out with two glasses of wine, and he stopped when he saw her—at the fire pit, ash on her knees, the trowel still lying there like evidence—and she watched his eyes do the thing they did, the measuring thing, cataloguing the scene, and she watched him decide, visibly, deliberately, not to ask.

"Burning old lyrics," she said. "Clearing out the notebook."

It was true the way a bridge deck is true—it held weight, barely, if you didn't overload it.

"Find anything worth keeping?" he asked, and handed her the glass, and his voice was gentle in the way she had learned to hear as both kindness and warning.

She looked at him—really looked, at this man who measured gaps in steel for a living and gaps in her sentences for free—and she thought about the impression in the paper, *while I'm here to say it*, and about a hospice in a river town giving a man until the leaves came down, and the leaves were already turning at the edges of their own yard.

"Yeah," she said. "One or two things."

She drank the wine. Inside, later, she would take out her guitar and play the song once, quietly, both verses, for the first time in thirty years, checking that her fingers still remembered what her mouth refused to say. And in the morning, when Bram left for work, she would stand in the doorway and watch his truck go and feel the strangest sensation, like a joint in a structure shifting under load—the feeling of a decision being made slowly, in the body, before the mind agrees to sign anything.

The letter was ash. The summons remained. Somewhere north, in a town that smelled like rust and river, a dying man was waiting on her answer, and the leaves were coming down.
