## 2. The Unrecorded

The song had a name. Ada learned it the way you learn anything that matters — sideways, at an hour when the world is not watching.

She called the station three times before anyone picked up. The first two attempts went to a machine with a voice like warm gravel announcing the request line hours, and Ada sat on the kitchen floor in her robe, phone against her ear, listening to that recorded voice repeat itself into the dark of her house while dawn came up gray behind the blinds. The third time, a man answered on the second ring, awake at 5:40 in the morning because, he said, some people are built for the blue hour and the rest of them just work there.

"WHKR, Harrow Bend. This is Delmar."

Ada had rehearsed nothing. She had planned to be casual, investigative, a woman with a journalist's light touch, but what came out was: "You played a song last night. Around one a.m. A woman singing with just a guitar."

Delmar made a sound like a man settling deeper into his chair. "The Pemberton tape," he said. "I get calls about that one every time I run it. Nobody knows what it is. Half of 'em think it's a lost Joni Mitchell demo."

"It's not," Ada said. Her voice did something strange on the way out — thinned, flattened, pressed between boards. "It's my mother."

The silence on the line was long enough that she could hear the station itself: a hum, a tick, somewhere a fan turning over air that probably smelled like burnt coffee and forty years of carpet.

"Well," Delmar said finally, gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing at the edge of something. "Then you'd better come see me, hadn't you."

* * *

He kept the cassette in a fireproof box under the board that held his turntables, which told Ada everything about how seriously he took it. The studio was two rooms above a hardware store on the main street of Harrow Bend, and it looked exactly as it sounded on air: cluttered, warm, lit like a confession booth. Delmar was sixty-something and shaped like a question mark, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and hands that handled the cassette as if it were a bird's egg.

"Arrived in March," he said. "No return address. Postmarked right here." He turned it over so she could read the label, written in ballpoint in a careful, slanted hand: *For whoever's still listening. Track one of three.*

Three. The words went through Ada like a nail finding a stud.

"There'll be more," she said.

"That's what I figured. Whoever sent it promised the next one if I played the first." He smiled, sheepish. "So I played it. Every Sunday at one a.m., for going on five months. You're the first person who ever knew what they were hearing."

Ada reached for the cassette and stopped herself, hand hovering. Her mother's handwriting. She knew it from grocery lists, from the margins of hymnals, from the notebooks — God, the notebooks, which no longer existed, which had gone into a oil drum in Uncle Raymond's backyard on a November afternoon when Ada was nineteen and stood watching the smoke with her father's hand heavy and helpless on her shoulder. She knew this handwriting the way you know a dead person's laugh when you hear it imitated on the street: instantly, painfully, from underneath.

But the label wasn't quite her mother's hand. It was *almost* her mother's hand. Someone who had watched her write for years. Someone who had loved watching.

"Who sent it?" Ada asked.

"If I knew, I'd tell you." Delmar set the cassette down and looked at her over his glasses. "But I'll tell you what I know, which is that it came from inside your family. The envelope had a note. One sentence." He pulled a folded scrap from a drawer, worn soft at the creases, and read it aloud: *"She wanted the songs to outlive the people who burned them."*

* * *

On the drive back — because of course she had driven to Harrow Bend the same day, three hours through rain that came and went like indecision — Ada let the highway unspool the past in front of her.

Her mother, Ruth, had written songs the way other people prayed: privately, constantly, and with the certainty that no one was listening. The notebooks lived in the piano bench, seven of them by the end, spiral-bound and water-warped, filled with lyrics and chord diagrams and little marginal notes in two hands — her mother's, and beneath hers, occasionally, a second handwriting answering hers like a call and response. *Try it slower here.* *This verse is the true one.* Ada had never asked who the second hand belonged to. Her mother had never offered. There were things in that house that were simply not discussed, the way weather in certain towns is never discussed because everyone is standing in it.

When Ruth died — quick, brutal, a winter pneumonia that took her in nine days — the estate fell to Raymond, her mother's older brother, because her father had signed things he didn't understand in the fog of grief, or perhaps because Raymond had put the pen in his hand and told him these papers were sympathy, were arrangement, were help. Ada was nineteen and didn't understand either until it was done: the royalties redirected, the publishing rights assigned to a company Raymond had quietly incorporated two years earlier, the house itself willed around her father in a trust he couldn't touch. When her father finally hired a lawyer, the lawyer found the signatures clean, the dates orderly, everything notarized by a man whose office sat above Raymond's insurance agency. The courts in that county moved like molasses moving uphill, and by the time anything could be contested, her father was dead too, and Ada had married Edmund partly, if she was honest, to have somewhere to stand that wasn't inside that story.

And the notebooks. That was the part she had never forgiven, the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed stone. Raymond had burned them. She'd asked him why, once, years later, at a funeral for someone else, and he'd said, with that easy charm that had always made strangers love him and family distrust him: "Ruthie wouldn't have wanted all that lying around. Private things. You know how she was." As if her mother's songs were shame. As if fire were tidiness.

Except now one of the songs was on the radio. Sung in her mother's voice — a voice Ada had not heard in fourteen years, and had not known existed outside her own memory. Recorded by someone with a guitar and a second handwriting. Someone who had kept copies. Someone who was mailing them, one by one, to a Sunday-night DJ in a town where everyone still told Raymond's version of Ruth Pemberton.

Someone who knew why the estate settled the way it did.

Ada gripped the wheel. The rain had stopped; the road shone like poured tin. She realized her jaw ached and unlocked it, breath by breath. Grief, she thought, is just anger that hasn't found its job yet. Hers had just been handed its assignment.

* * *

Edmund was home when she got there, which surprised her — he'd texted only *working late*, and usually that meant what it said. He met her in the kitchen with takeout containers he'd clearly not eaten from, and he looked at her face and did not ask *are you okay*, which was one of the thousand small mercies of him, and instead said:

"You went to Harrow Bend."

"I traced the broadcast." She hung her coat up. Took it down again. Couldn't remember why she'd hung it. "It's an AM station. The DJ has a cassette. My mother singing her own song, Edmund. A recording nobody knew existed. And there are three of them, mailed by somebody in the family, and the note said—" Her voice cracked on the last of it, and she hated that, and let it crack anyway. "The note said she wanted the songs to outlive the people who burned them."

Edmund stood very still. She watched him do the thing he did with bridges — assess load, check for stress fractures — except now the structure was her, and for once he seemed to know it.

"I'll come with you," he said. "Next week. I can take the days."

"No."

It came out flat and fast, before any better word could reach it. She saw it land. She saw it land the way a door lands — not slammed, exactly, but shut, the latch catching, the room on the other side suddenly elsewhere.

"Ada."

"The sale closes in two weeks. If I'm going to find out who has the other tapes, I have to be there, in that town, before Raymond signs the house away and the story gets sealed with it. And you—" She stopped. Started softer, because he deserved soft, because he had sat beside her at 1 a.m. and witnessed her come apart without flinching. "You measure gaps for a living. This isn't a gap. This is the whole bridge coming down, and I have to walk it alone or I won't know it holds."

Edmund nodded slowly, once, twice, a man accepting a load rating he didn't agree with. "Okay," he said. "Alone." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "Come home, though. Whatever you find. Come home."

She said yes. She meant it. But later, packing in the blue-dark of their bedroom, she caught herself looking at the suitcase and thinking a thought she immediately refused to finish: that some doors, once you hear them latch, you notice how quiet the hallway is on the other side.

The 6:10 bus out of the city was half empty and smelled of diesel and wet wool. Ada watched the bridges go by — Edmund's bridges, inspected, certified, sound — and then the city gave way to fields, and the fields to hills, and the hills, at last, to the long green shoulder of the valley where Harrow Bend lay waiting with its one radio tower, its closing sale, and its uncle.

In her bag, wrapped in a scarf, she carried a photograph of the piano bench. Taken the summer before her mother died. The lid open. Seven notebooks visible.

If even one page survived that fire, she would find it.
