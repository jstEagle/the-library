## 4. Practice Room 4

The rumor reached her the way everything reached her now — sideways, in fragments, carried by people who didn't know they were carrying anything at all.

Lior was emptying a trash bin on the third floor when she heard two students talking by the water fountain, their voices pitched low in that conspiratorial register students used when they believed the building was asleep. She kept her head down, kept wringing out the rag, and let the words come to her like weather.

"—Nakamura's piece, the one he's doing for juries. Nobody knows what it's called."

"Voss gave it to him. Before she got sick. He says she taught it to him measure by measure, wouldn't even write it down until he had it in his hands."

"She *gave* him an unpublished Voss?"

"That's what everyone says. That it's the last thing she ever—"

The fountain cut off. The students moved on, their footsteps dissolving down the corridor, and Lior stood with the rag dripping into the bucket, the water going cloudy around her knuckles.

*The last thing she ever.*

She knew the song before either of them said another word about it. She knew it the way you know your own pulse — not as information but as fact, as something happening inside you whether or not you consented. There was only one piece Ada Voss would have handed to a student without a name attached, only one melody that lived outside the catalog, outside the ledgers, outside every list of works the conservatory had ever printed. A song written at nineteen by a girl who played it exactly once for another human being, at midnight, in a practice room with the lights off because she couldn't bear to see her own hands while she played it.

A song that had never been recorded. Never published. Never performed.

Until now.

* * *

It took her three days to find out which room. Three days of moving through the building with her ears open, of pausing with her cart in corridors she had no other reason to be in, of listening through doors for a phrase she would have known anywhere — the ascending fourth that opened it, the way the left hand came in late, hesitating, like someone deciding whether to follow.

On Thursday evening she found it. Practice Room 4.

Of course it was Practice Room 4. The universe, if it had any sense of humor at all — and Lior had long suspected it did, a cruel one, patient as sediment — would have arranged nothing less. Room 4 was where she had taken her lessons with Ada in her second year. Room 4 was where Ada had first told her, tapping the fallboard with one lacquered nail, *You play like someone apologizing for taking up space. Stop it.* Room 4 was where Lior had sat afterward, alone, playing the same four measures for two hours until they stopped sounding like someone else's music and started sounding like hers.

The frosted glass in the door glowed amber under the corridor lights. Behind it, a shape moved — shoulders, arms, the particular forward lean of a pianist deep inside a phrase. And beneath the shape, muffled but unmistakable, the song.

Lior set her cart against the wall. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone cold around the mop handle.

She should walk away. That was the sane thing, the professional thing. She was staff. Staff did not stand outside practice rooms listening to students rehearse; staff cleaned up after them, wiped the smudges their foreheads left on the soundboards, collected the pencil shavings and the coffee cups and the occasional torn fingernail from between the keys. Staff were furniture that moved. Furniture did not listen.

She stood there anyway.

The song unfolded behind the glass, note-perfect, and each note landed somewhere in her chest like a stone dropped into still water. It was strange, hearing it in someone else's tempo. She had always taken the opening too fast — she could admit that now, twelve years and one career-collapse later — rushing toward the second theme the way she'd rushed toward everything back then, hungry, certain the good part was always ahead of her. This boy took it slowly. Almost tenderly. He let the opening fourth hang in the air like a question no one was in a hurry to answer, and when the left hand finally entered, it didn't hesitate so much as arrive, deliberate, a decision already made.

He had found something in it she hadn't known was there.

The fury came first. It came hot and total, rising through her like water through a wick, and for a moment she couldn't breathe around it. *Mine.* The word arrived whole, unbidden, ugly in its simplicity. That was hers. Nineteen years old, three in the morning, the conservatory's heating clanking in the walls, her fingers stiff with cold and feeling — that was hers, that midnight, those hours, the grief she'd poured into the middle section after her father's second diagnosis, the strange brightening at the end that she'd never been able to explain even to herself except as forgiveness for something that hadn't happened yet. No dying teacher had any right to give it away. No student had any right to play it. It had been heard once, by one person, under conditions of trust, and trust —

Trust. The word snagged. She stood in the corridor with her fury half-risen and looked at it, really looked, and saw underneath it the other thing, the older thing, the thing she'd been stepping over for twelve years: she had given it to Ada freely. Had played it for her at midnight precisely because Ada was the only person alive who would understand what it was. And Ada had kept it. Had kept it the way you keep a letter, the way you keep a promise — and now, apparently, the way you keep a will.

What right did Lior have to fury? She had abandoned the song herself. She had walked out of her own debut recital mid-phrase rather than finish it, had walked out of the conservatory, out of music, out of the entire life where that song existed, and had spent twelve years pretending the silence was a choice she'd made on purpose. You cannot abandon a child and then rage when someone else feeds it.

But knowing this did not kill the fury. It just made the fury more complicated, and complicated fury is worse than simple fury, because simple fury burns clean and complicated fury sits in you like wet coal, smoking.

Behind the glass, the boy stopped. Started again from measure thirty-two — the hard passage, the one where the harmonies slid sideways like ice shifting on a river. He fumbled it. Tried again. Fumbled it differently. On the third attempt he slowed it down to half speed, exaggerating each interval, and Lior's own fingers twitched against the mop handle, tracing the correction before he'd found it. *Thumb under earlier. Don't reach.* She caught herself mouthing it. She caught herself caring.

That was the worst part. Not that he was playing her song. That he was playing it *well*, and that some buried, unforgivable part of her leaned toward the door like a mother hearing her child laugh in another room.

* * *

She went back the next night. And the next.

She told herself it was coincidence, that her route happened to pass Room 4 at seven o'clock, that the third floor simply needed more attention this week. The lie was so thin she could see through it, but she wore it anyway, the way she wore the uniform, the way she wore the name tag that said H. WINTERBOURNE in block letters nobody read. Lies, she had learned, didn't need to be convincing. They only needed to be wearable.

The boy — Nakamura, Theo Nakamura, according to the rehearsal schedule taped beside the door — practiced the way she used to practice, or the way she liked to remember practicing: obsessively, unglamorously, repeating passages past the point of pleasure and into the territory where repetition becomes a form of prayer. He arrived at six-forty most evenings. He warmed up with scales played so quietly they sounded like rain. And then he worked on the song, always the song, forty minutes of it, fifty, his errors shrinking nightly, his phrasing growing teeth.

Through the frosted glass he was only a shadow, but shadows can be eloquent. She learned his posture. She learned the little forward dip he made before the coda, gathering himself. Once, on a Friday, she saw him stop mid-piece, sit motionless for a long moment, and then press both palms flat against the closed keyboard lid, the way you'd press your hands over a wound. She knew that gesture. She had invented that gesture, or thought she had, at nineteen, in this very room.

She began to hate him a little. She began, in the same breath, in the same heartbeat, to love him a little. Both feelings disgusted her.

On Sunday the building was nearly empty. Sunday nights the conservatory felt like a ship after the passengers had gone to bed — long corridors humming, radiators ticking, the whole structure breathing its old-building breath of dust and rosin and floor wax. Lior pushed her cart along the third-floor corridor slower than necessary, and when she reached Room 4 the light behind the frosted glass was on, and the song was already in progress.

She stopped. She told herself she'd stop after this phrase. Then after this section. Then, when he reached the coda, she'd keep walking, she absolutely would —

He played the coda the way she had never managed to play it. All those years ago she had rushed it, had driven it home like nailing shut a box, because the tenderness at the end frightened her — forgiveness for something that hadn't happened yet, how do you play that, how do you hold a note that belongs to a future self you don't believe in. But this boy held it. He held each chord past its written length, letting the overtones bloom and fade, and the ending didn't land so much as dissolve, the way fog lifts, the way a hand opens.

Lior stood in the empty corridor with her bucket and her mop and tears running silently down her face, and she did not make a sound, because making a sound would have meant admitting she was there, and admitting she was there would have meant answering the question of why, and she had no answer yet that wasn't shameful.

The music ended. The silence afterward was enormous.

And then, through the glass, she saw the boy turn his head. Toward the door. Toward her.

She couldn't say what betrayed her — the squeak of a shoe, the reflection of her cart's wheel in the corridor's polished floor, or something less mechanical, some animal awareness that performers have, that Ada had tried for years to teach her: *the room listens back, Lior, the room always listens back.* Whatever it was, the shadow behind the frosted glass went still, and stayed still, and Lior stood frozen on the other side of the door like a thief caught holding nothing, holding only her own attention, which suddenly felt like the most stolen thing in the building.

Then the shadow rose. Footsteps crossed the small room. The door handle turned.

Lior moved before she decided to move — backward, fast, grabbing the cart, retreating around the corner of the corridor with a gracelessness that would have shamed her if she'd had time for shame. She heard the door open fully. Heard the quiet of the practice room spill out into the corridor, smelling faintly of piano felt and cold coffee. Heard nothing else. No voice calling out. No footsteps following.

When she finally dared to look back, the corridor was empty, and the light in Room 4 had gone out.

But the door — she stared at it for a long moment, her heart slamming — the door had not been closed all the way. It stood open a hand's width, deliberately, the way you leave a door when you want whoever is standing outside to know that you know they're there, and that next time, they are welcome to come in.

Lior did not go in. She gripped her cart and walked, and the wheels rattled all the way down the corridor like a sentence being repeated.

That night, lying awake in her apartment above the dry cleaner, she understood two things with terrible clarity. The first was that the song had not survived despite her absence. It had survived *through* it — passed hand to hand like a coal that couldn't be allowed to die, and Ada had tended it, all these years, waiting for something. Waiting for whom?

The second thing was simpler and worse. Tomorrow evening, at six-forty, Theo Nakamura would unlock Practice Room 4 and warm up with scales that sounded like rain. And Lior Winterbourne, who had spent twelve years perfecting the art of almost entering rooms, would be standing outside that door again — unless she found, somewhere in herself, the courage to knock.

She fell asleep without deciding. But in the morning, dressing in her uniform, she caught herself checking the clock, and knew the decision had already begun to make itself.
