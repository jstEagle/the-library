## 7. The Visiting Hours

The care facility sat at the river's elbow, a long low building the color of wet sand, its windows facing the water as if the whole institution had been arranged to give its residents something to watch instead of think about. Lior had taken two buses to get there and still arrived forty minutes early, which she spent in the parking lot of a garden center next door, pretending to browse bare-root roses she had no intention of buying.

She told herself it was a routine visit. A wellness check on an elderly former teacher whose health, according to the conservatory newsletter — which she read in the break room with the same attention she gave to cleaning product labels — was "declining but stable." She told herself that janitors checked on things. It was practically her job description.

She did not tell herself why she had pressed her uniform flat that morning, or why she had bought the pears.

* * *

The lobby smelled of lilies and floor wax, and Lior recognized the wax immediately — a cheap industrial brand, slightly citrus, the kind that never quite dried and left every surface faintly tacky. Some part of her relaxed. Buildings like this one spoke a language she was fluent in: scuffed baseboards, the particular gray a mop-water bucket turns after an hour, the way receptionists' eyes catalogued visitors by class before they catalogued them by name.

"Visiting hours end at four," the woman at the desk said, without looking up from her crossword.

"I know."

"Who are you here for?"

Lior hesitated. "Ada Voss."

The pencil stopped moving. The woman looked up then, and her face did something complicated — recognition, curiosity, a flicker of what might have been pity. "Room 214. Second floor, river side." She slid a visitor's log across the counter. "She doesn't get many."

Lior signed. Her handwriting looked strange to her, looser than she remembered it, as if the hand belonged to someone who had been writing nothing but sign-in sheets and supply requisitions for twelve years. Which, she supposed, it was.

* * *

The corridor to Room 214 was lined with framed prints of famous paintings — Monet's water lilies, a Vermeer she couldn't name, all of them hung slightly crooked, all of them lit by fluorescent tubes that buzzed at a frequency just below hearing. Lior walked slowly. Outside the windows, the river moved in its unhurried way, brown-green and patient, carrying whatever it carried toward the sea without comment.

Ada Voss had taught her, once, that silence was not the absence of music but its container. *The rest is where the meaning lives,* Ada used to say, tapping the empty measures on the page. *Anyone can fill time. Only artists can hold it open.*

Lior had held twelve years open. She wondered if that counted.

Room 214's door stood ajar. Through the gap Lior could see a narrow bed, a window full of river light, and a wheelchair parked at an angle toward the glass, occupied.

Ada Voss had been small when Lior knew her — a compact woman who seemed assembled entirely of forward intention, spine straight as a metronome arm, hands that could silence a room by opening. The woman in the wheelchair was smaller still, folded into herself like a letter, wrapped in a shawl the color of moss. Her hair had gone fully white and was cut short now, practical, a stranger's haircut. But her hands rested on the arms of the chair in a position Lior knew intimately: loose, curved, hovering just above readiness, the resting posture of a pianist who has never quite agreed to stop.

Lior knocked on the open door anyway.

"You're late," Ada said, without turning around. "Or early. One of those. I've given up tracking which."

"It's Lior," Lior said. "Winterbourne."

"I know who it is." Ada still didn't turn. "I have ears. You walk like you're apologizing to the floor."

* * *

There were two chairs in the room, one clearly meant for visitors and one clearly claimed — stacked with newspapers, a cardigan, a paperback with a broken spine. Lior moved the newspapers to the windowsill and sat. From this angle she could see the river too, and understood the arrangement of the chair: Ada had positioned her guest so they would watch the water together rather than each other. Even now, even folded into a wheelchair, Ada Voss choreographed the room.

Lior set the pears on the bedside table. "These are from the market on Delancey. The one with the—"

"I know the one." Ada's voice was dry as old varnish. "You used to buy bruised ones because they were cheaper. You'd eat them over the sink so the juice wouldn't get on your scores." She paused. "That was a bad habit. Juice is acidic. So is shame."

"Is that what we're doing?" Lior asked. "Diagnosing my habits?"

"We're making conversation." Ada turned from the window then, and Lior braced herself for the full weight of those eyes — dark, quick, always three measures ahead of whatever you were playing. But the eyes that found her were slower than she remembered, clouded at the edges, though no less direct. They went over Lior's face the way Ada used to go over a student's fingering: noting everything, correcting nothing, yet.

"You look well," Ada said. "Tired, but well. There's a difference."

"I'm working at Halvorsen."

"I know that too."

"How?"

"The universe has a sense of humor, and I've always been one of its preferred correspondents." Ada's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "A nurse mentioned it. One of the night aides has a cousin on the custodial staff. Small world. Smaller profession." She lifted one hand from the chair's arm — the gesture cost her something, Lior could see, a fine tremor running through the wrist — and indicated the pears. "Are you going to cut one of those, or did you bring them purely as decoration?"

* * *

Lior cut a pear with the plastic knife from the tray on the dresser, and they ate in a silence that was not comfortable and not hostile but simply inhabited, the way two people can inhabit a room they both once lived in. The river light moved across the floor. Somewhere down the hall a television murmured game shows to no one. Ada ate her pear slices with a fork, delicately, refusing help, and Lior watched her hands and thought about how many hours she had spent watching them — demonstrating a chord voicing, tracing a phrase in the air, once, memorably, pressing flat against the lid of a piano to stop Lior mid-run and say, *You are playing at the music instead of through it. Again.*

"Why are you here, Lior?"

The question came without preamble, between slices, as if it were the most natural continuation of the meal.

"The newsletter said you weren't well."

"The newsletter also called Emil Brandt 'a visionary educator.' Newsletters are written by people paid to be wrong politely." Ada set down her fork. "Try again."

Lior looked at the river. A barge was passing, low and slow, loaded with gravel. "I heard the song," she said finally. "On the radio. In the recital hall. Someone was playing it — a recording, or a broadcast, I don't know. It was note-perfect."

Ada said nothing. The silence stretched, and Lior recognized its texture: this was Ada's pedagogical silence, the one she deployed when a student had said something true and needed to be made to stand inside it a moment longer.

"I wrote that song," Lior said. "At nineteen. I played it for you once. At midnight, in Practice Room 4, the night before my recital. And I never wrote it down. I never recorded it. No one else ever heard it." Her voice stayed level, which surprised her. "Except apparently everyone has heard it now."

"And?"

"*And?*" The word came out sharper than she intended. "Ada, that piece came out of me like — like blood. I don't even remember composing half of it. I was nineteen and miserable and it was the only true thing I ever made, and now some student is playing it because a dying teacher handed it around like—" She stopped. Breathed. "Like what? Like a party favor? Like a message?"

"Like a bottle," Ada said quietly. "With something in it."

"A message in a bottle needs an addressee."

"Yes," Ada said. "It does."

The barge cleared the window. The light changed, went silver, then ordinary again. Lior became aware that her hands had curled into fists in her lap and made herself open them, finger by finger, the way she'd been taught — *release the tension, Miss Winterbourne, the keys are not your enemy* — and found that the lesson applied to nothing anymore, or to everything.

"You could have sent it to me," Lior said. "Twelve years. You had my address. You had my mother's address. You could have written *one letter.*"

"To say what?" For the first time, something moved under the dryness of Ada's voice, something that might have been anger or grief or both wearing the same coat. "'Dear Lior, I have kept your song alive by feeding it to children because you threw away the only thing you ever made along with the only teacher who—'" She stopped herself. The sentence hung there, unfinished, its ending obvious to both of them.

"Say that," Lior said. "Finish that sentence."

"No." Ada picked up her fork again, examined a slice of pear as if it had personally disappointed her, and set it down. "You first. You've had twelve years to finish yours."

* * *

They circled each other for another hour. That was the only word Lior could find for it later — *circled*, like two animals negotiating the borders of a territory that had burned down years ago and neither would admit was gone.

Ada asked about her work at the conservatory and Lior answered in careful, neutral sentences — the east wing floors, the recital hall's temperamental radiator, the students who left coffee rings on the pianos. Ada listened with her head tilted, and Lior realized with a jolt that she was being assessed, that beneath the small talk ran the old current: *what has become of this person, what remains, what can still be built.*

Ada told her, in turn, about the facility in the tone of a critic reviewing a mediocre performance — the food ("aggressive blandness as a philosophy"), the physical therapist ("competent but rhythmless; everything he does is in four-four"), the view ("the river is doing its best"). She did not mention her diagnosis. She did not mention the future. When Lior asked how she was really doing, Ada said, "I'm dying, Lior, not stupid. There's a difference, though people confuse them constantly."

And through all of it, neither of them said the words. Not *Brandt*. Not *the compositions*. Not *the night you asked me what I was going to do and I said I didn't know and you looked at me like I had already answered*. Not *why didn't you fight*. Not *why didn't you make me*. The wound sat between them on the bedspread like a third visitor, acknowledged by its shadow, never by its name.

Once — only once — they came close. Ada had drifted into reminiscence, listing dead colleagues and demolished buildings, and she said, apropos of nothing anyone else would notice, "2009 was a good year until November."

Lior's mop-hand tightened around nothing. "Was it."

"October especially." Ada looked at the river. "The masterclass. That photograph they took — you remember the photograph. Both of us smiling like idiots. I looked at it for years afterward and tried to find the future in it. It wasn't there. Or it was there and I refused to read it." She turned back. "Did you ever see it? The photograph?"

"I saw it last week," Lior said. "In a storage closet. It's going to be thrown out."

"Good," Ada said. "Photographs lie by freezing. We were never that happy. We were only that unaware." She paused. "Which are you now?"

Lior stood, gathered the pear cores onto the plate, tidied the newspapers back onto the second chair — housekeeping reflexes, muscle memory of erasure — and found that her eyes were burning and that she would rather die than let them.

"Visiting hours end at four," she said.

"They end whenever you leave. That's the only rule that's ever mattered." Ada's hands had returned to their resting curve on the chair's arms. "Come again Thursday. The food is worse on Thursdays and I'll need something to complain about that isn't institutional."

Lior got as far as the door.

"Lior."

She turned. Ada was looking at her across the whole length of the small room, across the whole distance of twelve years, and her eyes had gone clear — completely, terrifyingly clear, the fog burned off, the old teacher standing at full height inside the ruined body.

"You always did come back through the service entrance," Ada said. "Even as a student. You'd come in through the loading dock rather than walk past the front office. I used to watch you do it and think, *there is a girl rehearsing for something.*" She let that sit. "I hope you're done rehearsing. The run-through is over. It's time for the performance."

Lior stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame and said nothing, because anything she said would have been either a confession or a lie, and she was no longer sure which one she could afford.

"Thursday," Ada said, and turned back to the river.

* * *

On the bus home, Lior sat by the window and watched the city assemble itself out of the dusk — streetlights coming on one by one, shopfronts glowing, the conservatory's tower visible for six blocks before the buildings swallowed it. Her phone buzzed against her leg. A message from the facilities supervisor, all business: *Schedule change — Brandt anniversary gala Friday night, all custodial staff on duty, formal service support. Uniform provided. Be there by 5.*

Lior stared at the screen for a long time. Twenty-five years of Emil Brandt. Champagne in the great hall. Students performing the stolen works while the man who stole them stood at the podium talking about originality. And her, in uniform, refilling glasses, invisible, the way she had been invisible at nineteen — present, complicit, holding the silence open like a rest that never resolves.

*The rest is where the meaning lives,* Ada had said.

Lior typed *Understood* and sent it, and then she sat with her forehead nearly touching the cold glass and watched her own reflection ride the dark streets beside her — a woman in a flattened uniform carrying a plastic bag of pears, rehearsing, always rehearsing, for something she still could not bring herself to begin.
