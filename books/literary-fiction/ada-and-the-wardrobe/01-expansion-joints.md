## 1. Expansion Joints

The crack in the south expansion joint had grown four millimeters since March.

Gideon Villalobos knew this the way other people knew their own pulse—not as a fact learned but as a condition inhabited. They knelt on the concrete deck of the Harrow Street Bridge at 6:47 in the morning, before traffic, before the sun had cleared the grain elevators, and pressed the tip of a crack gauge into the fissure like a doctor taking a wrist. Four millimeters. The number went into the logbook in Gideon's small, square handwriting, and the logbook went into the satchel, and the satchel would go into a filing cabinet in the municipal annex where it would be read by no one unless something fell down.

Thirty-one years of this. Twenty-two bridges under contract, though the Harrow Street was the one Gideon kept requesting back, and never examined too closely the reasons why. The paperwork said *senior inspector, continuity of assessment preferred*. What it meant was that Gideon had made themselves indispensable by being unremarkable, which was the only kind of safe there was.

They stood, knees cracking in sympathy with the structure, and walked the span.

The Harrow Street Bridge crossed the Callum River in a single tired arch, 214 feet of poured concrete and rusted rebar built in 1931 by men whose names were carved into a plaque nobody stopped to read anymore. Gideon had read it once. Had read it so many times as a child that they could still recite it in order: *Aldana. Baptiste. Okonkwo. Reyes.* A whole alphabet of families who had poured their summers into this arch and then scattered like spilled ball bearings when the work dried up.

Gideon did not look at the plaque now. There was a technique to not looking, and after thirty years they had perfected it: you let your eyes slide past the object the way water slides past a stone, acknowledging its existence only as resistance.

*Joint seven, hairline propagation, west face, 0.8 millimeters.*

*Spall, deck underside, station 4+20, exposed aggregate, no visible rebar corrosion.*

*Drainage scupper three, blocked, standing water, recommend clearing.*

The bridge spoke in damage and Gideon translated it into numbers, and the numbers went into the book, and the book told the truth about the bridge even though nothing else in Gideon's life did. This was, if Gideon had ever been honest enough to articulate it—and they were not—the thing they loved about inspection. Concrete could not lie. Concrete cracked exactly where it was hurt, wept exactly where it was wet, sagged exactly under what it carried. A bridge was the most honest thing Gideon had ever known, and they had arranged an entire life around listening to one.

The morning fog was burning off the river in long gray ropes. Below, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once been late for anything. Gideon leaned over the railing and watched it, and for eleven seconds allowed themselves to want nothing, fear nothing, remember nothing. Then a truck hit the expansion joint at the north approach with a sound like a rifle shot, and the wanting closed up again like a hand.

* * *

There was a smile Gideon used. It was famous, in the narrow sense that a few coworkers and one estranged sister could have attested to its existence. It was not unfriendly. That was the trick of it. It was warm the way a waiting room is warm—heated, lit, and designed to move you along. When the new hires asked Gideon questions, Gideon gave them the smile and answered precisely what was asked and nothing underneath it, and within a month they stopped asking. When the neighbors in 4B tried to chat by the mailboxes, the smile arrived like a door easing shut on a hydraulic arm. When Nell had called, years ago, in the last era when she called, and asked *are you ever going to tell me what's actually going on with you*, Gideon had smiled into the phone, which should have been impossible, and she must have heard it anyway, because she said, quietly, *right*, and hung up, and did not call again for six years.

The smile ended conversations. That was its function. Gideon had built it at twelve years old, out of the wreckage of a particular afternoon, and had maintained it since with the same diligence they brought to expansion joints: scheduled, thorough, never questioned.

Because here was the arithmetic Gideon lived by, though they'd have denied it under oath: attention cost. Every person who looked closely at you was a person who might see something. And Gideon had spent forty years making sure that if anyone ever looked—really looked—they would find nothing but competence. A man—or whatever Gideon was; the pronouns had sorted themselves out around thirty and Gideon had never made a fuss about it, another form of the same discipline—who inspected bridges flawlessly and wanted nothing and needed nothing and smiled correctly. A surface with no cracks worth gauging.

It worked. It worked so well that some mornings Gideon woke in the dark apartment on Delancey Street and felt less like a person than like a well-maintained asset awaiting assignment.

* * *

At nine they sat in the truck with the door open, drinking coffee gone bitter in the thermos, and performed the other ritual: the inventory.

Apartment: one bedroom, rented eleven years, walls bare except for a framed load-rating chart from the first bridge Gideon had ever certified alone. Furniture: functional, secondhand, arranged for efficiency of movement rather than comfort, because comfort invited lingering and lingering invited thinking. Food: the refrigerator held coffee, eggs, hot sauce, and the ghost of intentions. Social contacts: Doreen in the annex office, who had stopped asking Gideon to the holiday party four years ago and now simply left a plate of cookies on their desk with the tact of a woman feeding a stray she respected; Marcus, the junior inspector, who admired Gideon with a baffling, undiscouraged loyalty; and Nell, in Portland, who sent a card every birthday with a single line inside—*Still here if you ever want to talk*—and expected nothing, which was the cruelest kindness anyone had ever managed.

Family: one sister, alive, distant. Parents: gone, one by cancer and one by Florida, in that order. Friends: a category Gideon filed under *historical.*

The inventory took four minutes. It always took four minutes. Then Gideon capped the thermos, clipped the satchel, and went back to work, because work was the one room in the house of their life where the furniture never needed rearranging.

Under the arch, in the shade where the concrete sweated cool even in August, Gideon ran the sounding hammer along the spandrel wall, listening for the hollow note that meant delamination. Tap. Tap. Tap. Solid, solid, solid, hollow—there. They chalked a circle around it, neat as a signature. Somewhere above, traffic drummed across the deck, each vehicle a small confession of weight the bridge absorbed without complaint.

*You and me both,* Gideon thought, and then, annoyed at having thought it, wrote *spandrel wall, delaminated area approx. 0.3 m², monitor* in the logbook instead.

* * *

Lunch was a sandwich eaten standing up at the north abutment, because sitting down felt like conceding something. From there Gideon could see the whole span laid out against the sky, the arch reflected upside down in the slow brown river so that the bridge and its reflection formed a perfect eye, and Gideon standing on the bank in the middle of the pupil.

They had been coming to this bridge since before they could drive. Their father had hauled them along on weekends—*hold the light, mijo, hold it steady*—back when the bridge crew still worked out of the yellow shed by the east pier, back when the smell of the place was diesel and cut lumber and someone's mother's cooking drifting up from the shantytown of trailers and tarps the workers' families kept on the far bank. The company called it the camp. The workers called it something else, in a language Gideon had half-known once and lost on purpose, the way you lose a scar by growing new skin over it.

Gideon bit the sandwich and did not think about the camp. Did not think about Emeka's laugh, which had been so loud the herons used to lift off the shallows in protest. Did not think about the last summer, and the sound the world had made when it broke, which was actually very quiet—a man hitting water from height makes less noise than you'd think; it's the silence afterward that's loud.

Four millimeters since March. Everything else holding. Recommend continued monitoring.

That was the report on the bridge. Gideon had never written the report on themselves, but if they had, it would have read much the same: *structure aging, load-bearing capacity diminished, deterioration concealed beneath serviceable surface, recommend continued monitoring indefinitely, do not open to further traffic.*

* * *

The afternoon inspection took them onto the maintenance catwalk, the narrow steel walkway slung beneath the deck on brackets, sixty feet above the river. It was Gideon's favorite part of the job and the only admission of favoritism they permitted themselves, because under the deck there was no sky, no horizon, no people—just the ribs of the arch rising overhead like the inside of a whale, and the river murmuring below, and the particular cathedral quiet of structures that carry things.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

Solid, solid—

Singing.

Gideon stopped with the hammer raised.

It came from somewhere ahead, along the curve of the catwalk, thin and unhurried, a woman's voice working through a melody that didn't belong to any song Gideon knew. Not a hymn, not a standard. The words were neither English nor Spanish nor anything else Gideon could name, though something in the rhythm snagged on a hook forty years deep—a cadence, a rise and fall, like a voice they had heard across a worksite calling measurements or warnings or lunch.

The hammer lowered without Gideon deciding to lower it.

Nobody came under this bridge. That was the whole point of it. The city had fenced the approaches, the county had posted the signs, and the river itself discouraged swimmers with its cold brown current. In thirty-one years of inspections Gideon had encountered perhaps a dozen trespassers under the Harrow Street Bridge, and every one of them had been a teenager doing something stupid with a six-pack.

This voice was not a teenager's. It was old, and it was unafraid, and it sang like someone who had sung in this exact spot many times before and expected to sing here many times again—an expectation so at odds with everything the bridge was and was becoming that Gideon found themselves walking toward it before any decision had been made.

Around the curve of the arch, past rib four, the catwalk opened onto the little platform above the east pier, and there she was.

An elderly woman, small and straight-backed, silver hair braided close to her skull, standing at the rail with her face turned up toward the underside of the deck as if the concrete were a ceiling she owned. Her coat was good wool, decades old, brushed clean. She did not startle when Gideon's boots rang on the steel. She simply finished the phrase she was singing—let it resolve, the way you set down a tool properly instead of dropping it—and turned.

She looked at Gideon the way nobody had looked at Gideon in thirty years: directly, thoroughly, without hurry, as though reading a gauge.

"You," she said, in accented but precise English, "have your father's way of walking a catwalk. Heel first, always testing."

The river went on below. The traffic went on above. Gideon stood on the steel between them with a sounding hammer in one hand and thirty-one years of practiced nothing on their face, and felt, with genuine alarm, the smile begin to assemble itself—the warm, hydraulic, conversation-ending smile that had never once failed.

The woman watched it arrive. Something moved through her expression, brief and unreadable, like a fish passing under ice.

"Well," she said softly, in that language Gideon almost remembered, one word, two syllables, aimed at Gideon like a key at a lock. "So it's true. One of Aldana's children is still inspecting this bridge."

And she turned back to the rail, and lifted her face to the concrete, and began to sing again—as if Gideon were not a stranger, as if Gideon were not a threat, as if the question of whether Gideon would stay or go had already been answered somewhere Gideon hadn't been consulted.
