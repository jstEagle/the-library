## 6. First Lesson

The envelope sat on my passenger seat for two days before I drove it anywhere.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only honesty I've ever been good at. The envelope contained a formal notice of trespass, a copy of Ordinance 44-117 regarding unauthorized access to municipal structures, and a printed map of the Harrow Street Bridge with the public right-of-way highlighted in yellow. It was, in every particular, exactly what my supervisor had asked me to deliver. What it was not was a reason to drive forty minutes out of my route on a Tuesday morning when I had three culverts and a retaining wall also waiting for assessment.

I told myself it was efficiency. The bridge was already on my schedule. Delivering the notice personally would save the city a postage stamp and save Marisol Okonkwo the indignity of receiving certified mail like a criminal. I told myself I owed her that much courtesy after security had been called on her, after she'd stood on that catwalk singing to herself while a man with a radio decided she was a problem to be solved.

I told myself a lot of things that week. That's what routine is for. Routine is a story you tell yourself until you stop noticing you're the one telling it.

The morning was overcast, the kind of flat gray light that makes steel look honest—no glare to hide the rust blooms spreading along the truss members, no shadows to soften the section loss I'd been documenting in careful millimeters. I parked in the gravel pull-off below the east abutment, took the envelope, and did not take the clipboard, which should have told me something right there. An inspector without a clipboard is just a person walking.

She was already on the catwalk.

I don't know how she got up there. There's a locked gate at the base of the maintenance stair, or there's supposed to be—the lock has been broken since at least 2019, which I have noted in four consecutive reports, and which no one has ever acted on because noting things and acting on them are different departments entirely. She stood about thirty feet up, one hand resting on the railing the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of an old friend who'd gotten thin, and she wasn't singing this time. She was just looking at the river.

"You came back," she said, without turning around.

"I came to deliver paperwork." I held the envelope up as evidence. "Notice of trespass. If you're cited again, the city can bar you from the site permanently."

"And they sent you." Now she turned, and there it was—that expression I was beginning to recognize, the one that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. "Not a deputy. Not a letter. You."

"The mail is unreliable out here."

"Mm." She descended the stairs slowly, both hands on the rail, each step deliberate in a way that made me want to offer an arm and made me certain she'd refuse it. When she reached the bottom she took the envelope, weighed it once in her palm, and set it unopened on top of a concrete barrier block. "Thank you. Very official. Very frightening."

"It is serious. Third citation means—"

"Eleven," she said. "You found the eleven. I could see it in your face last time, that little twitch when I said the bridge belonged to my people. You went looking. You always go looking—you're a man who checks behind things." She said it without accusation, almost with approval, the way you'd compliment a dog for being well-trained. "So. Eleven citations in thirty years, and here you are delivering number twelve by hand instead of letting the machine do it. Why do you think that is?"

"Because I was in the area."

"You were not in the area. Nobody is in the area. That's rather the point of the area." She patted the envelope once, like it was a slow horse. "But all right. We'll let your reason stand. Reasons are load-bearing, even the bad ones. Especially the bad ones."

I should have left then. I had the professional satisfaction of a task completed, a defensible narrative, and a vehicle idling twenty feet away. Instead I heard myself say: "You said last time the bridge belongs to your people. Whose people?"

* * *

What she gave me first wasn't an answer. It was a word.

She climbed back up three steps of the stair so we were nearly eye level, and she settled there like a woman taking a seat she'd owned for decades. "My father worked this bridge," she said. "And his sister's husband, and four men from their congregation, and a dozen more whose names are in the pour stamps if you know where to look. They were guild men. Bridge builders—not the engineers with the drawings, the ones with the trowels and the wrenches and the rebar hooks. And the guild had a language."

"A language."

"A cant. A trade-tongue. Half Spanish from the Caribbean crews, half Yoruba from the West African ones, a spine of English underneath because you always need a spine. The bosses couldn't follow it. The bosses didn't need to, most days—but some days they very much needed not to be understood." Her eyes came to mine and stayed. "When the men voted to refuse an unsafe pour, they planned it in the cant. When they counted the shortcuts a foreman was taking, they counted them in the cant. It was a language built for the moments when speaking plainly would cost you your job. Or worse."

Words moved through me with no place to land. Somewhere under my sternum, old machinery shifted in its sleep.

"Most of it's gone now," she went on. "The men died, the children learned English at school and thought the cant was embarrassing, a secret code for poor people. My father kept it alive in the house; my mother pretended not to understand it, which meant she understood every word. And now—" she spread her hands, an economist presenting a final accounting, "—now it's me. I am the last mouth this language lives in. When I stop talking, it stops existing. There are recordings of dead languages in university libraries, inspector. Do you know what those are? They're languages nobody loved enough to carry."

The river ran beneath us, brown and patient. I gripped the railing because the railing was there.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you have careful hands." She nodded at them, wrapped around the steel. "I watched you work the other day, before security came. You touch a structure like it can feel you. Most men your age handle bridges like they're apologizing to them or blaming them. You listen." She paused. "I'm eighty-one years old. I don't have time to teach someone who isn't listening."

"Teach." The word came out flat. "Teach me what?"

"The cant. What's left of it. All of it, everything I have." She said it simply, the way you'd hand someone a heavy box—here, take this, no ceremony, mind your back. "Be my student. My last one."

* * *

I refused her.

I want that on the record too, since we're keeping records today. I looked at this old woman perched on a rusted staircase offering me the contents of her life, and I deployed the smile—the one I'd spent forty years perfecting, the one that starts at the correct muscles and reaches none of the correct places, the one that ends conversations the way a drawbridge ends a road.

"That's generous," I said, "but I'm not really— I inspect bridges. I don't speak languages. I appreciate the offer, though. Truly."

And she nodded, gracious as anything, and said, "Of course. Well. You know where to find me," and I walked to my truck and drove away with the envelope still sitting unopened on the barrier block, and I told myself the matter was closed.

It was not closed. Here is what happened instead.

That night I ate standing at my kitchen counter, as I do, and I caught myself saying a word under my breath. *Ojú-méjì.* Two eyes. She'd used it in passing, the way you'd leave a coin on a counter—she'd pointed at the joint where the main girder meets the pier and said, "In the cant, we called that spot *ojú-méjì*. Two eyes. Because it watches both ways." Just that. A passing thing. A crumb.

I said it again while washing my single plate. I said it in the shower. I lay in bed at eleven-forty and said it into the dark ceiling of my apartment, and the apartment—which contains one bed, one chair, four hundred and six books arranged by height, and a refrigerator holding condiments and regret—did not answer, and the word sat in my chest like a second heartbeat.

There is a word, she'd said, for the weight a thing carries quietly. She hadn't even taught it to me yet. She'd only mentioned that it existed, the way you mention a room in a house. And I wanted it. God help me, I wanted it the way a man wants water, without dignity, without explanation—and I could not stop thinking about why a language invented by striking workers would need a word for silent weight. What kind of people need that word so badly they build it to last?

People like the ones I grew up around. People whose voices I had spent forty years not hearing.

On Thursday I drove back to the bridge. I brought the paperwork again, because a man needs a reason, even a bad one. Especially a bad one.

* * *

She was on the catwalk again, of course. I'm convinced she slept up there some nights, though I never caught her at it. When my boots hit the first stair she said, without turning, "Ah. The mail service returns," and I felt the gloat radiating off her like heat off summer asphalt, and I felt her deciding, magnanimously, not to name it.

"I brought you a duplicate copy of the notice," I said. "In case you lost the first one."

"I lost nothing in thirty years on this bridge except friends." She turned and looked at the envelope, then at me, and let the silence do its work. Then: "Well. Since you're here, and since you've clearly come to learn and are only pretending otherwise—we may as well begin. Sit."

There was nothing to sit on. I leaned against the railing beside her, close enough to see the rust scale flaking off the top chord, and she began the way a good teacher begins: not with grammar, but with greeting.

"In the cant, you don't say hello," she said. "*Hello* assumes nothing has passed between you. The cant knows better. You say *kú ilé*—it means roughly, 'greetings to what stands.' You say it to a person, you say it to a bridge, you say it to a morning. Everything that holds you up deserves greeting." She demonstrated, and the words came out of her rounded and warm, vowels worn smooth like river stones. "*Kú ilé.*"

"*Kú ilé,*" I repeated.

"Better than I expected. Your mouth wants to rush it. Don't. This language was spoken by tired men at the end of double shifts—it moves at the speed of exhaustion, which is to say, honestly." She had me say it five times. On the fifth, something loosened in my jaw that I hadn't known was clenched. "Good. Now. The next word is the important one, and I teach it early because it sorts people. Some hear it and feel nothing. Those, I send home." She turned to face me fully, both hands on the rail. "The word is *àròyè*."

The sound went through me like current through wet steel.

I knew it. I knew it before she finished shaping it—I knew the shape of it in a grown man's throat, cracked with rage and grief, shouted across a work deck in a summer I had buried so deep I'd convinced myself the grave was empty. *Àròyè!* Someone screaming it, screaming it at the crowd, at the foreman, at the sky. Screaming it the day Emeka fell.

"What does it mean," I said, and my voice came out level, clinical, the inspector's voice, the voice I built for exactly this purpose, and I marveled distantly at how well it worked even now.

"Witness," she said. "One who sees. But it's heavier than our English word—a witness in the cant isn't someone who watches. Anyone can watch. *Àròyè* is one who sees and is therefore bound. To witness something in the cant is to carry it. You become responsible for what you saw, whether you speak or stay silent. Silence doesn't free you from witnessing. Nothing does." She studied my face. "It was the gravest word we had. When a man shouted *àròyè* across a job site, it meant: you saw what happened here. All of you saw. Now decide what you'll be."

The river kept moving. My hands on the railing were perfectly steady, because I had trained them, because I had trained everything, because for forty years I had built a self the way you build a false wall in front of a real one—load-bearing, seamless, inspected annually and found sound.

"Where did you hear that word?" Marisol asked softly. "Before just now. You recognized it. I watched you recognize it."

"I grew up near a lot of bridges," I said. "Construction sites. You pick things up."

"You pick things up," she repeated, tasting the lie the way you'd test suspect mortar—pressing her thumb into it, checking for give. She didn't push. That was the terrifying thing about her; she never pushed. She just left her thumbprint in the lie and moved on, and you knew she'd find it again later. "Well. *Àròyè*, then. Say it."

"*Àròyè.*"

"Again. It's yours now. That's how the cant works—you can't return a word once it's been given. It lives in you whether you feed it or starve it."

I said it again. And somewhere beneath the gray water, beneath forty years of silt, something that had been lying very still rolled over and began, slowly, to rise.

She dismissed me gently after that, pleading tiredness, and I descended the stairs with the duplicate notice still in my hand, undelivered, unnecessary, a prop from a play we were both finished performing. At the bottom I stopped and looked back up at her small figure against the trusswork.

"Same time Thursday?" she called down.

"I have inspections Thursday."

"You have inspections every day. That's what makes you easy to find." And she added, in the cant, soft enough that I almost missed it: "*Kú ilé,* Gideon."

Greetings to what stands.

I drove back to the office and sat in the parking lot for twenty minutes with the engine off, doing what I do best, which is nothing, which is watching, which is witnessing—and for the first time in forty years, the word for it felt like a debt coming due.
