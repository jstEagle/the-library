## 4. Inventory: Front Hall

The key Marisol had pressed into my hand the week before was brass gone soft at the edges, worn the way a river stone is worn — by repetition, by hands that no longer existed. I recognized it the way you recognize a face in an old photograph: with a small, involuntary flinch.

I let myself into the house on Rue Sabine at nine in the morning, which was already a violation of everything I believed about the place. The house belonged to night. It had always belonged to night. Even as a child I understood this without having words for it — that the rooms held their breath after dark, that the floorboards spoke only when they thought no one was listening, that Theo's laughter sounded different at two in the morning than it did at noon, thinner and more secret, like something being smuggled across a border.

Now it was morning, and the house looked embarrassed to be seen.

Dust lay over everything with the patience of snowfall. Not the aggressive dust of abandonment but the gentle kind, the kind that accumulates in a home where someone has simply stopped moving through it. It softened the edges of the hall table, blurred the spines of books nobody had reshelved, turned the mirror above the coat rack into a fogged window looking out onto a country that no longer existed.

I stood in the front hall with my clipboard and my boxes of catalog cards and my archival pencils (never ink; ink presumes permanence, and I have never trusted myself with permanence) and I thought: *I can do this. This is just a collection. Collections are my native language.*

This is what twenty years of night shifts teach you. They teach you that every object is a record, and every record can be classified, and classification is a form of love so controlled it doesn't burn anyone. I had spent two decades shelving other people's returns at three in the morning, learning the Dewey decimal system the way some people learn prayers — not for its meaning but for its order, its promise that everything, given time, has a place it belongs.

So: the front hall. Accession number one.

*Item 001: Coat rack, walnut, six hooks, c. 1968.* My mother's handwriting era. She had bought it secondhand from a woman who was moving to Lyon, and she had carried it home herself, four blocks, walking backward half the time because she refused to ask Theo — who was eleven and eager — to help. *Provenance:* purchased before my birth. *Condition:* stable; finish worn at shoulder height where coats hung heaviest. *Notes:* the fourth hook from the left still holds a scarf.

I wrote that last part because it was true, and then I stood there for a long moment because writing it made it more true than it had been a second earlier.

The scarf was my mother's, oxblood wool, moth-eaten at one corner. It had hung there so long it had become architectural. Removing it would be like removing a load-bearing wall. I catalogued it as *attached fixture, pending review,* which is librarian language for *I am not ready.*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about inventory work: it is grief performed at the pace of paperwork. You cannot rush it. You cannot skip ahead. The object in your hand demands to be described, and description requires attention, and attention is the cruelest gift you can give to a thing you're trying not to feel.

I worked the left wall first. Console table, marble-topped, chipped at the corner where Theo had fallen against it during a game of indoor soccer that our mother had forbidden and we had played anyway. I remembered the chip appearing. I remembered the sound — a bright ceramic *tock* — and Theo's face going white, and then both of us conspiring to move a ceramic bowl two inches to the left to hide the damage, a conspiracy that lasted exactly four days before our mother noticed, because our mother noticed everything except the things that mattered.

*Item 004: Ceramic bowl, blue glaze, hairline crack.* I did not write down what it had been used to hide. There is no field on the card for that.

The umbrella stand. Two umbrellas, neither of them ours — one left by a plumber in 1994, probably, one that might have belonged to a neighbor whose name I couldn't retrieve, which bothered me professionally. A librarian hates losing a name. Names are the whole trade.

And then the telephone.

It sat on the hall table's lower shelf, a cream-colored rotary phone with a coiled cord that had long since surrendered its shape, hanging in gray loops like something that had tried to climb out of itself. Our phone. The house phone. The phone that rang for birthday invitations and wrong numbers and Aunt Camille calling long-distance from Marseille to say nothing in particular for forty minutes.

The phone that did not ring the night Theo died.

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have. That night — the night I do not yet have the sentence for, the night I have spent twenty years not-saying — the phone sat on that shelf, six feet from the front door, twelve feet from the stairs, working perfectly. I know it worked because I had used it that afternoon, called a school friend about homework I had no intention of doing. The dial tone had been clear. The cord had reached as far as the kitchen doorway if you pulled it taut, which Theo and I had done once, out of boredom, until our mother shouted that we'd break it.

Nobody broke it. Nobody used it. On the night it mattered most, the phone in the front hall of the Delacroix house performed its function flawlessly by remaining silent, and everyone in the family agreed — agreed, the way you agree to a ceasefire — that there had simply been no one to call.

I catalogued it. What else was I going to do?

*Item 007: Telephone, rotary, cream enamel, Western Electric pattern. Functional as of last use. Cord frayed. Provenance: household installation, date unknown. Notes: retained for historical value.*

*Historical value.* I looked at those two words for a long time. Somewhere in the building of me, a person had decided that the way to survive unbearable things was to describe them in the passive voice of institutional paperwork, and now that person was standing in her dead brother's front hall writing *retained for historical value* about the phone that could have saved him, or could have summoned someone, or could have — and here my hand stopped moving — could have changed whatever it was that actually happened, if only anyone had thought to lift the receiver.

But that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question I had carried since I was fourteen, wrapped in cloth, buried under shelves, kept alive the way you keep a seed alive — dark, dry, barely.

I put the card in the box. I moved to the next item.

* * *

By noon I had catalogued seventeen objects and eaten half a sandwich I didn't remember making. Grief does that. It eats alongside you without contributing to the grocery bill.

The light in the front hall changed around one o'clock. Morning light comes through the transom window above the door in a hard white bar that crawls up the wall as the hours pass; by early afternoon it reaches the coat rack and sets the dust motes turning, hundreds of them, slow and golden, like the room is thinking. As a child I had watched that bar of light move the way other children watched television. It was the house's clock, its calendar, its slow announcement of the day passing. Theo used to put his hand in it and make shadow animals on the wall — a dog, mostly, always a dog, his hands were better at dogs than anything else.

I caught myself doing it. Standing in the light bar, fingers arranged into something between a dog and a failure, casting a wobbling shape on the wall where his had been.

I stopped. I wrote a card instead.

*Item 018: Transom window, original glass, one pane cracked (lower left). Light enters west wall, 10:40 a.m.–2:15 p.m., seasonal variation. Notes: favored by children of the household for shadow-play.*

That word, *children.* Plural. Present tense avoided, past tense implied, and somewhere in the grammar a boy dissolved politely into a category.

You see how it works? You see what twenty years of practice produces? I could turn anything into a catalogue entry. I could turn my own heartbeat into a catalogue entry. *Specimen: pulse, elevated, 96 bpm, cause environmental.* It is a magnificent skill and it is also, I understood clearly by mid-afternoon, a disease. The unsaid sentence doesn't die of neglect. It goes into the collection. It gets a number. And collections, unlike feelings, are built to last.

* * *

Marisol arrived at three, letting herself in with her own copy of the key, bringing with her the smell of cigarette smoke and department-store perfume and a paper bag of pastries she set on the hall table without ceremony.

"You look like you've been excavating," she said.

"I have been excavating."

She peered into my box of finished cards, and I felt the particular vulnerability of a writer whose drafts have been read. Her eyebrows went up at Item 007. "You catalogued the phone."

"It's an object of the estate."

"It's a phone, Ines."

"It's a phone that—" I stopped. Recalibrated. Chose, with the smoothness of long practice, a different ending. "—that hasn't rung in years. Dust in the dial mechanism, probably."

Marisol looked at me for a moment too long. She has her mother's eyes, wide-set and dark, eyes that were designed for noticing and had never once been apologized to. Then she shrugged and handed me a pastry and said, "The auctioneer wants the inventory in five weeks. Lot numbers, estimated values, condition reports. He said sentimental items should be flagged separately." She bit into her own pastry. "I told him this whole house is a sentimental item."

"He wasn't amused?"

"He fired me as a client for six minutes and then called back."

Despite everything — despite the dust and the phone and the scarf on the fourth hook — I laughed. It came out rusty, a door hinge in a house nobody lived in, but it came out. Marisol nodded as if she'd been paid to produce it.

We worked the right side of the hall together for an hour. She called out items; I wrote cards. She was faster than me and less careful, and twice she picked up objects I hadn't gotten to and turned them over in her hands with a familiarity that startled me — she'd spent childhood summers here, I remembered, back when the family still pretended to be a family. She knew where the loose floorboard was. She knew the hall closet stuck unless you lifted the door while pushing. She knew, and said casually, that our grandmother had hidden money in the umbrella stand, and shook it hopefully, and found nothing.

"Did you ever go up to Theo's room?" she asked, out of nowhere, holding a picture frame she hadn't yet shown me.

"No," I said.

"Liar," she said, not unkindly. "Everyone went up to Theo's room. It was the best room in the house. It faced the pond."

The frame came around. In it, a photograph I had forgotten existed: Theo at thirteen, squinting into summer sun at the edge of the water, one hand raised against the glare, mouth open mid-shout, mid-laugh, mid-word. Behind him the pond lay flat and silver and patient, the way it always did, the way it still does, keeping whatever it keeps.

"Lot number?" Marisol asked.

My pencil hovered over the card. The bar of afternoon light had reached the coat rack. The dust turned. Somewhere upstairs, in a house with no one in it, a floorboard settled with a sound exactly like a footstep, and then another, descending.

Neither of us said anything. But I saw Marisol's head come up, and I saw her look at the stairs, and I saw her decide — visibly, deliberately, the way you decide to swallow — not to say what we were both thinking.

"Theo's room tomorrow," she said instead. "Start at the top of the house and work down. That's how the auctioneer wants it listed."

"That's not how houses are inventoried," I said. "You start at the door."

"Well," said Marisol, picking up her keys, "we're not starting at the door."

She left. The house closed behind her like a book. And I stood in the front hall with the photograph of my brother shouting at the sun, and I understood that she knew something, or suspected something, or was simply afraid of the same room I was afraid of — and that in five weeks none of it would matter, because the house would belong to strangers, and the strangers would carry everything out through this exact doorway, past the coat rack, past the scarf, past the silent phone, and the door would close behind them too.

Upstairs, the floorboards were quiet again.

I wrote the lot number on the photograph's card. Then, beneath *notes,* in pencil, in handwriting steadier than I felt, I added a line I had no professional justification for:

*Subject appears to be saying something. Audio not preserved.*
