## 5. Mother, Declining to Comment

The bus to Fairhaven Gardens took forty minutes and smelled of diesel and someone's microwaved fish. Ines rode it with a canvas tote containing three things: a paperback they had no intention of reading, a thermos of coffee gone lukewarm by the second stop, and the certified letter, folded into quarters so many times it had developed a topography, little mountain ranges of crease that Ines kept smoothing and refolding like a worry stone.

They had not called ahead. Calling ahead gave Odile time to build fortifications. Odile Delacroix did not answer questions; she answered *approaches*, and she could repel an approach from a hundred yards out if she saw it coming down the drive. So Ines simply appeared, on a Tuesday, at 2:15 in the afternoon — the hour when the residents of Fairhaven Gardens were digesting lunch and the staff were digesting their own private despair, and nobody had energy for drama.

The lobby smelled of lavender and floor wax, an alliance of scents that had never once made peace with each other. A receptionist with a name tag reading BRENDA looked up.

"I'm here to see Odile Delacroix."

"Are you family?"

"I'm her child."

Brenda's face performed a small, professional sympathy. "She's in the sunroom. She's been — " Brenda paused, choosing her word the way Ines chose catalog headings — "lively today."

*Lively.* Ines knew what that meant. Lively meant Odile had already corrected someone's grammar, reorganized a jigsaw puzzle by edge pieces, and told the man from room 114 that his stories about Korea got longer every year while his facts got shorter. Lively meant her mother was armed.

* * *

Odile was installed in a wingback chair by the window with a view of the parking lot, which she had described, on previous visits, as "a study in human hope — everyone arriving convinced this visit will be different." She was doing a crossword in pen. Of course in pen. Odile had never in her life made a mark she wasn't prepared to defend.

She looked up over her reading glasses and did not smile.

"They let anyone in here now," she said. "I thought I saw a raccoon in the kitchen last week but apparently that was Gerald."

"It's good to see you too, Maman."

"Don't 'Maman' me in English sentences. Pick a language and commit." She set down the crossword. Her hands were smaller than Ines remembered, or perhaps Ines had simply never looked. Twenty years of looking away will do that. "You're thin. Are you eating, or are you one of those people who considers coffee a food group?"

"Coffee has antioxidants."

"So does hemlock. Sit down before you hover. You hover like your aunt Colette, God rot her."

Ines sat. The chair across from Odile was positioned, Ines noticed, exactly far enough from the wingback to make leaning forward conspiratorially impossible. Odile had arranged the furniture of her life the same way. Nothing within reach that could be used against her.

For a while they did the thing they always did, the dance both of them knew by heart: the weather, the food, the nurse Odile liked ("the only competent person in this building"), the nurse she didn't ("a child playing dress-up in scrubs"). Ines reported on the library. Odile reported on the indignities of assisted living, delivered in a deadpan so dry it could have struck sparks off the linoleum.

"The woman in 109," Odile said, "has taken up watercolor. She paints the same birch tree every day. Forty-one birch trees now, all identical, all terrible. I told her Monet painted the same cathedral repeatedly because the light changed. She said the light in her painting doesn't change because she closes the curtains." Odile's mouth did something at the corner that was almost, almost a smile. "I respect it, actually. Total artistic control through total darkness. There's a lesson there."

"There usually is, when you insult someone."

"I don't insult. I observe with consequences." She picked the crossword back up, then set it down again, which for Odile was the equivalent of fidgeting. "You didn't come here on a Tuesday for my health. You come Thursdays for my health, when the fish isn't served, because you claim the smell follows you home. Something's happened."

Ines had rehearsed this. On index cards, actually — old habit, the cards they wrote sentences on and never sent, the practice runs for conversations that never quite happened. They had drafted four openings. All four evaporated now, and what came out instead was the plainest version:

"The house is being sold. Marisol's auctioning it. Six weeks."

The pen stopped moving.

It was a small thing, the stopping. Anyone else would have missed it. But Ines had spent twenty years reading silences professionally — shelving books at 3 a.m. teaches you that a library is mostly silence arranged around occasional noise, and you learn to hear the difference between a silence that is resting and a silence that is working. This silence was working. It was hauling something heavy across the room between them.

"Sell it all," Odile said.

Too fast. That was the thing. Too fast, too smooth, the words pre-loaded like rounds in a magazine. *Sell it all* arrived without a single syllable of hesitation, and everything else Odile said came with hesitation, came with editorial review, came with the visible machinery of a woman who had spent seventy years making sure no sentence left her mouth without passing inspection. *Sell it all* had been waiting somewhere. Ines wondered, with a coldness spreading under their ribs, how long it had been waiting.

"That's it?" Ines said. "No commentary? No lecture about how Marisol couldn't organize a garage sale in an empty garage?"

"Marisol couldn't organize a garage sale in an empty garage. There. Commentary provided." But the pen stayed down. "What do you want me to say, Inès? It's a house. Houses are wood and bad plumbing and memories nobody needs at that price per square foot."

"You haven't been back since — "

"I've been back plenty of times. In my head. It's a terrible neighborhood. The commute is unbearable." She reached for the crossword again and this time Ines watched her hand, watched it find the pen and miss, watch her correct without acknowledging the correction. "Six weeks, you said."

"In June. The listing went up yesterday. There are photographs."

"Photographs." Odile said the word the way other people said *mold*. "Of what, the water stains? The porch where your father sat pretending to read the paper he'd already finished? Photographs of a house are like photographs of a hospital bed. Technically accurate. Completely beside the point."

"Maman."

"What."

"Why didn't you tell me Marisol contacted you?"

The silence again. Working. Hauling.

"She didn't contact me," Odile said. "Why would she contact me? I'm in a building that smells like lavender fighting floor wax. I'm not exactly the executor type anymore."

"She told me she sent you a letter. In March."

"A letter." Odile turned to the window, to the parking lot and its study in human hope. "This place loses mail. Half of it ends up in Gerald's room. He hoards it. He thinks he's still running a post office in Da Nang." She said it lightly, breezily, and Ines heard the breeze for what it was: air moving fast over something very still.

"Gerald's mail hoard was cleaned out in April," Ines said quietly. "Brenda told me. Everything got sorted and delivered. If a letter came in March, you got it in April."

For the first time since Ines had sat down, Odile looked directly at her child. Her eyes were still sharp, still the eyes that had once ended arguments with a single raised eyebrow, but there was something behind the sharpness now, something moving, like fish under ice.

"You've become a detective," she said. "All those years among the books. Dewey Decimal turns people into little policemen. Everything filed, everything cross-referenced."

"Somebody has to keep the records straight."

"Yes," Odile said. "Somebody always does." And then, briskly, terribly, she changed the subject. "Did you eat lunch? They do a passable quiche on Tuesdays if you get there before the woman in 109 picks all the crusts off hers. She thinks the crust goes to her hips. She's eighty-six. Where does she imagine it's going, Inès? At this point fat cells should be getting pensions."

* * *

Odile Delacroix had spent the morning losing an argument with a crossword.

Seven across: *What the past does, eventually* (five letters). She'd written FADES, crossed it out, written HAUNT, crossed that out too. The clue was badly constructed anyway. Crosswords had gotten sloppy, clue-writers afraid of difficulty, allergic to precision. In her day —

In her day. Her days were stacked behind her now like unread newspapers, and the trouble with newspapers was that they kept. She'd learned that the hard way, or she would learn it soon, though sitting in the sunroom with her pen and her pride she knew only that the letter from Marisol was in the drawer of her nightstand, beneath the Bible she never read and the photograph she never moved, and that she had not opened it, and that she had known its contents before opening it, the way you know weather from the ache in a joint.

*Sell it all.*

She'd said it fast on purpose. Speed was camouflage. Odile had built a whole life on the principle that the fastest answer is the least examined one, that if you move quickly enough past a wound nobody sees you flinch. It had worked for decades. It had worked the night it mattered most, God forgive her, it had worked beautifully, the whole town nodding along, *terrible accident, such a shame, boys and water,* and she had stood at the funeral in her black coat thinking: *this is the last sentence anyone will ever need about my son, and I am the one who chose it.*

She had chosen it the way you choose a tarp over a leaking roof. Not because it fixed anything. Because it stopped the rain from coming inside.

Now her child was sitting across from her asking questions in that flat librarian voice, and Odile recognized the voice, that was the hell of it. It was Theo's voice. Theo had the same trick, the same quiet way of asking a question that was really a door left open, patient, certain you'd walk through it eventually. And look where patience had gotten him. Look where the door led.

"You've gone somewhere," Ines said.

"I go places constantly. It's called having an interior life. You should try it, you've clearly been squatting in the exterior one for years." She tapped the crossword. "Seven across. What the past does, eventually. Five letters. Your generation is supposed to be good at riddles."

"Lingers," Ines said.

Odile looked at the grid. L-I-N-G-E-R-S. It fit. Of course it fit. She wrote it in pen, because she was Odile Delacroix, and she did not make marks she wasn't prepared to defend, and then she sat looking at the five letters as if they'd been left there by someone else.

"The house should have sold years ago," she said. "After the funeral. I said so. Everyone acted like I was monstrous. Your father wouldn't even discuss it. He sat on that porch until he died sitting on it, more or less, and then it was just me rattling around in a museum of things nobody wanted." She paused. "Including me. Rattling. Museum. You can work out the metaphor yourself, you're the one with the literature degree."

"You could have sold it yourself. Any time. You owned it outright after Papa."

"Yes, well." Odile aligned the pen with the edge of the crossword, a small act of order imposed on a disorderly world. "There were reasons."

"What reasons?"

"Reasons, Inès. Plural. With texture. Not everything fits on an index card." She hadn't meant to say that last part. It slipped out sideways, the way truth does when you've been holding it too long and your grip tires. She saw her child register it — saw the stillness come over Ines, the particular stillness of a person who catalogs things for a living recognizing a misfiled item.

"What index card?" Ines asked.

"No index card. Figure of speech."

"You've never once used a figure of speech about index cards in your life. You think index cards are vulgar. You told me in 1998 that writing thoughts on index cards was 'emotional bookkeeping,' and that emotional bookkeeping was how cowards avoided paragraphs."

Odile almost laughed. Almost. It caught somewhere in her chest and dissolved. "You remember everything. It's exhausting, being loved by someone with a perfect memory. No privacy at all."

"I'm not perfect. I forget plenty."

"Name one thing."

Ines was quiet a moment. "I forget to send things," they said. "Cards. Letters. Sentences. I write them and I keep them and I never send them. Twenty years of unsent mail in a shoebox under my bed."

"And why is that, do you suppose?"

"I assumed it was a flaw."

"It's a policy," Odile said. "A very sensible one. Unsent letters can't be answered." She said it lightly, and then heard herself, and the lightness curdled. She turned back to the window. A car pulled into the lot below, some daughter visiting some mother, arriving convinced this visit would be different.

"Maman," Ines said. "When I mentioned the auction. You answered before I finished the sentence."

"I'm efficient."

"You answered like someone who'd been practicing."

The sunroom noise went on around them — the television murmuring game shows, the birch-tree painter arguing cheerfully with a nurse about turpentine — but at the center of it, at the small table between the wingback chairs, there was a pocket of quiet with two people in it and thirty years of held breath.

"Practicing," Odile repeated. "Listen to you. Cataloguing your own mother." She gathered the crossword, the pen, the reading glasses, movements crisp, a woman closing shop. "Visiting hours end at four. Tell Brenda I behaved. Tell her I was lively."

"Maman—"

"And Inès." She stopped, half-risen, one hand on the arm of the chair, and for a moment — just a moment, a crack running fast through plaster — her face was not armored at all. It was tired. It was old. It was the face of a woman standing in a doorway listening to water somewhere in the dark. "Whatever Marisol told you. Whatever you found in that house. Some doors are stuck for a reason. The paint seals them shut on purpose. You force them, and you find out what the room's been keeping, and then you live with the smell."

She walked out. Back straight, steps even, the pen still in her hand though the crossword stayed behind on the table, abandoned, seven across filled in.

*Lingers.*

Ines sat alone in the sunroom for a long moment, then took out the folded certified letter, unfolded it, and read the auction listing again — the photographs, the dates, the line at the bottom about *contents sold as-is, all lots final.* And beneath the listing, in the margin, in their own handwriting from two nights ago, a note they barely remembered making, the pen moving while the mind was elsewhere:

*Ask her about the phone.*

They stared at it. They had no memory of writing it. But it was there in their hand, unmistakable, and it pointed like a compass needle at the front hall of the house on Rue Sabine, at the telephone that had hung on the wall beside the coat rack, twelve feet from the pond door, the whole night Theo drowned.

The phone that, according to the official story, had never rung.

Because nobody had called.

And Ines, at fourteen, crouched in the dark of the front hall, had heard it ring twice.
