## 7. The Woman Who Kept the Door

Edith Calloway had kept a habit for twelve years, and like all her habits it had begun as something reasonable and hardened into something else entirely.

Every morning, before she made tea, before she let the cat out or checked the barometer in the hall, she stood at the front window of the cottage on Corder Street and looked at the Ishikawa house across the lane. It was not sentimentality. She would have bristled at the suggestion. It was vigilance, though she could no longer have said what she was vigilant against. The house had been empty long enough that the sea had begun to take it — salt working into the brickwork the way water works into a biscuit, patiently, without malice — and the letting agency sent a man twice a year to mow the strip of garden and nail down whatever the winter had pried loose. Between those visits the house simply endured, shutters half-closed, gutters sprouting stonecrop, its windows giving back the grey sky so that from the lane it seemed to be all pupil, watching back.

She watched it the way other people watched weather. Not because anything would happen, but because once, something had.

On the morning the stranger came, Edith was on her second cup of tea and the light was doing that thing it did in late September, coming low off the water and laying itself along the lane like a plank you might walk out on. She heard the car first — they always heard cars here; Corder Street was too narrow for anything to arrive quietly — an engine labouring up from the harbour road, hesitating at the corner as if consulting itself, then rolling to a stop with the particular sigh of someone who has driven a long way and isn't sure why.

A man got out. Mid-thirties, perhaps. Dark coat, city shoes already wrong for the gravel. He stood beside the car for a full minute without moving, and Edith, who had spent a lifetime reading people through glass the way other people read newspapers, understood that he was not looking at the house so much as gathering himself against it.

Then he took out his phone and began to photograph it.

He photographed the whole façade, then moved closer and photographed the door, the boot-scraper, the iron railings with their paint gone to lace. He crouched to photograph the step where the stone had cracked. He photographed the upstairs window — the small one, the one over what had been the back bedroom — and when he did, he held the phone very still for a long time, longer than the picture required, and Edith felt something move through her chest that she had not permitted herself to feel in twelve years, which was recognition arriving ahead of thought.

Because she knew that stillness. She knew the way grief made people hold their bodies — not weeping, nothing so legible, but a kind of carefulness, as though the air around them were stitched and any sudden movement would pull the thread. The boy had stood exactly like that, the last time she'd seen him. Nine years old, wrapped in a blanket on her own settee while the fire brigade's lights strobed orange against her curtains, staring at his hands as if they belonged to someone who hadn't finished being explained to him yet.

The man lowered the phone. He looked up at the window again — the small one — and even across the lane, even through two panes of glass and forty yards of salted air, Edith saw him mouth something. One word, maybe two. His breath fogged faintly and was gone.

*Wren,* she said, inside the quiet of her kitchen. *Wren Ishikawa.*

The name sat in her like a swallowed stone. She had said it aloud only a handful of times since — to the social worker who never came back, to the constable who wrote it down wrong, to her husband Gerald, God rest him, who had listened and nodded and finally, gently, in the way of men who love practical women, suggested that some doors were better left closed. Gerald had meant well. Gerald had also died believing that Edith Calloway did what she was told, which was the single greatest misunderstanding of their forty-one years together.

She had written the letter. That was the thing no one knew — well, no one living. She had written to social services, twice, and once more after that to someone higher whose title she'd copied carefully off a leaflet in the doctor's surgery, and she had described what she'd seen on the night of the fire, and she had used words like *concerned* and *in my opinion* because she'd been taught that women of her age were believed in proportion to how unexcited they sounded. And nothing had happened. A file number had come back, eventually, on paper thin as onion skin. Then nothing. Then the family was gone — mother and boy vanished north somewhere, the father buried, the sister, the other one, unreachable or unwilling — and Edith had learned the lesson that towns like Greyhaven teach everyone eventually: that the truth is heavy, and nobody wants to help you carry it, and after a while carrying it alone starts to look, from the outside, exactly like madness.

So she had put it down. Not thrown away — put down, the way you put down a load you intend to pick up again. She had kept the carbon copy of her letter in the tin where Gerald used to keep the fishing licences, under the spare buttons and the dead torch. She had kept her eyes on the house. And every year on the anniversary — the third of October, though the date hardly mattered now, the fire lived in her more often than that — she had stood at this window and told the empty house, silently, *I haven't forgotten. Somebody has to not forget.*

And now somebody had come back wearing that somebody's face.

* * *

He walked the length of the garden wall, running his hand along it, and Edith found herself cataloguing him the way she'd once catalogued customers in the shop she'd run off the front room — haberdashery, mostly, buttons and thread and the small mercies of making do. He had Mariko's hands. That was unmistakable: long fingers, quick, restless, always on the verge of playing something invisible. He had David's jaw, poor devil, and David's habit of standing with his weight tipped slightly forward, as though perpetually about to apologise. But the hair, dark and unruly where it escaped from under nothing at all — that was Mariko's, and the way he tilted his head when he looked at things, listening almost, as if houses spoke and he'd been given the gift of hearing them.

He reached the gate. Stopped. Put his hand flat on the post the way you'd steady yourself against a ship's rail, and stood there so long that the gulls lost interest in him and went back to arguing over the harbour wall.

Edith's hand was on the latch of her own front door before she knew she'd moved. The cool brass under her palm. Twelve years of rehearsing this moment — she had, she admitted it, rehearsed it, in the bath, on sleepless nights, walking the cliff path — and in every rehearsal she flung the door open and called his name and the truth came pouring out of her like water from a knocked-over jug, clean and unstoppable.

But rehearsals are kind, and mornings are not. Because now she saw what the rehearsal had spared her: that the boy at the gate was not nine anymore, and not a boy, and that whatever he had carried north with him all these years, he had carried it the same way she carried hers — carefully, silently, in a container built for one. If she opened the door and poured, there was no telling what it would do to him. People broke in strange directions. She had seen it. She had, in a sense, been part of causing it once already, by writing letters that went nowhere and saying nothing that went everywhere — because silence travels in a town; it gets into the walls; it teaches children what must not be spoken.

Mariko had taught her son that lesson thoroughly. Edith had watched her do it, those last months — the woman hanging washing with her back to the lane, pleasant to everyone, telling no one anything, smiling at Edith over the fence with a smile like a drawn curtain. *Everything fine, Mrs Calloway?* Fine, fine. The word had come to sound like a lid.

No. Not yet. Let him look first. Let him stand where he needed to stand. There was a right order to these things, and after twelve years Edith trusted herself to know it better than any social worker with an onion-skinned reply.

* * *

She made more tea she didn't want, because her hands wanted occupation. Through the kitchen window she watched him try the gate — locked, of course; the agency kept it chained — and saw him lean through the bars instead, stretching to see down the side passage, and something about the gesture undid her a little. She remembered Mariko leaning through that same gate the summer they arrived, the boy on her hip, calling over: *Is it always this windy?* As if wind were a novelty. As if anyone could live beside the sea and not be shaped by it, bent slightly, like the trees on the headland.

The man — Wren, she would not stop thinking of him as the man now, that was the cost of the years — gave up on the gate and walked back toward his car. And then, instead of getting in, he did something that decided the matter for her.

He stopped in the middle of the lane, closed his eyes, and breathed.

Anyone else would have read it as a man stretching his legs. Edith read it as a man taking the town into his lungs on purpose — the salt, the creosote off the harbour sheds, the fish-and-chip fat that never quite left the air, the cold stone smell of the churchyard — breathing it in like a medicine he both needed and feared. She had seen returning emigrants do it at the ferry terminal. You could leave Greyhaven, but apparently it stayed in the blood like a tide table.

He opened his eyes and looked directly at her window.

Not at the cottage generally. At the window. At the exact pane behind which she stood, three feet back, teacup in hand, in the shadow where she could see out and the light could not find her. For a moment neither of them moved, and Edith had the vertiginous sense of the twelve years folding up like a concertina, so that it might have been yesterday, so that she might have been standing here in her apron watching Mariko carry the child in from the car, so that everything between then and now — Gerald's illness, the shop closing, the funerals, the slow settling of the town into its own dust — might be set aside like a newspaper she'd only pretended to read.

He couldn't possibly have seen her. She knew that. The glass was old and the room behind her was dim.

But he raised one hand anyway. Not a wave, quite. Something smaller. An acknowledgement, the way you'd raise a hand to a lighthouse.

*Well,* Edith said aloud, to the cat, who had come to sit on the cold sill and was also watching the man with the fixed attention cats give to birds and other inevitable things. *Well, then.*

* * *

He drove off slowly, the way people drive when they're leaving a place they intend to return to — checking the mirror more than the road. She watched the car diminish down toward the harbour, heard it change gear at the bottom where the road turned past the lifeboat station, and waited until the sound was gone entirely before she moved.

Then she went to the dresser in the parlour, the good one with the glazed doors, and took down the biscuit tin that had held sewing supplies for thirty years and other things for twelve. Inside, beneath a folded page of instructions for a cardigan she would never knit, lay the carbon copy of her letter, soft at the edges now, its purple type faded to the colour of a bruise healing. She did not read it. She knew it by heart, every measured, unexcited sentence. *At approximately eleven o'clock on the night in question I observed Mrs Ishikawa leaving the property on foot, in the direction of the coast road, carrying a suitcase. This was approximately four hours prior to the discovery of the fire. I mention this as it may be of significance. I am happy to assist further.*

*I am happy to assist further.* Four hours. She had put it in and they had done nothing with it, and the sentence had sat in the tin all this time like a bone stuck in the town's throat.

She put the tin back. She washed her teacup and dried it and set it in its place, and then she stood in her tidy hallway — Gerald's coat still on its hook, because she had never decided what one did with a dead man's coat — and she considered the question properly, the way she considered everything, slowly and with both hands.

He would come back. She was certain of it the way she was certain of weather. A man who breathed a town in like that wasn't visiting; he was resuming. And when he came back he would knock on doors, because that was what people did when they were digging, and there were only so many doors on Corder Street, and only one of them belonged to a woman who had seen a suitcase.

There was a version of tomorrow in which she kept the door shut. In which she let him knock and did not answer and let the truth stay in the tin another twelve years, safe as preserves, doing no one any good and no one any harm. Gerald would have preferred that version. So, probably, would Mariko, wherever she was — and Edith had wondered, over the years, whether Mariko was even alive, whether the silence from the north was the silence of estrangement or the silence of earth, and had never found a way to ask without revealing what she knew.

But there was another version. In that one, an old woman opened her door to a grown man with his mother's hands, and put the kettle on, and said: *I've been waiting for you to come. Sit down. There's something I saw, and I'm tired of being the only one who's seen it.*

The cat wound round her ankles, impatient for the door to be opened onto the garden, onto the ordinary day.

*Yes, yes,* Edith murmured. *Presently.*

She looked out the window one more time. The lane was empty. The Ishikawa house gave back the sky, all pupil, keeping whatever it kept. Somewhere north, presumably, a woman was forgetting things — Edith had heard that much through the grapevine of holiday-let gossip, that the mother still lived, that her mind was going like a tide going out, slowly and then all at once. Which meant the clock was running. Which meant that whatever Edith Calloway was going to say, she would have to say it soon, to someone, before the only other witness to that night drifted beyond the reach of ever confirming it.

She picked up the telephone directory, found the number for the nursery outside the village — Ruth's place; she'd ordered seedlings from it once, years ago, and hung up before speaking, a cowardice she still regretted — and wrote the number on the back of her shopping list. Then she wrote Wren's name underneath it. Then she sat down at the kitchen table with a fresh sheet of paper, because whatever she was going to tell that young man, she intended to have it straight in her own mind first, in order, unexcited, believable.

Outside, the tide was coming in over the sandbars, filling the channels it had emptied that morning, patient as ever, undoing and redoing the whole grey world. Edith dipped her pen.

*Dear Mr Ishikawa,* she wrote — and stopped, and looked at the words, and understood that she had just, after twelve years, opened the door.
