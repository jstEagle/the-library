## 11. Radiators and Testimony

The house had always talked to Wren. He had simply never listened to it on purpose.

He came home from Greyhaven with salt still in his hair and Edith Calloway's tea sitting in him like a stone, and instead of going upstairs to check on his mother he stood in the hall and did the thing he did in strangers' houses: he put his palm flat against the wall beside the radiator and waited.

In other people's houses it took minutes. The pipes were shy there, unaccustomed to attention. But this house knew him. It had raised him as much as his mother had, and when he laid his hand against the cool plaster it began at once, the way a dog begins at once for the person who feeds it—a tick in the boiler, a knock two floors up where the hot water pipe crossed a joist too tightly, the long sigh of the bathroom cistern refilling. Ordinary sounds. The vocabulary of any old Victorian house on a cold evening.

But underneath the ordinary, if he was patient—and patience was the one thing Wren Ishikawa had never run short of—there was the other layer. The one clients never heard and would have been frightened to hear. Every house that had stood longer than a lifetime held the shape of its years the way a riverbed holds the shape of the water. Pipes expanded and contracted according to habits laid down decades ago. A radiator that clanked at four in the morning was remembering a night someone had come home late, winter after winter after winter. A pipe that shuddered when the tap shut off was remembering haste.

He had known all this abstractly for twelve years. He had used it as a party trick for estate agents and a comfort for widows. He had never once turned it on his own house, because you do not interrogate the person who shares your bed.

Tonight he had no choice. Edith's voice was still in his ears—*she left the house alone, dear, carrying a suitcase, hours before*—and the ground under everything he believed had tilted, and he needed something true, something older than memory, something that could not lie or forget or be kind to itself. His mother's mind was going. His own mind he no longer trusted. But the house had been here the whole time. The house had watched.

He knelt at the radiator in the front room, the big cast-iron one under the window with the flaking green paint, and pressed his ear to it the way he pressed his ear to pianos, and he listened.

* * *

Mariko woke at half past nine and called down the stairs, "David? Is that you?"

Wren closed his eyes. The name landed in the hall like a dropped glass.

He climbed the stairs slowly, giving himself time to arrange his face into the expression he'd learned for these moments—not correction, not grief, just a mild pleasant blankness, the face of a man who might be anyone. She was sitting up in bed with her reading lamp on, her silver hair loose, looking toward the door with an expression of such ordinary expectation that it went through him like a nail.

"Hello," he said. "It's me."

"Don't be silly." She smiled. "Come in properly, David, you're letting the cold in."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Twelve years of practice. You didn't argue with the confusion; arguing frightened her. You stepped around it the way you stepped around a wet patch on a floor. "I've just come up," he said. "You should sleep."

"I was waiting." Her hands moved on the blanket, smoothing it, smoothing it again. "The car was so long. I kept thinking, he'll be cold, driving all that way in November." She looked at him then, really looked, and for a moment something flickered behind her eyes—the flicker he had learned to dread more than the confusion itself, because in the flicker she almost saw him, almost saw what she was doing, and the shame that crossed her face was worse than anything. Then it passed. "You'll want something to eat," she said briskly, in the voice of a much younger woman, a woman arranging a household. "There's pie."

"There's pie," he agreed.

She lay back. Within a minute her breathing changed, and she was asleep, or somewhere near it, her mouth slightly open, her hands still moving faintly on the blanket as though smoothing it in another room, another year.

Wren sat on the edge of the bed until his legs decided to hold him again. Then he went back downstairs, and put his hand on the radiator, and listened harder.

* * *

Here is what a house knows, and how.

Water remembers temperature. When the heating system in this house first ran—in the autumn of some year, the boiler man's sticker said 1994 but stickers lie, boilers get replaced, pipes don't—the pipes expanded with heat for the first time and set their tolerances. Everything after was variation on that first theme. And the first theme of this house, Wren realized now, kneeling in the dark of the front room with his ear against iron, was fear.

Not the ordinary fear of a new house settling around new occupants. Something specific. The hot water pipe that rose through the kitchen wall had a tremor in it, a fine fast vibration that came and went even when nothing was running, like a pulse. He had noticed it before and diagnosed it as a loose bracket. Tonight he understood that a loose bracket was only the instrument. The music was twelve years old—or rather twenty-six years old, because the move north had been 1997, and he had been nine, and he had thought they were simply moving, the way families moved, for work, for a change, for reasons children weren't told because reasons were adult property.

The tremor said otherwise. The tremor said *haste*. Someone had run this system hard and fast and irregularly—a woman alone, he could hear it, the pattern of use was a single person's pattern, showers at odd hours, kettles, a washing machine run at three in the morning because three in the morning was when you couldn't sleep—someone had moved into this house in winter with a child and a suitcase and whatever else fits in a car driven through the night by a woman who does not stop at service stations because service stations have lights and lights can be seen.

And underneath the haste, threaded through it like wire through cable, another sound. He had to strain for it. It wasn't a sound exactly; it was the absence of one, a deadness in certain pipes, sections of the system that had been sealed off and never used—old radiator connections capped and painted over, a fireplace bricked up, a door (he walked over and touched it) whose hinges had been oiled so often and so carefully that it made no noise at all, a silence maintained deliberately across decades, the way you maintain a grave.

A woman talking to herself. That was what the house held, in the end, distilled out of all those years of steam and expansion. Not conversations—there had been no one to converse with—but the rhythm of speech, the rise and fall of a voice keeping itself company, and the pipes carried the vibration of it the way railway bridges carry the vibration of trains long gone. He couldn't make out words. Houses don't keep words. They keep cadence. And the cadence of Mariko Ishikawa talking to herself in this house for twenty-six years was the cadence of a woman rehearsing. Going over something. Again and again and again, the same rises, the same falls, the same little catch where the voice would break and start over.

He took his hand off the radiator. The kitchen felt very cold.

* * *

At two in the morning he heard her on the stairs.

This was not unusual. She wandered, some nights; the doctor called it sundowning, which sounded too gentle, like a weather phenomenon, which perhaps it was. He got up and found her standing in the hall in her nightdress, facing the front door, her hand on the latch.

"Mariko," he said gently. "It's the middle of the night."

She turned. In the dark her face was unreadable, and then she said, in a low urgent voice entirely unlike her daytime voices, "We have to go before he gets back."

The floor seemed to tilt. He gripped the banister.

"Mum. There's no one coming. We're safe here."

"You don't understand." She was gripping the latch now with both hands, and her knuckles were white, and she was suddenly, terribly present—not confused at all, lucid in the way of dreams, lucid about the wrong decade. "He'll have seen the smoke. He always knows. Wren—" and there it was, his own name, arriving like a rescue boat, "—Wren, we have to pack."

"We're packed," he said, because it was the only thing that would reach her. "Everything's packed. We're going tomorrow. I promise."

She studied him for a long moment, deciding whether to believe him, and he saw himself as she saw him: a tall shape in the dark, a man's shape, trustworthy or not trustworthy, and he understood with a lurch that tonight she was not confusing him with his father. Tonight she was telling his father's son the thing she had never told anyone. The suitcase. The hours before the fire. The drive north through the dark.

"Tomorrow," she repeated, and let go of the latch, and let him lead her back upstairs like a child, and at her bedroom door she paused and said, "You're a good boy. You always were quiet. Quiet boys see everything." Then she slept, instantly, completely, the sleep of the truly exhausted, and Wren stood in the corridor outside her door for a long time, listening to her breathe, listening to the house breathe around her.

*Quiet boys see everything.*

Had she meant it? Would she remember saying it? Did it matter? He went back down to the front room because he could not bear to stand still, and put his hand on the radiator again, and the tremor was there, the pulse in the kitchen wall pipe, the haste, the rehearsal, and he thought: *you are a witness. You are the only witness left who cannot develop dementia.* And then he thought, with a bitterness that surprised him: *and you cannot testify either. You cannot be cross-examined. You will say haste, and fear, and a woman talking to herself, and you will never say why.*

* * *

He worked until dawn. This was how he processed things—he had done it since he was nine, since the night he stood in a garden watching flames and made no sound: he worked. He bled the radiators one by one, though none of them needed it, because bleeding a radiator meant holding a key against the valve and listening to the air escape, and each one gave him its small hiss and cough and then its steady note, and he tuned them without meaning to, adjusted the lockshield valves a quarter turn here, an eighth there, until the whole house ran at a level hum, every room the same temperature for the first time in years.

His mother had always kept the back rooms cold. He had never questioned it. Now, feeling the balance shift as the heat equalized, he understood it as one more entry in the testimony: she had lived for twenty-six years in a state of controlled discomfort, warmth rationed, doors closed, the house held like breath. As if comfort itself were dangerous. As if being warm and settled and visible were things that invited consequences.

By six the light was coming up grey over the rooftops, and he made tea he didn't drink, and sat in the front room with the box of letters on his knees.

Forty-one letters. He had read three. He had told himself he would read them in order, slowly, respectfully, the way you excavate a site rather than loot it. But Edith's tea-stone was still sitting in him, and his mother's voice from the stairhead—*we have to go before he gets back*—was rearranging everything he thought he knew, and the box sat there on his knees with the weight of a sleeping animal.

He had spent twelve years learning the art of almost saying things. He could feel the art failing him now, feel the almost collapsing toward the actual, and it terrified him, because the actual, whatever it was, lived partly inside his mother's deteriorating mind, and every week that mind lost ground. If there was a truth to be had from her—from the only witness who could confirm or destroy what Edith had told him—it was a truth with a closing window.

He turned the box over in his hands. Beneath the letters, at the bottom, pressed flat, there was something he hadn't noticed before: a photograph, face down, its corners soft with age. He slid it out.

A man stood on a beach he didn't recognize, squinting into sun, one hand raised in greeting or warning. On the back, in his mother's handwriting, younger and firmer than the writing on the envelopes: a date—the week after the fire—and beneath the date, five words:

*You were right about him.*

Wren sat very still while the house ticked and settled around him, its testimony finished, its witness dismissed, and outside the window the November morning came up slow and colourless over a town that had kept his mother's secret for twenty-six years.

The letter this photograph belonged to was in the box somewhere. He turned back to the envelopes, and found it third from the bottom, and his hand shook as he broke the seal.
