## 18. Deliveries

Ruth's kitchen smelled of loam and tomato vines, of cut stems bleeding green into water. She had cleared the table of its trays of seedlings and laid a tea towel down the centre like an altar cloth, and on it she had placed a single biscuit tin, empty, lid off, waiting. Wren stood in the doorway with the shoebox under his arm and felt the weight of it change—not lighter, exactly, but redistributed, the way a piano's tone redistributes when you finally get the pin torque right and the strings stop fighting each other.

"You brought them all," Ruth said. Not a question. She had washed her hands and put on a cardigan, and there was something almost ceremonial in her, the brisk nursery woman folded away and a different person standing in her kitchen, someone younger, someone who had once waited at windows.

"Forty-one," Wren said. "I counted twice."

"Of course you did." She almost smiled. "You're a tuner. You'd count."

He set the box down on the tea towel. For a moment neither of them touched it. Through the window behind Ruth he could see the polytunnels going soft and silver in the afternoon light, the rows of heathers and the stacked pots, a wheelbarrow abandoned mid-job with its handles pointing at the sky like something that had given up. A tractor droned somewhere out on the lanes. The ordinary world, going on, indifferent to what sat on this table.

"Where do we start?" Ruth asked.

"The beginning," Wren said. "I've read ahead. I know what's coming. You don't. I thought—" He stopped, surprised by his own sentence, by the fact that he was thinking of someone else's experience of the letters at all. Twelve years of practising avoidance, and here he was offering to walk someone through a fire in chronological order. "I thought you should get them the way she wrote them. One at a time. In order. The way the years went."

Ruth pulled out a chair for him and one for herself, and sat down opposite, and said, "Read them to me. I'll want to hear her voice. My eyes will want to skim, and I don't trust them not to."

So Wren opened the box, and took out the first letter, and began.

* * *

The early ones were light. That was the thing that undid him first—not the grief but the lightness, the sheer ordinariness of the opening letters, written in his mother's younger hand, rounder, quicker, the ink of someone who wrote letters the way other people breathed. Mariko at twenty-six, telling Ruth about a flat in Greyhaven with a damp problem and a landlord who played the trumpet badly at all hours. Mariko describing the sea in February, grey-green and muscular. Mariko reporting that David had got a position at the boatyard and came home smelling of varnish and salt, and that Wren—*your nephew, four months old today, Ruth, he has your exact frown, I swear it, he frowns at his own fists like they've betrayed him*—

Ruth laughed at that, a short startled sound, her hand going to her mouth.

"He does," she said. "He does frown like that. I watched him do it an hour ago over the biscuit tin."

Wren kept reading. His voice found a register he didn't know it had, something between reading aloud and speaking from memory, and the kitchen filled with a young woman he had never met: his mother before the silence, before the almost-saying, a Mariko who complained about trumpet players and asked after Ruth's garden and ended every letter with some small domestic blessing—*keep the window over the sink shut in this wind*—as if the two sisters were still close enough to share weather.

"Did you answer these?" he asked, pausing between letters.

"Every one." Ruth's hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the table might lift. "I answered every single one, and she never wrote back to say she'd got them. I used to think the post was eating them. Then I thought she was. I went back and forth between the two for years." She looked at the shoebox. "So she kept them. All this time. She read them and she kept them and she never said."

The fourth letter changed key. Wren heard it before he understood it—the way he could hear a cracked hammer felt before he could see the crack. The handwriting tightened. The sentences got shorter. *Ruth, I need to ask you something and I need you not to ask me why.* And then the question, which Mariko did not state, only circled: *If a person did something, and there was a child, and the child was asleep, would the child—* and then a line crossed out so heavily the paper had nearly torn, and then, in the space where the question should have been: *Never mind. How are the dahlias?*

Ruth made a sound Wren had no name for. Not a laugh, not a sob. Something a body does when a door it has been pushing against for forty years suddenly swings open onto a room it didn't want to see.

"That summer," she said. "That's 'that summer.' That's the one she meant in all of them."

"Yes," Wren said. And then, because he was tired of being a man who almost said things: "The fire was in September. I've done the dates. This letter's from July."

Ruth closed her eyes. Outside, the light was beginning to go amber, and it came through the window and lay across the shoebox like something spilled.

"Keep going," she said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon the way you work through a hard tuning: slowly, in order, no skipping. Wren read, and Ruth listened, and between letters they said very little, and what they said was mostly small—*pass the water, look at that handwriting, she always did her fives like that*—small things said loudly in the presence of large things, which was, Wren realised, a family skill on both sides of the table. They were both, he and this aunt he'd known for a fortnight, people who had survived by deciding what to believe. The letters were dismantling the decisions one envelope at a time.

The middle years were the hardest. Mariko north, Ruth south, and between them a correspondence that was all weather and no climate—reports of Wren's school, of the piano lessons he'd been given and abandoned, of the house on the hill with its pipes that knocked in frost. Once, twice, Mariko wrote: *You were right about him.* Never with the rest of the sentence. Never *about what*. Ruth, hearing it for the fourth time, finally put both hands over her face.

"She never said what I was right about. Not once. Not in forty years."

"Do you know?" Wren asked.

"I have three answers," Ruth said into her hands. "I've had three answers for forty years, and I rotate them depending on the day. One: she was right that David was dangerous. Two: she was right that I chose him over her when they split, and I did, God help me, I chose the easier parent. Three—" She lowered her hands. Her eyes were wet and furious. "Three: she was right that running was the only thing left, and she could never forgive me for making her see that."

"Which is it?"

"That's the question that's going to outlive all of us, isn't it." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she'd deadhead a plant. "Read the next one."

* * *

The light had gone from amber to copper when Wren reached the letters from the years he could remember himself—the years after the move north, after the fire, after the disappearance. His own childhood, arriving in his mother's hand, addressed to a woman he'd been told didn't exist. *Wren started at a new school. He doesn't ask questions. He's very good at not asking questions. I don't know whether to be proud or frightened.* He read that sentence aloud and had to stop, and Ruth reached across the table and put her hand over his where it lay on the paper, and they stayed like that for a while, two people holding still over a sentence that had taken thirty years to travel.

"He was describing me," Wren said finally. "In me. She was watching me become her."

"Yes," Ruth said. "And she was frightened of it. Look—" she turned the letter toward him, her finger under the next line— "*I could stop it. I could tell him everything. I could tell him and let him hate me or let him hate David or let him hate the whole bloody family, and at least he'd be saying something.*" Ruth's voice cracked on the last words. "She knew. She knew what the silence was doing, and she kept it anyway, because she thought the silence was the only thing keeping you safe. That's what I can't—Wren, that's what I can't decide whether to forgive."

"You don't have to decide today."

"No." She laughed, wet and short. "But I've had forty years of practice deciding nothing, and I'll tell you what I've learned: you don't get resolution. You get company. That's all there is on offer. Company."

The sky through the window was going the colour of slate now, and the polytunnels had turned to long dim ribs. Somewhere a rook called. Ruth got up without asking and turned on the kitchen light and put the kettle on, and the small domestic noise of it—the tap, the click of the switch, the tin of biscuits opened at last and set between them—was so ordinary that Wren felt his throat close. This, he thought. This is what the letters were for. Not the truth. The tea afterward.

* * *

The last letters were in the deteriorating hand he knew from shopping lists and notes left on the hall table—Mariko of the last five years, the words drifting on the page like boats off their moorings. One letter was mostly about a bird at the window. One was an apology with no cause, the same apology she had made to him a hundred times in the last two years, *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I don't know what for, but I'm sure I owe it.* And then letter forty, dated three winters ago, which began: *Ruth. I've started forgetting. I want to tell you before the forgetting takes it, but every time I sit down to say it plainly, my hand won't. So I'm doing it the way I've always done it. Slowly. In pieces. Forgive me. I'm sending you the whole box eventually. I just need to know you'd take it.*

Wren looked up. "She never sent it."

"No." Ruth's voice was very quiet. "She got as far as the stamps."

The last letter, forty-one, he had read alone in his own kitchen weeks ago—the instruction, the permission, *give these to Ruth when I can't remember why I kept them*—but he read it aloud anyway, because Ruth had asked to hear the whole of it, the way the years went, and because some things need to be said into a room with another person in it to become real. When he finished, the kettle had gone cold and neither of them had noticed.

Ruth sat for a long moment with her hands around the empty biscuit tin, as if it were the thing being held, and then she said: "There's a question I have to ask, and you can refuse."

"All right."

"That night. The fire. You were nine. You were in the house." Her eyes were steady and terrible. "What did you see?"

Wren felt the old reflex rise in him like water into a crack—the almost-answer, the deflection, the *it was a long time ago*. He let it rise, and watched it, and let it fall back down unspoken. Somewhere in the last four hours, between the trumpet-playing landlord and the dahlias and the crossed-out question, the reflex had worn through.

"I saw her in the garden," he said. "Standing in the grass. Watching the house burn. She wasn't screaming. She wasn't moving. She was just—watching. Like a woman reading a letter she'd been waiting years for." He heard the words leave him and was astonished at how ordinary they sounded, how survivable. "I've known that for thirty years, Ruth. I've never said it out loud before tonight."

Ruth nodded, slowly, and he watched her take it in—not as an answer, he understood, but as a weight to be shared, set down on the table between the biscuit tin and the shoebox, next to all the questions that would never be answered. Why the fire. What David had done. What *you were right* had meant, in the end, in the only sense that mattered. Those questions would go into the ground with all of them, unanswered, and what remained was this: two people in a kitchen, the truth passed hand to hand, incomplete but no longer carried alone.

"Thank you," Ruth said. "For delivering them. All forty-one."

"It took me twelve years to learn how to almost say things," Wren said. "These took one afternoon to actually say. That's the part I can't get over."

Ruth began stacking the letters back into the box in order, her movements slow and precise, and when she came to the last one she paused, and looked at him across the copper-lit kitchen, and said the thing that would keep him awake that night and set his feet on the road home before dawn:

"There's one more delivery, Wren. She's still alive. And there's a song she used to hum, when we were girls, that our mother taught us—and if any part of her is still in there, that song will find it. Will you drive me to her?"
