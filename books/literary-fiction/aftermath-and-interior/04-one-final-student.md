## 4. One Final Student

The mornings had begun to lie to Maren Oduya.

She would wake in the blue hour before the birds and feel, for three or four seconds, entirely well — the old body, the old lungs, the old appetite for tea and toast and the long work of a day. Then she would swing her feet to the floorboards and the truth would arrive like weather: the weight in her chest, the breath that came in installments, the strange new talent her hands had developed for dropping things. She had learned not to mourn those first seconds. In Ilvashi there was a word for a joy you knew was borrowed — *seluva*, literally *a lamp lit in a house already sold* — and she had decided, with the pragmatism of the dying, to be grateful for lamplight wherever it appeared.

She took her tea on the porch because the kitchen felt too large now, its distances exaggerated by her slowing legs. From the porch she could see the bridge.

The Kestrel Street footbridge had been her neighbor for fifty-one years. She had watched it carry children to school and lovers home at midnight and, once, a runaway bride in full white, heels in hand, laughing at nothing. It had rusted and been painted, sagged and been shored up, and through it all it had gone on doing the only thing bridges know how to do: standing between two places so that people could cross. She thought of it sometimes as the last member of her family who never asked her anything.

And now, every morning for six days, there was a woman on it.

Maren had seen inspectors before. They came in trucks with municipal lettering, in pairs usually, wearing vests the color of caution itself. They moved fast, photographed everything, spoke into recorders, and left behind a faint smell of diesel and indifference. This one was different. This one came alone, arrived early, and did something Maren had never seen an inspector do: she touched the bridge the way you touch a face.

On the sixth morning, Maren set down her tea and watched properly.

The woman — Elodie, according to the name stitched over her breast pocket — worked from one end of the span toward the other with a method that looked less like inspection than devotion. She knelt at each joint. She ran her palm along the railing steel before measuring it, as if introducing herself. When she found something — a crack, a corrosion bloom, a bolt weeping rust down the girder like an old wound reopened — she did not sigh or curse as the others did. She went still. She leaned close. She stayed with the damage as long as the damage needed, the way you stay with a grieving person, not to fix them but to witness what is true.

At the third expansion joint from the north end, the woman lay flat on her back on the deck, half under the structure, and remained there so long that Maren's tea went cold. When she emerged she had dust in her hair and a look on her face that Maren recognized from mirrors: the expression of someone who has just learned how much movement there is in a thing everyone believes is still.

*Yes*, Maren thought. *That's exactly it.*

She had refused eleven linguists. She kept count the way other people their age kept count of grandchildren. There had been the earnest doctoral student from the university who arrived with a recording rig and a list of two hundred prepared questions and asked, within the first hour, whether Ilvashi had a future tense — as if the whole language were a suitcase he intended to pack and ship somewhere else. There had been the foundation people who wanted to fund a "preservation initiative," which meant they wanted to own her voice while she was alive and curate her extinction afterward. There had been the young man who cried, which she might have forgiven, except that he cried about the language and never once asked about her brother, who had spoken it with her, and who was dead.

They all wanted the same thing. They wanted Ilvashi out of her — extracted, documented, archived, safe. None of them understood that a language does not live in a mouth. It lives in the space between mouths, in the agreement between two people that this sound means this ache. A language recorded but unspoken is not preserved. It is embalmed.

So she had said no eleven times, and she had expected to say no forever, and then the city sent her a woman who lay on her back under a dying bridge to listen to what it was trying to say.

* * *

By the ninth day, Maren had conducted a thorough and unsentimental assessment, the kind she imagined the inspector herself would approve of.

Load rating, first: Could the woman learn? Maren had taught Ilvashi twice in her life — to her brother, who absorbed it as children absorb everything, without effort or gratitude; and to herself, alone, for decades, speaking to keep the walls of the house from closing in. Teaching an adult was another matter. Adults came to a language carrying their own, and most of them never put it down; they translated everything back into the tongue they already trusted, and what survived the crossing was luggage, not life. But this woman had patience the way some people have height — structural, load-bearing. Maren had watched her spend forty minutes on a single bearing pin, not because she was slow but because she refused to guess when she could know.

Second rating: Why? Motive was everything. The linguists had wanted data. The foundation had wanted legacy, theirs. What did this one want? Maren didn't know yet, and that was precisely what interested her. Every refusal she'd ever issued had been easy because she could see the want coming a mile off, bright and grasping. This woman's wanting was buried somewhere even she might not have dug to yet.

Third, and Maren saved this for last because it was the rating that actually mattered: time. Her own time was short — the doctors had used words like *weeks* and *months* with the gentle vagueness of people describing weather — and a language could not be transferred in weeks. It could barely be introduced. Whatever she gave this stranger would be a fragment, a shard, a single room of a house whose full floor plan would die with her. Was a fragment worth anything?

She thought about her brother's hands. He had died holding hers, thirty years ago now, and in his last coherent hour he had recited the names of rivers in Ilvashi, all the rivers of the country they'd left as children, and she had answered each one, and neither of them had said anything else, because nothing else needed saying. That hour had lasted perhaps sixty minutes. It had carried her thirty years. A fragment, held rightly, can bear more weight than a whole.

On the tenth morning, she made the tea, carried it to the porch, and waited.

Elodie arrived at seven-forty, as she always did, parked at the north end, and walked out onto the span with her bag of instruments. Today she stopped at the joint where the deck had begun to spall, where the concrete had flaked away to show the rebar beneath like bone showing through skin. She crouched. She touched it. And then, for the first time in ten days, she looked up — straight across the water, straight at the porch, straight at Maren — and did not look away.

There was no embarrassment in it, no quick retreat to professionalism. She simply held the gaze, the way she held everything: steadily, until it told her something.

Maren raised her teacup an inch. An invitation, or as close as she had come to one in years.

It took the woman twenty minutes to finish her section. Maren approved of that too — she did not abandon her work for a stranger, even a stranger with tea. Then Elodie crossed the rest of the span, descended the far stairs, and stood at the gate of the crooked house with her instrument bag over her shoulder like a traveler arriving after a very long journey.

"Your railing on the north approach has lost about thirty percent of its section," she said. "I'll be recommending repairs."

"You'll be recommending closure," Maren said. "I've read your reports' older cousins. I know how they end."

Something flickered across the woman's face — surprise, then honesty, then the effort of deciding whether to argue. "The deck is still sound," she said finally. "The superstructure has years left. It's the connections that are failing." She paused. "I'm sorry. You didn't come out here to talk about the bridge."

"I came out here because you did." Maren gestured at the second chair. "Sit. The tea is cold but the chair doesn't mind."

Elodie sat. Up close she was younger than her hands suggested — hands that had done precise work for many years, knuckles like small knots. She sat the way she inspected: fully present, weight distributed, ready to move but in no hurry to.

"You watch me every morning," Elodie said.

"You inspect my bridge every morning. We're both guilty of observation." Maren poured her a cup of the cold tea anyway, because hospitality was a muscle and hers had not atrophied yet. "Tell me your name again. Not the vest. You."

"Elodie. Elodie Amberdale."

"Amberdale." Maren turned it over. "A place-name pretending to be a person's. Do you know where your name comes from?"

"A river valley, I think. Amber-colored water. Or maybe just mud."

"Mud is honest." Maren drank. The tea was bitter cold and she tasted it fully, because tasting was still available to her. "I'm going to ask you something strange, and I want you to answer it the way you answer your bridge — carefully, and only with what you actually find."

Elodie set down her cup. "All right."

"When you were lying under the deck on Tuesday. Under the third joint. What were you listening for?"

The silence that followed was a good silence — the silence of someone actually consulting themselves rather than composing a reply. Maren waited inside it with the ease of long practice. Silence was the native country of her language. Ilvashi had four words for different kinds of silence and she had spent seventy years fluent in all of them.

"The sound it makes when it moves," Elodie said at last. "Bridges are always moving. Thermal expansion, live load, wind. Most people think a bridge is still. It isn't. It's talking constantly — very quietly. I was listening to what it says when nobody's asking it to hold anything." She stopped, and a faint color rose in her cheeks, as though she had revealed more than she'd intended. "That probably sounds—"

"It sounds like the truest thing anyone has said on this porch in eleven years." Maren set down her cup. Her heart was doing something complicated, part grief and part lift, and she let it do it. "My language has a word for that. For the sound a thing makes when no one is asking it to bear weight."

Elodie went very still. Maren had seen that stillness before — in her brother, in herself, in the rare person who understands that a door has just opened and that doors, once opened onto certain rooms, cannot be shut again without cost.

"Your language," Elodie said carefully. "Ilvashi. I looked it up after the first day. There isn't much written about it."

"There is almost nothing written about it. There is me." Maren folded her hands over the blanket on her knees — when had the blanket become permanent? — and made herself say the next part plainly, because plainness was a kindness and she had little time for anything less. "I am the last person alive who speaks it fluently. Not the last who knows words of it — there are cousins in two countries who remember lullabies and curses. Fluent. The whole grammar. The verbs that change with who broke what. The silences. All of it dies when I do, unless I give it away first."

"I know about endangered languages," Elodie began. "I mean, I don't know, but I've read—"

"No." Maren's voice came out harder than she intended, and she let it. "You will not record me. You will not archive me. You will not write a paper. If you say yes to what I'm about to ask, you will not be a linguist. You will be a student. Worse — you will be an heir. And inheritance is not data. It is weight. Do you understand the difference?"

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. Then she did the thing that decided everything, the thing none of the eleven had ever done: she looked down at her own hands, as if consulting them, as if the answer lived in the same place her precision lived.

"Why me?" she said. "You don't know me. I inspect bridges. I—" She stopped. Started again. "Why would you give something like that to a stranger?"

"Because I have watched you for ten days ask a bridge what it says when no one needs it to hold anything," Maren said. "Because you lie on your back under failing things to hear them honestly. Because you didn't translate my question into something comfortable before answering it." She drew a breath that cost her more than she showed. "A language survives in the space between speakers. I need one more speaker. Not a scholar. A keeper. Someone who understands that things that move are still things that stand."

The river moved below them. Somewhere in the house behind Maren, the refrigerator cycled on with its small mechanical patience.

"What would I have to do?" Elodie asked.

"Come on Tuesdays. Learn until I can't teach. Speak it after I can't speak. That's all. That's everything." Maren smiled, and felt the smile reach whatever was left of her eyes. "It is also the largest thing anyone will ever ask of you. I promise you that."

Elodie was quiet for the length of ten heartbeats — Maren counted them idly, an old habit from waiting out storms. Then:

"Yes," Elodie said. And then, as if hearing herself: "Yes. I — yes."

Maren laughed, a rusty sound that startled a pigeon off the railing. "You said yes before you knew why. Good. The important yeses never wait for reasons. Reasons are what we build afterward to make the yes look sensible." She reached out and covered the younger woman's hand with her own, and felt beneath the cool skin the steady pulse of a person who still had decades left to carry what was coming. "Tuesday. Come hungry; I cook better than I breathe. And Elodie—" she held her gaze a moment longer, "—don't tell anyone yet. Not the city, not the universities, not whoever shares your house. Some things must root before they're exposed to weather."

Elodie nodded slowly, and Maren watched the strangeness of the covenant settle into her — the bewilderment not leaving, exactly, but making room for something firmer underneath it.

When the inspector had crossed back over the span — pausing once, halfway, to rest her hand on the railing, Maren noticed — the old woman remained on the porch with her cold tea and her loud heart, and permitted herself, for the first time in months, to imagine a Tuesday that existed past the one she might not see.

Then she went inside, opened the drawer where her brother's dictionary slept under a cloth, and began, with hands that trembled only a little, to choose the first word.
