## 5. Photographs of a Stranger

The box arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for nothing. Sundays belonged to the postcards, Mondays to the library cart, and the rest of the week Coral had arranged — without ever writing the arrangement down — into a geography of small duties that kept her from standing still long enough to hear herself think. A Tuesday had no defenses.

It came from her mother. Helen did not call ahead; Helen sent things the way weather sends things, without apology or explanation. The return label was typed, because Helen believed handwriting on packages invited theft, and inside, beneath three layers of newspaper dated from an era when newspapers were worth saving, was a shoebox. Not even a proper archive box. A shoebox, the kind Anders had worn through in a single season of cutting lawns, size eleven, the lid soft at the corners where his thumbs had gripped it.

A note, one line, in Helen's upright hand: *These were in the garage. I can't.*

Coral set the note face-down on the table, as if it might behave better blindfolded.

She made tea first. This was procedure. You did not open a box containing your dead brother's belongings without tea, the same way you did not perform surgery without washing your hands. She chose the chipped blue mug, the one Anders had chipped by using it to hold screws, and she sat down at the kitchen table where the light came in honest and gray, and she lifted the lid.

Photographs. Dozens of them, loose and sleeved and rubber-banded into little civilizations. And beneath the photographs, at the bottom, a film canister, two ticket stubs, a casino matchbook from a town four hours north that Coral had never once heard him mention, and a receipt for a storage unit — paid in cash, no name — dated five weeks before the fire.

She picked up the top photograph and understood immediately that she was in trouble.

* * *

Here is what you have to understand about Anders Nystrom, if you never got to meet him: he was funny in a way that cost him nothing and everyone around him everything. He was the kind of funny that got you asked to leave restaurants. He could do the voice of the automated pharmacy line so perfectly that their mother once argued with it for four minutes before realizing her son was crouched behind the cereal display, weeping with joy. He narrated their lives. He gave the neighbors names — not nicknames, full invented identities with backstories he'd deliver in installments, like serials. Mr. Okafor next door was "a retired pirate running from a curse." The woman who walked the wheaten terrier every morning at 6:40 was "clearly a spy, Coral, nobody stretches that consistently."

Anders was nineteen months younger than Coral and had spent those nineteen months, as far as anyone could tell, catching up. He read faster than she did. He burned brighter. He failed bigger. When he flunked out of the community college welding program — not for lack of talent, the instructor said, but because he stopped attending after inventing a reason to be needed elsewhere — he shrugged and said, "I'm a generalist," and Coral had laughed, and hated herself for laughing, and loved him so hard her ribs ached.

That was the inventory. That was the brother Coral had been mailing postcards to for six years: the pirate-curse man, the pharmacy voice, the boy who once convinced an entire bus that today was opposite day and watched, delighted, as strangers held doors shut for each other.

The photograph in her hands showed a young man sitting on the hood of a car she didn't recognize, in front of a building she didn't recognize, with a cigarette burning down between fingers she did not recognize, because her brother did not smoke. Her brother thought smoking was "a tax on people who are bad at math."

The young man was laughing at something outside the frame. He looked, Coral thought with a lurch, happy in a way she had no record of. Happy in a way that hadn't happened near her.

She turned it over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting that was his but looser, drunker, younger-feeling than any handwriting she knew: *R., June. He says the lake's haunted. It is.*

June of which year? Who was R.? What lake? There were lakes everywhere; they lived in a state that was mostly lakes pretending to be land.

She picked up the next photograph. Same car. Different young man — or maybe the same one, thinner, harder to tell — and Anders beside him, and this time Anders wasn't laughing. He was looking directly into the camera with an expression Coral had never catalogued. Not sad. Not scared. *Calculating.* Like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want anyone to see.

On the back: *He thinks I don't know. I know.*

Know what? Know what?

Coral put the photograph down very carefully, the way you put down something that might be listening.

* * *

Five months before the fire, Anders woke up at 5:50 a.m. for a shift he no longer had.

This was not denial, exactly. Denial would have been simpler. It was more like momentum — his body had learned the shape of the morning and saw no reason to unlearn it just because the shop had let him go over a shortfall in the till that everyone knew wasn't his, and that everyone also knew he'd take the fall for anyway, because Anders was the kind of guy who absorbed blame the way carpet absorbs smell: thoroughly, permanently, and with a faint air of having deserved it.

He lay in bed and listened to the house. Mom's alarm wouldn't go off for another twenty minutes. Coral's light was already on — it was always already on; his sister slept like a guard dog, one ear up — and he could hear the particular silence of her reading, which was somehow louder than other people's talking.

"Morning, warden," he said when he passed her door, and she said, without looking up, "You're up early," and he said, "I'm up employed," and she almost smiled, and he carried that almost-smile downstairs like a coin he'd found on the sidewalk.

The thing about losing your job quietly, in a small house, in a family fluent in the language of not-asking, was that nobody asked. This was the great gift of the Nystroms and also, though none of them would have admitted it, the great disease. His mother noticed the missing paycheck the way she noticed weather — acknowledged internally, never aloud. Coral noticed too. Coral noticed everything. That was the whole problem with Coral: she was a vault with eyes.

So Anders started driving out to the lake.

Not the haunted lake — well, yes, actually, the haunted lake, because R. had told him about it and R. turned out to be right, the place had a feeling to it, a low hum under the water like the lake was thinking. He'd go out there on Tuesdays, because Tuesdays were for nothing, and sit on the hood of the car he couldn't afford the payments on anymore, and smoke cigarettes he claimed to find disgusting, and do arithmetic.

The arithmetic went like this: the house owed money. Not drowning-money, but water-over-the-ankles money, rising. His mother would never say it. Coral would solve it somehow, silently, by eating less or selling something or taking a second job she'd describe as "a project." And Anders — Anders, who had absorbed the till-shortfall, who was a generalist, who had exactly one marketable skill left and it was being liked — Anders did the math and found that the only asset in the family's portfolio large enough to matter was the house itself.

Insured, naturally. Their mother was thorough that way.

He didn't think of it as arson at first. He thought of it as *a solution*, the way you think of a kidney stone as a pebble. Words come later. Words are always later.

What he thought about, mostly, out at the humming lake, was how to make sure nobody got hurt. That was the whole design constraint. Everything else was negotiable — the timing, the cause, the story — but nobody gets hurt. He ran scenarios like drills. He checked the smoke detectors twice, then bought new batteries for all of them with grocery money, and when his mother said, "New batteries?" he said, "Safety audit," and she nodded, because in this family you nodded and moved along.

There was one variable he couldn't drill for, and it had a name, and the name was Coral.

Because here was the flaw in the plan, the single point of failure: his sister did not sleep like other people. His sister heard things. And on the night it happened — whatever night it finally became — there was a nonzero chance, and he knew it, that she would hear him go out, or hear him come back, or simply *know*, the way she always knew, the way she'd known about the till, about the welding program, about the girl in eleventh grade whose name he'd never once spoken aloud.

He could lie to his mother. His mother wanted to believe him; that made it easy.

He could not lie to Coral. But maybe — this was the theory he built, night after night, out at the lake, flicking cigarette ash into the water like a man feeding something — maybe he wouldn't have to. Maybe she'd see, and know, and say nothing. His sister was the best keeper of secrets in three counties. She'd been keeping his whole life.

All he had to do was ask without asking.

* * *

By Thursday, Coral had gone through the entire box twice and organized it three ways: chronologically (impossible — half the photos weren't dated), geographically (worse), and emotionally, which was the only system that worked and also the only one that hurt.

The emotional sort produced three piles.

Pile one: the brother she knew. School pictures, beach trips, the infamous photo of him at fourteen holding a fish with an expression of profound personal betrayal. These she could look at the way you look at old currency — familiar, out of circulation, still legal tender somewhere.

Pile two: the stranger. The lake photos, the car-hood photos, the ones where he stood among people Coral had never met, wearing clothes she'd never seen, in a life running parallel to hers like a train on the next track over. In these he smoked. In these he wore a leather jacket that someone had clearly given him, because their mother would rather die. In these he was *known* — visibly, comfortably known — by a whole cast of characters who had never appeared in her inventory, who had attended his funeral, probably, and wept, and known things about him that she did not.

Pile three: one photograph. Just one, and it was the one she kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

In it, Anders stands in front of their actual house — their real house, the one that burned — at dusk, and the light is doing something strange, coming low and orange across the lawn, so that the windows glow like the house is already lit from some fire yet to arrive. He isn't looking at the camera. He's looking at the house. And his face —

His face is the arithmetic face. The calculating one. But softer now, sadder, with something underneath it that Coral spent a long time refusing to name and finally named anyway, alone, at midnight, with the tea gone cold:

Guilt. Pre-guilt. The expression of a man apologizing in advance.

On the back, in that loose pencil hand: *Forgive me. Both of you. You'll see why.*

Both of you.

Coral sat with that for a long time. *Both of you.* Him and Mom. Obviously. Obviously it meant him and Mom.

Except that the sentence had been written on a photograph, and photographs get seen by whoever finds them, and he'd kept it in a box, and boxes get found, and Anders — funny, careless Anders, who labeled everything, who narrated everything, who had never in his life done anything without an audience in mind — Anders had written *both of you* on the back of a picture of their house at dusk, and tucked it at the bottom of a shoebox, and Coral's ear, the one that was always up, the guard-dog ear, the ear that had heard him come home that night six years ago smelling of smoke and saying *I was at R.'s, I fell asleep, don't wait up* —

Her ear heard something now.

She spread the photographs across the table and looked at the stranger in them properly, without mercy, and the stranger looked back with her brother's exact face, and she realized with a cold sliding feeling that she had spent six years mailing postcards to a version of Anders who had never existed — the pirate-curse boy, the pharmacy voice — while the real one, this tired, smoking, calculating, pre-apologizing one, had been out at some haunted lake doing arithmetic about their house while she slept one room away, guarding nothing.

The receipt was still in the box. Storage unit, cash, no name, five weeks before the fire.

Storage units held things. Things like accelerants. Things like paperwork. Things like the contents of a house that someone intended, quietly, to preserve — or to remove.

Coral looked at the clock. It was Thursday evening, which meant tomorrow was Friday, which meant Saturday she'd write the weekly postcard, and for the first time in six years she had no idea what it should say. *Dear Anders, today I learned you had a friend named R. and a jacket and a plan.* The pen would refuse. The whole ritual would refuse.

She gathered the photographs back into the box, all except the last one, the house at dusk, which she propped against the chipped blue mug where she'd have to look at it every morning.

Then she did the thing she did instead of crying, which was make a list:

1. Find R.
2. Find the storage unit.
3. Ask Mom what she knew about the jacket, the lake, the smoking. (Crossed out. Mom knew everything; asking Mom was opening a different box entirely.)
4. Saturday's postcard.

She stared at item four for a long time. Then, beneath it, in smaller writing, she added a fifth line, and the fifth line frightened her more than the fire report ever had, more than the missing page, more than the word *arson* itself, because it meant the ritual was cracking, the vault was developing a voice:

5. Tell Edda Vask about the photographs. Sunday. Bring the box.

She didn't know why she wrote it. Edda Vask was a dictionary with legs, a strange old woman who wanted a student the way drowning men want rafts, and Coral had agreed to one lesson — one — out of pity and library etiquette and some itch she refused to examine. She had not agreed to bring her brother's ghost to the woman's kitchen table.

But the words were on the list now, and Coral Nystrom did not cross things off lists by denying them.

Outside, the evening came down gray and honest, and in the window the photograph glowed faintly, the house lit from within by a fire that hadn't happened yet, and her brother's face said *forgive me, both of you,* to a sister who was only beginning to understand how many versions of forgiveness she might owe — and how few of them she'd earned the right to give.
