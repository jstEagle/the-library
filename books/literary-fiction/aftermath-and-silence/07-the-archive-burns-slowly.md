## 7. The Archive Burns Slowly

Tomas found out about the library the way he found out about most things in his family: by accident, from a stranger.

He was in the break room at the transit depot, eating a sandwich he didn't want, when a woman named Priya from scheduling mentioned that her book club was having its last meeting at the Halcyon Branch. *Closing down,* she said, scrolling her phone. *They announced it Tuesday. They're clearing the whole building by the end of the month.*

He called Freya. She didn't pick up, which wasn't unusual. He texted: *Why am I hearing this from Priya Okafor-adjacent in scheduling?* She didn't answer that either.

So he did what he'd learned to do over the past three weeks, since the letter, since the photograph, since the truce with its one-week clock that had quietly become three weeks of Freya saying *I'm still cross-referencing* in a voice that gave nothing away: he went to the source.

The Halcyon Branch at four in the afternoon was a body already being divided. Yellow signs on the doors—*CLOSING MARCH 31. THANK YOU FOR 62 YEARS.*—and inside, the particular chaos of an ending: carts stacked with books in no order he could understand, a man on a ladder unbolting a shelf bracket like he was extracting a tooth. The lights were on but the building had the dimness of a place that had stopped believing in itself.

Freya was at the front desk, stamping a discard slip with the mechanical calm of someone performing surgery on herself.

"You didn't tell me," Tomas said.

She looked up. "It was in the staff newsletter. It's public information."

"I don't read your staff newsletter, Freya. Nobody reads your staff newsletter. That's the whole problem with this library."

A patron at the far end of the desk—a teenager with headphones—glanced over and away. Freya waited until he'd shuffled off with his stack of DVDs before she spoke, and when she did, her voice was pitched for Tomas alone, low and level.

"It was always closing, Tomas. You knew that. We both knew that. The reroute, the budget cuts, the whole thing. I just didn't want to talk about it while—" She gestured vaguely, a gesture that could have meant *while Mom was dying* or *while we were chasing her ghosts* or *while I was lying to you*, and Tomas understood, with a cold settling in his chest, that she meant all three.

"You're cross-referencing," he said. "That's what you said. Three weeks ago. The rent book, the archives. You said you'd found something."

"I'm still working on it."

"Show me what you've got."

"It's not ready."

"It's not *ready*." He heard his own voice go up and made himself come back down. "Freya. We had a deal. One week. We follow the letter, we follow the rent book, and then whatever we decide, we decide together. That was the deal."

"And we will. I just need—"

The shredder started.

It was down the hall, behind the staff door, a sound like a long sustained tearing, and Tomas watched Freya's face do something small and terrible: a flicker, a flinch, gone in half a second. But he'd spent his whole childhood reading that face. He'd had to. Their mother had been a locked room and Freya was the only window into it, and Tomas had learned to read the glass.

"What's back there," he said.

"Nothing. Records retention. It's standard procedure when a branch closes."

"Which records."

"Tomas."

"*Which records.*"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. The shredder answered for her, grinding on and on, patient as digestion, and Tomas was already walking.

"Tomas—" She came around the desk after him. "Tomas, don't—"

The staff room was narrow and fluorescent-lit, and along one wall stood four grey bins, the kind offices used for confidential waste. The nearest one was half full of paper strips, pale and uniform, like the innards of some enormous pale animal. On the table beside it sat a stack of folders with the Halcyon Branch crest on the tabs. *LOCAL HISTORY — MARROW STREET WARD. 1974–1991.*

The top folder was open. Inside, a page of handwritten minutes, a community association, dated June 1986. Someone had flagged it with a green sticky note, the kind Freya used, the kind Tomas had watched her use all his life, her little system of colored flags like a code only she could read. The note said, in her small square handwriting: *Adeyemi? Cross-check against rent book, p. 14.*

"Freya," he said, and his voice came out wrong, flattened, "what is this."

She stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, and he watched her decide—watched it happen in real time, the calculation behind her eyes, the weighing of what to give him. He'd seen their mother do the same thing a thousand times. The pause before the deflection. The smile that ended conversations.

"It's a shredding pile," Freya said. "It's nothing."

"It's flagged. You flagged it. *Adeyemi?*"

"It's a *question mark*, Tomas. It might not even be her. I didn't want to say anything until I was sure, because you—" She stopped herself, but too late, and they both heard what she'd been about to say: *because you get like this.*

"Because I what," Tomas said quietly.

"Tomas. Please. Let me finish the work and then I'll tell you everything, I promise—"

"You promised Mom you'd call the doctor. You promised me you'd tell me when the hospice nurse started overnights. You promised me one week." He picked up the folder. The pages inside were soft with age, mimeographed, the ink gone purple-brown. "You've had three. And the whole time, you've been feeding our mother's history into a *shredder*."

"I flagged those so they *wouldn't* be shredded—"

"And this one?" He pulled a second folder from the bin, unshredded, waiting. *Marrowside Tenants' Association — correspondence, 1988–89.* No flag on it. No green note. Nothing. "This one have a question mark too? Or does it just not matter because you already decided what it says?"

"It's not like that."

"Then what's it like, Freya? Tell me. Use words. You're so good with words."

Her face changed. He'd gone too far and he knew it, and some part of him that was still decent said *stop*, but a larger part—the part that had spent forty years being managed, soothed, deflected, kept in the dark by two people who loved him and had decided, apparently as a matter of policy, that love and information were opposites—that part wanted the wound.

"You know what's funny?" he said. "I used to think it was just her. Mom. The silence. I used to think, okay, she came from somewhere terrible, she survived something, she built the wall for a reason, fine. But you—" He held up the flagged folder. "You've had three weeks with a photograph of her laughing in front of a house we've never seen, with people we've never heard of, and your response was to sit here alone, at night, in a dying library, and *curate* what the rest of us get to know."

"I was protecting you."

"*There it is.*" He laughed, and the laugh was ugly. "You hear it? You hear how you sound? That's her sentence, Freya. That's word for word the sentence she used on us our entire lives. *I was protecting you.* Protecting us from what? From Lagos? From Adeyemi? From a man named Chidi who you've apparently known about for weeks and haven't said one word—"

Her face went white.

He stopped.

The shredder, down the hall, had gone quiet. In the silence Tomas heard the fluorescent lights, the tick of the radiator, his own breathing.

"Chidi," Freya said. Just the name. Her voice had gone very careful, the voice of someone standing at the edge of ice.

"You just said it," Tomas said. "You said *a man named Chidi*. I made it up. I was guessing. I pulled it out of the air because it was in the letter—Idris's letter, the one you wanted to burn, the one with the line *tell them Chidi kept his promise*—and you *said it back to me like you know who he is.*"

"Tomas—"

"How long."

"Please—"

"*How long have you known.*"

She looked at the shredder. She looked at the bins. And Tomas watched his sister—his competent, careful, infuriating sister, who had held their mother's hand through the last two years while he could barely stay in the room—make the choice. He watched her reach for the deflection. He watched her find it. He watched her open her mouth to give him the smile that ends conversations.

And then something in her broke instead.

"The same month," she said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "The same month she changed her name. Legally. I found it in the county records, it took me two days, she filed the name change in March 1990 and in March 1990 there's a deportation order for a Chidi Adeyemi, and the records list—" Her hand went to her mouth. "They list her as his wife, Tomas. They list *Adaeze*. Our mother was married before our father. She was married to him, and he was deported, and within—I found a death notice, I think it's him, I *think*, I'm not sure, I didn't want to tell you until I was *sure*—"

"Sure of what? That our father might not be our father? That Mom let a man be deported so she could stay? That she spent thirty years never saying his name and you spent three weeks doing the exact same thing?" He was shouting now. He didn't care. The teenager with the headphones was back in the doorway and Freya was crying and Tomas couldn't stop. "You hid it. You hid it from me, in the building where you *work*, surrounded by the records, and then the building starts closing and you know what happens to records in a closing building, Freya? They get *destroyed*. You were going to let it all burn down around you rather than say it out loud. You became her. You understand that? You *became her*. The whole time we were digging up her silence you were building your own, and you didn't even notice, you didn't even—"

"Don't," Freya said. It came out very small. "Don't say that."

"It's true."

"You don't know what it's like—"

"Neither did she! That was the whole point! Nobody knew what it was like for her because she *never told anyone*, and it ate her alive, and I watched it eat her alive, and now I'm watching you do it with better filing."

The words hung there. Freya had both hands over her mouth now, and above them her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath the fury, terrified—the look of someone who has just seen her own face in a mirror she didn't know was there.

The staff door opened. A woman in a cardigan—Tomas would learn later her name was Ruth, twenty-nine years at Halcyon—stood holding a fresh stack of folders, took in the scene, and quietly set them on the table next to the bins. On top of the stack: *Marrowside Community Hall — event permits, 1985–1992.* Freya's green flag was already on the tab. *Adeyemi table?*

Nobody moved.

"You flagged that one too," Tomas said. His voice had gone quiet, which was worse. "So which is it, Freya? Which ones do we get to know about, and which ones go in the bin? Because I'd really like to know the criteria. I'd like to know what I did to end up on the shredding pile."

"Tomas—"

"The library closes in nine days," he said. He put the folder down on the table, carefully, the way you put down something that might go off. "Nine days, and then every scrap of paper in this building that isn't in someone's basement is gone forever. Our mother spent thirty years making sure we'd never know who she was. You've got nine days to decide if you're going to do the same."

He walked out past the discard carts, past the man still unbolting shelves, past the yellow signs on the doors, out into the cold.

Behind him, faint through the closing door, he heard the shredder start again.

He stood on the steps of the Halcyon Branch with his hands in his pockets and his heart going like a fist on a wall, and he took out his phone, and he scrolled to the one contact he'd never called, the old man from the funeral who had pressed a letter into Freya's hands and vanished.

Idris Okafor.

Tomas pressed call before he could think better of it.

It rang once. Twice.

"Tomas." The old man's voice, warm and unhurried, as if he'd been waiting by the phone for three weeks. As if he'd known. "I wondered when one of you would call. Come to the house on Marrow Street. There are things you both need to hear, and I think—" a pause, gentle and terrible, "—I think your sister is not ready to hear them yet. Come alone."
