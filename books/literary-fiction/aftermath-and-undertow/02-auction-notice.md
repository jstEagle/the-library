## 2. Auction Notice

The notice arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like a small cruelty. Tuesdays were my efficient days—the days I stripped the practice hall floors and refinished them before the morning students arrived, the days I could move through the building like weather, noticed only as a change in pressure. A Tuesday notice meant I had to stand in the mail alcove of my apartment building, holding an envelope with a law firm's return address, while Mrs. Okonkwo's terrier sniffed at my ankles and the radiator clanked its one note.

I knew before I opened it. You always know. The body is faster than the mind about these things; my hands had gone cold at the sight of the firm's name—Halloway & Prue, which sounded less like lawyers and more like a pair of Victorian ghosts who collected debts—and by the time I'd slit the envelope with my janitor's key (a habit; it was the sharpest thing I owned), I had already read the letter three times in my head.

*Notice of Public Auction. Property: 14 Larkspur Lane, Lake Verne. Date: six weeks hence.*

The lake house. My mother's phrasing, when she'd finally deigned to mention it last spring, had been *the cottage*, as if shrinking the word might shrink the grief inside it. Sixteen rooms, a boathouse, a stretch of shoreline where the water went glassy at dusk and stayed that way all night, like something holding its breath. Fourteen Larkspur Lane was not a cottage. It was the stage set of our family's best decade, and now it was going under the hammer because Vivienne Delacroix had decided, in her way, that the past was a room you eventually aired out—or sold, whichever came first.

I stood there long enough that the terrier lost interest and Mrs. Okonkwo called him inside, and then I did what I always do with unbearable information: I folded it into quarters, put it in my uniform pocket, and went to work.

* * *

You can clean around anything. That's the secret of my profession, or at least of my version of it. Grief, dread, the memory of your brother's laugh coming through a practice-room door in a key he never quite mastered—you can mop around all of it. The floor doesn't care what you're carrying. It only wants to come up shining.

That night I worked the east corridor, the one with the senior studios, and I let myself do the thing I'm not supposed to do, the thing I've gotten very good at pretending I don't do: I listened.

Door twelve was open a crack. Inside, a boy—I could see his ankles crossed under the piano bench, one foot keeping time against nothing—was working through a Chopin nocturne with the particular fury of someone who loves the piece more than he can yet play it. He kept stumbling at the same bar. Over and over. And each time, instead of backing up to fix it properly, he hurled himself at it again, faster, as if speed could bully the notes into submission.

Theo used to do that. Theo believed, with his whole nine-year-old heart, that music was a contest between himself and the composer, and that the composer was winning.

I stood in the corridor with my mop dripping a slow dark line onto the tile and let the sound go through me. This is the arrangement I made with myself years ago: proximity without participation. I get to be near the music the way you can be near a fire in winter if you agree to stay outside the window. It's not warmth, exactly. It's the memory of warmth, refracted through glass, and some nights that's enough, and some nights—

Some nights the boy in door twelve got the bar right, finally, and played it through clean, and I had to turn off my hearing the way you'd close a door, because the joy in it was worse than the struggle. Joy was the thing we'd been rationed after. Joy was what the family budget no longer allowed.

I finished the corridor. I wrung out the mop. In the staff bathroom, under fluorescent light that flattered no one, I took out the folded notice and read it again, properly this time, letting the words land where they wanted to land.

*All contents of the premises shall be included in the sale unless otherwise claimed by lawful owners prior to auction date.*

Contents. The word sat there being innocent. Contents: the wicker chairs on the porch where Edmund held court every summer evening, dispensing opinions on his students' futures like a man dealing cards he'd already counted. Contents: the kitchen table where Vivienne arranged flowers with the severity of a woman pruning a hedge. Contents: the boathouse, the dock, the water itself—not for sale, the water, but included somehow, the way a wound is included in a photograph of a person.

And contents: the locked room at the top of the stairs.

* * *

Here is what most people know about the Delacroix house, if they know anything: that it was beautiful, that the Delacroixes summered there, that Edmund Delacroix wrote half his famous masterclasses on the porch overlooking Lake Verne. Here is what nobody knows, because we made sure of it: that the room at the end of the upstairs hall has been locked since the autumn Theo died, and that everything in it is exactly where he left it.

His sheet music, dog-eared, annotated in pencil so hard the pages tore. His shoes by the door, small, still holding the shape of him. The composition he was working on that last summer—eleven bars of something in D minor that stopped mid-phrase, the way a sentence stops when the person speaking it looks out the window and forgets they were talking. I have heard those eleven bars in my head for twelve years. I have never once allowed myself to hear them aloud.

The key to that room lived, for a long time, in my father's desk drawer, under a false bottom he thought none of us knew about. Then, at some point—it must have been eight, nine years ago—the key disappeared, and the lock was changed, and the whole subject was closed the way subjects are closed in my family: silently, thoroughly, with everyone agreeing not to remember the agreement.

My mother says the room should stay shut. My mother says many things in the tone of a woman closing doors.

I called her that night from my apartment, the phone hot against my ear, the notice spread flat on my kitchen table under the single lamp. She picked up on the fourth ring, and I heard the television behind her, muted, and the specific quality of silence she deploys when she already knows why you're calling.

"You got the letter," Vivienne said. Not a question.

"Six weeks," I said. "Mom, the auction includes the contents."

"It includes *junk*." A pause; ice shifting in a glass. "Yara, that house has been sitting there draining money for twelve years. Your father won't discuss it, so I've discussed it with the lawyers instead. It's time."

"Time," I repeated. The word tasted like the floor cleaner I use in the practice halls—industrial lemon, meant to suggest freshness, actually suggesting nothing but itself.

"To let go. That's what Dr. Reiner says. Grief that stays in a house becomes—" she searched for the phrase her therapist had supplied, and I waited, giving her time, the way you wait for a student to find a note, "—becomes architecture."

"Becomes architecture," I said. "Is that what you tell yourself about the locked room? That it's just... load-bearing?"

The silence on the line changed texture. Somewhere behind her, the muted television flickered blue against a wall I couldn't see.

"That room is closed, Yara."

"It's going to be opened, though. Isn't it? By strangers. An auctioneer's inventory crew, walking through with clipboards, opening every door because the notice says *all contents*. They'll catalog his shoes, Mom. They'll put a lot number on his music."

"They'll put a lot number on *furniture*," she said sharply, and then, softer, in the voice she used when she was deciding to be reasonable, which was worse than any sharpness: "Sweetheart. I know you keep things in that house. I know you think the house keeps them safe. But a house is just wood and water damage. Believe me. I've watched this one rot for twelve years."

"From the outside," I said. "You watched it from the outside."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again, her voice had the smooth, lacquered calm that I had spent my childhood mistaking for serenity and now recognized as effort—the enormous daily labor of a woman holding a lid down with both hands.

"Your father built his life on that lake. On those summers, those students, that porch. If the house goes, it goes quietly, and we all get to be people who once had a beautiful place instead of people who couldn't bear to look at one. There are worse fates, Yara. I've seen them. I've *lived* adjacent to them."

"And if I want to look?"

"Then you'll be looking alone." She said it without cruelty, which is the remarkable thing about my mother; she delivers her hardest sentences the way a bank delivers a foreclosure notice—politely, in writing, effective immediately. "The auction is in six weeks. Come say goodbye to the house if you need to. Say goodbye to the *room*, if you must. But don't stand in front of the bulldozer, sweetheart. It's very tiring, and the bulldozer always wins."

She hung up before I could answer, which was also a kind of signature.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I sat at my kitchen table with the notice and did arithmetic, which is what I do instead of crying—numbers being the one language in my family that never lied, even when the people fluent in it did.

Six weeks. The auction was public, which meant the house would sell to whoever bid highest, which meant strangers with clipboards, which meant the room at the end of the hall opened by hands that had never learned to be careful with what was inside it. Eleven bars of unfinished music, a pair of small shoes, a summer interrupted mid-phrase—all of it itemized, stickered, sold to the highest bidder who would never know what they were buying, or worse, would know and buy it anyway, because scandal memorabilia holds its value.

Unless the contents were claimed first. Unless someone stood in front of the bulldozer.

I looked at my hands under the lamp. Janitor's hands now, scarred with cleaning-solution burns, the nails cut short for gloves. Once, these hands had been insured, I was told, for an amount my father mentioned at dinner parties the way other men mention their boats. Once, these hands had been the whole point of me.

Twelve years ago, I had learned the family's first lesson: disappear, and you're safe. Be furniture. Be architecture. Let the house hold what you cannot.

But the house was going on the block, and with it everything we'd stored inside it—including, I suspected, including with a certainty that had been growing in me like water damage behind wallpaper, things that weren't mine to store. Things someone had put away deliberately. Things that said, in ink and dates and signatures, what really happened on the lake the summer Theo went out and didn't come back.

I had always assumed the truth was drowned. Sitting there in the lamplight, folding and unfolding the legal notice until the creases went soft as cloth, I understood for the first time that the truth might simply be filed. Upstairs. Behind a door my mother called closed and my father called nothing at all.

Six weeks.

I got out my calendar—the paper one, the one nobody sees—and wrote a single entry on the Saturday two weeks out, in pencil, because pencil is honest about being erasable: *Go to the house. Alone.*

Then, underneath, smaller, the way you write the thing you're afraid of:

*Open the door.*
