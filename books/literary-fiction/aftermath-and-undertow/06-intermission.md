## 6. Intermission

The girl practiced Chopin the way other people committed crimes—boldly, with witnesses, and no apparent fear of consequences.

I heard her through the door of Studio 4 on a Tuesday night, third week of October, while I was still wringing out my mop like it owed me money. The Nocturne in C-sharp minor. Everyone plays that one; it's the piece you learn when you want to sound deeper than you are. But this girl took the opening phrase and did something unforgivable to it. She slowed down. She let the melody hang there, unfinished, like a sentence she wasn't sure deserved its period.

I stood in the hallway with wet hands and listened until she stopped.

Then I made the mistake of looking through the little window in the door. She was maybe sixteen, dark hair escaping a bun, sitting the way Theo used to sit—leaning slightly left, as if her whole body were listening ahead of her hands. She caught me looking. Most students would have looked away, embarrassed at being observed by the cleaning lady. This one smiled and kept playing, as if to say: *yes, I know what this is worth.*

Her name was Noor Haddad. I learned it from the practice-room sign-up sheet, which I technically wasn't supposed to read but had been reading for twelve years, the way some people read horoscopes—a small daily act of faith that the future might contain something for them after all.

* * *

She started finding me. That's the thing about invisibility—it only works if nobody's looking, and Noor was constitutionally incapable of not looking. She'd appear in the corridor while I buffed the terrazzo outside the recital hall, and instead of walking past she'd sit on the radiator and talk, the way you talk to someone whose opinion can't hurt you because they don't officially exist.

"You played here," she said one night. Not a question. "The janitor who works nights said there used to be a Delacroix on the wall of honor."

"The janitor who works nights is me," I said. "And there used to be a lot of things on that wall."

"Yara Delacroix." She said it carefully, tasting it. "You won the Concours at fourteen. My teacher has a recording. You played the Rachmaninoff Third like you were defusing it."

"I was," I said. "It exploded anyway."

She laughed—too loud for the corridor, the kind of laugh that gets students reported—and then went quiet in a way that told me she'd been doing homework. "What happened?"

The mop handle was very interesting. "Life happened. It does that."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "But it's the one that comes free of charge."

She didn't push. That was the first thing I loved about her—not the hands, though the hands were extraordinary, not the ear, though the ear was frankly unfair—but the restraint. She could have pressed and pressed and gotten nothing, because pressing me was like pressing water. Instead she just nodded and said, "Okay. But someday I'm going to ask again," and went back into Studio 4 and played the Nocturne again, better, and I stood in the hall like a ghost attending its own séance.

Because here is what I hadn't said: she reminded me of Theo. Not in talent—Theo's gift was stranger, more angular, all counterpoint and trouble—but in the way music sat on her like it belonged there. Theo used to say playing felt like breathing for other people, except he'd never once been bored of breathing. He'd have been twenty-four now. He'd have hated the conservatory by then, probably, and written something furious and wonderful about it, and Edmund would have called it *promising* in that voice he used for things he intended to improve.

I started leaving the studio window uncovered when Noor practiced late. A small corruption of my duties. The floors got mopped slower on those nights. Nobody audited joy.

* * *

One Thursday in November she stayed past midnight, working through a piece I didn't recognize—modern, spiky, with these long silences built right into the score, rests you had to perform rather than survive.

"What is that?" I asked from the doorway. First time I'd ever spoken first.

"My own," she said. Then, quickly, like she was embarrassed: "It's not finished. It doesn't have an ending yet. Every time I get near the end, it turns into something else."

"Theo—" I stopped. Started over. "Someone I knew used to say a piece isn't finished when there's nothing left to add. It's finished when you stop being afraid of the ending."

Noor turned on the bench to look at me fully. "Who was he?"

And there it was—the open door, swinging wide, twelve years of wind coming through it. I stood in that doorway holding a mop like a staff, and I thought: *tell her.* Tell her about the lake, about the gala program Edmund wouldn't move even when the weather report came in, about how Theo went out on the water because our father said the rehearsal schedule allowed for exactly forty minutes of recreation and Theo, fifteen years old and obedient in the ways that mattered, believed him. Tell her about the letters in the attic, the ones with the board's letterhead, the ones that proved Edmund knew the dock pilings were rotten and scheduled the regatta anyway because cancellation would have cost him the season's centerpiece.

Tell her, and watch her face do what faces do when the story stops being a story.

"He was my brother," I said. And then, because the truth is a staircase and I only had one step in me: "He drowned. A long time ago."

Noor didn't say *I'm sorry*—another point in her favor. She just looked at me with those unafraid eyes and said, "Is that why you stopped?"

"It's why I stopped playing," I said. "The stopping everything else came later, and took longer."

She reached over and closed the fallboard over the keys, gently, like tucking something in. "You should come hear the ending," she said. "When I find it."

I almost told her everything that night. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now. I stood in that doorway and the words were right there, lined up, ready—and I swallowed them, because telling Noor meant making her a witness, and I knew what this family did to witnesses. We promoted them, or we buried them, or we made them custodians.

* * *

Edmund found me at the house.

I'd driven up on a Saturday morning to walk the property before the auction—six weeks had become three, and the auctioneer's signs had gone up at the road like accusations. His car was already in the drive, silver and immaculate, and he was standing on the porch with a clipboard, measuring the sunroom with his eyes.

"Dad."

"Yara." He smiled, and the smile still worked, that was the terrible thing. Twelve years and it still worked, warm as a lamp left on for you. "I hoped I'd find you here. Come sit. It's cold."

We sat on the porch swing, which creaked in a key I remembered. Below us the lake lay flat and gray, keeping its counsel.

"I've been thinking about this auction," he said. "It's undignified. Strangers walking through your mother's garden, appraising her china. Vivienne means well, but she's tired, and tired people make messy decisions." He set the clipboard aside. "So I've made an offer. Full asking price, cash, no contingencies. The bank will accept it today."

The relief hit first—I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Three weeks of dread, dissolved with one sentence. Then the second sentence arrived.

"There's one condition." He said it lightly, the way he announced program changes. "The contents come to me. Everything inside. Furniture, papers, personal effects. You'd sign a release—that's standard, Yara, it's how estate sales work—and take whatever you like from your own room. The rest stays intact. I'll keep the house as it is. A family archive, if you like. Nothing thrown away."

"An archive," I repeated.

"A memory," he said, and his voice did the thing it always did when it wanted to be mistaken for tenderness. "This house holds your brother. I know that better than anyone. I simply think his things deserve a custodian with... perspective. Not strangers at an auction, pawing through boxes."

*Perspective.* In Edmund's vocabulary that word meant *my version*, and *custodian* meant *guard*, and *archive* meant *sealed*. I thought of the attic, the letters in their folder, the board's letterhead crisp under dust. I thought of the locked room at the end of the hall where Theo's sheet music still sat on the stand, open to the piece he never finished, waiting twelve years for someone brave enough to play the ending wrong.

"You'd keep everything," I said slowly, "exactly as it is."

"Exactly." He smiled again. "And you'd be free, Yara. Truly free. No auction, no lawyers, no more of this—" he gestured at the house, at me, at the whole tangled decade between us—"digging. You could visit whenever you liked. You could finally rest."

And God help me, I wanted it. That's the part I keep turning over. He wasn't offering me a bribe; he was offering me my life back. The quiet one. The small clean one with the mop and the night shifts and Noor's music through the door. All I had to do was sign away the paper trail, let him curate the past, and go back to being invisible—which, if I'm honest, I had gotten very good at. Some days it was the only thing I was good at.

He held out a pen. He'd brought a pen. Of course he had; Edmund never entered a room without the means to close a deal.

I looked at the pen. I looked at the lake, flat and gray and full.

"Let me think about it," I said.

"Of course." He capped the pen, unhurt, patient, a man who owned time itself. "Take a day or two. But Yara—" he paused at the steps, and for one unguarded moment the charm flickered and I saw underneath it, the same thing I'd seen across the dinner table the summer Theo died: a man doing arithmetic. "Don't wait too long. Auctions have a way of surfacing things."

He drove off, and I sat on the creaking swing with the wind coming off the water, and I understood, finally, what he was really buying. Not the house. The house was a shell. He was buying the attic. He was buying the last copy of the truth and calling it an archive.

My phone buzzed in my pocket. A text from Noor, sent at 1 a.m. from Studio 4:

*Found the ending. It's in D minor. Come hear it?*

I stared at the screen for a long time. Then I typed back the only honest answer I had:

*I will. But first I have to go home and open a door.*
