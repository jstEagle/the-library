## 10. Coda, in D Minor

The key to the practice hall still worked. That was the first surprise.

I had assumed that after everything—the investigation, the resignation letter read aloud at a board meeting I was not invited to attend, the polite emails informing me my custodial position had been "restructured"—someone would have changed the locks out of spite or, worse, out of thoroughness. But no. The brass was cold under my thumb, and when I turned it, the mechanism gave its old familiar complaint, a click and a shudder, like a joint remembering an injury.

Twelve years of entering this building through service doors and side corridors, and here I was at nine o'clock on a Thursday night, letting myself in through the front of the practice wing like I owned it. Which, in a way I was still getting used to, I did not. The house on the lake—that I owned. The deed sat in a drawer in my apartment with my name spelled correctly on it for the first time in my life: Yara Delacroix, no longer a name to be managed but just a name, mine, slightly ridiculous, entirely true.

The hallway smelled the way it always had: floor wax and radiator dust and, underneath it, the ghost of a thousand hours of scales. Institutions have smells the way families have silences. You can leave them for a decade and walk back in and your body knows where it is before your mind agrees to.

* * *

I want to be honest about why I came, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I've learned to spend it carefully.

I told myself I came to say goodbye. The conservatory had been sold—new ownership, new name going up on the marquee by spring, some donor's surname in bronze letters where DELACROIX HALL used to be. My father's name, chiseled into the lintel in 1989, was already half-covered by scaffolding tarps. There is a particular kind of justice that arrives too late to feel like anything but logistics.

But that wasn't why. Goodbyes are what we tell ourselves when we can't admit we're coming back for something.

The truth is I'd been hearing the piece again. Theo's piece. The one he was writing the summer he died—eight bars of a melody in D minor that he played badly and loved completely, thumping it out on the lake house piano while the screen door banged and my mother called from the garden that if he broke another string she wasn't driving him into town for a replacement. He never finished it. He was fourteen. Fourteen-year-olds don't finish things; they abandon them magnificently, mid-phrase, certain the phrase will wait for them.

It hadn't waited. It had waited for me instead, which is a different thing entirely, and heavier.

For twelve years I could not hear D minor without leaving a room. At recitals I would study my program, my shoes, the exit signs. The key sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. And then, somewhere in the months since the dinner table shattered—since I asked my father the question and watched forty years of architecture come down around us—I noticed the stone had shifted. Not dissolved. Shifted. Enough that one night, doing inventory in the empty hall, I caught myself humming it. Eight bars. Badly. Standing in the dark with a mop in my hand like some tragic figure from a play nobody should write.

So: I came to play it. Or to try. Or to stand in front of the piano and fail at trying, which after everything might count as the same act.

* * *

The practice hall was the big one—the performance space, actually, though everyone called it the practice hall because that's where students were permitted to rehearse before they earned the real stage. The Steinway sat center floor under the work lights, lid closed, looking less like an instrument than a held breath.

I crossed the parquet in my sneakers. Old habit: I knew which boards creaked, had mapped them during years of silent passage, and I avoided them now not because I needed to be invisible anymore but because the skill doesn't unlearn itself. Some inheritances you don't get to refuse.

I lifted the lid.

The piano was out of tune. Of course it was; the tuners had been let go in the same restructuring that let go of me, and the instrument had spent months drifting, each string finding its own private opinion about pitch. A lesser pianist would have stopped there. A better pianist would have stopped there too, honestly. But I pressed middle D and heard it land somewhere near D, near enough, and something in me said: *this will do. This has always been enough. This is exactly how he left it.*

Theo's eight bars. I set my hands the way he used to—wrong, elbows too high, fingers flat—and played them.

Badly. Genuinely, comprehensively badly. My hands shook. My muscle memory had rusted into something closer to archaeology than technique, and the melody came out lopsided, a bird with one wing. I hit a wrong note in the fourth bar and flinched as if the room had witnessed me commit a crime.

And then—this is the part I keep turning over, the part I'll probably write about badly someday in a different book—I didn't stop.

That was new. For twelve years, wrong notes had been verdicts. Every mistake was evidence, every stumble a confirmation of the story we'd all agreed on: that Yara Delacroix was careless, that carelessness ran in her hands, that a boy went into the water because someone wasn't watching closely enough. I had internalized the sentence so thoroughly I'd stopped noticing I was serving it. Wrong note, guilty. Wrong note, guilty.

But the letters were in a safe-deposit box now, copies with the board's investigators, originals waiting for whatever came next. The story had been corrected in the public record, or at least amended. Edmund Delacroix had known the dock was rotten. Had known the water that June was high and cold and moving fast from snowmelt. Had known, and had scheduled the gala anyway, and had driven home past the lake with the windows up.

My father had been the careless one. And I was standing at a piano hitting wrong notes, and nothing happened. No verdict fell. The room just held the sound and let it go.

So I played the eight bars again. Better. Then again, better still, and somewhere around the fifth pass my hands remembered themselves—not the prodigy's hands, not the hands from the posters, but the ones underneath, the ones that had always just liked the sound of things. The melody opened up. I found where Theo had been going with it, or where I decided he'd been going, which after twelve years amounts to the same authorship. The phrase that stopped dead in bar eight—I carried it forward. Into a ninth bar. A tenth. It wasn't his ending. It couldn't be. But it was an ending, mine, built on his foundation the way you build anything on ground someone else cleared: imperfectly, gratefully, without pretending the ground was yours.

I played until the work lights hummed and my shirt stuck to my back and the out-of-tune strings made the D minor sound less like grief and more like weather—something that moves through and keeps moving.

* * *

When I finally lifted my hands, the silence afterward was different from other silences. Fuller. Like a room after guests have left happily rather than fled.

"You changed the ending."

Noor stood in the doorway. She had her coat on, her bag over her shoulder, the posture of someone who'd been about to leave and then very much hadn't. Her hair was escaping its braid the way it always did by evening. She looked, in the work light, suddenly older and younger at once—the way people do when they're deciding whether to say a true thing.

"How long have you been there?" I asked. My voice came out rough, unused to speaking at volume.

"Since the third time through." She came in a few steps, sneakered feet silent on the parquet—she'd learned the boards too, I realized. Of course she had. "The first two times you played it like you were apologizing."

"I was."

"The last time you played it like it was yours." She set her bag down on a front-row chair, an act of casual territorial claim that would have gotten me expelled at fourteen. "It's the piece, isn't it. The one from the file. Theo's."

Word traveled. Of course it did. The investigation had exhumed every document in the archive, and somewhere in the process a dead boy's unfinished manuscript had surfaced, catalogued, discussed in rooms I wasn't in. They'd asked me if I wanted it back. I'd said yes and then couldn't bring myself to open the folder for four months.

"It's his," I said. "I just—finished the sentence he started."

Noor nodded slowly, the way she did when she was filing something important. She was seventeen now, had been fifteen when I first started listening to her through the practice-room doors, playing Chopin like she was breaking into a house she'd been told she'd never own. She reminded me of Theo in ways I had never said aloud to anyone: the same appetite, the same refusal to play a phrase the same way twice, the same terrible beautiful confidence that the music would wait for her.

It had waited for me. I intended to tell her that, someday, when telling it wouldn't be a warning shot across her own future.

"Play it again," she said. "From the top. I want to learn it."

I almost laughed. "It's not finished-finished. It needs—"

"It's finished enough." She pulled out the chair beside hers and patted it, an absurd, bossy, wonderful gesture. "D minor, right? Show me the left hand."

And so, at nearly ten o'clock on a Thursday night, in a hall that would bear a stranger's name by spring, I sat down next to a seventeen-year-old and taught her the first eight bars of my brother's unfinished piece, my hands over hers at the difficult interval in bar six, both of us hitting wrong notes and neither of us stopping. The piano was out of tune. The radiators clanked. Somewhere a door banged in the wind off the lake, and neither of us flinched.

It was not forgiveness. I want to be clear about that, because forgiveness is a word people reach for too quickly, like a coat that fits anyone poorly. My father calls sometimes. I do not always answer. My mother sent a card at Christmas with no return address and a single line inside—*the garden survived the frost*—which is either an olive branch or a weather report, and I have decided I don't need to know which. Restoration is not on offer. Nothing here is being restored. The house is mine and cold and the locked room stays unlocked now, Theo's things in the ordinary light, his sheet music on the stand where anyone can see it.

But the sound was honest. For the first time in twelve years, the sound coming out of me matched what was actually in me—grief and love and theft and inheritance, all of it, nothing hidden, nothing arranged around. Just a woman at an out-of-tune piano, playing her brother's song badly, then beautifully, then badly again, while a girl who reminds her of him learns it bar by bar so it won't die twice.

We played it through four more times. On the last pass, Noor took the melody alone and I dropped out, hands in my lap, listening.

She carried it past bar eight without hesitating.

"That's where he stopped," I said quietly.

"I know," Noor said. "I'm not stopping."

Outside, the wind came off the lake and rattled the tall windows, and the work lights hummed their one long note, and the piece went on.
