## 2. Static Between Stations

I left the city at six in the morning, which is the hour when a person is most honest with themselves and least equipped to do anything about it. The rental car smelled of pine air freshener and someone else's decisions. I had packed light: two shirts, a suit for the lawyer, and the folder of estate papers that Wren had emailed twice and mailed once and finally read aloud to me over the phone in the tone she uses for reading obituaries.

"You could just sign everything," she'd said. "You don't have to come."

"I'm coming," I'd said, because it was easier than explaining why I wasn't sure I could. The house on Delmore Road had been sitting empty for eleven months, and somewhere inside it was my mother's piano under a sheet, and I had not yet figured out how to be in a room with a piano under a sheet.

The highway unspooled through country I pretended not to recognize. That was the trick of it — you can drive out of a place but not out of its radius. Every water tower had the wrong name and the right shape. Every field was the field behind our house, if you squinted, if you were tired enough, if you wanted it to be.

I want to be clear about what I am, professionally speaking. I am a man who can tell you the provenance of a cheese by its crumb structure. I have used the word *unctuous* without irony and been paid handsomely for it. For eleven years I have written about flavor the way a blind man might write about sunsets — with great authority and total faith in secondhand light. My tongue works fine. It reports nothing. Somewhere between one winter and the next, the wire between my mouth and whatever it is in me that *receives* got cut, and rather than announce this at a dinner party, I simply became very good at texture, temperature, and reputation.

Reputation carries a surprising amount of a meal. So does lighting.

So I drove north with the radio off, because radio is just opinions with a signal attached, and I was full up on those. But somewhere past the county line my hand found the dial anyway, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, and I surfed static for a while — farm reports, a sermon, a man selling trailers with the fervor of a man who has never sold anything else — until the tuner caught on something small and held it.

A guitar. One guitar, close-miked, slightly out of tune on the high E, which no professional would allow and no amateur would notice.

And then a melody.

I want to describe what happened to me accurately, and accuracy requires me to say that the first thing I felt was not wonder. It was annoyance. A territorial, petty, entirely human annoyance — *someone is playing her song* — because the melody was unmistakable. Four notes down, a hitch, then climbing. She played it every Sunday morning while the coffee went bitter in the pot, humming over the top of it because there were never any words. Twenty-some years of Sundays. Ansel used to sit on the kitchen floor with his back against the cabinets and listen with his eyes shut like the song was weather happening to him personally.

Except.

Except the woman on the radio was not humming.

She was singing words.

*"The porch light stayed on past reason,"* she sang, voice thin as onion skin, *"and the coffee went cold in the cup—"*

I did the math on the steering wheel. There are no words. There were never words. Mother hummed it the way other people pray, wordless and private, and when Wren once asked her what it was called she said, "It isn't finished," and laughed like that settled the matter forever.

But here were words, arriving one after another in her exact melodic footprints, and they were — God help me — *good*. Plain words. Porch lights and cold coffee and a boy's coat still hanging by the door. Words that sounded less written than remembered.

My hands were shaking before I understood why. Not fear exactly. Something adjacent to fear, the way a struck bell is adjacent to silence. I pulled onto the gravel shoulder without deciding to, because some part of me had concluded that driving and hearing this at the same time was more than one body should be asked to do.

The DJ came on after. His voice was old in the way of men who have been talking to strangers alone for decades — unhurried, a little grainy, fond.

"That was Lila Lockridge's unfinished thing, from a tape that showed up in my mailbox a few years back, unsigned, postmarked Marrow Bend. Played it every Sunday since. Some songs you don't finish. You just keep 'em where people can find 'em." A pause, paper rustling. "This is Hollis, and you're listening to WDOR, ninety-one-three, broadcasting from a closet in Harrow County because somebody's got to."

Marrow Bend. The name meant nothing and stuck anyway, like a burr through a sock.

I sat on the shoulder of Route 9 with my hazard lights ticking and listened to the rest of the hour. He played Hank Williams, then a local teenager singing into what sounded like a telephone, then Hank Williams again, and between each he talked — about the weather, about his hip, about a listener named Dot who'd lost her husband and liked the sad ones played slow. It was the worst programming I had ever heard and I could not turn it off. There was no taste in it anywhere, no curation, no reputation. Just a man and a signal and whoever happened to be within forty miles of his antenna, which apparently included me, sitting in a rented car beside a soybean field, crying in a manner I would have described in print as *uncontrolled* and privately knew to be something worse: grateful.

* * *

WDOR's studio turned out to be a converted feed store on the edge of Harrow proper, identifiable by a hand-painted sign and an antenna that looked like an apology. The ON AIR light was a literal bulb screwed into a fixture above the door, glowing even though the station was between programs, because Hollis told me later he liked people to know the room was always technically listening.

He was seventy if he was a day, built like a fence post that had decided to lean, wearing a cardigan the color of weak tea. When I walked in he looked at me for a long moment over his glasses.

"You've got her chin," he said.

"I—"

"Lila Lockridge's chin. Sat right there." He pointed at a wooden chair by the board. "Well. Didn't sit. Sent a tape. But I've imagined her sitting there enough times we're practically acquainted."

"My mother died last year," I said. "Eleven months ago."

"I know. You look like a man eleven months into it." He didn't offer sympathy; he offered coffee, which in that room amounted to the same thing. "Sit. You're Cyrus, I take it. The critic."

"The critic," I said, and heard how it sounded in that room — a room with a bulb for an ON AIR sign and a wall of tapes labeled in pencil — and for the first time in years the title embarrassed me slightly, like wearing a coat indoors.

"How do you know the song?" I asked. "How do you know it's hers?"

"Because the tape says so." He rummaged in a drawer and produced a cassette in a cracked case, the label handwritten: *L.L. — not done.* "And because I did my homework. Called around. Old friend of hers from the church choir confirmed the melody, said Lila used to hum it Sundays till the coffee got cold. Said there weren't never any words to it." He set the tape on the desk between us like evidence. "So either your mama wrote words and kept them secret, or somebody wrote words to her melody and mailed 'em to me from Marrow Bend. Either way, mister, somebody out there knows your family's business better than I'd comfortable say on air."

The room was very quiet. The bulb glowed. Somewhere a tape deck turned over with a soft mechanical sigh, patient as digestion.

"Can I hold it?" I asked.

Hollis hesitated — a real hesitation, the kind of a man who has handled other people's relics long enough to know their weight — and then slid it across the desk.

The plastic was warm from the drawer, or from my hand, or from nothing. The label's ink had gone brown at the edges. *Not done,* it said, in handwriting I had spent thirty years trying not to recognize and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face across a crowded station platform: the downward hook of the *t*, the *d* that never quite closed.

I picked it up and the room did something I have no vocabulary for. It thinned. The coffee smell sharpened until it was almost a sound. And underneath the hum of the equipment I heard — faintly, unmistakably, impossibly — four notes down, a hitch, then climbing, coming not from the tape deck, which was empty, but from everywhere and nowhere, the way music comes in dreams.

"Cyrus," Hollis said, and his voice seemed to arrive from a distance. "You've gone white as flour. Set it down if you need to."

But I didn't set it down. I held on, because the melody was opening like a door, and through it I could already see a kitchen — yellow light, Sunday morning, steam on a window — and someone small sitting on the floor with his back against the cabinets, eyes closed, listening like the song was weather happening to him personally.

"Hollis," I heard myself say, from very far away, "who sent this?"

"Postmark said Marrow Bend," he said, and the kitchen swung closer, and the flour-dust light of 1994 began to pour through me like the room itself had become the song.
