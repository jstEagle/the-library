## 6. Hollis's Ledger

Hollis's station smelled like dust cooked in sunlight, the particular smell of a building that had stopped pretending it needed to impress anyone. He made coffee in a machine older than both of us combined, and when he handed me the mug, I held it out of habit rather than desire. Taste had left me years ago, quietly, the way a tenant leaves without giving notice — one day I reached for flavor and found only texture, temperature, rumor.

"Drink," Hollis said. "It's terrible. I like it that way."

I drank to be polite. It was terrible. It was also, as far as my mouth could tell, warm and brown and nothing else, and I set it down on his desk among the ledgers with the careful disappointment of long practice.

He caught the gesture. Old men who run one-room radio stations catch everything; they have nothing else to watch.

"You didn't come all this way for the coffee," he said.

"I came because you played my mother's song."

"Your mother's." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. Then he nodded, slow, like a man confirming an old suspicion. "Figured somebody'd come eventually. Took longer than I thought."

"How long have you had the tape?"

"Nine years." He pulled open a drawer — not the neat one, the jammed one, the one you have to lift and wiggle — and took out a padded envelope gone soft and yellow at the corners. "Came unsigned. No return address worth anything. Just a postmark."

He slid it across the desk and I read the postmark before I read anything else, because I already knew, some animal part of me already knew, what it would say.

**MARROW BEND.**

The name landed somewhere below thought. A town I'd never been to, never heard of, a smudge of ink on a rubber stamp — and yet my hands went cold around the coffee mug I couldn't taste.

"There was a note," Hollis said. "One line. *Play this sometimes. Not too often.*" He shrugged. "So I did what he asked. Once or twice a year, late slot, when nobody's listening but the ones who need it."

"*He*," I repeated. "You know who sent it."

Hollis looked at me for a long moment. Outside his window, a pickup truck went by hauling something covered in a tarp, and neither of us watched it go.

"I knew him," Hollis said finally. "Not well. He came through here maybe fifteen years back. Drifter type, worked the diner off the highway, then stayed. Kept staying. There's a kind of man who stays places nobody stays." He tapped the envelope. "Ansel. That was his name?"

The floor of the room tilted, gently, the way a boat rocks when someone steps aboard. My brother's name in the mouth of a stranger, spoken casually, kept casually, for nine years while our family performed its grief and I performed my career and Ansel —

Ansel was alive. Had been alive. Mailing tapes from towns with names like Marrow Bend while we said Kaddish over an empty chair.

"He's alive," I said. It came out as accusation.

"Was, last I heard." Hollis's eyes narrowed. "Why? What'd he do?"

"Nothing," I said, and heard how strange that sounded, and didn't correct it. "That's the whole point. He did nothing. We did everything."

Hollis didn't ask what I meant. Maybe he'd spent enough decades behind a microphone to know that people will tell you everything if you just leave the silence open long enough. He let the silence sit there between us, and I fell into it.

"He sent other things," Hollis said at last. "Letters. Never asked me to mail them. Just — included them, every year or two, with a new tape or a note about the song. Said he couldn't finish the addresses." He reached under the desk and came up with a shoebox, actual cardboard, actual shoe-store printing faded on the side. "I'm not a post office. But I'm not a furnace either. Figured someday somebody'd come asking, and here you are, asking."

He put the box in my hands.

It weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it — nine years of letters, and they weighed almost nothing.

* * *

I didn't open them at the station. I drove to the edge of town where the road gave up on pavement and became gravel, and parked under a cottonwood, and sat with the box on my passenger seat like a bomb I'd volunteered to defuse.

The first letter was dated eleven years ago. The handwriting was Ansel's — I'd know it anywhere, that slanted, urgent script he'd had even as a kid, like the words were trying to outrun the pen — but older, weathered, the loops looser.

*Cyrus,* it began.

*I started this letter forty times. I've started it in diners and motels and once in a laundromat in a town whose name I've already forgotten. Here is what I know: if I send it, you'll come, and if you come, you'll ask why, and if you ask why, Dad will find out, and if Dad finds out, the whole careful thing we built comes down. So I write and don't send. It's a hobby. Some men whittle.*

*I heard Mom's song on a station out of — doesn't matter where. Somebody recorded her playing it, must have been years ago, must have been you humming along in the kitchen. I sat in my truck in a parking lot and listened until the signal died. She never finished it. You know that. She always said she was waiting for the middle. I think I might be the middle, Cy. I think that's the joke she'd have appreciated.*

I put the letter down. The cottonwood moved its leaves over me, making that sound like rain that isn't falling, and I picked the letter back up because putting it down wasn't living in any world I recognized.

*Tell Wren I'm not angry. Tell her she was right to stay. Somebody has to be the one who stays; I got elected to be the one who goes, and the vote wasn't fair, but I've stopped appealing it.*

*Don't tell Dad anything. Especially don't tell him I'm fine. It would ruin everything he believes about consequences.*

*Your brother,
A.*

My hands were shaking. I fumbled the next envelope open with fingers that wouldn't cooperate, and inside was a letter dated three years later, shorter, the handwriting tighter:

*Cyrus.*

*The radio man played her song again last night. I called in once, years ago, didn't say anything, just breathed into the phone until he hung up. I think he knows. I think he's known the whole time. There are people like that — they keep things on air so the rest of us can keep them alive.*

*I'm sick. Don't panic when you read this, if you ever read this, which you won't, because I still can't finish the address. It's nothing dramatic. Tired all the time. Doctors in these towns are mostly veterinarians with delusions. Anyway. If I ever do send one of these, it'll be because I ran out of time to be careful, and I want you to know now, in advance: don't be gentle with Dad. Be fair. There's a difference, and I spent seventeen years learning it the hard way.*

*A.*

Seventeen. He'd written it plainly, the way he wrote everything, no ceremony, and it still went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. Seventeen. The age he'd been the night he left. The night of *the trouble*, the family's one word for it, the word that closed doors in our house for thirty years.

I had built my whole adult life on the story that Ansel chose to go. Chose the road over us, chose whatever it was he'd done over his own name. I'd reviewed ten thousand meals on the strength of my detachment, my famous refusal to be moved, and underneath all of it was a seventeen-year-old boy driving away in the dark, and me, twelve years old, watching from the window, deciding right then that caring was a scam.

And now here was his handwriting telling me the vote wasn't fair.

* * *

There were nine letters. I read them all under the cottonwood as the light went from gold to copper to nothing, using the dome light when I had to, and by the sixth one something strange was happening to me.

The seventh letter mentioned coffee.

*I miss good coffee. The diner stuff is water that gave up. Remember Mom's percolator? The way it ticked while it heated, like a clock counting toward something? I'd give a year for one cup of that. Burnt, the way she made it. Deliberately burnt. She said she liked the bitter. I think she just liked that it woke you up twice.*

I read that, and I reached — automatically, stupidly, the way you reach for a light switch in a familiar room — for the cold cup of Hollis's terrible coffee still sitting in my cupholder.

And tasted it.

Bitter. Enormously, catastrophically bitter. Burnt, the way she'd made it, deliberate as a signature. It hit my tongue like a wave hits a swimmer who'd forgotten he was in the ocean — I actually gasped, sat forward, gripped the wheel. Nine years of eating descriptions, of writing the word *notes of* the way other people write *allegedly*, and now: coffee. Real coffee. Bitterness with a body to it, bitterness with history, bitterness that tasted — God help me — like our kitchen in 1994, like the percolator ticking, like Mom's hand on the counter, like Ansel at the door with his backpack already on.

Grief, it turns out, has a flavor. Nobody warns you. It's bitter and it's burnt and it arrives all at once after years of nothing, and it is so much better than nothing that you cry into a paper cup in a parked car under a tree, sobbing like a man rescued, because your tongue works, because your brother is dying in a town called Marrow Bend, because the last letter in the box — the ninth one, the newest, the one I saved for last the way you save the worst — had no date at all, just four lines:

*Cyrus.*

*I'm sending this one. Or I'm not. I'll decide tomorrow.*

*Marrow Bend is real. Look it up. The diner's called the Blue Plate. I close most nights.*

*Come hear the end of the song. I never learned the ending either.*

I sat in the dark with the empty cup in my hands, tasting bitter coffee and salt, and understood that I had already decided, sometime around the second letter, somewhere between the percolator and the veterinarians with delusions, that I was going.

Marrow Bend. The Blue Plate. My brother, closing most nights, keeping a stool warm at the counter of a life he'd never chosen.

I turned the key. The engine caught on the first try, the way engines do when the person holding the wheel has finally run out of reasons to hesitate, and I pointed the car south, toward a town no one in my family had ever thought to look — because looking, I understood now, was the one thing we'd all agreed, without ever saying it aloud, never to do.
