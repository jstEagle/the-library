## 4. Read Aloud, Before the Sale

The estate sale people arrived at seven with a van and a vocabulary. *Vintage*, they said of the armchair with the sprung left side where my mother sat for thirty years because the other chairs were for guests. *Collectible*, they said of the teacups she never used, still in their wedding-present box, tissue gone soft as moth wings. *Charming*, said a woman in good boots, tapping the wallpaper, and I nearly told her that my mother had hung that paper herself in 1987 and hated it from the first strip and never once said so.

But I was learning to say things now, so I said: "She hated it."

The woman blinked at me as though I'd spoken in tongues, which, in this house, I suppose I had.

"Sorry?" she said.

"The wallpaper. She hated it. She just never got round to changing it." I smiled. It felt like wearing someone else's coat. "You'll want to price it accordingly."

I had slept four hours on the sofa bed in the sitting room, my last night as a person who owned a mother's house, and I had spent them listening to the radiators tick like a patient clock counting down something I couldn't name. At six I'd made coffee in the kitchen—the last cup from the last tin in the cupboard—and stood at the window watching the light come up over the garden my mother had weeded on her knees every Sunday until her knees gave out, and even then she'd weed it sitting on an upturned bucket, because God forbid the neighbours think the Dravenmoors had let things go.

The Dravenmoors. As if there had ever been more than two of us, and one of us a rumour.

At half past six, while the sale people were still arranging their tables on the lawn, I went back upstairs and took the shoebox from where I'd hidden it—in my old bedroom, under the eaves, behind a stack of books I'd read at fourteen and never opened again. Forty-one letters. I'd read forty of them over three days, slowly, the way you eat something precious when you know there is no more where it came from. The last one I'd saved. I don't know why. Some animal instinct about saving the best bite, or the worst one. Perhaps both.

It was addressed simply: *To whoever is left.*

No name. No date. Just those four words in my mother's small, upright hand, the handwriting of a woman who had learned early that taking up less space on the page meant taking up less space everywhere, and that this was safer.

I carried it downstairs past the sale people, who were now arguing amiably about whether a walnut bureau was Georgian or merely aspirational, and into the kitchen, and I shut the door, and I stood at the counter where my mother had stood every morning of my childhood, silent as furniture, making breakfast like a woman defusing a bomb.

Then I did the thing I had been practising in the car on the drive up, alone, like a madwoman rehearsing a speech to an empty auditorium.

I read it out loud.

"To whoever is left," I said, and my voice came out strange—rusty, too loud in that quiet room, the voice of a girl who had done all her talking in ledgers. "If you are reading this, then I have managed to die without saying anything important, which will not surprise you."

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in a dead woman's kitchen, and it cracked halfway through and became something else, but I kept going, because Vera had told me—Vera, ninety and sharp as broken glass, who'd gripped my hand across the care-home table and said *she wasn't cold, love, she was careful, there's a difference, and nobody ever taught her the difference*—that the whole point of saying things was that you couldn't take them back.

"If you are Elodie," the letter went on, "and I hope to God it is Elodie, because if it is a stranger then I have failed twice—"

My voice caught there. Failed twice. As if failing me once were a debt she'd been carrying, itemised, interest accruing, for forty years.

"—then I owe you an apology, and since I could never say it to your face—I tried, once, when you were leaving for university; you may remember I gave you a casserole dish instead—here it is on paper, where I have always been brave."

*Here it is on paper.* My mother, confessing to cowardice in the same breath as admitting that paper was her native tongue. That the letters weren't a betrayal of her silence. They *were* her silence, unpacked, folded flat, stored behind a wardrobe for safekeeping.

"I am sorry," I read, and my voice broke properly this time, right on the word *sorry*, the way she must have known it would break in whoever read it, because she wrote it anyway, because some part of her wanted it to. "I am sorry for the quiet. I learned it young, before you were born, and I taught it to you without meaning to, the way you teach a child to flinch. You deserved a mother who spoke. Instead you got one who dusted."

*I am sorry for the quiet.* Five words. I had filled three pages of ledger trying to describe their absence and she'd needed five words to close the account.

"You are not invisible," the letter said. "I want you to know that I saw you. Every day. I saw you and I said nothing because saying things, in my experience, was how houses burned down. But I saw you. If you take one thing from this house, take that."

I put the letter down on the counter. My hands were shaking, and outside I could hear the sale people opening windows, letting strangers' air into rooms that had held only our air for decades, and I found that I didn't mind. Let it breathe. Let it be breathed.

There was one more paragraph. I picked the letter up again.

"Also," I read, and here my mother's voice—her real voice, the one from behind the wardrobe—came through so clearly I looked up, half expecting her at the door with the tea towel over her shoulder, "the wallpaper in the hall is hideous. I have hated it since 1987. Tell no one I said so. Or tell everyone. That's rather the point, isn't it."

*

By nine the cars were lining the street. Strangers moved through the house carrying my childhood out in their arms—a lamp here, a mirror there, the casserole dish (I kept the casserole dish; some inheritances are non-negotiable)—and I walked among them like a ghost at my own exorcism, answering questions, naming prices, saying things. Actual sentences, out loud, to actual people. *She read Dickens every winter. That chair was her favourite; mind the left arm. The garden blooms late but it does bloom.*

A man bought my father's desk, the one from the study, and asked if there was any story to it, and I heard myself say, "Only that she locked everything important inside herself instead of its drawers," and he laughed uncertainly and paid cash, and I decided that counted as honesty.

At noon I carried the ledger downstairs. Three pages of unsaid things, catalogued in my neat hand: *Never apologised for the Christmas of 2009. Never mentioned Aunt Vera. Never said what happened to the photographs. Never said she was proud of me. Never said she loved me.* I set it on the hall table, in the light from the fanlight, where anyone coming through the front door would see it first. Beside it I placed the shoebox, lid off, forty-one letters fanned like a hand of cards.

For a moment I almost took them back. They were hers. They were mine. The instinct to fold them away somewhere dark was so old and so fluent it rose in me like a first language.

Then I thought of whoever came next. A young couple, maybe, or a speculator, or some other daughter clearing out some other silence. They would find the ledger and the letters, and they would understand that this house had been full the whole time—full to bursting—with everything that was never said aloud, and that the walls had simply been waiting for someone to read them out.

"That's rather the point," I said to the empty hall, "isn't it."

And I walked out through the front door, past the sale table, past the woman in the good boots buying the teacups at last, down the path and onto the pavement, and I turned around and looked at the house—really looked at it, the peeling paint and the hideous wallpaper visible through the front window and the garden going quietly wild—and I said, out loud, on a public street, where anyone might hear:

"Goodbye, Mum. I saw you too."

A neighbour watering her roses glanced over. I nodded at her. She nodded back.

Then I got in the car, and I drove away with the windows down, talking the whole way—about nothing, about everything, my voice getting louder as the house got smaller in the mirror—and when my phone buzzed at the junction with an unknown number, I answered it on the second ring.

"Elodie Dravenmoor?" said a voice I didn't know. "This is going to sound strange. I'm calling about your aunt Vera. She says you have questions. And she says—" the voice paused, and I heard paper turning, "—she says there's a letter. One your mother sent after all. In 1994. To a man named Ansel."

The indicator clicked. Somewhere behind me, the house sold its last chair.

"Tell me everything," I said.
