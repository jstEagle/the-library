## 4. What Astrid Kept

The trouble with restoring photographs, Astrid had learned over eleven years at the bench, was that you could never unsee what the damage had been trying to hide. A water-stained wedding portrait didn't just show you a bride; it showed you the flood. A fire-scorched face showed you the fire. She had built a whole quiet career on coaxing people back out of their worst moments, and she had come to believe—gently, professionally—that everyone was a photograph someone had spilled something on.

Coral Vane was the most damaged print she'd ever seen walking upright.

She thought this not unkindly, sitting in her studio above the chandlery on Harbour Lane, with the funeral still on her like salt. Her hands smelled faintly of the fixative she'd been using all morning, and her tea had gone cold beside a photograph of a lighthouse keeper's family from 1961, three of whose faces she was rebuilding from nothing but shadow and guesswork. Guesswork, she was good at. But Coral Vane's face had needed no guesswork at all. It had been perfectly legible: a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate.

He had lied to her at the graveside. That was the thing she kept turning over, the way she turned a cracked negative toward the light. He hadn't lied badly—he'd lied beautifully, smoothly, the way a man lies who has practiced one lie for so long it has worn smooth as a river stone. *I'm afraid you've mistaken me for someone else.* Delivered while looking directly at her, which most liars couldn't manage. And then he'd walked away with his shoulders doing that careful thing, that held-together thing, and his hands—

His hands had shaken. She'd seen them. Restorers notice hands. Hands are where people keep what their faces won't hold.

Astrid set down her brush and looked, for perhaps the hundredth time that week, at the box on the corner of her workbench.

It was a biscuit tin, originally—a McVitie's tin with the picture worn off—and inside it were forty-one letters, tied in bundles with green garden twine, written in an unmistakable leftward-slanting hand that pressed so hard into the paper it embossed the pages beneath. Wren Vane-Hollis had written letters the way some people sang in the shower: constantly, joyfully, with total disregard for whether anyone wanted to hear it. She'd written them to Astrid during the two years they'd been inseparable, and then—after the fire, after everything—she had written them from wherever she'd gone, until the letters stopped.

Astrid had kept every one. Even the last one. *Especially* the last one.

She got up, made herself fresh tea out of the need to do something with her hands, and came back and untied the final bundle, because she had promised herself she wouldn't open the tin again until she understood why she'd gone to that funeral, and she was tired of promising herself things.

*Dear Astrid,* the last letter began. *Don't look for me. I know you will anyway, because you're you, so let me save us both the time.*

The handwriting had changed by then. The letters before the fire ran downhill, exuberant, full of crossed-out words and drawings of orchids in the margins. The letters after were smaller. Controlled. Written, Astrid had always suspected, by someone who had learned that being seen cost something.

*I'm fine. That's the first true sentence in this letter so enjoy it. The doctors say the scarring will fade, which is a lie doctors tell, and Mother says nothing at all, which is worse. Coral looks at me like I'm a window he broke. I can't live in a house where I'm the thing that happened.*

Astrid read it through twice, though she knew it by heart, the way you know a song you danced to at your own funeral-in-advance. Then she read the line beneath it, the one she'd underlined years ago in pencil so lightly you could erase the evidence:

*Coral thinks none of us saw what we saw. Tell him, if you ever find him, that Wren sees everything. She always did.*

That was the sentence that had put her on the train to the funeral. Not grief—though there was grief, seven years old now and settled into her like damp into a wall. It was that line. *Tell him.* Wren had issued instructions from her exile the way other people left recipes, and Astrid had carried this one around for seven years like a coin in her pocket, waiting for a vending machine.

And then Coral Vane had stood at his mother's grave in a coat too good for him, lying with his whole handsome ruined face, and Astrid had thought: *there's my machine.*

* * *

Her studio was her best argument against despair, and she looked around it now with the particular satisfaction of a woman surveying evidence. Photographs everywhere, pinned and sleeved and soaking in baths. A bride emerging from water damage. Two brothers from a farm portrait, one of whom had been cut out with scissors sometime in 1974 and whom Astrid was putting back in, because that was the job—you put people back in. On the wall above the bench hung her only framed piece: a photograph of two girls on a shingle beach, squinting into a sun that had bleached half the sky. Astrid at fifteen, all elbows and opinions. Wren at sixteen, mid-laugh, holding up a starfish like a trophy, one arm slung around Astrid's neck with the casual violence of total friendship.

People assumed Astrid kept it framed because it was beautiful. It was beautiful. But she kept it framed because it was the last photograph of Wren Vane-Hollis that existed anywhere, as far as Astrid knew—the fire had eaten the family albums, and after Wren vanished, no one took pictures anymore. You could restore almost anything, Astrid had learned. Except a person nobody photographed.

She had tried, over the years, to imagine Wren's face as it must be now. This was restorer's habit and also restorer's discipline: work from what remains, not what you remember. What remained was a scar described in a single unguarded letter—*it goes from my ear to my collarbone, like a lightning strike that decided to stay*—and a voice Astrid heard sometimes in dreams, saying *you're you, so let me save us both the time.*

The bell over the chandlery door downstairs clanged. Footsteps on the stairs, uneven, apologetic. Astrid knew those footsteps too, though she'd only heard them once—at a graveside, retreating.

She didn't turn around. "We're closed."

"I saw the light." Coral Vane stood in the doorway with his hands in his pockets, which she noted he did when he wanted them to stop shaking. Up close, out of the funeral's black, he looked less like an obituary and more like a man who had been ironed wrong. "You're the woman from the church."

"The woman from the church," Astrid agreed. "Also the woman you told you'd never heard the name Vane-Hollis in your life."

"And you're the woman who followed me here."

"Followed is strong. I live here. I have lived here for six years, which you'd know if you'd ever once opened the town newsletter, which features my studio every spring under 'Local Colour.'" She turned then, and let him see her looking at him properly, the way she looked at a damaged print: without flinching, without pity, cataloguing. "You came back to sell the house."

Something moved behind his eyes—surprise, quickly filed away. "How did you—"

"Small town. The house has been empty seven years and suddenly there's a skip outside it and a man measuring the windows. Also, you're Coral Vane. You were always going to come back for the sale. People always come back for the sale." She picked up her cold tea, considered it, and drank it anyway. "Sit down or don't. But since you're standing in my studio pretending we haven't met, I'll tell you how this works. I restore photographs. Do you know what that means, practically?"

"No," he said, honestly enough.

"It means I spend my days taking images that have been burned, soaked, torn, or abandoned, and finding out what's still true underneath. And the first rule of the trade—" she held up one finger, "—is that you cannot restore anything by agreeing with the damage. You want to know what's true? You start by refusing the version of events the fire left you."

The silence that followed had texture. Outside, a gull said something rude about all of them.

"You knew my sister," Coral said at last. Not a question. His voice had changed—lowered, the way voices do near deep water.

"I knew Wren. We were—" Astrid paused, choosing, for once in her life, precision over drama. "I was her best friend. From the year we were fourteen until the night of the fire. And afterward, for a while, by post." She nodded at the biscuit tin on the bench. "Forty-one letters. I kept them all. I kept them because she asked me to, and because I am, apparently, the kind of person who keeps things."

Coral's gaze went to the tin and stuck there. She watched him want it and refuse to want it, watched him perform indifference so poorly it was nearly tender. His hands had come out of his pockets. They were not quite steady.

"She wrote to you," he said. "After."

"After," Astrid confirmed. "For about eighteen months. Then the letters stopped coming, and no return address ever worked, and I did what she told me not to do, which was look. I looked for two years. I found nothing, which I now understand was her being extremely good at it." She stood, crossed to the tin, and rested her hand flat on its lid—not opening it, just establishing ownership, the way you'd put a palm on a horse's neck. "Here's what I'll say, and then you can go back to your hotel and rehearse more sentences. Those letters contradict the story you told at that funeral. The story your mother told the whole town, the story on the insurance forms—I've read the newspaper accounts, Coral, I'm thorough, it's my entire personality. And somewhere in the middle of all those versions is a girl who wrote to me that she *saw everything*, and asked me to tell you so."

"Tell me what?" His voice cracked on the second word, very slightly, a hairline fracture across an otherwise perfect surface. A restorer would catch it. Most people wouldn't.

"That's the interesting question," Astrid said gently. "Because I don't think you need telling. I think you already know exactly what she meant, and you've spent seven years standing very still so it wouldn't fall on you."

He laughed—one short, appalled breath of a laugh. "You don't know anything about our family."

"No," she agreed. "But I know about fires. My father was a volunteer firefighter for thirty years, and he taught me that every blaze has an origin point, and every origin point has a person standing near it, and the person is almost never a stranger." She let that sit between them, glowing quietly. "Come back tomorrow. Or don't. But if you don't, I'll bring the tin to the estate agent's office on sale day and read the letters aloud to whoever's buying the land. I'm told developers hate complications."

For the first time, Coral Vane looked at her without the performance—just a raw, exhausted flicker of something that might have been fear, or relief, or the specific terror of a man who has been carrying a box of his own for seven years and has just realized someone else can see its shape through the wrapping.

"Astrid," he said, testing the name. "That's not a local name."

"No. My mother was Norwegian and fanciful. It means *divinely beautiful*, which she'd have found funnier if she'd met me at fourteen." She picked up her brush again, dismissal and invitation in the same gesture. "Tomorrow. Bring your hands. They want to talk more than you do."

At the door he stopped, half-turned, and for a moment she thought he would say it—whatever *it* was, the thing pressing against the backs of his teeth. Instead he said, "Wren hated the sea. Did she ever tell you that? She only went to the beach for you."

And he was gone down the stairs, uneven footsteps fading into the gull-noise and the harbour sounds, leaving Astrid alone with her cold tea and her tin and the sudden, ringing certainty that she had just been given something. Not a confession. A coordinate.

She untied the green twine with steady fingers and pulled out the third letter from the top—the one dated nine days after the fire, the one she'd read a hundred times and never understood until this exact minute.

*I keep thinking about the glasshouse,* Wren had written, in that small controlled hand. *Everyone asks me what burned. Nobody asks me what I saw. Isn't that funny. Isn't that the funniest thing in the world.*

Astrid set the letter down beside the framed photograph of two girls on a beach, and began, carefully, to plan tomorrow.
