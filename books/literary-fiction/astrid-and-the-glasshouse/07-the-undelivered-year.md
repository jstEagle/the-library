## 7. The Undelivered Year

The postcard had a forwarding address on it. That was the thing I couldn't get past, standing in my mother's kitchen with the kettle ticking itself toward boiling and the card lying face-up on the counter like something that had crawled out of the sea.

*Addressee unknown — return to sender.* And beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand: *Forwarding address held: 14 Kestrel Row, Ashmouth.*

I knew 14 Kestrel Row. Everyone in Saltmere knew Kestrel Row; it was where the nursery workers lived, the ones who came in on the coast road with soil under their nails and salt in their hair. It was four miles from the glasshouse ruins. Four miles from the house I'd spent seven years pretending not to remember.

Which meant that for some portion of those seven years, someone at that address had been close enough to walk to our gate.

The kettle screamed. I didn't move to it. I stood there holding a piece of cardstock the weight of a moth and understood, with the slow terrible clarity of a man stepping off a ledge he's been standing on for years without noticing, that I was going to have to remember everything now. Not the curated version. Not the version I told strangers at funerals. The whole of it, undelivered, returned to sender, marked in red ink.

It started, as most catastrophes do, with arithmetic.

* * *

My father kept his debts the way other men keep stamp collections: lovingly, secretly, in albums no one was meant to see. I found them the winter I turned seventeen, in the drawer of his potting bench, beneath a false bottom he thought was clever. Pages of figures in his small, precise hand. The glasshouse—the pride of the Vane-Hollis name, the reason people drove out from the city to see our orchids breathing their steam into the cold—was mortgaged twice over. There were letters from men whose names sounded like furniture. There was a final notice dated eleven days before Christmas.

I did what any seventeen-year-old does with an unbearable truth. I sat with it for three weeks, telling no one, letting it grow teeth. Then I did the second thing seventeen-year-olds do: I solved it.

The solution arrived fully formed, which should have frightened me. Insurance. Fire. Accidental, electrical, tragic—a fault in the old wiring, everyone would say, the wiring was always suspect in that place, Dad had been meaning to rewire for years. Nobody would be hurt because nobody would be there. Wren would be away—she was always away in January, gone to stay with Aunt Ivy two counties over, gone with her sketchbooks and her grudge about the train timetable. My mother would take the payout, clear the debts, keep the house. My father would lose the glasshouse but keep his hands, and hands, I reasoned, could build another glasshouse. I was very good at reasoning. I had done it all myself, alone, in the dark, the way you do when you're seventeen and certain that adults are just children who've learned to lie more slowly.

I never asked my father if he'd considered it himself. I have wondered since whether the idea was already sitting in that drawer beside the debts, waiting for one of us to pick it up. He never said. He died before I could ask, and I have hated him a little for that, in the way you hate someone for leaving a room mid-sentence.

The night itself I remember in pieces, the way you remember anything you've decided never to look at directly: glimpses through fingers.

The smell of paraffin, which I'd carried from the shed in a can I'd used a hundred times legitimately. The particular dark of January on the coast, the kind of dark that has texture. The sound of the first pane going—not shattering, exactly, but sighing, a long hot crack running the length of the roof like the building clearing its throat. I stood at the edge of the property with the fire climbing the far wall and felt, for approximately ninety seconds, like a man who had saved his family.

Then I heard the scream.

You will want me to describe it. I can't, properly. What I can tell you is that a scream has a shape, and this one had my sister's shape, and it came from inside the glasshouse, from the propagation room where she sometimes slept in summer when the nights were warm and she wanted to be near the orchids she swore talked to her. She wasn't supposed to be there. Aunt Ivy's train had been cancelled—snow on the line, they said afterward—and Wren, furious at the waste of a free evening, had come home instead. She'd been home for hours. She'd been home while I stood in the shed reading debt notices by torchlight. She'd been home while I checked the insurance papers, while I planned, while I poured.

She got out. That is the sentence I built my whole life on: *she got out.* Through the side door, the one I'd left unbarred without knowing why—some animal instinct in me, apparently, that was smarter than my arithmetic. She got out with the burning roof coming down behind her and a falling strut catching her across the left side of her face and shoulder, and she got out, and she lived, and I have spent seven years being grateful in the ugliest possible way, the way where you're mostly relieved it wasn't worse.

She was fifteen. The burns took the left side of her face and much of her left arm, and took, along with them, the girl who drew orchids that seemed to breathe on the page. In the hospital she wouldn't look at mirrors. In the months after, she wouldn't look at windows. And then one day in April she looked at me—looked at me across the breakfast table with half her face and all of her eyes—and said, very quietly, "Don't."

One word. *Don't.* Don't apologize, don't explain, don't look at me like that, don't tell me it's fine, don't. I have turned that word over so many times it's worn smooth. I still don't know which don't she meant. Possibly all of them.

* * *

Here is what I understood only later, standing in my mother's kitchen with a returned postcard: my mother chose denial the way other people choose religion—completely, and with great comfort drawn from it. After the fire she never once said the words *insurance money*. She said "the tragedy" and "the loss" and "what the fire took," as though the fire had been weather, an act of God, a visiting angel with a flaming sword. When the payout cleared the debts she wept with relief and called it providence, and I sat across from her at dinner every night for a year knowing I was the providence, and saying nothing, because what was the alternative? To hand her the truth and watch her choose between her son and her sanity?

Wren made the choice easier, or harder, depending on how you hold it. Six months after the fire she was gone. No note, or none I ever saw. A bag missing, her sketchbooks gone, the small tin of pencils she'd kept since she was nine. Our mother told the town she'd gone to live with relatives abroad. Our mother may even have believed it. I think—I have always thought—that Wren left because staying meant being seen, and being seen meant being pitied, and being pitied meant being asked gentle questions by gentle people, and somewhere in those gentle questions the truth would surface like a drowned thing, bloated and undeniable. So she went. And I stayed, and inherited her absence along with my guilt, two heavy things carried in the same arms.

For years I waited to be caught. That's the part nobody tells you about guilt: it makes you patient. You become a man standing forever in a queue for a punishment that keeps being postponed. Every knock was the police. Every letter was a summons. When nothing came—when the fire was ruled accidental, when the inspector's report blamed the wiring I'd been counting on, when life simply went on with its appalling indifference—I discovered something worse than punishment: impunity. I had gotten away with it, and getting away with it was its own cell.

So I built the ritual. First the silence about my own name—I dropped the Hollis, let Vane go quiet, became just Coral, a man who stood in for someone at a job that didn't need him, in a flat that didn't fit him, living a life sized for a person slightly smaller than myself. And then, because silence curdles, the postcards. Weekly. To the burned address. Messages with no content, really—"The weather here is mild. Hope you're well."—mailed to a ruin, delivered to no one, an act of penance shaped like correspondence. If you mail enough letters to a ghost, you become a kind of ghost yourself. That was the arrangement. That was the bargain I'd struck: I would erase myself in exchange for my sister's erasure costing her less.

And all the while—this is the part that broke me open in my mother's kitchen—all the while there was a forwarding address. Four miles away. A real door, a real letterbox, a person receiving mail at 14 Kestrel Row while I posted my weekly penance into the smoke of a building that hadn't existed for seven years.

Had she known? Had the postal clerk's red ink been sitting in some dead-letter office all this time, patient as a debt? Or had someone—my mother, who must have requested the forward, who must have known where her daughter went even as she told the town otherwise—kept it deliberately, a door left unlocked in case either of us ever grew brave enough to knock?

The kettle had long gone quiet. Outside, the gulls were doing their evening argument over the bins. I picked up the postcard—my own handwriting, younger somehow, from the first year, before the messages went hollow—and read what I'd written to no one:

*I'm sorry. I'll find a way to say it properly someday.*

Seven years of Sundays, and I had never once managed to say it properly. I had mailed apology-shaped objects into ash and called it courage.

I set the card down next to Astrid's number, which I'd copied onto the back of an envelope and then ignored for three days. Two addresses now. One belonging to a woman who kept my sister's letters like relics, and one belonging, possibly, impossibly, to my sister herself.

Four miles. I could walk it before dark.

I put on my coat, and my hands were shaking, and I went anyway.
