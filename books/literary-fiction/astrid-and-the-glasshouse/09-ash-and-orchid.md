## 9. Ash and Orchid

The morning of the sale arrived the way weather does in that town: without apology. Rain had come in off the sea overnight and hung around like a relative who'd missed the funeral and wanted to make up for it. I stood at the kitchen window of the house on Marsh Lane with a mug of tea I had no intention of drinking, watching water bead and run down the glass, and thought: *this is the last time you will stand here.*

I'd expected to feel relief. That was the plan, anyway — sign nothing, say nothing, let the developer's money sweep the whole mess out to sea. Seven years of postcards to a burned address, and now the address itself would be someone else's problem. A man could rest after that.

Instead I felt like a coat left on a hook.

The house was empty around me. The estate agent's people had come through two days earlier with clipboards and a polite vocabulary — "characterful," they said, meaning sagging; "period features," meaning rot — and their voices had taken something out of the rooms. My mother's unopened mail was gone into a box marked MISC. Wren's drawings had been peeled off her bedroom wall by a woman named Sandra who said she was very sorry, love, but buyers liked a blank canvas. I'd kept three of them, rolled up under my arm like blueprints for a building that no longer existed.

I put the kettle on out of habit. The kettle was the only thing left on the counter, and it looked pleased about its promotion.

That was when the door went.

Not the bell — the door itself, a flat knock with the heel of a hand, the knock of someone who grew up in this house and never learned the bell because the bell had been broken since 2009. My hands went cold around the mug.

I opened the door and my sister stood on the step in the rain, hood up, hands in the pockets of a green waxed jacket, looking at me the way you look at a word you've been trying to remember all day.

"Your guttering's shot," she said.

"Wren."

"You look terrible, Coral." She pushed past me into the hall, dripping, and stopped in the middle of it, turning slowly, taking inventory the way I had. "Smaller than I remembered."

"It's the same size."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

She walked the house like an inspector from some bureau of lost time. I followed at a distance, the way you follow a dog that might bolt. In the sitting room she pressed her palm flat against the wall where the fireplace had been bricked over, as if checking for a pulse. In the kitchen she opened cupboards that contained nothing and closed them again gently, one by one, like she was putting children to bed.

"They told me Thursday," she said finally. "The sale. I read it in the planning notices. You can find out anything from planning notices if you're bored enough."

"Astrid," I said.

"Astrid." She said the name carefully, tasting it. "She came to the nursery on Tuesday. Stood there among the ferns for twenty minutes pretending to care about ferns, and then she told me you never stopped writing. Postcards. Every week. To a pile of ash." She turned and looked at me, and up close I could see what the fire had left on her — the shine of healed skin along her jaw and down her neck, catching the grey light, a map of the night drawn on her body. I had spent seven years not knowing whether she was alive. She had spent seven years being alive somewhere I couldn't reach, and neither of us had won anything from the arrangement.

"I didn't know where you were," I said. It came out smaller than I meant it to.

"You didn't *look*."

"Mum said—"

"Mum said leave it. Mum said she's happier. Mum said we don't talk about it." Wren pulled off her hood and shook the rain out of her hair, and for a second she was fifteen again, doing exactly this in exactly this kitchen, and my chest hurt. "And you believed her, because believing her meant you never had to say it out loud. Did you ever say it out loud? Once? In seven years?"

"To whom?" I asked. "To the post office?"

"Don't be clever. You were always clever instead of honest. It's your worst feature and you've polished it."

I set the mug down. My hands weren't shaking; that surprised me. They were very still, the way they'd been still the night I struck the match, and I understood suddenly that the stillness had never left, that I had carried it through every flat and every job and every postcard like a stone sewn into my lining.

"The fire was me," I said.

Wren didn't move.

"Dad's debts. The insurance. I found the policy in his desk and I did the arithmetic and I was seventeen and stupid, and I thought you were at Aunt Ivy's. I checked the bus timetable. I *checked*, Wren, I want you to know that, I wasn't careless, I was careful, and I was still wrong." The words came out of me the way water comes through a cracked dam — not fast, but unstoppable, and everything downstream ruined. "You were supposed to be at Aunt Ivy's."

"I came back early," she said quietly. "Because I missed home. Because I was fifteen and I missed my stupid brother and my stupid orchids."

"I know."

"Do you? Do you know what it was like, waking up in that hospital, and Mum telling everyone — telling *me* — that it was wiring, old wiring, a tragedy, an act of God? And you standing there at the foot of my bed with your face like a locked door, saying nothing, agreeing with her, letting God take the blame for you?" Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it stayed level, conversational, while her eyes did all the burning. "I lay there with half my face bandaged and I thought: he knows. He knows and he's going to let me carry it too."

"I thought I was protecting you."

"You thought you were protecting *yourself*. There's a difference and it took me seven years and a lot of very patient nurses to learn it, so I'll give it to you free." She sat down at the empty kitchen table, on a chair that would belong to strangers by nightfall, and folded her hands. "I never blamed you for the fire, Coral. I was angry about the fire for maybe a year. What I couldn't forgive was the silence. Every week. Every week for seven years, while you wrote to a dead address, you had a living sister and you chose the ash."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere down the lane a car door slammed — the agent's people, early, eager.

"I wrote them because I couldn't write to you," I said. "Every card was to you. I just couldn't put your name on it, because then you'd have to read it, and I hadn't earned—"

"You're right," she said. "You hadn't. But here's the thing nobody tells you about earning forgiveness: you don't earn it first and receive it after. It goes the other way round. Somebody has to hand it over before you've finished paying, or nobody ever finishes." She looked at her own hands, at the fine shine of skin across the knuckles. "Astrid handed it over on Tuesday. Told me you'd stood in a graveyard and lied to her face rather than give up our name. Told me about the postcards. I drove all night to be furious at you, and now I'm here and I'm mostly just—" she gestured at the bare kitchen, the bricked hearth, the kettle on its lonely counter, "—mostly just sad that we did this to each other in an empty house."

"I'm sorry," I said. Three words, seven years late, worth exactly what they cost.

"I know," said Wren. "You always were sorry. You were just never *sorry aloud*."

* * *

They came at eleven. The agent — a brisk, kind-eyed woman called Devi who had handled the sale with more gentleness than the process deserved — arrived with the buyer's representative and a folder of papers, and found us both sitting in the front room among the dust ghosts, side by side on the floor with our backs against the wall, like children waiting to be told off.

Devi looked at Wren, then at me, then at the folder.

"Mr. Vane-Hollis," she said carefully. "We don't have to do this today. There are provisions. If you want more time—"

I looked at Wren. Wren looked at the window, where the rain had thinned to a bright drizzle, where beyond the garden wall the black ribs of the glasshouse showed above the weeds like the skeleton of a whale that had swum inland and died.

"Sign it," she said.

"You're sure?"

"Coral." She nudged my knee with hers, the way she used to when we shared a pew and the sermon ran long. "It's a house. We thought it was the whole world, and it turns out it's a house. Let it go be somebody's project. Let them put in a stupid kitchen island." A ghost of a smile crossed the unburned side of her face. "But make them keep the glasshouse footprint. The land. Make it a condition — gardens, or nothing. Astrid mentioned the developer was flexible. Developers are flexible when you frighten them slightly."

So I signed. My name — the full name, Coral Vane-Hollis, the name I had buried and dug up twice in one season — went onto the line, and the pen scratched, and that was all it was. Paper. The house had already stopped being ours somewhere back in the empty cupboards; the signature was just the receipt.

Afterward Devi shook both our hands and said something kind about new beginnings, and the buyer's man measured the doorframe for reasons known only to doorframe measurers, and then it was quiet again, and we were alone in a house that belonged to no one for one last hour.

Wren stood in the garden in the clearing light, looking at the glasshouse ruins.

"You could grow things there again," I said. "If they keep it. Not orchids. Tomatoes. Something forgiving."

"Tomatoes," she repeated, with the scorn of a professional. "In *this* soil."

"Something forgiving," I said again. "I'm told it exists."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter — and then she cried a little, briefly and furiously, wiping her face with her sleeve as though the tears were someone else's and she was evicting them. I put my arm around my sister's shoulders for the first time in seven years, and she let me, and the rain held off, and the sea made its long indifferent noise below the town.

"There's a letter," I said. "On your kitchen table, when you get home. I wrote it last night. My real name on it this time."

"I know," said Wren. "Astrid told me to expect it. She also told me you're hopeless on the phone, so don't call. Write. You're better on paper."

Down the lane, walking up toward the house with a canvas bag slung over her shoulder and the uncertain stride of someone arriving at a party she'd organized herself, came Astrid.

Wren watched her approach, then glanced sideways at me, and the smile that spread across her face was pure fifteen-year-old mischief, undamaged by fire or time.

"Oh," she said. "*Oh.* This should be interesting."
