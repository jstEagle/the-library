## 2. The Notice

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Isandro would later consider unfair, because Tuesdays were already the day she trusted least. Bridges failed on Tuesdays. So did marriages, though usually more slowly.

It was certified, which meant someone had to sign for it, which meant the postal carrier stood on the porch at 4:17 in the afternoon holding out a clipboard while Isandro dried her hands on her jeans and wondered who on earth would send her something requiring proof of receipt. She signed *Isandro Zhou* in her careful inspector's hand — legible even under duress, a professional habit — and carried the envelope inside like it might detonate, or worse, like it might be boring.

The return address was the Office of State Asset Recovery. The paper was the color of old teeth.

She sat down at the kitchen table before opening it. This was instinct. Certain documents required sitting. She had learned this at twenty-three, when a structural assessment had come back on her first bridge assignment and she'd read the words *load rating: reduced* standing up and then had to sit down anyway, so now she simply started seated.

*Notice of Public Auction.* Her eyes went to the address line first, because that was how she read everything — location before content, structure before story.

**9 Alder Row.**

The kitchen did not change. The refrigerator hummed its one note. Outside, a neighbor's dog announced a squirrel with the full gravity of a national emergency. And Isandro sat very still, holding a piece of state paperwork, feeling the floor of her ordinary life tilt by perhaps two degrees — an amount no instrument would flag, but an amount she felt in her teeth.

Nine Alder Row. The house where she had lived for exactly one year when she was seven, under a name that wasn't hers, learning rules that weren't in any schoolbook.

* * *

Tomas came home at six-forty, as he came home every day, carrying his bag and the day's weather on his shoulders. He kissed the top of her head — she was still at the table, which should have been clue number one — and opened the fridge and said, "Is there any of that soup left?"

"There's soup."

He poured it into a bowl, microwaved it with the particular optimism of a man who believes leftovers improve with faith, and sat across from her. Only then did he register the envelope, the state seal, the fact that his wife had the expression of someone doing long division behind her eyes.

"What's that?"

"A notice." She slid it across the table. "The house on Alder Row. My mother's house. They're auctioning it."

Tomas read the way he did most things — efficiently, with his eyebrows doing the emotional labor. "Your mother's house? I thought your mother didn't *have* a house. You told me she moved around."

"She did. That's the point of the house."

"That doesn't—" He set the letter down. "Wait, this is the year you don't talk about? The hiding year?"

"The hiding year, yes."

"And the state owns it?"

"The state is selling it. There's a difference. The state owns things; selling them is how it stops having to think about them." She heard herself say it and recognized the sentence as one she'd use about a bridge slated for demolition. Some vocabulary follows you everywhere.

Tomas stirred his soup. "Huh." A pause. Then, with the gentle finality of a man closing a browser tab: "Well. It's not like you can do anything about it."

And there it was. Not cruelty — Tomas was not cruel, he was economical, which from certain angles looks identical — but the small flat sound of a door being considered shut. *Clutter*, his tone said. *Old paperwork. A dead woman's loose end.*

"Actually," Isandro said, "I could bid on it."

He looked up. Soup spoon suspended. In eleven years of marriage she had made perhaps four statements that caused his spoon to stop mid-air, and this was now the fifth.

"Bid on it. With what?"

"Money."

"I know how bidding works, Isandro. I mean — why? What would you even *do* with it?"

What would she do with it. She turned the question over the way she'd turn over a core sample, looking for the fracture line. What did one do with a house that had held one year of a hidden childhood? One did not live in it. One did not renovate it into a charming guest cottage with exposed brick and a story to tell dinner guests. One did not, presumably, burn it down, although she noted the fantasy arrived with suspicious speed.

"I don't know," she admitted. "But it shouldn't belong to strangers."

"It belongs to strangers *now*. It's belonged to strangers for twenty years. That's what the state means."

"That's what the state means," she agreed, "and the state has never once been right about anything I've inspected."

Tomas laughed — a real laugh, brief, surprised out of him — because she was rarely funny on purpose and it always landed harder for the rarity. He reached over and squeezed her wrist, once, the way you'd test a railing. "You're not serious."

"I'm always serious. That's my whole personality. Ask anyone."

"You're serious about bridges."

"Bridges are just houses that move," she said, "when they're failing."

He didn't have an answer for that, so he finished his soup, and the conversation ended the way their conversations about her mother had always ended: not resolved but *tolerated*, loaded within limits, deflection rated for residential traffic only.

* * *

That night she did not sleep so much as lie beside her sleeping husband conducting a structural review of her own chest cavity, and around one in the morning she gave up, dressed in the dark with the skill of a woman who has dressed in the dark many times, and took the car.

Alder Row was forty minutes away if you took the ring road, fifty-five if you took the honest route through town, and she took the honest route because at one in the morning honesty costs nothing.

The street had not improved. It had, if anything, committed further to its decline with the quiet diligence of a street that knows nobody important is watching. Alder Row ran two blocks between a shuttered laundromat and a church whose congregation had dwindled to a size that fit comfortably in one pew. The houses were narrow, wooden, built in the era when builders still believed small families deserved small dignity. Number 9 sat in the middle of the block with a plywood sheet over one ground-floor window and a municipal notice taped to the door, and it looked — she was surprised by this — *smaller* than memory insisted. Houses do that. They shrink in your absence the way clothes shrink in the wash, and you stand there at thirty-eight wondering how you ever fit inside a life that size.

She drove past it once at maybe twelve miles an hour, which on a residential street at 1 a.m. is the speed of either a lost person or a burglar, and she was aware she resembled both.

At the far corner she stopped, sat with the engine idling, and turned around.

Second pass. Slower. Ten miles an hour. The porch light was dead — had the porch light worked that year? She couldn't remember light in that house at all, only the quality of shadows, which is its own kind of lighting design. The attic window, small and square under the roofline, caught the streetlight and threw it back dull as a coin at the bottom of a fountain.

There. That window. Behind that window, for one year, there had been a child named something else — a name her mother had issued like a temporary work permit — lying on a mattress between boxes, memorizing the sound of the house settling so she'd know which creaks were the house and which were footsteps.

Isandro pulled over two doors down and turned off the engine and sat in the dark looking at the place where she used to be kept.

Here is what she remembered, in no order and against her will:

Her mother's voice through the floorboards, low and pleasant, saying *no thank you, we're fine* to someone at the door, and the specific temperature of relief afterward. The rule, delivered once, never repeated because it never needed repeating: *We do not answer the door. Ever. If I am not home, you are not home.* The smell of the hallway — dust and lemon polish, her mother polishing wood that was already clean, polishing as prayer. The game they played called Quiet, which was not a game, which was training wearing a party hat.

And underneath all of it, the thing she had never once said aloud in thirty years: that she had been *happy* that year. Not safe — happy. Because her mother, in that house, had been entirely present, entirely hers, a woman with nowhere else to be and therefore everywhere her daughter was. Love at maximum concentration. It had taken Isandro decades to understand that the concentration was the danger sign, the way sweetness in water can mean something upstream you don't want to think about.

A cat crossed the road in front of the car with the unhurried entitlement of a creature that owns everything. Isandro watched it disappear under the porch of number 11 and thought: *someone lives here. Something always lives here.*

She restarted the engine and drove home and lay back down next to Tomas, who murmured something in his sleep that might have been her name or might have been "quarterly," and stared at the ceiling until the ceiling became the underside of an attic roof, and slept at last the way inspectors sleep: lightly, on duty.

* * *

In the morning she did the responsible thing, which for Isandro meant research conducted with the intensity other people reserve for grief or romance.

The auction file was public. She read it twice at her kitchen table with coffee going cold beside her, and the bureaucratic language did its usual magic trick of making devastation sound like accounting. *Property acquired following lapse of tax obligations and failure of estate claimants to respond within statutory period.* Estate claimants. That was her. That was her mother's sister in another country who sent a card every few years containing nothing but weather. The state had knocked, politely, repeatedly, on the doors of everyone who might have cared, and found no one home — which was, Isandro thought, darkly, fitting. Nobody answered doors at Alder Row. It was practically the house's founding principle.

The reserve price was insultingly low. The inspection date was printed in bold: open viewing, ten days from now, one afternoon only.

Ten days.

She looked up from the papers and found Tomas in the doorway, dressed for work, watching her with the expression of a man who has come downstairs to find his wife having a relationship with a manila folder.

"You're still on this."

"I'm reading about it. Reading is free."

"Nothing is free. That's the first thing they teach you." He came over, kissed her hair, picked up his keys. "It's a house, Isandro. It's wood and bad wiring. Whatever happened there happened to a kid who grew up and married me and lives here now."

He said it kindly. That was the terrible part. He said it the way you'd reassure someone afraid of flying — *the statistics are on your side* — without understanding that she wasn't afraid of the plane. She was trying to remember why she'd boarded.

After the door closed behind him she sat for a while in the particular silence of a house that belongs fully to no one, and then she got out a pen and wrote on the back of the auction notice, in her inspector's hand, the only sentence that seemed true:

*Deficiency observed. Recommend immediate evaluation.*

Underneath, after a moment, she added the date. Because everything she touched got a date. Because a finding without a date is just a feeling, and feelings, unlike cracks, cannot be measured.

Then she folded the notice into her bag, where it rode with her all day like a passenger, and that night — she would do this again tomorrow, and the night after, a woman driving a slow loop past a shrinking house — she began, quietly and without announcing it to anyone including herself, to figure out what she had in savings.

Outside, the city hummed. Somewhere across it, nine Alder Row stood empty with its dead porch light and its sealed-up year, waiting with the patience of structures for someone to come take its measurements.

On the tenth day, there would be a viewing. On the eleventh, an auction. And Isandro Zhou, fluent in tolerances, fluent in load ratings, fluent in the exact width of a crack before it becomes a collapse, was about to discover that some numbers she had never learned to read were written on the inside of that house — and that other people wanted them too.
