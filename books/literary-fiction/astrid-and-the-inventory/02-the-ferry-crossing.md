## 2. The Ferry Crossing

The assignment came through on a Tuesday, in the flat administrative voice of all his assignments: *Structural assessment, former ferry terminal, Vinter Point. Condemned structure. Preliminary report required within thirty days.*

Jonah read it twice at the kitchen table while Marta's coffee maker gurgled behind him like something with a chest complaint. Thirty days. He'd had longer assignments and shorter ones; this one sat in the middle range, unremarkable, except that Vinter Point was ninety minutes north along a coast road that the state had stopped maintaining sometime around the invention of the interstate. He would drive up Monday, stay at whatever motel the town still supported, come home weekends. Two weeks, maybe three. A condemned ferry terminal was not complicated work. You measured what remained of it and wrote down how long it had left.

"You'll be gone how long?" Marta asked, not looking up from her tablet. She was doing the household accounts, or appearing to. Her thumb moved across the screen with the regularity of a metronome.

"Two weeks. Maybe three."

She nodded. The nod said: *noted, filed, no structural concern.* Somewhere in the last decade they had developed this shorthand of small permissions — he went, she stayed, both of them signed off on the other's absence without ever once discussing the terms. It occurred to him, watching her thumb move, that he could not remember the last time he'd asked her to come along on anything.

"I'll call," he said.

"Okay." And then, because she was fair-minded even in their erosion: "Eat actual meals. Not gas station ones."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was a version of them, he knew, for whom this exchange would have been the beginning of a conversation rather than its conclusion. He packed Sunday night with the efficiency of a man who had done it many times, and if his duffel felt lighter than it used to — fewer books, fewer things worth bringing anywhere — he didn't examine why.

* * *

The coast road north was beautiful in the way that abandoned things are beautiful, which is to say honestly. The highway gave way to a two-lane, the two-lane to a road with gravel shoulders and guardrails freckled orange with rust, and the whole time the sea kept pace on his left, gray-green and patient, throwing itself against basalt in long exhausted gestures. Jonah drove with the window cracked because he liked the smell of salt and cold air, a preference Marta had never shared, and by eleven he had passed the last town with a traffic light and entered a country of shuttered canneries and churches whose paint had been reduced to suggestion.

Vinter Point announced itself with a sign so weathered he had to slow down to read it: WELCOME TO VINTER POINT — POPULATION 612. Someone had crossed out the 612 and written 589 beneath it in marker, and someone else had crossed out the 589. Below that, in a third hand, smaller: *ask again in spring.*

The terminal sat at the end of a lane of buckled asphalt, where the land simply gave up and became a concrete apron running out into the water. He saw it first from above the curve of the road — a low shed-roofed building, a ramp angling down toward the tide line, two mooring dolphins standing offshore like drowned men who had given up swimming. The ferry hadn't run in nineteen years. He knew this from the file. When the bridge up the coast opened in 2004, the crossing had died in under a year, the way crossings do, and the terminal had been left to negotiate its own surrender with the weather.

It was losing, but slowly. That was the thing about marine structures — they failed with a dignity that highway bridges never managed. Salt got into everything, found every hairline, widened it by patience alone. Rust bloomed under paint like bruising under skin. Concrete spalled in rounds, exposing rebar that wept orange stains down the face of the pier like old tears. From the road, the terminal looked less like a building than like a large animal lying down to die, facing the water, because that was where it had always looked.

He parked, pulled on his hi-vis vest out of habit though there was no one for three miles to warn, and walked the apron with his bag of instruments. Calipers, crack gauge, rebound hammer, camera. The routine of it settled over him like a coat. Measure, record, photograph. The deck slab had longitudinal cracking consistent with chloride intrusion — he logged it. The ramp's steel stringers showed section loss at the splash zone — he logged that too, kneeling, calipers open like a doctor's hands. Forty percent loss at the worst point. In his report this would become a number, and the number would become a recommendation, and the recommendation would become a demolition permit, and one day a crew with an excavator would come and take this place apart and haul it away in pieces, and the sea would close over the pilings' stumps as if it had always intended to.

He was crouched at the ramp's mid-span, measuring a crack that ran the length of the concrete like a sentence someone had started and abandoned, when he heard the footsteps.

Not approaching. Already there. That was the strange part — a woman standing ten feet away on the ramp, and he had heard nothing, as if she had risen out of the concrete itself or been carried in on the wind off the water. She was very old, small inside a wool coat the color of wet slate, white hair cut short and practical, hands folded on the head of a cane that was actually a boat oar, varnish worn through to bare wood where her grip lived. She was looking at him with an expression of mild, unhurried interest, the way you might regard weather.

"You're late," she said.

"I— sorry?"

"The man from the county. For the words." She nodded, as if this explained everything. "I expected you in spring. But it's fine. They're ready."

Jonah straightened up too fast and his knee registered a complaint he ignored. "Ma'am, I think there's been a mix-up. I'm from the Department of Transportation. Bridge inspection division. I'm here to assess the terminal."

She considered this. Her eyes were pale blue, nearly colorless, the blue of ice over deep water, and they did not narrow or cloud the way he expected old eyes to. They just looked at him, steadily, with what he would later recognize as amusement.

"Assess," she repeated, tasting the word. "And what will your assessing find?"

"That the structure is beyond economical repair. I'm sorry. I know that's not—" He gestured vaguely at the water, the shed, the whole gray tableau. "I know these places matter to people."

"They matter," she agreed. Then: "You're not the one for the words."

"No, ma'am."

"Hm." She turned, then, and looked out at the water, and he understood himself to be dismissed, except that he didn't leave, and after a moment she spoke again without turning around. "Nobody is. That's the trouble. I've been waiting forty years for the one for the words, and everyone who comes to this ramp comes with a clipboard instead."

"I don't have a clipboard," he offered, stupidly.

"No. You have a little hammer." She nodded at the rebound hammer clipped to his bag. "You go around tapping things to hear if they're hollow."

He blinked. "That's — essentially, yes."

"My father did that with boats. Tapped the hull, listened. He could hear rot before anyone could see it." She finally turned back to him, and something in her face shifted, some door opening a crack onto a lit room. "What do you hear, when you tap this place?"

Jonah looked at the ramp. At the crack he'd been measuring, the exposed rebar, the rust bleeding down the concrete in long orange sentences. He thought about giving her the technical answer — delamination, spalling, section loss, serviceability — and found, oddly, that he didn't want to. It wasn't sentiment. It was more that she had asked the question in a way that deserved a real one.

"Hollow," he said. "In places. Sound in others. It wants to fall down, but it's in no hurry. Things built near water rarely are. The water does the work slowly enough that the structure gets used to it."

The old woman smiled. It transformed her face entirely, the way a current transforms still water, and for a moment he glimpsed whoever she had been at twenty, at forty — someone quick, someone who had laughed easily, someone men had probably been foolish about.

"Well," she said. "You listen better than the last one. He only measured." She began to move past him, up the ramp, her oar-cane striking the concrete in a rhythm like a slow clock. At the top she paused. "Astrid Vinter," she said, without turning. "The terminal has my name on it somewhere, if your paperwork cares. My grandfather built the ferry line. My mother sold tickets in that shed for thirty-one years. I was born in the room above it."

"I'm Jonah Nystrom," he said, to her back.

"I know. The county sends a letter before they send a man. I read everything they send me." She resumed walking. "Come have coffee before you go, Mr. Nystrom. There's a thermos in the shed and it's better than the motel's, which I also know about."

He should have declined. He had half a structure to assess and a form to fill out and a schedule, and schedules were the skeleton of his days — remove them and he wasn't sure anything stood up. But his feet carried him up the ramp after her anyway, and he told himself it was professional courtesy, access to the interior, a site walkthrough, nothing more.

* * *

The shed smelled of creosote and cold iron and, faintly, of woodsmoke, though he couldn't see a stove. Inside it was dim and dry and astonishingly intact — ticket window with its brass grill, wooden benches worn pale where sixty years of waiting passengers had polished them, a wall of pigeonholes labeled with destinations in faded ink. On the counter lay notebooks. Dozens of them. School exercise books, ledger books, the backs of calendars, all filled cover to cover with dense handwriting in an alphabet he didn't recognize — letters with hooks and crosses, marks above the lines like birds sitting on wire.

"What language is that?" he asked, before he could stop himself.

Astrid poured coffee from a dented thermos into two tin cups. "Mine," she said.

"It doesn't look written down anywhere. I've never seen it."

"No. Nowhere but here." She handed him a cup. Her hand shook slightly, and the coffee trembled but did not spill, as if it had learned accommodation. "Four thousand words, give or take. Fewer every year now — I lose them the way you lose teeth. Quietly, and always from the back where you don't notice until you smile." She lowered herself onto a bench with the care of someone handling a fragile cargo. "The council wants your report to say tear it down. And when it's torn down, this goes with it." She touched the notebook on the counter, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "Every word I ever kept."

Jonah stood there with his coffee going cold in his hand and felt something shift in his chest that he had no instrument to measure. He thought of his own house, of the rooms he and Marta occupied like careful tenants, of all the words they had stopped saying and how they hadn't lost them dramatically, either — just quietly, from the back, where you don't notice until you smile.

"I'm here to assess the structure," he said again, but it came out weaker this time, a man restating terms he no longer believed in.

"Yes," Astrid Vinter said. She drank her coffee and looked at him with those ice-blue eyes, patient as tide. "You'll be back."

He drove to the motel that evening through a rain that arrived sideways off the water, and he told himself the whole drive that she was wrong — that he'd file his report, recommend demolition, and never stand on that ramp again. But he caught himself, at a red light in the empty town, reaching for his phone to look up whether any dictionary, any archive, any university on earth held those hooked, bird-perched letters.

There was nothing. Four thousand words, and nowhere but her.

The light turned green. He didn't move. Behind him, a truck waited, then honked, and Jonah Nystrom lifted his foot off the brake and drove on into the rain, already composing — though he'd have denied it under oath — the list of questions he would ask her tomorrow.
