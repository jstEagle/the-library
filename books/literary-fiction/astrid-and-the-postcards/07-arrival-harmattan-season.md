## 7. Arrival, Harmattan Season

The plane descends through a sky the color of weak tea, and Zephyrine understands, before the wheels touch, that he has been lied to all these years — not by anyone in particular, but by memory itself. Memory had kept Lagos small. Memory had kept it the size of a boy's walk to school: the kiosks with their pyramids of soft drinks, the barber's pole spinning its red-and-blue lie, the gutter he jumped every morning like a ritual athlete. What lies beneath him now as the plane banks over Ikeja is a city of towers and cranes and unrepaired promises, a sprawl so vast and lit even in daylight that he grips the armrest like a man on a boat.

*This is not where I am from,* he thinks, and then corrects himself, because the thought is dishonest in a way he can no longer afford. *This is exactly where I am from. It simply declined to wait for me.*

Harmattan has come early, or late — he has lost the calendar of it — and the dust hangs over everything like a curtain drawn by an indifferent hand. Through the window the sun is a pale coin behind gauze. The runway appears out of the murk all at once, as if the ground had been holding its breath.

He has not slept. He has spent the flight doing what he always does with unbearable things: translating them. The safety card into cleaner English. The captain's announcement into something less anxious. His own dread into a manageable noun — *apprehension*, perhaps, or *anticipation*, both of which are lies but well-made ones, load-bearing lies. Somewhere over the Sahara he caught himself composing the opening line of a letter to his mother, thirty years too late, and stopped only when the man beside him asked for the time.

Immigration is a river moving in slow motion. He stands in the citizens' line because his passport still says what it says, and the officer looks at the passport, then at him, then at the passport again, the way one looks at a document that has aged badly.

"Welcome back, sir." A pause, the stamp hovering. "How long?"

"Two weeks."

"You will find it changed."

"I know," Zephyrine says. "I'm counting on it."

The officer smiles without smiling and stamps him through, and Zephyrine walks into Nigeria carrying two bags: one of clothes, one of notebooks. In the second bag, wrapped in a shirt, is a cassette player he bought at Heathrow after three shops told him nobody sold such things anymore. He does not know yet why he brought it. He knows only that some objects must be carried toward their meaning.

* * *

Arrivals is a wall of heat and noise and hands. Drivers thrust laminated signs at faces; families erupt around returning sons; a woman in gold weeps openly into her phone while a child circles her ankles. Zephyrine stands at the edge of it with his trolley, performing the one role he never rehearsed: the man who has come home and cannot find home.

Then he sees Tunde, and Tunde sees him, and for a moment neither moves.

His cousin is heavier than the boy in his memory, grayer, dressed in a pressed shirt the color of sand. But he stands the same way — weight settled low, arms loose, the posture of a man who has decided not to be surprised by anything ever again. Forty years ago this same stance guarded the corner shop's crate of Fanta from boys like Zephyrine.

They meet in the middle. The embrace is brief, correct, and devastating. Tunde smells of dust and petrol and something herbal Zephyrine's body recognizes before his mind does — his grandmother's ointment, or its descendant — and for one unguarded second he is seven years old again, being held by someone who was not going anywhere.

"You look well," Tunde says, stepping back, assessing. "London agrees with you."

"It agrees with me," Zephyrine says. "That's a good way to put it."

The English between them is careful, mutual, almost diplomatic. Each word selected, weighed, offered flat side up. Two men handling glass.

"The bags came?" Tunde asks.

"Both."

"Good. Good." He takes the trolley handle without asking, because that is what cousins do here, and Zephyrine lets him, because refusing would cost more than the surrender. "The car is outside. It is old, but it starts. Like me."

Zephyrine laughs — surprised by the laugh, by how easily it comes, as if some muscle had been waiting thirty years for this exact joke.

Outside, the harmattan has thickened. The car park is a brown sea, headlights swimming toward them like fish. Tunde loads the bags into a dented sedan whose dashboard hosts a small congregation of saints and air fresheners. They pull out into traffic that obeys no law Zephyrine remembers and yet flows, impossibly, the way blood flows — chaotic, purposeful, alive.

For ten minutes neither speaks. The radio murmurs in Yoruba, and Zephyrine catches words the way you catch leaves in a stream: *owo*. *Ile*. *Ojo*. Money, house, rain. His understanding is a roof with holes in it, and through the holes the language falls in cold drops.

"You don't listen anymore," Tunde says, not unkindly, eyes on the road.

"I listen," Zephyrine says. "I just understand less than I used to."

"And speak less."

"And speak less."

Tunde nods slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis he made years ago. "My children are worse," he offers. "Ayomide answers me in English half the time. She says Yoruba is for grandmothers." A beat. "She doesn't mean it as insult. She means it as weather report."

The honesty of it undoes Zephyrine slightly. In London, everyone protects everyone from the truth with upholstery. Here the truth sits in the car with them, third passenger, quiet, patient.

"Tunde." He says the name to steady himself. "The postcards. You said forty-one."

"I said forty-one." Tunde changes lanes with a courtesy that startles a danfo driver into blessing him. "You want to talk about it now? In the car? With the dust in your eyes?"

"No."

"Good. Because I have been practicing what I would say to you for twenty years, and none of it should be said in traffic." He glances over. "You are thinner than your mother described."

The sentence lands like a stone dropped down a well — they both hear it hit water a long time later.

"She described me," Zephyrine manages.

"In every card." Tunde's voice stays level, which somehow makes it worse. "You were the news of the family, Zephyrine. The son in London. She reported on you the way reporters report on wars they cannot attend."

Zephyrine turns to the window. The city scrolls past: a church the size of an airport, a mosque with a green dome, billboards selling data plans and salvation in equal fonts. Everything taller. Everything louder. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that he is inside it, the smell — dust and diesel and roasting plantain and wet earth under dry earth — the smell his body has been calling *home* in a language his mouth forgot.

* * *

They stop once, at a roadside stall, because Tunde insists. "You have not eaten since London. Airplane food is not food; it is apology."

They stand at a wooden counter drinking Fanta out of glass bottles, and Zephyrine watches the woman fry akara with the economy of someone who has done one thing ten thousand times. The oil spits. The harmattan sifts gold through the smoke. A boy of maybe six stares at Zephyrine with frank curiosity until Zephyrine stares back, and then the boy grins, gap-toothed, victorious, and runs off.

"He thinks you are a film actor," Tunde says.

"What do you think?"

"I think you are my cousin who left." No malice in it. Just inventory. "And I think the akara here is better than anything in your London, so eat."

Zephyrine eats. The first bite is so precisely, aggressively itself — hot oil, pepper, bean, salt — that his eyes sting, and he blames the harmattan, and Tunde generously pretends to believe him.

They drive on. The sun, defeated by dust, gives up around four and leaves the world in amber. Streetlights come on confusedly at mid-afternoon. Lagos in December wears its haze like a widow's veil, beautiful and obstructive, and Zephyrine begins to understand that he will not see this city clearly on this trip — not the city, and not whatever else he has come to find. Everything here arrives veiled. You learn to read the shape of things through cloth.

"Tell me about Ayomide," he says, to fill the silence, and immediately regrets the phrasing, which sounds like a form to be completed.

But Tunde takes it seriously, the way he seems to take everything. "My brother's daughter. Sixteen. Sharp like broken bottle. She wants to study linguistics — imagine, in this family, another one obsessed with languages." He shakes his head, but there is pride underneath, poorly hidden, the way pride usually travels in this family: disguised as complaint. "She sings also. Choir, school functions. Her voice is—" He searches. "It is not polite, her voice. It does not ask permission."

Something in Zephyrine's chest tilts.

"Does she sing in Yoruba?"

"When she can get away with it." Tunde looks at him sidelong. "Why?"

"No reason. Curiosity."

"Curiosity." Tunde repeats the word like a foreign coin he is checking for counterfeit. "Thirty years, and now curiosity. Okay. It is a start. Curiosity is a start."

* * *

Surulere announces itself before its name does. The roads narrow. The buildings lean closer, older, painted and repainted until paint becomes geology. There is the pharmacy, still there, sign faded to a ghost of red. There is the spot where the barbershop stood — gone, replaced by a phone-repair kiosk hung with chargers like wind chimes. There is the gutter. Still there. Still jumpable. Some things, at least, kept faith.

Tunde parks in front of a house Zephyrine's feet recognize before his eyes do: the compound wall, the mango tree grown enormous and slightly menacing, the blue gate now rust-brown. Tunde's shop occupies the ground floor — shutters down for the evening, a hand-painted sign reading *ADEYEMI & SONS GENERAL GOODS* that has clearly been repainted recently, hopefully, over a name that may no longer be accurate.

"Adeyemi & Sons," Zephyrine reads.

"My father's vanity. I have no sons." Tunde shrugs. "Repainting costs money. Truth costs more. I chose cheap."

Inside the front room above the shop, the light is the color of honey left too long. A fan turns its slow, useless devotion overhead. There is a sofa older than both of them, doilies on the armrests, a television muted on a football match, and on a shelf against the far wall —

Zephyrine sees it and stops breathing for a moment.

A shoebox. Not hidden, not displayed. Simply there, on the shelf, between a trophy and a tin of biscuits, occupying its space the way the dead occupy rooms: quietly, completely.

Tunde follows his gaze. "Tomorrow," he says gently. "Tonight you eat, you bathe, you sleep. The box has waited twenty years. It can wait one more night. Things that waited twenty years deserve to be opened in daylight."

Zephyrine nods, though every nerve in him leans toward the shelf like a plant toward light.

Dinner is rice and stew and fried fish, eaten across from each other at a table set for more people than are present. Tunde talks — about the neighborhood, the rezoning notices nailed to poles like arrest warrants, the bus route dying next month, the petitions, the meetings. Zephyrine listens, translating as he goes, converting grief into minutes and objections, and realizes halfway through that Tunde is watching him do it.

"You translate everything," Tunde says. "Even my talking. I can see it behind your eyes. My words go in, and something else comes out and sits there."

"That's my work."

"I know it's your work. I am saying it is also your wound." He pushes the fish toward Zephyrine. "Eat. Then sleep. Tomorrow—" he pauses, and for the first time since the airport, the careful English slips, and something rawer shows through, "—tomorrow you read what she wrote to you. And I will sit with you. Because nobody should open that box alone. Not even you. Especially not you."

Later, lying on a mattress in a room that was once his — the walls still bearing faint rectangles where his posters hung, the window still rattling in the same broken key — Zephyrine listens to Lagos breathe. Generators cough and settle. A dog states its case to the moon. Somewhere near, a radio plays, and beneath the static and the distance, a melody rises that he almost — almost — knows.

He lies very still, the way he did in the London dark, letting the past arrive without permission.

Through the wall, faintly, he hears Tunde moving below, and then the sound of a drawer opening, and closing, and opening again — a man keeping watch over forty-one pieces of paper the way other men keep watch over sleeping children.

Tomorrow, the box.

Zephyrine closes his eyes, and the dust of the harmattan settles over the whole city, over the living and the written-to and the unanswered, soft as a hand laid on a shoulder: *stay, stay, stay.*
