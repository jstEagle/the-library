## 11. Two Translators

The radio station sat behind a car wash on Adeniyi Jones, a bungalow that had once been a family home and now wore its conversion like an ill-fitting suit: living room turned reception, bedroom turned studio, acoustic foam nailed over what had clearly been a child's height chart pencilled on the doorframe. Zephyrine stood in the reception for a full minute before anyone noticed him. A ceiling fan turned with the unhurried confidence of something that had outlasted every government since it was installed.

"You're the translator." The woman came through the door marked ON AIR without taking off her headphones, letting them hang around her neck like a stethoscope. She was smaller than she'd sounded on the phone, quicker too, and she looked at him the way he imagined doctors looked at X-rays — searching for the fracture. "Funmi Adeyemi. You found me faster than I expected."

"You said you had a recording."

"I said I might have a recording." She held up one finger, a teacher's gesture, and led him down a corridor that smelled of dust and hot electronics. "There's a difference. Come and hear before we make promises to each other."

The studio was small enough that their elbows would touch if they both reached for the same fader. Two chairs, a mixing desk older than both of them, a computer that looked embarrassed to be there. Funmi cleared a stack of CDs off the second chair and gestured for him to sit.

"Twenty years ago," she said, settling across from him, "I was nobody. A corper, doing my youth service at a hospital in Yaba. I had a cheap recorder because I was writing songs and I didn't trust my memory — still don't, actually. And there was this woman." She paused, adjusting something on the desk that didn't need adjusting. "I've told this story badly so many times it's worn smooth. Let me tell it properly, just once."

Zephyrine said nothing. He had learned, in thirty years of sitting across from writers, that silence was the only honest invitation.

"She was in the corridor outside the ward. Not a patient — visitors' hours were over, so she'd been moved into the hallway, which is what we do with the dying when the beds are needed. She was humming. Not performing. Humming the way people hum when the song is the only thing holding them together." Funmi's eyes went somewhere else entirely. "And it was beautiful. Not pretty — beautiful. It had this structure underneath it, like a house you could live in. I stood there like a thief and recorded forty seconds of it on my little machine before a nurse asked me what I thought I was doing."

"And you never learned her name."

"I never learned her name." She said it flatly, the way you set down something heavy you've been carrying wrong. "By the time I worked up the courage to go back and ask, she'd been discharged. Or the other thing. I asked around the ward and got three different answers and no courage left. But I kept the recording. Twenty years, four apartments, one flood. I kept it because I felt — I don't know the word. Indebted? Like I was holding something that belonged to someone else's grief, and someday someone would come for it."

She turned to the desk. "You called the station after the broadcast. You said the song was your mother's."

"I said she wrote it. She never sang it for anyone but me. She couldn't have sung it in a hospital corridor—"

"Unless she didn't know she was singing it," Funmi said gently. "People do. At the end. The walls get thin."

* * *

She loaded the file while he watched the waveform assemble itself on the screen: a low, irregular mountain range, hiss along its base like fog.

"Forty-one seconds," she said. "I cleaned it up as much as I dared. Every time I filter the noise, I feel like I'm filtering her. So this is the raw version. Brace yourself or don't."

She pressed play.

Static first, then footsteps, a trolley's squeak, a voice saying something administrative and far away. And then, rising out of all of it like a face rising out of water:

Humming.

Zephyrine knew it before the first phrase ended. Of course he did. He had known it in the first bar on the radio, in Lagos, three weeks ago; he knew it in his spine, in the place where language doesn't reach. His mother's melody, note for note, the tune she used to sing half of and stop, laughing, saying *the rest isn't ready yet*, saying it every year of his childhood until the joke became a ritual became a wound.

But this was different from the radio version. This was slower. Unfinished. And underneath the humming — so faint he had to lean toward the speakers until his chest nearly touched the desk — there was something else. A texture inside the sound. A second voice woven into the first, not harmonizing exactly, but *carrying*, the way a river carries silt.

"Turn it up," he said. His voice came out strange.

"That's as far as it goes without falling apart."

"Please."

Funmi pushed the gain until the static bloomed, and there it was, unmistakable now, undeniable: words. Half-voiced words under the hum, syllables surfacing and sinking, and threaded through them, barely, a voice he had spent thirty years failing to forget.

His mother was singing. Not humming. Singing — quietly, privately, in a corridor where she believed no one was listening, the whole song. All of it. Including the verse she had never finished for him. Including the ending.

The room tilted. He gripped the edge of the desk.

"Play it again."

Funmi played it again.

This time he followed it like source text, the way he'd follow a difficult manuscript, tracking the melody against the buried words, and the words were there, they were *there*, worn thin by breath but present, and the last verse — the verse that had always stopped, the door that had always closed — opened. Her voice went through it. Faltering, quiet, going where she had never gone in front of him, and coming out the other side with a final line he could not yet parse but could feel, the way you feel the shape of a key through an envelope.

"It was finished," he said. "It was finished the whole time."

"Yes," Funmi said softly. "It sounds like it."

"She told me it wasn't ready. For twenty years she told me—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "She sang it for strangers in a corridor and told her own son it wasn't ready."

"Or," Funmi said, and waited until he lowered his hands, "she sang it for herself, once, at the end, because she thought no one she loved would ever have to hear it. There's a difference. Translators should know about the difference between what a sentence says and who it's for."

He looked at her. "You're a translator?"

"Aren't you?" She gestured at the waveform. "Forty seconds of noise, and you just read a woman's whole heart out of it. That's translation. I just steal melodies. What I heard that day was a song. What you heard just now was a message." She let the recording play a third time, quieter, almost a courtesy. "Who was it addressed to, do you think? The last verse?"

Zephyrine listened. The static. The trolley. The corridor of a hospital where his mother had lain on a gurney believing herself unheard, singing the ending she had withheld from him for two decades — and the ending, now that he forced himself to attend to the words instead of drowning in the voice, was not sad. It was not a lament. It was an address. A naming. A sending-forth.

It was, he understood with a vertigo that had nothing to do with grief anymore, a blessing. For him. Composed in secret, performed for an audience of linoleum, mailed by accident to a stranger with a cheap recorder, and arriving twenty years late via a Lagos radio station.

"She wrote me a letter," he said, "and posted it to the wrong world."

"And I'm the postman." Funmi ejected nothing, saved everything, then turned in her chair to face him fully. "Here's my problem, translator. I've carried this song for twenty years and sung it on air, and every time I sing it I feel like a man wearing another man's clothes. It fits. It shouldn't fit. I want to give it back. Properly. Recorded right, credited right, the last verse restored. But I can't restore a verse I can't hear under all that hiss. Can you?"

Can he. He thought of the notebook in his hotel room, the transcription of postcards like source text. He thought of the vocabulary gone missing, the English shadows where Yoruba words used to live. He thought of Ayomide, fourteen years old, correcting his tones with the merciless tenderness of the young.

"Not alone," he said. "Not yet. My Yoruba is — archaeological. I dig things up and half of them crumble."

"Then we'll be two translators," Funmi said. "You bring the language. I'll bring the ears. And between us—" she tapped the screen, where the waveform waited like a held breath, "—we owe a dead woman a clean copy of her own song."

Outside, the harmattan pressed its pale face against the window. Inside, Zephyrine played the recording a fourth time, and a fifth, and on the fifth hearing he finally heard the last line clearly enough to write it down, and his hand shook so badly the letters came out doubled, like something seen through water, like something surfacing at last into air.

When he looked up, Funmi was watching him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of someone standing at the edge of a debt they are finally able to pay.

"There's a cassette," he heard himself say. "In my cousin's keeping. In her handwriting. I haven't been able to play it yet."

Funmi nodded slowly. "Then maybe," she said, "we should go and find something that plays tapes."
