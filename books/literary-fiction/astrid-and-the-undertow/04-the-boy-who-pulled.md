## 4. The Boy Who Pulled

The dream did not begin as a drowning. It began, as it always began, with the smell of salt and cut grass — the particular perfume of that summer, which Petra had spent thirty years believing she had invented.

She was on the beach at Wrenlow. The sand was hot enough to hurt through her sandals, and the tide was out so far that the sea seemed to have lost interest in the land entirely, lying flat and glittering a quarter mile down. She was nine years old and carrying a yellow plastic pail shaped like a castle, and she was happy in the uncomplicated way of children who do not yet know what their mothers are capable of keeping from them.

*Come here,* someone said, or the wind said it, or the water said it. In dreams the voice has no throat.

She walked toward the sea. This was the part she had never remembered while awake: how easy it was. One step, then another, the wet sand firm underfoot, then the first cold slap of water at her ankles, her knees, her waist. She could see her mother's towel far behind her, a red rectangle on the dune line, and a woman on it who might have been reading. She could not see her face. Even in the dream, even now, she could not see her face.

The bottom dropped away.

That was the thing about undertows that no one tells you when you're nine: they don't announce themselves. There is no moment where the ocean says *now*. There is only the ordinary business of standing in water, and then there is no bottom where your feet expect one to be, and the world tilts, and the sea closes over your head like a door.

Petra went down.

The water was green and loud and full of light that came apart in her hands when she reached for it. Her hair wrapped around her face like seaweed. She kicked and the kicking did nothing; she screamed and the screaming made bubbles that rose without her. Below her was gray. Above her was a ceiling of silver that kept getting farther away, the way a dream makes distance out of nothing. Her lungs began to burn with a patience that frightened her more than the drowning did, because it meant her body intended to go on doing this for a while.

And then a hand closed around her wrist.

Not gently. That was what she had forgotten, all these years — that it wasn't gentle at all. It was a grip like a trap, like something with teeth, and it hauled her sideways through the water with a violence that felt almost angry. She caught one glimpse of the boy dragging her: dark hair plastered flat, eyes wide open underwater because he didn't know yet that you were supposed to close them, a white scar on his chin she'd never noticed before and would never forget after.

Elias.

He got his arm around her chest from behind and pulled, and they rose together through the silver ceiling into air so bright it was like being born. She coughed up half the Atlantic. He held her under the arms and dragged her backward through the surf, walking when he could, falling when he couldn't, until the water was only at their knees and then their ankles and then gone, and they collapsed on the wet sand with the waves licking at their heels like dogs.

For a long time neither of them moved. Gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their idiot joy. Somewhere down the beach a radio played something with horns in it.

"You were dead," Elias said finally. His voice shook. "You were dead and I got you."

"I wasn't dead."

"You were blue." He sat up, dripping, and looked at her with those wide-open eyes, and she saw that he was crying without any of the machinery of crying — just water running down his face, mixing with the seawater, as if grief were simply another kind of tide. "Don't tell," he said.

"What?"

"Don't tell anyone. Swear it." He grabbed her shoulders, and his fingers left marks she'd find bruises of later and never be able to explain. "If they find out I went in past the breakers, my dad will—" He stopped. Nine-year-olds don't finish sentences about their fathers. They just stop, the way you stop at the edge of a drop. "Swear it."

"I almost died," Petra said. It seemed important to establish this.

"That's why you swear."

So she swore. She swore on the yellow pail, which lay overturned nearby with its castle turrets full of sand, and on her mother, and on whatever else nine-year-olds hold sacred. And Elias nodded once, businesslike, the deal struck, and then he did the strangest thing: he laughed. A short, wild, astonished laugh, the laugh of someone who has just found out he is alive.

"We're not supposed to be friends," he said. "My dad says your mom's—"

He didn't finish that either. The sentence went wherever sentences about fathers go.

They walked back up the beach together, two drowned children wringing out their clothes, and behind them the sea folded itself flat again and pretended nothing had happened, which is, when you think about it, the ocean's whole profession.

* * *

Petra woke on Mrs. Adeyemi's sofa with a start so violent she nearly went off the edge of it.

For a disorienting moment she didn't know where she was — the room was dim, unfamiliar, smelling of lavender and old radiators — and some animal part of her brain insisted she was still underwater, still sinking, and she surfaced from the cushions gasping, one hand pressed flat against her sternum as if to confirm her own geography.

"Easy." Mrs. Adeyemi's voice came from the doorway, low and unhurried. She was holding two mugs, and steam rose off them in the lamplight. "You went down like a stone. Right in the middle of asking me about the Halvorsens' gardenias, if you can believe it. Just — mid-sentence, and down."

Petra pushed herself upright. Her mouth tasted of salt. That was the detail that undid her composure, more than the dream itself: her mouth actually tasted of salt, though she hadn't been near the water since the drive in, though Mrs. Adeyemi's house stood four streets back from the shore. She ran her tongue against her teeth and the taste stayed, stubborn, evidentiary.

"I was dreaming," she said.

"You were dreaming," Mrs. Adeyemi agreed, and set both mugs on the coffee table — tea for Petra, by the smell of it, laced with something medicinal — and lowered herself into the armchair opposite with the careful choreography of a woman whose knees had opinions. "You want to tell me what about?"

"No."

"Fair enough." She wrapped her hands around her own mug. Outside the window, Wrenlow was going about its evening: a dog barking three streets over, a car passing with its headlights sweeping the ceiling, the long sigh of the sea underneath everything, constant as breathing. "You've been asleep twenty minutes. I put a blanket on you. Hope that's not forward."

"It's fine." Petra took the tea. Her hands weren't steady, and she pretended they were. "I'm sorry. I don't usually — faint. Or fall asleep in strangers' houses."

"Forty years of neighbors doesn't make us strangers, love. Though I'll grant it's been a while." Mrs. Adeyemi studied her over the rim of her mug, and there was something in the look — not pity, exactly, but its close cousin, the look people give you when they've decided you're carrying something heavy and have elected not to mention it. "You asked about gardenias. Before you went down. Why gardenias?"

"I don't remember asking about gardenias."

"Well, you did. With real feeling." A pause. The dog stopped barking. "Your mother grew them, you know. Along the boathouse side. Every summer, same as clockwork. She'd be out there at seven in the morning in her dressing gown, deadheading like the fate of nations depended on it."

Something turned over in Petra's chest. "My mother didn't garden."

"Not in the city, maybe. But here?" Mrs. Adeyemi smiled at some private photograph. "Here she was a different woman. That's the thing about Wrenlow, pet. It keeps versions of people. You come back after enough years and you meet strangers wearing familiar faces — including your own."

Petra drank her tea because it gave her hands a job. The blanket had slipped; she pulled it back up, an absurd instinctive modesty, as if the dream had left her undressed. Twenty minutes. She had been out for twenty minutes and had lived an entire afternoon on a beach thirty years gone, and her body believed every second of it. Her wrists ached where the boy had gripped her. She turned them over in the lamplight. No marks, of course. No marks, and yet.

"Mrs. Adeyemi," she said carefully, "did I ever — when I was a child, staying at Halvorsen House — did anything happen? To me, I mean. An accident. Something in the water?"

The change in the older woman's face was small and total, the way a whole landscape changes when a cloud crosses the sun. She set her mug down. She did it slowly, deliberately, the way you set down something fragile when you've decided the conversation has become fragile too.

"Why would you ask that?"

"Please. Did I ever nearly drown?"

The silence stretched long enough that Petra heard the radiator tick twice. When Mrs. Adeyemi finally spoke, her voice had dropped into a register Petra hadn't heard from her before — quieter, older, the voice of a woman deciding, in real time, how much truth a person can hold.

"There was a summer," she said. "You'd have been nine. There was a day on the beach when half the town was out looking, and your mother—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started again. "Your mother never spoke of it after. Not once, not in forty years. And when a woman builds a wall that high, pet, you learn not to knock on it."

"But I was there. I was in the water."

"You were in the water," Mrs. Adeyemi said, "and you came out of it, and that was the miracle everyone agreed to celebrate by never mentioning it again." She leaned forward, and her hand covered Petra's where it gripped the mug — warm, dry, immovably kind. "There was a boy. Do you remember a boy?"

The name rose in Petra like a bubble through green water.

"Elias," she said.

Mrs. Adeyemi's breath went out of her audibly. For a moment she looked, of all things, frightened — this woman who had faced Petra down over tea and blankets and forty years of small talk now looked genuinely afraid, glancing at the window as though the name might have been overheard.

"You remember him."

"I dreamed him. Half an hour ago I'd never heard the name in my life, and now I—" Petra stopped. Steadied herself. "Now I can feel where he grabbed my wrists."

Neither of them spoke. Down the street, the sea went on with its long inhale, its long exhale, patient as a creditor.

"He was sent away," Mrs. Adeyemi said at last, each word placed like a stone across a stream. "That autumn. After. His family left Wrenlow and nobody ever said why, and the two households — yours and theirs — they didn't speak again, not that anyone knew. Your mother wouldn't walk on the south beach afterward. Wouldn't so much as look at it. We all thought it was grief, and maybe it was. But grief usually wants company, pet. Whatever your mother was carrying, it wanted solitude."

"The journal," Petra said. The thought arrived fully formed, cold as seawater. "Her journals. I've been cataloguing them for the estate sale. There are gaps. Whole summers missing. I thought she'd just stopped writing."

"Did you." Mrs. Adeyemi withdrew her hand and picked up her mug again, and her face closed like a shop shutter coming down at the end of the day. "Well. I've said more than I should have, and less than I know, and both of those are a kindness compared to what's in that house. You want my advice?"

"No," Petra said honestly.

"Good girl. Here it is anyway." She pointed one knotted finger toward the window, toward the dark where Halvorsen House stood shuttered and tagged for auction, a mile away and forty years deep. "Whatever your mother buried, she buried it deep, and she buried it there, in that house, and houses give things back. Especially houses about to be sold." She drank. Set the mug down with a click. "Go home, get some sleep, and tomorrow, if you must go digging — dig in the attic. Attics are where women like your mother keep the things they couldn't throw away and couldn't bear to look at."

Petra stared at her. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you fainted in my sitting room and called a dead woman's flowers by name," Mrs. Adeyemi said, "and because somebody in this town owes that boy an apology, and it isn't going to be me." She rose, joints cracking like small applause, and gathered the mugs. "Guest room's made up. Towels on the rack. Don't wander the beach at night — the tide comes in fast here, and it doesn't care what you've survived."

* * *

The guest room was small and clean and smelled of cedar, and Petra lay on top of the quilt without undressing, listening to the sea work the shore a mile off.

She should call Marcus. She should call him and say *the auction, the house, did you know about a drowning, did you know about a boy named Elias* — and she could already hear his answer, already hear the practiced smoothness of it, *Petra, you're grieving, you're exhausted, you're inventing drama out of packing boxes.* Marcus, who signed paperwork quickly. Marcus, whose signature was always dry.

Instead she lay in the dark and let the memory come, because she understood now that it was going to come whether she invited it or not, the way the tide comes, the way debts come due.

The yellow pail. The red towel. The bottom dropping away. The hand like a trap closing on her wrist — and beneath the terror of the drowning, which was terrible enough, she could feel the other thing assembling itself, the thing she had sworn on a plastic bucket never to tell: *if they find out I went in past the breakers, my dad will—*

Will what?

A ten-year-old boy, hauling a stranger's child out of the sea, and his first words, his only price, was silence. Not gratitude. Not glory. Silence. What kind of household taught a child that rescue was a debt better hidden than celebrated?

And her own part in it — God, her own part. She had sworn. She had walked back up that beach with salt drying on her skin and a secret hardening in her chest like a stone, and she had never told her mother, never told anyone, and her mother had known anyway — had somehow known — and had built forty years of silence on top of a child's silence, wall upon wall, until the whole summer was bricked over and Petra herself was standing on top of it, cataloguing teacups, calling it closure.

*Houses give things back.*

She got up. Crossed to the window. Below, Wrenlow lay dark except for the harbor lights, and beyond them the black water moved and moved, folding itself flat, pretending nothing had happened, which is the ocean's whole profession.

Tomorrow she would go back to Halvorsen House. Tomorrow she would climb into the attic, into the dust and the heat and the dark where her mother kept what she couldn't throw away and couldn't bear to look at.

Tonight she lay back down and pressed her thumbs into the insides of her wrists, where a boy's grip had landed thirty years ago, and waited for sleep like a swimmer waiting for the wave that would either carry her or take her under.

Somewhere between waking and dreaming, one image surfaced and refused to sink: a tin box, rusted shut, waiting in the dark above the boathouse — and her mother's handwriting on its lid, though she had never seen it, though she could not possibly have seen it:

*For Petra. When the tide goes out.*
