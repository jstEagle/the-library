## 5. Lot Forty-Seven

The auction house in Marlow Bay had once been a church, and no one had bothered to update it beyond hanging a banner where the stained glass used to be. LOT 47 — HALVORSEN HOUSE & GROUNDS, the banner read, in a font that suggested confidence and, beneath it, a phone number. Petra stood under it for a long moment, letting the absurdity settle over her like weather. Somewhere above her, God had presumably once been. Now there was a lot number.

The preview had been advertised in the regional paper — *Viewing 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., refreshments served* — and refreshments, it turned out, meant a folding table with a coffee urn and a tray of biscuits wrapped so tightly in plastic they looked embalmed. Petra poured herself a coffee she didn't want, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and drifted into the main hall where the auctioneer's staff had set up an easel with the particulars of sale: an architect's rendering of Halvorsen House drawn in that flattering style that made every sagging porch look like a promise.

She had told herself she wasn't going to come. She had told Marcus she wasn't going to come, which had been a mistake, because Marcus had immediately become suspicious in the way he became suspicious of everything she did now, ever since she'd driven to Wrenlow against his advice. *You're not a bidder, Petra. You don't have money. You're grieving. Grieving people do stupid things at auctions.* As if grief were a condition, like vertigo, that might strike her mid-bid.

She wasn't here to bid. She was here because she had spent two nights reading a journal with its final third torn out, and because Mrs. Adeyemi had called her *Pet* in a voice that cracked on the vowel, and because the house had been tagged for auction like an animal marked for culling and she needed to see who else had come to look at it. To see who else wanted it dead.

The hall was fuller than she'd expected. Off-season Marlow Bay didn't offer much in the way of spectacle, and a property auction was at least as good as a regatta. There were retirees in quilted vests, a young couple arguing quietly over a brochure, a man in a hard hat who looked like he'd wandered in from an actual job site. And near the front, surrounded by the particular gravity that money generates in a room — two suits, a woman with a tablet, a lawyer's posture — stood a man Petra recognized before she recognized him, the way you recognize a song from its first two notes.

He was taller than the boy had been. Of course he was. But he stood the same way, weight slightly forward, as if the ground were something he'd been asked to hold down. Elias Vane. She knew it the way she knew her own name, and then immediately doubted it, because the last time she'd seen that face it had been smeared with salt water and nine years old, and this man was forty-something and wearing a coat that probably cost more than her mother's entire estate would fetch.

*Elias Vane.* She'd looked him up the night before, finally, in the weak light of Mrs. Adeyemi's guest room. It hadn't been hard. Vane Development Group. A photograph in a business journal, him at the opening of some waterfront complex in the south, cut of jaw sharpened by money and gymnasiums, the caption calling him *one of the region's most ambitious developers*. There had been other photographs too, older ones, and in one of them — a society page thing from fifteen years ago — she could still see the boy, the set of the mouth before it learned to be a transaction.

She watched him work the room. That was the word for it — *work* — the way he moved from cluster to cluster, shaking hands he'd already shaken, saying something that made the woman with the tablet smile on cue. He didn't look at the rendering of the house. That was the first thing she noticed, the thing that made something cold start up in her chest: everyone else in the room looked at the picture of Halvorsen House. He looked at the people looking at it. The house was already gone for him. It had been gone for a long time. What remained was the site.

Petra set down her untouched coffee and crossed the hall before she could talk herself out of it.

Up close he was worse, or better, she couldn't decide. The polish had texture to it — a faint tiredness around the eyes, a scar she hadn't known about cutting through one eyebrow, thin and white as a fishing line. He was listening to the hard-hat man explain something about drainage, nodding at intervals that suggested he'd already decided the answer.

"Mr. Vane," she said.

He turned. And here was the thing she would turn over for days afterward, the thing she would describe to Mrs. Adeyemi over tea with a kind of helpless precision, hunting for the flaw in her own perception: for a quarter of a second — less, a half of a quarter — something moved behind his face. Not recognition exactly. Recognition is warm. This was the flinch of a man who has heard a floorboard creak in a house he thought he'd emptied.

Then it was gone, closed over like water.

"You'll have to excuse me, ma'am, I'm —" He gestured vaguely at the drainage man, at the room, at the machinery of his morning. "Are you looking for someone on our team? Rachel handles bidder registration."

*Ma'am.* The word landed like a small, precise insult. She was thirty-four. She had been nine years old in the water off Wrenlow beach, and he had been ten, and he had pulled her out by the arm hard enough to leave a bruise she'd shown no one, and he had made her swear on his father's pocketknife — a ceremony of enormous gravity at the time — that she would never tell, and now he was calling her *ma'am* and offering her a woman named Rachel.

"I'm not looking for Rachel," Petra said. "I'm looking at the house."

Something in the drainage man's posture said *this is above my pay grade*, and he drifted off with the instinct of the experienced. The woman with the tablet glanced over. Elias Vane gave Petra his full attention then, and she understood that this, too, was a technique — the total focus, the slight tilt of the head, the impression that you were the only person in the church. He'd learned it in boardrooms. But she'd seen the original version of it once, on a beach, when a soaked ten-year-old had crouched in front of her, coughing seawater, and gripped her shoulders and said, *Look at me. You're okay. Look at me.*

"You're interested in the property?" he said.

"I grew up in it. For one summer." She watched his face as she said it. Nothing. He was better than this. He had to be better than this; the flinch she'd caught had been an accident, a dropped stitch, and now he was reknitting. "Halvorsen House. I was nine."

"A lot of people have history with these old places," he said, and his voice was warm, regretful, the voice of a man at a funeral who has never met the deceased. "That's usually what they come to tell me. And I understand it. I do. But the house has been empty eleven years. It's past saving. What we're proposing is —" and here he did the thing she'd seen developers do in documentaries, the pivot to the horizon, the gesture that includes the future in the frame — "something the town actually needs. Year-round employment. A resort that isn't seasonal. The beach stays public. Better than public — *improved*. Right now that stretch of water kills someone every few years. No lifeguards, no infrastructure. We'd change that."

*The water kills someone every few years.* She felt the sentence go through her like a hook through cloth.

"It already did," she said. "Once. Almost."

He didn't ask what she meant. That was the answer, really. A stranger would have asked. A polite man would have asked. Elias Vane looked at her with his courteous, terrible attention and did not ask, because asking would have opened a door, and this man's whole architecture was doors kept shut.

"Ms. —?" he said, offering the space for her name the way a waiter offers the specials.

"Thornbury," she said. "Petra Thornbury."

And there it was again — the dropped stitch, faster this time, recovered almost instantly, but she was watching for it now and she saw it: the way his gaze went to a point just left of her eyes, the way his hand, which had been extended for a shake, paused in the air for a beat too long before completing its journey. He knew the name. He had known it since the journal article, probably, or since the auction notice went out and some lawyer mentioned the estate, the seller, *M. Thornbury*. He had known it and he had decided, in whatever room where he made such decisions, that she would be a stranger.

"Ms. Thornbury," he said. "Well. I hope whatever you're looking for today, you find it."

*Whatever you're looking for.* As if she'd wandered in off the street. As if the house were a curiosity shop and she a tourist.

"I'm not looking for anything," Petra said. "I'm deciding whether to hate you yet. I haven't decided. It's early."

The woman with the tablet made a small sound that might have been a swallowed laugh. Elias didn't smile. But something at the corner of his mouth — the mouth she remembered saying *swear you won't tell* — twitched, one muscle, involuntary, and was suppressed with a discipline she found genuinely frightening. That was the moment she understood the shape of him. The boy had been all surface feeling, all salt and panic and fierce gripping hands. The man had built himself over the boy the way you cap a well. And the cap was load-bearing. Everything he'd built — the firm, the resort, the jaw, the *ma'am* — rested on that cap holding.

"The reserve is substantial," he said, as if she'd said something else, something sane. "If you're considering a bid —"

"I'm not."

"Then I'm not sure what —"

"Neither am I," Petra said. "That's the honest answer. I don't know what I'm doing here. My mother died three weeks ago and left me a house full of locked drawers and an auction notice for a property I thought we sold when I was a child. So I'm here to look at the picture of the house on the easel and feel something, and instead I'm talking to a man who plans to bulldoze the only summer I remember being happy, and he's calling me *ma'am*."

The hall's noise — the murmur of vests and brochures, the coffee urn's gurgle — seemed to continue around them untouched, but a pocket of quiet had formed, a private weather. The woman with the tablet had turned fully around now, openly watching, and Elias Vane's jaw did something complicated.

"Ms. Thornbury," he said, and his voice had dropped its warmth like a coat. "I'm sorry about your mother. Genuinely. But I didn't make the house empty. I didn't let it rot for a decade. Whatever you've decided I am, I'm a man buying a piece of coastal real estate from an estate that's been trying to sell it since before you were —" he stopped. Recalculated. "For a long time."

"Since before I was what?" Petra said quietly. "Since before I was nine?"

He said nothing. The pocket of quiet deepened. Somewhere near the easel, the auctioneer's assistant was explaining the reserve price to the quilted vests, and the word *reserve* floated across the church like a bird.

"I think you should know," Elias said finally, and now the polish was back but it was the polish over effort, over will, "that this is happening whether you stand here or not. The codicil —" and she saw, with a strange lurch, that he'd said the word before thinking, that it was live in his mouth the way her mother's journal was live in her bag — "whatever attachment your family had to the boathouse, my lawyers are handling it. It won't hold. These things never hold. Sentiment doesn't survive contact with a title search."

"The boathouse," Petra repeated.

"The codicil," he said, as if correcting her, as if the word were his and she'd taken it. "The seller's condition. That any buyer preserve the structure. It's —" and for a moment, just a moment, the businessman receded and something older looked out of his face, something ten years old and soaked to the skin, something that had once gripped a nine-year-old's shoulders on a beach and said *look at me* — "it's not relevant."

"Everything in that house is relevant," Petra said. "That's the entire problem. Nobody relevant has been inside it for eleven years."

He looked at her then — really looked, the technique abandoned, the boardroom emptied out — and what was in his face was so close to fear that she took an involuntary half-step back. It lasted a breath. Then he turned to the woman with the tablet and said, "Rachel, can you make sure Ms. Thornbury has a copy of the particulars?" and the audience was over, the door shut, the well capped, and Petra was holding a glossy brochure for a resort that did not exist, printed over the grave of a house that did.

* * *

She stayed another hour, because leaving immediately would have felt like retreat. She studied the rendering until she could see through it — the infinity pool where the vegetable garden had been, the spa pavilion where the boathouse stood in the artist's version as a charming *restored heritage feature*, which was either an oversight or a lie, and she suspected it was a lie that had been an oversight. She listened to the auctioneer's assistant explain that bidding would open at the reserve, that the reserve was confidential, that interested parties should register with Rachel. She drank the embalmed coffee. She watched Elias Vane leave.

He left the way he'd arrived, through the cluster of people, shaking the hands he'd already shaken, and at the door he paused — one beat, half a beat — and looked back at the easel. At the house. Not at her. He looked at the picture of Halvorsen House for perhaps three seconds, and his face did nothing at all, and that nothing was the loudest thing in the church.

After he was gone, Petra stood under the banner with the lot number and took out her phone. Marcus had texted twice: *Did you go?* and then, an hour later, *Petra. Call me.*

She didn't call. She opened the browser instead and searched, with the dull methodical patience grief lends you, *Vane Development Group, Wrenlow, resort, planning permission*. The results loaded in a cascade. There was the firm's announcement, all glass and adjectives. There was the regional planning board's agenda, item fourteen. And there, third from the top, a local news piece from eight months ago, a photograph she had to zoom on twice to believe:

Elias Vane, at a groundbreaking ceremony forty miles up the coast, shaking hands with a man in a suit. And beside him, half out of frame but unmistakable, smiling the smile of a man standing next to money — Marcus. Her brother. At a Vane event, eight months ago, while their mother was still alive, while the auction notice was still a secret, while the house she'd thought was sold was quietly being readied for the block.

The caption named him. *Marcus Thornbury, regional finance consultant.*

Petra stood in the deconsecrated church with the brochure crinkling in her fist and felt the ground do the thing ground does in dreams. Outside, the sea was doing what it always did, coming in and going out, coming in and going out, patient as a debt.

She texted Marcus back, two words, and put the phone in her pocket without waiting for the reply:

*We need to talk.*
