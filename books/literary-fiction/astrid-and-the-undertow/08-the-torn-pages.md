## 8. The Torn Pages

Mrs. Adeyemi made tea the way other people performed surgery: with total attention and no wasted motion. She warmed the pot first. She measured the leaves by eye and by some private arithmetic she had never once gotten wrong in the forty years she'd lived on Pelican Row. She let it steep for exactly as long as it needed and not a second more.

Petra sat at her kitchen table — the same table, she realized now, where she'd eaten shortbread at nine years old, though the oilcloth had been replaced by bare pine — and watched her hands shake around the cup Mrs. Adeyemi set in front of her.

"You look like your mother," the old woman said, settling across from her. "When she was young. Before."

"Before what?"

Mrs. Adeyemi looked at her for a long moment. Her eyes were pale brown, nearly amber, and they did not flinch. "Before she learned to smile like that," she said. "The one that ends conversations. You do it too. I saw you do it to the fishmonger yesterday when he asked how long you were staying."

Petra's hand went to her mouth before she could stop it. A reflex. An admission.

"I don't know what I'm doing here," she said. It came out smaller than she intended. "I keep telling myself I'm cataloguing the house. For the estate sale. But I drove four hours past my brother's advice, and I slept on your sofa, and I dreamed about—" She stopped. The dream was still lodged in her throat like a stone: green water, a small hand gripping her wrist, a voice saying *don't tell*.

"Elias," Mrs. Adeyemi said.

Not a question. Petra's cup rattled against its saucer.

"How do you know that name?"

"Because I was here." Mrs. Adeyemi folded her hands on the table. They were dark, fine-boned hands, a pianist's hands gone knuckled with age. "Because everyone on this street was here, Petra. Because what happened that summer didn't happen quietly, whatever your mother told you afterward. It happened loudly. It happened for months. The only person it happened quietly *to* was you."

* * *

Outside the window, Wrenlow went about its off-season business. A gull argued with something on the roof of the shed. Down the lane, the sea made its long gray argument with the shore, patient, endless, indifferent to what it had almost taken thirty years ago.

"Tell me," Petra said.

Mrs. Adeyemi poured herself tea she didn't drink. Petra understood it was a stalling gesture, a way of arranging her face before she spoke.

"The boy's name was Elias Vane," she said. "His family rented the Corliss place for three summers running — the big shingled house at the north end, before the developers got it. His father was some kind of financier. London money. He wore linen suits even in the fog and he tipped too much, which is its own kind of insult if you think about it." A thin smile crossed her face and left again. "Elias was a quiet boy. Watchful. His father treated him like an employee who hadn't yet earned his wages. You'd see them on the beach, the two of them, and the boy would stand at attention like a soldier being inspected."

"And me?"

"You were inseparable from him by mid-July. Your mother used to sit up on the dune grass with her book and watch you two dig holes to China. She seemed pleased about it, then. I remember thinking so at the time. She liked that you'd found a friend, because you were a shy child — you hid behind her legs at the market for the first two weeks." Mrs. Adeyemi's voice gentled. "And then the eleventh of August."

Petra's mouth went dry. "I don't remember the date."

"You wouldn't. But this town does. There are people still living on this street who were on that beach." She said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather, and somehow that made it worse. "The tide was running hard that day. Riptide off the point — we'd had warnings posted all week. You went in past your knees. And then the water took you."

"I know," Petra whispered. "I remember the water."

"Do you?" Mrs. Adeyemi studied her. "Do you remember what happened after?"

Green light. The roar in her ears. A hand — small, bony, impossibly strong — closing around her wrist. Sand churning under her back. A boy's face above hers, white-lipped, saying words she had never been able to retrieve until last night on this woman's sofa.

*A boy pulled you out,* she'd thought, waking. Now she said it aloud: "Elias pulled me out. He saved my life. And then—"

"And then," Mrs. Adeyemi said, "your mother destroyed his family."

The sentence landed in the kitchen and sat there between them, enormous and cold.

"That can't be right," Petra said. "She never — she never even mentioned him. If she blamed someone, why would she never say so? Why would she raise me in a house where his name was never spoken?"

"Because blame doesn't need to be spoken to be practiced." Mrs. Adeyemi finally lifted her cup. Set it down again untouched. "Listen to me, girl, because I'm only going to say this once, and I'm going to say it plainly because you've had enough softness and silence to last a life. After the drowning, your mother stood up on that beach in front of half the town and accused the Vane family. She said the boy had lured you out past the markers. That he'd dared you. That his father's negligence — letting the children swim unsupervised — was criminal. She used words like *reckless* and *unfit*. She said them loudly. She said them to anyone who would listen, and then she said them to people who weren't listening, which is worse."

"She said Elias lured me out?" Petra felt the ground tilt under the table. "That's not — I don't remember daring. I don't remember anything except the water and the hand."

"Of course you don't. You were nine, and you'd nearly died, and within a month your mother had you on a train home and enrolled you in a new school in a new town, and every summer after that was mountains or lakes or your aunt's farm — anywhere but saltwater." Mrs. Adeyemi's amber eyes held steady. "A child's memory is clay, Petra. Your mother pressed her thumb into yours and shaped it while it was still wet."

"But why?" The question tore out of her louder than she meant. "Why would she lie? He *saved* me. What possible reason—"

"What reason does a frightened woman have to invent a villain?" Mrs. Adeyemi said quietly. "Think about it. Truly think. Not what she told the town. What she couldn't have told anyone."

Petra thought. And the thought arrived like cold water finding a crack.

She had been there. On the beach. Watching.

No — no, that wasn't memory, that was inference, that was the dream rearranging itself — but the dream had been so precise. The figure on the sand, motionless. The book lying open, pages lifting in the wind. She had assumed it was a stranger. She had assumed—

"She was there," Petra said. Her own voice sounded far away. "She was on the beach. She watched me go under."

Mrs. Adeyemi didn't confirm it. She didn't need to. Her silence was a door standing open, and everything Petra needed was on the other side of it.

"She froze," Petra said. Each word cost something. "My mother watched her daughter drown and she froze, and a ten-year-old boy did what she couldn't. And she couldn't live with that. So she made him the villain instead of the hero, because the alternative was being the woman who watched."

"Oh, she was cleverer than that, in a terrible way," Mrs. Adeyemi said. "She never quite said he saved you. Listen carefully — that was always the trick of it. *Lured you out. Dared you. Was responsible.* She built the story so that the rescue became part of the crime. If he hadn't taken you near the water at all, there'd have been nothing to be rescued from. Do you see? She made his heroism evidence against him."

Petra pressed both hands flat against the table, as if the wood might hold the world still. "What happened to him? To the family?"

"The war lasted eleven weeks. Your mother wrote letters — to the town council, to the newspaper, to the Vanes' landlord. Mr. Vane wrote back, twice, furious letters he read aloud at the pub until the publican asked him to stop. It split the street down the middle. Half the town thought your mother was grieving and deserved grace. The other half had watched a ten-year-old boy cough up half the Atlantic on that sand and couldn't stomach what she was doing to him." Mrs. Adeyemi's jaw tightened. "I was in the second half. I told her so to her face. She smiled at me — that smile — and never spoke to me again. Until today, I suppose, through you."

"And Elias?"

"He stopped coming to the beach. Then he stopped coming outside. You'd see him at the window of the Corliss place, just watching the street, like a kid waiting for a punishment he'd already been promised." Her voice roughened. "In late October, the vans came. The whole household, gone in an afternoon. No goodbyes. The father had business interests everywhere; he simply closed the account on Wrenlow. Some said your mother's letters reached his creditors. Some said he didn't need the excuse — that he'd decided the town wasn't worth the trouble. Either way, the boy was removed like a stain."

"Removed," Petra repeated. "He was a child. He saved a child's life and he was *removed*."

"And your mother kept the house," Mrs. Adeyemi said.

Petra looked up sharply. "What?"

"Halvorsen House. Everyone assumed the Vanes' departure ended the summers — that your family sold up like sensible people and moved on. But your mother bought it. Quietly, a few years later, through a solicitor in Ashcombe. She never stayed in it. Never rented it. Never sold it. Forty years she paid taxes and insurance on a shuttered house in a town she swore she hated." Mrs. Adeyemi turned her cup slowly on its saucer, one full rotation, like a dial being set. "People here called it eccentric. Grief does strange things, we said. But grief that keeps paying premiums on the scene of the crime — that's not grief, girl. That's a debt being serviced."

* * *

Petra walked back along the seawall without deciding to. Her feet knew the way the way her mouth knew her mother's smile — by rote, by inheritance, without consent.

The town was performing its November self: shutters half-closed, lobster boat up on blocks, the bakery selling bread to a population of twelve. People nodded to her. A man mending a net by the harbor wall looked up and said, "You're Marion Thornbury's girl," and it wasn't a greeting, it was an identification, the way you'd identify weather coming in.

At Halvorsen House she let herself in through the kitchen — the lock still stuck the same way, lift and push — and stood in the hall breathing the cold air of a house that had been holding its breath for decades.

Her mother had bought it. Had hidden it. Had filled it with furniture arranged like a stage set for a summer that never resumed: the wicker chairs, the driftwood mirror, the board games with their cellophane still sealed. A museum of the crime scene. Or a shrine. Or a cell.

Petra climbed the stairs. In the attic, under the eaves where the light came through the round window like a coin at the bottom of a well, she found the journal again — her mother's handwriting marching across decades of pages, meticulous, controlled, each entry a small performance of order.

She'd read the final third the night before Marcus called. Or she'd read what remained of it. The entries stopped in July of that summer — beach picnics, Elias's name appearing with careful casualness (*P. has made a friend at last, a serious little boy, rather solemn*) — and then the binding went thin. Torn out. Not ripped in haste: cut, close to the spine, with a blade. Deliberate. Archival. The work of a woman curating her own record.

Petra had told herself it was damage. Damp, mice, time. A convenient story, and she'd swallowed it whole, because the alternative was unbearable.

Now she sat on the attic floorboards with the journal open on her knees and counted the stubs of pages. Eleven. Eleven entries, give or take, spanning August through October — the drowning, the war, the vans. The exact season. Her mother had amputated the summer from her own history with a razor and kept the rest of the body intact, forty years of grocery lists and garden notes and complaints about Marcus's school fees, all preserved, all legible — except the eleven pages that mattered.

You don't destroy a record you're ashamed of, Petra thought. Shame burns things. This was different. This was surgical. This was a woman who wanted the truth to exist and not exist at once — cut free but kept somewhere, the way you'd keep a tooth, a letter, a key.

*Kept somewhere.*

Petra's breath fogged in the cold attic air.

Her mother had hidden a house for forty years. Had paid its debts in secret. Had maintained, beneath a life of practiced smiles and unopened drawers, an entire parallel architecture of concealment — and women like that, women who build vaults, do not burn their documents. They file them.

She looked at the journal's torn spine and felt something shift in her chest — not the dull weight of grief she'd been carrying since the funeral, but something with edges. Direction. Her mother had spent a lifetime smiling conversations to their deaths, and Petra had inherited the smile like a heirloom, wearing it at funerals and fishmongers' stalls, and underneath it her mother had filed away the one document that explained everything.

Find it, Petra thought. Whatever she couldn't burn, she couldn't throw away either. It's here. It's in this house, or it's wherever she kept the deed she also never mentioned.

Downstairs, her phone buzzed against the hall table. Marcus.

"You're still there." Not a question. His voice had the flatness he reserved for invoices and bad news. "Petra, the auction paperwork needs your signature by Friday. The solicitor has sent it twice. Can you please behave like an adult with responsibilities for five consecutive days?"

"There's something wrong with the house," she said. "With the sale. With all of it."

"There's something wrong with *you*, is what there is. Mom's barely in the ground and you're out there having feelings at the ocean. Sign the papers. Let the buyers deal with the rot and the codicil and whatever else the lawyers are fighting about—"

Petra went very still. "What codicil?"

A pause. One beat too long, the pause of a man checking which card he'd shown.

"It's nothing," Marcus said. "Legal boilerplate. Sign the papers, Petra."

But she was already reaching for a pen, already turning to the kitchen counter where the solicitor's letter lay beneath the fruit bowl, unread past its first paragraph — and at the bottom of the page, in language she'd skimmed like all the other deadening clauses, a single line now rose up at her as if printed in fire:

*Per the seller's codicil, any purchaser shall preserve in perpetuity the boathouse structure in its existing condition.*

Her mother had left a message. And Marcus had known.
