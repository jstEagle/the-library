## 2. Astrid

The name on the envelope was *Astrid*, and that was the first wrong thing, because Astrid was a woman's name, or so Lena had always believed, and her mother had never once mentioned a woman named Astrid in thirty-one years of careful, curated silence.

The second wrong thing was the address. Not a street in the city where her mother had spent her whole visible life, but a road out along the headland — the old lighthouse keeper's cottage, the one that had been a ruin for as long as Lena could remember, except it wasn't a ruin anymore. She drove out on a Tuesday with the box of letters riding shotgun like a hostage, and found the cottage restored, whitewashed, its windows reflecting the grey water back at itself. A man was splitting wood in the yard. He stopped when her car crunched onto the gravel, and he didn't smile.

She'd rehearsed this. Some version of it. *Excuse me, I'm looking for a man named Astrid, my late mother wrote him letters, they were never sent, I don't know why I'm here.* But standing in the yard with salt wind pulling at her coat, all of it collapsed into: "I'm looking for someone called Astrid."

The man set down the axe. He was maybe forty, dark-haired, with his sleeves rolled and sawdust on his forearms, and he looked at her the way you look at weather coming in off the sea — assessing whether to bring things inside.

"You're the Montrose daughter," he said.

It wasn't a question, which meant her mother's name had traveled ahead of her, which meant there were people in this town who remembered, which meant everything Lena had catalogued as clinical fact for three days was about to stop being clinical.

"Lena," she said. "And you are?"

"Tomas." He wiped his hands on a rag that only moved the sawdust around. "Astrid was my father."

*Was.* The past tense landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there.

"He died four years ago," Tomas said. "Spring tide took the boathouse the same winter. My mother says the house grieved before we did." His eyes went to the box under her arm. "Those are hers? The letters?"

"You know about them."

"I know my father kept a locked drawer his whole life and told us never to open it. I know after he died my mother opened it and found nothing but an empty space shaped like a bundle of paper, and she cried for a week without telling any of us why." He looked at the sea, then back. "So either you have the wrong box, or my father sent those letters back to your mother, or—" He stopped himself. Took a breath. "Come inside. I'll make coffee. I'm not promising anything else."

* * *

The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary, and Lena sat at a table worn smooth by generations of elbows while Tomas made coffee with the economy of a man who resented every motion. On the wall hung a photograph: a young man on a boat, laughing, one hand shading his eyes. Even in black and white, even squinting into sun, Lena could see it — the same bones as her mother's face. No. Not the same bones. Complementary ones. The way two halves of a coastline fit.

"Read me the first one," Tomas said, setting down two cups. "If I'm going to be complicit in whatever this is, I want to hear it from the source."

Lena hesitated. Her mother's voice had lived in her head for thirty-one years — dry, precise, allergic to sentiment. Reading these words aloud would put a stranger's voice in that space, a younger woman, a woman who wrote things like what was written on the first page:

*The barometer fell all night and I thought of you, which is how I know the instrument works. Fair warning: I am not a person who says things plainly. If you need plainness, look at the sky instead. Low pressure means I miss you. High pressure means I am pretending I don't.*

Tomas had gone very still. "That's not— people didn't write like that. People don't write like that now."

"She was twenty-three," Lena said. "I've never heard her sound like this. I've never heard her sound like anything." She turned the page. "There's a code in it. Weather terms. I think — I think the whole thing is a cipher. *Squall* might mean argument. *Fog* might mean—"

"Don't," Tomas said. "Don't decode it yet. Just read."

So she read. Letter after letter, her voice growing rougher as the afternoon light went copper and then violet over the water, and between them the romance assembled itself like a wreck rising from shallow water.

Her mother had met Astrid — Elias, actually; *Astrid* was the boat, his father's fishing boat, and he'd signed himself by the boat because his own name was the name of the man her family despised — in the summer of 1968, when she came to the coast to recover from something the letters never named. He taught her to read tides. She taught him that a restaurant meal could be a form of architecture. They met in the gap between her world and his, and for eleven months the gap held them both.

Then the twelfth letter, dated October, and the pressure dropping through every line:

*My father has learned the shape of your name. He said it at dinner the way you'd say a word you'd stepped in. There is a marriage arranged — not to me, don't flatter yourself, to my sister's future, to the restaurant, to the whole gray machinery of respectability, and I am expected to stand at the stove of it for the rest of my life and call the steam happiness. Fog all week. Fog in my chest. I keep writing the sentence where I tell you I'm choosing you and I keep arriving at the end of it and finding I can't lift the pen past the part where I imagine your face afterward. Squall warning. Squall warning. Squall—*

"That's where it stops?" Tomas asked.

"That's where that one stops." Lena smoothed the page flat with both hands, as if it might continue under pressure. "There are more. Six more. They get shorter. The last one is half a sentence."

Tomas stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there with his back to her, and she watched the muscles of his shoulders do something complicated.

"My father waited," he said finally. "Did you know that? He married my mother in '74, and my mother will tell you herself she always knew she was second choice, and she stayed anyway, and he was — he was a good husband. A good father. But every October he'd walk out to the point and stand there for an hour, rain or shine, and none of us ever knew why until right now." He turned around. His eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Your mother chose the stove. And then she put the evidence in a wardrobe and let both of them live sixty years thinking the other one got the better deal."

"I know," Lena said. It came out smaller than she intended. "I've been trying not to know it since Tuesday."

"Why bring them here at all, then?" He crossed back toward the table but didn't sit, braced above her like weather. "Why not burn them? Why not leave them in the house for the estate sale with the rest of the furniture?"

"Because she kept them." Lena heard her own voice crack and hated it and kept going. "Forty years behind a wardrobe. You don't keep a thing that long unless part of you was still deciding."

The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, the light failed by degrees, the way it does on the coast — not darkness falling but brightness being slowly withdrawn, like a hand.

"My mother is still alive," Tomas said abruptly. "Elias's widow. She lives in the annex. If those letters come into this house, she'll know within the hour, and I won't lie to her. So understand what you're asking."

"I'm not asking anything yet."

"No. You're reading love letters to a stranger in his dead father's kitchen, and neither of us has eaten since morning, and you keep touching the edge of that last envelope like it's going to bite you." He pulled out the chair across from her and sat, finally, heavily. "Tomorrow. Come back tomorrow and we'll read the rest. All of them. Including the unfinished one. And then we decide together what happens — because it stopped being your mother's story or my father's story somewhere around 1968, and God help us, it looks like it's becoming ours."

He said *ours* the way you'd say a word you'd stepped in.

Lena gathered the letters, and her fingers found the final envelope again — thin, half-written, heavier than anything else in the box. Through the window, the first stars were out over water that had gone the color of iron.

"One condition," she said, standing. "When we read the last one — whoever it turns out to be for — we read it aloud. Both of us. I don't want to find out alone."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face shifted, some load-bearing wall giving way.

"All right," he said. "But Lena — if that last letter is addressed to my father, and it ends mid-sentence, you should know my mother keeps his side of the correspondence. Every reply he ever wrote. In a drawer upstairs."

The floor seemed to tilt gently, like a deck at anchor.

"He answered her?" Lena whispered. "But she never sent—"

"We'll see tomorrow," Tomas said, "what my father was answering."
