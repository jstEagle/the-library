## 13. The Night Librarian's Method

Sleep did not come to the room above the diner. It came near her sometimes—circled the bed like a cat she'd once fed and then stopped feeding—and then left, offended, at some small sound: a truck downshifting on the river road, the pipes ticking in the walls, Marisol's radio downstairs playing something with too much accordion in it for three o'clock in the morning.

Quentin lay awake and listened to the town breathe. That was the trouble with Vellum Falls. In the city, insomnia was anonymous; you were one of ten thousand people staring at ceilings, and the ceiling didn't know you. Here, the dark knew her. The dark had known her at fourteen, standing barefoot on Halloway Street in a borrowed coat, watching water arc from the fire hoses and turn to steam against the west wall of her childhood. The dark remembered everything. It was the town that pretended to forget.

At four she gave up on sleep the way you give up on a book that isn't going to improve, and got up, and dressed, and took out what she'd bought that afternoon at the drugstore: a pack of index cards, a box of paper clips, a legal pad whose yellow was aggressive enough to feel like an accusation. She arranged them on the little table beneath the window, where the streetlight came through thin curtains and laid a pale rectangle across the surface, like a page waiting to be written on.

Then she did what she always did when the world became unbearable. She catalogued it.

* * *

People imagined that cataloguing was about order, but they were wrong. Cataloguing was about honesty. Every record in the Meridian Library's night returns came to her desk carrying some small lie—a mislabeled spine, a call number from a defunct scheme, a title that promised one thing and delivered another—and her job, twenty years of it now, was to look at each item until it told the truth about itself. What is this? Where has it been? Who touched it last? What does it want to be mistaken for?

She drew a line down the middle of the legal pad. On the left she wrote *What we are told*. On the right, *What the objects say*.

Under the first column: October 14th, fire at 14 Halloway Street. Cause of death, Tobias Coldharbour, twelve, smoke inhalation. Official determination, filed by Fire Marshal R. Grieves: electrical fault, aging wiring in the north wall, origin point the upstairs hallway. Insurance paid out to the estate. Estate administered by—she wrote the name slowly, letting the pen press hard enough to emboss the card beneath—Aldous Harrow, family friend, executor under a power of attorney signed by her mother two weeks before the fire, when her mother was already half-gone on the pills the clinic kept prescribing her. The house stood empty. Taxes went unpaid. And then, per the chain of title she'd pulled yesterday from the county records office with hands so steady they frightened her: transfer of deed executed October 17th, 1999, grantor *Quentin M. Coldharbour*, signature witnessed and notarized.

She was fourteen years old on October 17th, 1999. She had been staying with an aunt in Corvane, forty miles south, in a house that smelled of potpourri and grief, sleeping in a bed that wasn't hers, wearing clothes that weren't hers, signing nothing, seeing no one. She had not signed anything. She would have remembered signing. You remember the first time your name leaves your hand without your permission. It is not the kind of thing the body forgets.

On the right-hand column, under *What the objects say*, she wrote: *The signature is mine and is not mine.* She had studied it for an hour at the records office while the clerk waited with the polite patience of a man being paid by the hour. The forger had done competent work—the Q with its little tail, the descenders slanted just so—but competence was its own confession. Her handwriting at fourteen had been a mess of contradictions; she'd been teaching herself italic nibs from a library book and her letters lurched between styles depending on her mood. This signature was calm. Assured. Written by someone who knew who Quentin Coldharbour was supposed to become, which meant it was written by someone who had studied her later hand—or studied her mother's, which hers resembled. Either way: someone close. Someone patient. Someone who had time to practice.

You could tell a lot from a forged signature, if you'd spent twenty years looking at what people leave behind in books. Margins were confessional. People underlined the sentences that wounded them and doodled in the corners when they lied to themselves. A forged signature was a margin note on a whole life: *I was here, and I knew you wouldn't check.*

But the signature was only the door. Behind it was the fire.

* * *

She made herself coffee in the room's single cup, using the machine that gurgled like something drowning, and spread the index cards across the table in a grid. Timeline cards. One event per card, one card per fact, because facts kept honest in isolation and lied when crowded together. This was the method. This was the thing she knew how to do that no one in this town seemed to have done in twenty years: take every account, lay them side by side, and find where they refused to touch.

Card one: 9:47 p.m., first call to dispatch, per the newspaper clipping Marisol had given her—Marisol, who had pressed the yellowed page into her hands at 3 a.m. two nights ago with an expression like a woman handing over a live coal. *Harrow truck parked on Halloway Street,* Marisol had said. *Two men, unloading. I thought they were moving furniture in the dark, God help me.* And the volunteer crew delayed by a false alarm called in across town, by the rail yard, a report of smoke that turned out to be nothing.

Card two: 10:12 p.m., first engine on scene. Twenty-five minutes. She'd checked with Bess's paralegal by phone—Bess herself was in depositions all week, but the paralegal, a brisk young man named Theo, had pulled the response-time standards for rural volunteer departments and read them to her over a bad connection. Standard for a call within town limits: twelve minutes. They'd run drills. Everyone in Vellum Falls knew the drill schedule; the siren tested it every first Tuesday. Twenty-five minutes was not incompetence. Twenty-five minutes was architecture.

Card three: origin of fire, upstairs hallway, north wall, per the marshal's report. Electrical fault. Aging wiring.

She sat back and looked at that card for a long time.

Here was the thing about houses: they confessed. A burned building was a document, and fire marshals were its translators, and translators had loyalties. She was not an arson investigator. She was a night librarian who had spent two decades reading marginalia, and she knew that she was reaching past her training—but she also knew how to read a report the way she read a returned book, asking not *what does it say* but *what is it not saying*. So she had requested a copy of the full marshal's file through Bess's office, and it had come that morning in a padded envelope, forty pages of carbon-copy forms and grainy photographs, and she had read it twice in the diner booth with her toast going cold, and there was a thing in it that would not sit still.

The photographs.

Page thirty-one: interior, second floor, hallway, post-suppression. The caption said *origin area, general view.* The photograph showed the hallway after the fire and after the water—char on the walls, the floor blackened, the ceiling partially collapsed. And soaked into all of it, unmistakable even in a photograph taken days later, the pattern of the water damage: long vertical runs of staining down the north wall, pooling lines along the baseboard, the particular tide-marks that water leaves when it stands and recedes and stands again.

Water damage on the north wall. The wall where the fire was said to have started.

She turned the page. Page thirty-two: *origin area, detail.* Same wall, closer. And here the char pattern was visible, and the char told a different story than the report did. Fire climbs. Fire moves upward and outward from its origin, burning hottest at the source and leaving the classic conical patterns, the V-shapes that investigators read like arrows pointing home. But the char in the photograph ran low and lateral along the baseboard, spreading outward across the floor of the hallway toward the stairwell, and the lower portion of the north wall—the portion behind the baseboard heater cited as the fault point—showed comparatively light damage, scorching rather than consumption, while the burn deepened toward the center of the hall and toward the stairs. Toward Tobias's room. Toward the room where he had died, three doors down, with the door closed, because he was twelve and someone had told him to stay in his room until the adults came.

If the wiring in the north wall had failed—if that wall had birthed the fire—the wall would be the most burned thing in the photograph. Instead it was among the least. The fire had not started there. The fire had arrived there, moving fast and low, fed by something that burned hotter and faster than a hallway's worth of old wallpaper and pine.

Accelerant. The word arrived in her mind with the modesty of a word that has been waiting a long time in an anteroom. She wrote it on a fresh card and her hand did not shake, and she noticed that it did not shake, and that noticing felt like meeting a stranger who turns out to be yourself at an older age.

* * *

The sky over the river road was still dark, but the quality of the dark had changed—that thinning, grudging pre-dawn dark that means the night has begun negotiating its surrender. Quentin pinned the index cards to the corkboard above the desk, the one Marisol had put up years ago for postcards from former guests, and stepped back, and looked at the shape of what she'd built.

It looked like a constellation. That was the danger and the mercy of it. Alone, each card was a pebble: a response time, a photograph, a torn page, a truck seen by a woman who had spent twenty years telling herself she'd imagined it. Together they drew a figure in the sky, and the figure had a name, and the name was on the library wing and the clinic and the bandstand, engraved in bronze, polished by the thumbs of grateful citizens.

She thought about the way Aldous Harrow had gripped her hand at the diner. Warm. Dry. The grip of a man who owns the hand he is shaking and knows it.

And she thought about the missing page in the dispatch log—not lost, not damaged, but *torn cleanly*, which is a thing that requires access and calm and a reason. Logs don't tear themselves. Pages don't leave buildings. Somewhere in this town was a person who had stood at a dispatch desk twenty years ago and pulled a sheet of paper off a ring binder and torn it out, and that person had gone home afterward and eaten dinner and slept, or tried to, and had lived with the shape of that absence ever since, the way she had lived with the shape of hers.

Everyone in this story had a missing page. Hers was a brother. Marisol's was a night in October. The dispatcher's was a piece of paper. Even Ada—who had not yet answered the message Quentin had left at the auction house, *we need to talk, not about the auction, about before*—even Ada carried pages torn out of her own ledger; Quentin had seen them in her face at the catalog desk, in the way she'd said *my father flagged the file* and then stopped, as though the sentence had more rooms and she wasn't sure she wanted to lead anyone through them.

That was the method, really. That was the whole of it, the thing she'd never articulated because articulating it would have required admitting it was a philosophy: you cannot recover what people refuse to say. But you can recover what they've written down without meaning to. The world files itself. Every act leaves a record somewhere—in a logbook, a photograph, a stain, a signature practiced on scrap paper until it could pass. Silence is not emptiness. Silence is a document with the text removed, and the removal always shows, if you hold it up to the right light.

Faulty wiring. She almost admired it. Of all the causes available to a fire in an old house, faulty wiring was the kindest, the most forgivable, the one that blamed no one—blamed, in fact, the house itself, the poor old house with its aging bones, as though the house had died of natural causes at sixty-one instead of being murdered at fourteen Halloway Street with her brother inside it. Faulty wiring asked nothing of anyone. Faulty wiring let a town go back to sleep.

But the water stains on the north wall had testified otherwise, in their patient, tidal language, and no one had thought to silence them because no one had thought they were speaking.

* * *

Downstairs, the grill began to hiss. Marisol opened at five, had opened at five for thirty years, and Quentin heard the familiar percussion of the morning: the screen door, the coffee urns, the flat-top scraping. Through the floor came the smell of onions finding oil, and underneath it the river smell that got into everything in this town, the smell she had spent twenty years not missing.

She gathered the marshal's photographs back into their envelope, squared the index cards on the corkboard, and stood a moment at the window as the streetlight clicked off and the rectangle of pale light slid off the table like a page turning.

There was a next step, and she knew what it was, and she did not want to do it, which was how she knew it was right. The marshal's report had a name on it—R. Grieves—and R. Grieves, according to the directory Theo had sent, still lived in Vellum Falls, on Delancey Street, eleven minutes' walk from the diner. A man who had signed his name to a finding that his own photographs contradicted was either a fool, a coward, or a man who had been told what to find. Fools were rare in that job. Cowards could be reached. And men who had been told what to find often spent twenty years wanting someone to ask.

She would knock on his door today. She would bring the photographs. She would say the words *water damage pattern* out loud to the man who wrote *electrical fault*, and watch his face do whatever it was going to do.

She washed the single cup. She put on her coat. And as she descended the stairs into the onion-and-river smell of the waking diner, she understood that for the first time since the train had carried her north, she was not afraid of the day. Fear was still there—it would always be there, a lodger, not a guest—but it had been moved to the margins, where it belonged, where all the truest things get written.

Outside, the town was gray and quiet and keeping its secrets. She walked into it anyway, the envelope under her arm like a book she intended to return.
