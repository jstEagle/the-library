## 29. What Quentin Was Going to Say

Ada had carried the letter for twenty-three years, and in all that time she had never once told herself the truth about why.

She told herself it was an accident of filing. She was eleven, working the summer at the Meridian Library because her father believed children should learn the weight of other people's belongings, and a copy of *The Wind in the Willows* had come back through the night slot with something folded inside it, tucked into the gutter of page 114 where the river runs under the bridge. She'd meant to turn it in. She'd meant to put it in the lost-and-found box with the grocery lists and the pressed clover and the photograph of a dog that someone had written *Baxter, 1991* on the back in ballpoint. She had even picked up the box lid.

Then she'd seen the handwriting.

She knew that handwriting the way she knew the sound of her own front door—Quentin Coldharbour's letters, tall and narrow, leaning forward like they were trying to get somewhere faster than the sentence allowed. They were fourteen that summer, and Ada could have identified Quentin's hand from across a room, from across a river, from inside a burning building, though she would not think of buildings burning for another few weeks yet, and then she would think of nothing else for twenty years.

So: not an accident of filing. A theft. A small, deliberate theft committed by a girl who already understood that some things you keep because giving them back would mean admitting you'd read them, and reading them would mean admitting what you wanted, and wanting was the one thing Ada Reyes had been trained out of before she could walk. Her father sold other people's inheritances for a living. She had learned early that desire was a thing you handled with gloves.

She read the letter that night in her bedroom with the door closed and the lamp turned low, and when she finished she folded it back along its original creases and put it in the back of her copy of *A Wrinkle in Time*, and there it stayed through four moves, two funerals, one marriage that didn't take, and every year of the slow quiet ruin of Vellum Falls. She never reread it. That was the discipline. Once a decade, maybe, she would take it out and hold it without opening it, the way you hold a bird you've caught by accident, feeling its heart go against your palm, and then she would put it away again.

Now she took it out on a Tuesday evening in October, in the apartment above the diner, while Quentin Coldharbour sat at Marisol's kitchen table downstairs arguing timelines with Bess Okafor's paralegal over the phone, and Ada sat on the edge of her bed with the letter in both hands and her heart going like a bird's.

Dale Pruitt's statement had been filed three days ago. The hearing was set for the seventeenth. There were nine days left, and Ada had spent all of them doing arithmetic she hated: nine days until the deed hearing, some number of days after that until the county decided whether to renew the auction house contract, some number of days after that until the bank stopped being patient about the note on the building her grandfather had bought with money he'd earned honestly, which was more than anyone in this town could say about the Harrows. She had done the math so many times the numbers had worn smooth as river stones. And underneath the arithmetic, all week, one thought kept surfacing like a body in water:

If something happens—if Harrow does what Harrow does, if the last man who testified against a Harrow is not the last—Quentin will leave this town again, or be driven out of it, or worse, and Ada will still be holding a letter that was never hers to keep.

It was time to stop holding things.

* * *

She found Quentin on the reservoir steps.

Of course she did. Ada had known since the second day back that this was where Quentin went when the town got too loud, which in Vellum Falls meant any hour after ten p.m., when the mills' ghosts stopped rattling and the silence came up off the water like fog. Their old spot—the concrete steps that ran down to the intake basin behind the filtration shed, warm all summer, freezing all winter, the place where Quentin had taught her to read the river when they were nine and eleven. *You don't look at the water,* Quentin had said, twelve years old and certain, *you look at what the water is doing around things.* Around rocks, around pilings, around the legs of herons. The current always told you where the obstruction was. You just had to stop looking at the thing itself.

Ada had built an entire life on that lesson and only recently understood it applied to people.

Quentin was sitting on the third step with her coat buttoned wrong—one buttonhole off, so the whole line of her leaned crooked—and she didn't startle when Ada's footsteps sounded on the gravel above. She'd heard her coming. Quentin always heard her coming; it was one of the thousand small violences of knowing someone that well, that you could never arrive anywhere unannounced.

"I checked the diner," Ada said. "Marisol said you left half an hour ago."

"Couldn't sleep." Quentin didn't look around. "I'm cataloguing."

"The case?"

"Myself." A dry almost-laugh. "Accession numbers. Item one: fear of enclosed rooms, provenance unknown. Item two: inability to accept kindness, acquired circa 1998, condition poor."

Ada came down the steps and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, far enough that they didn't. The reservoir lay black and flat below them, holding the streetlights upside down, and somewhere out in the dark a loon made its one broken call.

"I brought you something," Ada said.

Her hands were shaking. She felt Quentin notice—felt it the way you feel weather change—and made herself open the bag anyway, and take out the book, and open it to the back cover, and slide the folded paper free.

Quentin looked at it for a long moment without touching it. In the light from the filtration shed she was pale, and older than forty-one, and younger than fourteen, all at once.

"What is it?" she said, and her voice had gone careful, the librarian's voice, the voice that handles rare things.

"It fell out of a book that came through the night slot. Summer we were fourteen." Ada held it out. "*The Wind in the Willows*. Page 114. I know exactly which page, because I've been avoiding rereading it for twenty-three years and apparently I memorized the location instead." She swallowed. "It's addressed to me, Quentin. It says *Ada* on the outside. I should have given it back. I didn't. I'm sorry. I'm giving it back now."

Quentin took the paper the way you take a diagnosis.

* * *

Later, Ada would try to reconstruct the order of events, and fail. Whether Quentin unfolded it slowly or all at once. Whether the loon called again or whether that was memory supplying sound where the silence needed filling. What she would remember precisely, with the terrible fidelity of the moments that reorganize a life, was this: the exact instant Quentin recognized her own handwriting, and the small involuntary sound she made—not a word, not a sob, something pre-verbal, a sound from before language, from the place where language gets lost—and the way her thumb moved once across the fold, back and forth, like smoothing a sheet over a face.

"Read it," Ada said quietly. "Out loud, if you want. Or don't. It's yours. It was always yours. I just—" Her voice cracked and she let it. "I couldn't give it back then. I was a coward at eleven and I was a coward at thirty-four and I'm trying very hard not to be one tonight."

Quentin read it silently first. Ada watched her lips move faintly, watched her eyes travel down lines she herself had never dared reread, and waited in the particular agony of a person watching someone else read their own past.

Then, without deciding to—Ada could see that, see the decision happen and get overridden—Quentin began to read aloud.

"*Dear Ada,*" she read. Her voice was steady for exactly one sentence. "*You were at the counter today and I practiced saying something to you the whole walk home and then I didn't say it, same as always. So I am saying it here, where saying things doesn't count.*

"*Tobias says I talk to books more than people and he's right but he's twelve so he thinks everything is funny. He asked me yesterday why I write letters to you if I never send them and I told him it's practice. He said practice for what. I said I don't know yet. But I do know. I want to be someone who speaks. Not like my mother who speaks to fill rooms, and not like Dad who speaks only when it's safe. Like the people in books who say the true thing at the bad moment. I want to be the person who says the true thing at the bad moment.*

"*Here is the true thing at this moment: when you laugh, the whole river looks different. When you went home early last Tuesday I sat on these steps until dark and it was just water. Same water. Different river.*

"*Tobias is going to be okay, I think. He's sleeping better. He saw something at the Harrow place—he won't tell me what, he says it's 'grown-up business,' which means it scared him—but he's been drawing again, boats mostly, and he laughed at dinner twice this week. Mom counted. I'm writing it down so somebody remembers that he laughed, in case nobody writes anything else down.*

"*If you're reading this, which you are not, because I am going to burn it in the sink like all the others—*

"*No. I'm not going to burn this one. This one I'm leaving in the book on purpose, which is the bravest thing I have ever done, and tomorrow I will tell you it's there, and you will either say something back or you won't, and either way I will finally be a person who speaks.*

"*Yours,
Q.*"

The last word hung over the black water.

Ada realized she was crying only because she felt Quentin's hand find hers in the dark—cold fingers, gripping hard, an anchor-line thrown—and realized that Quentin was crying too, really crying, the kind that has no manners, the kind that takes the whole body. Twenty years of catalogued silences coming due all at once, interest compounded nightly. Her shoulders shook. The letter trembled in her grip and she held it away from her face to keep it dry, some archivist's reflex surviving even this, protecting the document even as the person who wrote it came apart.

"You left it on purpose," Ada said, when she could speak. "You were going to tell me."

"I was going to tell you." Quentin's voice was wrecked and she didn't try to fix it. "Three days later the house burned down and Tobias—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "And I decided the letter was a lie. That girl who wrote it, the one who was going to say the true thing at the bad moment—I decided she died in the fire too, and I spent twenty years curating her estate." A breath that shuddered going in. "I never burned it. I never sent it. I just—filed it. In a book. Where you'd find it. God. Even at fourteen I couldn't destroy anything, I could only shelve it and hope."

"I found it the next morning," Ada said. "Before the fire. I read it the night before your house—" She couldn't finish either. The words *burned down* sat between them like a third person on the step. "I read it the night before, Quentin. I had all night to answer it. I was going to. I had it planned, what I'd say. And then the sirens started, and by noon you were a girl whose brother died, and there was no possible sentence I could put next to that. So I kept it. And keeping it became not telling you I kept it, and not telling you became twenty years, and—" She wiped her face with her sleeve, furious at the uselessness of the gesture. "I have spent my whole life being the person who doesn't say the true thing at the bad moment. Your letter is the reason I know that's what I am. I've been carrying my own indictment around in a paperback since I was eleven years old."

They sat with that. The loon called again, closer, or farther; distance means nothing on water after dark.

"Tobias laughed twice that week," Quentin said at last, very quietly. "My mother counted. I'd forgotten anyone counted." She looked down at the letter, at the line about the drawings, the boats, the *something at the Harrow place*, and Ada watched the grief in her face change temperature—watched sorrow become something harder and more useful. "He told me himself, in his own way. He told me in a notebook I found in his room, and I read it aloud in a hearing room last week, and Aldous Harrow walked out before I finished." She folded the letter along its old creases, gently, and then—this was the part Ada would keep forever, the image she'd carry past all the rest—she did not put it in her pocket. She handed it back.

"Keep it," Quentin said.

Ada stared. "Quentin—"

"You kept it safe for twenty-three years. Through four moves and a marriage and whatever my father's funeral was. That's not theft anymore, that's custody." The ghost of the old dry humor moved through her voice and was gone. "Besides. It's not mine either. It's ours. It's the only document in this whole case that isn't about what was taken from us. It's about what we were going to say and didn't." She looked out at the reservoir, at the inverted lights trembling on the black water. "I used to think I lost my voice in that fire. I've been telling myself that story for twenty years—that the smoke took it, that grief took it. But it's right here. I had it. I was going to use it. I just never did." She turned and looked at Ada directly, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "Until now. Now I can't shut up. Depositions, petitions, newspaper interviews. Bess says I should pace myself."

Ada laughed—a wet, broken laugh, but real. "You were insufferable at fourteen. You were going to be insufferable at forty. Good. Be insufferable."

"I intend to be."

For a while neither of them moved. Then Quentin reached over and took Ada's hand again, deliberately this time, no anchor about it, and laced their fingers together on the cold concrete between them, and neither of them said anything about it, because some things, finally, did not need to be said to count.

"Nine days," Ada said eventually.

"Nine days."

"After the hearing, whatever happens—you'll stay? In town? At least through—" She couldn't name what came after, because she didn't know what came after, because no one in Vellum Falls had ever gotten this far before.

"I'm staying," Quentin said simply, and it landed in Ada's chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling and not reaching bottom.

They sat until the cold drove them up the steps, and Ada walked Quentin as far as Halloway Street, and they parted at the corner where the boarded house stood pale under the streetlight, its windows dark, its future undecided, and Quentin touched the letter once through Ada's coat pocket—*keep it safe*—and went inside Marisol's, and Ada stood alone on the corner for a long moment, feeling the paper against her hip like a heartbeat.

She was halfway home when she noticed the car.

It had been parked near the filtration shed when they arrived—she'd registered it without registering it, a sedan, dark, engine ticking as it cooled. It was gone now. Cars moved at night; towns had people in them. She told herself that all the way up Water Street, and she almost believed it, right up until she passed the diner and saw, taped to the inside of the dark front window where the morning light would find it first, a single sheet of white paper, folded once, with ADA printed on the outside in block capitals that belonged to no one's hand—or everyone's, or one hand pretending to be no one.

She stood on the sidewalk a long time before she went and took it down.
