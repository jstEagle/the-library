## 3. What the Quarry Keeps

The hospital called at seven in the morning to say that Emeric Voss had begun calling for his daughter.

Not by name. The nurse on the line said it carefully, the way people handle broken glass: *he keeps saying a word we don't understand, over and over, like it's a person's name.* Rosalind knew before she heard it what the word would be. She had known since the lessons began, since the first evening in his salt-stained kitchen when he'd set two cups of tea down and said, in Undertow, *we begin where everything begins — with water.* The word was *marenne*, and it did not mean Maren. It meant the place where a tide turns and hesitates, the held breath between ebb and flood. He had named his daughter for the pause in the sea, and now he was calling for the pause itself, for the moment that had never come back to him.

She told the nurse she would come at noon. Then she sat in her kitchen with her hands flat on the table until the wood stopped trembling under them, and she understood that noon would be too late — not too late for Emeric, who might live another week or another hour behind his curtain of forgetting, but too late for her. She could not bring him comfort yet. She had to bring him truth first, and truth was kept in a house above a quarry, and she had not gone back there in thirty years.

* * *

The road out of August Hollow climbed through gorse and bracken, and the sea fell away below it in long silver shelves. Rosalind walked. She had thought about taking the bus and then about driving and had done neither; some distances should be paid for in the body. Her tool bag hung on her shoulder out of habit more than need — she had carried it every working day for three decades, and today it felt less like a trade than like an alibi. A woman with a tuning bag is a woman with somewhere ordinary to be.

The Voss quarry house stood where the lane ended, grey stone going green at the joints, its windows blind with salt-film. Below it, the flooded quarry had been drained two summers ago by the county — a safety order, a pump, a slow revelation of mud and rusted machinery and, at the bottom, nothing anyone had been looking for. The water that had filled it for thirty years was simply gone, and the pit lay open like a throat after speech.

Rosalind stood at the gate and let the house look at her.

Every house looks, if you know how to be looked at. She had learned that as a girl in this very doorway, learning from Emeric's wife — no, not learned; absorbed, the way children absorb weather — that a building is an instrument that never stops playing. Pipes carry its voice. Floorboards hold its memory in their grain. And a house where something terrible happened does not fall silent afterward. It repeats. It repeats the way a crack in a bell repeats, into every note, forever.

She put her hand on the gate latch. It gave with a sound like a swallowed word.

Inside, the air was cold and mineral. Dust lay over the hall floor in a fine grey nap, unbroken except by the tracks of gulls through a broken pane upstairs. She did not turn on lights. She didn't need them. She closed her eyes instead, the way she did before touching a piano, and listened.

The house groaned. Not the small complaints of settling timber — something deeper, slower, a sound that came up through the foundations from the drained pit below, the rock remembering pressure it no longer had. Water still moved somewhere in the old pipes, trapped in low bends, shifting with her footsteps. And beneath all of it, faint as breath, the house kept its rhythm: a creak-and-hold, creak-and-hold, from the landing above. The sound of a door swinging very slightly on its hinge, pushed by a draft that had been walking the same corridor for thirty years.

She followed it up.

The landing. Maren's room on the left, hers on the right, the bathroom between them with its claw-foot tub and its window over the quarry. She had been fourteen that summer, Maren sixteen, both of them half-wild with the freedom of the empty house while Emeric worked the fish market and his wife lay upstairs with the illness that would take her that autumn. They had spoken Undertow together in secret, the two girls, trading words like stolen sweets — *skell* for the shine on wet stone, *harrowen* for a wind that comes off land toward sea. Maren had been fluent the way her father was fluent, without effort, without thinking, and Rosalind had been fluent too, though she had spent thirty years pretending otherwise, letting the language go dormant in her mouth like a coin buried in a garden.

The bathroom door stood open. The draft moved through it, and the hinge sang its one note, and Rosalind stepped inside and finally let herself remember the whole night instead of the piece of it she had carried like a stone in her chest for three decades.

They had run the bath. That was the beginning everyone knew — Maren found in the flooded quarry at dawn, and the bath running over, and the town's quick arithmetic: a quarrel, a girl's spite, a drowned daughter. Emeric had never accused her. That was the unbearable part. He had only looked at her across the funeral kitchen with a face like a shut door and said, in Undertow, so no one else could hear, *I know what you did.* And she had not denied it, because she believed it herself. Because guilt is a language too, and she had spoken it fluently ever since.

But houses keep better records than grief does, and the house was telling her now.

She knelt at the tub and laid her palm against the cold enamel, and read it the way she read radiators. The overflow pipe still bore the mineral scar of decades of standing water — but the trap beneath the tub held a different story, a dryness, a gap in the scale, a section of pipe that had once run hot and then, abruptly, had not. She moved to the pipes along the wall, pressing her ear to them as she had pressed it to a thousand radiators in a thousand rooms, and the house spoke its sequence: bath drawn, taps open, and then — here, in the corrosion pattern, in the way the joint had been wrenched and reseated — the cold tap seized. Stuck open past its stop. A fault in the old fitting, the kind that gives no warning, that runs a bath over in minutes and floods a floor and sends two girls scrambling down the outside stair to shut off the mains at the wellhead by the quarry edge.

That was the part she had forgotten, or buried: they had both gone down. Wet night, dark, the path slick. She had reached the valve first. She had turned it, and turned to call to Maren that it was done — and the path's edge had given no cry, made no sound, offered no warning. The quarry had simply taken her, the way quarries do, silently, the way the tide pauses and does not come back.

And Rosalind, fourteen, alone in the dark with the valve in her hands, had run back up and found the bath overflowing and understood — wrongly, completely, permanently — that she had left the tap. That the water was hers. That Maren was hers.

She had never corrected the record. Not because she wanted Emeric's blame, but because correcting it would have meant saying aloud, *I was there, I saw her go, I did nothing, I ran* — and that confession was worse than guilt. Guilt could be carried. Witness had to be spoken.

So she had let him believe the smaller crime, and he had let her believe it too, each of them protecting the other from the true sentence, and the language they shared had gone silent between them for thirty years because it was the only tongue in which the lie could be undone.

Rosalind knelt on the dusty landing floor of the quarry house and wept the way the pipes dripped — slowly, without drama, from somewhere structural.

* * *

The hospital room smelled of antiseptic and cut flowers going over. Emeric lay small against the pillows, his hands — those precise, powerful hands that had tuned bells and boats and once, gently, a frightened girl's shoulders — quiet on the blanket. His eyes found her when she came in, and for a moment they were clear, and he said, in English, "You've been to the house."

"Yes."

"Did it tell you?"

"It told me everything." She sat down close, took his hand. "It wasn't the tap, Emeric. The tap seized. We went down together to the mains. I turned the water off. I turned around and you weren't—" Her voice failed, and she rebuilt it in the other language, the one only two living people could speak, the one built for tides and turning points. *I was there. I saw. I said nothing for thirty years because the truth was heavier than the lie.*

He was quiet a long time. His thumb moved over her knuckles, once, twice, finding the rhythm of it.

Then he answered in Undertow, and his answer was not absolution, which is a word handed down, but a lesson, which is a word handed over. *You do not say the word for stay,* he said, *by holding on. You say it by opening your hand and speaking it to someone. Say it now.*

"I'm staying," she said, in Undertow, and the word filled the little room like water filling a bell.

His eyes were drifting already, the tide going out of them, but he smiled. "Good," he said, in English, in the accent of the market and the boats. "Because next lesson—"

He didn't finish it. He slept. And Rosalind sat holding his hand as the monitors kept their patient rhythm, and understood that whatever came next, she would have to teach it to herself — and then, someday soon, to someone who hadn't been born when the quarry took Maren Voss, someone standing barefoot on the sand at August Hollow, waiting to be given a word.
