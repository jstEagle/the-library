## 19. What June Never Sent

The door opened wider on the ninth day than it had on the fourth, and by the twelfth it wasn't a door at all anymore — it was just a thing between us that June had stopped pretending mattered. She sat in the chair by the window with a blanket over her knees even though it was July, and she said, "Sit down, girl, before you wear a groove in my floorboards," and that was how it began. Not with an apology. Not with an explanation. With an old woman telling another woman where to put her body, as if I had always belonged in that room and we had both simply been waiting out a long delay.

She called me girl. Not Vesper. Not *her daughter*. Girl, the way you'd address someone you'd been expecting since before they were born.

"I know what you want," she said. "You've got that box in your car, or you've got it memorized, one of the two. You want me to fill in the blanks." She looked out the window at the water, which she did often, the way other people check a watch. "But blanks are comfortable. Blanks let you make up whatever version hurts least. So I'll tell it plain, and you can decide afterward what it was worth."

I sat. I had learned by then that June did not perform. Everything she said arrived already true, the way weather arrives.

* * *

"It was a Tuesday," she said. "Market day. Your mother and I ran the stall together eleven years by then — flowers in spring, tomatoes in August, chrysanthemums in the fall when everything else was dying and people still wanted something bright on their tables. We were good at it. Not just the selling. The *being*. Two women behind a stall learn each other the way sailors learn a coastline. Every rock. Every place the water goes shallow.

"You were four. Maybe four and a half. You had this coat — red, with a hood, and you refused to wear the hood up, so your mother was forever chasing you with it. That morning you'd appointed yourself my assistant. You had a little wooden crate you'd stand on so you could reach the counter, and you'd hand people their change with both hands, so solemn about it, like you were performing surgery." June's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You handed me a petal once, torn off a dahlia, and told me it was a present. I still have it. Pressed in a book. Don't ask me which book. I've moved it through four houses and I've never once told anyone why."

I didn't ask. My hands were in my lap and I was holding very still, the way you hold still when a bird has landed near you.

"Your father came at eleven. He'd never come to the market. Not once in eleven years. He thought the stall was beneath him — a hobby, an embarrassment, *playing shop*, he called it. So when I saw him walking down the row of stalls with his coat on like it was a courtroom, I knew. You know that kind of knowing? The body knows before the mind agrees to. My hands went cold around a bunch of sweet peas.

"He didn't shout. That's the thing people get wrong about men like him. He didn't need to shout. He walked up, and he said your mother's name the way you'd read a charge sheet, and he said, 'We're leaving. Now.' And she said, 'Not here, Gerald,' and he said, 'Yes. Here. So you understand there's no scene to make. There's no audience to appeal to.' He'd learned that about her — that she was brave with people watching and went quiet when it was private. He'd spent years making sure everything important happened in private.

"And then he picked you up. Off the crate. Just — picked you up, mid-transaction, mid-change-giving, you were holding somebody's forty cents, and he lifted you like luggage and said, 'Say goodbye to the lady.'

"The *lady*." June's voice caught on it, thirty years of catching. "Eleven years, and to him I was the lady at the stall. He knew exactly what we were. Don't ever let anyone tell you he didn't know. Men like him always know. That's the whole architecture of it — you can't forbid a thing you don't believe in.

"Your mother reached for you and he stepped back, and he said — and I have carried this sentence like a stone in my shoe for thirty years — he said, 'One more letter, one more visit, one more *anything*, and I take her for good. And you know I can. You know what people see when they look at a woman like you and a woman like me.' And then he walked down the row with you on his hip, and you were looking back at us over his shoulder, and you weren't crying. You were confused. You held out your little hand with the forty cents still in it, all the way down the row, like you thought the transaction was still open."

June stopped. She pressed her thumb into the blanket, into a fold, over and over, a small tide.

"Your mother didn't chase him. That's what I couldn't forgive for a long time — not him, *her*. She stood there. She stood there and her face did that thing it did, that going smooth, that closing, like a shop pulling down its shutter. And I stood there too. And the customer with the forty cents' worth of tomatoes stood there. And the market went on around us, because markets do. And I thought: she'll come back tomorrow. She'll come back and we'll fix this, we'll get a lawyer, we'll — I don't know what. We were twenty-nine and thirty and we thought the world was a place where things could be fixed if you loved them hard enough.

"She came back the next Tuesday. Alone. She sold flowers. She smiled at customers. She never once looked down the row toward the parking lot, where she'd have seen him if he came, which he never did, because he'd already won. And at the end of the day she said, 'I can't do this anymore, June. If I keep any of it, I lose her. He'll take her and he'll make sure I never find her. He told me what he'd tell the judge. He told me what he'd tell my parents. He's already told them.'

"And I said, 'So that's it. He says a word and we disappear?' And she said the worst thing she ever said to me. She said, 'You'll survive this. She might not.'

"Girl, I want you to understand something, because I've had a long time to get it right. She wasn't choosing him. Everyone in that story — me, the town, maybe even you — thought she was choosing him, choosing the marriage, choosing respectability. She wasn't. She was choosing *you*. She did the arithmetic that mothers do, the ugly arithmetic where you count yourself as nothing, and she paid. She paid with me. She paid with the stall and the dock and the photograph and every Tuesday for the rest of her life. And then she had to live in a house with the man who'd made her pay, and smile, and be his wife, and raise you, and never say one word about the price. That's not cowardice. That's a thirty-year sentence served without appeal." She looked at me then, directly, her eyes wet and furious on my mother's behalf. "You're allowed to be angry at her. But aim it at the right things. She wasn't silent because she had nothing to say. She was silent because saying it cost your face."

I said nothing. There was nothing in me yet that could come out as language. But she nodded as if I'd answered, and went on.

* * *

"I wrote to her," June said. "Of course I wrote. I wrote that same week — four pages, both sides, the ink smeared where I — well. It doesn't matter where. I wrote that I understood, that I'd wait, that we could be careful, that people had been careful for centuries and gotten away with their whole lives. I wrote that I loved her, which I had never once said out loud, because we were careful even with each other, careful in the way that means you think saying a thing might break it. I put it all in a letter because letters can't be interrupted. And I walked to her house at night — this was two towns over then, before she moved here — and everyone was asleep, and I slid it under the door.

"Under the door. Like a confession. Like something shameful." Her hand flattened on the blanket. "I've thought about that for thirty years. Why under the door. Why not hand it to her, why not ring the bell, why not stand in the yard until she came out. Because under the door was deniable. Under the door, she could pretend she never got it, and I could pretend I believed her, and we could both keep the story where it didn't hurt. I was a coward in an envelope, Vesper. I've made my peace with it, but I've never made it *true*, if you understand the difference.

"And she never answered.

"I waited a month. Two. I wrote again — shorter, that one, just: *Tell me you got it. Tell me to stop. Tell me anything.* Nothing. I wrote a third time and I got nothing, and by then your father had — well, you'll know this part, or you'll learn it — he'd been collecting her mail. Intercepting it. Every letter I wrote went into his hands, and every letter she wrote went into the same drawer, and the two of us sat at either end of that drawer for thirty years, each believing the other had chosen the silence."

She said it without drama. That was the terrible part. She said it the way you'd report a train schedule — the fact of it, flat, finished — and it was worse than any weeping could have been, because it meant she had said it to herself so many times it had worn smooth.

"I stopped writing in '94," she said. "Not because I stopped — you don't stop, that's the joke, nobody ever stops — but because I understood the answer. Silence *is* an answer. It's the answer people give when the true one is unaffordable. So I built a life. That's what you do. You build a life the way you build a seawall — not because it'll hold, but because standing still is worse.

"I taught school. Thirty-one years, fourth grade. I was good at it. Children that age are honest in a way adults find alarming, and I found it restful. I had a friend named Dorothy, and we traveled — Portugal, twice, and once a terrible trip to Arizona where the car broke down and we laughed so hard we cried, and I thought, *this is happiness, this is the thing, this counts.* And it did count. I want that on the record. The life I built counted. It wasn't the life. It wasn't Tuesdays at the stall with her hands next to mine and you handing me petals like they were the crown jewels. But it was a life, and I lived it awake, and I never once pretended it was enough, and I never once let it be nothing.

"I kept the coat." She nodded toward the closet, and I knew — I had found it in the stall, wrapped in tissue, thirty years folded. "Her coat. She left it on the stall hook that last Tuesday and never came back for it, and I couldn't — I couldn't sell it, couldn't wear it, couldn't throw it away. So when I cleared the stall out, I took it home. And then when I moved, it went into a box, and the box went to your mother's cellar, because — " and here June laughed, a short, cracked, astonished sound, "because she asked. Years later. She came to me once, just once, in 1998, in the market parking lot, and she didn't touch me and I didn't touch her, and she said, 'I have things of yours. Can I keep them somewhere?' And I said, 'You've been keeping them somewhere for eleven years, Beatrix. What's one more cellar?' And she cried, right there between two parked trucks, without any sound at all, and I stood there like a fence post, and we said goodbye, and that was the last time I saw her until your father died and she sent Hal with a note that said one word: *Soon.* And I waited for soon, and soon never came, because soon was thirty years long and neither of us was brave enough to walk to the other end of it."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs a clock did its patient work.

* * *

"I have something for you," June said, after a while.

She made me fetch it myself — she directed me to the bottom drawer of the dresser, under the sweaters, a flat tin, biscuit tin, rusted at the corners. Inside were letters. My mother's handwriting. Dozens of them, and my hands shook so badly I could hardly fan them out.

"She wrote to me," I said. Stupidly. As if the evidence required narration.

"Every year," June said. "Birthday, mostly. Sometimes just because the light did something and she thought of me. And I never opened a single one after 1994, because I'd decided what they were. Regret. Apology. *I'm sorry, but.* And I couldn't bear the *but*. So they went in the tin, and the tin went in the drawer, and I told myself I'd read them when I was old." She looked at her own hands, old as driftwood. "Well. Here we are.

"You read them. I can't. My eyes are bad and my courage is worse, and anyway they were never written to me, not really. They were written to the woman I was at thirty. That woman's gone. But you — " she pointed at me, and her finger trembled, and she let it — "you're made of her. Every letter in that tin is addressed to somebody who doesn't exist anymore, and you're the closest thing left. Read them. Read them out loud if you can stand it, because my ears work fine, and I have been deaf to her voice for thirty years and I would like to hear it before I go, even secondhand, even in yours."

I looked at the tin. At the top letter, unopened, the flap still gummed and yellowed, dated — I turned it over — three weeks before my mother died.

"June," I said. "There are nineteen letters here. And in the box at home, there are nineteen to you that she never sent. The last ones. She wrote them at the end. She was dying and she was writing to you and she couldn't — she didn't know where — "

"I know," June said softly. "Hal told me you had them. He's been carrying messages between us for thirty years, that man, and every one of them arrived too late or too early, and he never once got the timing right, because there was no right timing. There was only the drawer." She settled back into the chair, and closed her eyes, and said, "Start with the oldest. And don't skip the parts where she's angry at me. I've earned the angry parts."

I opened the tin all the way. The letters breathed out — paper and dust and, faintly, impossibly, the ghost of chrysanthemums.

And the first one, dated 1975, began: *June. I am going to say this in ink because ink can't be interrupted.*
