## 20. The Last Letter

The hospital smelled the way hospitals always smell, which is to say like a place where time has been replaced by a schedule. Vesper signed in at the desk at four in the afternoon on a Tuesday, and the woman behind the glass asked if she was family, and Vesper said yes before she understood that she was answering a different question than the one being asked.

Room 214. She had been there three times since the fourth visit, since the door opened an inch and then, on the fifth visit, all the way. She had read June nine letters so far, one per visit, sometimes two when June's voice was strong enough to say *again* and mean it. June listened the way people listen to weather reports for a coastline they love — attentively, without hope of changing anything, hungry for every detail.

But today the nurse met Vesper in the corridor before she reached the room.

"You should know she's tired today," the nurse said. "She had a rough night. She might not have much to give you."

"I'm not here to take," Vesper said, and heard how it sounded, and added, "I mean — I just read. That's all I do. I read and she listens."

The nurse studied her for a moment. "You're the one with the letters."

"I'm the one with the letters."

"She asks about you when you're not here." The nurse said it lightly, already moving, already busy, the way hospital staff say true things so they don't have to watch them land. "She calls you *the courier*. She thinks it's funny."

Vesper stood in the corridor for a moment after the nurse left, one hand on the wall, letting that settle. *The courier.* Her mother had written letters for thirty years and never sent a single one, and now Vesper was the postal system, the whole apparatus, the stamp and the truck and the tired man on the route. She thought: *I should have been this all along. We all should have been each other's couriers.* Then she thought: *That's not how silence works. Silence works by making you believe the letter is the dangerous thing, when really it's the not-sending.*

She went in.

* * *

June was smaller than she'd been a week ago. That was the thing about dying that nobody warned you about in advance — it happened in measurements. A centimeter of height. A spoonful less at every meal. The hands on the blanket were still elegant, still a painter's hands, the knuckles swollen but the fingers long and precise, and Vesper found herself looking at them and thinking about the paintings in the market stall, the harbor scenes, the woman in the red coat seen from behind at the end of a pier.

June's eyes opened when the door moved. It took her a moment to focus, and Vesper watched her do the arithmetic of waking — where, who, what day — and then watched the arithmetic resolve into something warm.

"The courier," June said. Her voice was sand and honey, worn thin but still sweet underneath. "Come to make a delivery?"

"Number ten," Vesper said. She held up the letter. She had taken to bringing only the one she intended to read, so June couldn't see the stack and calculate how many remained. It was a kindness Vesper wasn't sure was kind. "Unless you're too tired."

"I've been tired since 1994." June's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Sit. Read."

But when Vesper reached for the envelope, June's hand came up — slow, deliberate — and closed over Vesper's wrist.

"Wait." Her grip was light as a bird's but she did not let go. "Before you read it. Tell me something first."

"Anything."

"Tell me what you're going to do with the house."

Vesper blinked. "The house?"

"The house on Larch Street. The one with the wardrobe." June's eyes were clear and fixed on her, and Vesper understood that this was not a casual question, that June had been assembling it across nine visits the way she'd once assembled a market stall at dawn — piece by piece, in the dark, before anyone else was awake to see. "You've been reading her letters for weeks. You know what she was. You know what she kept. And in three weeks a man with a clipboard is going to decide whether that house gets sold to someone who'll knock it down for the view. So tell me. What are you going to do?"

"I told the lawyer to decline the offer," Vesper said. It came out before she had decided to say it, and she realized, saying it, that it was true — that somewhere between the stall and the hospital corridor she had already decided, the way you decide to keep breathing. "I'm keeping it. I'm — I don't know yet what I'm doing with it. But I'm keeping it."

June's hand tightened, just barely, on her wrist. "Good," she said. "Good girl." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "She'd have hated the clipboard man. She'd have stood in the yard with her arms crossed until he left. She was very good at standing in yards."

"I know," Vesper said. "I've seen the photographs."

"You've seen the photographs." June released her wrist and lay back against the pillows, and for a moment her eyes went somewhere else entirely — to a dock, maybe, in 1987, to a laughing woman and a child between them. Then she came back. "All right. Read me number ten."

* * *

Vesper opened the envelope. The paper inside was thinner than the others — her mother had been economizing toward the end of the letters' middle period, Vesper had learned, using whatever was in the house — and the handwriting was smaller, more cramped, as though the words had been written in a space too narrow for them.

She had learned to read her mother's handwriting the way you learn a new town: slowly at first, getting lost, then all at once, knowing exactly where everything is.

"June," she read aloud. "I saw a doctor today. I didn't want to write this letter, so I'm going to write it quickly, before the wanting-not-to wins."

June made a small sound. Vesper looked up. "Keep going," June said. "Quickly. Before the wanting-not-to wins."

"*The doctor used the word that doctors use when they mean soon. I'm not going to write the word down because writing it down makes it furniture. It would sit in the room with us. I'd rather it stay outside, in the yard, where I'm good at standing.*"

June laughed — a real laugh, cracked and short and true — and then pressed her hand flat against her sternum as if to hold something in place.

"*I have written you a hundred letters and sent none of them, and I know why, and the why is a small man with a small threat, and I have hated myself for thirty years in a very orderly fashion, one drawer at a time. But I want to say the thing I never said plainly, because I am running out of time to say things plainly, and plainness is the only currency worth anything now.*"

Vesper's voice caught. She stopped, breathed, kept going. In the bed, June had gone very still.

"*I loved you. I love you. Not in the past tense, not in the coded way, not in the way we said it sideways at the stall when the customers were close enough to hear. I loved you in the plain way. You were the true thing. Everything else was the arrangement.*"

"*I chose my daughter over you, and I would choose her again, and I have never once stopped grieving the choosing. Both things are true and I refuse, at the end, to pretend they cancel out. They don't cancel. They just sit together in the same room like two people who used to be married.*"

June was crying now, the way Vesper had learned she cried — without sound, the tears moving down her face in a straight, unhurried line, as though they had made this trip many times and knew the route. Vesper kept reading. Her own vision blurred and she let it, reading through the water, trusting the handwriting to hold her up.

"*I am dying the way I lived, which is to say quietly, and I am sorry for that, and I am not sorry for that, and I know you'll understand the contradiction because you were the only one who ever did.*"

"*One last thing. My daughter will find these letters. I don't know how or when — I only know that I hid them where I hid the things I couldn't bear to look at and couldn't bear to lose, and she is the kind of person who checks for water damage. I know her. I watched her for eighteen years from behind my own silence, and I know her.*"

"*If you are reading this to her, June — if she found you, if she is sitting where you are sitting — then she finished what I couldn't start. I hoped for it the way you hope for weather. I hoped without expecting.*"

Vesper stopped. She couldn't do it. The last lines swam on the page, and June was watching her, and the room was very quiet except for the measured breathing of the machines.

"Read it," June said softly. "She wrote it for both of us. Read it."

"*If you are reading this, Vesper —*"

Her own name in her mother's handwriting. Across thirty years, across the whole width of the silence, her name, spelled out in the cramped late-period hand, in a letter that was never sent, that was written to a woman she'd never heard of, that had waited behind a wardrobe for her the way her mother had waited her whole life for someone to check.

"*— I don't know what you believe about me, and I don't have time left to find out. But I want you to know that the silence was not the absence of love. It was love with nowhere to go. I hope you do better. I think you will. You have her hands, and she was never afraid of anything.*"

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. At the bottom of the page, in the corner, her mother had drawn a small, precise sketch — a market stall, awning, crates, a woman's silhouette behind it — no bigger than a thumbnail, rendered in three lines of ink.

Vesper folded the letter along its original creases. Her hands were shaking and the creases wouldn't cooperate and she gave up and just held it against her chest for a moment, the whole thirty years of it, the whole weight and lightness of it.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The machines breathed. Outside the window, the light was going the color of the harbor in June's paintings, that particular late-afternoon copper that Vesper had seen a hundred times in the stall and never once in her mother's house.

"She drew the stall," June said finally. Her voice was wrecked and she didn't try to fix it. "At the bottom of her letters. Did she do that in all of them?"

"In most of them," Vesper said. "I thought it was a signature."

"It was." June turned her head on the pillow. "The first day we worked together, I asked her why she'd moved here, of all places, and she said — this is the truth, this is the whole truth — she said, *I followed a rumor of a harbor.* And I said, what rumor? And she said, *the rumor that a person could start over somewhere the water is louder than the past.*" June's eyes closed. "The stall was the loudest place I ever lived."

"I know," Vesper said. "I've been in it. I have your coat. It's hanging in my mother's — in *my* — in the hall closet. I couldn't figure out why she kept it. Now I can't figure out how she stood it."

"She stood it the way you stand anything," June said. "Badly, and then anyway."

Vesper laughed, and it came out wet, and June smiled at her with the whole ruined tenderness of her face, and for a moment Vesper saw it — what Hal had seen, what the town had seen, what the child in the 1987 photograph had been standing inside of without knowing: the two of them, the true thing, the arrangement around it. Her mother's laugh in the photograph was the laugh of a person standing next to the loudest place she ever lived.

"June," she said. "The letter said she hoped I'd finish what she couldn't start. What does that mean? What was she starting?"

June was quiet for a while. The machines counted something for her, patiently, the way they had been counting for days.

"You," she said at last. "She was starting you. And then she stopped, because stopping was the price, and she paid it, and she never told you the bill existed." June's hand found Vesper's on the blanket — those painter's hands, cold now, precise still. "But that's not what she meant in the letter. In the letter she meant the letters. She meant: someone should have said all this out loud, years ago, to your face, in plain words. She couldn't. She hoped you could." Her grip tightened, feather-light and immovable. "So. Can you?"

"Can I what?"

"Say something plain." June's eyes were open again, fixed on her, and there was something in them Vesper hadn't seen before — not hope exactly, hope was too passive a word for it. Demand. The demand of a woman who had waited thirty years for a letter and was not going to wait one more minute for the thing she could still get. "Not hers. Yours. I've had her words all week. I want yours. While I still — " She stopped. Started again, plainer. "I'm running out of time to hear things plainly. So say one."

The room hummed. Vesper sat with it — the demand, the kindness of it, the terrible mercy of a dying woman refusing to let you hide behind a dead woman's handwriting.

And Vesper, who had made silence her native language, who had smiled her way out of every room that asked too much of her, who had cataloged a stranger's house and her own evasions room by room — Vesper opened her mouth, and found, to her astonishment, that the words were already there. They had been there for weeks, piling up in the cavity behind the wardrobe of her, waiting for someone to move the furniture.

"I'm angry at her," she said. "I was. For thirty years I thought she didn't want me, and it turns out she was paying for me, and nobody told me the price, and I'm still angry, but it's a different anger now, it's an anger with an address." Her voice was shaking and she let it shake. "And I'm glad she loved you. I'm glad there was one true thing. And I'm sorry — I'm so sorry — that it took all of us dying a little to say any of this out loud. And I kept the coat. And I'm keeping the house. And I would like — " she stopped, and started again, plainer, "I would like to come back tomorrow. And the day after. For as long as there are days."

June looked at her for a long moment. Then she lifted their joined hands — with effort, with the effort of a woman moving a mountain one inch — and pressed the back of Vesper's hand to her cheek, and held it there.

"There she is," June said. "There's her daughter."

* * *

Vesper stayed until the night nurse came in with the pill cup and the gentle, practiced way of ending visits. At the door she turned back. June's eyes were closed but her mouth was moving slightly, and Vesper realized she was saying something to herself, over and over, too quiet to be meant for anyone.

She leaned in.

"Plainly," June was murmuring. "Plainly, plainly."

In the corridor, Vesper took out her phone and called the lawyer, because it was after hours and he would not answer, and she left a message anyway, her voice low and steady in the fluorescent hum: "It's Vesper. I need you to draw up some papers. Not for the sale." She looked back through the little window in the door, at the small shape in the bed, at the hands folded on the blanket like a woman resting between brushstrokes. "I need you to tell me how someone adds a name to a will. Mine's changing."

She hung up and stood a moment in the corridor of the building where time had been replaced by a schedule, and understood that her time had just been given back to her — all of it, every unspent day — with a single instruction attached, written thirty years ago in a cramped and hurried hand:

*Run out of courage? No. Run out of time. Don't.*

She had eight letters left in the box.

She intended to read every one of them aloud.
