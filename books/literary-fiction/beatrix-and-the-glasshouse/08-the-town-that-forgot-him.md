## 8. The Town That Forgot Him

The train did not go all the way to the valley anymore. It stopped at a station that had been renamed — Yara could see the old name still ghosting beneath the new one on the enamel sign, VALLBRIDGE painted over something longer, something with more syllables and less optimism — and from there he had to take a bus that smelled of diesel and wet wool, driven by a young man who chewed gum with the metronomic patience of someone who had never once been late or early for anything.

Yara sat at the back with his bag on his knees and watched the valley assemble itself out of the window like a photograph developing in reverse. First the hedgerows, then the river, silvering between alders, then the long shoulder of the hill where the glasshouse stood — stood, he corrected himself, had stood, would stand for six more weeks — its ridge just visible above the tree line, catching the grey light the way it always had, like a held breath.

He had prepared remarks. That was the humiliating truth of it: he had sat on the train rehearsing sentences, small plausible ones, the kind a man might say if he were passing through. *I'm writing about the estate. A heritage piece. Before it comes down.* He had said them aloud once, quietly, into the collar of his coat, and had been alarmed by how easily they came, how smooth they were, how thirty years of professional lying had left him with a voice that could make anything sound reasonable. Even this. Especially this.

The bus let him off at the square.

He recognized the square before he recognized anything else about the town, because squares are the parts of places that refuse to change. Same cobbles, worn to a dull gloss down the center where feet had agreed upon a path. Same fountain, dry now, its stone nymph greened with lichen, holding her broken jug at the same apologetic angle. The bakery was still there but called something else; the ironmonger's had become a shop selling candles and things made of driftwood; there was a café now, which there had not been, with chalkboard menus and chairs that looked deliberately uncomfortable.

And there was a plaque.

It was set into the wall beside the old reading room, brass gone brown, and Yara crossed the square to it without deciding to, his body moving the way bodies move toward their own wounds. He read it twice.

*In memory of the Greenhouse Community, 1994–1999,* it said. *Whose ideals outlived their circumstances.*

Below, in smaller letters: *Five lives were touched by tragedy here. May the valley remember gently.*

*Five lives.* He counted them without meaning to, the way you count the beats of your own heart. Anselm. Beatrix. Himself. And then — he had to dig for the names, they came up slow as stones from a garden bed — Tomas and Ilse, the couple who had kept the bees. Five. As if the commune had been five people. As if there hadn't been eleven of them at the height, eleven names around the stove, eleven bowls in the cupboard Yara had washed every night. As if four people had simply been subtracted from history, quietly, the way you remove an ingredient that didn't work.

*May the valley remember gently.*

He stood there long enough that a woman coming out of the candle shop gave him the look locals give strangers who stare at walls — not hostile, just proprietary, a small tax levied on outsiders. He smiled at her. She did not smile back, exactly, but she softened by a degree, and then she stopped softening, and her eyes did something he would spend the rest of the day trying to describe. They went over his face the way a hand goes over fabric checking for flaws. Then they widened. Then they went carefully, deliberately blank.

"You'll be Mr. Voss," she said.

Not *you're* Yara Voss. Not *aren't you*. *You'll be.* As if he were a delivery she'd been told to expect and had hoped would get lost.

"I am," he said. "I don't think we—"

"No," she said. "We wouldn't have known each other. I was at school." She said it the way people say *I was abroad during the war*. She shifted her bag higher on her shoulder. "They're knocking it down, then."

"The glasshouse? Yes. In six weeks."

"Good," she said, and then, hearing herself, added, "Well. It's an eyesore. Has been for years. My mother used to say you could see it from the churchyard and it spoiled the view of the whole hill." She paused. "She's buried there now herself, so I suppose she can look at it as long as she likes."

It was almost a joke. It was the closest thing to warmth anyone would offer him that day, and he understood, standing in the cold square with the dry fountain at his back, that he would need to learn to live on things this thin.

* * *

He had booked a room at what had been the Station Hotel and was now called something with the word *Bothy* in it, though it was neither a bothy nor near the station. The woman at the desk was younger than him by twenty years and looked at his passport rather than his face, which he was grateful for. Faces were dangerous here. Names were worse.

"Voss," she repeated, typing. "Any relation to—"

"No," he said.

She glanced up. Whatever she saw in his face ended the conversation before it began. She gave him a key on an actual fob, heavy brass, and told him breakfast was until nine and that if he wanted dinner he should say so by four, because the kitchen was small.

His room looked out over the car park and, past it, the hill. He put his bag down and did not unpack. He took out his notebook instead — the private one, the true one, the one no editor had ever seen — and wrote at the top of a fresh page: *Day One.* Underneath, after a long while: *They've made it smaller. Five lives. Eleven bowls. Who decided?*

He knew who decided. Towns decide these things the way water decides downhill: collectively, without a single responsible party. A story too painful gets sanded down until it fits inside a plaque. He had done the same thing himself, professionally, for decades — taken the unmanageable truth of a kitchen or a life and planed it into three elegant paragraphs. He was in no position to resent the valley its revisionism. He had written the first draft of the lie himself.

But there was a difference, and he felt it in his chest like a splinter: he had lied to escape. They had lied to sleep.

* * *

In the afternoon he walked. He told himself it was reconnaissance — a journalist orienting himself — but his feet had their own itinerary, and it ran through his nineteenth year. The lane behind the mill, where the blackberries had come in so thick that August the birds got drunk on them. The ford, now bridged, where Beatrix had stood calf-deep in the water with her trousers rolled and dared him to say the temperature out loud, because saying it would make it real and she preferred weather to remain a rumor. The chapel hall where the commune had held its Friday meetings, still standing, now a yoga studio, its noticeboard offering *gentle vinyasa* in a font that had never known urgency.

At the corner of Mill Lane an old man was trimming a hedge with electric shears, and when he shut them off to watch Yara pass, Yara braced himself for the blankness again, the polite nothing.

Instead the old man said, "You're the cook."

Yara stopped. "I— yes. I was."

"Kessler," the old man said, wiping his hands on his apron as if they'd been introduced properly. "I had the dairy up the road. Your lot bought milk off me. Paid on time, which was more than most." He looked at Yara with the frank appraisal of a man who has spent his life judging livestock. "You were the quiet one. Did the cooking. Good bread." A pause, and then, with terrible mildness: "Shame, what happened."

"Yes," Yara said.

"Her fault, they said. The girl. The one who kept the plants." Kessler picked up his shears, considered them, set them down again. "Never sat right with me. Fires want three things and she'd only ever give them two."

Yara's mouth was dry. "You remember that?"

"Man my age remembers everything," Kessler said. "That's the trouble with living this long. Nobody asks you what you remember, so you end up telling hedges." He looked at Yara a moment longer, and there was something in the look — not kindness, nothing so warm, but a kind of patience, the patience of a trap left open. "You'll be wanting to talk to people. For your piece."

"How did you know I'm writing a piece?"

"Nobody comes back to Vallbridge for any other reason," Kessler said. "They come back for weddings, funerals, or articles." He picked up the shears again. "I'm at the top of the lane most mornings. If you want the dairy's version of events. Nobody ever asked for the dairy's version."

* * *

He ate dinner alone at seven, in a dining room built for forty and occupied by three: himself, a couple speaking Dutch over maps of coastal paths. The food was competent. He catalogued it automatically, the way his tongue had learned to substitute for itself — the lamb pink as promised, the jus reduced to a proper glaze, salt balanced, acid present — and the catalogue ran through his head in his own column's cadence, and he hated it, and he did it anyway, because it was the only machinery he had left for touching food.

Halfway through the main course, the door to the kitchen swung open to admit a tray, and he caught the smell of it — rosemary, fat, scorched fond lifting off a hot pan — and for one vertiginous second the smell was not smell but memory entire: the glasshouse kitchen, August, tomatoes split with heat on the sill, Beatrix at the table with her chin in her hand watching him cook, saying, *you season like you're apologizing to someone.* He gripped the edge of the table until the moment passed. The Dutch couple did not look up. Smells do that to people in restaurants all the time; it is one of the few griefs a dining room will forgive.

Afterward he walked out into the dark, past the pub where light and laughter spilled onto the cobbles, and he stood across the street from it for a full minute, testing himself, seeing if anyone would come out and know him. Two men did, arguing amiably about football, and passed within arm's reach of him without a glance. Thirty years ago one of those men — or their fathers, rather — had thrown a brick through the glasshouse gate with a note tied to it: *GO BACK TO WHERE YOU CAME FROM*, which had been funny, since most of them had come from twelve miles away. Yara had been the one who found the note in the morning. He had burned it in the stove before Beatrix could see it, one of the small protective lies of that year, the kind that feel like love at the time.

Now nobody threw bricks. Now there was a plaque that said *remember gently*, and a woman in a candle shop who went blank, and a dairyman at the top of a lane keeping a version of events like a jar of preserves against winter.

He turned toward the hotel and stopped.

Across the square, under the lamp outside the reading room, a girl of perhaps sixteen was pinning something to the community noticeboard — a flyer, a lost cat, he didn't care — and as she smoothed it flat with the heel of her hand, she hummed.

Four notes. Then four more.

Yara's whole body went still, the way water goes still over something deep. It wasn't the song — it couldn't be the song, the song existed nowhere except inside two skulls and one wind-up radio — but it was shaped like it, the same rising interval, the same little hesitation before the phrase came down, and for a moment he stood in the square of a town that had forgotten him, listening to a stranger carry a ghost of a melody that had never been written down, and he thought: *it survived anyway. Things survive anyway. Through girls who don't know what they're humming, through dairymen nobody interviews, through seeds in envelopes. Everything I buried is up and walking around.*

The girl pinned her flyer and went, humming, into the dark.

Yara stood a while longer. Then he took out his notebook, and by the light of the reading-room lamp he wrote the sentence he had been circling all day, the one underneath all the others:

*They didn't forget me. They forgot what I did. Those are different mercies, and I don't know yet which one I came here to refuse.*

He closed the book. Above the rooftops, past the chimney pots and the TV aerials, the hill was a blacker black than the sky, and somewhere along its shoulder the glasshouse stood in the dark with its broken panes, holding whatever it held — the plants, the shed, the notebook in the potting shed he did not yet know was there — waiting for morning the way it had waited thirty years: without complaint, without forgiveness, without going anywhere.
