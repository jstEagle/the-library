## 10. Odile

The morning the surveyor came, Odile was on her knees in the fern house with a trowel and a dead man's orchid, which is to say she was exactly where she always was, doing exactly what she had done for eleven years, when the world decided to interrupt her.

She heard him before she saw him — the particular crunch of a stranger's shoes on gravel. Everyone who belonged to the glasshouse grounds walked differently. The postman had a rolling gait, favoring his left hip; the boy from the pharmacy cut across the lawn at an angle, impatient, as if the grass owed him money. But this was a measured tread, deliberate, pausing at intervals the way people pause when they are measuring things with their eyes. Surveyors walked like that. She had learned their walk over the years the way farmers learn weather.

She did not look up. Let him find her among the ferns. It was better that way — let them see her first as part of the place, green-shadowed and unremarkable, so that when they understood she was not staff, not caretaker, not anything with a title they could use to dismiss her, the understanding would arrive too late for them to rearrange their faces.

The fern house had been her cathedral for a decade. Its glass roof had lost perhaps a third of its panes, and where the light fell through the gaps it fell in hard silver columns that moved with the sun across the flagstones, so that the room kept its own slow clock. The ferns themselves had gone feral in the most dignified way — tree ferns unfurling their croziers like questions asked every spring and never answered, maidenhairs colonizing the damp corners in black-stemmed crowds, one enormous staghorn mounted on a rotting plank that Anselm's people had nailed up before anyone could stop them, back when there were still people to stop. And in the far corner, in a terracotta pot glazed the color of old honey, the orchid. *Cymbidium*, she thought, though she'd never been certain of the species. It had belonged to a man named Tomas who had died in 2003 in a town two valleys over, and whose sister had written to the council asking if anything of the glasshouse might be preserved, and had received, Odile imagined, a letter full of regret and procedure. Odile had answered it herself instead. She still had the sister's replies in a shoebox: four letters over six years, each one thinner than the last, until the last one was only a card saying *thank you for keeping it alive*.

That was the work. That was the whole of it. Not rescue — she had stopped believing in rescue around the time the demolition notice went up on the gate, laminated against the rain, official as a scar. Custodianship. You kept things alive past all reason because someone had to, because the alternative was to be the kind of person who let a dead man's orchid die twice.

The footsteps stopped outside the fern house door. A shadow crossed the light columns.

"You're not supposed to be in here," the man said.

Odile sat back on her heels and looked at him properly for the first time. He was standing in the doorway with the awkwardness of a man who has entered a church mid-service — half in, half out, unsure whether to remove his hat or apologize. Tall, but stooped, as if he'd spent years making himself smaller in rooms where he was too visible. Gray at the temples, expensive coat wrong for the valley, city shoes already ruined by the gravel. His face was familiar in the way faces in newspapers are familiar — you've seen the shape of it without ever studying it.

"And you're not supposed to be here at all," she said. "So we're even."

"I'm with the survey." He said it smoothly enough, but something flickered behind it, some small hesitation, like a needle skipping a groove. "Structural assessment. I'm cataloguing the interior before the demolition."

"Are you," Odile said. She stood, brushing soil from her knees with the unhurried dignity of a woman who had nothing left to prove to anyone. "Then you'll want my list."

He blinked. "Your list?"

"The inventory." She wiped her hands on her thighs, leaving green-brown smears she didn't mind. "Of everything still living in here. Forty-one species in the main houses, nineteen in the cold frames, if you count the volunteers, which I do, because they count themselves. Three trees. One orchid that predates your demolition notice by forty years and will outlive your report by longer, if I have anything to say about it." She lifted her chin toward the far corner. "That one. It was Tomas's. He grew it from a cutting in 1987."

The man turned and looked at the orchid, and Odile watched his face do something complicated — watched recognition move through it like water through dry ground, darkening everything it touched. For a moment he seemed to have forgotten she was there.

"Tomas Lindqvist," he said quietly. Not a question.

Odile's wariness sharpened into attention. "You knew him?"

"No." Too fast. Then, more carefully: "I read about the commune. I'm — researching. For the heritage piece."

*The heritage piece.* She had heard that phrase three times now, from three different visitors, and each time it arrived wearing the same borrowed clothes. The local paper wanted it. A university archive wanted it. A woman with a clipboard and a lanyard had wanted it, and had lasted eleven minutes in the palm room before the smell of wet rot sent her back to her car. Nobody wanted a heritage piece. They wanted a paragraph, a photograph, a line for a funding application. They wanted the glasshouse reduced to something that fit in a drawer.

"Well," Odile said, "you'll want to see the palm room, then. If you're cataloguing." She picked up her trowel and her notebook — a fat, rubber-banded thing, swollen with years of entries — and pushed past him through the doorway, close enough to catch his scent: rain and wool and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, tomato leaves. The green sharp smell of crushed tomato stems. It hit her like a hand laid flat against her sternum, and she had no idea why, and she hated not knowing why.

She led him along the central path between the houses, the gravel giving way to cracked concrete where moss had written its own green history in the joints. The glasshouse rose around them — the great spine of it, iron ribs arched like the skeleton of a whale that had swum inland and died standing up. In summer the whole structure groaned and ticked as the sun expanded the metal, a sound like a ship at anchor, and Odile had spent enough nights inside it to know its voice the way you know a sleeping animal's breathing. Today it was silent under cloud, patient, waiting.

"They say it's unsafe," the man said behind her.

"It's held itself up for a hundred and thirty years without anyone's help. It doesn't need their opinion." She walked faster, annoyed at herself for defending it aloud, for revealing how much the defense cost her. "Mind the step. The flags shift near the aqueduct channel."

Inside the palm room the air changed — thickened, sweetened, took on the weight of decades of transpiration. Even gutted, even condemned, the room made its own climate, a stubborn pocket of another latitude. The palms had grown to the broken roof and beyond, fronds pushing through the empty panes into the open air like hands reaching out of water. Beneath them, the understory had organized itself without permission: self-seeded figs, a thicket of *Fatsia*, ferns spilling from the collapsed staging in green avalanches. On the far wall, a grapevine planted — she was almost certain — in the commune's first year had climbed through the rafters and fruited, absurdly, every September, small sour grapes nobody ate but everybody noticed.

The man stopped just inside the door and did not move for a long time.

Odile watched him watch the room. She had seen people react to the palm room in maybe a dozen ways — the clipboard woman's recoil, the schoolchildren's shrieking delight, the councilman's brisk appraisal, the old nun who had crossed herself without seeming to know she'd done it. But this man simply stood, and breathed, and his face did the thing that faces do when a memory arrives without being invited: the slight parting of the lips, the eyes going soft and fixed on some middle distance that wasn't in the room at all.

"You've been here before," she said.

"A long time ago." His voice had gone strange, flattened, careful. "When it was — different."

"When it was alive."

"Yes."

She should have pressed. She was good at pressing; it was one of her few social skills, honed on council clerks and insurance men and the occasional journalist foolish enough to print the word *eyesore*. But something in his stillness made her hold off, the way you don't startle a bird you want to stay on the branch.

Instead she opened her notebook to the current page and held it out. "Here. Since you're cataloguing. Everything that's survived, mapped and dated, with notes on condition. Eleven years of it. Your survey team can copy whatever's useful — I assume they'll want it for the demolition method statement, so they know what they're killing."

He took the notebook with both hands, the way you take something fragile, and read. She watched his eyes move down the page — *Ficus carica, wall-trained, fruiting annually; Vitis vinifera, rafter-grown, est. planting 1998–99; Phoenix canariensis, crown damage 2016 storm, recovering* — and saw him stop at the entry she always stopped at herself, the one she had written and rewritten until the ink shone:

*Tomatoes — self-sown from original stock, north bed. Third generation. Still fruiting.*

"From the original stock," he said. His thumb rested on the words as if checking whether they were warm.

"There were tomatoes here before the fire," Odile said. "Everyone says so. Old varieties, saved seed, passed around the valley for years after. These came up on their own in the north bed about nine years ago — probably from seed banked in the soil, lying dormant all that time. Nineteen-ninety-nine to whenever. Waiting." She shrugged, embarrassed suddenly by her own poetry. "Seeds do that. They wait longer than we think they can."

The man closed the notebook and held it a moment longer than necessary before handing it back. When he spoke, his voice had recovered its smoothness, but she had already heard the crack beneath it, and she was not a woman who forgot cracks.

"This is remarkable work," he said. "Truly. Has anyone — does the council know? About the inventory?"

"The council knows I'm here. They call me *the occupier*, as in *removal of the occupier will precede works*. They don't know what I know, because they've never once asked." She laughed, short and without humor. "Eleven years, and the only person who ever asked to see my list is a man from the demolition survey."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm not really with the survey."

"I know."

His head came up sharply. "You know?"

"You don't carry yourself like a surveyor. Surveyors look at buildings. You keep looking at the plants." She tucked the notebook back under her arm. "Also, you flinched at Tomas's name, and you smell like tomato leaves, and no surveyor walks the central path like he's remembering where the beds used to be." She nodded toward the floor, where his feet had come to rest — precisely, unconsciously — in the gap between two vanished raised beds, standing where a gardener would stand, where gardeners had stood for a hundred years. "So. Who are you?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Outside, wind moved through the broken panes and made the whole glasshouse sigh, a long exhalation, the building breathing around them. A frond detached somewhere high above and came down slowly, turning, and landed between them on the flagstones without a sound.

"Yara," he said finally. Just the name, offered like a coin he wasn't sure was still legal tender.

It meant nothing to her. She searched her memory — the commune histories she'd collected, the newspaper archives she'd requested and photocopied at the library in Marren, the interviews she'd taped with valley old-timers who remembered the fire and disagreed about everything except that it had happened — and found nothing. No Yara. Which was strange, because everyone who had been here in those days had a name, and she had all of them, filed in the shoebox alongside Tomas's sister's letters: Anselm, Beatrix, Marta, the Kessler brothers, the woman called Dove who'd left early and wouldn't speak of it. No Yara anywhere.

But he was standing in the middle of her palm room looking like a man returned to the scene of something, and liars, in her experience, did not stand that still.

"Yara," she repeated. "And you were here. Before."

"When I was nineteen." He said it the way people confess to crimes — quickly, watching her face. "One summer. I cooked in the kitchen house. I wasn't — I wasn't anyone important."

"Nobody who lived here was important," Odile said. "That was rather the point, I understand." She studied him a moment longer, weighing something, and then made the decision she would later mark as the hinge of everything — the moment the stranger became hers, in the possessive way you claim a stray. "Come on, then. If you're going to catalogue, you'll do it properly. There's a fig in the east wing that fruits twice a year and a camellia by the boiler house older than the town charter, and if you write down one word about *structural deficiency* while you're looking at them, I'll take my trowel to you."

Something happened to his face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile had once lived, lit briefly from within. "You've been here eleven years," he said. "Why? What keeps you?"

Odile turned toward the east wing so he couldn't see her answer land on her face.

"Someone has to remember what's alive," she said. "Before they knock it down."

Behind her, his footsteps followed — hesitant at first, then steadier, learning the path. And in the north bed, unseen by either of them, the third-generation tomatoes went on ripening in the gray light, patient as seeds, waiting for what they had waited all these years to wait for.
