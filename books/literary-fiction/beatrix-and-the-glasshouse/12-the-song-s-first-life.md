## 12. The Song's First Life

It began, as the best things in that summer did, with an argument about tomatoes.

Yara remembered the argument the way other people remembered weddings — in fragments of light and heat, with the details worn smooth by handling. It was the last week of August, the year the valley smelled of ripening fruit and woodsmoke from Anselm's evening fires, and Beatrix had declared, with the absolute conviction she brought to everything living, that the San Marzanos were ready to come off the vine.

"They need another week," Yara said. "They're still holding acid in the shoulders. You can see it — that green star at the stem."

"That green star is character." She was standing in the doorway of the potting shed, a basket on her hip, her forearms streaked to the elbow with soil. "You want to strip them the moment they're perfect. That's what you do with everything, Yara. You take it at its peak and you cook it down into something safe."

He had laughed at that, then. He had been twenty and could laugh at anything. "I'm a cook. Safe is my religion."

"No," she said, and her voice had that low, level quality it took on when she was entirely serious, the quality that made even Anselm pause mid-sentence. "Safe is your fear. It's not the same thing."

He would spend thirty years turning that sentence over, like a stone in a pocket, learning all its faces. That night, though, he only reached out and stole a tomato from her basket — one with the green star still on its shoulder — and bit into it standing there, juice running down his wrist, daring her to be angry.

It was sweeter than it should have been. Everything that summer was sweeter than it should have been, and none of them, not one of the eleven of them rattling around inside the glasshouse's iron ribs, understood that sweetness is a debt. It is always borrowed from somewhere.

* * *

The stove in the common room was a cast-iron monster salvaged from a farmhouse up the valley, and it had a personality the commune never managed to name. It drew badly. It filled the room with a low amber light that made everyone look like a painting of themselves, and it had a habit of ticking for an hour after the fire died, as though it were still thinking.

On the night the song was born, the stove was doing its slow thinking, and the rain had come at last after three weeks of heat — a real rain, the kind that arrived like a guest who intends to stay, drumming on the glass roof until the whole building hummed. Yara was at the long table with his notebook, drafting the week's menu around what the garden had surrendered: the last courgettes, beans gone tough at the seams, a glut of tomatoes Beatrix had finally, grudgingly, allowed him to pick. He was writing the word *confit* and crossing it out and writing it again, because that was the whole of his ambition in those days — to make something so simple that no one could accuse it of trying too hard.

Beatrix was at the far end of the room with her guitar, which she almost never played for anyone. He knew this the way he knew the stove's moods: by observation, without comment. She'd bought it at fourteen with money from selling eggs, and she tuned it the way other people prayed — privately, repeatedly, with an expression that permitted no witnesses. When she played, it was usually alone in the glasshouse after the others slept, and Yara had more than once stood in the dark of the tomato rows listening to the blurred ghost of it through the glass, feeling like a trespasser in the best sense of the word — the sense in which trespassing is just another name for longing.

But that night she played in the room. That night, for reasons she never explained and he never asked, because asking would have been like switching on a light to watch a moth, she sat with the guitar across her knees and her bare feet on the cold flagstones and began, quietly, to find something.

He pretended to work. This was the discipline of his love in those days: to appear occupied. He crossed out *confit* and wrote *confit* again and let his ears do all the living.

What she was finding had no words at first. Just a phrase of melody, four notes descending, then a turn upward that didn't resolve — she'd play it, stop, frown, play it again a half-step differently, and each time it was as though she were trying on a coat that almost fit. The rain kept its own rhythm on the glass. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door closed and no one came down, because it was past eleven and the commune's unwritten law held that the common room after eleven belonged to whoever was in it.

After a while she began to hum, and the humming became syllables, and the syllables became words, and Yara set down his pen because his hand had begun to shake in a way he found embarrassing and unaccountable.

He could not, even then, even at the time, even with the whole of his attention given over to it, reconstruct the words afterward in their proper order. They were about a house made of light. About something planted that would outlive the planter. About a door that opens both ways. They were simple words — she was not a poet and never pretended to be; her genius was for melody and for meaning what she said — and they went through him like water through cloth, dyeing everything.

Because he knew — sitting there with his useless menu and his crossed-out word, twenty years old and certain of nothing except the smell of the rain and the sound of her voice — he knew that she was singing about the glasshouse. That she had taken the whole improbable experiment, Anselm's borrowed utopia with its rota disputes and its leaking panes and its rationed sugar, and she had found the true thing buried in it, the seed under the compost, and she was singing that. Not the politics. Not the people, even. The *place*, and the possibility the place stood for: that a handful of flawed, hungry, stubborn young people had built something alive out of glass and intention, and that it might outlast them.

And underneath the tenderness of that, in a stratum he would not examine for thirty years, was the other knowledge, the one that arrived like cold air under a door: *she is singing this to the room, but she is the only one in it who could have written it, and when it is finished it will belong to her alone, and I will have been present at the only performance it will ever receive.*

He was right about that last part. He was right about nearly everything that night, which is the cruelest thing he could say of it.

* * *

"Play it again," he said, when she stopped.

She looked up. In the stove light her face was half amber and half shadow, and there was something startled in it, the look of someone surfacing from deep water. "It's not finished."

"It's finished enough."

"It's not finished, and it's not — " she touched the strings, damping them, a small dead chord — "it's not for playing. Not yet. I don't know what it is yet."

"It's the truest thing I've heard in this house," Yara said, "and this house is full of people saying true things at each other across rotas."

That made her laugh — a short, surprised sound, quickly pressed down, because Beatrix was economical even with joy. She looked at him for a long moment across the length of the table, over the ruins of his menu, and something passed between them that he would later understand had been a door opening. Not a declaration. Nothing so legible. Just the sense, sudden and total, that the two of them occupied the same country and had only just discovered the border was open.

"Come here, then," she said. "If you're going to be the audience, you can at least be close enough to hear properly."

He crossed the room and sat on the floor beside her chair, his back against the stove's warm flank, and she played it through again, and this time he did not pretend to do anything else. He just listened with his whole body, the rain keeping time above them, the tomatoes in their basket going soft and sweet in the dark, and he thought: *I will remember this. Whatever else happens, I will keep this.*

He had no idea, then, how much the *whatever else happens* would come to weigh.

* * *

She played it a third time because he asked, and a fourth because she wanted to — he could tell the difference; the fourth had a looseness in it, a pleasure, her foot keeping time against the flagstone. And then she stopped mid-verse and said, "There's a bit missing. After the second chorus. Something about — I don't know. About what you'd carry out of a house if you could only carry one thing." She frowned at her hands. "I can't find it. It'll come or it won't."

"It's already the best song anyone's written in this valley," Yara said. "Possibly this decade."

"You're biased."

"Comprehensively."

She smiled down at him, and the stove ticked, and the rain went on, and for a while neither of them said anything, because there are silences that are just the absence of noise and there are silences that are a room built for two, and this was the second kind, and Yara sat inside it and thought, with the simple greed of the very happy: *more of this. Let there be more of this.*

"Sing it once more," he said. "Before anyone else hears it. I want to have heard it first. I want — " and here he faltered, because the true shape of the want was too large and too strange to say aloud — "I want it to be ours for one night. Before it belongs to the world."

"It's never going to belong to the world," Beatrix said, with a small dry laugh. "It's a song about a glasshouse in a valley with eleven people in it. The world has other plans."

But she sang it again. Once more, the whole way through, the missing verse still missing, a gap in the middle like a window left open — and he understood even then, in the animal way one understands such things, that the gap was not an accident. That the song could not be finished because what came after the second chorus hadn't happened yet. That she had written the melody of a future she could feel coming but not yet see, a future that would ask what a person would carry out of a burning house, and what they would leave, and who they would save, and who they would, without meaning to, without even deciding to, simply fail.

He clapped, softly, so as not to break it. She bowed from the chair, absurd and grave. And then Anselm's tread sounded on the stairs — they both heard it, that measured, deliberate descent — and Beatrix's hands came down flat on the strings, killing the sound, and her face closed like a book.

"Tomorrow," she said quietly, to Yara, to the stove, to the rain. "I'll finish it tomorrow."

She never did. Not that year. Not for thirty years. And Yara would come to understand, with a clarity that arrived only after everything else had been lost, that the song had been finished all along — that the missing verse had been waiting for them both, out in the dark past the glass, patient as a seed — and that the cost of hearing it sung whole would be exactly, precisely, everything.

* * *

But that was later. That night there was only the stove ticking down, and the rain easing toward morning, and Beatrix putting the guitar back in its case with the tenderness of someone laying down a sleeping child. Yara walked her to the foot of the stairs, and at the bottom step she paused, and without looking back she said, "You'd carry the seeds, wouldn't you. If it came to it. Out of all of this — you'd carry the seeds."

"Yes," he said, and meant it, and would spend three decades learning what a small, insufficient word *yes* could be.

She went up. The stairs creaked their familiar four creaks. The stove gave its last tick and went silent, and Yara stood alone in the amber dark of the common room with the rain finishing above him, and he took out his notebook — the same notebook, he would realize much later, that would one day surface in a potting shed in the hands of a stranger — and beneath the crossed-out menus he wrote a single line:

*She sang a song tonight that no one will ever hear twice. I was there. I was the whole audience. God help me, I was enough.*

And in the morning, Anselm would ask him at breakfast, in that mild voice of his that was never mild, where Beatrix had gone the night the generator failed — and the summer, which had seemed so sweet, so endlessly, undeservedly sweet, would begin, note by note, to turn.
