## 13. Power in the Greenhouse

The first thing Anselm took was not their money or their bodies or even their names. He took the salt.

Yara understood this only years later, in the way one understands a poison long after it has finished its work — by tracing the symptoms backward to the source. At the time, in the summer of 1999, the salt had seemed like a joke, almost a kindness. Anselm stood in the kitchen of the glasshouse commune one grey morning in June, holding the ceramic jar in both hands the way a priest holds a reliquary, and said: *Flavor is a luxury. We are here to learn what we actually need.* And then he carried the jar out of the kitchen and locked it in the toolshed, and everyone laughed, because Anselm had a laugh that made you want to be in on whatever he was laughing at, and because it was only salt.

Yara had been at the commune for eleven days. He was nineteen years old, and he had arrived with a duffel bag, a knife roll inherited from a dead uncle, and the conviction — common at nineteen, fatal at any age — that he was finally about to become the person he was meant to be. The glasshouse sat in the crook of the valley like a hand cupped to hold water: a Victorian iron-and-glass structure the length of a church, attached to a stone farmhouse, surrounded by four acres of exhausted but forgiving soil. There were nine of them that first summer, later eleven, later seven. Anselm had found the place through an inheritance he never explained and a bankruptcy he described as a persecution, and he had gathered them the way a magnet gathers filings — each one pulled out of some scattered, failing life and aligned, without their quite noticing it, along a single axis.

Yara had come from a restaurant kitchen in Lyon where a chef had thrown a sauté pan at his head and, worse, had been right to. He had answered an advertisement in a counter-culture gazette: *Seeking hands for a living experiment. We grow what we eat. We eat what we grow. We are done with the world that measures worth in wages.* He had cooked his first communal meal on his second night — a ratatouille from vegetables so fresh they still carried the heat of the soil — and he had watched nine people eat it in the long amber light of the glasshouse's western wing, and he had felt, for the first time in his life, that feeding people might be a form of love rather than labor.

Anselm had eaten slowly, deliberately, and then looked up and said, "You understand something the rest of us are only guessing at." And nineteen-year-old Yara had glowed like a filament.

That was the first transaction. He would not recognize it as one for thirty years.

* * *

By July, the salt was only the beginning, and no one was laughing.

The rationing arrived the way weather arrives — gradually, then all at once. Anselm announced at evening council that the harvest projections were short. There had been blight in the east beds; the tomatoes were running two weeks behind; the well was lower than it should be in a wet year. All of this was true, or true enough. The commune ate what it grew, and what it grew that July was not enough. So Anselm instituted portions. He weighed them himself, at the head of the table, with a brass scale he had bought at a flea market in Valence — a beautiful object, a Victorian balance with two shallow pans, and he weighed out rice and beans and the first small green tomatoes with the gravity of a man dispensing sacrament.

And here was the mechanism, the thing Yara would spend decades not thinking about: Anselm's own pan was never light. He served last, which looked like sacrifice. But the portions he served himself were calibrated to the gram, and the portions he served the others were calibrated to their usefulness. Yara, who cooked, ate well. Marisol, who ran the irrigation, ate well. Old Tomas, whose hands were failing and who had come to the commune to die among growing things, got less. When Tomas complained — mildly, apologetically, in his cracked Occitan-inflected French — Anselm did not raise his voice. He simply looked at Tomas with an expression of profound, patient sorrow, the look of a father watching a child refuse medicine, and said, "You're taking more than your season deserves," and the table went silent, and Tomas went to his bunk without eating, and the next morning he worked in the beds for eleven hours as though trying to earn back a place he had never formally lost.

Yara saw it. That was the horror, the specific horror, the one he would carry in his body like a swallowed stone: he saw it, and he said nothing, and worse — he benefited, and the benefiting felt like being loved.

Because Anselm did not only ration. He praised. He had a genius for praise, a connoisseur's palate for it, and he dispensed it with the same brass-scale precision. He would stand behind Yara at the stove — the great iron range in the farmhouse kitchen, wood-fired, temperamental — and watch him work, and say nothing for a long time, and then say one sentence, exactly calibrated: "The others eat and feel lucky. You make them feel *kept*." And Yara, who had spent his childhood being told he was too soft, too slow, too much, would feel those words go through him like warmth through cold hands. He cooked better after that. He cooked *for* Anselm's approval, though he told himself he cooked for the community. He stayed up late inventing dishes from rations, stretching a cup of rice into a feast with wild fennel and sorrel from the ditches, and when the table applauded — and they did applaud, they had a tradition of it, hands on the table rather than in the air, a soft drumroll of gratitude — Yara knew, in some sealed-off chamber of himself, that the applause was traveling a circuit that ended at Anselm, that every meal was a vote, that the food was a currency and Anselm was the mint.

Beatrix saw it too. He knew that because of the way she stopped eating at the table.

* * *

She had come in May, three weeks before him, from the coast — a seed-saver, a quiet one, with forearms brown from other people's gardens and a habit of speaking only after everyone else had finished, so that when she finally spoke, the silence she'd been holding seemed to pour into her words and give them weight. She kept the nursery wing: the propagation benches, the cuttings, the seed inventory, the delicate work. She and Yara found each other the way two people on the night shift of the same institution find each other — through hours, through the shared language of early mornings. She would come into the kitchen at dawn with lettuce still cold from the night air, and he would have the fire lit and the kettle on, and for twenty minutes before the others woke, the commune was just the two of them and the smell of woodsmoke and wet glass, and they talked about nothing — about whether basil could be persuaded to overwinter, about whether the swallows nesting in the palm room were the same pair as last year — and it was in those twenty minutes, more than anywhere else in that whole burning year, that Yara had been happy.

It was Beatrix who first said it out loud, in August, in the palm room, with the heat pressing down through the glass like a held breath.

"He's not feeding us," she said. "He's teaching us to be grateful. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Yara had been chopping — something, anything, his hands needing occupation the way other men's hands needed cigarettes. "The harvest *is* short," he said, and heard, even as he said it, the borrowed timbre of his own voice. Anselm's cadence. Anselm's reasonable, regretful reasonableness.

Beatrix looked at him. She had a way of looking that made you aware of your own face, of what it was doing. "You've eaten better than Tomas all summer," she said. "Did you weigh your own plate? Did anyone?"

He hadn't. He hadn't wanted to. That was the thing he would never be able to explain to anyone who hadn't lived inside it: the system did not require your belief, only your appetite. You did not have to agree that Anselm was right. You only had to be hungry, and to notice who was fed, and to draw the obvious conclusion about what kept the portions coming. The control was not in the rules. It was in the *incentives*. Anselm had built a machine that ran on the most ordinary human material there was — the wish to be valued — and it required so little maintenance. An occasional punishment. An occasional grace. The punishments were public and quiet: the sorrowful look, the demotion from a work rotation, the evening council where your small failure was examined with clinical tenderness until you confessed to sins you hadn't known you'd committed. The graces were private and enormous: a hand on the shoulder, a confidence shared, a portion of honey from the stores that no one else saw him give.

By September, the commune had a vocabulary of its own, and the vocabulary was Anselm's. *Season* meant your current standing — you were *in season* or *out of it*. *Clearing* meant a public correction. *Rooting* meant the deep commitment Anselm demanded, and those who were *rooted* — Yara among them, Yara proudest of all — sat closer to the head of the table, were trusted with the stores, were permitted small liberties the others were not. It was a feudalism of affection, complete with its own heraldry, and Yara had a coat of arms and a title and a plate that was never light, and he told himself every single day, in the kitchen, with his hands in other people's food, that he was one of the good ones.

* * *

The dissent, when it came, came over water.

The well had been failing since August — that much was real, that much Anselm had not invented. But the rationing of water was where his method revealed itself completely, because water could not be disguised as anything but survival. He instituted it at council: drinking water from the rain barrels only, irrigation halved, washing restricted to two basin-days a week. And Marisol — Marisol, who had built the irrigation system with her own hands, who knew the hydrology of the valley better than the agricultural office did — stood up and said, "The east spring is running. I checked it Tuesday. We're not short of water. We're short of your permission to use it."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Yara had ever heard. Somewhere in the glass a swallow shifted on its nest. Anselm looked at Marisol for a long time, and then he did the thing that Yara understood only later was the whole engine of him: he *thanked* her. He thanked her for her vigilance, for her care, for her willingness to speak hard truths. And then he said, gently, "The spring water carries silt from the upper pasture. I've had it tested." — he hadn't, there was no test, there would never be a test — "and I can't in conscience serve it to this community. I know that's hard to hear. I know it would be easier to believe we're being cheated than to accept that the land itself is asking something of us. But we came here to listen to the land." And he sat down. And the council, relieved — that was the word, Yara could feel it in the room, the collective exhale of people who had been braced for a fight and were handed a parable instead — the council moved on.

Marisol left eleven days later. Not dramatically. She packed in the night, and by morning her bunk was stripped and her tools were gone from the shed, and Anselm said at breakfast, with genuine-seeming grief, "Some people come here to build and some people come here to be right," and everyone understood that Marisol had been the second kind, and Yara ate his breakfast, and his plate was full, and he did not say a word.

He did not say a word. Thirty years later, with the taste gone out of his mouth and the glasshouse scheduled for the wrecking ball, that sentence was still the truest thing he knew about himself, and the one he had spent a career of ten thousand adjectives building architecture around so that he would never have to look at it directly: *he did not say a word.* He had watched a woman be disappeared — not with violence, nothing so legible as violence, just with portion sizes and parables and the slow arithmetic of who was fed — and he had eaten well, and slept soundly, and risen at dawn to the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of Beatrix coming in with cold lettuce, and he had loved those twenty minutes so much that he had let them purchase his silence, day after day, the way Anselm's brass scale weighed out everything else.

And Beatrix — Beatrix was not Marisol. Beatrix did not leave. Beatrix began, in the small hours, in the propagation wing, to do something quiet and patient and infinitely more dangerous than argument: she began to organize. He saw the signs before he understood them — the hushed conversations that stopped when he came around the corner of the glass; the seed inventory kept now in her own hand in her own book rather than the communal ledger; the way Tomas and the two newcomers, Ines and Pavel, had started eating together at the far end of the table, a small republic of the underfed. She was building something, and he knew it, and part of him — the part that still glowed like a filament when Anselm said his name — was afraid of it.

* * *

The last council of September was the one Yara would relive, in one form or another, for the rest of his life.

Anselm had a new announcement. There had been, he said, *drainage* — a word that entered the commune's vocabulary that night and never left. Resources leaking. Trust leaking. Someone had been taking from the stores. Someone had been talking to the valley — to the shopkeepers, the farmers, the people in the village who had always regarded the glasshouse with a mixture of curiosity and contempt. Someone, Anselm said, with that sorrowful patience, was *out of season*.

And he looked — not long, not pointedly, just long enough, just pointedly enough — at the far end of the table.

Yara sat at the near end. His plate was full. The stove behind him in the kitchen still held the warmth of the meal he had made, the meal that had earned the soft drumroll of hands on wood, and he could feel the whole architecture of his small happiness standing around him like the glass itself — transparent, apparently open, and everywhere load-bearing. Beatrix was watching him from the far end of the table. Not accusing. Worse than accusing: she was watching to see what he would do, the way you watch a person standing at a door in a fire, and her face held the open, unguarded hope of someone who still believes the person she loves is going to come through it toward her.

Anselm said, "Yara. You cook for everyone here. You'd know, better than anyone, if the stores were short."

And the table turned to him — nine faces, then seven, lit by the oil lamps, waiting — and Yara felt the question settle over him like a hand on the back of the neck. Because it was not a question about stores. It was a question about allegiance, asked in front of everyone, and there was only one answer that kept his plate full, and they both knew it, and Beatrix knew he knew it, and the horrible thing, the thing he would spend three decades not tasting anything in order not to taste, was that some part of him — the nineteen-year-old part, the part that had arrived with a duffel bag and a knife roll and the conviction that he was finally becoming himself — some part of him was *grateful* for the question. Grateful to be chosen. Grateful to be asked to prove, one more time, that he was rooted.

He opened his mouth.

And Beatrix, at the far end of the table, very quietly, moved her hand — the smallest motion, barely a breath of movement — and touched the pocket of her apron, where the seed book was, where the list of names was, where the plan was, the escape she had been building all September, the door she had been holding open for him without ever yet asking him to walk through it.

He saw it. He understood it. And the lamps burned, and the swallow shifted in the glass above, and Anselm waited with the patience of a man who has never once needed to hurry, because the machine always did his waiting for him.
