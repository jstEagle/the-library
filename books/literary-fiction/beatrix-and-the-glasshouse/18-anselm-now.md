## 18. Anselm Now

The retreat was called Solstice Rise, and it sat on the ridge above the valley like a man who had bought the view rather than earned it.

Yara saw it first from the road that switchbacked up through the pines — a long low building of pale timber and glass, terraced gardens falling away beneath it in obedient tiers, a swimming pool the color of a held breath. Everything about it was composed. Everything about it was calm. And everything about it, he thought as he parked his rental car between a Range Rover and an electric sedan with a license plate from three countries away, had been purchased with money that had to have come from somewhere.

He knew where.

He sat in the car for four minutes — he counted them on the dashboard clock, because counting was what he did instead of breathing when the old dread came up through the floorboards of himself — and watched people move along the gravel paths in linen and serenity. A woman in her sixties stood at the herb garden with a wicker basket, cutting stems with scissors, her face tilted toward the sun with the expression of someone who had been told, and had paid to be told, that she was healing.

Anselm had built a church out of the same material he'd once used for a cage: other people's longing.

Yara got out of the car. The air up here smelled of pine resin and cut grass and, faintly, of money — that scentless scent of new cedar and filtered water and laundry dried in mountain wind. His stomach, which had been quiet since the train, began its slow rotation.

"You're expected," said the young man at the reception desk, before Yara had given his name.

Of course he was. Anselm would have known the moment Yara crossed the valley line. There had probably been a text message, a photograph, a name passed along some local nervous system that still remembered 1999 the way a body remembers a fracture. *Yara Voss is back.* The valley had one long memory and two short ones, depending on who was asking.

"He's in the glass pavilion," the young man said. "Past the pool. He asked me to tell you—" and here the young man hesitated, clearly reciting, "—that lunch is at one, but you'll want to come hungry."

*Come hungry.* Thirty years, and the man was still rationing him.

* * *

The glass pavilion stood apart from the main building, down a path lined with lavender gone slightly woody with age. It was smaller than the glasshouse had been — a fraction of the scale, a tenth of the ambition — but the proportions were deliberate, and Yara understood, walking toward it, that this was not imitation but quotation. Anselm had built himself a miniature of the thing he'd burned, the way a man who has destroyed a painting commissions a print and hangs it where he has to pass it every day. Or where everyone else has to pass it. With Anselm it was never possible to separate the penance from the performance.

Inside, the light fell green-gold through vines trained along wires overhead — tomatoes, Yara noted, and figs, and a grapevine heavy with fruit nobody would ever harvest because harvesting was labor and this was a place for being looked after. The floor was pale stone. In the center, at a table set for two under the largest vine, sat an old man reading a newspaper he was clearly not reading.

"Yara Voss," Anselm said, without looking up. "You've kept your hair."

It was such an Anselm sentence — a compliment shaped like an inventory, warmth administered like medicine, precise dosage — that Yara felt thirty years fall away and found himself, absurdly, nineteen again, standing in a doorway waiting to be told whether he'd done well.

"You haven't," he said.

Anselm laughed and folded the paper. "No. Well." He gestured to the chair opposite. "Sit. Eat. You look like a man who reviews food for a living and forgets to have any."

The table was laid with bread, oil, tomatoes sliced and salted, soft cheese, figs split open, a carafe of water with mint in it. A meal assembled from the garden around them, or made to look as though it were. Yara sat. He looked at the tomatoes — dark-shouldered, heirloom, the variety Beatrix had grown by the hundred in the old house — and understood that they were not lunch. They were an opening move.

"You knew I'd come," Yara said.

"I knew you'd come eventually." Anselm poured the water himself, no staff, no witnesses. That too was deliberate. "When I heard you were doing your heritage piece. The valley archive, the commune, all that." He smiled. His face had gone to leather and ruin, but the smile was intact, preserved like something pickled, and behind it the eyes were exactly the eyes Yara remembered: pale, patient, amused in advance. "I thought, there he is. The boy who could make anything taste like something. Come home to write the menu."

"I'm not writing about you."

"Everyone writes about me. They just don't always know it." Anselm tore a piece of bread, dragged it through oil, ate it with the unselfconscious appetite of a man who had never once doubted his right to consume. "Tell me about the piece. What's the angle? Nostalgia? Cautionary tale? The great dream that failed?" He waved a hand at the vines above them. "I can save you the research. It didn't fail. Look around. It graduated."

"It looks like a hotel."

"It looks like whatever the person paying for it needs it to look like. That was always the secret, Yara. You knew it better than most. You fed forty people on affection and thin soup, and every one of them believed they were rich. Belief is the only economy there is. I just stopped pretending otherwise."

There it was. No apology, no deflection, no shame — Yara had prepared himself for denial, for the smooth elderly revisionism he'd encountered in the town below, and instead Anselm handed him the truth whole, like a stone, daring him to hold it. It was disorienting. It was, Yara admitted to himself with a lurch of self-disgust, almost impressive. Power recognizing power across a table. Two men who had built careers out of other people's appetites, one with words and one with hunger, sitting in a glass room full of stolen sunlight.

Except one of them had never sent anyone into a fire.

"The tomatoes are good," Anselm said. "Eat one. You're famous for eating."

"I don't taste anymore."

The silence that followed lasted perhaps three seconds, and in it Yara watched something move behind Anselm's eyes — not surprise, he'd known that already, the valley kept records — but calculation. Fast, fluid, the flick of a man reassessing a board. Then the smile reassembled itself.

"Psychogenic, I heard. After the fire." Anselm said the word *fire* the way other men said *weather*. "That's a hard thing. Losing taste. For a man like you." He pushed the plate an inch closer. "Though I sometimes think it must be a kind of freedom. All those years you spent telling people what things tasted like — wasn't that always a fiction anyway? Taste is just memory plus suggestion. You taught me that, actually. That summer. You'd describe the soup and everyone would swear it was the best they'd ever had. It was cabbage and patience, Yara. Cabbage and patience and your voice."

"I was twenty."

"You were brilliant at twenty. You're brilliant now, or you wouldn't be here." Anselm leaned back, laced his fingers over his ruined stomach, and regarded Yara with something that in a kinder man might have been fondness. "So. Let's not waste each other's time. You didn't drive up this mountain for tomatoes."

"No."

"You're writing the true story."

It wasn't a question, and Yara didn't insult either of them by making it one. He took a tomato slice instead, mostly to have something to do with his hands, and held it on his tongue like a coin he couldn't spend. Nothing. Salt-texture, cold flesh, the ghost of a flavor remembered rather than received. Beatrix's tomatoes had tasted like August. These tasted like a photograph of August, printed by a man who owned the negative.

"I'm gathering material," Yara said.

"Mm." Anselm poured olive oil into a saucer, tore more bread, moved pieces on a board only he could see. "Then let me offer you some. Free of charge. Because I like you, and because I'm old enough to enjoy the irony." He chewed, swallowed, wiped his mouth with a linen napkin folded into a perfect triangle. "Whatever you think happened in that house — and I know what you think, the whole valley knows what you think, you've spent thirty years thinking it very loudly in restaurant columns nobody reads closely — you should ask yourself why you're the only one still carrying it."

"People died."

"One person died. Old Marek, whose heart was going anyway, and who would have died that winter in a hospital bed with plastic flowers, which is worse if you ask me." Anselm's voice stayed level, reasonable, the voice of a man explaining interest rates. "And the house burned, and the dream ended, and everyone scattered and built lives. Except you. You built a career on it. Every review you ever wrote, every star you ever awarded or withheld — that was the fire, Yara. You've been litigating it in public for thirty years and calling it criticism. I read your column. It's very good. It's also a confession, if anyone bothered to read it properly. Fortunately for you, nobody does."

The words went in clean, no resistance, the way the worst truths do. Yara sat very still and let them, because flinching would confirm them, and confirming them would cost him the only currency he'd brought up this mountain: the appearance of a man with nothing to hide.

"That's quite a theory," he said.

"It's not a theory. It's a diagnosis. I ran a community for eleven years; I know how guilt metabolizes." Anselm refilled Yara's water without asking, an intimacy performed as dominance. "Now. Here's my material, since you're collecting. One: the fire was electrical. The insurance investigators said so, the council accepted it, and the wiring in that place was a death trap we all complained about for years. That's not even a lie, it's just the boring truth. Two: whoever left the gate open that night let the wind in, and the wind turned a small fire into the end of everything, and I have never known who it was, and I have never cared, because blame is a luxury for people with nothing to build. Three—"

He paused. He picked up a fig, considered it, set it down.

"Three. Beatrix Halloway organized the exodus. She turned the house against me, she planned to strip the place of everything we'd built — the tools, the seed stock, the accounts — and leave the rest of us holding nothing, in October, with winter coming. That's not slander. That's what happened. Ask anyone who was there and hasn't spent thirty years in love with her."

"And you cornered her about it," Yara said quietly. "The night of the fire. In the packing shed."

Something crossed Anselm's face then — fast, quickly filed away, but Yara caught it, and it was not guilt. It was pleasure. The pleasure of a man discovering that his opponent has shown him his hand.

"I talked to her," Anselm said. "She was upset. People get upset. And then there was a fire, and a tragedy, and a young man gave a statement to the police that was frightened and confused and mostly accurate, and life went on." He spread his hands, palms up, a gesture of terrible innocence. "What do you want, Yara? Do you want me to say I'm sorry? Fine. I'm sorry. I'm sorry a girl with good intentions and bad judgment burned down the only home forty people had, and I'm sorry an old man died, and I'm sorry you lost your tongue over it, though between us, I think you lost it long before the fire. But sorry doesn't build anything. I built this." He gestured at the pavilion, the ridge, the serene people cutting herbs below. "Out of the same longing she had. Same material. Different hands."

"You destroyed her."

"I survived her." For the first time, a hairline crack — not in the certainty, but in the patience. Anselm's voice dropped half a tone, and the drop was more revealing than anything he'd said yet. "She was going to take it all apart, Yara. Everything we grew. And you know what? She'd have been right to. It was rotten at the center. I knew it was rotten. I built it rotten, the way you build anything that stands. But I wasn't going to stand in the ruins of my own work while she walked off with the seeds." He stopped. He heard himself. Something flickered — the briefest contraction, a muscle remembering an old wound — and Yara understood that he had just been given, free of charge, the closest thing to a confession this man would ever produce.

*She walked off with the seeds.*

Beatrix's seed library. The drawers, the envelopes, the labels in her handwriting. Seeds saved from the glasshouse, dated the week of the fire. She hadn't stripped the commune. She had carried it out of the flames in paper envelopes, alone, and spent thirty years keeping it alive while this man built a spa on the ashes and called it graduation.

Yara kept his face still. It was the hardest acting of his life, harder than any review, any dinner, any lie. Across the table, Anselm recovered, smoothed the moment flat, and reached for the oil.

"So," Anselm said lightly. "Here's what I imagine happens next. You go away, you write your heritage piece, and it's fair, and it's sad, and it says the commune was beautiful and flawed and the fire was an accident of wiring and wind, and everyone gets to rest. Or—" he smiled again, and this time the smile had teeth in it somewhere, deep, "—you write something else. And then it becomes a matter of lawyers, and archives, and testimony, and poor Odile whatever-her-name-is gets evicted from a condemned building she's been squatting in, and Beatrix Halloway gets to relive the worst year of her life in front of strangers, and you get to find out whether the truth tastes like anything, which — no offense — I doubt."

"You know about Odile."

"I know about everyone. It's my only talent." Anselm rose, joints cracking, an old tree shifting in wind, and looked down at Yara with an expression that was almost tender. Almost. "Lunch is at one. Stay for it if you like. The chef trained in Lyon. He's wasted here, but he's happy, and happy is what we sell." He gathered his newspaper, then paused, and delivered the last line gently, the way you'd set down something fragile:

"You should eat the tomatoes, Yara. They're from her strain. I bought the rights to the seed stock years ago, from a bank in the city. She never claimed them." A beat. "Everything grows here, in the end. Even her."

* * *

Yara walked back down through the lavender with his hands in his pockets and the mountain air scoured clean of scent, as always, as ever, and thought about the chess game he'd just lost without either king being touched.

Because that was what it had been, and he was honest enough — finally, expensively honest — to admit it. Anselm had opened with the truth, disarming him. Had answered accusation with confession-shaped confidence. Had named Yara's own guilt before Yara could deploy it, turning his weapon into evidence against him. And had closed with the tomatoes — *her* tomatoes, bought legally, grown commercially, served as taunt and trophy both — to demonstrate that in Anselm's world, everything, absolutely everything, including Beatrix's thirty years of quiet guardianship, could be converted into property.

Power recognizing power. And power, Yara thought bitterly, winning.

But.

Walking down, he turned the conversation over the way he'd once turned a sauce, looking for what was missing, and found it near the bottom: Anselm had never once asked what Yara knew. Not one question. A man truly confident in his position asks questions; a man managing a threat gives speeches. Anselm hadn't wanted information. Anselm had wanted to control the record before it was written — which meant there was a record to control. Which meant there was something in the fire's true account that the electrical report and the insurance file and the police statement did not cover.

Something Anselm had done, or failed to do, or seen done, that thirty years of prosperity had never managed to bury deep enough to stop him checking the ground above it.

At the car, Yara paused and looked back up at Solstice Rise, pale timber and obedient gardens, the glass pavilion glinting among the pines like a shard of the old house embedded in the mountainside, working its way slowly toward the surface.

He got in, started the engine, and drove down toward the valley, toward the fence, toward the derelict glasshouse and the woman living inside it who had catalogued every surviving plant for years — and who, he realized with a cold, rising certainty, had been inside that potting shed far longer than he had, and knew far more about what was hidden there than he did.

He needed to see Odile. Tonight. Before Anselm's people did.
