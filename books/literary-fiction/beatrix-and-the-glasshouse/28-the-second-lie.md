## 28. The Second Lie

The hotel in the valley town had been a coaching inn once, and it kept the habits of its former life: narrow stairs, a desk by the window positioned as if for someone waiting for a coach that would never come, water pipes that knocked twice before surrendering their heat. Yara set up his laptop on that desk because it was the only surface in the room that felt like work. He opened a blank document at nine in the morning, and by nine he had already written the first sentence in his head, polished it, and hated it.

*Anselm Vane does not so much enter a room as arrive in it.*

No. Too flattering by half, and flattering in the way Anselm would notice — the way of a man who had spent thirty years being described by people who wanted something from him. Anselm would read that sentence and know, instantly, that the man writing it had already surrendered. Yara deleted it before it existed and started again.

He was good at this. That was the terrible thing, the thing he kept circling like a tongue probing a broken tooth: he was *good* at this. Thirty years of restaurant criticism had trained him to find the flattering angle on any subject, to locate the one detail that would make a reader lean in, to build an edifice of praise out of load-bearing specifics. A beet could become a meditation on patience. A chef's cruelty to his staff could be reframed as rigor. He had never once, in three decades, published a sentence he could not defend, and he had defended sentences that deserved no defense at all.

The profile of Anselm Vane was due in nine days. The retreat — Solstice House, it was called, a converted farmstead with a cold-plunge pool and a yurt — would be photographed by a professional who made everything look like forgiveness. Yara's column would run beneath the photographs like a blessing. And somewhere beneath the blessing, folded into the architecture of the piece so carefully that no editor would ever find it, would go the sentence that ended the other story: the fire, the commune, the blame, sealed and signed by the only surviving witness with a public name.

He had told himself, on the drive back from Solstice House, that this was strategy. That publishing the profile was the price of access, and access was the price of the truth, and the truth could wait nine days — no, could *arrive* in nine days, delivered by his own hand, once Anselm's guard was down and the litigation threat had dissolved into a signed-off feature. A lie now to buy a truth later. An investment. He had used the word *investment* to himself, standing in the parking lot, and had almost believed it.

He typed: *The road to Solstice House climbs through orchards that have been giving up their fruit to the same families for a century.*

True, at least. He had driven that road. The orchards were real. He had watched an old man shaking down windfall pears into a bucket, and the sound of the pears hitting the bucket's tin bottom had been so exactly the sound of August, of 1999, of the glasshouse kitchen where Beatrix dropped tomatoes into a colander with the same dull percussion, that Yara had pulled over and sat with his hands on the wheel until his breathing normalized.

That was the thing about the valley. It kept doing that. It kept handing him the past in pieces, disguised as landscape.

* * *

He worked through the morning the way he worked through a tasting menu he couldn't taste: with precision, with rhythm, with the hollow competence that had been his signature for thirty years. By noon he had twelve hundred words. The retreat's history — Anselm had bought the farmstead in 2004, after a decade of "consulting work" whose nature Yara did not investigate because investigating it was not in the contract. Anselm's philosophy, delivered on a walk through the herb garden: *the body keeps the score, but it also keeps the receipts, and my work is helping people pay what they owe.* Yara had written that down at the time because it was quotable, and only later, typing it up, heard what was inside it — a man who had spent his whole life collecting debts from other people.

*Anselm Vane's genius is that he has never confused kindness with softness.*

There it was again, that word. *Genius.* He stared at it. In the old days he would have left it. In the old days he had left words like that in a hundred pieces about a hundred people, and some of those people had deserved it and some had been monsters with good plating, and he had never once let the distinction slow his hand.

He got up and made coffee he couldn't taste. The hotel's coffee was the color of varnish and probably tasted of it, and the absence of that knowledge — the simple, ordinary fact of not knowing what his own mouth was doing — sat in his chest like a stone he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for an organ. He drank half the cup standing at the window, looking down at the street, where a woman was sweeping her doorstep in long, unhurried strokes, and he thought: *she can taste her coffee. She will never think about it once in her life.* He envied her with an envy so pure it was almost restful.

Then he sat back down, and the lie waited for him where he'd left it, patient as a dog.

* * *

The seed labels were in his jacket pocket. He had carried them out of Beatrix's library four days ago without asking — had palmed the envelope the way he'd once palmed sugar sachets in restaurants, a critic's small theft, and had told himself he would return them. He hadn't returned them. He had told himself he would stop carrying them. He hadn't stopped carrying them. They rode in the inside pocket, over his heart, a small paper weight, and every day he told himself the same three things about them: that they were evidence, that they were evidence of nothing, and that he was not ready to know which.

He took them out now without deciding to. His hand simply went to the pocket, the way a hand goes to a wound.

The envelope was soft at the corners from handling. On the front, in Beatrix's handwriting — he had forgotten, or had pretended to forget, how particular her handwriting was, the way she crossed her t's with a single impatient stroke that sometimes overshot into the next word — she had written:

*Glasshouse tomatoes — saved Aug. '99. Keep dry. Keep faith.*

And below, in smaller letters, as if the second thought had cost her more: *For whoever comes after.*

The week of the fire. She had been planning her escape, organizing the others, carrying the weight of a dozen people's freedom in her hands — and somewhere in that same week she had stood in the glasshouse, in the heat, and saved the seeds. Had labeled them. Had thought about *whoever came after*, about a future she could not see, in a place she already knew she might not survive leaving. While Yara was — while Yara was doing what he had done. Whatever it was he had done. He still could not write the sentence even in his own head; it arrived always as a shape, a silhouette at a doorway, Anselm's voice saying *you'll tell me, or you'll be on the wrong side of that door when it happens.*

He had been on the wrong side of the door anyway. That was the joke. That was the thirty-year joke.

He set the envelope on the desk beside the laptop, and the screen's glow fell across it, and the handwriting caught the light, and he looked at the words *keep faith* for a long time.

* * *

The strange thing — the thing he would remember afterward, the thing that finally made him understand that the body keeps accounts the mind refuses to audit — was that his hands stopped working before his mind did.

He was typing. He had twelve hundred words and a deadline and a structure, and he was typing the section about the cold plunge, the section where he would describe the shock of cold water as a kind of honesty, a phrase Anselm had fed him and Yara had swallowed whole — and his fingers slowed. Not stopped; slowed, the way an engine slows when it's running out of something. He typed a sentence and it came out wrong. He typed it again and it came out wronger. He looked at what he'd written and it was fluent, it was publishable, it was exactly the kind of sentence he had built a life out of, and reading it was like reading a menu.

That was how he thought of it, in that moment: *a menu.* Descriptions of food for people who would never eat it. Descriptions of a man for readers who would never meet him. He had spent thirty years writing menus, and he had told himself it was literature, and the truth was that he had been translating everything into a language with no taste in it because he no longer had any, and he had made the whole world over in the image of his own mouth.

He pushed back from the desk. The chair complained. The window showed the street, the sweeping woman, the ordinary afternoon, and he stood there with his hands hanging at his sides and noticed — really noticed, for the first time — that his stomach was clenched. Not metaphorically. Physically, a fist of muscle under his ribs, and his jaw ached from clenching, and there was a sourness at the back of his throat that he could not taste but could *feel*, the body's memory of taste persisting like a phantom limb.

His body was refusing. That was the only word for it. His body had heard the terms of the bargain — the profile for the silence, the lie now for the truth later, the same trade he had made at nineteen with better posture — and his body had voted no. His hands had gone on strike. His stomach had filed its objection. Somewhere below language, in the old animal rooms of him, something knew what he was doing and would not co-sign it.

He sat back down. He typed: *There is a honesty in cold water that—* and deleted it, and typed: *Anselm believes—* and stopped, because Anselm believed nothing; Anselm *deployed* belief the way he deployed other people's confessions, and Yara knew this, had always known it, had known it in 1999 when the food rations tightened and the dissenters stopped being invited to dinner and everyone smiled harder to prove they weren't afraid — and he had smiled. He had smiled and cooked and kept his head down and told himself he was keeping the peace, when what he was keeping was his own skin, and when the moment came when keeping his skin and keeping Beatrix were finally, irreconcilably different things, he had chosen, and he had chosen the way he was choosing now: fluently, professionally, with a first draft already forming.

*No,* his body said. The sourness rose. He swallowed it down and it came back up, patient, insistent, the body's one-word argument repeated like a wave against a seawall.

* * *

He tried to work around it. He was a professional; professionals work around things. He skipped the cold plunge section and went to the quotes, the good ones, the ones that would carry the piece — and found that every quote now read to him as a confession. *People arrive carrying a story about themselves,* Anselm had said, *and my work is helping them put that story down.* Thirty years ago Anselm had helped a whole commune put down its story, one rationed meal at a time, and had helped Yara put down his most thoroughly of all, had taken the boy's fear and shaped it into testimony, and the testimony had become the official story, and the official story had become Yara's career, and here was the architect of all of it, quoted warmly in Yara's own draft, being handed a fresh generation of bodies to work with.

*He has built a place where people can finally tell the truth about themselves.*

Yara read that sentence four times. It was the centerpiece. It was the sentence the whole profile orbited. And it was, he understood now, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like a bone setting — it was the most obscene sentence he had ever written, because Anselm had built exactly one such place, once, and it had been made of fear and hunger and a glasshouse that burned, and the truth about *Yara* had been the first thing to die in it.

His hands were shaking. Not metaphorically. He watched them, with a strange detachment, the way he'd once watched a chef's hands tremble after service — adrenaline with nowhere to go. He pressed them flat against the desk and the shaking transferred to the desk, to the envelope, and the envelope shivered in the laptop's glow, and *keep faith* shivered with it.

He thought about what the profile would do. Not abstractly — specifically. It would run on a Thursday. By Friday it would be excerpted. By the following week it would be the thing people cited, the definitive word, the seal. Beatrix had spent thirty years as the commune's designated villain — he knew that now, had heard it in the town's careful silences, in the way the old neighbors said her name like a coin they were afraid to spend — and this profile would re-mint Anselm, would launder him, would hand him thirty more years of authority with Yara's byline as the certificate of authenticity. And Beatrix would read it. She would be in her seed library or her small house above it, and someone would send it to her, or she would see it herself, because you always see the thing that's coming for you, and she would read Yara Voss's warm, fluent, masterful profile of the man who broke everything, and she would know exactly what it meant. She would know it was the second lie. She would know he had chosen again.

*Keep faith,* she had written, in the week of the fire, to whoever came after.

He was the one who came after. He was the only one who came after.

Yara closed the laptop. Not dramatically — he pressed the keys the way you close a door on a room you're not done with but can't stay in. The screen went dark and the desk was just a desk and the profile was just twelve hundred and forty words sitting in the machine's dark memory, unfinished, unsendable, alive.

He picked up the envelope. He held it in both hands, the way you hold something that might be a relic or might be a verdict. The paper was warm from his pocket. Through the thin paper he could feel the seeds themselves, tiny and hard, each one a small dense fact: *these existed, she saved them, the fire did not get everything.*

He had thought the choice would feel like a choice. That was the naivety he was only now, at fifty, beginning to see through — that he'd imagined some dramatic moment, a fork, a devil at a crossroads. But it wasn't like that. It was like this: a hotel desk, a cold coffee, a draft on a screen, an envelope in his hands. The choice was being made right now, in the ordinary machinery of his body, and his body had already made it. His hands had refused. His stomach had refused. The sour wave rose again and this time he let it, let his throat work around it, and the feeling that came with it — bitter, mineral, unmistakable, *there* — was the closest thing to taste he had felt in thirty years.

His body, it seemed, was willing to taste one thing: the cost.

He sat down again. He opened the laptop. The cursor blinked in the dark of the unfinished profile, patient as Anselm, patient as the past.

And then someone knocked on the hotel room door — twice, firmly, the same rhythm as the water pipes — and through the wood came a voice he did not know, a young woman's voice, low and even, saying:

"Mr. Voss. I know what you're writing. I think we should talk about the night of the fire."
