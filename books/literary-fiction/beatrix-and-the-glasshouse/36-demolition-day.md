## 36. Demolition Day

The machines came at dawn, the way ruin always does — punctually, and without malice.

Yara had imagined them for weeks: yellow, patient, enormous, their hydraulic arms folded like sleeping insects on the flatbeds beyond the gate. But nothing in his imagination had prepared him for how quiet they were before they started. The excavators sat in the grey light with their engines off, and the valley held its breath around them, and for a few minutes it was possible to believe that nothing was going to happen at all — that the whole apparatus of destruction had been towed up the mountain road by mistake.

He stood at the fence with his hands through the chain-link, palms flat against the cold wire, as if he could hold the glasshouse back by touch alone. Behind him, gravel crunched. He didn't turn. He knew both sets of footsteps now — Odile's quick and uneven, favoring her left knee after thirty years of hauling water; Beatrix's slower, deliberate, the walk of a woman who had learned to arrive places without hurrying toward anything.

They flanked him without a word. Three pairs of hands on one fence. It would have looked, from the road, like a photograph of protest. It was not that. It was something older and stranger — a vigil, or a farewell, or simply three people keeping an appointment with a building that had made them and unmade them and was now, at last, being permitted to die.

"You're early," Odile said.

"So are you."

"I didn't sleep." She said it without apology, the way she said everything. "I kept thinking I'd hear glass breaking in the night. Wind, probably. Or foxes. But I lay there listening to this place breathe, and I thought — well. If it's going to go out, I want to be awake for it."

Beatrix said nothing. Yara glanced sideways at her. She was dressed as she had been at the hearing, in the same olive coat, but today she wore work gloves pushed through her belt and a canvas satchel across her body, and her hair was tied back with what he recognized — the recognition arriving in his chest like a swallowed stone — as a strip of blue cotton. The same kind of cloth she'd used to tie back her hair in the summer of 1999, when she worked the tomato rows. She had kept something. Of course she had kept something. She had spent thirty years keeping things while the rest of the world threw them away.

The first engine turned over at 6:47. It coughed, caught, settled into a low diesel growl, and the sound went through the fence and through Yara's hands and into whatever part of him still lived in that building. A man in a high-visibility vest climbed down from the lead machine's cab, walked to the gate, and unlocked it with a key on a lanyard, and the ordinariness of it — the *bureaucracy* of it, the padlock swinging open like any padlock on any morning anywhere — was somehow worse than fire would have been.

"They'll start with the palm room," Odile said quietly. She had told them this the night before, over thermos coffee in the potting shed, laying it out like a triage plan. "It's the structural anchor. Once that spine comes down, the rest goes fast. We have maybe two hours of salvage window before it's unsafe to be inside."

"Two hours," Beatrix repeated.

"For thirty years of a building." Odile shrugged. "That's generous, actually. Most places get less."

---

They went in together through the gap in the north wall where the fencing had been cut for the surveyors, and the glasshouse received them the way it had always received people: with a wash of green smell and filtered light and the faint, mineral coolness of air that has passed through leaves. Even condemned, even half-empty, even stripped of its boiler and its benches and most of its glazing, the place still breathed. Yara felt it against his face like a hand.

The palm room stood at the center, its iron ribs rising four stories into a roof mostly open to the sky now, and in the grey morning light the remaining fronds looked less like plants than like the raised arms of figures in an old relief, frozen mid-surrender or mid-praise, impossible to say which.

"We split up," Odile said, all business, pulling her list from her coat. "I've marked priorities. The archive crates are already out — those went to the library van yesterday. What's left is what can be carried. Seeds, cuttings, tools, documents. Anything metal worth keeping. And—" she hesitated, and Yara saw her look at Beatrix, and saw Beatrix give the smallest nod, "—the stove."

"The stove," Yara said.

"It's cast iron. Two hundred kilos if it's an ounce. There's a trolley in the fern house." Odile's voice roughened slightly. "It's the only thing in this building that was ever warm on purpose. I'm not leaving it for scrap."

No one argued. They separated into the green dimness, and the machines outside began, very softly at first, to test their arms.

---

Yara worked the west corridor, where the tomato beds had been. The beds themselves were long gone — rotted boards, stolen timber — but the soil remained, dark and stubborn, and pushing up through it, absurdly, defiantly, were the volunteers. Self-seeded tomatoes, descendants of the 1999 crop gone feral, small-fruited and bitter-skinned, the kind of plant that survives precisely because no one wants it enough to cultivate it. He knelt among them with a trowel and lifted them one by one, balling the roots in damp newspaper as Beatrix had taught him when he was nineteen and knew nothing, her hands over his, saying *firm but gentle, firm but gentle, you're not burying it, you're introducing it to its new home.*

He had forgotten, until this moment, that she had ever touched his hands.

He worked until his knees ached and his arms were full of wrapped seedlings, and then he carried them out to the staging area by the gate, where Odile had laid out a system of crates and labels with the precision of a field botanist, and where Beatrix was standing before the great iron stove with her gloves on, studying it the way one studies a face in an old photograph.

"It's smaller than I remember," she said, without turning.

"Everything is."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Not everything. Some things are exactly the size they were. We just built rooms around them so we wouldn't have to stand next to them." She put her hand flat on the stove's cold door. "This is exactly the size it was."

Outside, the first excavator rolled through the gate. The ground shuddered underfoot, a long low tremor that traveled up through the soles of Yara's shoes and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and every bird that had been nesting in the palm room's broken roof rose at once, a dark exhalation against the brightening sky.

---

They worked through the morning in a rhythm none of them named but all of them obeyed: carry, wrap, label, carry again. The machines took the east wing first. From inside the west corridor Yara heard it happen — the groan of iron, the long crystalline roar of glass surrendering all at once, a sound like a chandelier the size of a cathedral being dropped — and he did not stop working. None of them did. That was the strange grace of the day, he thought later: there was no time to stand and mourn, because mourning was a luxury of the idle, and they were not idle. They were a salvage crew. Grief would have to wait its turn behind the wheelbarrows.

Odile directed everything, tireless, her list growing shorter, her voice growing hoarser. She carried the last of the fern house's stock — maidenhairs and hart's-tongues, rescued from a decade of her quiet illegal watering — and set each pot in the crates with the tenderness of a woman moving children out of a burning house, which, in every sense that mattered, she was.

It was Beatrix who found the tin.

Yara heard her make a sound — not a word, not quite a cry, something between a breath and a bell — and he came around the corner of the potting shed's ruined annex and found her kneeling in the rubble of a collapsed shelf, holding a rusted biscuit tin with both hands as though it might still detonate.

"What is it?"

She didn't answer. She pried the lid, which came away with a shriek of oxide, and inside, nested in paper gone soft as cloth, were jars. Small preserving jars, sealed with wax, labeled in a handwriting Yara knew better than his own — the quick forward-leaning script, the crossed sevens. Seed packets. Dozens of them. Tomatoes, basil, climbing beans, marigolds, the old commune varieties, saved and sealed and hidden here in the last week before the fire, dated in her hand: *August 1999.*

"I hid these," Beatrix said. Her voice was very steady, which is how he knew. "The night before. I buried them under the floor of the potting shed and I told myself I'd come back for them, and then—" She stopped. The machines roared somewhere behind them, taking another rib of the roof. "Then I never came back. Thirty years, and I built my whole life on seeds I carried out in my pockets, and I forgot — no. Not forgot. I *chose* not to remember that these were here, because remembering meant coming back, and coming back meant—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Yara knelt beside her in the dust, and did not touch her, and did not speak, because there are moments when the only honest thing a person can offer is his silence, offered fully.

It was Odile who finally broke it, appearing with the trolley, practical as sunrise. "Stove," she said gently. "If we're doing it, we do it now."

---

Moving the stove took all three of them and nearly an hour. They levered it onto the trolley with crowbars and planks, inch by inch, the iron groaning, and wheeled it out through the north gap in a procession that felt, absurdly and undeniably, like a funeral procession — three mourners and the coffin of warmth itself. When they set it down beyond the fence, in the grass by the road, Beatrix laid her palm on it once more and left it there a long moment, and then she straightened, and untied the blue cloth from her hair, and let the wind take her hair loose, and said, "All right. Let them have it."

The palm room came down at noon.

They stood together outside the fence to watch, because Odile had forbidden anyone to be inside past eleven, and because some things should be witnessed from a respectful distance, the way eclipses are. The excavator's arm rose against the white sky, paused at the top of its arc with a terrible patience, and then fell, and the great iron spine of the palm room buckled inward with a sound Yara felt in his teeth and his chest and the backs of his eyes — a sound like the building exhaling thirty years in a single breath. Glass went everywhere and nowhere. Dust rose in a slow gold column, catching the midday sun, and hung over the valley like a curtain being drawn.

Beatrix did not cry. Odile did not cry. Yara discovered that he was not crying either, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that this was not numbness. This was what it felt like when grief and gratitude arrive at the same moment and agree, for once, to share the room.

"It was a good building," Odile said at last. "It kept things alive that had no business staying alive. Including us."

By mid-afternoon the glasshouse was a skeleton, and by late afternoon it was a suggestion, and by the time the light went long and amber across the valley it was a memory with edges — ribs of iron jutting from heaps of glass and timber, the volunteers' green shoots already looking, impossibly, at home in the wreckage.

They made the last trips together, carrying out what remained: the crates of seedlings, the tins of seeds, Odile's tools, a single intact pane of glass that Beatrix insisted on taking — "for the light," she said, and would not explain further. The workers in their vests watched them with the neutral courtesy of men who demolish things for a living and have learned not to ask why other people grieve them.

At the gate, as the last machine fell silent and the foreman began coiling cables, Beatrix stopped and turned back once. Yara watched her look — not at the ruins, but at the space above them, the exact height and shape of where the roof had been, as if she could still see it there, printed on the evening air.

"Well," she said. "That's that."

And Yara opened his mouth to answer — to say something worthy, something final, something with the weight the moment deserved — and found that he had no words at all. Only a dryness in his throat, and a strange tightness across his tongue, and above the valley, far to the west, a bank of cloud the color of bruised slate, gathering.

The wind changed. It came down off the hills carrying dust and diesel and, beneath them, unmistakably, the smell of rain.

*Something is coming,* he thought, and could not have said whether he dreaded it or had been waiting for it all his life.
