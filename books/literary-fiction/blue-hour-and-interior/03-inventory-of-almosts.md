## 3. Inventory of Almosts

The shoebox lived on the top shelf of Mirabel's bedroom closet, behind a box of winter gloves he had not worn since he moved to the city, behind a stack of tax folders he kept out of a superstition he could not name. It was a shoebox in the most literal sense—brown cardboard, the lid softened at the corners, *MARTINO & SONS — FINE LEATHER* stamped in gold that had mostly rubbed away—and it had traveled with him through three apartments the way other men traveled with photographs of their children.

He took it down on the first Sunday of the month, which was also the day he mailed the postcard, though the two rituals had never been formally connected. The postcard went out into the world; the drafts stayed in. One was a letter with an address. The other was a letter with no address at all, no stamp, no hope of arrival—just the sentences he could not make almost-true enough to survive the walk to the mailbox.

Tonight he sat on the floor of his bedroom with the box between his knees and the lamp angled low, because there was a part of him that did this work the way a man visits a grave: respectfully, quietly, without wanting to be seen doing it.

Twelve years of drafts. He had never counted them before. He counted them now, the way you count the stairs of a house you grew up in, not because you don't know the number but because the counting is itself a form of return.

Sixty-one. Sixty-one unsent letters, most of them on index cards, some on the backs of receipts, two on hotel stationery from a conference in Atlanta where he had gotten drunk enough to write three pages and sober enough, by morning, to fold them into the box instead of the mailbox.

He lifted the first one out. The ink had gone the brownish color of old blood, or of tea left standing. His own handwriting at twenty-four was a stranger's handwriting—looser, the letters leaning as if walking into wind.

*I should have said it was my fault. I should have said it before the smoke. Emeka, I'm sorry about the piano, I'm sorry about the money, I'm sorry about the look on your face when I—*

The sentence stopped there. It had stopped there for twelve years. He knew the rest of it by heart; he simply had never been able to write it down, as though writing it would make it more true than it already was, and it was already true past all bearing.

He set it on the floor beside him and took up the next.

*They say the house is gone. I keep driving past the lot. I don't know why. I guess I thought grief would have a shape I could recognize from the road.*

* * *

The argument had been about a piano.

That was the sentence Mirabel gave people, in the rare moments anyone asked, because it had the virtue of being true and the mercy of being small. A piano. As if the whole catastrophe could be contained in an object you could put in a truck and drive away.

It had been their mother's piano, or it had been nobody's piano, or it had been Emeka's piano—depending on which of them you asked, and on which of them was still speaking to you. It had come with Adaeze from Enugu, shipped across the ocean in a crate lined with palm fronds, and it had sat in the front room of the house on Delmore Street for twenty years, going slightly out of tune, gathering the particular dust of a room no one sat in. Emeka played it. That was the whole of the trouble. Emeka played it every evening, badly at first and then, over the years, with a fluency that seemed to embarrass him, so that he only played when he thought the house was empty, and the family learned to be empty around him without saying so.

Mirabel had come home that October with a plan. He was twenty-four and full of plans the way a boy is full of hunger—indiscriminately, painfully. He had gotten a promotion at the freight company, a real one, with a salary, and he had decided the family needed to sell the piano. Not because they needed the money. Because the money would mean the house could be repainted, the porch repaired, the family lifted by one visible increment out of the neighborhood's slow decline, and because—and this was the part he could not say then and could barely say now—he was tired of a house organized around a silence. Tired of tiptoeing past the front room at seven o'clock every evening. Tired of loving a brother through a closed door.

*You want to sell Mama's piano,* Emeka had said, standing in the doorway of the front room with his hands in his pockets, in the tone of a man confirming the terms of a bet he already knew he'd lost.

*I want to sell a piano. It's a piano, Emeka. It's out of tune. Nobody plays it.*

*You don't know who plays it.*

*I know who plays it.*

And Emeka had looked at him then—Mirabel could still summon the look, could still feel it land on his chest the way a thrown ball lands, with weight and intention—and said, quietly, in the voice he saved for the truest things, *You know, and you'd sell it anyway. That's the difference between us, Mirabel. You'd sell it anyway.*

The argument had gone on for two hours. It had stopped being about the piano within the first ten minutes, the way arguments between brothers do; it had become about everything that had never been said in that house, all the almosts, all the tenderness that had been routed through silence for so long that silence was the only language either of them spoke fluently. Emeka had said that Mirabel was the son who got to want things out loud. Mirabel had said that Emeka was the son who got to be loved without earning it. Both of them had meant the opposite of what they said. Both of them had known it. Neither of them had stopped.

It was Mirabel who had knocked over the lamp. It was Emeka who had left the house, slamming the front door hard enough to rattle the piano strings, a sound like a struck chord, a sound Mirabel would hear for the rest of his life at the bottom of every quiet night.

The fire started three hours later. Electrical, the report said. The old wiring in the walls, the wiring their mother had been asking their father—before he left, before he became a name—to fix for a decade. The fire had nothing to do with the argument. The fire had nothing to do with the piano. Mirabel had spent twelve years knowing this and not believing it, the way you know the earth goes around the sun and still feel, every morning, that it rises for you.

* * *

He worked through the box in order, the way he always did, because the drafts were a calendar. Each one was dated on the back, in the corner, small. Month by month, year by year, the archive of a man learning to say almost-true things and then not sending them.

*Dear Emeka. The lot where the house was has weeds now, tall ones. I stood there for an hour today and a woman asked me if I was waiting for someone. I said no. I don't know why I said no. I was waiting for someone.*

*Dear Emeka. I sold the piano after all. I'm sorry. I sold it to a church in Linden and I went to see it there once and a girl was playing it, badly, and I stood in the back and cried like something out of a bad novel. You would have laughed at me. I wish you would laugh at me.*

*Dear Emeka. Mama made your favorite on your birthday. She didn't say your name. Nobody said your name. We ate it anyway. It tasted like a door.*

He had gotten better at them, over the years. That was the terrible thing he had never admitted to anyone: the drafts got better. The early ones were all wound and accusation and apology tangled together, sentences that broke off mid-clause like the man writing them had been interrupted by his own grief. The later ones were calmer. Almost-true. He had learned to write the way the postcards read—the postcards he actually mailed, the few sentences a month, tender and oblique, *The maples on Delmore are turning. I got the promotion. I still play the song sometimes but I can never get the bridge right.*

The bridge. He stopped with an index card halfway to the box and sat with that for a moment.

They had written a song together, the two of them, the summer before the fire. Emeka at the piano, Mirabel with a notebook, because Emeka could play anything but could never finish anything, and Mirabel couldn't play at all but could always see the shape of an ending before it arrived. They had worked on it for a month in the front room, door open for once, and it had a verse and a chorus and a bridge that Emeka kept changing every time he played it, and they had never written the last verse. *We'll know it when we get there,* Emeka said. *A song should end at the place it was always going.* They never got there. The song had no ending because the house had no future, and after the fire Mirabel could not play it, could not even hum it, because the bridge lived only in his hands' memory of Emeka's hands, and there was no instrument in his apartment, and he had never once, in twelve years, gone looking for a piano.

He filed that thought away the way you file away a sound you heard at night—not examining it, just noting that the house had made a noise.

* * *

Near the bottom of the box were the two from Atlanta, the hotel stationery, three pages in a handwriting gone loose with drink. He almost didn't read them. He read them.

*Emeka. I got drunk tonight and I want to tell you the thing whole, so here it is. The night of the fire I wasn't thinking about the piano. I was thinking that if the piano was gone you would have to talk to us. You would have to come out of that room and be in the family with the rest of us. I wanted to break your door down and selling the piano was the crowbar. That's what I meant. That's the thing whole. And then the house burned and you were in it and I have spent every day since thinking that I got exactly what I wanted, a family with no silence in it, because there was no one left to be silent about.*

He had woken the next morning in the hotel, read it over twice, and put it in the box. Not because it was false. Because it was true, and he had not yet earned the right to say it, and some part of him had understood—even then, even at his worst—that a truth said before its time is just another kind of violence.

He wondered, not for the first time, whether the postcards were the same thing. Twelve years of almost-true sentences, mailed monthly to a burned lot, tender and useless. Was that penance or was it avoidance? Was he practicing the apology or rehearsing his way around it?

The lamp buzzed. Outside, the city did its night business, a siren unwinding somewhere in the middle distance, a neighbor's television murmuring through the wall. Mirabel sat on the floor of his ordered life with sixty-one unsent letters spread around him like the pieces of a game whose rules he'd forgotten, and he understood, suddenly, with the clarity that comes only when you are very tired, that the box was not an archive. It was a room. It was the front room of the house on Delmore Street, rebuilt in cardboard, and he had been walking past it once a month for twelve years, and he had never once gone in and finished the song.

*Dear Emeka,* he wrote, on the sixty-secondth card, the pen moving before he decided anything. *I heard something today I need to ask you about, except there is no one to ask, and I am beginning to suspect that this has been arranged.*

He looked at what he had written. He had not meant to write it. It had come out of the pen the way the truth comes out of the drunk—sideways, unwilled, wearing its nightclothes.

Because here was the thing he had been circling all evening without landing: the drafts had never been answered. Of course they hadn't; they were unsent; they lived in a shoebox. But neither had the postcards been answered, the sixty-odd postcards mailed to a lot where nothing stood, and he had always told himself that was the nature of the arrangement—you write to the dead because the dead cannot complicate things—and yet tonight, holding the box, he found himself counting backward through the years and noticing what he had never noticed before: that his mother had never once asked him what he wrote on those postcards. Not once in twelve years. Not *what do you say to him, Mirabel?* Not *do you think it helps?* Adaeze, who asked after every cousin's fever and every neighbor's garden, who interrogated the weather, had never asked about the postcards, as though she already knew what was in them. As though the contents had been, in some sense, confirmed to her.

He sat with that the way you sit with cold air coming in under a door—first noticing the draft, then noticing that the door is supposed to be shut, then noticing that someone must have opened it.

He put the cards back in the box in order. He put the box on the shelf. He did not sleep well, and in the morning, standing in his kitchen with the phone in his hand, he dialed the number he had dialed every Sunday for twelve years, and this time, for the first time, he was calling to find something out.

"Hello?" His mother's voice, warm, unhurried, already reaching for the kettle he could hear in the pause.

"Mama. It's me." He looked out the window at the ordinary street. "I have a strange question."

"Ah-ah. Strange questions before breakfast. What is it?"

He opened his mouth. And found, standing at the threshold of it, that he did not yet know how to ask a woman who answered questions with tea.
