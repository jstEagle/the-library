## 2. Boxes Without Labels

The boxes lived in the loft, and the loft lived above the house the way a held breath lives above a body.

Lena found them on the fourth morning, when the numbness had settled into something almost comfortable, almost useful. She had developed a rhythm by then: coffee at seven, inventory ledger open at eight, room by room, item by item, her handwriting small and neutral in the columns. *Mahogany hall stand, water damage to base. Set of six dining chairs, two with loose joints. Books, assorted, condition poor.* She was good at this. She had been good at this since the funeral, since the moment she'd taken the clipboard from the funeral director's assistant and discovered that grief, reduced to a list, could be carried.

The loft ladder was aluminium and complained under her weight. She went up with a torch in her teeth and the ledger under her arm because even here, even now, she was working. That was the arrangement she had made with herself: she was not mourning, she was *administering*. The house was an estate. Her mother was a line item. Everything could be survived if it was written down.

The loft was low and hot and smelled of insulation fibres and forty years of dust that had once been the house's breath. Her torch found them against the far gable, where the roof sloped low, stacked with a precision that startled her: cardboard boxes, the old brown kind, their flaps tucked in rather than taped, and on every one, in her mother's upright hand, a single notation.

*1974.*

*1979.*

*1981.*

*1983.*

*1988.*

Nothing else. No *kitchen*, no *winter clothes*, no *Lena — school things*. Just years, the way a hospital chart records dates, the way a prison ledger does. Seven boxes in all, and the years stopped in 1988, which Lena noted with the detached part of her mind the way you note a temperature. 1988 was the year the photographs in the hallway stopped being black and white. 1988 was the year her mother, according to nothing Lena could name, had seemed to fold slightly, like a letter returned to its envelope.

She should have carried them down one at a time and added them to the ledger. *Cardboard boxes, contents unknown, seven.* That was the protocol. That was the whole point of the protocol.

Instead she sat down on a joist with her skirt dusted grey and opened the 1974 box where she sat, because her legs had decided before she had.

The flaps came away with a sound like a sigh held for fifty years.

Inside, on top, was a photograph.

Her mother — young, so young, younger than Lena was now — was laughing. Not smiling. *Laughing*, head tipped back, one hand caught mid-gesture as if she'd just said something outrageous and was waiting to see who'd dare answer. She was standing outside a building Lena didn't recognise, a low brick hall with tall windows, and around her were strangers: a tall man in a brown suit with his arm half-raised in answer to whatever she'd said, a woman in a patterned headwrap with a baby on her hip, two or three others caught at the edges, blurred with motion, mid-laugh themselves. Everyone in the photograph was laughing. Everyone except the camera, which had caught them at the exact moment when laughter is still happening, not yet remembered.

Lena had never seen her mother laugh like that. She searched the woman's face for the mother she knew — the careful, contained woman who pressed her lips together at jokes that went too far, who laughed the way you sign a document, briefly and in the required place — and could not find her. The woman in the photograph was someone else. Someone with a whole face.

She turned it over. On the back, in pencil, in that same upright hand: *Blue hour, hall steps.* No names. No year. As if the people in it were so obvious they needed no naming, and the only thing worth recording was the light.

Lena put the photograph face-down on her knee, carefully, the way you put down something sleeping, and looked deeper into the box.

Cassette tapes. A dozen of them, maybe more, in their cracked plastic cases, each labelled in the same hand but this time with writing she couldn't read. Not handwriting she couldn't read — *writing*. The labels were covered in marks that were almost letters, letters that had been dreamed by someone who had only had letters described to them: some with dots above, some with marks beneath like little accents of impatience, some that were just lines with the confidence of a signature. Underneath the unfamiliar script, in smaller English, her mother had written numbers. *Lesson 1. Lesson 2. Lesson 3.* The numbers went on past the box, and Lena understood with a small cold drop in her stomach that the tapes in the other boxes would continue them, that this was a sequence, a curriculum, that her mother had been — what? *Taught* something. For years. By someone.

She lifted one tape out and held it to the torchlight. Through the little window she could see the brown ribbon wound tight, patient, holding whatever voice it held. Her mother's voice, presumably. Her mother's voice at twenty-six or twenty-seven, saying things Lena would not understand, in a language Lena could not name, to a person Lena could not see.

She had known her mother for forty-one years. She could not name the language.

* * *

Her brother rang at noon, as he rang every day now, his voice arriving down the line with the briskness of a man who had already solved the problem.

"Progress?"

"I'm in the loft."

"The loft." A pause in which she could hear him recalculating, the way he recalculated everything, converting her days into his schedule. "Lena, listen. Don't go through it up there. Just get a skip. One skip, maybe two. Everything that isn't furniture goes straight in, and you've saved yourself a fortnight. Mum was a hoarder of the sentimental kind, you know she was. Every birthday card since 1962. You'll drown in it."

"She labelled them," Lena said. "The boxes. Just years."

"Years?"

"1974. 1979. Up to '88. Nothing else."

Another pause. When he spoke again his voice had the careful lightness he used when approaching anything that might have feeling in it, the way you'd approach a wasp. "Well. That's Mum, isn't it. She never could just say what a thing was." He laughed, a short administrative laugh. "Skip, Lena. I'll ring the hire place this afternoon, they can drop it Monday. You'll feel better once it's gone. Empty house, empty head. Then we get it on the market before autumn and you can come home."

*Home.* His flat in Leeds. Her flat in Bristol. The word skipped over this house entirely, this house where their mother had lived for fifty-three years, as if home were a thing you could only rent.

"I'll think about it," Lena said, which between the two of them meant *no*, and he knew it meant no, and he sighed the sigh of the practical, and said he'd book the skip anyway, no harm in having it on the drive, and rang off before she could become a person with opinions.

She stood in the kitchen with the photograph in one hand and the tape in the other and looked at the drive where the skip would sit, orange and absolute, a mouth waiting to be fed. *Everything that isn't furniture.* The boxes would go in. The boxes with the years on them. And that would be that — one more silence, efficiently disposed of, no skip required for the silence itself because silences, unlike cardboard, were free.

She put the kettle on because her hands wanted a task that wasn't this.

* * *

That afternoon she brought the boxes down. All seven, two at a time, her arms aching, dust in her hair, and stacked them in the front room where the light came through the net curtains in the late afternoon and made the dust on their lids shine like frost. She did not put them in the ledger. She noticed herself not putting them in the ledger, noticed the blank line waiting, and left it blank, and felt — the first feeling in eleven days, small and sharp as a paper cut — *guilty*. Guilty, as if the boxes were guests she'd failed to announce.

She opened 1979 because it was next, because the sequence had a claim on her now, and found more tapes and, beneath them, a child's exercise book.

It was a school exercise book, the cheap kind, orange cover soft with handling, its corners rounded to felt. On the front, in the same almost-letters, a title, and beneath it in English: *my words*. Not *my vocabulary*. Not *notes*. *My words*, in the handwriting of a grown woman, in a book made for a child, and when Lena opened it she understood: the pages were ruled for eight-year-olds, tall and generous, and her mother's writing walked along the lines enormous and careful, each new word written out three times, four times, five times, the way a child writes, the way someone writes when the writing itself is the learning. Words in that dotted, accented script, and beside each one, in her mother's hand, an attempt at English — and beside some of the attempts, a second hand, a different pen, correcting. Not correcting the Yorùbá. Correcting the English. *No — closer to "the light is breaking" than "the light breaks." Try again.*

A second hand. Patient. Present. A teacher's hand, in the margins of her mother's sentences, for years.

Lena sat in the blue-tinged light with the book open on her knees and read her mother learning. Page after page. *Try again. Try again. Better. Better.* And then, three-quarters through, the pages went blank. Mid-word, almost — the last entry was a word begun, two syllables written, the third abandoned, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. After that, nothing. Blank ruled lines all the way to the back cover, like a road that stops at a hedge.

The house was very quiet. The kind of quiet that has a shape.

Lena looked at the embossed half-word on the empty page and felt the numbness crack — not break, not yet, but crack, a hairline of light through the plaster of her. Her mother had been someone's student. Her mother had had a *second hand* in her margins, a voice saying *try again*, and had stopped — mid-syllable, mid-sentence, mid-something — in 1988, and had never said a word of it to either of her children, had folded the whole language away in a loft with the Christmas decorations, and had lived the last thirty-five years with a face like a closed envelope.

*Blue hour, hall steps.* A brick hall with tall windows. Lena looked up, out of the window, across the overgrown garden, over the wall — and saw it. The hall. Their neighbour all her childhood, the place with the tall windows where the lights came on in the evenings and voices she'd never listened to spilled out onto the steps. She'd walked past it a thousand times. She had never once gone in.

There was a paper notice stapled to its doorpost. Even from the garden she could see the council's blue and white, and the word she could read at that distance, the word councils print largest: *DEMOLITION*.

The tape was still in her hand. She turned it over. *Lesson 1.*

Somewhere across the road, behind those tall windows, someone had once said *try again* to her mother, in a hand she might still know.

Lena went to find the tape player.
