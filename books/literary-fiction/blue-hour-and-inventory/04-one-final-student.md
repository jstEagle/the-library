## 4. One Final Student

He came on a Tuesday, the day Lena had designated for the kitchen, and he arrived with an umbrella even though the sky was doing nothing worse than brooding. She saw him first through the frosted pane of the front door—a shape, upright and unhurried, waiting as though patience were a country he held citizenship in.

When she opened it, he was smaller than he had seemed across the street at the hall, but his eyes were the same: quick, dark, amused by something she couldn't see yet.

"Mrs. Yarrow," he said. "Or is it Miss? I have been practising this sentence for two days, so please don't interrupt it." He drew a breath like a man about to dive. "My name is Tomas Adeyemi. I live above the community hall, which the council has decided to murder in six weeks' time. I am ninety-one years old, and I am asking you for one hour a day until then."

Lena stood in the doorway of her mother's house, holding a roll of packing tape like a weapon she had no intention of using.

"An hour a day," she repeated. "For what?"

"To learn," he said, as if the word were self-explanatory, as if learning were simply what one did when a stranger knocked.

"Learn what?"

He smiled then, and there was something in the smile that unsettled her more than the request—something that suggested he knew things about her mother that she did not, that he had been standing on his side of the street for decades watching windows light up and go dark.

"Yorùbá," he said. "Well. A version of it. My mother's version—the one she carried from Lagos in 1958, folded up small so it would fit through customs. Nobody speaks it anymore except me, and lately I've been talking to the radiators, who are poor conversationalists." He glanced past her into the hall, at the boxes stacked against the stairs. "I saw your exercise book. Through the window. Green cover, ruled pages, the handwriting going from careful to furious around page twelve."

The tape unspooled slightly in her hand. "That's my mother's."

"I know," Tomas said gently. "I gave it to her."

* * *

She made him tea because refusing felt ruder than agreeing, and because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't cataloguing. He sat at the kitchen table—the one she'd listed in her inventory as *table, pine, water-marked, £0 sentimental value*—and wrapped both hands around the cup as though it were something rescued from a fire.

"One hour a day," he said again. "Six weeks. That's forty-two hours. In forty-two hours I can't make you fluent—I can barely make myself understood to God these days—but I can teach you the sounds. The sounds are the country. The grammar is just the paperwork."

"Why me?" Lena asked. "Why not—anyone? There must be classes. Apps. YouTube."

Tomas looked at her over the rim of his cup with an expression of vast, gentle pity.

"You think language lives in apps?" He set the cup down. "A language dies the way a hearth goes cold. Not all at once—one morning you notice nobody has lit it, and by then the stones are only stones. I am the last person alive who lights this particular fire, Mrs. Yarrow, and I am ninety-one, and last Thursday I couldn't remember the word for *rain*, which frightened me so badly I sat down in the middle of the pavement." He paused. "It came back. But it took its time about it."

"And my mother?"

The question came out before she could stop it, sharp-edged, and she watched him receive it without flinching.

"Your mother," Tomas said, "was my first student. Nineteen seventy-one. She came to the hall on a wet Wednesday because she'd heard someone speaking it through the door and stood outside listening until I opened it and found her there, soaked, embarrassed, furious at herself for being caught wanting something." His voice softened. "I taught her for six years. Then one day she stopped coming, and left a sentence half-finished on my blackboard, and I have been keeping it warm ever since."

"Keeping it—"

"Warm," he said. "Like a plate for someone who might still come home."

Lena looked at her hands. Somewhere upstairs was a box marked only *1977*, sealed with tape gone amber, and she thought suddenly, absurdly, of her mother sealing it—that deliberate press of the dispenser, the decisiveness of it—and felt something crack sideways in her chest.

"I don't know anything about any of this," she said. "I'm here to inventory the house. For sale. My brother wants it done by the end of the month."

"The house can wait," Tomas said. "Houses are patient. It's languages that die of neglect." He leaned forward. "One hour. Every day until the demolition. If after the first lesson you want me gone, I'll go, and I'll never trouble you again, and we will both pretend this conversation happened to other people."

And that was how, Lena would reflect later, she lost the argument without ever having agreed to it: not through persuasion but through the sheer gravitational pull of a man who had been rehearsing a knock on her door for two days and was not going to be turned away by a woman holding packing tape.

"All right," she heard herself say. "One hour. Starting now, apparently, since you're already sitting down."

"Now," Tomas agreed happily, "is an excellent time."

* * *

The first thing he taught her was not a word. It was a sound.

"Say *gb*," he said.

"Gb," Lena said.

"No." He winced theatrically. "You said *g* and then *b*, two strangers shaking hands. They must be married. One sound. The back of your tongue comes up—" he tapped his own throat, "—and stops the air completely, then releases it with the lips together. Like a small cork coming out of a bottle. *Gb.*"

"*Gb.*"

"That was a bottle opening in another room. Closer."

"*Gb!*"

"Closer. You're strangling it. Don't strangle it—it's a friendly sound. Your mother used to say it sounded like a frog approving of something."

"A frog—" Lena laughed despite herself, a short startled bark that surprised them both. "She talked about the lessons? With you?"

"She talked about everything with everyone except the people she should have," Tomas said, and moved briskly on before the sentence could land. "*Gb*. Again."

They worked through the hour like that, and it was the strangest hour Lena had spent in years—stranger than the funeral, stranger than signing the crematorium forms, because those had required nothing of her body but stillness. This required her mouth to betray her. There was a sound, *p̀*, a falling tone that her tongue kept flattening; there was the nasal hum of vowels that seemed to travel through the bones of her face rather than her throat; there was *ṣ*, which Tomas demonstrated with such relish that the teacups on the counter seemed to lean in.

"Your mouth is English," he observed, not unkindly, somewhere in the second half hour. "English is a very tidy language. Everything happens at the front, behind the teeth, like people queuing politely. This language happens in the back, in the chest, down where the feelings live. That's why it feels wrong. It *is* wrong—for your mouth. For now."

"It feels like being asked to sing while chewing," Lena said.

"Yes!" He beamed. "Exactly that. Your mother said precisely that, in 1971, and I told her the same thing I'll tell you: the awkwardness is the honesty. When you sound ridiculous, you're close. Fluency is just embarrassment you've grown fond of."

Outside, the afternoon went greyer, and rain began—not the dramatic kind, but the fine persistent kind that made the window look frosted from the inside. Lena noticed she had not once thought about the inventory. The list sat unfinished on the counter—*armchair, floral, one arm loose*—and for fifty-three minutes she had not cared, and the not-caring felt like setting down luggage she'd forgotten she was carrying.

At some point Tomas began counting in the language, teaching her numbers the way you'd teach a child, and she got to four before stumbling, and he corrected her, and she tried again, and got to seven, and he clapped his hands once, softly, like a man applauding a candle staying lit.

"Seven," he said. "*Èje.* Do you know why that number matters? Because in the old counting, seven is the number of completion—not finished, mind you, but *complete*. A thing with all its parts present." He looked at her steadily. "Your mother reached seven too. Then eight. Then further. Much further."

"And then she stopped."

"And then she stopped," Tomas agreed. He gathered himself up from the chair with the slow architecture of the very old, joints announcing themselves like floorboards. At the door he paused, umbrella in hand, and turned back.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Same hour. Bring the green exercise book. Page twelve is where she got angry, and anger is where the real lessons start."

Then he was gone down the path, under his unnecessary umbrella, a small upright figure crossing the wet street toward the condemned building where he lived alone above a hall full of chairs no one would sit on again.

Lena closed the door and stood in the hallway of her mother's house, in the smell of dust and lemon polish, and realized her cheeks ached—from laughing, or from trying to make sounds her mouth refused, or from something else entirely.

Upstairs, box *1977* waited. And now, annoyingly, so did page twelve.

She climbed the stairs, found the box, and stood over it with the packing knife in her hand for a long moment—long enough to hear, absurdly, Tomas's voice in her head: *the awkwardness is the honesty.*

She cut the tape.
