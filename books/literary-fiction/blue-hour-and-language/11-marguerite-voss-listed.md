## 11. Marguerite Voss, Listed

The Municipal Records Office on Rue Pétain opened at nine, and Zephyrine was there at eight-forty, standing under the awning like a man waiting for a dentist who had already extracted everything worth extracting. He had rehearsed the visit the night before, standing in his kitchen with a mug of tea going cold in his hand. He would say: I'm looking for information about a person. He would not say: my mother wrote forty-one letters to a daughter I didn't know existed and every one of them came back unopened, and now I need to know if the addressee is alive, because if she is, then everything I have spent nine years mourning has been misfiled.

He would say: I'm looking for information about a person.

At nine-oh-two the clerk unlocked the door — a broad, unhurried woman with reading glasses on a chain and the air of someone who had heard every possible reason a human being might need a piece of paper — and Zephyrine followed her inside into the smell of dust and toner and old glue, which was, he realized with a small private shock, the smell of his own life.

"Records requests are at the second counter," she said. "Vital statistics is third. If it's property, that's downstairs."

"It's a person," he said.

She looked at him over her glasses, unhurried. "They usually are."

* * *

Her name was Madame Ferrand, and she worked the vital statistics counter with the patience of a woman who had once, presumably, been young and fast and had since discovered that slowness was simply more accurate. She took his request form, read it twice, and asked the question he had been dreading.

"What is your relationship to the person you're inquiring after?"

Zephyrine had prepared for this too. He had prepared three answers. The first was *family*, which felt like a theft. The second was *no relation*, which felt like a lie. The third, the one he actually said, was:

"I'm trying to find out."

Madame Ferrand considered this for a long moment. Somewhere behind her, a fan turned its head slowly from side to side, like something deciding whether to believe him.

"That's not one of the boxes," she said finally, tapping the form.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

She took out a fresh form, crossed out nothing, added nothing, and slid it back across the counter. "Name of the person sought."

"Marguerite Voss." His voice did something strange on the second word, a small subsidence, as though the name weighed more than he'd expected. "Born — I think — 1961. In Varennes-sur-Loire." He gave the date from the letters, the birthday his mother had marked every year in her small slanted hand even after the envelopes began returning: *Happy birthday, Marguerite. Forty-one letters and I have never once wished you this on time.*

Madame Ferrand wrote it all down without comment. "And your interest is genealogical?"

"Yes," said Zephyrine, and then, because lying to this woman felt like lying to a lighthouse, "also no."

The fan turned. Madame Ferrand looked at him over her glasses for what felt like the length of an entire fiscal year.

"You'd be surprised," she said, "how many people come through here looking for someone they're not sure they're allowed to look for." She stamped something. "I'll have the search done by Thursday. Don't call. Come in. It's better if I can see your face when I give you the answer."

"Why?" he asked.

"Because answers change faces," she said, "and I like to know which face went with which answer. It helps me sleep."

* * *

Thursday arrived the way Thursdays do, which is to say without any regard for what Zephyrine wanted from it. He told himself all morning that he would go at lunchtime, then at four, then before closing, and at ten past five he was still at the post office, alphabetizing a tray of commemorative stamps nobody had asked him to alphabetize, when Odile appeared at his elbow with two coffees from the machine down the street.

"You've reorganized the Marianne series three times today," she said, setting a cup by his hand. "In three different orders. Chronological, alphabetical, and — I want to say — by how sad their faces look."

"They all look sad. That's the point of Mariannes."

"Mm." She leaned against the counter and watched him with the particular patience of a woman who had spent eleven years working a post office window and could diagnose a human being from thirty feet by the way they held an envelope. "You've been strange for two weeks. Stranger. You were already strange; this is a new stratum of strange. And last week you bought forty stamps for Saint-Aubin-les-Bains, which is—" she counted on her fingers theatrically— "a city you have never once mentioned in eleven years, in a region you once described, and I quote, as 'geographically hostile.'"

"It's very flat," Zephyrine admitted. "The flatness is hostile."

"So." She wrapped both hands around her coffee. "Are you going to tell me, or am I going to have to invent something? Because I warn you, my inventions are vivid. Last month I decided the man who mails himself envelopes full of sand was exiled royalty. I've given him a whole backstory. His name is Gerald."

Zephyrine looked at the coffee. Steam rose off it in a thin thread, the way smoke had risen off the biscuit tin's contents the night he burned his first draft letter, and he thought — not for the first time — that everything in his life lately came in pairs: steam and smoke, hope and terror, the living sister and the dead address.

"There's a person," he said. "I'm trying to find out if she's real."

"And?"

"And apparently she is." He had gone that afternoon, between the stamp trays. Madame Ferrand had slid a single sheet across the counter and watched his face while he read it, exactly as promised. *VOSS, Marguerite Élise. Born 14 March 1961, Varennes-sur-Loire. Marriage record: Delacroix, Tomas, 1989. Occupation per tax registry: accordeur de pianos.* A piano tuner. Two towns over. Alive. Alive, married, employed, real, twenty-six kilometers east, tuning pianos while Zephyrine sorted other people's mail eleven years without knowing she existed.

"A piano tuner," he said aloud to Odile now, because saying it made it no less absurd. "My — the person is a piano tuner. She makes pianos agree with themselves for a living."

Odile was quiet for a moment. When he glanced up, her expression had lost its teasing edge entirely, and what remained underneath was so gentle it was almost unbearable.

"Zeph," she said. "Whose daughter is she?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. The post office hummed around them — the tick of the date stamp, the shuffle of the sorting cage, somewhere a customer arguing mildly about parcel insurance — and none of it touched the small cold space where the answer lived.

"My mother's," he said.

Odile set down her coffee. She did not ask the next question, the obvious one, the one hanging in the air like weather. Instead she pulled a pen from the breast pocket of her uniform — the good pen, the one she guarded like a dragon guards the last gold coin — wrote something on the back of a dead letter slip, folded it once, and pressed it into his hand.

"My address," she said. "Home, not the hostel where I stayed that year you don't remember. In case you ever need somewhere to land."

"I have an apartment," Zephyrine said, bewildered.

"You have rooms," Odile said. "There's a difference. Ask anyone who's ever fallen out of the sky." She picked up her coffee again, and the teasing came back into her voice like a lamp coming back on, but her eyes stayed serious. "You don't have to use it. You don't even have to keep it. Just — it exists. Sometimes knowing a thing exists is most of what it does."

He put the slip in his breast pocket, over the heart, without quite deciding to, and later that night he would find it there and stand holding it under the lamplight for a long time, this small rectangle of dead-letter paper with Odile's looping handwriting on it, thinking: *two addresses now. One I'm terrified of, and one I'm grateful to. That's double what I had last month.*

* * *

That night he tried to write to Marguerite Delacroix, née Voss, piano tuner, half-sister, stranger.

The first draft began: *Dear Madam,* and got as far as *I am writing regarding correspondence addressed to you between the years 1974 and 2003* before he understood that he sounded like a records office, or worse, a debt collector, and that no one on earth opens a letter that begins *regarding correspondence.* He read it twice, held it over the sink, and struck the match he now kept — absurdly, shamefully — in the drawer beside the cutlery, like salt, like something a kitchen ought to have.

The paper went up quickly. He watched the words curl and brighten and go black, ran the tap, stood listening to the water.

The second draft began: *Dear Marguerite,* which was better, and then immediately: *You don't know me, but we share a mother,* which was worse, because it was true in the way a bone sticking through skin is true. He sat with it for an hour. He tried *I have recently learned* (evasive), *For most of my life I believed* (accusatory), *This will be difficult to read* (presumptuous — how would he know what was difficult for her to read? She had read forty-one letters' worth of nothing). At midnight he wrote a single sentence he actually liked — *Our mother had beautiful handwriting, and she used it mostly to apologize to you* — and then ruined it by following it with three paragraphs of explanation that explained nothing, and he burned that draft too, and washed the ash down, and stood in the dark kitchen with wet hands.

The third draft he wrote at two in the morning, in longhand, on the backs of returned postcards, because it seemed fitting to spend his mother's failed mail on his first attempt at successful mail. It began: *Dear Marguerite. My name is Zephyrine Halloran.* And then, honestly, terribly: *I have been afraid of this sentence for six weeks, so please forgive whatever follows it.*

He got four lines further. Four lines about the wardrobe, the tin, the seals unbroken. And then he stopped, because the next sentence — the true next sentence, the one his hand refused to write — was *and I would like to know you,* and wanting things, he was discovering, was a muscle he had let atrophy so thoroughly that using it again felt like learning to walk on a broken foot.

He did not burn the third draft. He put it in the biscuit tin, on top of the forty-one sealed letters, where it lay like a forty-second letter, unsent, in the family tradition.

Then he sat at the kitchen table until dawn, doing what he did instead of sleeping these days, which was arithmetic of the heart: *she is fifty-one. She has been fifty-one for some months. Our mother was thirty when she left, which means Marguerite was thirteen, which means she remembers. She remembers everything I was protected from, and I remember everything she was abandoned during, and between us we might assemble one whole childhood, if either of us survives the assembly.*

* * *

On Saturday he did not go to her city. He told himself he was going to the library, which was technically true, in the way that a man fleeing a house is technically going outside.

The municipal library on Place aux Herbes was the other place Zephyrine had always felt safe — archives, indexes, the great comforting lie that everything could be catalogued and therefore contained. The librarian at the reference desk was a young man named Baptiste with wire-rimmed glasses and the serene competence of someone who had never once been defeated by a filing system.

"I'm looking for," Zephyrine began, and stopped, and started again. "There's a piano tuner. In Saint-Aubin-les-Bains, or near it. Delacroix. I wanted — I don't know what I wanted. A listing. Something ordinary."

Baptiste tapped at his keyboard with two fingers, unhurried. "Delacroix. Piano tuning. Saint-Aubin." A pause. The rain against the high windows filled it. "There's a listing from the trade directory — 'Delacroix Accordage, rue des Tanneurs.' And a mention in the regional paper, 2019, an interview." He turned the monitor toward Zephyrine. "There's a photograph."

Zephyrine had known, in the abstract, that there would be a face. He had not prepared for the face.

She had their mother's jaw. Not similar — identical, the same strong line and slight asymmetry, the chin that tilted a half-degree left, the feature he had seen every morning of his childhood in the bathroom mirror and mourned every morning since. She was smiling in the photograph, standing beside an upright piano with her tuning tools laid out on the lid like surgical instruments, and she was smiling the way their mother smiled in the few photographs that survived the fire — crookedly, as if laughing at something just outside the frame.

"She looks like—" he said, and couldn't finish.

"People usually do," said Baptiste gently, turning the monitor back. "Would you like me to print the article?"

"Yes," said Zephyrine. "No. Yes."

Baptiste printed it. Zephyrine read it standing at the reference desk, right there, unable to wait. Most of it was about pianos — her father had been a tuner, she'd inherited the business, she believed every piano remembered how it was played (*"A piano is a diary that pretends to be furniture,"* she told the interviewer, and Zephyrine laughed out loud, one startled bark, in the middle of the quiet library, because he had grown up with a woman who talked to furniture too) — but near the end, asked whether she'd always lived in Saint-Aubin, she said: *"No. I was born in Varennes. My mother left when I was thirteen. I used to write to her care of her old landlady. The letters came back. After a while I stopped. Tuning teaches you patience — you can't force a string into tune. You loosen it, you listen, you wait for it to find the note itself. I suppose I'm still waiting for a few strings."*

Zephyrine read it three times. Then he folded the article along Baptiste's crisp crease and put it in his breast pocket, on top of Odile's address, over the heart, where his life was apparently accumulating now.

"Thank you," he said.

"Anything else?"

Zephyrine looked out the tall windows at the rain coming down over the square, at the gray light going silver at the edges, and thought about strings. About loosening. About waiting for a note to find itself.

"One thing," he said. "Do you have anything on the blue hour? Photographically. Light levels. That kind of thing."

Baptiste blinked, then smiled as if this were the most reasonable request of the day. "Civil twilight. Give me a moment."

While he searched, Zephyrine took out his notebook — the inventory notebook, tea at seven, postcards at noon, silence as discipline — and beneath the last entry, in handwriting that shook only slightly, he wrote:

*M. Delacroix, rue des Tanneurs, Saint-Aubin-les-Bains. Piano tuner. Waits for strings. Jaw: ours.*

Underneath that, after a long pause:

*Draft four tomorrow. No burning.*

* * *

He walked home the long way, past the post office, where the evening shift was lighting the lamps behind the glass, and he thought about Odile's slip of paper in his pocket and the article folded over it and the whole small archive he was becoming, a man who carried his hopes in his breast pocket like contraband.

At home, the biscuit tin waited on the table. He lifted the lid. Forty-one sealed letters, patient as seeds. One unsent draft, his own, four lines long, trembling at the edges where the match had almost reached it.

He took out a fresh postcard — blank, unbought sentiment, no picture — and wrote on it, in his best handwriting, the handwriting his mother had given him:

*Marguerite —*

*I found your letters. All forty-one. They were never delivered, but they were never thrown away either, and neither, it turns out, were you. I'm coming to Saint-Aubin. Not yet. Soon. I'm practicing the sentence.*

*Your brother,*
*Zephyrine*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he did not burn it, and did not mail it, and did not file it away. He propped it upright against the lamp on the kitchen table, facing the room, facing him — the first piece of mail in nine years addressed to the living, waiting in plain sight, asking him daily, patiently, the way a tuned string asks a hammer: *well? Are you ready to hear the note?*
