## 18. Reading Aloud

The first evening, neither of them touched the tin for an hour.

Zephyrine had arrived with it under his arm like a bomb he'd defused badly, wires still hanging out, and Marguerite had taken one look at his face and said, "Tea first. We're not doing this on an empty stomach." She made tea the way he imagined their mother might have—strong enough to stand a spoon in, in mismatched cups, one of which had a chip in the rim that she turned toward herself out of habit, as if the flaw were a guest to be shielded from embarrassment.

They sat in her kitchen, which smelled of lemon oil and piano felt. A tuning fork lay on the table beside a dish of olives, and when Marguerite spoke she had a habit of tapping it lightly against the wood, a soft *hnnn* that hung in the air like a question mark. Zephyrine found he liked it. It meant the silences were punctuated rather than empty.

"So," she said, when the tea had gone from scalding to merely hot. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one."

"Over how many years?"

He had done the arithmetic on the train, in the margin of a newspaper he hadn't read. "Nineteen seventy-one to nineteen eighty-eight. Then a gap. Then three more in ninety-four, the year before she died."

Marguerite turned the tin slowly in her hands, her thumb tracing the lid's faded lithograph of a seaside promenade. "She was twenty-two when I was born," she said. "Which means she was writing these when she was your age. When she was older than you are now, some of them." She looked up. "Do you want to do this together, or do you want me to leave the room while you read them, and then we compare?"

"Together," Zephyrine said. Then, honestly: "I think if I read them alone I'll stop after the first hard one and never come back."

"Then we'll read them aloud," Marguerite said, in the tone of a woman who had spent thirty years tuning pianos and knew exactly how much force a thing could take before it broke. "Out loud. It's harder to lie to yourself when your own voice is saying the words."

She took the first envelope, held it to the light like a jeweler assessing a stone, and slit it open with the thin blade she used for piano keys. She cleared her throat. She began.

*Dear Marguerite. I have written this letter eleven times and burned it ten. This is the one that gets to live, so I will try to make it worthy of surviving...*

Zephyrine had read the first three letters alone, by lamplight, in the week of vertigo after finding the tin. But hearing them was different. Hearing them in Marguerite's voice—her voice low and unhurried, with a slight catch on certain words, *worthy*, *surviving*—was like hearing a song he'd only ever seen as sheet music. The letters stopped being evidence and became a person again. Their mother's person. Not the silent woman who'd made him toast every morning and never explained the photographs with the man turned away, but someone who had spent decades writing to a daughter who never answered, because she'd decided she deserved the silence.

"Wait," Zephyrine said, when Marguerite paused to blow her nose. "Go back. *I don't ask you to forgive me. I ask you to let me keep trying, because trying is the only prayer I know.*"

Marguerite read it again. They sat with it.

"She prayed like an accountant," Marguerite said finally, and Zephyrine laughed—a surprised, wet sound—and then they were both laughing and crying at once, which he would learn over the next three evenings was simply the weather of that kitchen.

* * *

The second evening, they had a system. Marguerite read the letters addressed to her; Zephyrine read the ones that were clearly drafts of the same letters, crossed out and revised, because their mother had apparently never sent a sentence she hadn't first interrogated. They alternated. They kept a running commentary in the margins of a notepad—Marguerite's notes were brisk and numbered; Zephyrine's drifted into small sketches, and when she saw one, a little drawing of the biscuit tin itself, she tapped the tuning fork and said, "You draw."

"Not really."

"That's what people who draw say." She tore the page out and put it on the refrigerator under a magnet shaped like a lemon. It was such a small act of domestic annexation that Zephyrine felt his throat close. He was forty-one years old and no one had put his drawing on a refrigerator since he was nine.

The letters from the middle years were the hardest and the funniest, which Zephyrine was beginning to understand was not a contradiction. Their mother wrote about small things with enormous tenderness—a neighbor's dog, a recipe for plum cake that never worked, a radio program about migrating birds that she listened to every Thursday "because the birds get to leave and come back, which is more than I've managed." She wrote about a boy sometimes, obliquely. *My son lost a tooth today and put it under his pillow for a coin he no longer believes in. Faith is like that. You keep the ritual after the belief goes, and sometimes the belief comes back to find the ritual waiting.*

"Me," Zephyrine said quietly. "That's me. I remember the tooth. She gave me a fifty-cent piece and I told her the tooth fairy wasn't real and she said, *no, but I am, and I'd like to be the one who pays you.*"

Marguerite set down the letter. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she said, "I always knew about you. You know that, don't you?"

He didn't, actually. He shook his head.

"Maman—" she stopped, corrected herself, and he watched her decide, deliberately, to say the word *mother*, in English, as a gift to him. "Mother told me when I was fourteen. She came to visit—she visited twice a year, always for a weekend, always leaving before Monday, and I never understood until later that Monday was when the letters stopped being possible and the guilt started being necessary. She sat me down at this table and she said, *You have a brother. He doesn't know about you. I couldn't tell him, because telling him meant telling him why I left, and he's too young to carry that.* And I said, *Does he know about me?* and she said no, and I remember being furious—not at her. At you. Isn't that absurd? I was furious at a boy I'd never met because he got to be the one who didn't know. He got to be innocent. I had to be the one who knew and couldn't say."

"I wasn't innocent," Zephyrine said. "I was taught to forget. There's a difference, but I'm not sure it's a better one."

"You were a child."

"So were you."

They looked at each other across the kitchen, across the tin, across the forty-one opened envelopes now arranged in chronological rows on the table like a timeline of a country that no longer existed. Then Marguerite got up, opened a drawer beneath the telephone, and took out an envelope so old and soft it seemed woven rather than folded.

"I wrote back," she said. "Once. I was seventeen. I waited until she'd gone home on her Sunday, and I wrote her a letter, and I mailed it to the address she'd always left with me, in care of a poste restante. It came back two weeks later. *Addressee unknown.* She'd moved. Or she'd changed the poste restante. Or—" Marguerite's voice did something complicated. "Or she'd made a mistake. She was capable of mistakes. I spent years believing she'd done it on purpose, that she'd given me a dead address so the door would stay shut. It took me until I was forty to consider that she might have simply been afraid, and fear makes bad addresses of everything."

Zephyrine took the returned envelope. Her handwriting—his handwriting's older cousin, the loops slightly more generous—was on the front. He didn't open it.

"Read it," Marguerite said. "It's yours too. Half of everything in this house is yours too. I've been doing the math since yesterday and I keep getting that answer."

He slit it open. It was short. A seventeen-year-old's letter, furious and tender in alternating lines. *I don't forgive you yet. I want you to know that I'm keeping a space at this table anyway, because I don't know how to do otherwise. If you ever get this: I play piano. I'm told I'm good. You've never heard me, and I've decided that's the thing I mind most.*

Zephyrine read it aloud, because Marguerite was right, it was harder to lie with your voice. When he finished, the kitchen was very quiet except for the tuning fork, which Marguerite had picked up and was holding against her sternum without striking it.

"She never heard you play," he said.

"No."

"Then we should fix that. Tonight. I don't care how late it is."

Marguerite laughed and cried and said the piano was out of tune, and he said, "You're a piano tuner," and she said, "Exactly. I can't stand hearing it," and then she played anyway—something slow and a little sad and then, without warning, something quick and joyful that made her husband, Théo, appear in the doorway in his dressing gown with two glasses of something amber, having apparently been listening from the stairs for some time, because he said, "She only plays that when she's happy or heartbroken. I've learned to bring glasses either way."

* * *

The third evening, they reached the last letters—the three from 1994, the year before the fire, the year before her death. Zephyrine had been twenty-eight. He had been living two streets from her. She had been writing to Marguerite from a kitchen he had eaten in.

The first of the three was short. *I am old enough now to know that some doors you have to leave open without walking through them. I am leaving this one open. If you come to it, I will be here. If you don't, I will understand, because I built the wall that keeps you out and I have no right to complain about the view.*

The second was longer, and in it their mother did something Zephyrine had never heard her do in life: she told a joke about herself. *Your brother, I am told, is a serious young man who organizes his bookshelves by the color of the spines. I don't know where he got it. Neither of his parents could organize a drawer. Perhaps seriousness skips a generation, like red hair. Perhaps it is a punishment for something. I hope, if it is a punishment, it comes with the bookshelves.*

"That's me," Zephyrine said. "That's—I do that. I do it by color. How did she—"

"She watched you," Marguerite said gently. "She watched you the way I watch pianos. From a respectful distance, listening for what's out of tune."

The third letter was the last. It was dated in October, and the fire had been in November, and Zephyrine had not known until that moment that there was a letter written in the space between the last ordinary month and the last month at all. It was very short.

*Dear Marguerite. The house across the street had a fire drill today, everyone standing on the lawn in their coats, and I thought: there is a version of my life in which everything I own is in a box and I am standing on a lawn deciding what matters. I think the answer is: the letters. I think I have always known the answer is the letters. I will keep writing them until I run out of postage or courage, whichever fails first. With all my love, always, your mother, Odette.*

"Odette," Marguerite said. "She signed Odette. She never signed Odette."

"Maybe she knew," Zephyrine said.

"Maybe she was just tired of pretending to be someone who didn't sign her name."

They sat with the last letter between them for a long time. Then Marguerite did something that Zephyrine would remember for the rest of his life, though he couldn't have said why it undid him the way it did: she folded the letter along its original creases, slid it back into its envelope, and placed it in his hands.

"Take it," she said. "You take the last one. I have the first one—the one from seventy-one, the *eleven times and burned it ten* one. We'll split the inheritance. Forty-one letters, two children. It doesn't divide evenly, but nothing about her did."

He held the envelope. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"Marguerite," he said. "There's something I haven't told you yet. In the tin. There was a letter addressed to me. From her. Unsent. She wrote it the year before she died and never mailed it, and it ends in the middle of a sentence, and the last complete thing it says is *ask your father*."

Marguerite was quiet. Outside, the streetlamps came on, that hour when the light neither stays nor leaves, and Zephyrine thought, absurdly, *she would have loved this light, she photographed this light, and here it is, arriving on schedule, for two of us instead of one.*

"Aldous," Marguerite said. The name sounded strange in her mouth—unpracticed, a word from a foreign language she'd only read, never spoken. "I know the name. She said it exactly twice in my whole life. Once when I was small, as a monster in a story, and once when I was grown, as something sadder than a monster." She tapped the tuning fork against the table, once, and let the note ring until it died. "Do you know where he is?"

"Seaside town. Two hours by train. He grows tomatoes." Zephyrine heard himself say it and almost laughed. "I've been. We talked. It's—I don't have the story yet. I have the shape of the story, which is worse."

"Then you have to go back," Marguerite said. "And Zephyrine—" she reached across the table and put her hand flat on the tin, on the whole burned-and-surviving archive of their mother's trying, "—when you go, I want you to know that whatever he says, whatever it turns out she was running from, there is one fact that doesn't change, and it's this: she wrote forty-one letters and never once blamed you. I've read every word. You were the one innocent thing in her whole account. Whatever else is true, that's true."

Zephyrine looked down at the last letter in his hands, the one signed *Odette*, and thought about the vacant lot with the wildflowers, the address that was no longer an address, the anniversary that was coming whether he was ready or not.

"I know a place," he said slowly, "where she used to live. Where the house used to be. There's nothing there now but weeds and light. I've never gone with anyone. I've never gone at all, really—I just write to it."

Marguerite stood, and took her coat off the hook, and held it without putting it on.

"Then we'll go together," she said. "You can introduce me."
