## 4. The Night Shift

### Devi

Devi noticed the box before she noticed anything else about the new one — Noor, that was the name on the schedule, though it still looked strange to her, a single word where a person should be. The box was cardboard, soft at the corners with age, and Noor carried it the way Devi's father used to carry his medals: close to the chest, both hands, as if it might be taken.

It was Tuesday, or what passed for Tuesday when you worked nights. The conservatory had no windows in the service corridors, so time arrived by other means — the hum of the boiler kicking on at eleven, the distant clatter of Marcus emptying bins in the east wing, the smell of Tomás's coffee drifting up from the loading dock like weather. Devi had worked enough night shifts to know that buildings talked if you listened long enough. Hospitals talked about pain. This place talked about music. Even empty, even condemned, the halls held a residue of sound the way her mother's saris had held the smell of sandalwood years after they stopped wearing them.

She found Noor in the practice wing corridor, standing under the flickering fluorescent with the box against their sternum, not moving. Just standing. Devi had seen people stand like that before — outside ICU doors, mostly. People who had just learned something and hadn't yet decided what shape it was going to make of them.

"You'll get a crick in your neck," Devi said, "standing under that light. It strobes at exactly the wrong frequency."

Noor blinked at her. Up close their face had the particular pallor of someone who slept badly and ate worse. "I didn't hear you come up."

"Twenty-two years of nursing," Devi said. "You learn to walk quiet around people who are thinking." She nodded at the box. "Heavy?"

"No." A pause. "Yes. I don't know."

That answer, at least, was honest, and honesty was currency on the night shift; there wasn't enough of it anywhere else. Devi jerked her chin toward the stairwell. "Break room. There's coffee that tastes like punishment and biscuits Tomás pretends he didn't buy. You look like a man who could use both."

"I'm not—" Noor started, and then seemed to lose the end of the sentence, the way people do when they've been alone too long and conversation has become a foreign language they once spoke fluently. "I'm not really company tonight."

"Good," Devi said. "Neither am I. We can be bad company together. It's less lonely than being bad company separately."

She didn't wait for an answer. That was the trick of it — she'd learned this on the wards, with patients and with her father, who had become a kind of patient himself since the stroke — you didn't ask permission for kindness, because permission gave people the chance to refuse, and refusal was just loneliness choosing itself again. She walked, and after a moment she heard Noor's footsteps behind her, hesitant, then following.

In the break room, Marcus had already claimed the good chair, the one with three legs and dignity, and Tomás was pouring coffee into mugs that had lost their handles sometime during the previous administration. The radio on the shelf played something low and orchestral, half-swallowed by static. Devi took the box from Noor's arms — gently, asking nothing, just lifting it away the way you'd lift a burden from someone too proud to set it down — and placed it on the table between them all.

Nobody asked what was in it. That was the whole art of the thing. Devi watched them all perform it without rehearsal: Marcus sliding the biscuits across, Tomás filling a mug and setting it near Noor's hand without looking at them, the radio filling every silence that didn't want to be filled. Four people who had been made invisible by the day world, sitting in a room with a box none of them would open unless invited.

Devi thought about her father, asleep in the care home across town, his hands folded on the blanket like a man waiting for a train that had been cancelled decades ago. She thought about how the worst part of caring for him wasn't the work. It was watching him be unseen by everyone who didn't love him. And she thought, looking at Noor's face over the rim of the mug — that hollowed, watchful face — that here was somebody nobody had seen in a very long time, including, possibly, themselves.

"Eat," she said. "Whatever's in the box will keep. Boxes always keep. That's the trouble with boxes."

### Marcus

Marcus had driven a bus for nineteen years, and the first thing you learned driving a bus was reading people through the way they sat. He'd carried ten thousand strangers past ten thousand lit windows, and he could tell you within three stops which marriages were ending, which kids were running away, which old men were riding the route one last time because tomorrow they'd give up the ghost or the fare. So when Noor Halloran — because that was who it was, Marcus had known since week two, he'd seen the kid play Chopin on the local news back when the news still covered things like prodigies — came into the break room carrying that box like a heart transplant, Marcus read the posture immediately.

*Somebody died,* he thought. *Or something did.*

He'd been fired for refusing to drive a bus with a cracked axle bracket, and the union had folded like wet paper, and now he pushed a mop through the same building where he used to wait out his layovers, listening through the doors to whatever rehearsal was bleeding into the lobby. He'd never told anyone here that. It seemed beside the point. The point was that institutions broke people in the same way everywhere — quietly, procedurally, with paperwork — and the people they broke tended to find each other eventually, usually around a bad coffee machine at three in the morning.

So he did what bus drivers do. He made space.

"There's a seat," he said, and kicked the folding chair out from under the table with one foot, catching it before it clattered. "Structurally unsound but loyal."

Noor sat. That was progress. In six weeks of shared shifts, Noor had perfected the art of being present and absent simultaneously — said good evening, answered questions in four words or fewer, cleaned the recital hall with a devotion that Marcus privately found suspicious, because nobody mopped like that unless the floor meant something to them.

The box sat on the table. Cardboard, old, taped shut with tape gone amber. Somebody had written on the top in faded ballpoint, but the writing faced away from Marcus, and he didn't lean over to read it, because you didn't read other people's mail. Nineteen years of buses taught you that too. People told you things on buses they'd never tell their families, and the whole covenant of it was that you kept your eyes forward.

Tomás was telling a story about the flood of '94, when the basement of the west wing filled with two feet of water and the sheet music library floated like leaves, and Marcus let the story wash over him the way he let most stories wash over him, comfortable, until he noticed that Noor's hand had come to rest on the box. Not holding it. Resting. The way you rest your hand on a sleeping animal.

"What's the route tonight?" Marcus asked, because direct questions were kinder than careful ones. Careful questions made people feel like patients. Direct ones made them feel like colleagues. "East wing first? Because if it's east wing, I'm trading with you. That radiator in Studio C sounds like it's dying and I don't want to witness it twice."

"The whole practice wing is coming down next month," Devi said. "They're clearing it out."

"Everything?" Marcus asked.

"Everything," Noor said. Their voice was rough, like a door opening after years. They cleared their throat. "Wardrobes, pianos, everything."

"Pianos," Tomás repeated, with the particular grief of a man who had polished instruments for thirty years. "There are pianos in there older than me."

"They're junk," Marcus said, and immediately regretted it, because Noor flinched — actually flinched, a small motion of the shoulders, like a man hearing a bone crack. Marcus raised his hands. "Hey. I'm sure they're beautiful junk."

"They're not junk," Noor said quietly. And then, as if surprised by their own voice, as if the words had come out of some locked room: "None of it's junk. It's just — abandoned. Those aren't the same thing."

The radio crackled. Somewhere above them, the building settled, the way old buildings do, sighing into their foundations. Marcus looked at the box, at the faded handwriting on its top edge, and thought about all the undelivered things he'd carried in nineteen years — the grocery bags left on seats, the wallets turned in unopened, the woman who rode his route every Sunday for a year with a cake box on her lap and never once told him whose birthday it was. People carried things. That was the whole human business. Carrying things and hoping somebody would ask, and being terrified if they did.

He didn't ask. He slid the biscuit tin closer instead.

### Tomás

Tomás had been at the conservatory longer than the current director, longer than the asbestos abatement report, longer, he suspected, than several of the load-bearing walls. Thirty-one years. He had buffed the terrazzo in the main hall for galas and scrubbed vomit from the green room after student parties and once, memorably, retrieved a live peacock from the organ loft during a production of *Hänsel und Gretel*, an incident that had never been explained to his satisfaction. He knew the building the way priests know churches — not the architecture but the accumulation. Every hall held its services. Every room held its dead.

And he knew Noor Halloran. Had known since the first week, when the new hire walked into the main hall and stood beneath the portrait — the official one, the gilt-framed one of the twelve-year-old prodigy in white tie, hands resting on an invisible keyboard — and Tomás had watched the recognition move across the young man's face like weather. Not surprise. Something sadder. Recognition of yourself in a frame, hung by other people, in a building that had forgotten your name.

Tomás had said nothing. Thirty-one years teaches you when silence is the greater courtesy. But he had begun leaving things: the good mop, the one that didn't shed. The corner assignments with the best acoustics. And on the third Thursday of every month, when the board met upstairs and the building emptied early, he had made sure the break room coffee was fresh, because he had noticed that Noor lingered those nights, drawn toward the sound of voices the way plants lean toward light without knowing they're doing it.

Now the box sat on the table, and the boy — man, Tomás corrected himself, though the portrait made it difficult — sat among them with his hands around a mug he wasn't drinking, and Tomás recognized the expression because he'd worn it himself once, forty years ago, in a different country, holding a letter that had changed the shape of his life. The expression of a person who has just learned that the past is not past. That it waits. That it files itself patiently behind wardrobes and inside walls and in the hearts of old men, and comes due when you least have the funds to pay.

"The flood of '94," Tomás was saying, wrapping up the story, "took the Beethoven first editions. All of them. The director cried. I swept up the water and thought: paper dries, but grief floats. You cannot sweep it up. It only goes somewhere else."

Devi snorted softly. Marcus grinned. But Noor looked up, and their eyes were wet, and Tomás understood that the words had landed somewhere private.

"That's true," Noor said. "About grief floating."

"It's true about most things," Tomás said. "Water finds its level. So does sorrow." He rose, joints announcing themselves, and refilled the mugs around the table — a ritual, a liturgy, the oldest sacrament of the working night. When he reached Noor, he paused, and let his hand rest briefly on the box's cardboard shoulder, the way you'd steady a stranger on a moving train.

"Whatever is in there," Tomás said, "it has waited this long. It can wait until you've eaten. Nothing in this world is improved by meeting it hungry."

Noor looked at the hand on the box, then up at Tomás, and something moved behind their eyes — suspicion first, the reflex of a person who has been handled by institutions, then something warmer, more frightened, more hopeful. The look of someone deciding whether to be known.

"How long have you worked here?" Noor asked.

"Long enough," Tomás said, "to know that the piano in the main hall has not been tuned since March, and that whoever has been dusting it anyway is doing it wrong, and that I have not reported them." He straightened, and gathered the empty tin, and did not look at Noor's face while he said it, because some mercies are easier delivered sideways. "A person should be allowed one secret in the building where they grew up. Even the building agrees. Why do you think it hides things behind wardrobes?"

The radio drifted. Outside, the blue hour had come and gone without witnesses, as it did every night, generous and unnoticed. Devi was saying something about her father, and Marcus was laughing, and the box sat on the table between them all, patient as a held breath.

Later, when the shift ended and the others dispersed into the gray morning, Tomás watched Noor carry the box back down the corridor toward the practice wing — not hiding it now, but not setting it down either. Still carrying it. But slower. As if the weight had redistributed itself among four sets of hands, even if only one pair was doing the lifting.

Tomás switched off the break room light and thought about the letters he had never sent, in a language and a country and a life thirty years gone, and he thought: *Tomorrow, maybe, the boy will say a name. Tomorrow, maybe, we will find out who M. Adeyemi is.* 

Because Tomás had read the envelope upside down, weeks of habit, a lifetime of reading the world sideways — and he had recognized the name the way you recognize a face in an old photograph. Mariama Adeyemi. Who had sung in the conservatory choir, once, before she vanished. Whose voice Tomás had never forgotten, and whose child was now carrying her mail through the dark.

He pocketed the break room key and went home to sleep, and did not yet say a word.
