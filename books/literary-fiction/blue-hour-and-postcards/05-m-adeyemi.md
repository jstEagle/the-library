## 5. M. Adeyemi

The city records office opened at nine, which meant Noor had four hours after the shift ended to become a person who could stand in line.

They went home first — the rented room above the laundromat on Corvus Street, where the dryers rattled the floorboards in a rhythm that had once, in a crueler mood of insomnia, sounded like a metronome. They showered with the water as hot as they could bear, as if the night's dust and the box's dust were the same contamination, and dressed in the only clothes that weren't gray with work: a black sweater gone soft at the elbows, trousers that still remembered a shape. In the mirror, a stranger adjusted a collar with hands that knew exactly how strangers' collars should sit. Twenty years of stage deportment doesn't leave you. It just waits for an occasion.

The box sat on the desk where they'd left it, forty-one letters bound in a rubber band gone brittle as pastry. Noor did not touch it. Touching it felt like touching a wound to check whether it was still bleeding. It was. Obviously it was.

Instead they took the last letter's envelope — no, not the last; the *earliest*, dated the spring before Noor was born — and copied the return address onto their palm in ballpoint: **E. Voss, 14 Kestrel Lane**. An address that no longer existed, swallowed decades ago by the riverfront redevelopment, but that wasn't the point. The point was the addressee, written in Elias Voss's small, upright hand, so controlled it looked typeset:

*M. Adeyemi.*

Not Mariama Halloran. Not even Mariama Adeyemi-Halloran, though Noor had seen that hyphenated name on school forms, on recital programs, on the one passport photo that survived in the tin box under their bed growing up. *M. Adeyemi.* Her maiden name, alone, unadorned — the name she'd worn when she and Elias were whatever they had been. He hadn't written to the woman she became. He'd written to the woman he'd lost.

Which meant the letters were addressed to someone who might have stopped existing entirely.

* * *

The municipal records office occupied the old customs house downtown, all granite and brass and the smell of cold paper, and Noor stood in the queue behind a man disputing a property lien and a woman trying to prove her grandmother's birth for citizenship, and understood suddenly that everyone in this line was doing the same thing: excavating. Trying to establish that someone had been here. That a life had left sediment.

The clerk was young, wire-rimmed glasses, a nameplate that said R. OKONKWO, and when Noor slid the request slip across the counter — *Mariama Adeyemi, b. approx. 1958–1962, last known address Alder Street* — the clerk looked up with the particular wariness of someone who processed grief daily and had learned to ration sympathy.

"Relationship to the record?"

Noor had rehearsed this. They'd rehearsed it on the bus, watching the harbor slide by. *Daughter,* they'd planned to say. *I'm her daughter.* But the word caught the way a note catches when your wrist locks — and what came out instead was:

"I'm checking on her behalf."

Okonkwo's eyes did something brief and professional. "Family member?"

"Yes." That much was true. Whatever else had rotted, that was load-bearing and true.

The clerk turned to the terminal. Noor watched the screen's glow reflect in the lenses and tried to read the data upside down, a skill learned over years of shared stands and conductors' scores, and failed. The keys clattered. Somewhere behind the partition a fax machine whined like something dying.

"Adeyemi," Okonkwo said. "Common enough spelling variants. A-d-e-y-e-m-i?" Noor nodded. "There's a voter registration from '98, Alder Street address. Then—" the clatter stopped, resumed, stopped again, "—a change-of-address filing, 2004. Outbound."

"Outbound where?"

"That's the thing." Okonkwo swiveled the monitor, hesitated, then seemed to decide Noor's face warranted the courtesy. "There isn't one. I mean, there's a filing — she filed it, or someone filed it for her — but the destination field is blank. Just says *relocating out of metro area*. Which happens more than you'd think. People leaving don't always know where they're going. Or they don't want the paper trail."

Twenty years ago. Noor did the arithmetic without wanting to. Two decades ago, Noor had been nineteen, mid-catastrophe, mid-flight themselves — the year of the collapsed competition, the year of the hand, the year they'd walked out of this city with a single suitcase and a teacher's voice in their ears saying *you'll come back when you're serious again.* The year their mother had already been gone three years. And now here was the paperwork ghost of her going: a change-of-address form with its heart cut out, filed the same season Noor fled in the other direction. Two people leaving the same city within months of each other, neither knowing the other was leaving, both blanks in each other's destination fields.

"Is there anything else? Anything recent?"

Okonkwo scrolled. Frowned. Scrolled back. "Utility deposit refund, 2004, mailed to the Alder Street address, returned undeliverable. And—" the pause stretched, "—there's a pension inquiry. State teachers' fund. She taught, apparently. Elementary. There was an inquiry about benefit disbursement eight years ago, from a P.O. box in—" Okonkwo squinted, "—Marlow Heights. That's out past the reservoir. Route 47 territory, if the 47 still went out that far."

"It does," Noor heard themself say. "It goes out that far."

"Then that's probably your best thread." Okonkwo printed something, stamped it, slid it across. "I can't give you the box number — privacy rules — but Marlow Heights is small. One post office. One grocery store, if it still has that. Places like that, people find people. If she wants to be found."

*If she wants to be found.* Noor took the printout without looking at it. The paper was warm from the printer, absurdly warm, like something alive.

* * *

Outside, the light had gone the flat pewter of early afternoon, and Noor stood on the customs house steps holding a piece of paper that said, in effect: *your mother may be alive, forty minutes away by bus, and has been for years.*

May be. Eight years since the pension inquiry. People went quiet in eight years. People died in eight years. Noor thought of the letters upstairs in the rented room, forty-one envelopes addressed to a woman who had possibly been dead while Noor mopped around her portrait, and felt the vertigo open beneath them again — the specific vertigo of learning that the past is not a sealed room but a corridor with doors you never noticed, some of them recently used.

They rode the 47 back toward the conservatory district without deciding to. Their body chose it, feet carrying them to the stop the way hands once found middle C in the dark. The bus was half empty, Tuesday-quiet, and Noor took a window seat and watched the route unspool backward through neighborhoods it wore like loose clothing: the warehouse conversions, the halal grocery with its awning faded to the pink of old skin, the laundromat (not theirs), the church with the hand-lettered sign — *REVIVAL THIS SUNDAY — ALL ARE WELCOME* — the welcome underlined twice, as if the sign-writer had doubted it.

Route 47. The bus of their childhood. Every Saturday for eleven years, this route, this direction or its reverse: home to the conservatory, conservatory to home, Mariama beside them with the canvas bag of sheet music on her lap, calling out stops like stations on a private railway. *That's where Mrs. Okafor lives, the one with the roses. That's where your father worked, before. That's the hill I fell down pregnant with you, God, I was sure I'd lose you.* The city narrated by a mother's voice, every block annotated. Noor had assumed those annotations were trivial. Now, riding the line as an adult, they realized they had lost the entire map — could not have said which corner held which memory — and the loss felt less like forgetting than like amputation.

At Marlow Junction the bus paused at a red light long enough for Noor to see the post office: a squat brick building with a wheelchair ramp and a notice board fluttering with community papers. Taped to the board's center, official cream stock against the weathered cork, a document Noor recognized by its shape before reading a word of it.

**PUBLIC NOTICE — SERVICE CHANGE.**

*Metro Transit hereby gives notice that effective the first of next month, Route 47 will be rerouted... service to Marlow Heights, Reservoir Road, and Alder Street corridor will be discontinued... riders are advised to consult the revised system map...*

Alder Street corridor.

The words went through Noor like a needle finding the one live wire. Alder Street — the address on the earliest envelope, the address in the records file, the street where Mariama had raised a child alone on a teacher's salary and a pianist's abandoned ambitions. The reroute notice didn't say *demolished* or *condemned*; it didn't need to. Everyone knew what discontinuation meant for a neighborhood like Marlow Heights. First the bus, then the customers, then the stores, then the people — a slow evacuation disguised as a scheduling decision. The city had done it to the riverfront districts twenty years ago. It was doing it again. Power deciding whose pasts remained accessible, whose addresses got to keep existing on a map.

And Noor had six weeks. Maybe less. Maybe the woman in the pension file was dead, and maybe she wasn't, but either way the window was closing — not slammed shut, nothing so dramatic, just easing down like a sash window in an old house, quietly, on the fingers of anyone too slow to pull them out.

Noor got off at Marlow Junction without planning to. Stood on the curb while the 47 sighed away southward. The post office notice board creaked in the wind. A woman was posting letters at the slot inside — Noor could see her through the glass, feeding envelopes in one by one, unhurried, as if the mail would wait forever, as if anything would.

They did not go in. Their legs simply declined, some older wisdom overriding the younger impulse, and Noor stood on the pavement of a neighborhood they'd left at nineteen and understood that they were not ready — that walking into a post office and asking after M. Adeyemi required a version of themselves that did not yet exist, that had to be built first, letter by letter, out of everything still sitting in the box on the desk above the laundromat.

Forty-one letters. Forty-one reasons. And somewhere along this corridor of shuttered storefronts and geraniums in paint cans, a woman had lived them all without ever receiving one.

The light changed. Nobody crossed. Noor crossed anyway, alone, against the signal, because the body wanted motion, any motion, away from the glass door and the woman mailing her letters into a world that still delivered.

On the ride back, dusk came down blue over the reservoir, and Noor pressed their forehead to the window and made the only vow available to a person with no right to vows: *before the route dies, before the last bus runs, I will know. Even if she slams the door. Even if she never forgives. I will stand on Alder Street once, as myself, and let her look at what became of us both.*

The bus hummed. The window was cold. Behind them, Marlow Heights shrank into the blue hour, and somewhere in it — or nowhere in it — a post office box waited with eight years of silence in it, and a name that Noor had spent twenty years unable to say aloud:

*Mariama.*

They practiced it against the glass, mouthed it, no sound. It fit the mouth better than expected. It fit like something remembered rather than learned.

Tomorrow night there would be floors to mop, and Devi's coffee, and Marcus's radio murmuring in the loading dock, and Tomás's slow circuits with his cart. Tomorrow night the box would be waiting. But tonight, riding the dying route home, Noor allowed themselves one clear thought, sharp as a struck key:

The letters had an address after all. All forty-one of them. It was forty minutes away, and it was closing.
