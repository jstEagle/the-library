## 7. Aunt Odile's Ledger

Aunt Odile lived eleven miles out of town, past the feed store and the shuttered drive-in, in a brick ranch house that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated surprises. The lawn was mowed to a military tolerance. The hedges were squared. Even the wind chime on the porch — copper pipes, tuned to some minor key — seemed to ring only when it had permission.

Beatrix sat in her car at the end of the driveway for six minutes by the dashboard clock, watching the house the way she used to watch the fire from the ridge road: unable to approach, unable to leave.

She had dressed for the visit like a woman going to a deposition. Plain blouse. Hair pinned. The postcard — no, not a postcard this time; a folder of questions she'd written out at the stationery shop counter between customers, on cream stock with Ruth's good fountain pen — sat on the passenger seat like a witness she'd subpoenaed.

Three years, and she had been inside Odile's house exactly twice: once for the reading of the will, once to sign a form releasing her mother's jewelry from an estate lockbox. Both times Odile had served coffee in cups too small and spoken in sentences too short, and both times Beatrix had left feeling like a tenant in her own grief.

But the demolition notice had changed the arithmetic. Forty days. And somewhere between the county clerk's office and her apartment, between the sanitized fire report and the missing witness statement, a question had begun to grow teeth in her: *Who inherits a burned house? Who wants it?*

Odile did. Odile wanted it gone so badly she'd petitioned the county twice before the order came through.

Beatrix got out of the car.

* * *

The door opened before she knocked. Odile Fairweather — born Lockridge, married badly, widowed worse — stood in the doorway in a gray cardigan buttoned to the throat, as if even in July she were braced against something.

"Beatrice." She always said it that way, the old spelling, the old weight. "I wondered how long the notice would take."

"The notice?"

"To bring you to my door." Odile stepped back to let her in. "Coffee?"

The living room was a museum of careful nothing. Doilies under lamps. A grandfather clock that ticked but had never, in Beatrix's memory, told the right time. Photographs on the mantel arranged chronologically like evidence: Odile's wedding, Odile's widowhood, Odile at Halcyon House one summer in the nineties, squinting into the sun with Elias on her hip, both of them laughing at whatever was just outside the frame.

Beatrix took the coffee because refusing would take longer than drinking it.

"I'll be direct," she said.

"You're always direct. It's your worst quality." Odile settled into her chair with the economy of a woman who had rehearsed every conversation of her life in advance. "Ask."

"Halcyon House. You inherited it outright. Mom's share lapsed when she—" Beatrix stopped. When she died, two years after Elias, of the slow kind of grief that doctors give polite names. "When she passed. So it's yours. And you've wanted it demolished since the ash cooled."

"Sentiment is expensive," Odile said. "Taxes on a ruin are more expensive."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I owe you."

The clock ticked its wrong time. Beatrix set down the cup. "There's a ledger," she said. "Ruth mentioned it. From the estate settlement. An inventory of the house's contents — what was assessed, what was claimed, what was disposed."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes. Not guilt. Beatrix knew guilt; she carried her own daily, wore it like a coat that never came off. This was faster than guilt. This was calculation deciding which door to show her.

"There's an inventory," Odile said. "Every estate has one."

"I want to see it."

"Why?"

Because the fire report says undetermined. Because a witness statement went missing. Because you've spent three years pushing a bulldozer toward that house like it owes you money. Because last night a dead man's song came through my radio and somebody in this town is holding his notebook like it's theirs.

What she said was: "Because Elias kept things. Notebooks. Tapes. He labeled everything. If there's an inventory, then either his things are listed — or they're missing. And either one tells me something."

For a long moment Odile looked at her niece the way one looks at weather approaching across open water. Then she rose, without hurry, and left the room. Beatrix heard a drawer open in the study, paper shifting, the particular silence of a woman deciding, page by page, what could survive being seen.

She came back with a green ledger, cloth-bound, corners worn soft. She set it on the coffee table between them like a card played face-up.

"Estate inventory," she said. "Done by the assessor's office, March after the fire. Everything salvageable. Most of it wasn't." Her finger traced down columns of cramped type. "Parlor furniture — disposed. Kitchen effects — disposed. Books, water-damaged — disposed. Clothing — disposed." The word *disposed* repeated down the page like a refrain, like a hymn sung over a grave. "You can read it yourself. I have nothing to hide."

And Beatrix believed her, which was the strange part. Odile Fairweather hid plenty — she could feel the hidden things stacked in the rooms of this house like furniture under sheets — but not in the ledger. The ledger was clean. The ledger was almost aggressively clean, each line item accounted for, each disposal signed and dated.

Except.

Her eye caught on the attic section near the bottom. *Attic contents:* two trunks of seasonal clothing, disposed. A roll of carpet, disposed. Boxes of Christmas ornaments, disposed.

And then a gap. A blank line where a line should be. Not crossed out — simply absent, the way a tooth is absent, the tongue returning again and again to the space.

"Aunt Odile," Beatrix said slowly, "what was in the attic the week Elias died?"

The clock ticked. Outside, the wind chime rang its minor chord, uninvited.

"Why do you ask that?"

"Because the inventory skips it. There's a line missing. Whoever wrote this listed Christmas ornaments, Odile. Nobody lists Christmas ornaments and forgets the rest of an attic."

Odile's hand came to rest flat on the ledger, possessive, closing it. But she didn't take it away. And when she spoke, her voice had changed register — lower, wearier, the voice of a woman setting down something heavy she'd been carrying up too many stairs.

"He'd taken over the attic," she said. "That last spring. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Of course you didn't. You were in the city. He called me instead." Something flickered across her face that might have been hurt, quickly filed away. "He was up there all hours. Carried equipment up those stairs — a recorder, microphones, stands, cables everywhere like black vines. Your grandmother's quilts nailed over the windows. He said the acoustics were good up there. Said the old house *sang.*" Her mouth thinned. "I told him he'd burn the place down with all that wiring. Old house, old circuits. I told him."

The words landed wrong and Odile knew it the instant they left her mouth; Beatrix watched her flinch at her own sentence, watched her reach for her coffee cup and find it empty and set it down again with slightly too much force.

"You told him," Beatrix repeated quietly.

"I told him," Odile said, steadier now, daring her. "And yes, Beatrice, I'm aware of how that sounds. Say what you've come to say."

"What happened to the equipment?"

"Gone."

"Gone how?"

"Gone." Odile spread her hands. "After the fire, when the investigators cleared us to enter — what was left of entering — I walked the house myself. I owed your brother that much. The attic floor was half gone, charred through. The roof above it open to the sky. And there was nothing up there. No machines. No stands. No cables. Fire doesn't selectively eat electronics, Beatrice. It eats everything or it leaves skeletons. There were skeletons of everything else — the trunks, the frames, the metal hinges of the ornament boxes fused into little sculptures. But no recorder. No microphones. Nothing."

Beatrix felt the room tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when someone shifts their weight below decks.

"When did you tell the police?"

A pause. Long enough to have texture.

"I didn't."

"You didn't tell the police that several thousand dollars of recording equipment vanished from a fatal fire?"

"I told the insurance company," Odile said. "They weren't interested. Equipment isn't covered separately under a standard policy, and they'd already decided the fire was electrical. One more anomaly in a burned house is just weather to those people." She met Beatrix's eyes. "And I didn't tell the sheriff because I knew what he'd say. He'd say a young man in financial trouble sold his gear before the fire and pocketed the cash. That's what everyone would say. That's what *you'd* say, if you were being honest."

"I'm not the one who needs to be honest here."

"No?" Odile leaned back. "Then let me ask you something, since we're conducting inquiries. Elias was twenty-six years old, unemployed, living rent-free in a house he didn't own, spending money he didn't have on equipment he couldn't afford. Where do you imagine that money came from? Where do you imagine the *equipment* came from?"

"I don't know."

"No. You don't. You know songs and cipher games and the boy who held your hand under the table at funerals. You don't know what he became in the last year, because you weren't here." She said it without cruelty, which made it worse. Cruelty would have been easier to dismiss. "I loved that boy. Whatever you think of me, I loved him. And I watched him spend his final year turning Halcyon House into a machine for talking to the future, because nobody in the present would listen to him. Including you."

The words went in like splinters, deep, and Beatrix let them, because some part of her had learned that anything sharp enough to hurt was probably true enough to keep.

"The equipment vanished," Beatrix said. "Before or after the fire?"

"If I knew that, I'd have told someone."

"But you have a suspicion."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Outside, a truck ground past on the gravel road and faded. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into something almost confidential, and Beatrix understood that she was being shown a door Odile had never intended to open — shown it deliberately, the way one feeds a dog one wants occupied elsewhere.

"There was a man," Odile said. "Who worked around the house that year. Handyman work. Roof patch, gutter clearing, hauling what needed hauling. He was around the property more than he was employed to be, if you understand me. Always had a reason. Always a ladder to move, a tool to return." She turned the ledger toward Beatrix and tapped one line near the bottom — *Misc. labor, D.W., $180, paid cash* — the initials sitting there in the assessor's neat hand like a confession nobody had read.

"D.W.," Beatrix said.

"Deacon Wray." Odile pronounced it carefully, tasting it. "He did six months upstate, starting the winter after the fire. Arson. A barn over in Corbett County — nothing to do with us, the papers were clear on that, different fire entirely. He's out now. Been out since spring." She closed the ledger and rested her hands on it. "I'm not telling you he took your brother's machines. I'm telling you that when I walked through that attic and found it stripped bare, the first name that came into my head was his. And the second thing that came into my head was that I was glad. Because a thief gives you a fire that makes sense. A thief means it was about the *things.*"

"And if it wasn't about the things?"

"Then it was about the house," Odile said, "and I want the house gone before anyone starts asking what else it was about."

The clock ticked its wrong hour. Beatrix stood, and her legs felt distant, borrowed. At the door Odile stopped her with one hand on the frame — not blocking, almost steadying, the gesture of an aunt from another lifetime.

"Whatever you think you're digging toward," Odile said, "ask yourself what Elias would have wanted dug up. He sealed those windows himself. He nailed the quilts over them. Some things a person records knowing no one should ever play them back."

"Then why record them?"

"Because the dead get the last word," Odile said. "It's the only privacy any of us are guaranteed." She released the doorframe. "Goodbye, Beatrice."

* * *

In the car, Beatrix wrote on the back of a stationery-shop postcard, her handwriting shaking:

*D.W. = Deacon Wray. Attic stripped. Recorder gone. Cash labor, $180. Out since spring.*

She stared at the initials until they blurred. Somewhere in this town, a man with a limp was walking around with her brother's voice in a box — the songs, the unfinished ones, maybe the finished one, maybe the voice she'd heard at 2 A.M. bleeding through the static, real and impossible and *held by someone.*

She started the engine. Eleven miles of road between her and the pawnshops on Delancey Street, and forty days minus one on the clock.

She drove.
