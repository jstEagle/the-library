## 8. The Pawnshop Man

The recorder had a name before it had a history: a Tandberg 9100, four-track, walnut veneer peeling at the corners like sunburned skin. Beatrix knew it the way she knew her own handwriting — Elias had saved for it through two summers of hauling shingles, and when he finally brought it home to Halcyon House he carried it up the stairs like an infant, both arms, chin tucked against the top of it, and set it down on his desk in the attic room where the light came in slanted and gold through the one window that didn't stick.

She had not thought about the recorder in three years. That was its own kind of confession — that grief is selective, that it hoards some objects and abandons others without explanation. She had thought endlessly about the piano, about his boots by the back door, about the coffee mug with the chipped handle that she still could not throw away. But the recorder had simply vanished from her mind along with everything else that burned, filed under *lost* the way the fire report had filed the cause under *undetermined*. It was Aunt Odile who put it back into her hands, or the shape of it, anyway, two days ago, over tea gone cold between them: *He'd been recording up there all week. On that machine of his. And when I went through what was left — nothing. No machine, no tapes. Fire doesn't take just one thing, Beatrix.*

Fire doesn't take just one thing. But a person does.

So now it was Thursday, her day off from Meridian Stationery, and she was standing on Delancey Street in front of a shop whose window held, in descending order of dignity: a saxophone missing two keys, a wall of guitars strung like cured fish, a glass case of watches stopped at forty different hours, and a hand-lettered sign reading WE BUY TOOLS · INSTRUMENTS · CAMERAS · MISC. The word *misc.* did more work than any word she had ever seen. Behind the glass, in the gloom past the saxophone, a man sat on a stool reading a paperback with a broken spine.

The bell above the door was brass and gave a flat note, half a step off, and Beatrix thought absurdly that Elias would have known which note it was trying to be.

Inside smelled of dust and machine oil and something faintly sweet, old varnish maybe, the smell of things that had been loved once and priced since. The man looked up. He was sixty-something, wide through the shoulders in the way of men who had done lifting work and then stopped but kept the frame, with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and a face like a map of somewhere dry.

"Help you?"

"I'm looking for a tape recorder," Beatrix said. "A Tandberg. Four-track. Walnut case."

Something moved behind the man's eyes — not recognition exactly, but the flicker of a man deciding how much of his memory he could afford to sell today. He took the glasses down out of his hair, put them on, and looked at her properly, which was worse than not looking at all.

"Tandberg," he repeated. "Norwegian machines. Good ones." He said it the way a priest might say a rival church's creed — respectful, noncommittal. "You looking to buy or looking to find?"

The question landed with a precision that told her he already knew the answer. "Find," she said. "It belonged to my brother. He died three years ago. It disappeared from his house before the fire was even cold."

The man set the paperback down on the counter, facedown, keeping his place. He studied her the way he might study a watch brought in for appraisal — turning her over, listening for the tick.

"You're the Lockridge girl," he said. Not a question. "From the paper. The house on Corben Hill."

"The house on Corben Hill," she agreed. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Three years of practice at saying things steadily.

"I'm Sol Perlmutter," he said, as if this settled something. Then he came around the counter, slowly, favoring his left knee, and turned the OPEN sign in the door, and flipped the deadbolt, though it was barely eleven in the morning and the street outside was empty as a drained pool. When he turned back he said, "Come sit down. This is a sitting-down story."

* * *

There were two chairs behind the counter, both mismatched, both upholstered in a fabric that had been green sometime during another administration. Sol lowered himself into one with the care of a man negotiating with his own joints, and Beatrix took the other, and between them, on a low shelf, sat a row of cassette decks in various states of dismemberment, their guts exposed — capstans and heads and belts like small black snakes.

"Three years ago," Sol began, "end of October, cold enough you can see your breath. Man comes in here maybe nine at night, ten minutes before close. I remember because I remember everybody who comes in the last ten minutes, because they're always either desperate or drunk or both." He rubbed his knee absently. "This one wasn't drunk. Desperate, maybe. He had a recorder under his arm wrapped in a moving blanket, and he wanted cash, and he wanted it fast, and he didn't want to write anything down."

"What did he look like?" Beatrix asked, though her heart had already begun to answer for him.

"Big. Not tall big — thick. Work-built. Maybe fifty, hard fifty. Walked like his left leg was shorter than his right, or like somebody had made it that way for him." Sol's eyes came up and held hers. "And he had ink on his right forearm, rolled-up sleeve, no attempt to hide it. Numbers. Prison numbers, the kind they do in block letters so they read across a yard."

Beatrix's hands, folded in her lap, had gone very still. "Did you buy it?"

"I bought it." He said it plainly, without apology, and she understood that apology would come later, if it came at all, and that it would be attached to something else. "Paid him eighty dollars for a machine worth four hundred, and he took it, because eighty dollars was what he needed and four hundred was more than he could wait for. That's the whole business model of a place like this, Miss Lockridge. I'm not proud of it. I'm just good at it."

"And the paperwork? You said he wouldn't write anything down."

"He wouldn't. Which should've been my first warning and wasn't, because the second thing I noticed was the machine itself." Sol leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his voice dropped into the register men use when they're about to say something they've rehearsed. "A Tandberg 9100 doesn't come in here every week. Doesn't come in here every decade. And this one had a name written inside the battery cover, in marker, the way musicians mark their gear. I saw it when I opened it up to test the transport."

Beatrix's mouth had gone dry. "What name?"

"*Elias,*" Sol said. "Just that. *Elias,* in block capitals, and underneath it a smaller line, handwritten, that I couldn't make sense of then." He paused. "I can make sense of it now, given what you just told me. It looked like a date. A date and a time. Like somebody logging a session."

The attic room. The slanted gold light. Elias hunched over the machine with his headphones on, mouthing lyrics to himself, oblivious to her standing in the doorway, and the little spiral notebook beside him where he wrote down takes — *take 3, good, keep; take 4, rush the bridge, scrap* — dates and times in his cramped forward-leaning hand. She had seen that notebook a hundred times. She had not seen it in three years either. Everything in that attic had evaporated, except the machine had not evaporated. Machines don't evaporate. Machines get carried down stairs by men with bad legs and prison ink, in the dark, while a house burns.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You could have said you never saw it."

Sol looked at her for a long moment. Outside the window a delivery truck went by, shaking the glass case of stopped watches into a brief silent tremor.

"Because I kept something," he said. "And I've been waiting three years for the person it belongs to walk through that door."

* * *

He led her into the back room, past a curtain of plastic strip blinds, into a space that was less a room than an archaeology of the town's discarded lives: bicycles, exercise equipment being used as clothes racks, boxes stenciled ESTATE in someone's dead father's handwriting. Sol went to a metal filing cabinet, unlocked it with a key from a ring of perhaps thirty, and drew out a shoebox. Not a metaphorical shoebox. An actual shoebox, soft-cornered with age, the brand name faded to a ghost.

"When I tested the machine," he said, holding the box against his chest like something rescued from water, "there was a cassette in the well. Half-spooled. Somebody had been recording and stopped mid-take, or mid-argument, or mid-whatever it was." His thumb moved over the box lid. "I should have put it back and sold the deck clean. That's what the law says, more or less. Possession's possession. But I played it."

"You played it," Beatrix repeated. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

"One time," Sol said. "In the shop, after close, on a deck I trust. One time, and then I never played it again, and I never sold it, and I never threw it away, and I have asked myself approximately every day since why." He held the box out to her. "I think the answer is that some things aren't inventory. I think the answer is I was waiting for you, even though I didn't know your name until I read it in the paper, and even then I figured you'd never come, because nobody comes looking for anything in a pawnshop. They come looking to forget."

Beatrix took the box. It weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible thing about cassettes, she thought — that a human voice, a whole life's worth of a human voice, weighed less than a letter.

Her fingers were not steady. She made herself open the lid anyway.

The cassette lay in a nest of cotton padding Sol had clearly arranged himself, carefully, the way you'd lay out something surgical. It was a standard ninety-minute shell, the label handwritten in Elias's slanted script — she knew it instantly, would have known it blind, by the way the *E* kicked its leg out — and the label read:

**SIDE B — DON'T.**

Side B — don't. Meaning: Side A was for playing. Side B was not. Meaning: Elias had marked his own tape the way he'd marked their cipher sentences, the way he'd taught her, at eight years old, cross-legged on the attic floor with a flashlight under a blanket, that some sentences are only safe when translated — that a message meant for one person must be armored against everyone else. *Don't* was armor. *Don't* was a door with his handwriting on it.

"Is there a Side A?" she managed.

"There was a Side A," Sol said. "There were six cassettes total in a rubber-banded brick inside the blanket with the machine. Your man sold me the lot. I called a friend at the college radio station the next week — WQXR-adjacent, the community outfit out past the rail yard — and moved five of them through legitimate channels, donated, receipt on file, because I didn't want them in my drawers and I didn't want them trashed." He hesitated, and Beatrix watched the hesitation move across his face like weather. "That was three years ago. Last month, a DJ from that same station comes in asking after vintage decks, and we got to talking, and she mentioned she plays things sometimes that people swear don't exist. Songs with no catalog number. Ghost tracks."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree. Marisol Vega. The song at 2 A.M. The melody only two living people had ever heard, and one of them was ash.

"That's where my song came from," Beatrix said quietly. "One of those five."

"Could be," Sol said. "I never wrote down titles. I've regretted that for three years too, if you're keeping a list." He nodded at the box in her hands. "But that one — the one in your hand — I kept. Because of what was on Side B."

"You said you played it once."

"I did." Sol's jaw worked. "And I'm going to tell you what I heard, Miss Lockridge, and then I'm going to let you decide whether you play it, because it isn't mine to play twice and it isn't mine to withhold. What I heard was your brother's voice, clear as Sunday, doing what I'd guess was a soundcheck — counting off, testing levels, saying the date. And then, maybe eight minutes in, the tape keeps rolling when it shouldn't. He forgot to stop it, or he meant to leave it running. And there's a second voice. A woman's voice. Older. And they are arguing about money, and about papers, and the woman says a sentence I have turned over roughly ten thousand times since, because I couldn't figure out who she was talking to."

Beatrix felt the room go narrow and bright at its edges, the way it did before a migraine, the way it had done the night of the fire when she stood at the end of the driveway and watched the windows fill with orange.

"What sentence," she said.

Sol recited it slowly, the way you'd recite something learned under duress:

"'Sign them over tonight, or I swear to God I will bury you in the paperwork myself.'"

The bell above the door gave its flat note — no, she realized, the bell hadn't rung; the truck outside again, or her own pulse. Sol was watching her with the careful kindness of a man who has handled a great many broken things and knows the weight class of this one.

"There's more," he said. "After the argument dies down, the tape runs quiet a long while. And then, near the end — last minute, maybe less — your brother talks again. Just him. Low, like he's talking to one specific person and knows she'll hear it someday." Sol's eyes didn't leave hers. "He says a name. And Miss Lockridge, I'll be honest with you — the name he says sounds a hell of a lot like yours."

* * *

She walked out of the shop with the shoebox held against her sternum, both arms, chin tucked — carrying it the way Elias had carried the machine up the stairs, though she didn't notice she was doing it until she was three blocks gone.

Delancey Street narrowed toward the rail yard, and the afternoon had gone the color of old pewter, and Beatrix stood at the corner under a dead streetlight and understood, with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like sentence, several things at once.

The recorder had not burned. A man with a limp and prison ink had carried it out of Halcyon House on the night her brother died — before the flames, or during, or after, but *out*, and had sold it for eighty dollars within the week, which meant the taking was casual, transactional, the act of a man clearing a house he believed unwatched. Which meant the fire and the theft were the same event wearing two coats. Which meant the official report — *origin undetermined*, witness statement missing — was not an absence of truth but a construction around it.

And at the center of it, sealed in a plastic shell lighter than a letter, was Elias's voice saying *don't*, and a woman's voice saying *bury you in the paperwork*, and — at the end, alone, low, meant for her — her own name.

She had spent three years mailing postcards to a ruin because she could not say one sentence. Now the sentence had a soundtrack. Now the dead man had left her instructions, and the instructions were the opposite of everything she had practiced: not *don't*, but *listen*.

Beatrix stood at the corner until the light changed twice, and then she did not cross toward home. She turned the other way, toward the bus stop, toward the rail yard, toward the squat cinderblock building with the antenna on its roof where a DJ named Marisol Vega played songs that didn't exist — because the five tapes Sol had moved through legitimate channels had gone to her station, which meant the rest of Elias's voice was sitting in some station closet three miles away, and whatever was on Side B, she was not going to hear it alone.

Behind her, in the window of the pawnshop, the wall of stopped watches hung in the dark, forty different hours, none of them moving.

Ahead of her, the sky over the rail yard had begun, very faintly, to bruise toward evening — the blue hour coming early, coming for her, patient as debt.
