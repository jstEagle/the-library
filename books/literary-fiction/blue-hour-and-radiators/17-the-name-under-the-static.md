## 17. The Name Under the Static

The station smelled of burnt coffee and warm electronics, a smell that had soaked into the walls over decades and now belonged to the building itself, the way a body belongs to its habits. Marisol's studio was small — two desks pushed together, a mixing board older than Beatrix, monitors with foam padding peeling at the corners like old fruit. A window looked out onto the dark parking lot where moths threw themselves against the sodium light in slow, devoted arcs.

"You're sure you want to hear this," Marisol said. It wasn't quite a question. She was already pulling up the file on her laptop, the cassette's contents digitized the night before while Beatrix sat in her car outside the station for forty minutes, gathering the courage to come in.

"No," Beatrix said honestly. "But I'm going to."

Marisol nodded as if that were the correct answer, maybe the only one she respected. "That's how it goes with ghosts. You don't want them. You just open the door anyway." She slid one headphone across the desk toward Beatrix, then hesitated, hand still on the earcup. "One thing first. House rule. Whatever's on this tape — you can stop us anytime. Say *stop*, I lift the fader, we take five minutes. No shame in it. I've had grown men cry into my board over three seconds of a dead wife's laugh."

"Has anyone ever asked you to stop?"

"Once. Then he came back the next week and asked me to play it again." Marisol set the headphones down. "Grief's like that. It wants to be heard more than it wants to be survived."

Beatrix put the headphones on. The world went soft and woolen around her. On the desk between them, the cassette lay in its clear plastic case like an exhibit, like a relic, like something that should have been buried with him.

"Side B," Marisol said. "Track four is where the floorboard starts picking things up. Elias recorded most of it close-mic'd, but the room was cheap and the walls were thinner than his talent. Everything underneath bled through." Her finger hovered over the spacebar. "Ready?"

"No," Beatrix said again, and this time Marisol smiled, a small crooked thing, and pressed play.

* * *

Elias's voice filled her head before his words did.

That was the thing no one warned you about — that the voice arrives first, whole, undamaged by time, and the body recognizes it before the mind can brace. Three years of silence, and then suddenly her brother was singing in her skull, half-finished, humming through a verse he hadn't written lyrics for yet, tapping the guitar's body with his knuckle the way he did when he was thinking, *tck tck tck*, a sound so specific and so his that Beatrix's eyes flooded before she could decide whether to let them.

"He hums when he's stuck," she said, not meaning to speak aloud.

"Yeah," Marisol said quietly. "He does."

The song broke off mid-phrase. A chair creaked. Footsteps — Elias leaving the attic room, or crossing it — and then the recording became what it had always secretly been: not a session but a room. The microphone kept running, faithful and indiscriminate, and beneath the hiss the house itself began to talk.

A radiator clanked somewhere below. A door opened and closed on another floor. And then, faint as weather, voices.

Marisol's hands moved across the board with the tenderness of a surgeon. She rolled back thirty seconds, isolated a frequency band, cut the rumble below 120 hertz, eased the presence up around 2 kilohertz where consonants lived. The voices rose out of the static like figures wading toward shore — still blurred, still wavering, but *shaped* now, human, argumentative.

"—told you I'd handle it," said the first voice. Male. Young. Elias.

The second voice answered, and the answer was lost under a burst of tape hiss, a long sibilant wash like wind through wires.

"There." Marisol leaned in. "Roll back again. I want to catch the tail of that word."

They worked like that for an hour, maybe two. Beatrix lost track. Time in the studio moved differently — each ten-second fragment of conversation stretched into minutes of forensic attention, played and replayed until the syllables wore grooves in her memory. She learned the argument the way you learn a song on the radio you hate: involuntarily, completely.

Money. That much was clear early. Elias saying *I don't care about the money* with a flatness that meant he cared about exactly nothing else. The other voice — low, controlled, wearing patience like a coat — repeating phrases that the tape mangled but whose rhythm Beatrix began to dread: *you don't understand what's at stake*, *it's not your name on anything*, *this stays in this family*.

"It's a woman," Marisol said at some point, sitting back. "You caught that, right? Second voice. Pitch sits lower when she's being careful, but it slips. Listen—" She rolled back again, soloed the channel, and there it was: a vowel opening upward at the end of a sentence, *fam-i-ly*, the *y* climbing into a register no man's throat would find naturally.

"I know," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, as if someone else were using her mouth. "It's Odile."

She had known since the second pass, if she was honest. Known the way you know a face in a crowd you've spent your life avoiding. Aunt Odile's voice had narrated every Christmas of her childhood, every scolding, every toast; it lived in her the way the cipher lived in her, carved somewhere below language. Hearing it argue with her dead brother about money and papers and family was not a revelation. It was a confirmation arriving three years late, dressed in static.

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. "You okay to keep going?"

"The tape doesn't care if I'm okay."

"No," Marisol agreed. "It really doesn't." She reached forward and rolled the audio back again. "There's more. Last night, when I digitized it, I heard something at the very end of track four. Under everything. I couldn't isolate it — didn't have the right tools, didn't have the time. But it's there. A third voice."

Beatrix's hands, resting on the desk, went very still.

"A third voice," she repeated.

"Elias. The woman. And someone else. Farther from the mic. Downstairs, maybe. Or—" Marisol stopped herself, which was its own kind of mercy. "Let's just see."

* * *

The work after that was slower, finer, the audio equivalent of brushing dirt from bone.

Marisol brought out software Beatrix didn't pretend to understand — spectral views where sound became landscape, ridges and valleys of color, the argument rendered as terrain they could study instead of merely endure. She found the stretch near the end of the fight where all three voices overlapped, Elias rising, the woman sharpening, and beneath both, at the edge of audibility, a third thread of speech, thin as wire.

"Floor transfer," Marisol murmured, mostly to herself. "Whoever this is, they're a full floor down. Maybe the parlor. Sound travels up through old joists like water through paper." She applied noise reduction in careful increments, each pass shaving a little more hiss, a little more of the radiator's clank, a little more of the tape's own aged breath. With each pass the third voice came closer to the surface, and with each pass Beatrix felt something tighten in her chest, some animal instinct that wanted to say *stop* — Marisol's own house rule, offered freely, no shame in it — and found that she could not make her mouth form the word.

Because some part of her, some part that had been listening for three years without admitting it, already knew who was speaking.

"Okay," Marisol said softly. "Here we go. From the top of the overlap."

She pressed play, and the argument swelled — Elias's voice cracking now, actually cracking, a sound Beatrix had only heard twice in his life, once at their father's funeral and once the night he told her he was never coming back to this town — and beneath it, filtered up through sixty years of joists and plaster and one catastrophic fire and three years of Beatrix's silence, the third voice spoke.

*"You promised me you wouldn't say anything."*

Five words. Slightly warped by distance, the vowels smeared, the consonants softened. But the pitch was unmistakable. The cadence was unmistakable. The little upward lilt on *anything*, the way the sentence ended almost as a question because the speaker had never in her life made a statement she wasn't prepared to retreat from —

Beatrix knew that voice the way she knew her own heartbeat. Better. She had spent twenty-six years hearing it from the inside.

"That's me," she said.

The words came out level, almost conversational, and then the room tilted.

Not metaphorically — or not only metaphorically. The studio seemed to list, the monitors swimming, Marisol's face suddenly very far away behind glass. Beatrix gripped the edge of the desk. The moths outside the window kept throwing themselves at the light. Somewhere in the building a compressor cycled on with a shudder, and all of it happened at a great remove, because Beatrix Lockridge was currently listening to her own voice arguing with her dead brother on a tape recorded the week he burned, a voice three years younger, a voice from a night she had spent three years failing to remember and three years succeeding at forgetting, and the voice was saying *you promised me you wouldn't say anything* —

"Stop," she whispered.

Marisol lifted the fader instantly. Silence rushed in, huge and total, the silence of a room with good insulation and bad news. Beatrix pulled the headphones off and set them down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something breakable when your hands are shaking too badly to trust.

"Take five," Marisol said. "Or fifty. Or forever. Your call."

Beatrix didn't answer. She was looking at the cassette in its case, at the little spools of brown tape wound tight inside, holding everything. Holding him. Holding Odile. Holding *her*.

"I was there," she said.

It was the first time she had said it aloud in three years. Not thought it — she had thought it a thousand times, in the gray hours, in the shapeless way thoughts avoid becoming confessions. Said. Aloud. To another person. The words left her mouth and hung in the studio air like smoke.

Marisol didn't move. Didn't lean in, didn't soften her face into sympathy, didn't do any of the things people do that make confession impossible. She just waited, hands folded on the desk, a woman accustomed to receiving transmissions from the dark.

"That night. The fire. I was at Halcyon House." Beatrix's voice sounded strange to her, stripped, unpracticed. "I've told everyone — I told the police, I told the county, I told myself — that I hadn't been there in weeks. That I'd gone home early. That I don't remember why Elias—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a hole. In my memory. Right where the important part should be. I've been mailing postcards to a burned house for three years because I can't finish the sentence, and apparently—" she gestured at the cassette, at the spectral view still glowing on the laptop, the argument frozen mid-waveform, "—apparently I finished it out loud, that night, and the house wrote it down for me."

"You think you're on this tape," Marisol said carefully, "arguing with him. Before the fire."

"I don't think. I *heard* myself." Beatrix laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Three years younger. God. I even sound younger. I sound like someone who still believed the family version of events."

Marisol was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Do you want to know what I hear? Professionally?"

"Yes."

"I hear a scared person. Whoever's downstairs — whoever that voice belongs to — they're not angry. They're afraid. Listen to the spacing." She rolled the audio back a few seconds, touched the fader up gently, and let the five words play once more into the padded room. *"You promised me you wouldn't say anything."* "Hear that breath before 'promised'? That's not accusation. That's someone begging. There's a difference, and it matters."

"Begging him not to tell," Beatrix said. "About what?"

"That," Marisol said, "is the question, isn't it. And it's yours, not mine." She turned back to the board, businesslike again, giving Beatrix the gift of procedure, of task, of something to do with her hands. "There's more after that line. Another exchange, maybe fifteen seconds, before the tape runs out or the fight moves out of range. We can map it. Clean it further. Get you every word that's recoverable." She glanced sideways. "If you want."

Outside, the sky was doing the thing it did at four in the morning, turning the color of dishwater, the sodium light in the parking lot growing pale and embarrassed. Beatrix thought of the postcards stacked in her closet at home, two hundred and fourteen of them, three years of sentences that circled and circled and never landed. She thought of the cipher Elias invented when they were children, the one where dangerous truths got translated into harmless ones, and how she had understood even then, at nine years old, that some sentences are only safe when translated — and how she had built her entire adult life into a kind of cipher, everything true encoded, nothing legible, not even to herself.

Especially not to herself.

"Play it," she said. "All of it. Whatever's left."

Marisol studied her for a beat, then nodded and rolled the tape back.

The argument resumed. Elias's voice, frayed now, moving away from the microphone — footsteps, the attic door, hinges complaining. The woman's voice following, lower, urgent, the words dissolving into the tape's dying fidelity: *not here, not now, someone will—*. And then the third voice, Beatrix's voice, younger, frightened, rising up through the floorboards one last time before the recording starved of signal, and this time the words came through clean enough to hurt:

*"Then fix it. Fix it tonight, or I swear I'll—*

The tape ended. Not faded — ended, cut, the spool running out mid-syllable, her own threat amputated at the wrist.

The studio held its silence. The compressor cycled off. A moth hit the window glass with a small, fatal tap.

"Fix what?" Marisol asked quietly. It was not an idle question. It was the question, and they both knew it, and it sat between them on the desk next to the cassette like a third party.

"I don't know," Beatrix said.

And the terrible thing — the vertigo of it, the free-fall — was that she was telling the truth. The memory was still a hole. But holes, she understood suddenly, sitting very straight in the humming dark of the studio, are not empty. They are shaped. And hers had just acquired edges: a promise broken, a demand made, a threat begun and unfinished, spoken in her own voice on the last night of her brother's life.

She stood up. Her legs held, which surprised her.

"I need to go somewhere," she said.

Marisol looked up. "Where?"

Beatrix was already reaching for her coat, her mind moving faster than her fear now, one step ahead of the vertigo instead of inside it. "To ask my aunt," she said, "what I promised Elias I wouldn't say."

* * *

The drive across town took eleven minutes and lasted approximately a decade. Dawn was breaking over the ridge in bands of iron and rose, and Halcyon House rose up at the end of Lockridge Road the way it always did, black-windowed, patient, its remaining walls holding the morning light the way cupped hands hold water. The demolition notice flapped on the fence, white and official, forty days reduced now to a number Beatrix refused to calculate.

She parked at the gate and sat with the engine ticking and looked at the house that had recorded her.

*Fix it tonight, or I swear I'll—*

Swear she'd what? Tell someone? Leave? Burn it down herself? The sentence had no ending, and the night had no memory, and between those two absences her whole life had been constructed, postcard by postcard, almost-saying by almost-saying.

Her phone buzzed in the cupholder. Marisol.

*Cleaned another section while you were driving. Track five, first minute. You'll want to sit down. Call me.*

Beatrix stared at the screen. Below the message, a second bubble appeared, and then a third, Marisol typing and deleting, typing and deleting, the ellipsis pulsing like a pulse.

Finally:

*Beatrix. The voice on track five isn't yours.*

*It isn't hers either.*
