## 19. Odile Denies Everything

Aunt Odile lived in a brick colonial on Corvair Street that looked like every answer to a question no one had asked. Beatrix had been inside it twice since the fire—once for the reading of the will, once to collect a box of Elias's winter coats that smelled of cedar and someone else's mothballs. Both times the house had struck her as a museum of surfaces: polished walnut, brass hinges, photographs of dead Fairweathers arranged in strict chronological procession down the hallway like witnesses waiting to be called. Nothing in it was soft. Nothing in it was allowed to be.

She parked at the curb and sat with the cassette in her lap for four full minutes. The tape was in a sandwich bag now, because she had read somewhere that evidence should be protected from the air, and because handling it bare-handed had begun to feel like handling a bone. Through the windshield she could see Odile's kitchen window, and behind it the silhouette of a woman moving with the unhurried precision of someone who had never once been surprised by anything.

Beatrix had rehearsed this on the drive over. She had rehearsed it in the shower, in the stockroom at the stationery shop, on the walk to Marisol's tower. In every rehearsal she opened strong. *Aunt Odile. I have something you need to hear.* In every rehearsal Odile's face did something it had never done in Beatrix's living memory: it changed.

She got out of the car. The October air had a tinny, end-of-season taste to it, and the maples along Corvair were letting go of their leaves in reluctant handfuls. The doorbell made a sound like a struck tuning fork. Somewhere inside, a dog that Odile did not own—Beatrix was almost sure Odile did not own a dog—barked twice and stopped, as if corrected.

The door opened. Odile stood in a cardigan the color of wet slate, her white hair pinned with the severity of a verdict. She was seventy-one and stood like a fence post driven into clay.

"Beatrix." Not a greeting. An identification.

"I need to talk to you."

"You're talking."

"Inside."

Odile's eyes did the arithmetic—Beatrix's face, the bag in her hand, the hour, the season—and Beatrix watched her arrive at *no* and then, deliberately, override it. Curiosity, in Odile Fairweather, was not warmth. It was inventory.

"Ten minutes," Odile said, and turned down the hall without waiting, which was her way of opening a door wider than any invitation.

* * *

The kitchen was white and gray and lit by the flat afternoon. Odile poured coffee without asking, into two cups, and set Beatrix's down with a small ceramic clink that sounded, in that room, like a gavel. Beatrix did not touch it. She set the sandwich bag on the counter between them.

Odile looked at it the way one looks at a dead bird on a porch. "What is that."

"It's a cassette."

"I can see it's a cassette."

"It's Elias." Beatrix heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt, which surprised her; inside, everything was moving like water finding a crack. "It's his voice. Songs he recorded in the attic, that summer. And underneath the music—" She stopped. Made herself go on. "Underneath the music there's a fight. Through the floorboards. Two voices. One of them is yours."

The kitchen held very still. The refrigerator hummed. Outside, a leaf let go of the maple and took its time coming down.

"You should have that looked at," Odile said. She picked up her coffee. "Tapes degrade. You get bleed-through, you get—phantom voices. People hear what they're primed to hear." She sipped. "You've been primed for three years, Beatrix. I'd worry about anyone who wasn't."

"It's you." Beatrix kept her voice low. That was the discipline she'd learned from three years of postcards: the lower the voice, the more it carried. "I had it cleaned. There's a woman at the radio station who does audio, she does it professionally, and we ran it until there was nothing left of the static. It's you, and it's someone arguing about the deed, about the papers, about money Elias found out you'd borrowed against the house. And then there's a car in the driveway, and then there's someone leaving with a satchel, and then—" Her throat closed on it. She made herself finish, because Elias had never gotten to finish anything. "Then the fire."

Odile set down her cup with exaggerated care, as though the cup were the only thing in the room capable of breaking. "And you brought this to me. To my kitchen. Instead of to the police."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was the question Beatrix had not rehearsed an answer to, and Odile knew it. That was the thing about her aunt—she had spent seventy-one years finding the seam in any fabric and putting one finger through it. Beatrix stood there with her mouth open and the truth sitting in her chest like a stone: *because I wanted to give you one chance to be innocent. Because if I take it to the county, it stops being Elias and becomes a case file. Because I am not sure what I did that night, and I was hoping you'd tell me.*

"Because he was my brother," Beatrix said.

"That," said Odile, "is not an answer. That is a sentiment. I asked why."

The refrigerator hummed. Beatrix became aware, with a strange clarity, of the photographs in the hallway—three generations of Fairweathers, all of them looking at the camera the way Odile was looking at her now, as if the camera owed them money.

"Because I wanted to hear you deny it," Beatrix said finally. "Out loud. To my face."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes then—quick, subterranean, gone—and Beatrix would spend a long time afterward trying to name it. It wasn't guilt. Guilt would have been almost a relief. It was closer to what a gambler feels when a card turns over early.

"Deny it," Odile repeated. She laughed, one syllable, dry as a receipt. "All right. I deny it. There. To your face. I was not in your brother's house the night he died, I did not argue with him about any papers, and I did not leave with any satchel. I was at Marian Kessler's, playing bridge, and Marian Kessler will tell you so, because Marian Kessler has never once in her life had an interesting evening and would remember one." She folded her hands on the counter. "Is that what you needed? Will that let you sleep?"

"No," Beatrix said. "Because you just told me the house was *his* house. 'Your brother's house.' But it wasn't, was it. Not by then. You'd mortgaged it. You'd encumbered it so many times it was more bank than home, and Elias found out, and that's what the argument was about."

For the first time, Odile went quiet in a way that had weight to it. Not the silence of innocence. The silence of a woman deciding, with visible deliberation, how much of the board to show.

"You've been busy," she said.

"I've had forty days."

"Ah." Odile's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "The demolition notice. You think that's a threat. You think I called the county." She shook her head slowly, and Beatrix hated how convincing she was—hated the way conviction and performance wore the same face in that family. "Beatrix. The house was coming down the day Elias died in it. I have been trying to give that property a decent burial for three years, and the town has been thanking me for it. You're the only mourner left. That's not a crime. That's arithmetic."

"You borrowed against it."

"I borrowed against everything I have ever owned, because that is what this family does. We inherit things and then we bleed them." Odile said it flatly, without shame, and that flatness was its own confession—just not of the one Beatrix had come for. "Your grandfather did it to the orchard. Your father did it to the orchard's memory. I did it to a house that was already half ash in my heart. None of that makes me an arsonist."

"But it makes you a motive."

The word landed. Beatrix watched it land—watched it hit Odile somewhere below the collarbones, in the place where even the best-built people keep their soft machinery—and watched her aunt absorb the hit and straighten, the way she had straightened at every funeral, every foreclosure, every hard frost.

"Yes," Odile said quietly. "I suppose it does. And you know what I've learned about motives in seventy-one years, Beatrix? Everyone has one. The county has one. The bank has one. That DJ woman playing ghost songs on the radio has one. And you—" she looked at her niece with something that was almost, terribly, tenderness, "—you have the largest one in this whole sorry county. You have three years of guilt with nowhere to put it. A person like that will find an arsonist in her own shadow."

* * *

Beatrix had expected the coldness. She had even expected the performance of it. What she had not expected—and what unseated her, what made the next few minutes happen the way they happened—was the flicker.

It came when she said, "Deacon Wray."

The name went through the kitchen like a draft. Odile's hand, reaching for the coffee pot, stopped mid-air—a full second, maybe two, a held breath of a hand—and then completed its journey and poured coffee that no one would drink.

"I don't know that name," Odile said.

"You do. He was your handyman. He fixed the porch railing at Halcyon House two summers before the fire. He's got a record now, arson, upstate, and he got out last spring, and last week somebody visited him at the halfway house on Delmore Road, and I don't think it was a social call."

"I don't know that name," Odile said again, and this time the repetition did the opposite of what repetition is supposed to do. The first denial had been furniture. The second was a door being held shut.

"You visited him," Beatrix said. "Or you sent someone. And two days after that visit, the man who saw your car in that driveway is going to disappear, the way the recorder disappeared, the way the witness statement disappeared from the fire file, the way everything in this family disappears except the houses and the debts—"

"Enough."

It was not loud. That was the frightening part. Odile said *enough* the way a judge says it, and the kitchen obeyed, and Beatrix realized her own hands were shaking around the sandwich bag.

And then Odile frightened her.

It was not a raising of the voice. It was a lowering. It was Odile setting down the coffee pot and coming around the counter, slowly, her cardigan whispering against the cabinets, until they were close enough that Beatrix could smell her—camphor and wool and something medicinal underneath—and see, for the first time at this distance in three years, the actual topography of her aunt's face: the fine map of veins at the temples, the small tremor in the left eyelid that Beatrix had forgotten she'd inherited.

"You want to know what I'm frightened of?" Odile said, very softly. "I'll tell you, since we're being honest in my kitchen. I'm frightened of you. Not of your tape. Tapes are nothing, tapes are for lawyers, and my lawyer eats tapes for breakfast. I'm frightened of a girl who spent three years writing letters to a burned house, because I know what that is. I did it myself, after your grandfather died. I wrote to an orchard. Do you understand? Grief like that doesn't want the truth. Grief like that wants a *shape*, and it will carve one out of anything—out of a pawnshop, out of a DJ, out of a handyman with a limp—and it will not care what it cuts away to get there."

"I'm not—"

"You are your father's daughter and you are a Fairweather and you are building a story," Odile said, "and the story has a villain, and the villain is convenient, and the story is keeping you alive. I know. I know because I have done it. But listen to me now, girl, because I am going to say the one true thing in this house."

And then she was vicious. Not shouting—vicious the way winter is vicious, all at once and everywhere.

"That house ate your brother long before the fire," she said. "It ate him the way it ate your father, and your grandfather, and it would have eaten you if you'd stayed. Elias was in that attic with a four-track and a head full of songs because the house was the only place he could breathe, and that is not love, Beatrix, that is *infestation*. You think you're defending him. You're defending the thing that consumed him. And you're doing it because if the house is innocent, then someone has to be guilty, and you cannot bear—" her voice caught, one hairline fracture, sealed instantly, "—you cannot bear the arithmetic of a fire with no one to blame."

The room rang with it. Beatrix stood very still. Somewhere far off, the dog barked again and was corrected again, and the afternoon light had gone the color of old paper.

"You're lying," Beatrix said, but it came out thin.

"I have never once lied to you," Odile said. "That's the tragedy of this family. We never have to."

* * *

Beatrix made it to the hallway before her legs argued. The photographs watched her go—generations of the same jaw, the same eyelid tremor, the same talent for standing very straight while everything underneath went soft. She stopped, without meaning to, at the one photograph she always stopped at: Odile at nineteen, standing on the porch of Halcyon House in a white summer dress, one hand on the railing Deacon Wray would one day repair. She was laughing in it. Beatrix had never once, in her entire life, seen Odile laugh, and here was proof that the machinery had once worked, and somehow that was the most damning evidence in the whole county.

"She loved that house," said a voice behind her. Odile had followed her into the hall, quiet as a draft. "That's the part your story doesn't have room for. You need me to be a woman who burned down a house she hated. I'm a woman who burned down a house she loved, because loving it was killing me slower than fire would." She paused at the end of the hall. "Take your tape to the police, Beatrix. I mean that. Take it tomorrow. Because when you do, they'll pull the file, and they'll find what I found when I pulled it two years ago—and you'll learn what I learned about that night, and you will wish to God you'd let the house come down in peace."

"What's in the file?"

Odile opened the front door. The cold came in around her cardigan like a second garment.

"Ask yourself why you really left that night," she said. "And ask yourself why you've never once finished the memory."

The door closed with the small, precise click of a lock that had never needed forcing.

Beatrix stood on the porch a long time, the sandwich bag cold against her palm, and the last thing she saw before she drove away was the upstairs window of the colonial—the one that faced, if houses could be said to face anything, in the direction of Halcyon House—and a curtain that moved, though there was no wind.

She did not go home. Not right away. She drove past the shop, past the tower, past the county building with its lit windows and its paperwork in order, and it was nearly dark when she finally parked outside her own apartment—and that was when she saw it, from the street: her window, third floor, standing open an inch, swaying in the October wind, when she had locked it that morning.

The stairwell smelled of dust and someone else's cigarettes. Her door was ajar.

The cassette was still in her hand. Everything else was gone.
