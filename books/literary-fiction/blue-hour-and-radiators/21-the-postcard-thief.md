## 21. The Postcard Thief

The apartment looked like it had been searched by someone polite.

That was the first thing Beatrix noticed, standing in her doorway at six in the morning with her coat still on and the key still in the lock, as though politeness were a clue in itself. The cushions were back on the sofa. The books were squared on the shelf, spines aligned, though two of them were upside down. The kitchen drawers had been closed again after being emptied onto the floor and swept back in — she could tell because the whisk lay on top of the spatulas instead of beneath them, because the takeout menus were fanned rather than stacked, because a person's hands leave a signature the way handwriting does, and these hands had tried to erase themselves and failed.

They had taken three things. The cassette. The shoebox of postcards — all two hundred and thirteen of them, every unsent confession of three years, gone. And nothing else. Not the silver bracelet her mother had left her, not the envelope of cash in the flour canister, not the laptop sitting in plain view on the desk like an invitation.

Whoever it was hadn't come for money or electronics. They had come for the record. For the evidence of what Beatrix had been saying, night after night, year after year, to a burned house that couldn't answer.

She stood in the middle of the room for a long time without moving. The radiator ticked against the silence. Outside, a garbage truck groaned through its rounds, and somewhere below a dog was being scolded for something minor, and the town went on being the town, ordinary and complicit, while her apartment held its breath around her.

*Stop digging or be dug under.* The note was still there, weighted under the upside-down book, the letters cut from newsprint in a way that felt almost quaint, almost theatrical, as if the thief had learned threatening from movies. She picked it up again and read it a fourth time, a fifth, looking past the words to the paper itself. A grocery circular. The *Gazette*, probably — the same stock the shop sold by the bundle for packing.

The shop.

She put the note down carefully, the way you put down something venomous, and went to the window, and did not look at anything in particular, and let the thought arrive at its own pace, because she had learned — three years of learning it — that if you chased a thought too hard it lied to you.

* * *

By nine she had a list, written on the back of a deposit slip in the break room of Fairweather & Sons Stationers, where she was employed and where, this morning, she could barely stand to be.

Everyone, she wrote at the top. Then, beneath it:

*Aunt Odile.* Obvious. Motivated. Capable of hiring it done. But Odile had lawyers now, circling like gulls over a fish market; Odile did her damage through paperwork and deniable intermediaries, and besides, Odile didn't know about the postcards. Did she? Beatrix turned the question over and found it soft at the center. Nobody knew about the postcards. That had been the entire architecture of the ritual — that they went out into the world addressed to a ruin, delivered to no one, witnessed by no one. Two hundred and thirteen confessions mailed into a void, which is the only place a confession is safe.

*Deacon Wray.* He had motive to want the tape gone, if he'd been paid once already for his silence. But Deacon was hiding somewhere with his own fear for company; he didn't have the polish this burglary required. The man who'd searched her apartment had folded the throw blanket. Deacon Wray struck her as a man who had never folded anything in his life.

*Odile's lawyer.* A name she didn't know attached to a firm she did — Halloway & Pruitt, whose letterhead had begun appearing on everything lately, formal and glacial. Possible. Lawyers subcontracted unpleasantness the way other people subcontracted roofing.

*Marisol.* The thought arrived and she hated it instantly and made herself keep it anyway, because the whole lesson of the last month was that the people you exonerated by love were exactly the ones you needed to examine by light. Marisol knew about the tape. Marisol had handled it. Marisol had quit on air, publicly, spectacularly — and what if quitting had been cover? What if the threats had been staged, the sacrifice performed, all of it to move the cassette from one pair of hands to another without suspicion?

No. Beatrix pressed her thumb hard against the pen until the plastic creaked. No. She had watched Marisol's face when the last song ended and the dead air opened up like water. You couldn't perform that. And Marisol didn't know about the postcards either.

Which was the problem. She sat back. Everyone on the list had reason to want the tape. Only one person in the world had reason to want the postcards — because only one person in the world knew they existed.

Only one person had ever known where she kept them.

* * *

It had been Ruth's idea, originally. Or not the idea — the idea had been Beatrix's, born in the blue hour of the first anniversary, when she'd walked out of the shop at closing with a stack of postcards she had no intention of sending and nowhere to put them. She remembered standing on the sidewalk with them clutched to her chest like a wounded bird, and Ruth locking the door behind her, and Ruth looking at the stack, and Ruth saying nothing, which was Ruth's particular genius: she could make a silence do the work of a hundred questions.

Beatrix had kept them in a drawer at home for the first months. Then the drawer became a shoebox, and the shoebox migrated — under the bed, behind the winter coats, finally to the closet shelf in a hatbox that had belonged to her grandmother. And every week she came into the shop and bought more postcards, always the same kind, the heavy linen stock with the faded engraving of Halcyon House on the front — the house before the fire, porch columns white against a sky that no longer existed anywhere but in ink — and Ruth rang them up and never once said, *You've bought four hundred of these, Beatrix, where are they all going?*

Never once asked. Which meant, Beatrix understood now, standing very still in the break room while the kettle ticked toward boiling, that Ruth had known. Had known and had answered the question before it was asked, with silence, the way you answer a knock you've been expecting.

But knowing wasn't stealing. Knowing wasn't ransacking. There was a distance between reading a woman's grief across a counter and breaking into her home, and Beatrix wanted — needed — to believe the distance was unbridgeable.

Then she remembered the day of the demolition notice.

Forty days posted on the fence, the town celebrating, and Beatrix had come into the shop shaking, and Ruth had poured her tea from the back-room kettle without asking, and Beatrix had talked — more than she'd ever talked — about the county, about the deadline, about how the wrecking ball would fall on whatever the postcards were still trying to say. And Ruth had listened with her hands folded on the counter, and at the end she'd said, "Where do you keep them? In case of fire."

In case of fire. As if it were practicality. As if any woman who had lost a brother to flames would find that phrase comforting rather than surgical.

Beatrix had told her. Hatbox. Closet shelf. Top shelf, because Beatrix was tall and no one else came to her apartment anyway.

The kettle screamed and she didn't hear it for a moment. Then she crossed the break room and lifted it off the heat with steady hands, because her hands had learned steadiness the way soldiers learn marching — not because the ground isn't shaking but because falling down helps nothing.

* * *

Ruth Ellery had worked for Odile Lockridge for eleven years.

Beatrix found this out in forty minutes, in the public library, in the local history room that smelled of dust and binding glue, with the town's accumulated paper spread around her like a crime scene of its own. The *Gazette* archives first: a photograph, 1998, ribbon-cutting at the renovated community hall, and there in the second row, younger by two decades and unsmiling in a way that the caption called *dignified*, stood R. Ellery, "longtime assistant to Mrs. Odile Lockridge." Eleven years of assistantship. Eleven years of typing Odile's letters, filing Odile's papers, answering Odile's door. Eleven years ending — Beatrix checked the dates against what she knew — roughly two years before the fire.

Two years before the fire. Long enough to leave. Short enough to know everything.

And then the employment gap, and then: Fairweather & Sons, part-time, then full-time, then owner when old Mr. Fairweather retired and sold the shop to the quiet woman everyone agreed was dependable. Dependable Ruth. Discreet Ruth. Ruth who noticed everything and said nothing, who had spent a career being paid to notice everything and say nothing, and who had simply transferred the skill, intact, from one employer to a stationery counter, where the merchandise was paper and the customers were confessions.

Beatrix sat with the photograph until the library lights buzzed on for evening, looking at the young Ruth's face, trying to find the woman who had poured her tea, who had stocked the linen postcards without being asked, who had once — she remembered this with a lurch — once slipped an extra card into Beatrix's purchase and said, "On me. You seem like you're working on something long."

Working on something long. Three years. Two hundred and thirteen cards. Had it been kindness? Had it been surveillance? Was there a difference anymore, from inside a life that had been watched so patiently?

Because here was the thing she couldn't get around, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: whoever had taken the cards had taken *all* of them. Not sampled. Not photographed and returned. Taken. And the note said stop digging — which was Odile's grammar, Odile's threat, the language of a woman who buried things and called the burial estate management. If Ruth had done this, Ruth had done it for Odile, eleven years of loyalty reaching across two decades to close one last file.

Unless.

Unless Ruth had done it to protect them.

The thought arrived sideways, unwelcome, and refused to leave. Because Beatrix had spent one sleepless hour already imagining those cards in Odile's hands — *I'm sorry, I should have told someone, Elias I heard the car, Elias I saw the satchel, Elias why did you make me promise* — imagined Odile reading her way through three years of a grief she had manufactured, and the imagining had been unbearable in a way that had nothing to do with exposure and everything to do with violation. Those sentences had been addressed to a burned house. To Elias. To the dark. They had never been meant for the living, and least of all for her.

If Ruth understood that — if Ruth, who had read them, or some of them, or enough of them — then taking the box might be the cruelest mercy available. Get them away from the apartment before Odile's professionals came back better prepared. Hide them. Keep them the way they'd been kept all along: unread by the wrong eyes.

Or that was the story a traitor would tell herself. Or the story a loyal friend would tell herself about a traitor. Beatrix had learned, in the last month, that every version of events came with a narrator, and every narrator had a debt.

* * *

She closed the shop herself that night, because Ruth had left early — Ruth, who had never left early in three years, who had called at noon with a voice like smoothed gravel and said her sister needed her in Corvallis, wouldn't be back till late, could Beatrix manage the close?

Her sister in Corvallis. Beatrix had said of course. Beatrix had hung up the phone and stood in the aisle of envelopes feeling the whole shop tilt around her, because in eleven years she had never once heard Ruth mention a sister, and because she had checked — quietly, at lunch, from the payphone outside the diner — and there was no listing for ELLERY in Corvallis, and there was no listing for ELLERY anywhere within fifty miles except the small brick bungalow on Delancey Street where Ruth had lived alone since her husband died, according to the tax rolls, according to the librarian who knew everything and charged nothing.

So Ruth had lied. Mildly, plausibly, in the voice of a woman who had lied before — professionally, expertly, for eleven years — and the lie was hanging in the air of the empty shop like smoke.

Beatrix locked the register. She turned the sign. She stood a moment in the lamplight among the pens and papers and the wall of postcards, that bright gallery of places none of the customers had ever been, and she thought about the linen stock with the engraving of Halcyon House, the card Ruth had ordered special years ago, before Beatrix ever worked there — she'd checked that too, in the wholesaler's catalog, the item marked DISCONTINUED 2003, last case sold to F&S Stationers — and she thought: she ordered them before I needed them. Before the fire. Years before. Why does a stationer in a town this size order four hundred postcards of one house?

Unless the house mattered to someone who mattered to Ruth. Unless the order had been placed back when Ruth still typed Odile's letters and filed Odile's papers, back when Halcyon House was just the family seat, and the postcards were — what? Inventory for a project? A favor? A way of keeping the house's image circulating, the way you keep a portrait up after the subject dies?

Or the simplest answer, the one she'd been circling for hours: Ruth had ordered them because Odile asked her to, and Ruth had spent twenty years doing what Odile asked, and the habit had outlived the employment the way a scar outlives the wound.

Beatrix turned off the lamps one by one. In the dark the shop smelled of paper and glue and cold metal, honest smells, and she hated how much she wanted them to still be honest.

She stepped out onto the sidewalk and locked the door, and the key made its small reliable sound, and down the street the town glowed its few orange lights, and above it all the sky was doing the thing it did here in autumn — going that deep bruised blue that Elias used to call the blue hour, the hour he claimed the world told the truth because it was too tired to lie.

"Blue hour," she said to no one. "Tell me the truth."

The truth, as far as she could assemble it, fit in her cupped hands like something broken: Ruth had known about the cards. Ruth had known where they lived. Ruth had been Odile's for eleven years and had lied about her whereabouts today, on the day after the burglary, which meant either that Ruth was guilty or that Ruth was afraid, and those two conditions, in Beatrix's recent experience, wore identical coats.

There was one way to know. It was ten blocks to Delancey Street, and the night was cold, and she had spent three years mailing her courage to an address that couldn't receive it. Tonight the address had a lit window. Tonight the address might answer.

Beatrix pulled her coat tight and started walking, and behind her, in the dark shop, on the counter where she'd left it, the burglar's note lay face-up in the streetlight through the window — *stop digging or be dug under* — and she realized, ten steps out, that she had left it there on purpose, and that she was not entirely sure for whom.
