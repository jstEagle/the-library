## 25. Twenty Days Left

The county administration building smelled of toner and old coffee and the particular despair of linoleum that had been mopped for forty years by the same indifferent mop. Beatrix stood at the counter of the Records and Permits office with her folder squared in both hands, and she watched a woman named—according to the little plastic tent—DARLENE count paperclips with the serenity of someone counting rosary beads.

"Help you?" Darlene said, without looking up.

"I'm here to file a petition to halt a demolition order. Pending investigation."

Darlene looked up then. She had the face of a woman who had heard every sentence the county could produce and had been unimpressed by all of them. "Which property?"

"Halcyon House. Ashford Road."

Something flickered behind Darlene's eyes—not recognition exactly, but the shadow of it, the way a fish's shadow crosses a pond. Everyone in Marlow County knew the address. Everyone had an opinion about the address. The opinion, mostly, was *finally*.

"Form 12-C," Darlene said, sliding a sheet across the counter. "Petition for Emergency Stay of Scheduled Demolition. You'll need grounds. Documented grounds."

Beatrix had documented grounds. She had spent eleven days assembling them the way she had once, as a child, assembled arguments for why she should be allowed to keep a wounded crow in the attic—carefully, obsessively, with the full knowledge that the adult world would find a way to say no. She had copies of the fire report with its origin listed as *undetermined*, which was a word that meant *we didn't look*. She had the missing witness statement, its absence noted in the margin of the file in someone's faded blue pen. She had the pawnshop receipt, photographed through glass because the pawnbroker had refused to hand it over without a subpoena. She had Deacon Wray's half-burned notebook of testimony, photocopied page by page at the library for ten cents a sheet, Marisol feeding the machine quarters like a woman feeding a slot machine she intended to beat.

And she had the tape. Or the memory of the tape, and the transcript Marisol had made of it at three in the morning, headphones on, one finger raised to silence Beatrix every time a word surfaced from the static like a body surfacing in a river.

She laid it all on the counter. Darlene looked at the pile the way one looks at a cat that has brought in something dead.

"You'll want to fill out the form," Darlene said.

* * *

The waiting room of the County Commissioner's office had four chairs, three of them broken in ways that suggested they had been broken for years and no one had ever been moved to fix them, which Beatrix thought was probably the most honest thing in the building. She sat in the fourth chair with her folder on her knees and listened to a clock that ticked slightly behind the clock in the hallway, so that the two of them argued in a language of small disagreements, and no one ever won.

Commissioner Hale received her at 2:40, eleven minutes after her appointment, and shook her hand with a grip calibrated to convey fairness. He was a broad, ruddy man with the tan of someone who golfed and the eyes of someone who had learned, long ago, that most problems were actually paperwork problems, and paperwork could be filed.

"Miss Lockridge." He said her name gently. Everyone said her name gently now, the way you'd speak to someone standing at the edge of a bridge. "I've read your submission."

"Then you know the fire report is incomplete."

"I know the fire report is *closed*," Hale said, settling behind his desk with the sound of a man arriving at a destination. "There's a difference, and I understand you feel it, but the law works on the second thing, not the first."

"The fire was ruled undetermined. Undetermined isn't closed. Undetermined is *unfinished*."

Hale folded his hands. "An investigation has since been opened into certain—matters. I'm aware. That investigation doesn't automatically stay a demolition order. The structure is condemned. It's a hazard. There are children who cut through that property, Miss Lockridge. There's asbestos in the shingles. The county has a responsibility."

"The county had a responsibility three years ago," Beatrix said, "when it accepted a fire report with a missing witness statement and didn't ask where the witness went."

Something moved at the corner of Hale's mouth. Not a smile. A flinch, maybe, wearing a smile's coat. "That's not my office."

"Whose office is it?"

"That's not—" He exhaled through his nose, a controlled little weather system. "Miss Lockridge. I'm going to speak plainly, because I think you deserve plain speech, even if you won't like it. The paperwork on this demolition is in order. Every signature, every notice, every posting. It was in order when the order was signed and it's in order now. If I halt it on the basis of—" he gestured at her folder, at the photocopied notebook, the transcript, the receipt photographed through pawnshop glass—"*this*, I open the county to liability from every contractor, every insurer, every neighbor who's been waiting three years for that lot to be cleared. You're asking me to spend the county's money and credibility on a theory."

"It's not a theory. There's a tape."

"There's a tape of an argument," Hale said. "Through a floorboard. That your—forgive me—that your DJ friend enhanced on equipment I'm told is a modified eight-track in a radio station's basement. You'll forgive me if I don't condemn a building over it."

Beatrix felt the old heat rise in her, the one she had been swallowing for three years, the one that tasted like copper and like her brother's name. She made herself breathe. She had learned, in the stationery shop, selling sympathy cards to the grieving, that the worst thing you could do was let them see you shake. They needed your stillness more than your sympathy.

"Twenty days," she said. "That's all I'm asking. Stay it twenty days. Let the investigation—"

"The investigation will take what it takes," Hale said. "The demolition will take what it takes. I'm sorry about your brother. I mean that. I knew Elias a little—he built my neighbor's fence, did beautiful work. But grief isn't grounds, Miss Lockridge. Form 12-C requires grounds."

He slid the form back across the desk. It had a red DENIED stamp on it now, though Beatrix had not seen him stamp it, had not heard the thump of it, and she understood that the stamp had been waiting for her before she ever arrived, that it had perhaps been waiting for three years, that the whole building was a machine for producing that stamp and the stamp was the only honest thing in it.

"Is there an appeal?" she asked, because the form said there was, in small print, the way forms always offered you a door that was really a wall.

"District court. You'd need a lawyer. You'd need a bond. And candidly—" he stood, extending the handshake again, the fairness grip, the grip that meant *this is over*—"candidly, you'd lose."

* * *

She sat in her car in the county lot for a long time. The November light came down flat and gray, the light that made everything look already photographed, already archived. Through the windshield she could see the flag on the pole out front, hanging limp, and beyond it the water tower with MARLOW painted on its side, the O faded so it read MARL W, and no one had ever fixed it, and everyone had stopped seeing it.

Her phone buzzed. Marisol.

"Well?" No hello. Marisol didn't believe in hello; she believed in signal, not noise.

"Denied. Paperwork's in order."

Marisol was quiet on the line. Beatrix could hear the station behind her—the hum of equipment, someone laughing far away, the ghost of a song between songs. "You want me to say something encouraging or something true?"

"True."

"True is, I've been reading the county code at two in the morning, which is what my life has become, and here's what I found: a demolition order can be stayed by the commissioners, by the court, or by a finding from the state fire marshal that the original investigation was compromised. We don't have the marshal. We don't have the court. And we don't have the commissioners, because you just met the commissioners."

"Thank you, Marisol."

"But," Marisol said, and Beatrix could hear her leaning toward the microphone she wasn't speaking into, the way she leaned toward everything, as if the whole world were a broadcast waiting to happen, "here's the other true thing. The paperwork's in order. That's their whole case. That's *all* their case. You know what that means?"

"That they win."

"That they're *afraid*," Marisol said. "Nobody hides behind paperwork unless they're afraid of what's outside it. Deacon's notebook says what he saw. The tape says what it says. Odile's lawyer has been circling the halfway house like a vulture with a briefcase. They're not worried about asbestos, Beatrix. They're worried about the twenty-first of December, when that ball swings, and there's no house left to dig under."

Beatrix looked at the DENIED stamp through the plastic sleeve of her folder. Red ink, slightly crooked, the way stamps always were, the human hand underneath the machine.

"Deacon signed the affidavit," she said. "But a signed affidavit in a drawer is just paper. Hale said it himself. Grief isn't grounds."

"Then stop bringing them grief," Marisol said. "Bring them something they can't file."

* * *

That evening she walked out to the house.

She hadn't meant to. Her feet made the decision somewhere around the corner of Delano Street and she let them, because arguing with her feet had never once worked. The town was putting up its Christmas lights early this year, or maybe on time and she had lost track of time the way you lose track of a word by saying it too many times. *Merry* and *Christmas* in green and red bulbs along Main Street, and a plywood nativity in front of the hardware store with one wise man face-down in the frost, toppled by wind, waiting for someone to right him.

Ashford Road was quieter. Ashford Road was always quieter, as if the street itself had been holding its breath for three years.

The demolition notice was still nailed to the fence, weather-curled now, the county seal gone soft at the edges. **NOTICE OF DEMOLITION. STRUCTURE CONDEMNED. SCHEDULED: ON OR ABOUT DECEMBER 21.** Twenty days. Someone had written something in marker across the bottom—kids, probably—**GOOD RIDDANCE**, and beneath that, in different handwriting, smaller: *my grandma lived here you little shit*.

Beatrix stood at the fence a while, reading the two sentences over and over, the whole town in miniature, the celebration and the grief, both in marker, both permanent, neither one true alone.

Through the bare sycamores she could see the house. Half of it, anyway. The east wing was gone—had been gone since the fire, a black jaw of joists open to the sky—and the rest stood in that state ruins have, which is a kind of patience. The parlor windows were dark holes. The chimney still stood, absurdly, the way chimneys do, the last organ of a body to stop working. In the blue hour the house turned the color of a bruise healing, purple going gold at the edges, and Beatrix thought: *twenty days*, and thought: *he built Commissioner Hale's neighbor's fence, and Hale knew, and Hale shook my hand*.

She had her keys in her pocket. She still had keys. Nobody had ever asked for them back, which told her something about how much anyone had ever cared who walked through that house and what they carried out of it.

But she didn't go in. Not tonight. Tonight she pressed her palm flat against the cold chain-link and let the wire print itself into her skin, diamond after diamond, and she said, out loud, to the house, because there was no one else to say it to:

"They said the paperwork's in order."

The house said nothing. The house had been saying nothing for three years, and she had mailed it two hundred and fourteen postcards of nothing back, and between them they had built a whole correspondence of silence, the longest relationship of her adult life.

* * *

Ruth was closing the shop when Beatrix came in the next morning, though Beatrix wasn't scheduled. The bell over the door did its small bright thing. Inside, the shop smelled as it always did—paper, gum arabic, the vanilla ghost of old glue—and the radiators along the wall ticked as they warmed, that particular arrhythmic knocking that Beatrix had grown up hearing at Halcyon House too, because every old building in this town spoke the same radiator language, and she had not been able to hear it for three years without her chest doing something unsanctioned.

"You look like the form came back stamped," Ruth said. She was behind the counter, sorting envelopes by size, her hands doing the work while her eyes did the other thing, the watching thing. Beatrix had known for weeks now that those eyes had been paid, once, to watch her. She had decided to let it stand, the way you let an old scar stand. Ruth had returned the postcards. Ruth had wept over the counter like a woman returning stolen bread. Some debts you collect and some you just carry.

"Denied," Beatrix said. "Paperwork's in order."

Ruth nodded slowly, the way people nod at funerals. "That's what Odile used to say." She stopped, as if she'd said too much, which she had, which was the point of Ruth now—she said too much, she couldn't help it, the dam had cracked and everything came through it sideways.

"What do you mean, that's what she used to say?"

Ruth set down the envelopes. She wiped her hands on her apron, though they were clean. "When I worked for her. Before. Anytime anything was—wrong. The fence that fell on the Hendersons' dog. The well water that went bad. She'd sit at that desk in the study with her tea and she'd say, 'The paperwork's in order, Ruth,' like that was the same thing as *nothing happened*. Like paper was the world." Ruth's mouth twisted. "I thought she meant she was careful. It took me years to understand she meant she was *covered*."

Beatrix stood very still. The radiators knocked. Outside, a truck went by—she could hear the hiss of it, and beneath the hiss, faintly, the sound of a chainsaw starting somewhere across town, someone else's tree coming down, the season of taking things down.

"Twenty days," Beatrix said.

"Twenty days," Ruth agreed. Then, quietly, in the voice of a woman who had spent years reading other people's unsent sentences and had learned what they were for: "So what are you going to do with them?"

Beatrix looked at the wall of the shop, at the corkboard where the local notices lived—lost cat, piano lessons, firewood for sale—and at the blank space below them, and she thought about what Marisol had said. *Bring them something they can't file.* She thought about the county's machine, all its forms and stamps and orderly paper, and she thought about what paper couldn't do. Paper could be denied. Paper could be lost. Paper could be burned, and had been, twice now, once in a fireplace in Halcyon House and once in a sink in her apartment, her own hand holding the match.

But a voice. A voice on the air, in every kitchen and truck cab and waiting room in the county, in the ears of the woman counting paperclips and the man golfing and the kids who cut through the property and the grandmother's grandchild who'd written *you little shit* in marker—a voice couldn't be denied. It could be pulled from the schedule. It had been. But it couldn't be unstamped once it was stamped on ten thousand ears.

"I'm going to need a long-range antenna," Beatrix said, "and someone crazy enough to broadcast without a license from the top of a condemned radio tower."

Ruth looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached under the counter and came up with the shop's cash box, the old tin one, and set it on the counter between them with a click that sounded, in the quiet shop, exactly like a key turning.

"How long," Ruth said, "do you need me to close early?"

* * *

That night, at her kitchen table, Beatrix wrote postcard number two hundred and fifteen. She had been writing them to a dead address for three years; this one, for the first time, had a living destination—the station, Marisol's show, care of the tower on Delano Hill. She wrote the date. She wrote the number of days remaining: *19*. And then she sat with the pen hovering, the way she had sat with it hovering two hundred and fourteen times before, over the sentence that lived in her chest in cipher, in Elias's invented alphabet, the sentence she could read in the dark with her eyes closed but had never once said aloud in the language other people spoke.

*I know what you did, and I stayed anyway.*

Not yet. Not on paper, not in plain ink, not where it could be stamped or filed or denied. In nineteen days the ball would swing, and the house would come down, and everything the house kept would come down with it unless she did the one thing the county's paperwork had no form for.

She finished the postcard with just the date and the number and a single line: *Meet me at the tower. Bring everything.*

She addressed it. She stamped it. And for the first time in three years, she did not mail it into the dark.

She drove it there herself.
