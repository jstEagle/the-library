## 18. Dana's Stage

The bar was called the Rusty Anchor, which was a lie on two counts: nothing about it was rusty in any picturesque way, and the only anchor within forty miles was bolted to the fire escape of a bait shop that had closed during the first Bush administration. Dana Kowalczyk had a Tuesday residency there, which meant she played to whoever wandered in off Route 9 looking for a bathroom and stayed for a beer. On this particular Tuesday, that audience consisted of a man asleep over his crossword, a woman watching a fishing show on mute, and me.

I'd called ahead. That felt like progress. A month ago I would have driven four hours and lurked outside her soundcheck like a process server.

"I want you to teach me the song," I said, when she came off the little stage with her guitar still strapped on, because apparently we were doing this without preamble. "The verses Odette never finished."

Dana looked at me the way people look at weather they've decided not to take personally. She was smaller than she seemed under stage lights, mid-forties, hair going gray at the temples in a way she'd clearly stopped fighting sometime around a divorce or a diagnosis or both. She set her guitar in its stand and considered me.

"You're the brother," she said. "Camille's brother."

"Theo."

"She said you measured things." She said it without accusation, just placing me, the way you'd note a load-bearing wall. "Bridges, right?"

"Bridges."

"And now you want to learn a lullaby your mother wrote and never finished, from a stranger who learned it off a cassette in a kitchen in 2003." She pulled out a chair across from me, turned it backward, sat. "Sure. Okay. But I should tell you what I know, which is less than Camille thinks and more than your father wants anyone to know, and none of it comes free."

"What does it cost?"

"Beer. And you have to sing back. Badly is fine. Silence isn't."

I ordered two beers from a bartender who moved like the crossword man owed him money, and when I came back Dana had the guitar in her lap again, tuning by ear, not looking at me.

"Camille gave me the tape the summer her mother went into the hospital the first time," she said. "Heart thing. She showed up at an open mic in Duluth with a shoebox of cassettes and this look like she was handing over evidence. She said, 'Nobody in my family will listen to these, so somebody has to.' I listened to all eleven tapes in one night in my car in a Kmart parking lot. Your mother's voice—" Dana paused, found the word, rejected it, tried again. "Your mother's voice was *unhurried*. You don't hear that anymore. Everybody sings like they're being timed."

I knew the unhurried quality. It was the voice Odette used when Raymond left the room, the register that lived below his hearing, low and even, singing while she kneaded bread or mended the tarp on the roof with clothesline and a bent fork. I had spent thirty years filing that voice under *background*.

"The song wasn't finished," I said. "On the recording. There's a verse that just stops."

"It doesn't stop. It *waits*." Dana played the opening figure, four notes, deceptively simple, the kind of melody that sounds like it was always there. "Odette wrote a bridge she never resolved. Camille told me your mother used to say she couldn't finish it until she knew how it ended. Not the song. The—" she waved a hand, "—the situation. Whatever the song was about. Which, if you ask me, was your father, though nobody asked me."

The beer arrived. Dana took hers without breaking eye contact with the fretboard.

"So here's what I can give you. The three verses as written. The chord under the missing bridge — she hummed it once on tape four, right before the recorder clicked, and I've been playing around it for twenty years like a man circling a hole in the ice. And the discipline." She finally looked up. "You will want to fix things as you learn them. Add a harmony, smooth a phrase. Don't. Your job is to hold the shape she left and let her fill it. Can you do that?"

"I inspect bridges for a living," I said. "My entire professional life is finding the crack and refusing to pretend it isn't there."

Dana smiled for the first time. "Then you might be the only Delacroix who can learn this."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon, the bar emptying its small population around us until it was just the bartender restocking and the fishing show flickering blue against the far wall. Dana taught the way good inspectors train apprentices: slowly, then all at once, with no tolerance for flattery.

The first verse I knew from the radio version, though hearing it stripped down was like seeing a familiar building with the siding removed. The second verse I half-knew, and where I didn't, my hands remembered anyway — muscle memory from decades of listening to Odette hum it into the dishwater, into the phone cord, into the blue hour on the porch when she thought both children were asleep. My body had been keeping her music in escrow all these years, waiting for someone to file a claim.

The third verse undid me a little. It was addressed, unmistakably, to a child. *Close your eyes, the water's talking, / let it say what it will say.* I had been that child. I had been sung to. I had absorbed it as ambience, the way you absorb the smell of your house, and it had taken a radio in a rented car and a shoulder pulled onto gravel to make me understand that ambience was love, that love had been playing the whole time at a volume my father had trained me not to hear.

"You're doing the thing," Dana said, mid-afternoon, when I stopped to blow my nose with a bar napkin.

"What thing."

"The measuring thing. You keep stopping to check whether you're feeling it correctly. Like there's a gauge." She strummed a lazy chord. "There's no gauge. Sing the wrong note and the world continues. Watch."

She sang the wrong note. Deliberately, grotesquely wrong, a clunker that made the sleeping crossword man stir. The world continued. The fishing show continued. Somewhere a cooler compressor kicked on.

"Noted," I said.

"Your sister used to do it too. Freeze up every time she got close to the bridge. Took her two years to get through it without apologizing." Dana tuned a string that didn't need tuning. "You two are very alike, you know. Both archivists. Both convinced that if you handle the past carefully enough it won't bite."

"Does it bite?"

"Only when you grip it too tight. Or not at all."

We ran the bridge section — the hole in the ice — maybe fifteen times. Dana played the unresolved chords and I sang up to the edge of them and stopped, and every time it felt like stepping off a beam with no rebar below. The fourth time I stopped early. The ninth time I overshot and landed somewhere ugly. By the twelfth I was laughing at myself, a rusty sound I barely recognized, and Dana said, "There it is," as if she'd been waiting for that specific failure all day.

At some point the light through the front windows went amber, then copper, then that particular bruised gold that Verdon Falls locals call the blue hour even though it contains no blue, only the memory of it. I noticed I hadn't checked my phone in five hours. This was either growth or negligence, and I'd stopped keeping records.

That was when the door opened and Camille walked in.

She stood in the doorway with her keys still in her hand, taking in the scene: me with a borrowed guitar — Dana's spare, a beat-up parlor model with a repaired headstock — and sheet paper covered in my cramped notation, and two empty glasses, and the unmistakable posture of a person mid-lesson.

"You started without me," she said.

It wasn't an accusation, exactly. It was worse. It was the voice of someone who had expected to be excluded and had come anyway, carrying her grievance like a packed lunch.

"We've been here since noon," Dana said mildly. "You could've been here since noon."

"I had to move Mom's appointment. Dr. Okafor wanted to talk about medication timing, which takes forever, because he explains things." Camille dropped into the chair beside mine, close enough that our elbows nearly touched, and stared at the lyric sheets. Her handwriting was on one of them — the transcription she'd made for Dana years ago, the one Theo had found in the emptied drawer, dated the year of the choir. Seeing it here, in daylight, next to my own notes, did something strange to the geography of blame. "You wrote it out in standard notation."

"Somebody had to," I said. "Your chord symbols were a war crime."

"They were *functional*."

"They were hieroglyphics. What is this, a seven-something?"

"A seven-*flat*-something. It's a color, Theo. You wouldn't understand, you think in load ratings."

And then, absurdly, we were arguing about notation, really arguing, voices up, the bartender pretending to inventory pretzel bags, and it was the most sibling conversation we'd had in twenty years. Underneath it ran the other argument, the big one, the waterline confrontation from last week with its accusations and revelations — *you sold her songs, you kept her drawer, you left, you stayed* — but tonight the big argument sat quietly in the corner like a dog that had already been walked, and what was left was just two middle-aged people fighting about flat-sevenths, which is its own kind of peace.

"Play it," Camille said finally, when we'd run out of notation to fight about. "Both of you. I want to hear what you've got."

Dana looked at me. I looked at my hands.

"He freezes at the bridge," she reported.

"I do not freeze. I pause strategically."

"You freeze."

"Play the damn song," Camille said.

So we played it. Dana led, I followed, and it went fine until it didn't — until the bridge, where I stepped off the beam and Camille, without being asked, without warning, came in underneath me with the low harmony our mother used to hum, the part that lived below Raymond's hearing, and my voice cracked clean in half and I kept going anyway, cracked, because the world continued, because there was no gauge.

We were bad. I want to be clear about that. We were genuinely, structurally bad — Camille sharp where I was flat, me rushing where she lagged, neither of us able to find the key the other was living in. Dana winced twice and corrected us once and then visibly decided to let history happen. The crossword man woke up, looked at us with an expression of profound review, and left.

And we laughed. In the middle of the second chorus, at the exact moment our two voices collided on a chord that existed nowhere in Western music, Camille snorted — actually snorted, a sound I hadn't heard since we were kids hiding from Raymond in the root cellar — and I lost the lyric entirely, and Dana played on alone beneath us like a ferryman who'd seen worse passengers, and we laughed until Camille wiped her eyes and said, "God, we sound like the church choir after the roof leak," and I said, "Worse. The roof leak choir could read," and she hit me with the lyric sheets, and it hurt, and it was fine.

When it settled, when the laughter drained out and left that raw quiet behind it, Camille turned her chair to face me fully.

"Mom used to harmonize with herself," she said. "Did you know that? When Dad was at the plant. She'd sing the melody and then sing under it, this low part, like she was keeping herself company." She looked at her hands, which were our mother's hands. "I always thought that was the saddest thing I ever heard. Now I think maybe it was the bravest."

"Dana says the bridge is unfinished because Mom couldn't resolve it until she knew how it ended."

"How what ended?"

"The situation." I hesitated. "You. Me. Him. All of it."

Camille was quiet a long moment. Outside, the copper light was failing toward actual dark, and the fishing show had gone to static, and the bartender flipped a chair upside down onto a table with the tenderness of long habit.

"Then we finish it," she said. "Not the way he'd want. Not tidy. We finish it the way she'd want — with everybody in the room, singing badly."

Dana struck the opening figure again, softer this time. "From the top," she said. "And this time, Theo, when you get to the bridge — don't step off. Build it. Right there, out loud, with your sister. That's what the song's been waiting for."

I put my fingers on the strings. Camille leaned in, close enough that I could feel her breath, off-key and unbothered. And I thought about the memory wing, and the common room, and our mother in her wheelchair by the window where the light comes in at exactly this hour, and I heard myself say:

"Saturday. We do it for her Saturday. Before the forgetting gets any further."

Camille nodded once. Dana smiled and counted us in.

But it was Camille, on the pickup, driving us both back toward Verdon Falls in the dark, who said the thing that rearranged the week: "Theo. There's something in the shoebox I never told you about. Something that isn't music." Her hands stayed steady on the wheel. "You need to see it before Saturday. Because if we're going to sing in front of him — you need to know what else he kept."
