## 7. Estate Sale Ghosts

The library in Harrow Point occupied what had once been a sea captain's house, and it still smelled faintly of salt and old varnish, as if the building itself had never quite come ashore. Ada had spent whole childhood afternoons there—Einar reading naval history he was too young for, Ada hiding in the biography stacks eating contraband gumdrops—and walking through the door now felt like putting on a coat that had been tailored for someone slightly smaller.

She went to the computer terminal the way she went to most things these days: with the grim efficiency of a woman defusing a bomb she'd personally wired.

*Estate sale. Harrow Point.* She typed it into the search bar and waited while the machine thought about it. The results were thin. Harrow Point had a population of eleven hundred people and a newspaper that came out Thursdays whether or not anything had happened since the last Thursday. But there it was, third result down, dated fourteen months ago:

**ESTATE SALE — ODETTE CRANE — 14 LARKSPUR LANE**

Ada read the listing twice. Odette Crane. She knew the name the way you know a word from a language you used to speak. The Cranes had lived two houses down from the Iversens, set back behind a hedge of rugosa roses, and Odette had been one of those women who appeared at moments of civic crisis—a flooded culvert, a lost dog, a funeral—with exactly the right implement: a shovel, a flashlight, a casserole.

It was Odette who had come to the house the week after the fire with a cardboard box and a no-nonsense expression, while their mother sat motionless at the kitchen table like a woman waiting for a tide that wasn't coming.

"You can't keep all this in here," Ada remembered her saying gently, nodding at Einar's things—the guitar leaning against the wall, the notebooks stacked by his bedroom door, rescued from the smoke because they'd been in a drawer, because drawers didn't burn. "Not with the girls in the house."

And their mother had said nothing, which meant yes, and Odette had carried the box out to her Buick, and that had been that. Ada had been nineteen and grieving in the aggressive, self-protective way of nineteen-year-olds, which is to say she had not asked where the box was going. She had not followed it. She had let her brother's handwriting leave the house in a stranger's station wagon because looking at it hurt, and she had told herself ever since that this was the same thing as not being able to help it.

The listing said the sale ran Friday and Saturday. Estate sales were usually within thirty days of a death, which meant—

Ada did the arithmetic and then made herself do it again, slower, because the first answer was wrong in a way she didn't want to be true.

Odette Crane had died last spring. More than a year ago.

Which meant the notebook had changed hands at an estate sale held by a dead woman's family, who would have had no idea what they were selling. Which meant Theo hadn't bought it from anyone who knew Einar. Which meant the thread Ada was holding led back to a house on Larkspur Lane that had been empty for over a year.

She printed the listing. The librarian, a woman named Mrs. Tillinghast who had been old when Ada was nine and had achieved a kind of fossilized magnificence since, watched her do it over the top of her glasses.

"Iversen," Mrs. Tillinghast said. It wasn't a question. Small towns kept ledgers of their own.

"Hello, Mrs. Tillinghast."

"You got your mother's chin." She said it the way other people said *you've grown*. "Heard you were back in town. Heard you were asking about songs."

Ada's stomach performed a small, cold maneuver. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody tells me anything," Mrs. Tillinghast said with dignity. "I just know things. It's the curse of working the front desk." She slid a slip of paper across the counter. On it, in fountain pen, was an address. "If you're going out to Larkspur Lane, you should know the Crane house isn't empty. Odette's nephew came up from Massachusetts to deal with the property. He's been there three weeks painting the shutters, which is what people do instead of deciding things."

"Thank you," Ada said.

"Don't thank me yet." Mrs. Tillinghast returned to her stamping. "I haven't told you the part about the other fellow."

Ada stood very still. "What other fellow?"

"A man came in around February. Wanted the back issues of the *Courier*, the ones covering the fire. I helped him find them myself." She looked up, and her eyes, behind the bifocals, were sharp as tacks. "He was very polite. Bought me a coffee from the machine, even though the machine coffee is free. He asked about the Crane estate sale, and about the Iversens, and then he thanked me and left, and I remember thinking he had the look of a man doing research for something he hadn't decided to write yet."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Gray at the temples, but young under it. Nice coat. City coat." She sniffed. "Out-of-towners always have nice coats and bad shoes."

"Did he leave a name?"

"He signed the guest register." Mrs. Tillinghast turned the register around and flipped back through the pages—February, March, January—until she found it and tapped it twice with one lacquered nail.

Ada looked down at the signature. The handwriting was small and careful, the letters upright, disciplined, the handwriting of someone who had learned penmanship somewhere it mattered.

*D. Halloran.*

"Do you know him?" Mrs. Tillinghast asked, watching her face with the frank appetite of the long-retired.

"No," Ada said, which was true, and also felt like the beginning of a longer answer she didn't have yet.

* * *

Larkspur Lane was a quarter mile of gravel off the coast road, lined with the same rugosa hedges that had been there twenty years ago, gone leggy and wild, blooming their pink salt-spray blooms like they'd been paid to. The Crane house sat at the end, white clapboard gone gray at the corners, and it was very much not shuttered. A ladder leaned against the east side. A radio played somewhere inside, low, something orchestral. And the shutters—two of them, freshly painted a deep harbor green—hung straight and new against the weathered siding, so that the house looked like a face half-awake, one eye made up, the other still sleeping.

Ada stood at the edge of the driveway for a long moment, doing the thing she did, the inventorying, the risk assessment, the quiet tallying of exits. Then she thought about Einar's notebook sitting in a stranger's apartment in some city, water-stained and sung, and she walked up the drive and knocked.

The man who answered was perhaps fifty, tall, gray at the temples, wearing paint-flecked khakis and a flannel shirt buttoned to the throat despite the warmth. He had a painter's brush in one hand and the mild, wary expression of a man who had already been asked by three neighbors whether he was going to sell.

"Morning," he said.

"Mr. Crane? I'm sorry—I'm looking for Odette Crane."

Something moved across his face, quick as a fish under ice. "You're a little late," he said. Not unkindly. Just flat, the way grief goes flat when it's been handled often enough.

"I heard. Last spring. I'm sorry for your loss." Ada held out the hand that wasn't gripping her tote bag strap. "My name is Ada Iversen. My family lived on Gullwing Road—two houses down. Odette took care of some of my brother's things, after our fire."

The wariness left him all at once, replaced by something more complicated. "The Iversens," he said. "The house fire. God. That was—" He counted backward. "Nine years?"

"Nine years this October."

"And you're the sister." He stepped back and opened the door wider. "Come in, please. I'm Daniel. Danny. Odette was my aunt. I'm the nephew with the shutters."

Inside, the house smelled of paint and, underneath it, of lavender and old paper—Odette's house still, ghosted with her. The furniture was shrouded in sheets except for one armchair by the window, which was bare, as though the family couldn't bring themselves to cover the place she'd actually sat. Danny noticed Ada noticing it.

"We argue about that chair every visit," he said. "My wife says cover it. I say she'll want to sit down when she gets back." He shrugged, embarrassed. "I know how that sounds."

"It doesn't sound any way at all," Ada said, and meant it, and something in her chest loosened a quarter turn.

Danny brought coffee without asking—Odette's percolator, he admitted, still in service, because "the coffee tastes like her opinions"—and they sat in the kitchen, which was bright and cluttered with drop cloths and paint cans and, on the table, a shoebox of photographs he was evidently sorting through. He pushed the box toward her halfway through the second cup.

"You can look, if you want. There might be pictures of your street. Odette photographed everything. Weddings, funerals, floods, the time the Grange hall roof came off. She was the town's unofficial archivist and she never once asked permission."

Ada turned the photographs over slowly. Here was the Grange hall mid-disaster, its roof peeled back like a sardine tin. Here was a christening, blurry, joyful. Here was Gullwing Road in autumn, and there—her breath caught—was the Iversen house, whole and golden-lit, with a bicycle abandoned in the yard and a figure on the porch steps too far away to identify but wearing, Ada was almost certain, the green sweater Einar had refused to take off between September and Thanksgiving of 1998.

"That's my brother," she said. Her voice came out strange.

"Einar," Danny said quietly. "Aunt Odette talked about him. After." He turned his cup a slow circle on the table. "She took his things because nobody else could touch them. Your mother couldn't be in the room with them. And the sisters—you were both—" He stopped himself, courteous. "Anyway. She kept them in the spare room for years. Said they weren't hers to throw away and weren't hers to open."

"But she sold them," Ada said. "At the estate sale. There was a notebook. Song lyrics."

Danny's face did the fish-under-ice thing again, and this time Ada recognized the movement for what it was: a man deciding how much truth a stranger could carry.

"She sold everything," he said finally. "I want to be honest with you, Ms. Iversen, because you have the look of someone who's been lied to recently and didn't enjoy it. By the time Aunt Odette died, she hadn't been fully herself for two years. The family hired a company to run the sale—they price things by the table, you understand, forty cents for spoons, two dollars for lamps. Nobody appraised a notebook. If it went out in a box lot, it went out in a box lot." He rubbed his jaw. "Though I'll tell you what I do remember, because it's been sitting wrong with me since spring."

Ada set down her cup. "Tell me."

"The sale was in June. Big turnout—Odette was loved, and small towns turn out for estate sales the way they turn out for fires. And near the end of the second day, a man came through. Tall, gray at the temples, city coat in June, which everyone noticed because it was eighty degrees." Danny smiled thinly. "Bad shoes, though. I remember thinking, there's a man who dresses for the version of himself he's planning to become."

Ada's skin prickled. "D. Halloran," she said. "Was that his name?"

Danny stared at her. "How do you—"

"He signed the library guest register in February. He was asking about the fire, and about your aunt's sale."

"February," Danny repeated slowly. "Before the sale?" He shook his head. "No—no, wait. He came twice. Or—" He frowned, digging. "There were two men. Or one man twice, I honestly couldn't swear. One before the sale, who asked Odette—this I know, because she told me on the phone, delighted, she thought it was romantic—he asked if he could see the spare room. The one with the musician's things. He said he was writing something. She liked writers; she let him in."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "And the second time?"

"After the sale. In July, maybe August. He knocked on the door and asked whether a notebook had been sold, a specific one, and Odette—by then she was confused more days than not—she told him everything was gone, sold, and he asked who'd bought it, and of course nobody knew, the company kept no records past the cashbox." Danny spread his hands. "He left his number. Asked us to call if it turned up. We never called. I'm sorry. By the time we found the card again, cleaning out her desk, it seemed—it seemed like a closed door."

"Do you still have the card?"

Danny was already up, moving to the sideboard, pulling open drawers with the increasing urgency of a man who has suddenly realized a detail matters. "She kept everything. Rubber bands, batteries, cards from plumbers dead ten years—" Papers whispered. A drawer stuck; he yanked it. "Here. Business cards. A thousand business cards."

He dealt them onto the kitchen table like a losing hand of solitaire: electricians, roofers, a piano tuner, a lawyer, a landscaper, a veterinarian. And there, third from the bottom, a plain white card with no logo, just a name and a number and, beneath them, in small type:

**D. HALLORAN — RESEARCH & RECORDS**

Ada picked it up. The card stock was good. The printing was letterpress, which was either pretension or craft, and she genuinely couldn't tell which.

"Research and records," Danny said, reading over her shoulder. "That's a real profession? Sounds like something out of a movie."

"It's a real profession," Ada said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Archivists use it. Investigators. Historians." She turned the card over. The back was blank, except—she tilted it toward the window light—for a faint impression, as if something had once been written on the card while it lay atop another piece of paper. She couldn't make out the words. But the pressure of the pen was there, a ghost of handwriting pressed into blankness, the way Einar's songs had been pressed into her, present and illegible at once.

"Ms. Iversen." Danny had gone quiet. When she looked up, he was watching her with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of a person standing in the wreckage of a story they'd believed was finished. "That man asked about the notebook eight months before you did. Maybe more. Whatever he's looking for—" He hesitated. "He was looking for it first."

Ada stood in Odette Crane's kitchen, in the smell of lavender and paint, holding a dead woman's card in her hand, and outside the window the harbor light was beginning to go long and gold, sliding toward blue.

Someone else had come back for Einar. Someone careful, patient, methodical—someone who dressed for the version of himself he was planning to become. Someone who had reached the spare room before the sale, and the cashbox after it, and who had left a phone number that no one had ever called.

Ada folded the card into her pocket next to the postcard she hadn't mailed, and asked Danny for one more thing: the name of the company that had run the sale.

"They're in Rockland," he said. "But Ms. Iversen—companies like that don't remember a notebook."

"No," Ada agreed, pushing back her chair. "But somebody in Rockland bought one."
