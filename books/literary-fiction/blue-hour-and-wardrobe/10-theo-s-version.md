## 10. Theo's Version

He was waiting outside the bar at nine in the morning, which told me something about him before he said a word. Nobody waits outside a bar at nine in the morning unless they've been awake since six thinking about it.

"You came," Theo said. He had a paper cup of coffee in each hand and offered me one like a peace treaty. "I wasn't sure you would. You had the look yesterday of a woman deciding between hitting me and leaving."

"I considered both."

"And?"

"Neither won. Yet." I took the coffee. It was terrible—diner coffee, burnt twice—but my hands wanted something to hold, and that was apparently the job it was applying for.

Theo nodded as if this were a reasonable answer, which I was beginning to suspect was his default setting. He was younger than I'd expected from the voice on the radio. Late twenties, maybe. A beard trimmed with more optimism than skill, a canvas jacket with sawdust in the seams. He looked like a man who fixed things for a living and sang about them on weekends.

"There's a place up the road," he said. "My friend's boathouse. It's got good light and bad heating and nobody around to hear us butcher anything. If you want."

I want, said something in me that I didn't authorize. What I said out loud was, "I want to see the notebook again first."

"It'll be there. I don't go anywhere without it anymore." He said it lightly, but there was weight under it, the way there's weight under ice on a pond in March. "It's kind of become a problem, honestly. My girlfriend says I sleep with it closer than I sleep with her."

"You have a girlfriend?"

"Her name's Priya. She's a nurse. She thinks the notebook is haunted and she might be right." He smiled, and for a second I saw what the radio heard—the thing in his face that made strangers feel like they'd been let in on something.

We drove up Route 1 in a pickup truck that smelled of pine shavings and old cigarettes, and Theo talked the whole way, not because he was nervous—I think he genuinely didn't know how to be quiet—but because he seemed to understand that silence would give me room to change my mind. He told me about the band, three guys and a fiddle player named June who was better than all of them combined. He told me he'd grown up in Waldoboro, forty minutes south, that his father had worked the granite yards. He told me he'd found the notebook at an estate sale in Harrow Point two years ago, in a box marked MISCELLANEOUS, five dollars, and that he'd known within ten seconds of opening it that he'd stolen something, even though he'd paid fair.

"That's how it felt," he said, eyes on the road. "Like theft. Like I'd bought somebody's letters by accident."

"Whose estate sale?"

"A woman named Crane. Odette Crane. Old house on the point road, past the church." He glanced over. "You knew her?"

The name landed somewhere under my ribs. Odette Crane had brought us rhubarb every June. Odette Crane had held my mother's elbow at the funeral. I hadn't thought about her in nine years, and now here she was, dead, apparently, and selling my brother's handwriting for five dollars a box.

"No," I said.

* * *

The boathouse sat on a gray cove, listing slightly to starboard like an old man leaning into a story. Inside: a woodstove, a couch with a blanket that had once been plaid, instruments hanging on nails like tools. A guitar, a mandolin, a banjo nobody loved. And on the workbench, centered, deliberate, a notebook.

I picked it up the way you pick up a bird that's flown into a window—carefully, half expecting it to be gone.

Water damage had bloomed across the cover in brown continents. The spine was cracked. But inside, Einar's handwriting marched on, slanted and impatient, lyrics crossed out and rewritten, chord diagrams in the margins drawn with the same care he'd used on his maps of imaginary islands when we were kids. I turned pages. My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that.

"He wrote small," Theo said quietly. "To fit more in."

"He wrote small because paper cost money and he never had any." I said it without thinking, and then heard how it sounded—not grief exactly, but its cousin, the one that lives next door and comes over uninvited.

Theo didn't fill the silence. That was new for him, I could tell. He let me read.

Most of the songs I knew. Not knew—*knew*. Knew the way you know a scar. There was the one about the ferry, the one about the winter the lobster boats stayed tied up, the one he used to hum while fixing the porch rail, over and over, driving our mother half mad. Seeing the words under the melody was like seeing the face of someone you've only ever heard through a wall.

And then the last song. *Blue Hour.* The one from the radio.

I read it twice. Three times. Something was wrong with it, some wrongness I couldn't locate, like a picture hung a quarter-inch crooked.

"Play it," I said. "The way you recorded it."

* * *

He played it sitting on a stool by the window, the cove going silver behind him. His voice was different without the radio between us—rougher, more careful, the way a person is careful in a stranger's house. June's fiddle part he whistled, badly, and apologized for.

And then we reached the third verse, and he sang words I had never heard:

*"Whatever I kept, I kept it too long,*
*whatever I broke, I broke it alone—"*

"Stop," I said.

He stopped. The last chord hung in the cold air of the boathouse and slowly died.

"That verse is wrong."

His jaw tightened. "It's not wrong. It's what I could read."

"It's not what he wrote." I was on my feet without having decided to stand. The notebook was still open on the workbench and I carried it to him like evidence at a trial. "Look. Third verse. The ink ran, fine, but look at the descenders. Look at the shapes underneath the smear. That's not 'kept.' The word has four letters and starts with an F."

Theo took the notebook. He looked at it for a long time. I watched him do what I imagined he did with everything—turn it over, hold it to the light, take it seriously.

"Show me," he said finally. "I've stared at this page for two years. I got as far as 'the tide takes back' and then I gave up and wrote my own."

"'The tide takes back what the tide gives twice,'" I said. "That line survived. It's after the smear."

"The tide gives twice," he repeated. He said it the way a man tests ice. Then he started picking the chords, slow, finding where my brother's line lived inside the melody he'd already built around his own. "Sing it. Please. You'll know if it fits. I've been guessing for two years and I'm tired of guessing."

"I haven't sung since—" I stopped. Since the funeral, when I stood in the back of the church and mouthed the hymns like a coward. Since before that, really, since Einar left for Portland and the duets stopped and singing became a thing that belonged to a house that burned down.

"Since a while," I finished.

"Yeah," Theo said. "Me neither, actually. Before the band. Six years of nothing, and then I found a dead man's notebook and suddenly I couldn't stop." He shrugged. "Grief's funny that way. It doesn't ask if you're ready. It just shows up with material."

I laughed. It startled both of us. It was the first laugh I'd had in nine years that didn't feel borrowed.

So I sang.

It came out rusty and low and it cracked on the second phrase, and none of that mattered, because the line fit. It fit the way a key fits a lock you'd stopped believing opened. *The tide takes back what the tide gives twice*—Einar's cadence, Einar's stubborn hopefulness, the boy who believed the sea was fundamentally generous even after it took our father's boat, even after it took, eventually, everything.

Theo played the turnaround and looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: a man watching the past walk into the room and sit down.

"What's the rest of it?" he asked softly. "Under the smear. Can you get it?"

I bent over the page. The water had done its work well—nine years of damp in some barn or basement before Odette Crane boxed it all up. But water runs downhill, and Einar had written on lines, and the pen had pressed hard enough in places to leave grooves. I tilted the page against the window light until the indentations threw tiny shadows.

"*F-faith... faithless,*" I murmured. "*Faithless harbor... no—faithful harbor, faithful harbor, I left you dry—*"

"Faithful harbor," Theo whispered. He wrote nothing down. He didn't need to; I could see it landing in him whole. "That's the whole song, isn't it? It's not a love song. Or—it is, but not the way I sang it. It's a song about leaving a place you love and hoping it forgives you."

"Yes," I said. And then, because it was true and because the boathouse was cold and honest: "He wrote it on our porch. The summer before the fire. I used to pretend I wasn't listening through my bedroom window. I know every song in that notebook, Theo. I just never saw the words."

"Why not?" It wasn't an accusation. It was genuine puzzlement, the puzzlement of a man who had driven two hours with a dead stranger's notebook because a woman glared at him in a bar. "If they were yours—if you knew them—why did it take a radio station to bring you here?"

Because I fled. Because I built a life with walls and called it order. Because answering the question meant admitting the question existed.

"Because knowing a thing and claiming it are different animals," I said. "I knew the songs. Claiming them meant coming back here. I chose not to come back."

"For nine years."

"For nine years."

He nodded slowly, and then did the thing that made me decide, later, that whatever else he was—thief, intruder, stranger—he was also a witness, and witnesses are rarer than thieves. He handed the notebook back to me.

"Then it should live with you," he said. "Or with whoever he'd want it to live with. I only ever borrowed it. Badly."

I held the notebook and looked out at the cove, where the tide was doing exactly what my brother had promised—giving, taking back, giving again—and felt something shift in my chest that I would spend the rest of this story learning the name of.

"We should record it properly," Theo said. "The real version. All of it. Before—" He stopped himself.

"Before what?"

He hesitated. He was bad at hesitating; his face broadcast everything like a lighthouse. "There's a verse marker in the margin," he said. "Next to the third verse. Just initials. I always figured it was who the song was for. I couldn't make out the letter. I thought maybe it was an M."

I looked down at the margin. The initials were small, tucked into the corner like something hidden. Water had gotten most of the first letter, but the second was clear enough—a sharp, upright stroke that could only be one letter.

Not M.

V.

"Who's V?" Theo asked.

And I stood in a stranger's boathouse holding my brother's heartbeat in my hands, and discovered that I did not know—and that the not-knowing had teeth.
