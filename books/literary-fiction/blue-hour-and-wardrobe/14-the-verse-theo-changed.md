## 14. The Verse Theo Changed

The notebook had been sitting on the dresser in my rented room for three days, and I had been treating it the way you treat a wasp on a windowsill—aware of it, respectful of it, unwilling to make any sudden moves.

It wasn't mine. That was the trouble. I'd held it in Theo's kitchen while he made coffee he clearly didn't drink, and I'd turned its water-swollen pages with the delicacy of someone handling a bird that might still be alive, and then I'd given it back, because it wasn't mine. But I couldn't stop thinking about page forty-one.

Theo had mentioned it almost casually, the way people mention the one thing they know will hurt you. "There's a verse I couldn't read," he'd said, wiping down a counter that didn't need wiping. "Third verse of 'Blue Hour.' The ink bled. I did what you do—I filled in the gap with something that fit the melody. It's probably wrong."

"Probably," I'd said, and left it there, because at that point I still believed the song was a love letter to some girl I'd never met, and I wasn't ready to meet her.

But I'd spent two nights now lying under the hum of the hardware store's sign outside my window, doing what I do for a living, which is reading closely. Proofreading is not glamorous work, but it teaches you something most people never learn: that meaning hides in small places. A comma can reverse a sentence's whole intention. A single changed word can turn a confession into an accusation. And a verse that doesn't scan right—a line with too many syllables crammed into it, a rhyme that lands slightly off—is usually not bad writing. It's writing that's trying to say something the writer can barely stand to say.

Einar was many things, but he was never a sloppy writer. He was a boy who alphabetized his cassette tapes. If his third verse was a mess, it was because the mess was the point.

In the morning I walked back to the bar in Rockland, which took most of an hour by bus, and found Theo loading equipment into a van that had once been blue and was now mostly rust and optimism.

"You came back," he said, and I braced for smugness and found none. Just a kind of careful gladness, like he was holding a door open and wasn't sure I'd walk through it.

"I need to see page forty-one again."

He set down the amp he was wrestling. "Okay."

"It's not a request I'm making lightly." I heard how stiff I sounded, how much like a certified letter. I tried again. "I've been thinking about the third verse. About why it was illegible."

"People spill things on notebooks."

"Einar didn't spill things. Einar blotted things. He used to write letters and then go over them with a ruler to make sure his margins were straight." I heard myself say *used to* and felt the old vertigo, the past tense like a trapdoor. "If that verse is unreadable, somebody made it unreadable. Maybe him. Maybe the water. But I think—" I stopped, because the thought was still forming even as I said it, taking shape out of my mouth like breath in cold air. "I think it wasn't a love song. Not the way we've been hearing it."

* * *

We sat in the van with the doors open, our legs hanging out over the asphalt, the notebook between us like a patient on a table. Theo had a flashlight, which seemed absurd in the daylight until I saw why he'd brought it: held low and angled across the page, it threw every indentation into relief. Ghost-writing. The pen had pressed hard enough to leave tracks in the paper even where the ink had drowned.

"There," I said. "See the strokes? Under where the ink ran?"

Theo leaned in. He smelled, faintly, of coffee and guitar strings and the particular staleness of vans. "That's... words. There are words under the smear."

"He wrote it twice." My throat had gone tight. "Or he pressed hard the first time and traced it again, and the second pass is what bled. Look at the length of the lines. They're longer than the others in the song. Longer than the melody has room for."

"So he was cramming."

"He was cramming." I laughed, or something adjacent to laughter, and it came out cracked. "You want to know how I know? Because I proofread parking ordinances for a living, and angry people write long. Guilty people write long. People who are sorry write *long*, Theo, because 'I'm sorry' is four letters and what they actually mean takes sixty words, and they try to fit all sixty into the space where four should go."

Theo was quiet for a moment. Then: "Read me what you can make out."

I bent close. The ghost-strokes were maddening, half-alphabet, half-suggestion, but I had spent nine years reading Einar's handwriting on postcards—the slant of his lowercase e's, the way his t's crossed left to right like he was striking matches—and your brother's hand is a language you never fully forget, the way you never fully forget a house's creaks.

"*V,*" I read slowly. "*I watched it start and I said nothing. I stood in the yard and I let it—*" The rest dissolved into pulp and shadow. "*—was mine, the lamp was mine, the night was mine, and you paid—*"

I stopped.

The van, the lot, the gulls arguing over the dumpster behind the bar—all of it went very far away, the way sound does underwater.

"The lamp," Theo said carefully. "Ada. What lamp?"

I couldn't answer him yet. Something was rearranging itself inside me, furniture being dragged across floors I hadn't entered in nine years. There had been a kerosene lamp—of course there had been, Einar kept one, he liked it because it made the porch feel older than it was, he'd bought it at a tag sale for a dollar and been insufferably proud of his bargain. And Vera—

Vera, who had lived with us one winter when I was fourteen, who slept in the room off the kitchen, whose laugh I could still produce on demand in my memory like a recording. Vera, who was gone the morning after the fire without a goodbye, sent *home*, as if a foster placement were a country you could be deported from. Vera, whose name began with V, and who had been so thoroughly erased from our family's story that I had needed a dead man's marginalia to remember she existed.

The song wasn't a love letter. Or it was, but not the kind Theo had sung—not the kind you sing to someone you want. It was the kind you sing to someone you owe.

"It's an apology," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, flattened, like it had been ironed. "The whole song. 'Blue Hour' isn't a love song, Theo. It's a confession. He wrote it *to her*. And then either he couldn't bear to finish it or he finished it and couldn't bear anyone to read it, and he pressed so hard the pen went through his own words."

Theo sat with that. To his credit, he didn't fill the silence with music, which is what most musicians would have done, and what I would have forgiven but never forgotten.

"The verse I wrote instead," he said finally, "is about her hair. Golden, I think. Something about a window."

"It's lovely," I said. "It's completely wrong."

"Yeah." He exhaled. "Yeah, I figured, hearing you say it."

* * *

Here is what I want to be honest about: my first instinct, underneath everything, was to take the notebook and run.

Not steal it, exactly. Nothing so dramatic. Just—borrow it indefinitely. Take it back to my room above the hardware store and keep it the way I'd kept everything else for nine years, sealed and private and safe, a relic on a shelf. That was my native language. Preservation. I had built a whole liturgy around it: the postcards, the unshared grief, the loneliness arranged so neatly it looked like order. For nine years I had been the sole custodian of Einar Iversen, and custody, I was only now beginning to understand, is just another word for keeping something from breathing.

But an apology that no one hears isn't an apology. It's just weight.

And if the song was testimony—if Einar, in whatever tangle of guilt and love he carried, had written down that the fire started with his lamp, that he had watched it begin and said nothing, that Vera had *paid* for it—then it wasn't a relic at all. It was evidence. Evidence of what, I didn't yet know. Of an accident. Of a cowardice. Of a family that had solved its pain by amputating a sixteen-year-old girl and never speaking her name again. Maybe all three.

But evidence belongs to more than one person. That's practically the definition.

"Theo," I said. "I want to restore the verse. The real one. What he actually wrote."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside the van, a man wheeled a hand truck of empty kegs across the lot, whistling something with too many notes in it.

"You understand what you're asking," Theo said. "It's not restoration like a painting. Half those lines don't exist anymore. We'd be reconstructing. You'd be guessing, and I'd be setting your guesses to melody, and whatever we ended up with wouldn't be his song. It'd be his song's ghost."

"I know ghosts," I said. "I've been living with one for nine years. I'm qualified."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and surprised, and I realized it was possibly the first joke I'd made since arriving in Maine, and possibly the first in years before that. Ruth back home would have celebrated. Ruth would have thrown a party.

"What do you need?" Theo asked.

"Time with the page. A lamp—we're apparently a lamp-oriented operation, which feels thematically correct. And..." I hesitated over the last part, because it was the part that cost me. "And Maren."

"Maren?"

"My sister. Half-sister. She grew up in that house too. She knew Vera better than I did—she was younger, but she was *there*, and I wasn't, not really, I was fourteen and in my own weather system." I picked at a loose thread on the van's seat. "If we're going to rebuild a verse Einar wrote to Vera Lindqvist, I need someone who remembers her voice. Her actual voice. I have her laugh and a winter and nothing else."

"And you haven't talked to her yet."

"We've exchanged civilities. Civilities with teeth in them." I looked out at the gulls. "She's selling the land, Theo. Five weeks, maybe less now. She thinks I came back to judge her for it, and honestly, maybe I did, a little, when I got off the bus. I'm not sure anymore what I came back for. But I know what I'm staying for."

"Which is?"

I turned page forty-one gently, the way you'd turn a blanket over someone sleeping.

"A dead man tried to apologize for nine years and never managed to send it," I said. "Somebody should deliver it."

* * *

Theo drove me back toward Harrow Point that afternoon, the van rattling along Route 1 with the windows down, salt air coming through in gusts. He talked about the band—two guys, both patient with him, both bewildered by this whole Harrow Point business—and about how he'd found the notebook at Odette Crane's estate sale, boxed with a fondue set and a child's rain boots, priced at two dollars.

"Two dollars," I said. "My brother's soul went for two dollars."

"I'd have paid two hundred."

"I know. That's the thing that saves you." I meant it, and I think he knew I meant it, and we rode the rest of the way in a silence that had gotten comfortable, which surprised us both.

At the edge of town he pulled over near the hardware store, and before I got out he said, "One condition."

I braced. Everyone in this town had conditions; conditions were the local currency.

"When we get the verse back—when we know what he wrote—you don't decide alone what happens to it. You, me, Maren. Whoever else needs to be in the room." He tapped the notebook's cover where it sat between us. "This stopped being private the day I sang it on the radio. I've felt sick about that ever since, if you want the truth. So whatever we do next, we do it out loud. No more sealed rooms."

"No more sealed rooms," I agreed, and heard, in my own voice, the sound of a woman agreeing to demolish the architecture of her entire adult life.

I was halfway up the stairs to my room when I saw Maren standing at the bottom of them, holding a cardboard box with both arms, her face doing something complicated.

"You need to come to the cottage," she said. "Tonight. I found something in Mother's attic, and I've been sitting with it for six hours trying to decide whether to burn it, and I can't decide, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I thought maybe you'd want to decide with me."

Behind her, through the hardware store window, the evening light was starting its slow turn toward violet.

The blue hour was coming. And for the first time in nine years, I wasn't going to spend it alone.
