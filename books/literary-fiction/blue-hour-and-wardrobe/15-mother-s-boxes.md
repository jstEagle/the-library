## 15. Mother's Boxes

The attic of the cottage smelled the way it always had: cedar shavings, old wool, and the faint sweet rot of a house that had been holding its breath for nine years. Maren had opened the hatch at seven in the morning with a thermos of coffee and a legal pad titled INVENTORY, and by nine she had learned that her mother had kept everything, and that everything was mostly junk.

She sat cross-legged on the plywood subfloor in a shaft of light from the gable window, surrounded by cardboard boxes labeled in her mother's tidy hand. Not *Christmas decorations* or *winter clothes*, the way normal people labeled boxes. Her mother had labeled them like a woman expecting an archivist to arrive someday and judge her.

*Tax records 1988–1994. Keep.*
*Mittens, unmatched. Decide.*
*Einar — schoolwork.*
*Einar — schoolwork (more).*

Maren held that last one on her lap for a while without opening it. The tape had yellowed and gone brittle at the edges, and when she finally worked a fingernail under it, it came away in a long curl like something shedding skin.

Inside: spelling tests with red stars. A book report on *Treasure Island* in which eleven-year-old Einar had written, in his enormous looping cursive, "The moral of this story is that maps are only useful if you are brave enough to leave." Their mother had starred that sentence too, twice, as if grading it.

"You sentimental old woman," Maren said out loud, to no one, and her voice cracked in the middle of *woman*, so she drank some coffee and moved on.

That was the trick of packing up a dead parent's house. You couldn't do it all at once. You did it in doses, the way you'd take a medicine that was also a poison. A box of mittens. A box of schoolwork. A box of Christmas ornaments wrapped individually in newspaper from 2003, each one swaddled like a tiny corpse, and Maren laughed at herself for thinking that, and then didn't laugh, because half of them were ornaments Einar had made in grade school — popsicle stars, a dough angel with one wing — and grief has a sense of timing that no one else appreciates.

By noon she had filled three trash bags and one box marked *Goodwill* and had cried exactly once, briefly, over a photograph of the four of them at the Harrow Point lobster festival, back when their mother still smiled with her whole face and Ada still stood close enough to touch.

Then she found the trunk.

It was under the eaves where the roof sloped low, behind a roll of insulation and a broken dehumidifier, and it was not a box at all but an actual steamer trunk, brass corners gone green, with a luggage tag that read *A. IVERSEN* in faded ink. Their grandmother's, probably. Maren hauled it into the middle of the floor, wincing at the scrape, and knelt in front of it feeling like an archaeologist who suspects the dig is about to get complicated.

The latch was stiff but honest. It opened.

On top lay a quilt she recognized — the blue-and-white one from the daybed in the downstairs room, the room that had been Vera's, that winter that nobody talked about. Under the quilt, letters.

Not just a few. Bundles of them, tied with kitchen twine, dozens upon dozens, envelopes soft with age. Maren lifted the first bundle and read the return address and felt the attic tilt slightly, the way a boat tilts when you stand up too fast.

*Vera Lindqvist, 44 Millbrook Road, Waterville.*

Postmarked March. The year after the fire.

Maren's hands went cold around the bundle, which was absurd, because the attic was hot enough to hatch eggs. She untied the twine — her mother had tied it in a bow, which somehow made it worse, a bow, like a gift being saved — and slid out the top letter.

*Dear Astrid,* it began. *I know you said not to write, so I'm sorry. I'll keep it short.*

It was not short. It was four pages, in a teenager's careful hand, and Maren read all of it standing up because sitting down felt too much like settling in, and she did not want to settle in. She wanted to put the letter down and label a box MITTENS, UNMATCHED and go back to the manageable grief of objects.

*I got a job at the diner on Main. The woman there is nice. I sleep okay most nights. I wanted you to know that, so you wouldn't worry. I wanted to ask if Einar is sleeping okay. Please tell me he is. Please tell him I think about what I'd say to him every single day and then I don't say any of it because what would be the point. Tell him the song isn't lost. I remember all of it.*

Maren set that one down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be loaded.

There were more. Years of more. Letters postmarked from Waterville, then from Bangor, then from Portland, the handwriting growing up across the envelopes — the round teenage loops tightening, slanting, becoming an adult's hand. One a month, sometimes two. Maren counted without meaning to: sixty-one letters. Sixty-one letters, and every envelope had been opened, which meant her mother had read them, which meant her mother had known —

Known what? That Vera wrote. That Vera asked about Einar. That Vera remembered the song.

And never answered. Never told them. Never once, at any dinner table or funeral reception or awkward parking-lot encounter in the years after, had Astrid Iversen said, *Vera writes to me. Vera asks how you're doing.* She had carried a living correspondence in a trunk under the eaves like contraband, and had let her daughters believe the girl had simply gone home, wherever home was, and closed the door behind her.

"Oh, Mom," Maren said, and sat down after all, right there on the subfloor among the mittens.

She read until the light through the gable window went from white to gold. She read about a waitress job and a night course and a man named Dale who turned out badly and a dog named Biscuit who turned out wonderfully. She read about a greenhouse job — *they let me have the orchids, can you believe it, me* — and about Christmases alone that were described cheerfully enough that Maren could see straight through the cheer to the loneliness underneath, because she'd been writing the same kind of sentences herself for years, in her own head, in the voice you use so people won't worry.

And she read, again and again, in different words, the same two questions. *How is Einar? Does he hate me?*

The last letter was dated eight months before the fire took the question off the table forever. After that, nothing. Either Vera stopped writing, or her mother stopped keeping. Maren stared at the empty space at the bottom of the trunk where a sixty-second letter would have fit and understood that she would never know which, and that this was its own small cruelty, a cruelty her mother had arranged and then died before having to explain.

Maren wiped her face with the heel of her hand and told herself, firmly, that this was enough revelation for one day. She had a closing in five weeks. She had a lawyer meeting Thursday. She had a spreadsheet of debts on her laptop that she looked at approximately as often as she looked at the sun — aware of it, unable to stare directly at it.

She should repack the trunk. Tie the bows back. Put it under the eaves. Let the sale erase it along with everything else; that was the whole point of selling, wasn't it, that other people would carry the boxes away and she would carry nothing.

Instead she lifted out the last layer.

Under the letters, flat and official-looking, was a manila folder with a coffee ring on the corner. *MAINE STATE FIRE MARSHAL'S OFFICE* was stamped across the front in blue. Maren knew what it was before she opened it. Some part of her had known since she was nineteen and heard her mother on the phone late one night saying, in a voice Maren had never heard before or since, *"Undetermined doesn't mean nothing happened."*

The report was thin. Six pages. Cause of fire: **UNDETERMINED**. Origin: northeast bedroom. And there — Maren felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a missing stair — there was a gap. Page four was gone. Not torn out crudely; removed cleanly, the way a page is removed when someone wants the document to look complete to anyone who isn't counting. The pagination jumped from 4 to 6. Someone had even corrected the footer numbers in pen, neat little asterisked notes in her mother's handwriting: *see attached,* and then no attachment.

Maren sat in the gold light with the report in her lap and did the arithmetic she had spent nine years refusing to do.

The fire started in the room Vera shared with Einar. Their mother had sent Vera away the next morning, before the ashes were cool, before the funeral, before anyone could ask her anything. Maren had been twenty-two and grieving and grateful, honestly grateful, to have someone to quietly blame, and their mother had made sure the blame had nowhere to land — no address, no phone number, no forwarding. *Vera went home,* Astrid had said, and everyone nodded, and the family story sealed itself shut like a jar.

But undetermined doesn't mean nothing happened. And a mother doesn't exile a seventeen-year-old girl at dawn unless she's afraid of what the girl might say. Or afraid of what she herself might have to admit. Or — and here was the thought Maren had been circling for nine years, the one shaped like a stone in her shoe — afraid of what her own son might have done, and determined that no one, least of all the girl who'd been in the room, should ever have to say it out loud.

Einar had slept with a kerosene lamp sometimes. He read at night. Everyone knew that. Nobody had ever said it mattered.

Maren looked at the gap where page four used to be and thought: *Mom, what did you take?*

And then, because she was her mother's daughter and had inherited both the secrecy and the stubbornness, she thought the second thing, the harder thing: *What am I going to do?*

Because here was the ledger she actually kept, the real one, the one not on her laptop. On one side: five weeks to closing, debts with names and interest rates, a buyer's lawyer already sending cheerful emails with attachments. On the other side: sixty-one unanswered letters and a missing page, and a sister — two sisters, really, she supposed, if she was going to start telling the truth — who deserved to see this before the trunk went to whoever bought the land and everything on it.

If she buried it, the family story stayed sealed. Clean. The version where the fire was weather and bad luck and the girl went home. She could pack the trunk away and sign the papers and drive south and never think about page four again, except that she would, every day, forever, the way you think about a letter you never mailed.

If she showed it — to Ada, who was in town now, staying at the Anchor Inn and asking questions around town like a detective in a paperback; to Theo with his water-stained notebook full of songs addressed to V.; possibly eventually to V. herself — then the story came apart in her hands, and whatever they rebuilt from the pieces would not be the story she'd grown up inside. But it would be true. And it would be shared. And God help her, she was so tired of being the only one carrying it.

Her phone buzzed on the floorboards beside her. A text from Ada, of all people — the first Ada had initiated in nine years, which Maren noted and refused to be moved by:

*Found out Odette Crane sold Einar's things. Going through the estate sale records tomorrow. Thought you'd want to know.*

Maren looked at the message for a long time. Then down at the trunk. Then at the folder with its neat asterisks and its hollowed-out middle.

*Come by the cottage tomorrow,* she typed, thumbs moving faster than her caution. *Before you go anywhere. There's something you need to see.*

She hit send before she could delete it, then set the phone face-down on the subfloor, untied one of her mother's careful bows, and began, with hands that almost didn't shake, to put the letters in order by date.

Outside, the light was going blue at the edges. Somewhere down the road, the survey stakes stood in the tall grass like little wooden accusations, and five weeks had become four and a half, and the truth sat in an attic trunk with a coffee ring on its cover, waiting to be either buried or spoken.

Maren poured the last of the coffee, cold now, and drank it anyway.

"Okay, Mom," she said to the empty attic. "Your move."

But it wasn't her mother's move anymore. It was hers.
