## 1. Poste Restante

The postcards came in packs of twenty from the stationery shop on Wexford Lane, the one with the bell over the door that had stopped ringing properly years ago and now produced only a dull, apologetic clank, like a spoon dropped in another room. Xanthe Thackeray bought them every six weeks or so, always the same kind: plain card stock, unlined, with a faded photograph of a seaside town they had never visited. The sea in the photograph was the colour of tarnished pewter. A pier extended into it and stopped, amputated by the frame's edge, going nowhere. Xanthe liked that about it. It seemed honest.

Every Sunday evening, after the light had gone the colour of weak tea and before the radiators began their nightly percussion, Xanthe sat at the kitchen table — a table that belonged to the flat, not to them, its legs shimmed with folded beer mats left by a previous tenant — and wrote one postcard. One per week. They had been doing this for four years, which meant two hundred and eight postcards, give or take the weeks when grief had been too heavy to lift and the cards went unwritten, though those weeks were rarer than Xanthe would have admitted to anyone, because the ritual was the one appointment they never broke. Not for illness, not for the flu that had flattened them the February before last, not even for the night their water pipes burst and they'd spent three hours mopping with bath towels while the ceiling above them bloomed brown like a bruise coming up. They had written that week's postcard sitting on an overturned bucket, sleeves rolled, ink smudged where the damp paper took the pen too greedily.

The address was always the same:

*Marion Thackeray*
*14 Alder Row*
*Ashcombe*

There was no 14 Alder Row anymore. There was no Alder Row at all, strictly speaking; there was a gap in a terrace and a chain-link fence hung with a council notice gone soft and grey in the weather, and behind the fence a rectangle of scorched earth where the house had stood until the fire took it eleven years ago, on a night in November that Xanthe could still reconstruct with terrible precision if they let themselves: the smell of smoke reaching them across town, the orange glow reflected low on the clouds like a second, dishonest sunset, the phone call, the drive, the crowd of neighbours in dressing gowns, and their mother standing barefoot on the pavement holding nothing, having saved nothing, saying over and over, *it's all right, it's all right,* in a voice that made it clear nothing was.

But the postcards were not addressed to the fire. They were addressed to the house as it had been, and to the woman who had lived in it, and the postal service — this was the small miracle Xanthe had never questioned, the way one doesn't question gravity or the tide — returned them all, months later, stamped *ADDRESSEE UNKNOWN / RETURN TO SENDER* in red ink that bled slightly into the card stock, so that each returned postcard arrived looking faintly wounded. Xanthe kept them in a shoebox under the bed. Two hundred and eight wounded postcards, or nearly. A correspondence conducted entirely with the dead letter office of the Royal Mail.

They wrote in English mostly, because grief is lazy and falls back on the mother tongue even when the mother tongue is part of what you've lost. But certain words only worked in the Language — the private dialect their grandmother Odile had invented, or perhaps assembled, stitching together French and Welsh and pure invention into something spoken by exactly three people in the world, then two, then one. *Seluneth.* *Vessamine.* *Orrel.* Words with no translation, words that carried whole rooms inside them. Xanthe used them sparingly on the postcards, the way you'd use a family silver spoon for medicine — carefully, because there were no replacements.

Tonight's postcard lay blank on the table. Outside, rain was doing its patient work on the windows, and the streetlight below turned each drop briefly amber as it fell, so that the window looked like a slow curtain of sparks. The flat around Xanthe held its usual silence, which was not really silence at all but a collection of small sounds that had agreed to be quiet together: the fridge humming its one note, a pipe ticking somewhere in the wall, the neighbour's television murmuring through the plaster like a voice heard through water. Xanthe had lived here five years and had never once put a picture on the wall. This was not an oversight. It was a policy. Pictures implied duration. Nails implied belief in the future. The flat was furnished entirely in the conditional tense — the sofa that would do for now, the bookshelves that had come with the let, the curtains chosen by someone whose name Xanthe didn't know and whose taste they inhabited like a borrowed coat. When friends — and Xanthe still had one or two, patient creatures — visited, they said things like *you should really make it yours*, and Xanthe would smile and say *yes, I should*, and both parties understood that this would never happen, the way you understand about gym memberships and learning the cello.

Because a rented life was easier to leave. That was the arithmetic of it, the cold little sum Xanthe performed nightly without knowing they were performing it: nothing owned, nothing anchored, and therefore nothing that could burn down while you slept. Eleven years ago Xanthe had learned what a house does when it dies. It takes everything with it — the photographs, the letters, the christening gown, the grandmother's notebooks full of a language only she could write, all of it gone to ash and water-rot in a single night, so that afterwards there was no past to touch, only descriptions of a past, hearsay, memory degrading with every retelling like a photocopy of a photocopy. Their mother had stood on the pavement with empty hands. And Xanthe had stood beside her and felt, beneath the horror, beneath the love, something shameful and small and true: relief. Because Marion Thackeray had been a difficult woman to love — loving her was like embracing a door that might open or might swing shut on your fingers — and the fire had, in one stroke, ended the argument between what Marion was and what Xanthe needed her to be. No more waiting for her to soften. No more dreading the phone. The fire had done the grieving's rough preliminary work, and what was left for Xanthe was only the guilt of being relieved, which is a grief with no bottom to it, no floor you can stand on and weep properly.

So: the postcards. Four years of them. A correspondence with a ruin.

Xanthe picked up the pen. The nib caught on the card's toothy surface, hesitated, then flowed.

*Mama,* they wrote. Always *Mama*, never *Mum*, because Odile had insisted on the French and the habit had outlived her, outlived everything, a word surviving like a seed in frozen ground.

*This week it has rained for six days. I bought a coat I don't need. There is a woman at work who says "bless you" when you sneeze and means it, actually means it, and I have started sneezing near her on purpose, which tells you everything about the state of me.*

The pen paused. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere down the street a car passed, dragging its hiss behind it like a scarf.

*I dreamt about the garden at Alder Row. In the dream the roses were blooming out of season, all at once, the way they did the summer you were well, and I knew even inside the dream that it was a lie the dream was telling me, but I stayed anyway. Vessamine, Mama. I don't have another word for it.*

*Vessamine* — the word Odile had coined for a happiness you didn't trust, a joy that felt like the pause before bad news. Xanthe had looked it up once, years ago, in the hope that some dictionary somewhere had smuggled it in. Nothing. It existed in exactly one place: the mouths of the dead, and the shoebox under the bed.

*I am writing to tell you that Aunt Delphine died on Tuesday.*

There it was. The sentence Xanthe had been circling all week like a bird over water. They had known it was coming — Delphine was eighty-nine and had spent the last year describing her own decline with the brisk cheer of a woman reading a train timetable — but knowing had not prepared them for the specific silence of it, the way a whole generation can fit inside one death and vanish. Delphine had been the last one who remembered Odile's voice, the last who could pronounce *seluneth* correctly, with the soft landing on the final syllable. The last Thackeray who remembered anything, now, except Xanthe. And Xanthe's memory was a rented room too — borrowed, provisional, full of other people's furniture.

*The funeral is Friday. I don't know what to wear. I don't know what to say. I keep thinking about how Delphine used to call us "the leftovers" — you and me and her, after everyone else scattered — and how she said it fondly, and how I laughed, and how now I am the leftover of the leftovers, the remainder when the division is done.*

Xanthe sat back. The card was nearly full; the pewter sea and the truncated pier stared up from the picture side, patient, indifferent. Down in the street a fox screamed once and fell silent, and the fridge hummed on, and the radiator knocked twice like a polite visitor and gave up.

Here was the thing Xanthe had never written on any postcard, the thing that lived below all the weather reports and small confessions like groundwater under a field: *I never said goodbye to you. You burned, and I stood on the pavement, and I was relieved, and then you were gone, and I have spent four years writing to an empty lot because I cannot finish a sentence I never started.* There was a letter — their mother's last letter, posted the week before the fire, found later among Delphine's papers — and Xanthe had never opened it. It sat in a drawer in the bedroom, sealed, its flap yellowed, addressed in Marion's slanted hand. Four years Xanthe had known it was there. Four years of taking it out sometimes and holding it, feeling through the paper the thickness of whatever was inside, the way you'd feel a bone through skin. Unopened, the letter could still contain anything. Opened, it could only contain what it contained.

The pen hovered. Then Xanthe bent and finished the card.

*I will go to the funeral on Friday and stand up straight and be composed, because that is what Thackerays do, we compose ourselves like flower arrangements, symmetrical and scentless. If anyone asks after you I will say you are at peace, which is a lie, because I don't know where you are. Nobody knows where anybody is. That is the whole trouble.*

*Seluneth someday, Mama. Orrel for now.*

*X.*

They signed it, stamped it, propped it against the fruit bowl to wait for morning. Then Xanthe washed their single cup, dried it, set it back on the shelf that was not theirs, and stood a moment in the middle of the rented room with the rain coming down outside, listening to the flat hold its breath around them.

Two hundred and nine postcards, tomorrow, into the dead letter dark.

And on Friday — though Xanthe did not know it yet, could not have imagined it, standing alone in the amber lamplight with their mother's name drying on the card — a stranger in a grey coat would turn from the graveside and speak a word that had been silent for eleven years, and the ground under Xanthe's rented life would shift for the first time, quietly, like something turning over in its sleep.
