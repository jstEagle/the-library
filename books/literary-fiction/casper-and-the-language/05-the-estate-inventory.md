## 5. The Estate Inventory

The house smelled the way grief smells when it has been left to steep: camphor and old roses and the faint sweetness of dust burning on radiators that hadn't run since spring. Delphine's house — though it had been the grandmother's house first, and before that something older still, its bones set in the years when this street had a different name. Xanthe stood in the front hall with a legal pad and a pen they didn't yet believe in, because how did one inventory a life? *One armchair, wine-dark, sprung at the left seam. One teapot, chipped, still faintly of bergamot.* The pad felt like a joke someone else was telling.

Casper arrived at nine, in the same grey coat, carrying a thermos and a roll of bin bags he set down by the door as if apologizing for them. He stood a moment in the hall, head tilted, reading the house the way Xanthe had once watched their mother read a page of sheet music — not with the eyes but with the whole body.

"You knew her," Xanthe said. Not Delphine. They both knew who.

"I knew every room of her," Casper said. "Where the floor dipped. Which windows stuck." He looked at Xanthe, then away. "I don't know where anything is kept. She kept things from me too."

They began upstairs, because beginning downstairs felt like beginning at the ending.

* * *

The attic was where the house kept its throat.

Casper had been in it twice in twenty years — once to fetch a suitcase, once to catch a pigeon — and both times the grandmother had stood at the bottom of the ladder and watched him come down, as though the space above the ceiling were hers alone, a country she permitted no ambassadors. Now he climbed into it with Xanthe behind him and the bulb swinging on its cord, and the dust rose up around them like something exhaling.

Trunks. Boxes. A dress form wearing a housecoat gone grey with age. And along the far wall, under a sheet, a low shelf of notebooks — forty of them, fifty, their spines soft with handling, their covers the marbled cardboard of a stationer's that had closed before Casper was born.

He knew them on sight. He had never seen them before in his life.

"She wrote in them," he said. His voice came out smaller than he intended. "Every evening. I used to hear the pen through the floor of my room. I thought it was a mouse."

Xanthe pulled the first notebook free and opened it, and the light in the attic seemed to change — or perhaps it was only the two of them changing, the way a room changes when a window is unshuttered. The pages were ruled in the grandmother's upright hand, but the words were the Language, all of it: the vocabulary Xanthe had carried in fragments since childhood, laid out whole, alphabetized, cross-referenced, annotated in a smaller, fiercer script. Grammar tables. Verb paradigms for tenses English didn't bother to have. A tense for things that happened in a house that no longer exists. A tense for loving someone you are not permitted to name.

"She documented it," Xanthe whispered. "All these years I thought — I thought we were the only ones, that it would just *end*, that when I died it would be like a language going under the sea—"

"She was keeping it alive for someone," Casper said. "She just never said who."

He reached past Xanthe for the second notebook, and their hands touched on the cover, and neither of them moved for a moment longer than was strictly necessary.

* * *

Downstairs, the inventory went strangely.

Xanthe catalogued the dining room and found, in the sideboard drawer beneath the good linen, a christening gown folded around a lock of dark hair tied with ribbon gone brittle as wasps' wings. No name attached. No date. Xanthe wrote *textile, infant, provenance unknown* on the pad and then crossed it out, because provenance unknown was a lie of paperwork, and the house was full of those.

In the parlour there were photographs, and the photographs were wrong. There was Delphine at every age. There was the grandmother, Vivienne, sharp-eyed and beautiful, standing in the garden Casper had tended. There was Xanthe's mother at nine, gap-toothed on the front step. And there — in three separate frames, always at the edge, always half-turned away, as though the camera had caught him by accident — a boy. Dark-haired. Perhaps six. Perhaps seven. Xanthe held the frame up to the window light and felt the floor of the day tilt beneath them.

They did not say anything. They set the frame face-down on the table, which was not cataloguing, and moved on to the silver, which needed polishing and was easier to think about.

* * *

Casper found the letters on the second afternoon, in a biscuit tin inside a hatbox inside a wardrobe that had not been opened, by the smell of it, since the war.

He had not wanted to come back. He had spent the night turning over the boy in the photographs — the dark hair, the set of the shoulders — and telling himself that half the children in the county had dark hair. But Delphine was dead and the house was empty of anyone to forbid him, and so he came, and he climbed the wardrobe's ladder-stool, and the tin was there as though it had been left out for him, which was the kind of thing Vivienne Thackeray would have done, arranging a discovery she would not live to see.

The letters were addressed to *C*. Not to a name. To a letter.

He did not open them. He sat on the stool with the tin in his lap and the attic dust settling on his shoulders like a hand, and he read the postmarks instead, and the postmarks went back thirty years, one every year, always the same week — the week, he realized with a cold, rising certainty, of his birthday. He had never known his own birthday precisely; the children's home had guessed. Vivienne had never guessed anything in her life.

He put the lid back on the tin. He carried it downstairs. He did not say, *These are for me.* He set it on the dining table among the inventoried silver and said, "More papers," in a voice he was proud of, and Xanthe looked at the tin, and then at him, and then at the tin again, and said nothing at all.

* * *

That evening Xanthe sat in Delphine's kitchen with the notebooks spread around them like a tide line, reading.

The Language had rules Xanthe had never been taught. Their mother had given them only the words that were safe — *seluneth*, the small words for food and weather and goodnight — and had gone silent, always, when Xanthe asked why the words felt older than the house. Now the notebooks answered in Vivienne's hand, patient as a schoolmistress: the Language was not a dialect, it was a *decision*, built word by word across decades, a place to live in when no place would have her. There were words for kinds of silence. There were eleven words for door. There was a word — *amareveth* — that the notebook glossed, in English, in smaller letters, as *a child one must give away, and does not stop loving, and does not say*.

Xanthe read the glossary entry four times. The kitchen clock ticked like something counting down to an admission.

They thought of the boy in the photographs. They thought of the tin on the dining table, addressed to a letter instead of a name, because a name would have been evidence and Vivienne Thackeray had spent a lifetime ensuring there was none — except, Xanthe thought, except she had written everything down, she had documented *everything*, the woman who hid a son had built a museum of her own secrecy, and perhaps that was the whole of her, the hiding and the keeping, the same act performed in opposite directions.

Xanthe closed the notebook gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping person.

* * *

Casper did not sleep. He lay in the spare room — Xanthe had said *you can't commute from wherever you commute from, stay, we'll work faster* — and listened to the house settle, and thought about the word he had heard through the floor all those years ago.

In the morning he found Xanthe already at the dining table, the tin between them like a fourth person.

"I didn't open them," Xanthe said.

"I know. I saw."

"They're addressed to C."

"Yes."

The light came through the lace curtains and lay across the table in small broken pieces. Casper sat down. Neither of them touched the tin. Between them, unspoken, sat the whole architecture of it: the christening gown, the boy in the frames, the word for a child given away, the letters that arrived every year on the birthday a children's home had had to guess. Every object in the house had been quietly testifying for two days, and now the room was so full of evidence that speaking would have been almost redundant — and also impossible, because to say it aloud would be to make Vivienne's choice real in a way that paper had mercifully refused to be.

"She wrote everything down," Xanthe said at last. "The whole Language. Every word."

"I know."

"But some things she only wrote *around*."

Casper looked at the tin. The biscuit brand on the lid had been one Vivienne kept in the garden shed for the days he worked through rain; she would bring two biscuits out on a saucer and they would eat them without speaking, and it had been, he understood only now, the most honest conversation they ever had.

"There's one more box," he said. "In the attic. Under the dress form. She told me once — she was ill, near the end, she held my hand and said, *when I'm gone, look under the dress form* — and I never did. I couldn't. Delphine paid me to leave the week after the funeral, and I told myself it was because there was nothing left for me here."

"And was there?"

Casper looked at the tin. At the boy in the photograph he had not mentioned. At Xanthe, who had the grandmother's exact way of waiting for an answer without demanding one.

"I think," he said, "there was everything left for me here. That was the problem."

They went up to the attic together, the two of them and the swinging bulb, and Casper lifted the sheet from the dress form, and beneath it — flat, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the same brittle ribbon as the lock of hair — lay a box with a name on it, written in Vivienne's upright hand, the first time in thirty years that anyone in that house had dared to write it down.

The name was Casper.
