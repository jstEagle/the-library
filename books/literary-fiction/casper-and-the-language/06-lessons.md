## 6. Lessons

We began with the word for rain.

Not *rain* — the Language had no such plain word, nothing so general. Grandmother had built it the way she built everything, out of distinctions nobody else thought worth making. There was a word for rain that arrived after long dryness, and a word for rain that fell on a roof you were leaving, and a word — my favorite, as a child — for the rain that came down while the sun was still out, which she called *vessilin* and said was the sky apologizing for something it would never name.

I wrote *vessilin* on the back of an old receipt with a pen that was running out, and Casper looked at it the way a man looks at a photograph of a place he's been in a dream.

"Vessilin," he repeated. His accent was wrong in a way I couldn't fix, the vowels rounded where hers had been flat as river stones. But he said it with a kind of hunger, and I understood then that I wasn't teaching him so much as feeding him.

"It means—" I started.

"I know what it means." He said it gently, the way you'd correct a child. "She told me. The sky apologizing." He turned the receipt over in his hands. "She used to stand in the garden when it happened. Just stand there with her sleeves getting wet, like she was receiving an apology on someone else's behalf."

We met on Tuesdays and Thursdays, because those were the days the estate lawyers didn't need the house, and the house was where the words lived. Delphine's sitting room, with its sheets over the furniture like a room holding its breath. I'd bring a thermos of coffee; he'd bring the garden in with him, soil under his nails no matter how he scrubbed, the smell of cold green things clinging to his grey coat.

I had a system, at first. Notebooks, columns, the scholar's approach — nouns here, verbs there, the grammar I'd half-inherited and half-reconstructed from memory and from the four postcards a month I'd been writing to a dead woman at a burned-down address. I laid it out like a lesson plan because a lesson plan was safer than what was actually happening, which was that every word I gave him was a word I was taking out of the sealed room where I'd kept them and handing to a stranger to hold.

* * *

The trouble started in the third week, with the past tense.

The Language had two past tenses, and Grandmother had drawn the line between them in a place no other language draws it. There was the past that was finished — the past you could close the door on — and there was the past that was still happening somewhere, the past that continued to occur in some other room of the world whether or not you attended. My mother's death, I had always written in the first. Grandmother's death, in my postcards, I'd drifted into the second without noticing.

Casper caught it on a Thursday.

"You've mixed them," he said. I'd written a sentence aloud, translating something banal — *the kettle is broken, we should buy a new one* — and he was frowning at my grammar the way Delphine used to frown at the cousins' table manners.

"It's a kettle, Casper."

"You used the closed past for the breaking and the open past for the brokenness." He said it without apology, this man who three weeks ago had known perhaps two hundred words of the Language and now corrected my syntax like he'd been speaking it since the cradle. "She'd have boxed your ears."

"She'd have boxed yours for knowing that."

"She did, once. For less." He almost smiled. Then, quieter: "The open past is for things still happening. The kettle's brokenness is still happening. It's still broken. That's the whole point of the tense — you don't get to finish a thing just because you've stopped looking at it."

I sat with that. Outside, it was beginning to rain — the ordinary kind, the kind the Language dismissed with a single syllable, *sen*, barely a word at all.

"My mother," I said, and then stopped, because I had not planned to say it, and my mouth had gone ahead without the rest of me.

Casper waited. He was good at waiting. Twenty years of tending a garden will teach a person that most things grow if you simply refuse to leave.

"I never said goodbye to her." The sentence came out flat, factual, the closed past tense of a confession. "She was in the hospital for eleven weeks. I visited every Sunday and I talked to her about the weather and my job and whether the nurses were stealing her biscuits, and every Sunday I thought, next week I'll say it, next week I'll say the real thing. And then there wasn't a next week. She died on a Tuesday. I wasn't there."

*Sen*, said the rain against the window.

"She left a letter," I went on, because now that the door was open the whole room wanted out. "The hospital gave it to me with her things. Sealed. She sealed it herself — licked the envelope in the ward, someone said, asked for the glue stick because she didn't trust her own spit at the end. Four years. I've moved it twice. It's in my flat in a drawer with the spare batteries and the takeout menus, and I have never — I can't—"

I stopped. Casper reached across the sheet-draped armchair and did not touch me, which was somehow more intimate than touching. He laid his hand on the armrest an inch from mine and left it there, a word unspoken in a language we both knew.

"In the Language," he said, "what's the word for a thing you keep but cannot open?"

I shook my head. "There isn't one. She never made one."

"Then we'll have to invent it," he said. "She'd have liked that. A language that can't grow is just a museum."

* * *

After that, the lessons changed shape. Less grammar, more confession — or perhaps there was no difference, in that house, in that tongue. The Language had been built, I was coming to understand, by a woman who needed words for things English refused to hold. There was a word for grief that arrived late. A word for loving someone you were angry at. A word — *mirravel* — for the specific tenderness of watching someone sleep when you are not sure they will wake, which I taught him and watched him go still as a held breath, and I knew then that he had used it, in his head, at some bedside, for years, and that the bedside had been hers.

"You loved her," I said. Not a question. Not an accusation either, though I felt the shape of one in my mouth and chose not to give it weight.

"She taught me to read," Casper said. "Properly, I mean. I could read before — signs, forms, the backs of cereal boxes. She taught me reading like it was a country you could live in." He turned his coffee cup, not drinking. "Your grandmother was the first person who ever asked me a question and waited for the whole answer."

"And Delphine paid you to leave."

"And Delphine paid me to leave." He said it steadily. "And I took the money, which is its own confession, and I have spent twenty years spending it badly."

I should have asked then. I should have asked what he was to her, what she was to him, whether the garden was a garden or something else — the question the whole family had been circling since he stood weeping at the back of the funeral. But the afternoon light had gone the color of weak tea, and he looked so much like a man holding something fragile that I let it go, the way you let a bird go, not out of kindness but because closing your hand would tell you nothing you were ready to know.

Instead I taught him *mirravel*'s sibling — the word for watching someone sleep when you are sure they will wake, which is a different word entirely, warmer, almost domestic, and he laughed for the first time since I'd known him, a rusty sound, a door opening in a house long shut.

* * *

It was near the end of the sixth week that I brought the letter.

I hadn't planned to. I'd walked to the house with it in my coat pocket the way you carry a stone you intend to throw into a river and cannot yet release. All through the lesson — we were doing colors, the Language had eleven words for green and Casper was learning them with the diligence of a man planting seedlings — I felt it against my ribs, that small heavy rectangle, my mother's handwriting on the front in ink that had begun to fade at the edges like something drowning slowly.

At the door, leaving, I stopped.

"She's in the drawer," I said. "My mother. The letter. It's been four years and I write to a house that burned down because I can't open an envelope."

Casper stood in the doorway with the evening behind him, and the light made his grey coat look like weather coming.

"Bring her here," he said. "Next Thursday. We'll read it together. In the Language, if it's in the Language. And if it isn't—" he paused, and I saw him choose his words with the care of a man transplanting something, roots and all, "—then we'll translate it. Word by word. However long it takes. Some things shouldn't be read in a hurry."

I walked home in the dark with the letter against my heart and did not sleep, and all the way I could feel the envelope's edge through the wool of my coat, thin as a blade, patient as a seed.

Thursday was six days away. On Wednesday, the cousins' solicitor's letter arrived at my flat, and it was not about the letter in my drawer at all.
