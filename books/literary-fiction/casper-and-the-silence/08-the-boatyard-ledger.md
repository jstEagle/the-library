## 8. The Boatyard Ledger

The accounts lived in the back office of the boatyard, in a green metal cabinet that had not been opened properly since his father died. Casper knew this because he was the one who had locked it. He had turned the key with the particular relief of a man closing a door on a room where something embarrassing was kept — not shameful, exactly, but private in a way that felt like an accusation. Four years of dust on the handle now. He wiped it with his sleeve and thought: *this is what I've become, a man who wipes dust off his father's secrets so he can go looking for money.*

Outside, the yard went on making its small noises. The travel lift ticking as it cooled. Gulls arguing over something on the slipway. His two remaining employees — Dougie and a boy called Tam who smelled permanently of chips — were scraping antifoul off a fishing boat that would never fish again, but nobody had told it that yet.

Casper sat down at the desk that had been his father's and pulled out the first box.

* * *

He had never been good with money, which was a strange confession for a man who ran a business, except that running the boatyard had never really been about running a business. It was about keeping a thing going. His father had understood this. His father had treated the yard less like an enterprise than like a heartbeat — something you maintained without examining too closely, because examination was how you found arrhythmia. Every winter, when the work thinned and the harbour froze at its edges, Harold Nystrom would sit in this same chair with the ledger open and a pencil behind his ear, and he would make the numbers behave through sheer force of habit. Not cleverness. Habit. He had a way of writing figures that made them look inevitable.

Casper did not have that way. Casper's figures looked like what they were: hopeful guesses in a strong hand.

Six weeks to the auction. The solicitor's letter lay on the corner of the desk where he'd thrown it, and every time his eye caught the words *guide price* it performed a small act of violence against him. He had done the arithmetic a dozen times, on the backs of invoices, on the steam-fogged window of the office, once in the sand with a stick while Tam watched him as though he'd gone religious. The answer was always the same and always impossible. To bid on Coldharbour House he would need to raise more money than the boatyard had cleared in any three of its best years combined.

Which meant the loan. Which meant the yard itself, signed away, mortgaged to a bank in Inverness whose manager had a handshake like a wet fish and eyes that moved from your face to your collateral before you'd finished your first sentence.

*You're mortgaging your future for a graveyard.* That was Fiona on the phone last night, her voice doing the thing it did when she was trying to be kind by being brutal — a voice she had learned, God help her, from him. He had said something about family, about duty, about not letting strangers strip the house for salvage while the paint was still on the walls, and he had heard himself say it and known, even as the words left him, that they were Sunday clothes. Words you put on for church. Underneath them was the plainer truth, which was that Casper Nystrom could not bear the idea of the house changing hands because the house was the last fixed point in a life that had otherwise consisted entirely of things being taken away. His mother's mind. His sister. His father, then the yard's good years, then the yard's adequate years, then this. A man who has lost enough starts building walls around whatever is left, even if what's left is only a building with dry rot and a view.

So: the cabinet. Because if there was money anywhere, it was here, in the paper sediment of forty years.

* * *

The boxes came out one at a time. Invoices from chandleries long closed. Wage packets from men whose names he half-knew, faces surfacing like fish — Alec Tait with his burned forearm, the Muir brothers who'd both drowned within a decade of each other and neither in a boat built by the yard, which his father had taken as a professional wound. Insurance renewals. A receipt for a secondhand lathe, 1987, £340, the single best purchase in the company's history according to his father, who mentioned it annually.

And ledgers. Eleven of them, cloth-bound, spines cracked, arranged chronologically with a precision that made Casper's throat tighten. His father had been illiterate in everything but boats and books; give him a hull or a column of figures and he was fluent. Casper lifted them out and stacked them on the desk and the stack made a sound like a slow drumroll.

He started with the most recent and worked backwards, because he was looking for money, not meaning. The recent ones gave him nothing new — the same slow arithmetic of decline he lived inside daily. Materials up, work down, the fishing fleet shrinking year on year until the harbour held more memories than hulls. He skimmed. He turned pages with the impatience of a man reading for one number only.

It was in the fourth ledger back — 1994, the year Zephyrine left, though he didn't let himself think that yet — that the pattern appeared.

A payment. Monthly. Small at first, then not small. Written in his father's hand, in the neat inevitable figures, under a heading that was not a heading at all but just two letters set apart from the rest of the columns as though the accountant in Harold had refused to name it and the man in Harold had insisted on recording it anyway.

*M.C.*

January 1994: £40. February: £40. Then June of that year it jumped to £75, and stayed there, and climbed slowly across the years the way rents climb, or pensions, or anything that intends to continue. Casper turned page after page and the letters marched on. M.C. M.C. M.C. Through the bad winter of '98 when the yard laid off four men. Through the year his mother stopped recognising the harbour. Through 2003, when Casper himself had come home with his marriage already sinking and his father had said nothing, only handed him a paintbrush. M.C., every month, without fail, without explanation, right up to the final ledger, where the payments stopped in March — the month his father died — mid-year, unfinished, like a sentence cut off at the throat.

Forty years. Casper sat back in the chair and the chair complained under him and he barely heard it.

He knew who M.C. was. Of course he knew. There was only one person those initials could belong to, and she had lived in the east wing of Coldharbour House for as long as there had been an east wing, sewing by the window, speaking a language to his sister that he had never been permitted to learn. Maren. Maren Coldharbour — no, that wasn't right either, she'd never been a Coldharbour, she'd just *been*, the way weather is, permanent and unexplained. M.C. His father had paid her. For decades. Quietly, monthly, in a ledger he kept separate in his head from all the other honest columns, and had never once — Casper searched his memory now with the desperate thoroughness of a man ransacking a flooded house — never once said her name to his son in connection with money.

Hush money. The phrase arrived first, ugly and immediate. His father paying for silence. Paying the old woman to keep whatever she knew inside the walls of that house. Casper stared at the columns and tried to imagine what a sewing woman from the east wing could know that was worth thirty years of a boatyard's thin profits, and his imagination, which had always been his weakest muscle, offered him nothing but shapes.

But then — gratitude? The other word arrived slower, wearing different clothes. Because look at the amounts. Hush money didn't rise gently over the years like a wage. Hush money didn't survive the winter of '98, when his father had sat at this very desk until two in the morning deciding which men to let go, and still paid M.C. her seventy-five pounds without blinking. You don't protect a secret past the point where protecting it costs you men. Unless it isn't protection. Unless it's the opposite — unless it's thanks, or debt, or love of some grim northern variety that expresses itself exclusively in bank drafts and silence.

His father had loved few things and said the word never. He loved the yard. He loved Casper's mother in the way of a man watering a plant he admires but doesn't understand. And Maren — Casper tried to remember his father and Maren in the same room and found almost nothing. Politeness at Christmas. A nod across the churchyard. Once, memorably, nothing at all: a dinner where the old woman sat at the far end of the table and his father did not look at her for two hours, and Casper had been fourteen and had noticed the not-looking and filed it away in the place where children keep the things that will later explain their lives.

Not-looking. That was a form of looking. Casper understood this now, sitting in his father's chair with the dead man's handwriting under his thumb. You don't look at someone for two hours unless looking means something.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon without noticing the light change. Dougie put his head round the door at five, saw the expression on Casper's face, and withdrew without comment, which was the correct maritime instinct. By six the office was blue with dusk and Casper had the earliest ledger open on his knee, the one from 1963, bought new that January according to the invoice tucked inside, and he was turning toward the first entries with the feeling of a man walking down a corridor toward a door he has always known was there and has always chosen not to open.

There. First page, third entry, dated the eleventh of March, 1963. *M.C. — £10.* And beneath it, in his father's hand, younger then, harder, a single word he had written and then ruled through so thoroughly the pen had gone through the paper:

Casped tilted the page to the window's last light and read the ghost of it backwards, from the indentations, the way you'd read a watermark.

*Owed.*

His father had owed her. From the very beginning. Ten pounds a month in 1963 was not hush money; ten pounds a month in 1963, from a young husband with a new baby and a struggling yard, was a tithe. Was penance. Was the beginning of a debt that Harold Nystrom had spent his entire working life repaying without ever once telling anyone what it was for.

Casper closed the ledger. The sound it made was small and enormous.

He sat in the darkening office and thought about his mother, who had died in that house with her mind already gone, calling sometimes for a name nobody in the family owned. He thought about Zephyrine up there now, alone among the tagged furniture, cataloguing the past for strangers. He thought about the phone call nine years ago that had ended everything between them, and how he had hung up first, always first, because hanging up first was the closest thing to power he'd ever learned.

And he thought: *she'll know.* His sister, who knew everything, who had always gotten to be the one who knew things — she would look at these initials and these dates and she would understand in an hour what he couldn't understand in a day. The thought should have bittered him. Instead it did something worse. It made him want to drive north tonight, ledger under his arm, and stand in whatever room she was working in and say: *look at this, tell me what my father was paying for, tell me what we were.*

He didn't go. He put the ledgers back in order, all eleven, squared their edges the way his father would have, and locked the cabinet, and stood a moment with the key in his fist.

Then he took it back out, and put the 1963 ledger in his coat pocket instead, and drove home along the dark water road with the book riding against his ribs like a second heart.
