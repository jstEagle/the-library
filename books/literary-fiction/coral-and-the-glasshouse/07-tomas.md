## 7. Tomas

Letter nine sat on the kitchen table between us like something that had been dug out of a wall, which, in a sense, it had. The envelope was soft at the corners from a decade of dark, and Lior's handwriting on the front—*Ada*—looked younger than I remembered my own hand being. Rounder. Less careful. A hand that hadn't yet learned what it was capable of breaking.

We had agreed to the protocol the way two engineers agree to anything: in writing, more or less. One letter per evening, read aloud, no interruptions except for questions of fact. Ada had insisted on that clause—questions of fact—and I had signed off on it without thinking through what facts she might want.

Eight evenings had passed. Eight letters of apology circling without landing, of bridges described in increasingly desperate metaphor, of me at thirty-four trying to say *I am not well* in the only language I trusted, which was load paths and deflection and the slow creep of steel under stress. Ada had listened to all of them with what I had come to think of as her stenographer's face: level, attentive, giving away nothing. She took actual notes sometimes. I tried not to watch her do it.

Tonight she came down with the wine already poured—two glasses, which was new—and sat across from me with her sleeves pushed up, the way she did when she meant business. The work light we'd rigged over the table because half the kitchen bulbs were packed gave everything a garage-sale glow.

"Nine," she said.

I picked it up. My hands were steady, which felt like a betrayal of the moment. Somewhere in the walls the house ticked and settled, emptying itself around us.

"*Ada,*" I read. "*I keep starting this letter and stopping. There is someone. You may already know that, or you may have known it for months while I told myself I was a closed book. His name is Tomas.*"

I heard myself say the name out loud in our kitchen for the first time. It went into the room and just stayed there, occupying space, taking up a chair.

I kept reading.

"*His name is Tomas Vidal. He works the night inspection rotation out of the Delaney yard. We met on the Corben Street span last March when his crew flagged a bearing I'd cleared. He was right about the bearing. That's how it started—not with a look, not with drinks. With him being right about a bearing and me being grateful to be corrected by someone who wasn't afraid of me.*"

Ada's pen moved. Actually moved. She was writing something down.

"*I want to tell you what it is so you don't imagine it as something else. It isn't passion. I know that's the word everyone would reach for, and it would be a lie, and I've spent enough of this year lying by adjective. What it is, is quiet. When I'm with him I don't have to translate. Do you understand what I mean by that? At home I feel like a document written in a language nobody here speaks, and every day I do the translating myself, both directions, and I'm tired, Ada. With Tomas there is no translation. I say a thing and it arrives. That's all. That's the whole sordid affair: I said things and they arrived.*"

My voice caught on that last line, not from emotion but from recognition—the old wound of it, the young idiocy of it. I looked up. Ada was watching me now, pen still, face still.

"*I'm not telling you this to hurt you. I'm telling you because you deserve a report and not a rumor. You always said you'd rather have the bad numbers than the comfortable ones. So these are the bad numbers. I'm sorry they're mine.*"

The letter ended there. No signature—just the date, which I wouldn't read aloud, though Ada could see it upside down if she wanted, and she wanted; I watched her eyes drop to it.

The house ticked. The refrigerator hummed its one note. Somewhere outside a car went past with its radio too loud, some song about wanting things, and then it was gone.

Ada set down her pen. She squared the page against the table edge, a small precise gesture, aligning my confession like a form to be filed.

Then she asked her first question.

"When."

It wasn't even really a question. It came out flat, declarative, a datum requested.

"March," I said. "Through—" I did the arithmetic I had sworn I'd never need again. "Through the following February. Eleven months."

She wrote it down. *March–February.* Eleven months, entered into evidence.

"Where," she said.

"Mostly his place. On Kessler Road. Above the garage, the apartment with the—there were skylights." I stopped myself from describing the skylights. "Sometimes the truck. Twice, God help me, at the inspection depot, which I'm only mentioning because you asked where and I've decided to answer completely."

"Completely," she repeated, without inflection, and wrote something I couldn't see.

"How long," she said. "Each time. On average."

That one knocked the wind out of me in a way none of the others had. Not *how long did it go on*—she'd gotten that—but *how long each time*, as if I were reporting dwell times, as if we were auditing. And maybe we were. Maybe that was exactly what this was: an audit, eleven years overdue, conducted by the person who had kept the books.

"An hour," I said. "Two. Sometimes we just—" I stopped. "Sometimes nothing happened. Sometimes I drove there and sat in the truck outside his building for forty minutes and then drove home. Those nights counted too. They counted more, maybe."

Ada looked at me then, fully, for the first time since I'd begun reading. Her face did the thing I will remember until I die, which is nothing. It did absolutely nothing. No flinch, no fury, no grief arriving on schedule. Just those gray eyes going over me the way I go over a weld—looking for the crack, finding it, noting its location, moving on.

"Why didn't he come to the house?" she asked. "You never brought him here."

The question was so far outside the range of questions I'd braced for that I actually laughed—a short, appalled sound.

"I—no. Never. This house was—" I gestured at the bare walls, the stacked boxes, the whole stripped skeleton of the place. "This was the structure. You don't bring the flood into the structure."

"Mm," said Ada, and wrote something down.

I should explain what her restraint was doing to me, because it was doing something terrible and specific. If she had thrown the wine glass I could have worked with the wine glass. If she had cried I could have knelt and done the penance choreography, the whole liturgy of it, and we both knew that liturgy by heart even if we'd never performed it. Rage is a load path. It gives the force somewhere to go.

But Ada asking *where* and *when* and *how long* and writing the answers down—that was not a load path. That was a survey. She was measuring the damage the way I measure damage: calmly, completely, with the terrible patience of someone who intends the record to outlive the emotion. And underneath the calm, I could feel it, the way you feel a bridge humming before you can hear it—something enormous holding itself very still.

"You're not going to ask why," I said. My voice came out wrong. "Aren't you going to ask why?"

"I asked why eight letters ago," she said. "You answered it tonight. You said you were tired of translating." She turned the notebook toward me briefly, and there it was, my own sentence copied out in her small square handwriting, dated. "I'm collecting facts now. The whys are in the letters. You wrote forty-one whys. I'm letting you have them."

"And what are you doing with the facts?"

She capped the pen. Set it down parallel to the notebook, deliberate as a surgeon laying down an instrument.

"I'm deciding," she said, "what weight they can carry."

* * *

Afterward—I don't know how long after; the work light made the hours shapeless—we sorted boxes. That was part of the protocol too, unspoken but real: the readings couldn't be allowed to sit in the room alone or they'd metastasize, so we followed them with manual labor, hands busy, the body given something honest to do while the mind processed.

We were packing the hall closet when Ada held up the umbrella stand—that ugly cast-iron thing shaped like a heron that my mother had given us and that neither of us had ever liked—and said, without looking at me:

"He was right about the bearing, you said. In the letter. That's how it started."

"Yes."

"So the affair began with professional respect."

"It began with—" I searched for it. "With relief. I told you. It wasn't—"

"You said that too." She wrapped the heron in newspaper, three turns, taped it. "Relief. Being seen without translation." She set the bundle in the box marked HALL and stood there a moment with her back to me. "Do you know what I did during those eleven months, Lior? While you were being seen?"

I waited. The tape gun clicked once in her hand.

"I translated," she said. "Both directions. Every day. For a man who was getting his translation done elsewhere." She put the lid on the box. "Anyway. The closet's done."

And she carried it out to the landing, and I stood in the empty closet smelling other people's shoe leather and old rain, and understood that shame, real shame, doesn't burn. It doesn't rage or flood or collapse anything. It just sits there, exactly the size of itself, weighing exactly what it weighs, waiting to be measured.

Tomorrow was letter ten. I already knew what was in it. I had reread them all, that first night, alone in the bathroom with the fan running, cataloguing the detonations ahead like defects on a span.

Letter fourteen was coming. Letter fourteen was the one I dreaded above all the rest, and I had six evenings left to figure out whether there existed any tolerable way to read your own handwriting aloud to the woman you'd abandoned on the worst night of her life—when she didn't yet know it had been the worst night of her life.

But that was six days away. Tonight there was only the heron umbrella stand, wrapped like a corpse, and Ada's footsteps going upstairs, even and unhurried, and the fact—entered into evidence, dated, filed—that she had not yet raised her voice, and that I would have given anything, anything at all, to hear her do it.
