## 17. Tomas Returns

The message came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Catastrophes in Lior's experience preferred Tuesdays—the day with no identity, the filler between the week's load-bearing days, the expansion joint of the calendar where everything structural was allowed to shift.

It arrived by email, of all things, to an address Lior checked perhaps twice a month for bridge certification notices. The subject line read: *An old colleague?* As if Tomas had spent a week drafting the fiction that they had ever been colleagues, as if the word could be laid over what they'd been like a thin plate of steel over a crack—covering it, changing nothing about what lay beneath.

*I'm ill,* the message said. *Not dramatically. Tired, mostly, in a way the doctors keep taking notes on without answering. I've been reading about your bridges. You're still good at your work. I have some things I never said and I find, at my age and condition, that unsaid things have started to weigh more than said ones. If you're willing. No obligation. —T.*

Lior read it four times at the kitchen table, in a house so empty now that sound behaved differently—arriving too fast, leaving too slowly, every footstep an announcement. Forty-one letters down to thirty-four read. A mattress on the kitchen floor. Ada at her sister's until Thursday, sorting the last of her mother's china into boxes labeled in Ada's precise hand: *FRAGILE*, as if anything left in this house weren't already.

The old protocol would have been silence. Eleven years of marriage had trained them both in it; the affair itself had been conducted largely in silences, in the negative space of schedules, in the eloquent nothing of *I'll be late* and *don't wait up*. Silence had been the native language of the whole catastrophe. And here was Tomas, ten years later, asking—in writing, no less—for speech.

Lior wrote back the same evening, before the courage could evaporate: *Thursday afternoon. I can give you an hour.*

Then sat staring at the sent folder, feeling like an inspector who has just signed off on a span they know, in their professional bones, is compromised.

* * *

Tomas lived in a ground-floor flat off Meridian Road, behind a hedge gone leggy with neglect, in a building Lior remembered from the year itself only as a hallway and a door and the particular smell of eucalyptus oil that Tomas burned because he claimed it kept his head clear. The eucalyptus smell hit Lior at the threshold like a time capsule breaking open—not painful, exactly, but vertiginous, the way stepping onto a familiar span after years away makes you doubt your memory of its pitch and sway.

Tomas answered the door himself, which surprised Lior more than it should have. In the intervening decade Lior had privately promoted him to a kind of mythic status—the great escape, the door out—and myths don't answer their own doors in cardigans. But there he was: thinner than memory, gray at the temples in a way that looked earned rather than styled, wearing a cardigan the color of weak tea over a shirt buttoned one button too high, as if he'd dressed by instruction rather than instinct.

"Lior." He said it carefully, testing the weight of the name. "You look—" He stopped, visibly editing. "Thank you for coming."

"You look tired," Lior said, because the alternative pleasantries had all died in transit.

"I am tired. It's the primary symptom. Come in, mind the—" He gestured vaguely at a stack of medical folders on a side table, each one tabbed in different colors, an organizational system that told Lior more about his current life than any greeting could. A man who color-coded his illness was a man who had made peace with paperwork.

The flat was smaller than the year remembered it. That was the first disillusionment, delivered before Lior even sat down: rooms shrink when you stop needing them to hold something enormous. There was the same sagging bookshelf, but the books on it were different—field guides, mostly. Birds. Fungi. A fat volume on lichens with a bookmark sticking out of it like a tongue. On the wall where Lior half-remembered a print of some brooding seascape there was instead a child's drawing, crayon-bright, signed *MAYA AGE 7* in the triumphant capitals of the recently literate.

"Your niece's?" Lior asked, nodding at it.

"My daughter's," Tomas said, and watched Lior absorb it. "She's nine now. She lives with her mother in Leeds. I see her alternate weekends and every other Wednesday, per an arrangement my solicitor describes as 'workable' and I describe as survivable."

"You have a daughter."

"I have a daughter," he agreed. "Tea? I only have the ordinary kind. I gave up the fancy stuff when I stopped trying to impress anyone."

"That was a while ago, then."

"It was," Tomas said, with the ghost of the old smile—the one that had once seemed like being seen, and now just looked like a man being polite in his own kitchen. "A while ago."

* * *

He made the tea with his back to Lior, and Lior studied the flat the way they'd study a structure: reading the cracks, the deflections, the places where load had settled over time. A walking cane leaned against the counter, unadorned, practical. A pill organizer the size of a tackle box on the windowsill, its little doors labeled M T W T F S S in someone's careful handwriting. Two armchairs angled toward each other near the window, both worn at the same spots—a configuration that suggested visitors, plural, regular ones. Someone had built a life here. Not a dramatic life. A maintained one.

"So," Tomas said, sitting down across from Lior with the small controlled exhale of a man whose body now charged him for every transition. "You got my message."

"I did."

"And you came. I want you to know I didn't expect that. I ran probabilities. Most of them ended with the email deleted unread."

"I nearly did delete it."

"What changed your mind?"

Lior turned the teacup in their hands. The honest answer was: *Ada. Thirty-four letters. A marriage currently undergoing controlled demolition in a house with no furniture.* What they said was: "I'm practicing saying yes to hard appointments."

Tomas nodded as if this made sense, and maybe it did. Illness gives people a fluency in euphemism.

"I should tell you how this looks from inside," he said. "Because I suspect you've carried a version of me around for ten years, and I'd like to correct the record while correction is still cheap."

"All right."

"In your version, I imagine, I'm the exit. The open window. The year you climbed out of your life through me." He said it without accusation, almost fondly, the way you'd describe a role you'd been cast in against your will. "And I remember that year completely differently. Do you want the true version?"

"That's why I'm here."

"The true version," Tomas said, looking out the window at his leggy hedge, "is the loneliest year of my life."

Lior waited. Outside, a magpie worked along the hedge top with the busy indifference of a creature with no unresolved correspondence.

"You have to understand what I was," Tomas said. "I was thirty-one, newly separated, living in a flat I couldn't afford, doing work I'd stopped believing in, and along comes this person—brilliant, funny, so tightly wound you could hear the tension hum if you stood close enough—who looks at me like I'm the answer to a question. Do you know what that does to a lonely man?"

"It feels like being seen," Lior said quietly. "Without translation."

"Yes." He turned back. "Exactly that. And I believed it for about four months. And then I started noticing the translation problem runs both ways." He set his cup down. "You were never actually *with* me, Lior. You were *away* from her. Those are different addresses, even if the postcode's the same. I'd sit across from you and watch you compose sentences you'd never send—I didn't know then that you literally did that, though God, it explains so much—and I realized somewhere around month five that I wasn't a person to you. I was a pressure valve. And pressure valves don't get taken to meet anyone's friends. Pressure valves don't get Sunday mornings. I spent that entire year waiting for you to arrive and you never once fully did. Even in my bed you were halfway to somewhere else. Usually, I think, halfway home."

Lior's throat was tight. "You never said."

"Said what? 'Excuse me, I notice I'm being used as an architectural feature'?" He shook his head. "I was lonely too, and lonely people take bad arrangements, because a bad arrangement is still an arrangement. That's the thing nobody tells you about escape hatches. You jump through them and land in the same weather."

The magpie outside had found something worth investigating and was worrying at it. Lior watched it rather than watch Tomas, because watching Tomas had become suddenly difficult.

"Why write to me now?" Lior asked finally. "Why reopen it?"

"Because I'm dying at a reasonable pace, and dying makes you audit." He smiled briefly. "Occupational habit of yours, I understand. Auditing. Well, it's mine now too. And in my audit, that year kept flagging. Not as the great romance—God, no, it was shabby and furtive and we ate a lot of sad takeaway—but as the year I treated another human being worst. Not cruelly. Worse than cruelly: *carelessly*. I let you believe I was a door out, knowing full well I was a wall with a painting of a door on it. When you finally asked me—do you remember what you asked me?"

Lior remembered. The parking garage, the rain, the question shaped like *come with me* without either of them saying it aloud.

"I refused," Tomas said. "And you looked at me like I'd betrayed you. And I let you go on thinking that, all these years, because it was easier than telling you the truth, which is that I refused because I knew—knew with total certainty—that you weren't leaving your marriage for me. You were leaving it *at* me. Like a coat check. And I didn't want your marriage. I wanted company. There's a difference, and I was too much of a coward to name it while it mattered."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the building a washing machine entered its spin cycle, a low industrial hum, the sound of someone else's ordinary life proceeding.

"I built a whole architecture on you," Lior said. "Load paths. Exits. I had you rated for capacity you never had."

"I know." Tomas said it gently, without triumph. "That's rather what I wanted to tell you. You've spent ten years, I think, keeping me as the road not taken. The viable alternative. And I'm here to report from the alternative, Lior: the weather's identical. I got the daughter, eventually, out of a different disaster. I got the lichens. I got this flat. And I got sick, the way everyone gets sick, alone-ish, with a pill organizer and alternating weekends." He spread his hands. "There was never a door. I'm sorry. I know you needed there to have been a door."

Lior looked at him—at the cardigan, the folders, the crayon drawing of a stick-figure family under a scribbled sun—and felt something in the chest give way, not painfully, the way old ice goes. For eleven years Tomas had been the counterfactual, the proof that another life had been available and declined. And here was the man himself, reporting that the other life was this: tea, tabs, a hedge that needed cutting, loneliness with better furniture.

"I should go," Lior said.

"You probably should." Tomas struggled up with the help of the chair arm, waved off Lior's offer of a hand with a flicker of the old pride. At the door he paused. "One more thing. Free of charge, as we say in my line."

"Which is?"

"When you write your reports—your bridge reports—do you ever write *it held*?"

"Sometimes. When it's true."

"Mm." He opened the door. Eucalyptus and cold air. "I hope somebody writes it about you. About the two of you, I mean. Whatever the two of you are now. *It held.* Past tense or otherwise. Somebody ought to say it out loud, before the demolition people come."

* * *

Lior drove home with the windows cracked despite the cold, needing the noise. The route passed the Harrow Street overpass—Harrow Street, always Harrow Street, the novel of their marriage written in steel overhead—and on impulse took the access road and parked below it, engine ticking, looking up at the underside of the span where the light came through in long gray bars.

Ten years ago, driving to Tomas's, Lior had passed under this same bridge and thought: *this is the night it ends, one way or the other.* Tonight the thought was quieter: *the hatch was painted on. The wall was always a wall.*

And yet. Sitting in the cold car under the bridge, Lior found the disillusionment was not entirely grief. There was something else under it, the way there's something under every inspection report that surprises you: relief. The terrible arithmetic of the last six weeks—forty-one letters, Ada's flat voice, the pregnancy she'd carried alone, the sentence in letter nineteen that Lior would regret until the end of their days—all of it had rested on a hidden assumption: *there was somewhere else to go.* Remove the assumption and the ledger changed. Not forgiven. Recalculated.

Ada would be home Thursday. There were seven letters left unread, and the final conversation waiting at the end of them like a span not yet load-tested.

Lior took out the phone and typed, deleted, retyped:

*Tomas remembers the year differently than I did. I want to tell you about it before Thursday. Before letter twenty-eight. It belongs in order.*

Sent it. Watched the screen. Three dots appeared, vanished, appeared again.

Then Ada's reply, arriving with the speed of a woman who had learned, over six weeks, to answer hard messages fast:

*Come tonight. Bring the wine. We can do twenty-eight early.*

Lior put the phone down on the passenger seat and looked up once more at the underside of the bridge, at the hairline map of cracks the city paid them to monitor and the city chose, most years, to tolerate.

Then started the car, and drove toward the house with no furniture, where the one person who had a right to every fact was waiting with two jam jars and the last of the good wine.
