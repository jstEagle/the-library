## 1. The Tuning Fork Year

Every third Thursday of the month, for fifteen years, I have tuned the Verrone grand.

I could tell you it's a job. I have told people that. The piano is a Bösendorfer, 1938, walnut case gone the color of strong tea, and it goes out of true the way old houses do—slowly, then all at once, usually after the first cold snap when the radiators wake up and start their argument with the season. A piano in a house like that needs a tuner the way a marriage needs a witness: someone who shows up regularly, notices everything, and keeps her mouth shut.

I am very good at keeping my mouth shut. It's the one instrument I never had to learn.

The ritual goes like this. I take the 42 bus from my stop on Delancey, ride it eleven minutes past the laundromat with the broken clock and the church whose bells ring four minutes late because somebody in 1961 decided punctuality was vanity. I get off at Halsey. I walk the last block because the Verrone house sits at the top of a rise where the street gives up its grid and curves, and buses don't like curves, and neither, apparently, does time.

The house is brick, three stories, built for a family twice the size of the one that lived in it. There's a boiler in the basement older than me, cast iron, temperamental as an opera singer, feeding nine radiators—nine, I counted them once during a slow passage of tuning, one per room plus the hallway, like the house grew a radiator wherever it expected grief or company to arrive.

The radiators are how I know things.

Not gossip—I want to be clear about that, because Marta, my landlady, asked me once if I spied on them and I was so offended I didn't speak to her for two days, which she took as confirmation. It isn't spying. It's listening. There's a difference, though I'll admit the difference is mostly a matter of what you do afterward, and I've never done anything afterward, which is maybe why I get to call it listening.

A house with steam heat talks. The pipes knock when the pressure shifts; the valves hiss; the radiators tick as the iron expands, each one at its own tempo depending on its distance from the boiler and the thickness of its walls. Auguste Verrone taught me this, back when he was alive and I was nineteen and knew nothing except that pianos were made of felt and wood and wire and were therefore, technically, furniture. He put his palm flat against the hallway radiator on my first visit and said, *Listen, Edie. The house is keeping time.* And it was. It always has been. Every third Thursday I come up the walk and before I even touch the piano I stand in the hall with my coat still on and let the house tell me what kind of day it thinks it's having.

Then I go to work.

* * *

Here is what the radiators told me over fifteen years, catalogued the way I catalogue everything, in the mental ledger I keep the way other people keep diaries:

In March of my second year, the upstairs front radiator—the one in the bedroom that used to be Auguste's study—developed a knock with a doubled beat, irregular, arrhythmic, like someone tapping two fingers instead of one. I mentioned it to Mrs. Verrone, Léonie, who thanked me and called a plumber, and three weeks later Coral stopped coming down for breakfast and started coming down for lunch, and by June there was a bassinet in the hall and a second small bed in the room above the music room, and the doubled knock turned out to be nothing, just air in the line, but I have never entirely believed in coincidence where pipes are concerned. Houses anticipate. That's what nobody tells you about houses.

In November of my fourth year, the dining room radiator screamed—a high thin whistle, sustained, the sound of a valve failing—and while Mr. Okafor the plumber wrestled with it in the basement, the whole house filled with voices raised not quite to shouting but close, Auguste's voice climbing that particular staircase of pitch that means a man is losing an argument about money, and another man's voice I didn't recognize, smooth, unhurried, the voice of someone who wins arguments about money by refusing to climb any stairs at all. Theo. I didn't know the name yet. I learned it later, from the way Coral said *Uncle Theo* the way children say the names of weather systems.

The whistle got fixed. The argument, as far as I could hear through the floors for the next several years, did not.

In February of my ninth year, all nine radiators went silent at once—not off, they were still hot, I checked, I put my hand on every single one like a doctor taking pulses—but silent, no ticking, no expansion creak, as if the house itself were holding its breath. I sat on the piano bench with my tuning hammer in my lap for forty minutes waiting for the sound to come back. It came back around dusk, all together, a soft chorus of ticks like rain starting. Later I learned that Léonie had died that afternoon, in the hospital, and that Auguste had been sitting alone in the music room since morning, and I have thought many times since about whether a house can grieve by proxy, whether the iron simply matched him, the way a piano matches the hands that play it. I don't believe in ghosts. I believe in resonance. It amounts to nearly the same thing and requires less explaining at parties.

And in October of my eleventh year, the hallway radiator began ticking in triplets. Regular, patient, deliberate triplets—tick-tick-tick, pause, tick-tick-tick—which is not a pattern steam makes. Steam stumbles. Triplets are chosen. I stood in that hallway for a long time, longer than professional dignity allows, and finally went up to the landing, and found Coral, age seven, sitting on the floor with a wooden spoon, tapping the base of the radiator in threes, watching the dust jump off the top of the valve.

"You're supposed to be quiet," she said, without looking up. "Papa's writing."

"Then we'll both be quiet," I said, and sat down on the step across from her, and for twenty minutes we tapped nothing and heard everything, and it was the closest I ever came, in those years, to being inside the family rather than adjacent to it.

She pressed one key of the piano for me that day—one key, middle C, because I told her every piano has one note that's more honest than the others and she wanted to meet it. She pressed it and looked at me like I'd shown her a door. I left early that Thursday. I remember that. I remember leaving early and walking down the hill faster than I needed to, because something in my chest had started to itch, and I didn't yet have a word for it.

* * *

The word, if you're wondering, arrived years later, and it wasn't a word so much as a realization, which is that invisibility is comfortable right up until you notice you chose it, and after that it's just a smaller kind of loneliness with better lighting.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. This is the year of the tuning fork, and I should stay in it.

This most recent Thursday—it was the third Thursday, it's always the third Thursday, Auguste set the schedule himself in my first month and I have never once deviated, which tells you something about me that I'd rather show than say—I came up the hill at ten in the morning as usual, and the house sounded wrong before I even reached the porch.

Wrong how? Grief has a pitch. I've already said that, or I will say it later, but let me say it here too because it matters: when a house loses someone, the radiators don't tick the same way. They tick shorter. Clipped. As if the iron has less to say and says it quickly to avoid saying more. The Verrone house has been ticking short for eight months now, since the funeral, since the winter Auguste didn't survive, and every third Thursday I climb the stairs to the music room and tune a piano that hasn't been played—truly played—in all that time, and the silence under my hands has a texture I could describe to you the way a blind person describes a face.

Nobody answered the door. That was new. For fifteen years someone answered—Léonie, then Auguste, then Coral, then, briefly, both parents in a doorway together looking like a photograph of themselves. Today the bell rang into emptiness and came back to me flattened, the way sound comes back in an unfurnished room.

I let myself in with the key I keep on the same ring as my own, because Auguste gave it to me in my second year and said, *A tuner who waits to be let in tunes a polite piano,* and I have never known exactly what he meant and have never stopped thinking about it.

The hall smelled of cold dust and, faintly, of tea gone cold in a pot somewhere upstairs. Nine radiators, all ticking short. I stood in the hall with my coat on, doing my listening, and the house told me what kind of day it was having: an empty one. A kept one. A house that was being maintained rather than lived in, which is a different animal entirely, the way a museum is a different animal from a home.

I hung my coat on the hook that has held my coat for fifteen years—the hook is mine in the way that nothing else in that house has ever officially been mine—and I climbed to the music room.

The Bösendorfer sat under the tall window with its lid closed. Dust on the lid, fine as flour. I opened it and the smell came up out of the soundboard, spruce and old glue and the ghost of rosin, and I pressed middle C, the honest note, and it was a quarter-tone flat and sagging, the way a voice sags when the person using it has given up on being heard.

"All right," I told it. "I'm here."

I talk to pianos. Marta would tell you this proves I shouldn't live alone. Pianos don't answer, which is precisely why I talk to them.

I worked. A-440 on the fork, then the temperament octave, then outward, string by string, mute strip in and out, hammer and star head, the little felt wedges going between the courses like shims in a conversation. It takes two hours if the piano hasn't been abused and four if it has, and this one hadn't been abused, only neglected, which is worse, because abuse at least means someone was touching it.

Somewhere in the second hour, in the middle of tuning the tenor break—that stretch of strings where the piano stops sounding like silverware and starts sounding like weather—the hallway radiator changed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only honesty I'm reliably good at. The radiators had been ticking short all morning, clipped, grieving. At 11:40 or thereabouts, the hallway radiator—the one Coral tapped in triplets when she was seven—went quiet. Fully quiet. Then it started again, and the new rhythm was not short, and not triplet, and not anything steam makes.

It was a rhythm. Deliberate. Patient. Four beats, rest, four beats, rest. Like someone counting. Like someone practicing.

I sat with my tuning hammer raised over a pin block and did not move for a long time. The house has spoken to me for fifteen years and I have translated it faithfully into my private ledger—arguments, births, a death, a child with a wooden spoon—and I have never once written anything down, never once said anything aloud, never once done anything afterward. Listening without consequence. Invisibility as a discipline.

But this was different. This was not the house talking about its people. This was the house talking *to* me, or near enough that the distinction stopped mattering, because four beats and a rest is a message, and messages have senders, and senders are people, and people in that house were supposed to be finished.

I finished the tuning. Of course I finished the tuning. I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a piano half-done; it's the one form of completion available to me. I closed the lid, wiped the dust from the fallboard with the chamois I keep for that purpose, and stood in the music room doorway looking down the length of the hall toward the front door, and the radiator ticked its four beats, and rested, and ticked them again.

Coral's car wasn't outside. I'd noticed that on the way up, the absence of the little grey hatchback in its usual spot, and I'd thought nothing of it, because adults leave houses, that's what they're for.

But the rhythm behind the wall was not the rhythm of an empty house. And it was not, I realized as I stood there with my kit bag in my hand, the rhythm of grief either.

Grief ticks short. This ticked long. This ticked like rehearsal.

I let myself out, locked the door, and stood on the porch a moment in the cold, and below me at the bottom of the hill the 42 came grinding around the curve, four minutes late, ringing its bell at the corner out of habit or irony. My bus. My route. Eleven minutes back to Delancey and the life I keep there, which is small and clean and entirely unsupervised.

I went down the hill. I got on the bus. I sat in the third row, where I always sit, and watched the Verrone house shrink behind us, and the itch under my sternum—the one from the year Coral met middle C, the one I have managed for fifteen years with routine and craft and the excellent anesthesia of other people's pianos—itched.

And underneath the diesel rattle and the schoolchildren and the woman behind me narrating her grocery list into her phone, I could still feel it, the way you feel a wrong note in your teeth long after the chord has ended:

Four beats. Rest. Four beats.

Somebody in that house was practicing something. And for the first time in fifteen years, I wanted—actually wanted, with the specific hunger of a woman who has trained herself out of wanting—to know what.
