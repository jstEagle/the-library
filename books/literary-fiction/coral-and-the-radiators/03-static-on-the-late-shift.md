## 3. Static on the Late Shift

Insomnia, in my experience, is not an absence of sleep. It is a different room you live in at night, with its own furniture and its own rules. I have kept that room for years now—the same narrow bed, the same ceiling stain shaped like a hand reaching for something just out of frame—and over time I furnished it with small rituals. Tea gone cold on the sill. The streetlight through the blinds laying its ladder of amber across the floor. And the radio.

The radio was Marta's idea, actually. Years ago she caught me in the kitchen at three in the morning, standing barefoot with a spoon in my hand and no memory of why, and she said, *You need a voice in the house besides your own.* So she gave me her old transistor set, the one with the dial worn smooth from her late husband's thumb, and told me to keep it low. Not off. Low. There's a difference, she said. Off is silence. Low is company.

So that's what I did. Every night, the late shift of the airwaves: jazz stations fading in and out as their signals wandered, farm reports from counties I'd never see, the long-haul trucker shows where lonely men called in to describe the weather on Route 9. I never listened the way you listen to music. I listened the way a tuner listens—to the texture underneath. The hiss between stations. The way a voice sits in a room. Fifteen years of tuning pianos will do that to you. You stop hearing notes and start hearing the space they're trying to fill.

The night it happened was a Tuesday in October, though I only learned the day later, when I went back through my own calendar looking for evidence, the way you check a receipt after you've been robbed.

I remember the cold first. The radiator in my building had gone quiet around midnight—that particular silence pipes make when the boiler downstairs gives up for the night, a silence with weight to it, like a held breath. I'd been awake since two, watching the ceiling-hand reach, and somewhere past three I reached for the radio out of habit more than hope.

Static. The dial found nothing for a long moment—just that gray wash, the sound of the atmosphere itself talking to itself. Then, drifting up from the bottom of the band, a voice.

"—continuing our midnight series, *Unfinished Voices*, tonight featuring a premiere recording of a work recovered from private papers. The composer describes it as a lullaby written for his daughter. Here is 'Coral's Sleep,' by Theo Verrone."

I want to tell you that I sat up. That my heart did something dramatic. But the truth is stranger and worse: for the first several seconds, nothing happened at all. I lay there perfectly still, the way you do when a sound enters a room that doesn't belong to any sound you know, and some animal part of you decides that stillness is safety.

Then the piano started.

It was a simple figure. Four notes descending, then a fifth that refused to resolve—hung there, turning in the air like a leaf that can't decide which way to fall. Any listener would have heard a pretty melody, maybe a melancholy one. A competent little nocturne, the kind of thing conservatory students write for their mothers.

But I wasn't any listener.

I knew those four notes the way you know the sound of your own front door closing behind you. I had heard them played badly on a piano with a dead G in the middle register, in a drafty music room that smelled of rosin and pipe smoke and the particular dust of old sheet music. I had heard them hummed, half-embarrassed, by a man standing at a window with his back to me, watching rain come down on a street where a girl was walking home from school. *For Coral,* he'd said, without turning around. *Someday I'll finish it. Someday I'll get it right enough to give her.*

Auguste Verrone's lullaby. Unfinished. Unrecorded. He had refused every offer to commit it to paper in full, refused the university archivists who came sniffing after his manuscripts, refused even to play it complete for anyone—including, in the end, me. It existed, he said, only in the house. Only in the rooms where Coral had grown up. A song, he told me once, is not an object; it's a place, and places can't be stolen, only left.

And now it was coming out of a radio the size of a loaf of bread, in a performance—clean, recorded, professionally mic'd—with his name attached to it. His brother's name. Theo Verrone.

The announcer came back after, warm and unhurried, the voice of a man reading someone else's obituary: "Verrone, who resides locally, tells us the piece dates from his early years and was dedicated to family. A full album is expected next spring."

*His early years.* Auguste wrote that lullaby the winter Coral turned nine. I know because I tuned the piano the week he started it, and the week he abandoned it, and every week between, and I watched the manuscript grow and stall and grow again on the stand like a tide chart. Theo was living in Rotterdam that year. Theo sent postcards. Theo appeared twice, at Christmas and at a funeral, wearing a coat too good for the neighborhood and a smile calibrated to it.

I got up. I don't remember deciding to. My body simply stood, the way a struck fork stands and sings, and crossed the cold floor, and turned the radio up—not loud, but past *low*, into the territory Marta had warned me about. The lullaby was ending. The last phrase circled back to that unresolved fifth, and then—

And then it resolved. Cleanly. Confidently. A final chord laid down like a signature.

Auguste never resolved it. He said the ending would come to him when Coral slept through a whole night without calling for her mother, and that night hadn't come before—before everything. Before the hospital. Before the funeral I stood at the edge of, in the back, invisible, holding my tuning case like a shield. The lullaby had ended nowhere, suspended, forever falling. That was the truth of it. That was the shape of his grief and his love, and I had carried that unresolved fifth inside me for fifteen years the way you carry a stone in your shoe—so long you forget it isn't part of your foot.

Someone had finished it. Someone had recorded it. Someone had given it away.

I stood in my narrow kitchen in the blue hour before dawn, radio hissing, and felt the floor of my life tilt—not dramatically, not all at once, but with the slow sick certainty of a piano going flat, one string at a time, until the whole instrument is wrong and only the tuner knows.

Because here is the thing I could not escape, standing there with the cold coming up through the floorboards:

*I had known.*

Not about the recording. Not about the theft, if it was theft—I didn't yet have the word for it, and wouldn't for weeks. But I had known the lullaby existed. I had known it was unfinished. I had known Auguste wanted, near the end, near that last terrible winter, to finish it and record it properly, and I had said no. Or worse than no—I had said nothing. I had done what I always did in that house, what I had made an art and a livelihood and finally a personality out of doing: I had stayed quiet. I had tuned the strings and kept my hands busy and let the silence do my talking for me, because silence was safe, because silence was how a nineteen-year-old apprentice with no family and no claim survived in a house that worshipped a genius, because silence was—

Because silence was a choice. And I had told myself for fifteen years that it was mine alone. That invisibility was something that happened to me, a condition, a weather. Standing there, I understood for the first time that it might be something else entirely. Something practiced. Something shared.

Someone else in that story had also spent years not saying the true thing. Someone else had let the lullaby stay buried, or unfinished, or unclaimed—and now, apparently, had decided to say it in the worst possible way: loudly, publicly, under the wrong name.

Or—my mind offered this in the gray light, cruel and precise—maybe there was no one else. Maybe Theo really had found papers. Maybe Auguste, in some hour I never witnessed, had handed his brother the fragment the way you hand off a burden you're tired of carrying. Maybe the theft had no thief but time.

But I didn't believe it. Tuners learn to trust the ear over the explanation, and my ear told me the ending on that broadcast was wrong—not musically wrong; technically it was fine—but wrong the way a forged signature is wrong. All the letters correct, none of the pressure right. Whoever finished that lullaby had studied the fragment. Had practiced it. Had gotten close enough to it to imitate the handwriting of it.

Close enough to have been in the house.

I turned the radio off. Off, not low. The silence that followed had a different pitch than the silence before—a thinner one, an accused one. Outside, the sky was doing its slow dishonest brightening, and down in the basement the boiler coughed itself awake, and the radiators began their morning clatter, ticking out the only honest account of the night I would get.

I thought about Coral. She'd be thirty now. I hadn't seen her since she was nineteen and leaving for school, hauling a suitcase down those front steps while I knelt in the parlor pretending to work, both of us silent, both of us experts. Did she listen to late radio? Would she hear it? Would she recognize four descending notes and an ending that shouldn't exist? Would she feel what I felt—that specific vertigo of hearing a private place described aloud by a stranger?

A lullaby is not an object, Auguste used to say. It's a place.

And someone had just put that place on the open market.

I washed my cup. I dressed for work. I took the 42 bus across town to tune a spinet for a widow who talked the entire hour about her son in Phoenix, and I nodded in the right places, and my hands did their flawless, invisible work, and all the while a single question ticked beneath my ribs like a valve about to fail:

If the lullaby was out in the world now—if the secret I'd helped keep was already sold—then what exactly was I still keeping?

And who, in that locked house on Delancey Street, had decided fifteen years of silence was finally worth breaking—without saying a word to me?

The answer, I understood even then, lived behind a door I would have to knock on. And the bus route that would take me there was scheduled, I would soon learn, to disappear.
