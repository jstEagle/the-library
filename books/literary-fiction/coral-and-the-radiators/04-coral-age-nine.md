## 4. Coral, Age Nine

The tuner came on Thursdays, and Thursdays were the only days the house made sense.

I knew this before I knew how to read a clock. I knew it in my teeth, the way you know rain coming, because Thursday had a sound: the front door's particular groan, then the case of tools set down on the hall floor — not dropped, *set*, always set, like she was laying down something alive and didn't want to startle it — and then the long quiet of the music room where my father's piano lived under her hands.

Her name was Edie. Edmund Delacroix, which was a boy's name, which delighted me beyond reason when I was seven and still felt transgressive at nine, though I never said so out loud. She was young then — nineteen, twenty, though to me she might as well have been forty, because she was an adult and adults were all one weather system. But she was a different kind of adult. She was the kind who knelt.

That was the first thing. Everyone else in my life stood over me. My father bent down sometimes, when he remembered, but bending wasn't kneeling; bending was a favor he did you from his height. Edie knelt. She knelt to look under the piano, she knelt to open the lower panels, she knelt once in the hallway for a full minute to listen to the radiator by the coat closet, her ear almost touching the iron, and when I asked her what it said she looked up at me with those gray eyes and said, "It says somebody ran the hot water too fast and now it's complaining about it."

"What does it complain about the rest of the time?"

"Everything," she said. "Same as everybody."

I decided on the spot that she was the only person in the house who told the truth.

* * *

My father's piano was a Bösendorfer, black as a closed eye, and it occupied the music room the way a bear occupies a cave — everything else arranged itself around its moods. Papa tuned it himself sometimes, or thought he did, turning pins with a wrench and humming, and afterward the piano would sulk for days, certain notes gone sour at the top like milk left out. Then Thursday would come, and Edie would sit on the bench with her back very straight and her tuning fork struck against her knee, held to her ear first — always to her ear first, listening to the note inside herself before asking the piano to agree — and the piano would come home.

I watched from the doorway. This was allowed. Watching was free in our house; speaking cost extra.

She worked in a sequence I came to know like a psalm. The felt strips across the strings. The mute wedges, little rubber tongues. The hammer she used on the pins, small taps, smaller than you'd think — I expected violence and got instead this endless patient persuasion, quarter-turns, eighth-turns, the piano argued with and coaxed and finally, grudgingly, agreeing. And between every few notes she'd play a chord, soft, testing, and tilt her head, and the chord would hang in the room like a bird deciding whether to land.

Once I asked her how she could hear whether it was right. "Doesn't it just sound right or wrong?"

"Right is a place," she said. "You have to know where it is before you can tell if you've arrived."

I thought about that for years. I'm thinking about it now.

* * *

The key was middle C. That was our ceremony, ours alone, invented without either of us ever announcing it.

The first time, I must have been six. She'd finished, packed her tools, and I was standing in the doorway doing my usual statue impression, and she looked at me — really looked, the way nobody looked at children, like I might contain information — and she slid sideways off the bench and patted the wood beside her.

"One key," she said. "Pick any one."

I picked middle C because it was in the middle and I was a literal child. I pressed it and the note rose up through my finger bones into my arm and I swear to God into my chest, and Edie watched my face while it happened, not the piano, my face, and whatever she saw there made her nod like something had been confirmed.

"Good choice," she said.

After that it was law. Every Thursday, after the last pin was settled and the last chord had landed where it belonged, she'd move aside and I'd get my one key. Sometimes I chose middle C. Sometimes, feeling bold, I chose the A above it, or once — once — the E flat below, dark and strange, and she raised her eyebrows and said, "Listen to you," and I glowed for three days.

One key. Never two. I understood even then that the limit was the gift. If she'd let me play whole pieces I'd have been a child being tolerated. One key meant I was being trusted with something that mattered, and trust, in that house, was rarer than money.

Because money there was, or the shape of it. The Verrone house was big and warm and full of good furniture going slightly shabby in the way of furniture no one wants to admit they can't replace. My father composed — everyone said *my father the composer* the way they'd say *my father the doctor* — and composition, I gathered from overheard kitchen talk between Mama and Mrs. Okafor who cleaned, paid like poetry and ate like plumbing. There were years we were rich and years we were proud, and the difference between them was invisible if you were nine, except that some Thursdays the tuner didn't come, and those weeks the piano went sour and so did the light in the house.

But Edie came even in the thin years. I found out much later she took payment in leftovers sometimes, in my mother's casseroles sent home wrapped in foil. At nine I only knew that Thursday was fixed as scripture, and that when the door groaned and the tools were set down gently in the hall, the whole house exhaled.

* * *

She taught me things without teaching me, which is the only way teaching works on a child.

She taught me that buildings have voices. "Put your hand here," she'd say, pressing my palm flat against the radiator in the music room, and I'd feel the tick-tick-tick of expanding iron, irregular, arrhythmic, and she'd say, "Now wait." And we'd wait, both of us crouched by the window like conspirators, until the ticking organized itself into something steadier, and she'd say, "There. It's done arguing. Now it's thinking."

She taught me that silence isn't empty. When my parents fought — and they fought in the low register, doors closed, words worn smooth from use — Edie was there one such Thursday, and she didn't pretend not to hear, and she didn't comment either. She just played a chord, very softly, and let it die, and played another, and after ten minutes of this the voices upstairs stopped, not resolved, just stopped, like the chords had absorbed them. I asked her if she'd done that on purpose. She said, "A room will hold a fight for you if you ask it nicely."

She taught me that my father was frightened. Not by anything he said — he was all warmth and big gestures, lifting me onto his shoulders, calling me *piccola stella*, playing me fragments of things and watching my face the way Edie watched it, hunting for confirmation. But I noticed that when Edie opened the piano and he drifted in to stand behind her, he held his breath. He held his breath while she tested his instrument, this man who wrote symphonies, waiting to see if she'd find something wrong. And she never humiliated him. Whatever she found wrong, she fixed quietly and mentioned lightly, or didn't mention at all. I loved him more for his fear and loved her more for her mercy, though I couldn't have named either at nine. I just filed them, the way children file everything, in the enormous unsorted drawer of the heart.

* * *

The autumn she left, I was nine, and it happened the way weather happens — suddenly, completely, with no negotiation.

It was October. The maples on our street had gone the color of the piano's inner workings, brass and copper, and I remember that specifically because I'd been allowed, that day, to look *inside* the piano while she worked — a promotion, unprecedented, my head practically in the cabinet while she pointed out the hammers, the dampers, the golden forest of strings. "This is where it lives," she said. "The sound. Everything else is just the body around it."

I was so happy that day. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept: I was happy, and I had no idea.

The next Thursday the door didn't groan. The tools weren't set down in the hall. I waited in the music room through the morning, through lunch, through the long gray afternoon, and the piano sat there with its lid closed like a face turned away, and nobody came.

I asked Mama. She was peeling apples at the sink and she said, without turning around, "The tuner won't be coming anymore, sweetheart."

"Why?"

"The piano doesn't need it as often now."

That was a lie. Even at nine I knew it was a lie, because the piano needed it exactly as often as it had always needed it, which was every third Thursday, forever, amen. I asked Papa and he gave me a different lie, warmer, worse: "We're trying someone new, piccola. A man from the conservatory. Very good." And a man from the conservatory did come, eventually, a large competent person who smelled of cigarettes and tuned the piano correctly, technically, the way a substitute teacher takes attendance, and the piano sounded fine afterward and I hated it, hated the fine correct sound, because it wasn't arrived anywhere, it was just standing in the right place without knowing why.

Nobody would say where Edie had gone. Nobody would say why. It was as if she'd been erased by committee — each parent holding a piece of the eraser, neither willing to do the whole job. I got fragments. From Mrs. Okafor, later, in the kitchen, thinking I wasn't listening, which I always was: *"left sudden, poor girl, no notice, and after all these years"* — and something else, half-heard, that I didn't understand then and understand entirely now: *"and him not even saying goodbye to her, after everything."*

Him. My father. After everything.

I sat with that fragment for a long time, the way you sit with a puzzle piece that clearly belongs somewhere but whose picture you can't yet see. What everything? What had passed between the composer and the tuner that made a goodbye impossible? Children assume the world runs on simple machinery: people leave because they're called away, because they're sick, because they die. The possibility that a person could leave because of something unsaid — that a whole human being could be subtracted from your Thursdays by a conversation that never happened — that was beyond my arithmetic at nine.

So I did what I could. I kept the key.

Not a real key — the privilege. I kept middle C. After the conservatory man left each time, I'd lift the fallboard myself, unauthorized, and press it, once, and listen to whether the note landed where Edie had always made it land. For a few weeks it did; her work holds, that's the thing about her work, it holds like masonry. Then it began to drift, a hair flat, then flatter, and I pressed it anyway, every week, pressing a note that was slowly leaving, the way you call a phone number you know has been disconnected just to hear it not connect.

Mama caught me once, in November, frost ferned on the windows. She stood in the doorway with laundry on her hip and watched me press the key and watch the hammer rise and fall, and something crossed her face — I see it now, I couldn't read it then — something like guilt wearing the mask of annoyance.

"Coral. Close the piano."

"I'm just—"

"Close it. It's not a toy."

It wasn't a toy. That was the entire point, the whole theology of the one key, and she of all people should have known it, and she stood there not knowing it, or knowing it and refusing it, and I closed the fallboard because I was nine and obedience was the only currency I had, and the piano shut its eye again.

* * *

Here is what I know now, thirty years and one lifetime later, holding it up to the light the way Edie held her tuning fork to her ear before asking the world to agree:

She mattered. Not the way the piano mattered, not furniture-of-the-soul, useful and invisible. She mattered the way a season matters. She was the reason Thursdays had a shape, and when she went, the shape went with her, and no one in that house ever said her name again — I checked, over the years, casually, the way you check a wound — no one, not once, not even my father, especially my father, who kept her absence the way other men keep letters, hidden and reread in the dark.

And here is what I suspected even at nine, in the wordless way children suspect things, and what I will know for certain soon enough, when a woman with gray eyes and a case of tools comes up my walk under a pretense so thin we'll both pretend not to see through it:

People who leave abruptly are not finished. They are interrupted. And the house keeps the rest of the sentence.

I heard it first through the radiator, years later — a rhythm behind the wall of the locked room, deliberate, recent, alive — but that night in October when I was nine, all I had was a disconnected note and a question I carried out of childhood and into the marrow of me:

What did my father say to her — or fail to say — that made a goodbye impossible?

I've spent my whole life since, I realize now, composing the answer.
