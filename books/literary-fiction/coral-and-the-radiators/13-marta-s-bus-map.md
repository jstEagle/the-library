## 13. Marta's Bus Map

Marta kept her kitchen table clear the way other people kept their consciences—nothing on it that couldn't be moved in a hurry. So when I came down the stairs at eleven at night carrying a shoebox of old transit maps and my whole unwritten life, she didn't ask questions. She just took the kettle off its hook and set it on the burner like she'd been waiting thirty years for me to finally bring something heavy into her kitchen.

"I need the table," I said.

"You have the table." She pulled two chairs out with her foot. "You want tea or you want the good tea?"

"The good tea."

"Then this is serious." She reached up to the cabinet above the fridge where she hid the tin her sister sent from Lisbon, the one she only opened when somebody died or got married. "Which is it?"

"Neither. Both. I don't know yet."

She measured leaves into the pot with the ceremony of a woman defusing something. I spread the maps.

They were beautiful, in the way dead things are beautiful. The 42 route as it had been in 1987, when it still ran all the way down Corvair Street past the tannery. The 1994 revision, after they'd trimmed the loop around Delancey Park. The one from 2009 with the little printed notice about seasonal schedule adjustments that had never un-adjusted. And the newest one, the one I'd taken from the depot rack three days ago, with the reroute notice printed in gray so small you'd think the city was embarrassed by it: *Effective next month, Route 42 will be discontinued between Halden Avenue and the river terminus.*

My entire geography. Every street I'd ever been someone in, reduced to a gray sentence.

Marta set down the tea and studied the maps over her glasses without touching them. She was seventy-one and had hands that knew better than to reach for things that weren't offered.

"So," she said. "The house."

I looked up. "What house?"

"The one you come back from smelling like radiator dust and old grief, every third Thursday for fifteen years. The one you've never once invited me to see you walk toward." She sat down across from me. "Edie. You have the table. Use it."

And here is the thing nobody tells you about silence: it isn't empty. It's load-bearing. Fifteen years of things unsaid had gotten stacked inside me like cordwood, and I had always assumed that if I ever started pulling logs out, the whole structure would come down on my head. I had built a life the way Auguste built his lullaby—one hand hovering over the keys, never landing.

But the floor was already dropping. It had dropped the night the radio played Coral's lullaby and credited a living man. It dropped again behind the locked music room wall, where grief had a rhythm too deliberate to be mourning. The structure was coming down either way. The only question left was whether I'd be standing under it when it fell.

So I told Marta everything.

I told her about nineteen, about arriving at the Verrone door with a tuning fork and no luggage worth mentioning, about Auguste hearing me hum his unpublished étude through an open window before he ever heard my name. I told her about learning to hear buildings—the way a house keeps time, how radiators are just pipes with opinions, how every family has a key signature. I told her about Coral at nine, pressing the middle C with one finger like it was a doorbell to somewhere better, and how I let her press it exactly once per visit because Auguste said a child should learn that some things are rationed.

I told her about the night I overheard what I was never meant to hear. I made myself say it plainly: Theo's voice through the study door, low and reasonable, talking about arrangements, about who a legacy really belonged to, and Auguste's voice answering with a tiredness I could feel through the wood—and me standing on the stairs with my tool case, choosing the stairs, choosing down, choosing out into the cold with my ears full of something I would carry for twenty years and never once set down.

"He asked me," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize. "At the end. His last winter. He asked me to help him finish the lullaby and record it. He said—" I stopped. Started again. "He said, *you're the only one in this house who listens, Edie.* And I said no. I said it wasn't my place. I said it like it was humility."

Marta poured the second cup. Her face gave away nothing, which is why I could keep going.

"It wasn't humility. It was the same move I'd been making since the stairs. Refusing to be a person in that story. And he died with it unrecorded, and I told myself that was grief, that was fate, that was any word except *mine*."

"And now?" Marta said.

"Now the song is on the radio. Played by someone alive. And there's a piano being practiced in a locked room in that house, badly, recently, by someone who doesn't want to be heard—which is a language I happen to be fluent in." I flattened the newest map with both palms. "And in four weeks this route stops existing, and whatever window I have to walk through that door as more than the woman who tunes the piano closes with it."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below us, our building's own radiators knocked twice and settled, and I swear I understood them for once: that's the sound of heat leaving a system that held it too long.

Marta drank her tea. Then she stood, went to the dresser in the hall—the one she'd never let me help move, not once in nine years of tenancy—and came back with a photograph.

It was a young woman in a nurse's uniform, standing outside a hospital I recognized from the map, the one the 42 used to pass before the trim of '94. Dark hair, squinting into sun, one hand raised mid-wave at whoever held the camera.

"My sister, Luzia," Marta said. "Before Lisbon. Before the tin." She set it on the table among my dead routes. "In 1971 we worked the same ward. There was a doctor. Married, but the marriage was a radiator that clanked and never warmed anything—you know the type, you probably tuned his piano." I almost smiled. "He used to stay late on my shifts. We talked about everything except the thing. For six years, Edie. Six years of almost. And then he transferred cities, and I told myself it was better this way, cleaner, and I booked a boat, and I let the whole harbor go quiet."

"What happened to him?"

"I don't know. That's the price of the ticket." She turned the photo so it faced me. "Luzia knows. She found out years later, from a colleague. He asked about me, near the end of his career. Asked whether the Portuguese nurse with the laugh had ever said anything about him. And do you know what I had said? Nothing. My whole answer, across an ocean and thirty years, was nothing."

The clock in the hall did its work. I let it.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked, though I already knew, the way you know a chord before you hear it.

"Because you're rehearsing." She tapped the shoebox. "All these maps. All this telling-me. You're not confessing, girl. You're running lines. You're figuring out how the sentence goes so that when you're standing in front of the person it belongs to, it comes out right." She sat back down. "Good. Rehearse with me. I'm cheap and I know the acoustics."

I laughed—a real one, surprised out of me—and then, because the room was warm and the tea was the good kind and some doors, once unlocked, swing easy, I kept going.

I told her about Coral answering the door at thirty, formal and urgent, hiring me for a restoration neither of us believed was about pianos. About tea circling like cats. About the way Coral mentioned the radio premiere with a casualness so carefully built it might as well have been scaffolding. About Theo's charm, which I described accurately: the smile of a man handling furniture he intends to sell. About the rhythm behind the locked wall—not grief, not memory, but practice. Rosin dust fresh enough to date.

"And you think it's her," Marta said. "The daughter. Playing her father's song in a locked room."

"I think it's someone who writes the way I talk," I said. "With the door shut."

Marta considered that. Outside, the last bus of the night sighed past at the corner—still the 42, still running the old loop, for five more weeks. Its headlights swept the ceiling through the curtain gap, a slow white tide, and for a second the whole kitchen was lit like a stage.

"When I first came to this building," Marta said, "the landlady before me told me: the pipes will tell you who's home. I thought she was being poetic. She wasn't. She was giving me instructions." She looked at my maps, at the gray print, at the shoebox. "You listen to houses. Fine. But listen to me: a house can tell you who's home. It cannot tell you who stays. That part somebody has to say out loud."

*Somebody has to say it out loud.* I wrote it in my head the way I'd write a marking in a score—*espress., non troppo*—because I knew I'd need it soon, standing in a hallway with a key I hadn't found yet under a valve I hadn't checked, saying a sentence I hadn't finished rehearsing.

We worked until two in the morning. Marta got out a pencil and drew the current route freehand from memory, correcting the city's own map, adding the stops that mattered and weren't printed: the bakery that closed, the hospital, the park, Corvair Street. "If they erase it," she said, "then we keep the copy." It was the first time I'd heard her say *we* about anything bigger than the boiler.

At the bottom of the newest map, in the margin, she wrote one line and boxed it:

**Halden Ave → river terminus: last service, [date].**

Underneath, without asking permission, she added:

**Edie goes first.**

I looked at it a long time.

"There's one more thing," I said. "Auguste hid things. Always in the boiler room. Valves, panels, behind the third one—I don't know why I'm sure, but I am. If there's a message in that house with anyone's name on it, it'll be taped inside valve three, next to a furnace older than both of us."

Marta capped the pencil. "Then tomorrow you take the 42," she said, "and you go put your hand on that valve. And Edie—" she waited until I looked up, "—when you find whatever he left you, you don't come back here to rehearse it. You go say it to the person it's for."

The radiators ticked once, all down the hall, like a count-in.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in my room above Marta's kitchen listening to the building breathe and doing what I'd done for fifteen years, cataloging sounds—but differently now, the way a musician reads a score she's about to perform. The pipes under my floor ran hot around one, which meant Marta was up, which meant she was sitting with her sister's photograph, keeping her own vigil, and I found that I loved her for it in a way that frightened me slightly, because loving people is the opposite of invisibility and I was badly out of practice.

At dawn I packed the tool case. Not the light kit I brought for routine tunings—the full case, the one with the borescope and the long picks, the case you bring when you intend to open something.

On my way down, Marta was at the stove, and she pressed a paper-wrapped bundle into my hands without comment. Inside: two of the almond biscuits she only made at Christmas, and a folded square of paper. On it, in her pencil, she had copied the boxed line from the map.

*Edie goes first.*

The 42 came around the corner at 6:40, headlights still cutting the blue dark, and I raised my hand like a girl in an old photograph, and it stopped for me.
