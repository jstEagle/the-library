## 19. The Night Before the Reroute

The city gave the 42 route a funeral and called it a service improvement.

There were flyers at the stops—glossy, municipal blue, a smiling bus rendered in clip art—and a notice taped inside the shelter on Delancey that had already begun to peel at one corner, curling like something trying to escape. *Effective Monday, Route 42 will be discontinued. Riders may use Routes 17 and 63 via transfer at Halloway Station.* A transfer. As if you could transfer a neighborhood. As if twenty-two minutes of rattling past the Verrone house could be exchanged for two buses and a walk through a part of town that had never once heard Auguste Verrone's piano.

I stood at the shelter for a long time reading that notice, though I'd read it three days ago when Marta spread her maps across the kitchen table and traced the old route with a blunt finger, naming every stop like a rosary. Delancey. Corvair. The foundry wall. Ashbury Terrace. The Verrone corner, which had never been called that on any map but had been called that in my head for fifteen years.

Tomorrow was the last night. Tonight was the last full day.

And tonight I was going to break into a transit depot.

Not break in, exactly. That's what I told myself on the walk to the garage where I kept the van, my breath coming out in white flags under the streetlights. Not breaking in if you have credentials. Not breaking in if the storage locker is technically abandoned property, unclaimed for fifteen years, scheduled for disposal in the great bureaucratic purge that follows any route cancellation. Not breaking in if the key fits, because keys are invitations, and Auguste Verrone taught me that a house—or a locker, or a life—tells you what it wants opened if you listen long enough.

But it was breaking in. I knew it was. My hands knew it; they wouldn't stop their small tremor against the steering wheel, the tremor of a string wound too tight, and I thought: this is what Coral has felt like for months. This is what it costs to stop being invisible. The price of a voice is always paid in advance, in shaking hands.

* * *

The depot sat on Marrow Street like a sleeping animal, all corrugated flank and dead windows, ringed by chain-link topped with wire that wasn't electrified anymore but liked to pretend. The night crew worked out of the east annex; the main bays closed at eight. I had learned all of this from a man named Osgood who ran the lost-and-found window back when I was nineteen and rode the 42 home from Auguste's lessons with my tuning fork in my coat pocket and my heart in my throat. Osgood was long retired, but his replacement—a heavyset woman named Doreen whose name tag I could read from six feet because she leaned into everything she did—was the same species of person: someone who had spent thirty years guarding other people's forgotten things and had developed, as a result, a weakness for anyone who came to claim them.

I wore my good coat. The one with the embroidered name patch from the Piano Technicians Guild, the one that made me look like what I was instead of what I felt. In my pocket: the brass key from valve three, warm from my hand, and the note folded beneath it so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth.

*For whoever hears the house finish my sentence.*

I still hadn't figured out how a sentence gets finished by a house. But I was starting to understand that Auguste had never meant the radiators. He'd meant me. He'd meant Coral. He'd meant whoever was stubborn enough to keep listening after the composer stopped speaking.

"Depot closes at eight," Doreen said through the window slot, not looking up from her crossword.

"I'm not here for the depot." I slid my guild card under the glass. "I'm here about a storage locker. Bay C, row four. It's been flagged for disposal with the route closure."

Her eyes came up. They were the eyes of a woman who had heard every story ever invented and believed perhaps nine of them. "That's transit authority property."

"It's a piano tuner's kit," I said. "Belonged to Auguste Verrone. He stored it there because he used to take the 42 home from the concert hall and he didn't trust the instruments to the cold." All true. Every word of it true, which is the best kind of lie there is—the kind that isn't one. "He died fifteen years ago. Nobody claimed the locker because nobody knew it existed until last week, when we found correspondence referencing it. I'm the estate's technician. I can show you the letter of authorization."

I could not show her the letter of authorization, because there was no letter of authorization. There was a photocopy of a page from Auguste's ledger that Coral and I had aged with tea and a hair dryer at her kitchen table at two in the morning, both of us giggling like arsonists, Coral saying *I can't believe we're doing this* and me saying *we're not doing anything illegal, we're doing paperwork,* and both of us knowing that the line between those things is drawn in pencil and smudges easily.

Doreen looked at the card. Looked at me. Looked at the clock on the wall behind her, which said 7:41.

"Bay C's got bad lighting," she said. "You'll want to be quick. And sign the log." She pushed a clipboard through the slot. "Purpose of visit."

I wrote: *Recovery of client property.*

Under purpose, in smaller letters, because my hand had gone honest without permission: *It took me long enough.*

* * *

Bay C smelled the way all depots smell—diesel and dust and the ghost of ten thousand commutes—and the lights buzzed at a frequency just wrong enough to set my teeth on edge, a half-step flat, and some traitor part of my brain wanted to tune them. Row four was against the far wall, under a pipe that dripped at intervals of roughly eleven seconds. I counted while I walked. Eleven seconds. The radiators of the Verrone house ticked at eleven seconds when the boiler was balanced right. I don't know if that meant anything. I've stopped believing things mean nothing.

Locker 4-19. The key went in stiff, then smooth, then turned with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: a steamer trunk, olive drab, military surplus, the kind Auguste bought everything in because he said the army knew how to build a box. On top of it, a canvas tool roll I recognized—I'd oiled that leather a hundred times—and beneath the roll, when I lifted it with both hands because my arms had gone strange and weightless:

The sketchbook.

I want to tell you I was calm. I was not calm. I sat down on the concrete floor of Bay C, next to a dripping pipe, in a pool of buzzing half-light, and I held Auguste Verrone's sketchbook against my chest the way you hold a child, or a wound, or the last thing left of somebody, and I cried the way I hadn't cried at his funeral—quietly, uselessly, with my whole body.

Because here is what I had refused to know for fifteen years: he had kept it. The sketchbook with the lullaby fragment in it, the melody he hummed to baby Coral in the kitchen while the radiators ticked, the eight bars he'd played for me one October evening and asked, in his careful accented voice, *Edie. You hear where it wants to go?* And I had said nothing. I had looked at the floor and said nothing, because saying yes would have meant I belonged in that room, and belonging terrified me more than poverty ever had. I had been raised to be furniture. Furniture doesn't answer. Furniture doesn't get evicted.

So he'd put the book in a trunk in a locker by the bus line, and he'd died with the lullaby unfinished, and I had spent fifteen years telling myself the silence was mine alone, my private guilt, my native language—and then a radio had played that melody at midnight and the whole architecture of my silence had come down at once, because the song was out in the world wearing another man's name, and grief, I learned, has a pitch, and so does betrayal, and they are only a semitone apart.

The book was swollen with damp at the edges but sound at the spine. His handwriting marched across the pages—themes, fragments, marginalia in three languages, little drawings of the house's boiler system annotated like a score (*valve 3 = E flat, always*). And there, two-thirds through, the lullaby. Eight bars in his hand. And below them, in fresher ink, in a hand I now knew as well as my own—

Coral's continuation. Sixteen bars. The sentence, finished.

I was still sitting on the floor when the lights changed.

They didn't go out. They shifted—that particular depot trick where the motion sensors give up on a bay and drop it to emergency dim, and the shadows stand up and take shape. One of the shapes had been standing by the roll-up door for some time. I knew that before I saw him clearly. I knew it by the cologne, expensive and impatient, the same scent that had drifted through the Verrone parlor last week attached to a man handling his brother's legacy like furniture.

"Hello, Edie," Theo Verrone said.

* * *

He came down the row slowly, hands in the pockets of a coat that cost more than my van, and he looked at the open locker, and the trunk, and the sketchbook in my lap, and I watched him do the arithmetic. He was good at arithmetic. He'd been doing it for months—royalties siphoned through a shell company, a listing quietly prepared for the house, a radio premiere brokered with paperwork that made him look like a steward and behave like a thief.

"Doreen said a technician," he said. "She didn't say which one." He crouched, unhurried, until we were at eye level, which was a courtesy and a threat at once. "Give me the book, Edie."

"No."

"No." He tasted the word. "You know what I admire about you? Fifteen years in that house and nobody remembers your face. You could've been the perfect witness. Instead you're the perfect accomplice—to what, exactly? To Coral's little fantasy?" He gestured at the sketchbook. "Eight bars. That's all Auguste wrote. Eight bars of a cradle song he never finished because he was dying and he knew it. Everything after that is Coral's invention. Which means the copyright is Coral's. Which means—" he smiled, and it was almost kind, which made it worse— "the fraud is hers, not mine. I registered a reconstruction. She wrote a forgery. There's a difference, legally. Ask any lawyer."

"The difference," I said, "is that she finished it. And you stole it."

"I *administered* it." He stood, tired of crouching, tired of the floor, tired of me. "Auguste gave me the lullaby before he died. Told me to see it into the world. I have witnesses."

"You have lawyers."

"I have results." He turned toward the bay door, then back, and something in his face loosened—not kindness, but the relief of a man dropping a mask he'd worn too long. "Here's what happens now. You put the book back in the locker. The disposal crew crushes it Thursday. Coral signs the settlement papers tomorrow—she will, because she's her father's daughter and her father's daughter avoids conflict the way you avoid eye contact—and the house sells before the reroute guts the value, and everyone walks away richer except the people who deserve it. Or." He let it hang.

"Or?"

"Or you explain to Coral why you've known since the broadcast that the melody was real. Why you sat in her kitchen drinking her tea while she lied to you about losing a key she never lost, and you lied right back about hearing a radio play a song only three people alive ever heard. Two silences, Edie. Very touching. But silences stack. Hers on top of yours on top of his. Eventually the whole house comes down." He checked his watch. "Last bus is at 11:40. I'd make a decision."

And there it was. The thing he'd come to do. Not to take the book—he couldn't, not without a scene, and Theo Verrone did not do scenes. He'd come to remind me of my nature. To hand me back my invisibility like a coat check ticket and wait for me to claim it, because in nineteen years of knowing our family—no. Fifteen years of knowing his family, he had never once seen me speak.

He was right about everything. That was the terrible thing. I had known since the broadcast. I had drunk Coral's tea and circled the truth like a cat and let her believe her secret was safe from a woman who was keeping an identical one. Silences stack. Houses fall.

Theo turned for the door.

"The night he asked me," I said.

He stopped. Didn't turn. The emergency light made his shadow long and thin as a crack in a wall.

"October. Nineteen years ago—you weren't there, you were in Zurich, you were always in Zurich. He played me eight bars of a lullaby and asked if I heard where it wanted to go." My voice came out low and steady, which surprised me, because inside I was nineteen again and drowning. "And I said nothing. I looked at the floorboards and I said nothing, because answering would have meant I was allowed to be in the room. Do you understand? I was nineteen years old, I was living above his garage because my mother got evicted and he heard me humming his unpublished étude through a wall and he didn't call the police, he hired me. He handed me a life. And when he asked me the only question that mattered—*do you hear it*—I chose the floor."

Now Theo turned. Slowly. Watching me the way you watch weather change.

"He died with that song unfinished because I was a coward," I said. "Fifteen years I tuned his piano and catalogued his family's arguments and births and deaths through the radiators and told myself silence was a profession. It wasn't. It was a debt. And I am done paying interest on it." I stood up, sketchbook in both hands, and my legs were shaking and my voice wasn't, and I understood in that moment that this was the thing I had withheld at nineteen—not information, not evidence, but simply this: *I hear it. I hear where it wants to go. Let me answer.*

"So no, Theo. I won't put the book back. And tomorrow Coral isn't signing your settlement, because tomorrow morning she and I are going into the music room with dated manuscripts and a recording rig and we're going to put both names on the label—hers first. And when your lawyers come, they can explain to a judge why the executor of an estate registered a copyright he knew was fraudulent. Because you knew. You handled Auguste's papers. You knew eight bars from a finished work." I stepped past him, close enough to smell the cologne over the diesel. "You've spent a year treating that family like furniture. I know furniture, Theo. I was furniture for fifteen years. Furniture remembers."

For a moment he said nothing at all. Then, quietly, almost respectfully, the way one professional acknowledges another's technique: "You rehearsed that."

"Every word," I said. "For fifteen years."

* * *

Doreen buzzed me out at 9:12 and told me the lighting in Bay C was a disgrace and she'd file a report. I signed the log. Under purpose of visit, I crossed out my earlier entry and wrote: *Claimed.*

Outside, Marrow Street was cold and enormous and lit like a stage waiting for its cast. The sketchbook rode inside my coat, against my ribs, where I could feel it with every heartbeat—eight bars of Auguste, sixteen of Coral, and between them the seam where a dead man's sentence met his daughter's answer.

11:40. Last bus of the 42, last night of the route, and somewhere between here and the terminus, Coral Verrone was standing at a stop in the dark, holding a thermos of tea she'd made for two, waiting to find out whether the woman she'd hired had chosen the floor or the room.

I started running for the corner of Delancey, and the city, for once, ran with me.
