## 21. The Recording

The morning of the recording, I arrived at the Verrone house two hours early, the way you arrive at anything that matters—early enough to be alone with it first.

Coral had left the front door unlocked for me. A note taped to the glass at eye level: *Music room's open. Coffee's on. Don't touch the piano until I'm down.* The last line was her father's handwriting in spirit if not in ink, and I smiled at it standing on the porch like a fool, holding my tuning kit against my chest the way other people hold bouquets.

Inside, the radiators were already talking. October had turned cold overnight and the boiler had woken with a groan I knew from fifteen years of third Thursdays—that low complaint of water finding its old channels, iron remembering iron. But the sound was different now, and it took me a moment to name the difference. The grief-pitch was gone. For years the pipes behind Auguste's music room wall had carried a rhythm unlike mourning: deliberate, recent, human. Coral practicing. Coral almost-saying-it through the wall while the whole house pretended not to listen.

Now the radiators just ticked. Warm, ordinary, alive. A house that had finished its sentence.

I set up in the music room. The door stood open—the key from valve three hanging on a nail beside the frame now, where anyone could see it, which was the whole point. Sunlight came through the east windows at an angle that made the dust move like slow notation. The piano sat under its shroud, and I lifted the wool off it the way you'd lift a sheet off a face you loved.

It was out of tune. Of course it was. Fifteen years of neglect will do that, and then some—a piano doesn't just drift, it *grieves*, strings sagging toward their own private silences. I opened my case and laid out my tools in the order I always did: mutes, hammer, felt strips, the fork. A-440. The one fixed point in a drifting world.

Then I sat down on the bench and didn't touch anything for a long time.

Because here is what nobody tells you about tuning: you are not correcting the instrument. You are negotiating with it. Every string has a memory of where it wants to live, and your job is to hear both that want and the truth, and find the place where they can coexist. Auguste taught me that. *You don't force a note home, Edie. You listen until the note tells you where home is.*

For nineteen years I had thought that was a lesson about pianos.

* * *

Coral came down at nine, hair still damp, carrying two cups and a folder thick enough to stop a door. She'd slept, or she hadn't; either way there was a steadiness in her face that hadn't been there when we confronted Theo with the dated manuscripts ten days ago. That morning she had shaken. This morning she looked like someone who had decided something and let the decision do the trembling for her.

"Two hundred copies," she said, setting the coffee down on the windowsill. "That's what he pressed before the label pulled it. Two hundred CDs with his name on them, sitting in a storage unit in Hackensack."

"Poetic," I said. "He'll die surrounded by his own fraud."

"He'll die rich, if we're slow." She sat on the bench beside me—not at the keys, not yet—and opened the folder. Inside were the documents we'd assembled like a case for a court that hadn't convened yet: Auguste's original sketchbook pages, photographed and certified; Coral's reconstruction manuscript, dated by a forensic document examiner who worked cheap because she'd once been a Verrone student herself; the deposition from the radio engineer confirming that the "Verrone Lullaby" credited to Theodore Verrone had arrived with no provenance, no prior recordings, no performance history—which is impossible for any real piece of music. Music leaves footprints. This one had appeared out of clean air, because it had lived for thirty years inside one woman's hands and one dead man's unfinished pages.

"The label's lawyer called yesterday," Coral said. "They're pulling the recording pending review. They want us to submit our documentation." She paused. "They want a new recording."

"There it is," I said.

"There it is." She looked at the piano. "Both names?"

"Both names. Composer and performer, or composer and—" I gestured at my kit. "Whatever I am."

"Tuner," she said. "Collaborator. Witness." She said the last word carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "My father used to say every finished piece needs one. Someone who heard it become itself."

I picked up my coffee so my hands would have somewhere honest to be. "He said that to me once. About the lullaby. He wanted me to help him finish it. Record it." The confession came easier than it ever had—practice, Marta would say. Everything is practice. "I refused. I told him it wasn't my place. He died with it unrecorded and I told myself for twenty years that I'd been respectful."

"And now?"

"Now I think respect was the word I hid behind. Same word you used, letting Theo put his name on it. We were both being respectful right up until it became theft."

Coral didn't flinch. She'd stopped flinching somewhere around the night she broke open in this room and told me everything—the fragment her father played her at nine, the way she'd carried it through boarding school and grief and Theo's cheerful paperwork, reconstructing it bar by bar from memory because the manuscript pages had gone missing into her uncle's careful custody. "Yes," she said simply. "We were."

She flexed her fingers. "So. How do we do this? You tune, I play, someone hits record?"

"No," I said. "Not quite."

* * *

Here is what I proposed, and here is why it mattered:

A piano recorded after a standard tuning sounds correct and dead. Every interval locked to the grid, every overtone obedient. But a piano tuned *live*, around a performance—tuned by ear to the specific piece being played, its temperament stretched and shaded to flatter the harmonies of that one work—that piano sounds like a room where something true happened. Old recordings have it. The ones made in houses, not studios, by engineers brave enough to let the tuner work while the pianist warmed up, the two crafts braided together, correction and creation happening in the same air.

Auguste had described it to me exactly once, late, wine-warm, in this very room. *Someday, Edie, someone will record me that way. Tuner at my elbow, adjusting as I go. The piano learning the song while I learn it.*

"Someday is today," I told Coral. "I tune as you play. I'll need you to run it for me twice—just for me, no tape—so I know where the harmonies lean. Then we record, and I adjust in real time. Unisons mostly. Maybe pull the treble a few cents sharp where the melody climbs. It'll sound strange to you, hearing me work while you play. Ignore me. Play through me."

"And if you make a mistake?"

"Then the mistake is on the record too, and it's ours." I heard myself say *ours* and let it stand there, uncorrected. Some notes you don't tune back.

* * *

She ran it twice.

The first time was cautious—her father's opening phrase rendered like a museum piece, beautiful and embalmed. I listened with my hands flat on the soundboard, feeling the wood answer her, mapping where the bass third swelled sour under the lullaby's turn to the relative minor, where the middle register went glassy on the repeated figure that was, I understood now, a child's name set to rhythm. *Co-ral. Co-ral.* Three notes falling like footsteps on stairs.

"Stop," I said gently, halfway through. "You're playing him."

"I'm playing what I remember."

"You're playing what you think he'd have wanted. Play what you wrote. It's yours. The manuscript says so, the dates say so, and more importantly—" I tapped the soundboard, and the whole piano hummed agreement. "This says so. Instruments know authorship. They always have. He taught me that too, though I don't think he knew he was teaching it."

The second run was different. Somewhere in the fourth bar she stopped performing her inheritance and started speaking her own sentence, and the lullaby shifted under her hands the way a face shifts when someone stops posing. It was still Auguste's melody—he'd written the seed, eight bars, the falling figure—but Coral had grown it into something with her own weather in it. A middle section he'd never have written, restless, modulating where he'd have stayed home. And then the return, the eight bars coming back changed by their journey, the way a child comes back from school changed.

I followed her with my hammers and my felts, working the unisons as she moved, tightening a wobbling string between phrases, easing the octave that barked on the fortissimo she hadn't planned but committed to anyway. My hands knew the work. My ears knew the work. What was new was doing it in the open, audibly, my small metallic clicks and thocks woven into her music like a second instrument—the sound of maintenance, of care, of the labor that holds a house together and never gets named on the label.

Except this time it would be.

* * *

We recorded at noon, with the sun full on the floorboards and the radiators ticking their warm, ordinary tick. Coral's friend Devi ran the recorder—a portable rig, two microphones, nothing fancy. "Houses are better studios than studios," Devi said, placing a mic near the open soundboard and one back by the door to catch the room. "Studios try to erase the space. This space has things to say."

It did. It said forty years of Thursday tunings. It said a girl pressing one key under a quiet apprentice's hand. It said a man dying with eight bars unfinished and a daughter finishing them across a gap no one should be able to cross, and crossing it anyway.

Devi counted us in. Coral began.

I won't describe the take. Descriptions are how musicians lie. I'll say only this: about ninety seconds in, a radiator behind the east wall clicked three times in the exact rhythm of the opening figure, and Coral laughed—a real laugh, mid-performance, ruining nothing, becoming part of everything—and kept playing. And I, kneeling at the piano's flank with my hammer raised over a string that needed two cents of truth, held the stroke until the phrase ended, then set it home soft as a heartbeat.

Correction and creation together. Both names on the label.

When the last chord decayed—we let it ring until the room itself went silent, which took nearly ten seconds, the house reluctant to let go—nobody moved. Then Devi said, quietly, "That's the one," and Coral put her forehead against the fallboard and cried the way people cry when something long broken gets fixed: not sadly. Relieved.

I packed my tools slowly. Under the piano, in the dust, I found a rosin crumb and didn't sweep it away. Evidence of practice. Evidence of devotion. Let it stay.

* * *

Theo's collapse, when it came, was less dramatic than either of us had braced for. Fraud, it turns out, is a structure built entirely out of silence, and we had stopped being silent.

The label withdrew his recording within the week. The royalty account froze pending audit, and the audit found what audits find: eleven years of payments routed to an entity that shared an address with Theo's boat. The estate lawyer—a dry, decent woman named Okafor whom Coral had retained the day after our confrontation—filed the authorship claim with our documentation attached, and Theo's own signature on the radio submission became the noose: sworn attestation of sole authorship, dated, witnessed, and contradicted by manuscripts whose ink predated his claim by decades.

He called the house twice. Coral let the machine take it. The first message was bluster. The second was four seconds of breathing and a click, and somehow that was worse, and better.

The house sale died quietly. Without the reroute panic propping up his timeline—because the city, in the small mercy of bureaucracies, had agreed to rename the terminus stop for Auguste Verrone rather than simply erase it, and a renamed stop, absurdly, had stabilized the block's value—there was no fire sale to force. Theo retreated to wherever men like Theo retreat. I imagined him in Hackensack among two hundred mislabeled CDs, and I found, checking myself honestly, that I didn't hate him. Pity was closer. He had spent his whole life handling his brother's legacy like furniture, moving it, pricing it, never once sitting down to play it. That's not evil. That's just poverty of a kind money can't fix.

"He'll contest it," Coral said, the evening the freeze notice came. We were in the kitchen, drinking tea out of mismatched cups, the recording safely backed up in three places.

"Let him," I said. "Truth keeps better books than he does."

* * *

That night I stayed late. Coral had gone up to bed, and I sat in the music room with the lamp on, running my cloth over the keys, closing the piano for the night the way you'd tuck in a child.

On the music desk lay the sketchbook—the original, retrieved from the depot locker, returned here where it belonged. Auguste's hand marched across the pages: études, fragments, grocery lists wedged between dynamics, and there, near the end, the eight bars that had started everything, with his final annotation beneath them, the one I'd read a dozen times now and would read a thousand more:

*Finish it, whoever hears the house finish my sentence.*

Underneath, smaller, added later in pencil, in Coral's hand: *Done. — C.V., with E.P., who heard.*

With E.P. Who heard.

I closed the sketchbook and rested my hand on the piano's lid, and the radiators ticked around me like a room full of small clocks all agreeing on the hour.

Fifteen years I had tuned this house from the outside of its life, cataloging what I heard and saying nothing. Now my initials were penciled into its permanent record, two letters in graphite, and they weighed more than any will.

Tomorrow there would be lawyers, and mastering sessions, and the slow public business of setting a name straight. Tomorrow Coral's daughter—due in the spring, Coral had finally told me, hand on her stomach like a secret she'd decided to stop keeping—would someday need someone to teach her what the pipes were saying.

But tonight the house was warm, the song was sung, and for the first time in my life I had not almost spoken.

I had spoken. It was on the record. Both names.

I turned off the lamp and let the dark come in, and the radiators kept talking, and this time, I talked back.
