## 3. The House Above the Waterline

The bus to Balisong Cove took four hours, and Wren spent all of them rehearsing questions he would never ask.

He had them written down, actually, in a small notebook he used for interviews—*When did you first realize the language was dying? Who else spoke it? What did the village eat before the relocation?*—and the act of writing them had felt professional, contained, like laying out knives before service. He was good at questions. Questions kept him on the right side of the table, the side where you asked and the other person answered, the side where nothing was required of you except attention, and attention, Wren had learned, could be performed so convincingly that even he believed it.

The bus left the expressway and dropped into a landscape that got poorer and greener in alternating stripes. Rice, nipa, a gas station, rice again. Somewhere past the last town with a Jollibee, the sea appeared on the right, gray-silver and enormous, and Wren felt the particular dread he always felt near water, which was the dread of a thing that reminded him of his mother without being able to say why. She had loved the sea. She had also loved sinigang, and rain, and the specific way his name sounded when she called it through the house, and the sea did not get to have her. He looked at his phone instead.

The driver let him off at a junction of gravel and tricycle tracks. A hand-painted sign said BALISONG COVE, and under it, in smaller letters, someone had added *WELCOME HOME* in English, which struck Wren as either very hopeful or very ironic, and he suspected the painter had not considered the difference.

He walked the last two kilometers because there was nothing else. The road became a path became a suggestion. Fishing boats sat up on blocks like sleeping animals. A dog watched him pass with the professional indifference of a dog who had seen journalists before. The houses were small, concrete-block and wood, some of them clearly abandoned, their windows dark, their roofs furred with moss, and between the occupied ones hung laundry, nets, the smell of smoke and salt and low tide. The village did not look dying, exactly. It looked like a sentence someone had stopped writing halfway through and never picked up again.

He found the house above the waterline the way you find anything in a village that size: by asking, and being pointed at, and walking.

It stood on a low rise at the edge of everything, a stilted wooden house with a corrugated roof gone the color of old coins, a porch that faced the water like an audience. Bougainvillea climbed one post with the aggression of something unpruned for decades. A hammock hung empty. On the porch, in a plastic chair with a broken arm, sat a small old woman shelling peanuts into a bowl, and she watched him come the whole way up the path without moving, without smiling, with the unhurried assessment of someone who had been watching the water for eighty years and was not going to be impressed by one more thing arriving out of it.

"You're the writer," she said, before he could speak.

"I'm Wren Reyes. From—"

"I know who you're from. You think I don't read?" She gestured at the chair across from her, a second plastic chair, also broken-armed, as if the pair had been purchased together and failed together. "Sit. You look like the walk hurt you."

"It's a beautiful walk," Wren said, and sat.

"It's a walk," she said. "Beautiful is what people say when they don't know the words." She cracked a peanut between her fingers with a sound like a knuckle. "Magdalena Villaruel. But everyone calls me Lola Inday, even people who are not my grandchildren, which is most of the people left here. You want coffee?"

"I'd love—"

"I don't have coffee." She said it without apology, almost with satisfaction. "I have water, and I have soup, because I was cooking soup when your editor's letter came and I have not stopped being annoyed about it since. Sit there. Don't touch anything."

She went inside, moving slowly but with a kind of internal certainty, as if the house rearranged itself around her rather than the reverse. Wren sat in the broken chair and looked at the water. It was low tide. The exposed flats shone like hammered pewter, and children were out on them, bent over, gathering shells or small fish, their voices carrying in fragments. He got out his recorder, set it on the table between the plastic chairs, and then, for no reason he could name, moved it to his bag.

Lola Inday came back with two bowls on a tray that had once, judging by its design, belonged to something more prosperous. The soup was clear, pale gold, with fish and green papaya and leaves he recognized and could not name, and the smell of it reached him a full second before the bowl arrived—fish, sourness, something green and wild underneath, the smell of a kitchen he had stood in before, decades ago, in a different life, and he felt the familiar flatness descend over him like a hand laid gently on the back of the neck.

She set the bowl in front of him. She did not take one for herself.

"Eat," she said. "Then tell me what it tastes like. That's the price of the interview. One sentence. You're a famous critic, they tell me. One sentence should be easy."

Wren picked up the spoon. He understood, in the way you understand a trap after you've stepped in it, that this was not hospitality. It was a test, and it had been a test since the letter, and possibly since before the letter, and he sat there holding the spoon like a man holding a live wire.

He tasted it. That is, he performed the tasting: the broth on the tongue, the fish flaking against the palate, the sour note arriving late. He knew what it was. He had eaten ten thousand soups. His training rose up around him like scaffolding, and he built the sentence out of its familiar parts.

"It's delicate," he said. "The sourness is restrained. The fish is fresh—you caught it this morning, obviously—and the leaves give it a kind of... wildness. It's a soup that tastes like this place."

The words came out smooth. They always came out smooth. That had been the horror of the last three years, how smooth they were, how the machinery of praise and description ran on its own fuel regardless of whether anything was actually burning.

Lola Inday looked at him. She did not look angry. She looked like a woman reading a letter she had already received once, years ago, from someone else.

"That's what it tastes like," she said, "in a magazine."

"Lola—"

"Magdalena. Or Inday, if you must." She took the bowl out of his hands—not snatched it, took it, the way you take something from a child who is playing with a knife—and set it on the tray. "My mother made this soup with batwan that grew on the bluff, before the bluff eroded. My daughter made it with fish she caught herself, the morning she died, and I will tell you that story never, so don't ask. The leaves are *saluyot* and something your word for it doesn't exist, because the thing your word doesn't exist for is half of what the soup is." She sat back in her broken chair. "You tasted it. I watched you taste it. And then you told me a sentence that could be printed on a menu in Makati."

Wren said nothing. The sea made its small sound against the flats.

"Your editor wrote me a very nice letter," she went on. "'The last speaker of a dying language.' 'A cuisine that will vanish with her.' Very sad, very respectful, the way people are respectful about things they've already decided are over. He wants fifteen hundred words. He wants you to ask me about the typhoon, and the relocation, and how it feels"—she said *how it feels* with a delicacy that was more violent than shouting—"to be the last one."

"Those are the questions that—"

"Those are the questions that let you leave at four o'clock and write it in an air-conditioned room and get paid and never come back." She cracked another peanut. "I've had writers here before. Three. One cried, which was at least honest. One stole my photographs and never sent them back. One wrote a lovely piece and I had my grandnephew read it to me twice, and in all of it, do you know what was missing?"

"What?" Wren asked, because he was supposed to.

"Me." She said it simply, the way you'd say *salt*. "There was everything about the language, the tragedy, the dying, the beautiful doomed village, and I was furniture in my own house. So. No interview."

Wren felt something move in his chest that was not quite anger and not quite relief. "Then why did you let me come all this way?"

"Because I wanted to see you." She looked at him with eyes that had gone pale as sea glass but missed nothing. "I read your column. My grandnephew prints it out for me. You're very good. That's the terrible part. You write about food like a man describing a woman he's never touched." She let that sit. "So I wanted to see the hands."

"The hands?"

"You write with them, in the photographs. Very expressive. A cook's hands, a pianist's hands." She shrugged. "They're fine hands. They're not the problem." She pointed one small brown finger at him, at the center of his chest, with an accuracy that made him sit back. "The problem is in there, and it's not my business, and I'm not going to ask. But I'll tell you what I told the other three, and they didn't listen either."

She leaned forward. The bougainvillea dropped a petal between them, bright as a wound.

"I am the last speaker of Binukid-ni-Dagat. Not the last *knower*. The last *speaker*. Do you know the difference? A knower has it in a drawer. A speaker has it in the mouth. When I die, the mouth closes, and everything my mother said, and everything her mother said, all the songs and the curses and the names of fish that don't exist in your language because your language never needed them—" she spread her hands, and for a moment the sharpness went out of her face and something vast and tired looked out, "—it goes where the village went. And your fifteen hundred words will not follow it there. Words about a language are a coffin. The language itself is a boat. I am not giving you a coffin."

"So what will you give me?" Wren asked. It came out quieter than he intended.

Lola Inday sat back. She picked up the bowl of soup—his bowl, the one she'd taken away—and held it a moment, and then, to his surprise, she set it back in front of him, still warm, still steaming faintly in the sea air.

"Nothing today," she said. "But I'm old, and old women get to change their minds, and I change them slowly, so you have time. Come back when you've thought about what I said. Not about the interview. About the soup." She nodded at it. "Eat that. It's good. It deserves better than it got."

* * *

He ate it.

He ate all of it, slowly, while she shelled peanuts and watched the water and said nothing at all, and the strange thing—the thing he would turn over for the whole four-hour bus ride home, and the night after, and the night after that—was that under the flatness, under the glass wall he had lived behind for three years, something in the soup was *reaching* for him. He couldn't name it. He couldn't taste it, not really, not the way the word *taste* was supposed to work. But he could feel the shape of where it would be, the way you can feel the shape of a missing tooth, and it was the most honest thing his mouth had reported to him in years.

When he finished, she took the bowl without a word and stood.

"Go home, Wren Reyes," she said, from the doorway, already half inside her house, already done with him. "And when your editor asks what happened, tell him the truth. Tell him the last speaker refused to be a story."

* * *

On the bus back, the sea ran alongside him for twenty minutes and then gave up, and Wren watched it go without the usual dread. He opened his notebook to the page of prepared questions and looked at them for a long time. *When did you first realize the language was dying? Who else spoke it?* Dead questions. Coffin questions.

He turned to a fresh page, and before he knew he was going to do it, he wrote down the one thing from the day that had lodged in him like a splinter: *a word for a leaf that has no English word, and the shape of a taste I couldn't reach.*

He stared at it. Then, beneath it, in smaller handwriting, almost against his will:

*Go back.*

His phone buzzed. His editor: *How was the interview? Need copy by Friday.*

Wren looked out the window at the darkening fields, at the lights of the towns coming on one by one, and typed: *There wasn't one. She refused. But I think she'll see me again.*

He hit send before he could delete it, and then he sat with the sentence, feeling—for the first time in three years—like a man who had been caught in a lie and was, inexplicably, grateful.
