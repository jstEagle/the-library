## 5. A Hundred Tides

The bus to Balisong Cove left at five-forty in the morning, and by the third Saturday Wren had stopped setting an alarm for it. His body simply woke, the way it had once woken for service at his mother's table—some older clock ticking beneath the one he'd wound around his career. He rode with fishermen and a woman selling live crabs and two teenagers who slept against the fogged windows, and he watched Manila peel away behind him like a bandage coming off.

He had told himself this was research. A feature required immersion; immersion required repetition. His editor had loved the phrase *embedded critic* and used it twice in the pitch memo. But on the bus there was no editor, no deadline, no byline. There was only the highway unsprolling south and the small hard fact that he had not missed a Saturday yet.

Lola Inday was waiting on her porch when he arrived, always before he arrived, though he never saw how she knew the time. She sat in a rattan chair worn to the color of weak tea, and beside her on the rail sat a second chair—a plastic monobloc, sun-bleached, clearly placed there for him. He had never been invited to sit in it. It was simply there, and so he sat.

"Student," she said, which was how she greeted him now. Not his name. The word in her language was longer, had more teeth in it: *mag-aaral sa dagat*, learner of the sea. She said it the way other people said *boy* or *come here*, without ceremony, and every time it landed in his chest like a coin dropped into a dry well.

"Mag-aaral sa dagat," he repeated.

"Worse than last week."

"I practiced."

"You practiced wrong." She waved at the water as if it were a blackboard. "The sea does not care if you are correct. Say it again."

He said it again. Somewhere down the beach a dog barked twice and stopped, as if even the dog had opinions about his accent.

* * *

The lessons had a shape now, though she would never admit to a curriculum. Some days it was single words, held up to the light like shells. Some days it was phrases, whole breaths of language that seemed to have been built for weather rather than grammar. And some days—and these were the days Wren had begun to live toward—she talked.

She talked while shelling peanuts, while sorting dried fish, while watching the tide do its slow arithmetic on the shore. Her memories came out in Binukid-ni-Dagat first, murmured like prayers, and then she'd translate—not all of it, never all of it, but enough. Enough to make him understand that the language wasn't dying of neglect. It was drowning slowly, holding its dead.

"The cove had four hundred voices when I was a girl," she said one morning, her hands moving through a basket of small silver fish. "Four hundred voices, and you could hear all of them from my mother's porch. Not because we were loud. Because the words carried. Words built for wind." She held up a fish, tiny and iridescent. "This one. In Tagalog, *dilis*. In English, anchovy. In our tongue—" she said a word that sounded like a wave folding over on itself, "*silaw-silaw*"—"and the word means not just the fish but the way they move together, the flashing, the whole silver argument of them. You cannot say *silaw-silaw* and think of one fish. The word itself is a school."

"And when the fish are gone?"

"Then the word waits." She dropped the fish back into the basket. "Words are patient. People are not."

Wren switched on his recorder out of habit, and she smacked his hand without looking at it.

"No machine today. Today you carry it." She tapped her temple. "If it is only in the machine, it is luggage. If it is in you, it is blood."

* * *

The children found him in his second week and never really let go.

It started with three of them—two boys and a girl with a shaved head and a stare like a customs officer—who watched him struggle through vocabulary on the seawall and began offering corrections with the merciless honesty of children everywhere. Then it was seven. Then eleven. They called him *the student*, in English, delighted by the joke of an old man being a student, and they appointed themselves his examiners. They quizzed him on the path to the well. They made him name the parts of a boat. When he got a word right they clapped once, in unison, a sound like a door closing on his old life.

"They like you," Lola Inday observed, without looking up from her net-mending.

"They're laughing at me."

"Of course. That is also liking." She pulled a knot tight. "In the city, people laugh at you behind your back. Here we are honest. Better for the soul."

One of the boys, the smallest, whose name was Toto and who had decided Wren was personally responsible for his education, took to walking him to the bus stop on Saturdays, quizzing him the whole way. *What is the word for the rope that holds the boat? For the rope that holds the net? For the rope that holds nothing?* There were different words for all three. There were different words for everything here. The language had as many names for water as the city had names for money, and Wren began to understand that this was not poverty of vocabulary but wealth—he had spent his life in a language with one word for grief and a hundred for profit, and here it was inverted, here the sea had been given its full inventory.

* * *

On the fifth Saturday, the rain came.

He felt it before he understood it. He was standing at the seawall with Lola Inday, mid-lesson, when she stopped talking and lifted her face into the wind like a woman listening for a knock. The air changed. Something green and mineral moved through it, something that raised the hair on his arms, and the light over the water went the color of pewter.

"What is that?" he asked. "What's happening?"

"That," she said, "has a word."

And she gave it to him: a long word, rolling, that began deep in the chest and ended almost in a whistle—the smell of rain arriving over the sea, the specific scent of a storm still too far to see, carried ahead of itself on the wind. There was no English for it. There was no Tagalog for it either, she said, though Tagalog had gotten close with *amoy ulan*. This was closer. This was the exact thing, named exactly.

"Say it."

He said it. Wrongly.

"Again."

Again. Less wrongly.

"Now breathe it in while you say it. The word lives in the nose, not the mouth."

He breathed in—that green mineral darkening air—and said the word, and for one strange moment the word and the smell were the same thing, fused, indivisible, and he felt something move in him that was not memory exactly but adjacent to memory, the way a neighbor's music comes through a wall. His mother's kitchen had smelled like this before storms, windows open, rice on the stove, her saying *ulan na naman* with that particular resignation that was half complaint and half love.

"Lola," he said carefully, "what did you feel, the first time you smelled rain over the sea?"

She was quiet a long moment. The first drops came down fat and warm, spotting the dust.

"I felt that God was breathing," she said. "I was six. My grandmother told me the word and I thought, someone has caught God's breath and put it in a word, and now I can hold it." She looked at him. "That is what a language is, student. Not words. Held breaths. Everyone who spoke it is gone, and the breaths remain, waiting for a mouth."

The rain arrived properly then, drumming on the nipa roof, and they moved under the eaves and drank ginger tea and she taught him three more words for rain—rain that falls straight down, rain that falls sideways, rain that falls on water but not land—and he wrote none of them down, because she had forbidden the recorder and he was beginning to understand why. What you write down, you can lose. What you carry, you must protect.

* * *

He began, without noticing, to be folded in.

It happened in increments too small to announce themselves. The sari-sari store owner started setting aside the good ice for him. An auntie named Maring began leaving food on the porch rail when she passed—first a banana wrapped in leaf, then a small bundle of puto, then, one Saturday, a covered bowl of ginataang hipon with a note in careful English: *For the student.* The fishermen nodded to him now instead of studying him. Old Emong, who mended nets by the chapel, grunted what Wren had come to understand was a greeting.

None of it was spoken. None of it needed to be. In Manila, belonging was negotiated—in contracts, invitations, reservations, follower counts. Here it simply accrued, like silt, like salt, until one day you looked down and realized you were standing on ground.

The strangest part was how it felt. Unfamiliar. Almost alarming. Warmth, Wren discovered, was a sensation he had no practice receiving. Praise he could handle; praise was professional. But Maring's bowl of shrimp in coconut milk, left without a note some weeks and with a note others, undid something in him he didn't have a name for. He ate it sitting on the seawall, and it tasted—well, it tasted of nothing, as everything tasted of nothing, but it *felt* like something, warmth moving down through his chest, and he sat there longer than he meant to, watching the boats come in.

He was learning a language for a story. That was what he told himself. That was what he would tell his editor. But on the bus home, with Toto waving from the road until the bend took him away, Wren caught himself rehearsing vocabulary not for the notebook—for himself. Saying the words in the dark of the bus like a man counting money he hadn't earned yet. *Silaw-silaw. Mag-aaral sa dagat.* The long word for rain arriving over the sea, which he now said every time he smelled it, which he now smelled everywhere, in the city, off the Pasig, in the wet street outside his building, the word arriving in his mouth before the smell finished arriving in his nose.

His apartment, when he got back to it, seemed smaller. Cleaner. More rented than ever. He stood in the middle of it holding his bag of dried fish and shell-shells the children had given him, and he thought: I am collecting a life in a place that is not mine, and leaving my actual life in a box in the sky.

He slept badly and happily.

* * *

By the eighth Saturday, he could count.

"One hundred words," Lola Inday said, "before the interview. That was the agreement."

"I know."

"How many do you have?"

Wren closed his eyes. He went through them the way he'd once gone through a tasting menu—course by course, each one distinct, each one located somewhere in his body now rather than on a page. Water words. Wind words. Fish words. Kinship words. The insult Toto had taught him that made Lola Inday laugh until she coughed. The lullaby fragment Maring's daughter had sung him, three lines, melody intact.

"Ninety-one," he said.

Her eyebrows went up—one millimeter, no more, but from her it was applause.

"Nine more," she said. "Then we talk about your article."

"About the interview."

"About whether there will be one." She turned back to her net, and he saw that her hands had slowed. Just slightly. Just enough. "A writer with ninety-one words might still write about me instead of with me. We will see what the last nine do to you."

He laughed, and she didn't, and the laugh died in his throat.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean," she said, threading the needle through the mesh, not looking up, "that the last nine words are not like the others. I have been saving them." She tied the knot, bit the thread, and finally looked at him, and her eyes were the flat gray-green of the sea before weather. "They are not words for the sea, student. They are words for what the sea takes."

The tide was going out. Down the beach, the children were shouting in two languages and half of a third, and the light was turning gold, and Wren sat very still in his plastic chair above the waterline, understanding suddenly that he had not been learning a language at all.

He had been being led somewhere. Word by word, tide by tide, ninety-one steps deep—and the last nine waited below the waterline, in the dark, where she kept the thing she had never told anyone.

The rain word rose in his throat, unbidden. He breathed in. God's breath, held.
