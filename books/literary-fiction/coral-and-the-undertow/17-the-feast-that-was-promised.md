## 17. The Feast That Was Promised

The wish came out of her sideways, the way all her important things did.

We were on word sixty-three — *bagat*, the sound a pot makes just before it boils, when the water first begins to argue with itself — and she was tired in a way I had learned not to mention. Tiredness was a subject she treated like an insult. But that afternoon she let me see it, let her hands rest flat on the blanket while I repeated *bagat* until she nodded.

Then, without looking at me: "The notebook. You have copied how many?"

"Thirty-one recipes translated. Fourteen cooked."

"And how many are in the book altogether?"

I had counted so many times the number lived behind my teeth. "Fifty-two."

"Fifty-two." She said it like a debt being read aloud. "Then you will cook them all. One day. All of them, at once, at long tables, and everyone will eat and say the names of the dishes in Binukid-ni-Dagat before they take a bite. That is how a dish goes somewhere, Wren. A recipe that is only read dies in the drawer. A recipe that is eaten out loud keeps walking."

She turned her head then and looked at me with those eyes that had been seeing through me since the first bowl of soup. "You will do this. Not Jun. Not the barangay captain with his ribbon-cutting instincts. You."

"Why me?" It came out smaller than I meant it to.

"Because you are the one who owes," she said, and closed her eyes, and slept, and left me sitting there with fifty-two ghosts and a promise I hadn't technically made yet but already had.

* * *

Jun took it better than I expected, which is to say he was angry for exactly one evening and then became terrifyingly competent.

"She wants a feast," I told him outside the hospital, under the fluorescent hum of the corridor where the vending machine ate coins. "All fifty-two dishes. In her language. Before—"

"Before." He finished my sentences now. We'd gotten good at that, the way people get good at anything by doing it too often. "You know what you're asking. Half these dishes need fish we can't buy — specific fish, from specific water. There's one in there that needs sea urchin roe gathered at dawn. Who gathers sea urchin anymore? Aling Nena is seventy-four."

"I know."

"And the coconut crab one. That's a two-day preparation. And—" He stopped himself, pressed his knuckles against his mouth, and I watched him do what I had watched him do for weeks: wrestle the resentment down and lose to love. "Okay. Okay. Give me the notebook page. Let me see the list."

That was the thing about Jun I was slow to understand: he had refused the language as a boy because refusing it hurt less than wanting it. Now the want had come back with interest, and he threw himself at logistics the way other men throw themselves at grief.

He borrowed a tricycle and went to the cove that same night. By morning he had done something I could never have done: he called a meeting, and the village came.

* * *

They gathered in the communal kitchen — the same open-sided hall of concrete and capiz windows where the aunties had laughed me through my disastrous kinilaw — and Jun stood at the front with the notebook propped open on a chair like scripture, and I stood beside him feeling like a translator who hadn't earned the language he was translating into.

"The feast will be in three weeks," Jun said. "Every dish from Lola Inday's book. Cooked here. Eaten here. Named aloud in the old language before anyone takes a bite."

Silence. Then Aling Nena, tiny and iron-spined, said, "Who cooks the *tinumtok*? My mother made that. Nobody living has made it since."

"You did," said a voice from the back — old Ben, the net-mender whose hands had taught mine to stop fearing rope. "Nineteen eighty-something. Your mother's death anniversary. I remember. It was too salty."

"It was *not* too salty, it was correct, your tongue rotted from beer." But she was already moving toward the notebook, already reading, and I saw the exact moment the recipe reached her — her lips forming the words in a language she'd been told for forty years was useless — and her face did something complicated and wet.

That was how it went, dish by dish. Jun read the name; someone in the room claimed it or didn't. When nobody claimed a dish, someone claimed the person who used to make it, and then claimed the duty anyway. The *kinunot* needed flaked stingray — "Romy still dives, ask Romy." The rice cakes wrapped in banana leaf needed banana leaves — "the Sarmientos' yard, their trees are older than the church." The fermented fish, the one that smelled like a dare, needed six days minimum, which meant starting immediately, which meant Aling Nena marching out of the meeting mid-sentence to begin.

By noon the wall of the communal kitchen was papered with a hand-drawn chart, fifty-two lines, each with a name beside it. Some dishes had three names, aunties arguing over custody. Two had none, and sat there like empty chairs at a table.

"We'll figure those out," Jun said quietly.

"No," I said. "We'll ask her. She'll know who they belong to."

He looked at me. "She's in a hospital bed, Wren."

"She taught me forty-nine words from a hospital bed. Ask her anyway."

* * *

We asked her anyway. She knew instantly, of course. The unclaimed dishes were her daughter's.

The first was a soup I'd seen in the notebook and never asked about — *lawod-lawod*, named for deep water, built around a fish so small you ate it whole. The second was a sweet: cassava and young coconut steamed in leaves, the kind of thing a child begs for.

"My Luningning made both," she said. Her voice was steady in the way that costs everything. "She was eleven. She cooked them for my birthday, the last birthday before the typhoon. Everyone praised her. She stood on a bench and bowed like a mayor." A pause. "So those two dishes, no one cooks them. Someone else cooks them, and Luningning cooks through them. You understand?"

"Yes," I said, and found that I did, in the place where my mother's recipe card lived under my tongue.

"Wren." She beckoned me closer, and her grip on my wrist was still strong, still the grip of a woman who had hauled nets. "At the feast, when the *lawod-lawod* comes to the table, you say her name first. Before the dish name. Say: *This is Luningning's.* In our language. Can you say it?"

She taught me the sentence right there, syllable by syllable, correcting me twice, and when I finally got it whole she released my wrist and lay back against the pillows looking, for the first time since the stroke, not diminished but full.

"There," she said. "Now she goes somewhere."

* * *

The weeks turned into a kind of weather of their own.

The cove reorganized itself around the chart on the wall. Fishermen traded shifts to dive for sea urchin. The Sarmiento family surrendered their banana leaves with ceremonial complaint. Teenagers who had spent their lives embarrassed by everything old suddenly wanted to learn the toast, the blessing over the food, the work song that went with grating coconut — because, as one of them shrugged to me, "if we're going to eat fifty-two dishes we might as well know what we're saying." I taught them what I could, which was imperfect, which was the point. Lola Inday had told me once: *Speak it badly, daily, out loud. Perfection is another way of staying silent.*

I commuted between worlds. Manila pulled at me — my editor calling about assignments I no longer wanted, the rival critic's profile still circulating like a stain, the apartment waiting spotless and rented-feeling — but each time I got on the bus north, the city lost weight in my chest with every kilometer. By the time the cove appeared, salt-smelling and half-ruined and more like home than anywhere I'd ever paid rent, I was light.

Jun and I fell into a rhythm neither of us named. He handled the village; I handled the translations, checking every rendered recipe against Lola Inday's corrections, cooking test batches in the communal kitchen while aunties supervised with the mercy of drill sergeants. We argued about portions. We argued about whether the feast should be at sunset or dawn. We argued about whether to invite the journalists who had started sniffing around — *last speaker of dying language hosts final feast* wrote itself, and Jun wanted them barred at the waterline.

"No press," he said. "This isn't content."

"It's not ours to hide either," I said. "It's hers. And she always said a language spoken only indoors is already dead."

We brought the question to her. She listened, eyes closed, hands folded, letting us finish the way a judge lets lawyers exhaust themselves.

"Let them come," she said. "But they eat what everyone eats, sit where everyone sits, and if they write, they write the words correctly or not at all." A thin smile. "And tell them the fish was caught by Romy, who is seventy-one, so they understand what a miracle costs."

* * *

Nine days out, everything was on schedule, which should have comforted me and instead made me nervous the way calm water makes sailors nervous.

Forty-six dishes claimed and testing well. The fermentation crocks buried and breathing. The long tables — borrowed doors laid across trestles, planed smooth by Ben and his grandsons — stacked under the house above the waterline, waiting. Jun had even solved the seating: enough benches for the whole village plus the nurses who'd been stealing vocabulary during hospital shifts, plus, at Lola Inday's instruction, "the ones who left and might come back." She wouldn't say who. She liked keeping one door of a story shut until the right chapter.

I was mending a net with Ben that afternoon — my stitches still ugly but functional, which Ben declared was "more than most priests manage" — when I felt it start.

Not anything dramatic. Just a phone call from Jun, standing too far away across the sand, and the way he held the phone, and the way he looked up and found me before he'd even finished listening. Some messages travel faster than signal.

I dropped the net and walked toward him and the whole distance of the beach I was doing arithmetic I hated: nine days. Nine days and fifty-two dishes and one woman in a hospital bed forty minutes away, and the tide of her going out faster than any chart could predict.

"She's sleeping," Jun said when I reached him. "The nurse said today was hard. She said don't come tonight. She said—" his voice caught, steadied, "—she said the feast doesn't move. Whatever happens. The feast doesn't move."

We stood there in the failing light, two men holding a phone between them like a candle.

Above the waterline, the tables waited. Under the ground, the crocks breathed. On the wall, fifty-two names in chalk, and two of them belonging to a girl who had bowed on a bench like a mayor forty years ago, waiting to go somewhere.

Three weeks of hope, braided tight with dread, and now the dread pulling harder.

I looked at the sea, which gave nothing away, and made myself say it out loud, badly, in her language: *We will be ready.*

The words went out over the water. Whether they went somewhere, I couldn't yet know.
