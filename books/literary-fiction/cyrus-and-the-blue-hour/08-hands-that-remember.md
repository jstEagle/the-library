## 8. Hands That Remember

The practice rooms at Alder Conservatory were named after donors, which meant that for twenty years students had been saying things like *I'll be in Kowalski until six* and *Kowalski smells like despair*, and no one had ever once thought about the Kowalskis themselves. Emrys thought about them now, on her knees in Room 4, scrubbing a scuff mark off the baseboard that some ambitious violinist had left with the toe of his shoe — probably mid-phrase, probably convinced that genius excused him from physics.

It did not. Physics was patient. So was she.

She had developed a system over her three years of nights: Room 1 through 6 first, because those had windows and caught the morning light and showed every streak; then the two windowless ones, where the fluorescent hum made everything look already clean. She worked top to bottom, left to right, the way Marguerite Voss had taught her to learn a piece — never letting your hands discover the geography by accident, always knowing what came next before it arrived.

*You don't practice until you can play it,* Marguerite used to say, tapping the score with one lacquered nail. *You practice until you can't play it wrong.*

Emrys sprayed the baseboard and thought: *I can't play anything wrong anymore. That's not the same as playing.*

The scuff came off. The room forgave the violinist. Emrys did not, particularly, but she was working on forgiveness in the abstract, the way you work on a language you might someday need — a few phrases here and there, badly pronounced.

* * *

Room 6 was the problem child. It always was. The radiator under its window clanked like a man trying to get out of a trunk, and the floorboards near the door had warped just enough to catch a mop head, so that cleaning it felt less like custodial work and more like wrestling something old and stubborn that had opinions about being handled.

Tonight the radiator was quiet, which somehow made it worse. Emrys stood in the doorway with her cart and listened to the silence the way other people listened for footsteps. The room held a piano — an upright, old, its walnut case gone amber at the edges where decades of forearms had leaned. A piano out of tune. She could tell from across the room, the way you could tell a joke was going bad from the first sentence: something in the air already wincing.

She should have known better than to go closer. But the mop bucket had a squeaky wheel, and the squeak had covered her approach, and suddenly she was standing next to the instrument with her hand hovering over the fallboard like a person deciding whether to touch a sleeping animal.

Out of tune pianos were, professionally speaking, her jurisdiction. She logged them. There was a form. The form asked for the room number, the nature of the deficiency, and whether the issue impeded instruction. Emrys had filled out this form eleven times in three years, always in her small careful janitor's handwriting, always leaving the last question blank or writing *N/A*, because nothing in this building impeded instruction anymore, least of all her.

She lifted the fallboard.

This was not on the form.

The keys were yellowed unevenly, more at the ends, where sunlight had gotten at them through all those years of lessons. C4 had a hairline crack down its middle that someone had filled with wax, long ago, badly. She knew that crack. She had known that crack when she was nine years old and the wax was fresh, because she had been the one who chipped the key, slamming down a passage of Clementi with more conviction than accuracy, and Marguerite had looked at the damage and then at Emrys and said, *Well. Now the piano has a scar too. You'll match.*

Emrys stood there with her hand on the fallboard and felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it does on a boat when you've forgotten you're on water.

She hadn't touched a piano in twenty years. Not properly. There had been the lobby keyboard at a hotel in Brussels, once, drunk enough to think it was funny, four notes before the shame arrived like a bailiff. There had been the toy piano in a friend's apartment, ironic, tiny, safe precisely because it couldn't hold a real note. But a real piano, out of tune, cracked, waiting — no. Her hands had spent two decades learning other work: mops, sponges, the particular grip required for a toilet brush, the wrist motion of wringing a cloth dry. Hands forget nothing. They just get reassigned.

And yet they were moving now. Without her permission. Her right hand drifted onto the keys and settled into position — thumb on F, fingers curved, that impossible relaxed arch that Marguerite had drilled into her with a pencil tapped against knuckles — and the hand fit. That was the horror of it. Twenty years of bleach and bristle, and the hand still fit the shape of playing like a word fits its definition.

*Don't,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had signed the resignation letter at nineteen and meant it, the part that kept the inventory of unsaid things locked in a mental drawer the way other people keep passports.

But the body, as it turns out, is not governed by the sensible part. The body is governed by older clerks, night-shift workers, the ones who file things by feel. And somewhere in that vast unlit archive, a clerk pulled a folder marked *Chopin, Op. 28 No. 20, first four bars* — the C minor prelude, the first thing Marguerite ever assigned her, the piece every student got first because Marguerite believed you could hear a whole soul in its opening chords — and handed it to the hands without asking anyone upstairs.

Emrys played four bars.

They came out slow and wrong and unbearably beautiful. The piano's flat E sagged under the chord like a tired shoulder. The third bar blurred where her ring finger had lost its independence, the muscles gone soft and strange, and the sound smeared the way grief smears — recognizable, ruined, still itself. Four bars. Chord, chord, the little descending line like someone walking away down a corridor, the final chord that doesn't resolve so much as sit down heavily and refuse to discuss it further.

Then she stopped.

The stopping was worse than the playing. It went through her like a current — shoulders first, then ribs, then breath — and she heard herself make a small sound, involuntary, the kind of noise you make when you burn your hand on a pan: surprise and pain arriving together, refusing to be separated. She pulled her hands off the keys and pressed them flat against her thighs and stood there shaking, actually shaking, her whole body doing a thing she had not authorized, while the last of the sound died in the strings above her and the room returned to its radiator-less silence.

*What are you doing,* she said aloud, to herself, to the piano, to the clerk in the archive. Her voice sounded borrowed. *What are you doing. What are you doing.*

Twenty years. She had built twenty years out of not-this. She had made a career — a modest, honest, invisible career — out of the negative space where music used to be. And four bars of a Chopin prelude on a broken upright had come along and put one finger through it, gently, the way you test whether a wall is load-bearing.

Her eyes were wet. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, roughly, the way you'd deal with a leak you didn't have time to fix, and reached for the mop with hands that would not entirely stop trembling.

That was when she saw him.

Dev Anand was standing in the doorway of Room 6, holding a cardboard box of archival folders against his chest, and he had clearly been there for some time. Long enough. His face did the thing she was coming to recognize as his signature move: nothing moved, but everything rearranged. He didn't step forward. He didn't say *that was beautiful* or *you still have it* or any of the sentences that would have required her to either lie or bleed. He just stood there, one shoulder against the frame, and let the moment stay hers.

"Baseboard in Room 4," Emrys said finally, because her mouth needed to do something normal immediately or she was going to come apart in front of a man she'd known for three weeks. "Scuff mark. Violinist. I got it."

"I see that." He glanced at the mop, then at the piano, then — carefully, deliberately — at neither. "The piano's out of tune."

"It's been out of tune since approximately the Clinton administration." Her voice steadied around complaint; complaint was a handrail she could grip. "I've filed forms. Eleven forms. Facilities says there's no budget for tuning rooms nobody uses."

"Somebody uses it," Dev said mildly. He set the box down on a chair. "Someone plays it. Badly, and rarely, and only four bars at a time."

The words landed with terrible precision. Emrys looked at him. He looked back with the expression of a man who has catalogued ten thousand fragile documents and knows exactly how much pressure paper can take.

"You're here late," she said.

"The Voss materials. I couldn't leave the 1970s correspondence half-processed; it felt rude to them." He said it without irony, which was one of the things about him that undid her slightly — that he meant it, that letters from fifty years ago deserved courtesy. "Also I wanted to ask you something, and I find questions go better in buildings after midnight. Everything's more honest at this hour. Buildings especially."

"What's the question?"

He hesitated. She watched him decide, visibly, to set the question down and pick up a different one. "The conservatory," he said instead. "You've worked here three years. Before that?"

"Before that I cleaned offices downtown. Before that I cleaned a hotel in Brussels." She picked up her spray bottle and aimed it at the window glass with great professional focus. "I contain multitudes, and most of them hold sponges."

"Emrys."

Nobody said her name like that. Most people rushed it, tripped on the *y*. Dev gave each syllable its own room, the way you'd handle a document with a torn edge.

"You know," he said, conversationally, looking at the piano rather than at her, "when I was processing the auction records, I cross-referenced the donor lists from the Alder benefit galas in the nineties. Marguerite Voss chaired the artists' committee for years. There's a photograph from the 1993 gala — a girl at the piano, maybe fifteen, dark hair, absolutely ferocious posture. The caption names her." He paused. "Fairweather. E. Fairweather. First prize, the year before."

The spray bottle was very interesting. Emrys studied its label as though it contained instructions for surviving this.

"They misspelled it in the program the next year," she said. "*Fareweather.* Like weather that goes away."

"Did you correct them?"

"I stopped going to galas."

Dev nodded slowly, absorbing this the way he absorbed everything — without flinching, without filling the silence with comfort, just taking it in and shelving it somewhere careful. She hated how much she liked that about him.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the four bars were the C minor prelude. Opus 28, number 20. Marguerite started every student on it. Talia's file—" He stopped himself. Actually stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Emrys watched him swallow the sentence whole.

"Talia's file," Emrys repeated.

"There's a letter in the box," Dev said quietly. "From 1972. I wasn't going to mention it tonight. It's late, and you've had—" he gestured, delicately, at the entire scene, the piano, her red-rimmed eyes, the general wreckage "—an evening. But I found an address. Or the start of one. Oregon. A name: Talia Reyes, adopted, records sealed, but the agency merged twice and the second merger left everything uncatalogued, and uncatalogued means findable, if a person is stubborn and has no hobbies."

Emrys set down the spray bottle. Her hands had almost stopped trembling. Almost.

"You could have led with that," she said.

"I could have. But then you'd have walked out of here thinking about Oregon, and you wouldn't have thought about what happened in this room just now. And I think—" he chose the words like a man crossing ice "—I think what happened in this room matters more than you want it to. The body keeps better records than any archive I've ever worked in. Yours just opened a file it's had sealed for twenty years."

"That's a terrible metaphor. Archives don't work like that."

"No," Dev agreed. "People do, though."

He picked up his box. At the doorway he paused, and without turning around said, "Room 6 gets tuned Thursday. I called facilities this afternoon and paid for it myself. Consider it archival preservation — the instrument is period-appropriate to the collection." And then, before she could assemble a response: "Oregon, Emrys. When you're ready. Not before."

His footsteps went down the corridor, unhurried, and faded.

Emrys stood alone in Room 6 with the untuned piano and the clean baseboards and the four bars still hanging in the air like smoke that refused to acknowledge it had been extinguished. She looked at her hands. They lay at her sides, ordinary, chapped at the knuckles, smelling faintly of lemon disinfectant — and underneath all of that, patient as the radiators, keeping their own inventory.

*Four bars,* she thought. *God help me, I remember the rest.*

She clicked off the light, and in the dark, before the door closed behind her, she found herself already composing the letter she'd sworn not to write — seven drafts' worth of beginnings, all of them starting the same way:

*Dear Talia Reyes. Our mother played Chopin to strangers, and I have spent my life not playing it to anyone. I think we may be each other's missing pages—*

No. Too much. Too soon. She locked the practice room door and carried the key, and the unfinished sentence, down the corridor toward morning.
