## 21. Two Names on the Deed

*Emrys*

The kettle in Marguerite's kitchen — my kitchen now, ours, though the pronoun still caught like a splinter — had a whistle that landed a quarter-tone flat. Talia noticed it the first week and said nothing. I noticed it the first morning and said nothing either, which was how I learned we were related: not by blood, which the paperwork confirmed, but by the shared conviction that naming a flaw aloud might somehow make it permanent.

Three weeks into the teaching studio's life, we had developed a system. I taught mornings — adult beginners, mostly, people who arrived clutching their instruments like apology letters to their younger selves. Talia handled afternoons and the administrative chaos she called "triage," because apparently running a hospice for eleven years made scheduling piano lessons feel like a vacation. We overlapped at five o'clock, when the last student left and the house exhaled.

That Tuesday, the overlap began with an argument about the radiator.

"It's clanking," Talia said from the hallway, where she stood with her coat half off, one arm trapped, looking like a heron mid-transformation.

"It's singing."

"Emrys. It sounds like someone is being murdered down there with a wrench."

"That's a B-flat. Approximately." I kept sorting sheet music. "Marguerite used to say every house has a pitch. You tune yourself to it or you fight it forever."

"What did she say about houses that clank?"

"She didn't have this problem. She had money and radiators that behaved."

Talia freed her arm and hung her coat on the hook — her hook, third from the left, claimed without discussion sometime in week two. She came into the front room, which had been Marguerite's parlor and was now the studio proper, its two pianos angled toward each other like old friends who'd run out of things to say but liked the company anyway.

"You named it?" she asked, nodding at the upright near the window.

"Bessie."

"You named the radiator?"

"The piano. The radiator is unnamed because it hasn't earned it yet."

Something happened then that I was still learning to read: the corner of Talia's mouth moved, not quite a smile, more like a smile checking whether the room was safe. "You're strange," she said.

"I've been told. By professionals."

She sat down across from me at the grand — Marguerite's Steinway, the one I'd played the Berceuse on during the blue hour, the night everything turned over. Talia never touched the pianos. I'd noticed that early. She dusted them, tuned them (she'd found a tuner in Coos Bay who charged fair rates and told long stories), moved them when the floor guys needed clearance. But her hands stayed in her lap around them, folded, patient, like hands waiting outside a hospital room.

"I signed the leave extension today," she said. "Through spring."

I looked up. "Yeah?"

"Yeah. My supervisor thinks I'm having some kind of crisis. She keeps sending me articles about burnout." A pause. "Are you having a crisis? Should I be reading those articles too?"

"I think we're both having several. They're just quiet ones."

"Huh." She considered this. "Mine are usually loud."

"So I gathered. From the parking lot."

The parking lot. We did not speak of the parking lot often, but we spoke of it sometimes, the way you touch a bruise gently to check if it's healing. That afternoon in October when I'd sat in my car for forty minutes rehearsing sentences and she'd walked out of the hospice and looked directly at me through the windshield — that moment had become, without our deciding it, the founding document of whatever this was. Two strangers recognizing something they couldn't name. Everything since had been footnotes.

"The sign looks good," Talia said, changing course. "Dev hung it straight this time."

"He measured it twice."

"He always measures twice. It's very calming. Like watching someone fold fitted sheets correctly."

We sat in the quiet a moment. Outside, the light was doing what the light did here in late afternoon — going gold and slow, pooling in the yard where the demolition notice used to be nailed to the fence post. Dev had framed the notice itself, actually, and hung it in the back hall with a small placard: *Exhibit A: What Almost Happened.* Talia had rolled her eyes. Dev had pretended not to see. These were also rhythms we were learning.

"Talia," I said, before I could stop myself, which is how most true things left my mouth these days. "Do you want to learn?"

Her head tilted. "Learn what."

"Piano. You sit next to them all day. You never touch them."

For a long moment she looked at the keyboard the way she must have looked at the photograph I'd left on her porch — evidence of a woman who chose music, the deathbed sentence she'd carried for thirty years. Then she shook her head, once, firm.

"Not yet."

"Not no."

"Not no," she agreed, and stood up to go make tea, and the flat-whistling kettle sang its wrong note, and neither of us mentioned it, and that felt like progress.

* * *

*Talia*

Here is what nobody tells you about finding family as an adult: there is no script. With patients' families, I always knew my role. I was the calm one, the translator between grief and medicine, the person who explained what dying looked like so it stopped looking like failure. I had language for all of it.

I had no language for *my sister plays Chopin when she thinks I'm not listening.*

Because she does. I've heard her. Evenings, after I go upstairs to the blue room — my room, the one that faces the ocean side, the one with Marguerite's old quilt that Emrys swears she doesn't mind me using and I swear I don't mind using — I hear the piano start downstairs, soft, ten or fifteen minutes, never longer. She thinks I'm asleep. Or she thinks I'm polite enough to pretend I'm asleep. Either way, I lie there under the quilt and listen to my mother's house fill up with the thing my mother chose over me, and here is the strange part, the part I haven't said out loud:

It doesn't hurt anymore. Mostly.

Some nights it hurts a little. I'm not a saint. I'm a hospice nurse who took leave from hospice work to live in her birth mother's house with a sister I met through a photograph on a porch, and anyone who claims that arrangement comes pain-free is selling something.

But mostly it sounds like weather. Like rain on a roof you're safe under.

Thursday morning I came down early to find Emrys already at the big piano with a student — Marcus, retired mail carrier, sixty-eight, learning his first scale with the concentration of a man defusing a bomb.

"No, flatter," Emrys was saying. "Your thumb wants to celebrate. Don't let it celebrate."

"My thumb has earned a little celebration," Marcus said.

"Your thumb has earned nothing. Again."

I put coffee on and listened to them work through four bars of something simple, over and over, and watched Emrys do the thing she did with beginners that I suspected she'd never done with prodigies: she let them be bad. Cheerfully bad. Bad without consequence. Every time Marcus stumbled she said "good, again" like stumbling was the whole assignment, and maybe it was.

When he left, she came into the kitchen rubbing her hands like she'd been out in cold air.

"He's getting it," she said. "Slowly. He'll play a whole piece by Christmas."

"You love this."

"I tolerate it enormously."

"You love it. Your face does a thing."

"My face does not do a thing."

"Your face absolutely does a thing. It goes soft. Like bread."

She stared at me. I stared back. This was new territory — teasing — and we both knew it, and neither of us retreated, and I felt something shift in my chest that I decided not to examine until later, possibly never.

"There's mail," she said finally, retreating to safer ground. "From the conservatory. And a postcard from Dev. He says the archive's been cited in a journal article. He enclosed the citation. Of course he enclosed the citation."

"Of course he did." Dev Anand, archivist, occasional visitor, man who measured picture frames twice and once spent forty minutes explaining acid-free folders to me with genuine passion. He'd driven down last weekend with a box of duplicates from the Voss archive and stayed for dinner, and afterward Emrys had said, "He's coming back in December," in a voice I recognized from decades of nursing — the voice of a person announcing a feeling while pretending it's logistics.

I'd said nothing. Sisters don't rat each other out that fast. We'd only just started.

"Also," Emrys said, and hesitated, and I braced, because her hesitations always preceded either tenderness or catastrophe and they sounded identical. "There's an envelope here with your name on it. Oregon postmark. I didn't open it."

My name. Handwritten. I took it and knew the handwriting before I read the return address: Rosa Delgado, my supervisor, the one with the burnout articles.

Emrys watched my face. "Bad news?"

"Rosa wants to know when I'm coming back." I set the envelope down. "She's not pushing. She's just asking. There's a difference, and she knows there's a difference, and she's giving me room to answer honestly."

"And can you?"

I looked out the kitchen window at the yard, at the fence post, at the studio sign Dev had hung straight. Somewhere inside me, eleven years of holding other people's hands at the end turned over like a sleeper.

"I don't know yet," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said in weeks, and Emrys nodded like that was an acceptable answer, which maybe it was, here, in this house, where we were apparently building something out of acceptable answers and unplayed scales and a radiator that sang B-flat.

*Emrys*

On Sunday it rained, the first real storm of the season, and no students came, and Talia and I ended up in the attic because the roof leaked over the northeast corner and someone had to move the boxes.

"Someone," Talia clarified, hauling a crate labeled *XMAS — DO NOT OPEN BEFORE THANKSGIVING (M.V.)* toward dry ground, "is defined as both of us, equally, because we're partners in this disaster."

"We're co-signers of a disaster. It's different. Legally distinct."

"Is it?"

"I have no idea. Dev would know."

"Everything's Dev would know with you lately."

I nearly dropped my end of the crate. She said it lightly, tossing it over her shoulder like loose change, already moving to the next box, and I stood there in the dim attic with rain drumming the roof and understood that I had just been seen — fully, accurately, by the person with the least history of seeing me — and that she wasn't even going to wait around for my reaction.

"Talia."

"Mm?" She didn't turn around.

"When did you figure that out?"

"Figure what out?" But her shoulders had gone careful, the way they did when a patient's family asked the question they weren't ready to have answered.

"You know what."

She set down the box. Turned. Rain-light came through the small round window and made her face half silver. "Week three," she said. "You reorganized the whole filing cabinet the day before he visited and then pretended you hadn't. Nobody alphabetizes by accident, Emrys."

"I alphabetize recreationally."

"Sure you do."

We stood there. Downstairs, the radiator clanked its flat B-flat, and the rain came harder, and I thought about how for twenty years I had catalogued silences, kept inventories of unsaid things, treated speech like a currency I couldn't afford — and how lately the words just fell out of me around this woman, clumsy and unvetted, like coins from a torn pocket.

"He makes me nervous," I admitted. "He reads things. He notices. He noticed I was Fairweather from a program in 1996 and he just... held onto it, politely, for months, like a coat check ticket."

"That's horrifying," Talia said. "Being perceived."

"Completely unbearable."

"And yet."

"And yet," I said, and picked up my end of the crate, and we carried it down the stairs together, matching steps without discussing it, the way you do when you've shared a house long enough that your bodies have negotiated things your mouths haven't.

At the bottom she paused. "For what it's worth," she said, not looking at me, addressing the banister, "he measures twice because he cares about things staying up. Walls, frames, whatever else." A beat. "People."

Then she went to make lunch, leaving me in the hallway with the rain and the crate and my heart doing something unscheduled.

* * *

*Talia*

The first snow came in December, early and wet, melting on contact, and with it came the evening I finally touched a piano.

It wasn't planned. Emrys had gone to the conservatory for some board meeting — she went to those now, sat at the table, spoke her piece, came home exhausted but unbent, which still struck me as miraculous, like watching someone walk on a leg that had been broken for years. I was alone in the studio closing up, stacking music, turning off lamps, and the grand was just there, lid open, keys catching the last gray light.

I want to be clear about what I felt standing in front of it: not longing, exactly. Something older and more complicated. Every piano in my childhood had been a closed door. My adoptive mother had sold ours when I was nine, after the divorce, and never explained why, and I'd built a whole architecture of meaning around that absence — *music was the thing that got taken; music was the thing she chose over me; music was not for our family* — and now here I was, forty-nine years old, standing in my birth mother's house in front of her actual piano, and the architecture was rubble, and I didn't know what to build instead.

I pressed one key. Middle C, probably. I wouldn't know.

It rang out into the empty room, round and plain and honest, and nothing terrible happened. No ghost appeared. No verdict descended. It was just a note, hanging in the air of a house that had learned to hold hard notes.

I pressed another. Then, hunting, the one next to it.

That's how Emrys found me twenty minutes later — standing at the piano picking out a broken, wandering line of single notes, coat still on, snow melting in my hair — and here is the part I will tell almost no one: she didn't say anything. Didn't startle me, didn't announce herself, didn't make it A Moment. She just came and sat down beside me on the bench, close enough that our shoulders touched, and played the same three notes back, slowly, so I could find them again.

"Again," she said softly, in the voice I'd heard her use with Marcus. The voice that meant stumbling was the assignment.

I played them again. Wronger, then less wrong.

"Good," she said. "Again."

Outside, the snow kept falling and failing to stick, and the radiator sang its flat note, and somewhere in the walls the old house settled around us like a body relaxing, and I thought: *this is what she never gave me, and this is what she left me anyway.* Not the lessons. Not the legacy. Just this — a sister who says *again* instead of *why didn't you sooner*, a bench wide enough for two, a beginning with no guarantees attached.

We played three notes, over and over, for a long time.

Then Emrys glanced at the clock and winced. "So," she said, in the tone of a person about to ruin a perfectly good evening. "Dev called. From the conservatory. The board meeting ran long, and there's — apparently there's news. About the east wing funding. He wants us both there tomorrow."

I looked at her face, at the worry moving behind it like weather.

"What kind of news?"

"The kind," she said, "that he wouldn't explain over the phone."
