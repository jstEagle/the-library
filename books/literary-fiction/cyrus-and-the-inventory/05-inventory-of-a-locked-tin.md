## 5. Inventory of a Locked Tin

The tin was the color of a winter sky, if the sky had been asked to apologize for something. Biscuits, once. Some brand my grandmother bought on Sundays and pretended was a luxury. The lid stuck the way old lids do, with a small sucking sound, like the tin taking a breath before confessing.

I sat on the concrete floor of the storage unit with my coat still on, because taking it off would have meant admitting I planned to stay a while. The hospital bracelet lay beside me on the flattened cardboard that had once held my grandmother's good dishes. *Baby Boy Iversen* — no. Not Iversen. That was the point. The bracelet said a name I'd heard exactly once before, spoken by a stranger at my mother's grave, and it said it in the small stamped letters of a hospital that had closed before I was born.

I had driven here straight from the cemetery, or near enough. Two days after. Long enough to be respectable, short enough to still be angry, and I was angry in a way that had nowhere to put itself, which is the worst kind, the kind that turns on the furniture, on the dog, on yourself.

The tin was heavy in the way paper is heavy when there's a lot of it. I lifted the lid.

Letters. Dozens of them. Envelopes without stamps, sealed and never sent, each one addressed in my grandmother's upright hand to a name that was not the name anyone in my family had ever spoken. The oldest envelope had gone soft at the corners, foxed with age, the ink of the address faded to the color of weak tea. The newest looked like it had been written last month, though my grandmother had been dead eleven years, so no. The newest was from the last year of her life, and the handwriting had gone thin and careful, the hand of a woman conserving her strength for the important work.

Thirty years of letters. To a son. A son who had stood in front of me at my mother's funeral holding a photograph like it was his only document, his only proof, and who had said the name like a password he'd carried for fifty years in case anyone ever asked.

I should have burned them. I want to be honest about that. There was a moment — I remember it precisely, the smell of dust and cold rubber in that unit, my breath fogging — when I genuinely considered walking out and leaving the tin shut, letting the past stay packed the way we'd packed it, in boxes marked *KITCHEN* and *BOOKS* and *DO NOT OPEN*, which was my mother's labeling system for everything that hurt.

Instead I opened the first one.

---

*My darling boy,*

*You are four months old today, though of course you don't know it, and I don't know it either, exactly, I am counting from the date they told me and I count it every month the way other women count rent.*

*The couple who took you are named Halvorsen. The social worker told me that much and no more, and I have decided to be grateful for it, because a name is a thing you can hold. I say it to myself at night. Halvorsen. It sounds like a place where it snows. I hope it snows where you are. I hope you are warm.*

*I want to tell you why, and I find I cannot, not yet, not on paper that might outlive my courage. What I can tell you is that it was not because I didn't—*

The letter stopped there. Mid-sentence. Mid-apology, I would come to learn, because they all stopped there, or somewhere like there, and the envelope was sealed anyway, as if my grandmother had decided that an unfinished apology, sealed and addressed, was still worth the postage of intention.

I opened the second. 1964. The third, 1966. I opened them the way you open a door you know is going to hit you, flinch first, read second.

*You would be six today. I baked a cake. I don't know why I baked a cake, you weren't here to eat it, your mother ate it, that is, I ate it, I am still not used to being only one person.*

*I saw a boy at the market with your same cowlick. I followed him down two aisles before I understood what I was doing. The boy's mother found me and asked if I needed help and I said no, madam, thank you, and I have never told a bigger lie in a smaller number of words.*

*I am writing to tell you that I think of you every—*

Sealed. Unsent.

* * *

By midnight I had read nineteen of them and the storage unit had gone from cold to that particular cold that gets into your bones and stays, and I had not moved except to open envelopes, and my knees ached, and I did not care. I read them in order because I am my grandmother's granddaughter and order is how we do grief, alphabetized, catalogued, under control.

The letters changed as the years changed. The early ones were desperate, wet with it, the ink actually blurred in places where I had to admit to myself what the blurring was. Then the seventies came and the letters got practical, which was worse. She wrote him lists. She wrote him the family recipes. She wrote him how to fix a carburetor and how to fold a fitted sheet and what to say when a girl's father answered the phone, and every one of these letters ended the same way, mid-apology, mid-reach, a hand extended into the dark and then withdrawn.

*I have enclosed the recipe for the lemon cake though I have nothing to enclose it in because this letter will not—*

*Your grandfather — not your grandfather, the man I married, I must stop doing that — the man I married says I should stop writing these. He says a letter you don't send is a wound you keep picking. He is not wrong. He is also not the one who—*

I laughed at that one. Alone, on a concrete floor, at two in the morning, I laughed, and it came out wrong, more like a cough, and after it I had to sit with my head against the tin for a while.

Because here is what I could not get around, what I kept trying to get around like a truck trying to pass on a hill with no room: she had thirty years. Thirty years of Sundays, of birthdays, of the exact date each month. She had the name Halvorsen like a stone in her shoe and she had paper and she had ink and she had, God help her, a mailbox at the end of a walkway she walked every day of her life, and she never once, not once in thirty years, put one of these letters in it.

I knew about not sending things. I had a shoebox of my own, didn't I. I had a decade of postcards addressed to a burned house, postcards I paid postage on, mind you — I paid real money to a real postal service to carry my words to a pile of ash and a lot where weeds had opinions — and I had told myself for ten years that this was different, that this was a ritual, that this was grief with good posture. Sitting on that floor reading her handwriting I understood, with the slow sick drop of a floor giving way, that it was the same thing. It was exactly the same thing. She had her tin and I had my shoebox and we were both mailing our love to addresses that could not receive it, and calling it peace.

I was furious at her. I want that on the record. Furious the way you're only furious at the dead, which is to say completely, with no possibility of resolution, an anger with no exit. She had lied to us — by omission, by that great soft blanket of silence families weave and then suffocate under — she had let my mother grow up as an only child, let me grow up believing the family was three people and a fire. She had a son. She had a son and she wrote to him like a woman praying to a saint she was too proud to name aloud, and then she sealed it and put it in a biscuit tin and lived her whole visible life as if the tin were empty.

And underneath the fury, in the basement of it, where I keep the things I don't say out loud even to myself in the shower: I wanted to forgive her. That was the humiliating part. I sat there at three in the morning wanting to forgive a woman who had been dead eleven years, wanting it like thirst, and I couldn't figure out what the wanting was for, since she wasn't there to receive it, and forgiveness with no one to receive it is just — what? More unsent mail.

* * *

The last letter was dated eight days before she died. Her hand had gone from careful to monumental, each letter a small act of engineering.

*My darling boy, I am told I should make arrangements and I have made them, all but one. The tin goes to your sister — that is, to my daughter, that is, to Ruth, who does not know you exist, and perhaps that is a cowardice and perhaps it is a gift, I have run out of time to tell the difference.*

*If you are reading this, then someone opened it who should not have, or the world has grown kinder than I planned for. Either way: I looked for you. I want that written down somewhere. In 1971 I found the agency and I wrote and they wrote back and they said you were settled and happy and that contact would be a disruption, and I believed them, because believing them meant I could stop, and stopping was the only thing that let me get up in the mornings and be a mother to the child I still—*

It ended there. Of course it ended there. Eight days left in her life and she still couldn't finish the sentence.

But I sat very still on the floor of the storage unit, and the cold came up through my coat, and I read the sentence again. *I looked for you.* In 1971. The agency said no. She had asked. She had actually, once, in the middle of all that sealing and shelving, picked up the telephone of the world and asked, and been turned away, and had gone home and written a letter about it and sealed it and put it in the tin.

Which meant the story I had inherited — the story of the woman who gave a baby away and never looked back, the story that made the fire almost bearable, that made our smallness feel chosen — that story was wrong. Or not wrong. Incomplete. It had a hidden room, and I was sitting in it, and the room was full of mail.

I thought about Cyrus. Standing at the grave with his photograph and his terrible calm, saying the name like he'd practiced it in the car, which he obviously had. He wanted the truth of why. He'd said it plainly, no drama, a man with a terminal diagnosis asking for one document: the reason. And I had given him nothing, taken the photograph, and watched him walk away the way you watch someone carry something heavy down a long road.

The tin held the reason. Thirty years of reasons, all of them unfinished.

It was nearly five in the morning when I finally stood up, knees screaming, letters repacked in their order, the tin under my arm like something rescued from a fire. Which, I suppose, it was.

I drove home through streets going pale at the edges, and I did not decide anything, because I am not a woman who decides anything before coffee, but when I got to my kitchen I set the tin on the table instead of in a drawer, which for me is practically a press release.

And then I noticed it, sitting on my counter where I'd dropped it two days ago with my keys: the photograph. Three children on a porch. My mother, squinting. A boy with a cowlick. And on the back, in my grandmother's hand — a hand I now knew better than I had ever known it in life — the same upright letters, and beneath the names, one line I had not been able to read at the cemetery and could read now, in my own kitchen, in the first gray light:

*If you find them, tell them I—*
