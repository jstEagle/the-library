## 7. Postcards, Undelivered

Cyrus found the postcards on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day things fall apart in my experience — never Fridays, when you've braced for it, but Tuesdays, when you're just trying to get the recycling out.

He'd been living in my spare room for three weeks by then, long enough to have opinions about my coffee and none about my life, which was the arrangement we'd signed, metaphorically, in blood. He was better than advertised as a houseguest. He washed his own dishes. He laughed at the right moments of my jokes and let the wrong ones die with dignity. His illness sat in the house like a large piece of furniture nobody mentioned — we'd arranged our paths around it so smoothly that sometimes I forgot it was there until I bumped into it, usually at night, hearing him cough through the wall.

I was at the shop where I freelance-copyedit cookbooks when he called.

"Mara," he said. "I have a question, and I want you to know I'm asking it with love."

"That's how all terrible questions introduce themselves."

"I was looking for the vacuum bags."

"And?"

"And I found them. In the hall closet. Behind approximately four hundred postcards."

The kitchen went very quiet around me. Or maybe that was me.

"They're organized," he continued, because Cyrus had never met a silence he wouldn't fill with more rope to hang himself. "By year. There are rubber bands. There's a system, Mara. You have a filing system for—"

"Don't," I said.

"—for mail addressed to a house that burned down eleven years ago."

I hung up on him. Then I drove home anyway, because apparently hanging up on a dying man is only effective if you then go home and confront him, which tells you something about me that I'd rather not examine.

* * *

He was sitting at my kitchen table when I got there, the shoebox open in front of him like an autopsy in progress. He'd laid a few postcards out flat, face up, the way you'd handle evidence or butterflies. My handwriting stared back at me from a decade's worth of glossy fronts: lighthouses, sunflowers, one truly regrettable ceramic frog from a rest stop outside Reno.

"You went through my closet," I said.

"I went through your closet looking for vacuum bags," he said. "The going-through was incidental. The finding was—" He paused, choosing the word with the care of a man who had learned, somewhere in thirty years of being given away, that words were the only luggage that couldn't be confiscated. "Inevitable? You keep them in alphabetical order by year."

"Chronological order."

"See, that's worse."

I took the chair across from him. I did not take the postcards. "Say whatever you're going to say."

"Okay." He turned one over — the lighthouse, from 2019, Maine, though I'd never been to Maine; I'd bought the postcards in bulk from a museum gift shop online, which felt like a confession in itself. "Grandma," he read. "The weather here is fine. Thought of you today. Love, Mara."

"That's not the incriminating part."

"No," he agreed. "The incriminating part is that there are a hundred and twelve of these, and they all say some version of the same thing. *Thought of you today.*" He looked up. His eyes were doing something I hadn't seen before — not pity, thank God. Pity I could have thrown him out over. This was closer to recognition, which is much harder to survive. "You've been writing to her for ten years."

"To the house," I said. "Not to her. She was already gone before the first one."

"The house isn't reading them either, Mara."

"The house kept her. That's different."

He nodded slowly, and I braced for the therapy voice, the gentle-voiced man about to explain my grief back to me like a lecture I hadn't paid for. Instead he said: "The address still works?"

"What?"

"The address. Number fourteen Alder Lane. Does the mail still go?" He tapped the box. "Because if the postal service has been accepting these for a decade, someone at that office knows you. Someone reads 'the Yarrow house' and puts it in a drawer, or a bin, or — God — delivers it to whoever lives on the lot now, and they have a drawer full of your grief, and honestly that's the most interesting thing I've heard all year."

"It goes to a dead-letter office," I said. "I looked it up once. Undeliverable mail gets held, then destroyed. Six months."

"So every six months, the government incinerates your letters to your grandmother."

"When you put it that way—"

"Somebody should put it that way. Somebody should have put it that way nine years ago." But he wasn't laughing. He turned the lighthouse card over again, read it once more, and set it down with a gentleness that made my throat hurt. "Why 'thought of you today'? Why so small?"

"Because anything bigger would be a lie," I said, before I could stop myself. "Anything bigger — *I miss you, I'm sorry, I should have called that night* — anything bigger turns into a letter you can't finish. Believe me. I know about letters you can't finish. I grew up in a house full of them."

That landed between us the way things land between us now: heavily, but without breaking anything. We'd learned each other's load-bearing walls.

"She didn't call that night either," Cyrus said quietly. "The night of the fire. In case you were keeping score. She told me once — no. She wrote me once, one of the ones in the tin. She said she'd meant to check the wiring for six years."

"We're not doing this."

"We're absolutely not doing this," he agreed. "We're doing something else. Hand me a pen."

"Why?"

"Because you're going to write today's postcard, and I'm going to help, and you're going to hate it, and then it's going to be done." He said it the way he said most things now — with the particular authority of a man whose deadline made everyone else's negotiable. "Come on. Ten years of *thought of you today*. Don't you want to tell her one true thing before the dead-letter office does its job?"

* * *

Here is what I should have said: *No. Get out of my ritual. It's mine. I built it out of the wreckage and I maintain it the way you maintain anything you built out of wreckage — badly, daily, and with tremendous secrecy.*

Here is what I actually said: "Fine. But I pick the postcard."

I picked the worst one. A blank white card, no picture at all, purchased in a moment of ambition years ago and never used. Blank fronts are for people who believe they'll eventually have something worth saying that doesn't need a sunset behind it. I'd been avoiding it for eight years.

Cyrus uncapped his pen — his own pen, a fat black fountain thing he carried everywhere, the one extravagance of a man who owned six shirts — and slid it across the table handle-first, like a duelist offering me the choice of weapons.

"Start with the date," he said.

"I know how to start a postcard, Cyrus. I've written a hundred and thirteen of them."

"A hundred and twelve," he said. "This one counts double. It's the first honest one."

I wrote the date. My hand did its familiar loop-de-loop, the handwriting of a girl who'd won a penmanship ribbon in fourth grade and had been coasting on it ever since. Then I sat there.

"Dear Grandma," I tried.

"Too far away," Cyrus said immediately. "She raised you. You slept at her house four nights a week until you were eleven. Start with what you'd actually call her."

"Nana."

"There you go." He leaned back, arms crossed, insufferably pleased. "Nana."

*Nana.*

The word sat on the card like a bird that had landed and might leave. I hadn't written it in eleven years. Everyone else in my life had been edited down to Mom, to Mara, to the careful present tense of a woman who curates her past the way museums curate — gloves on, nothing touched, everything labeled. Nana belonged to the burned house. Nana belonged to the smoke.

"I used to think," I said, not writing yet, "that if I stopped sending them, it would mean she was really gone. Like the mail was proof of life. Mine, I mean. As long as I was writing to her, some version of us was still — operating."

"And now?"

"Now I think I've been mailing letters to a locked room and calling it a relationship."

Cyrus didn't say *good* or *insightful* or any of the words a lesser stranger would have reached for. He said, "Tell her about the funeral."

"Cyrus."

"Tell her about her daughter's funeral. Tell her you wore the gray coat she hated. Tell her forty-one people came and half of them were coworkers who thought it was a retirement party." His voice had gone soft, but not fragile-soft — deliberate soft, the softness of a scalpel. "You stood at that grave performing a woman you invented, Mara. I watched you do it. I've spent my whole life watching people perform families. You're good. You're very good. And she would have seen through it in four seconds, because she saw through everybody, and then she would have made you a sandwich and told you the truth."

My eyes were doing something embarrassing. I let them, mostly, and blamed the onions I had not cut.

*I wore the gray coat,* I wrote. *The one you hated. You'd have hated it at the funeral too. Forty-one people came. Half of them thought it was a work event. Nobody cried except a man named Cyrus, whom you also never met, though it turns out you knew him longer than anyone.*

Cyrus read it upside down, which took him a while, and when he finished he didn't say anything for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice had a crack in it he didn't bother hiding.

"You're putting me in the postcard."

"You're in the story now. That's what happens when you break into closets."

"I was looking for vacuum bags."

"You were snooping."

"I was snooping," he conceded, "with a medical excuse."

I kept writing. It came easier after that, the way water comes easier once you've broken the ice on the well — not warm, not kind, but moving. I told her about the storage unit and the tin. I told her I'd read every letter, including the ones that ended mid-sentence, mid-apology, the pen evidently lifted from the page by whatever she couldn't say in thirty years of trying. I told her I was furious at her. I told her fury, I was learning, was just love that had run out of places to knock.

And then, because Cyrus was watching me with those patient, rationed-out days of his, I wrote the sentence I'd been circling for a decade:

*I don't forgive you yet. But I've stopped pretending I don't want to.*

I signed it. Not *love* — not yet, that word was still in the shop being repaired — but *Mara*, plain, unarmored, the whole name, no smile attached.

Cyrus took the card when I offered it, read it front to back twice, and then did something I will remember for exactly as long as I live, however long the universe grants me the privilege: he licked the stamp and pressed it on, crooked, and said, "There. Now it's real. Now it can be destroyed by the federal government like all your other honest mail."

I laughed. Actually laughed, ugly and surprised, in my own kitchen, at my own grief, with a dying stranger holding my postcard like it was a live grenade he'd defused with a fountain pen.

"I'm mailing this one tomorrow," I said. "From the actual post office. With a witness."

"I'll drive," he said. "I like errands. Errands make me feel like a person with a future."

That shut me up for a second. He noticed it shutting me up, and smiled the small, crooked smile he deployed when he wanted to prove he could still surprise people.

Then he said, casually, the way you mention the weather: "Oh — while we're confessing things. There's a letter in my coat pocket I need you to deliver someday. Not today. But soon. And Mara?" He tapped the shoebox, gently, once. "It's not one of hers. It's one of mine."

He left the kitchen before I could ask, which was becoming his signature move, and I sat there a long time among a decade of undelivered postcards, holding a pen that wasn't mine, understanding that the ritual had just loosened its grip — and that something else, something with a coat pocket and a deadline, was tightening its own.
