## 8. Cyrus Comes Home

The trouble with building things, Cyrus Reyes had learned, was that everything you built could be taken apart. A house was just a promise made out of lumber, and a promise was just a sentence someone hadn't finished yet. He'd spent nine years inside a sentence somebody else had finished for him — *Cyrus Thackeray, arson, nine years* — and now that he was out, two years out, he had developed a professional and personal preference for joints that held, for glue that cured, for doors that swung on hinges he had hung himself with his own two hands and could account for down to the screw.

He was sanding a stair rail in the garage he'd converted into a workshop when the song came on.

Later, he would try to reconstruct the moment — the radio, the hour, the light coming through the window in that particular late-October slant that made sawdust look like something holy — and he would fail, the way you fail to reconstruct the exact shape of a blow. What he remembered was this: his hands stopped. Not slowed. *Stopped*, the way a clock stops, all at once, everywhere.

*"...and that was a rare one, folks, a demo out of the Kestrel Music archive — 'Cyrus's Lament,' written by Dell Harrow, recorded 2003, never officially released..."*

Cyrus stood in his own garage, in his own town, forty miles from Marrow Bend, and listened to his mother sing.

She'd written it the winter he was fourteen. He knew this the way he knew the grain of white oak — not because anyone had told him, but because he'd been there, in the kitchen, doing homework he was pretending to do while she sat at the table with a legal pad and a pencil worn down to a stub, humming, stopping, humming again. She'd written forty songs like that over the years, on legal pads, on the backs of envelopes, once on the inside of a cereal box, and she had never recorded a single one of them. When he'd asked her why — he was fourteen, he asked everything why — she'd said, "Because a song is a door, baby. You don't open it until you know what's on the other side."

He hadn't known what that meant. He was fourteen. He'd thought it was one of those things mothers said instead of answering.

The song was called "Cyrus's Lament" because it was about him. That was the part that made his chest do what it was doing now, which was nothing, which was the dangerous kind of nothing, the held-breath kind. It was a ballad about a boy who left home in the dark and the mother who watched him go from the window, and it was beautiful, and it was hers, and the man on the radio had just said *written by Dell Harrow* in the easy, untroubled voice of a man reading off a cue card, and Cyrus's hands, which could plane a board flat to within a hair's width, had stopped working entirely.

The song ended. The DJ said something about Kestrel Music's reissue series, "preserving the legacy of America's unsung songwriters," and Cyrus thought, with a clarity that arrived like cold water: *They didn't even change the title. They didn't even have the decency to change the title.*

Then he sat down on the stairs he'd built, in the house he'd built, and put his head in his hands, and stayed there a long time.

* * *

People assumed prison taught you hardness. What it actually taught you, in Cyrus's experience, was inventory. You learned to count what you had — a bunk, a tray, a window with a view of a wall, a mother's voice in your head singing a song she never let anyone else hear. And you learned, above all, to count what you'd lost, because the counting was the only thing they couldn't take away from you. They could take the visit. They could lose the letters. They could let nine years go by without a single person from your family standing on the other side of the glass. But they couldn't stop you from counting.

He'd counted for nine years. Then he'd stopped.

The stopping had happened around year six, and it hadn't been a decision so much as a surrender, the way a man stops waiting for a ship. Tamsin had come exactly twice. Once in the first year, when Tamsin had cried so hard the guard had nearly ended the visit, and once in the third, when Tamsin had sat down, opened their mouth, and said, "Cyrus, I—" and then the buzzer had gone and the visit was over and Cyrus had laughed, actually laughed, walking back to his cell, because of course. Of course. Even the universe couldn't get Tamsin Thackeray to finish a sentence.

His mother had never come at all. He'd told himself for years that it was the money, the distance, the shame — pick one, they all fit. Then the letters from her stopped, and the letters *about* her started arriving from a care facility in a town he'd never heard of, and a social worker with kind eyes explained, gently, the word *dementia*, and Cyrus had sat in the visitation room alone and done the math on a life.

Here was the thing nobody tells you about being the one who took the fall: it doesn't make you noble. It makes you a man in a small room with a lot of time and one question, and the question is *why didn't they fight for me*, and there is no answer that survives nine years of turning it over. So you stop turning it over. You put it down. You pick up a trade. You learn that wood doesn't lie to you, that a board is what it is, that if a joint fails it fails for a reason you can find and fix. You build a small house with your parole money and your parolee's wages, and you live in it alone, and you tell yourself this is peace, and most mornings you almost believe it.

And then a radio plays a song your mother wrote in a kitchen that doesn't exist anymore, under a stranger's name, on a program sponsored by the man who — Cyrus had never said this out loud, had never even finished the thought in his own head, but the thought was there, it had been there since the night of the fire, patient as rot — the man who burned it down.

* * *

He didn't sleep that night. He did what he always did with a problem he couldn't solve: he made coffee and stood at his workbench and looked at the wood.

The wood didn't have answers. The wood never did. But there was a rhythm to it, a conversation between grain and blade, and somewhere around three in the morning, planing a piece of cherry he had no project for, Cyrus arrived at the fact he'd been circling: the song was a door. His mother had said so. *You don't open it until you know what's on the other side.*

Someone had opened it. Someone had walked through with a wheelbarrow and taken everything.

And there was a second fact, arriving on its heels, less welcome: the song was called *Cyrus's Lament*, and Cyrus Thackeray was a man whose name had been in the newspaper, whose name a certain kind of person might remember, and if a radio DJ could pull that recording out of an archive and say it out loud to a whole listening area at midnight, then other people could hear it too. People like Tamsin. Tamsin, who had once told him — in one of the two visits, the first one, through tears — *"She sang it for you, Cyrus. She never sang it for anyone else. She sang it for you."*

He set down the plane. He looked at his hands. They had sawdust in every crease, and they were steady now, which was its own kind of information.

* * *

The next morning he drove to Marrow Bend for the first time in eleven years.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. He was good at telling himself things; he'd had practice. But the truth was simpler and he knew it: a man doesn't drive forty miles to look at a burned lot unless the burned lot is the door, and the door is open, and something on the other side is calling him by name.

The lot looked smaller than grief remembered it. That was the first thing. The second thing was that someone had kept it — not mowed, exactly, but kept, the weeds beaten back from the foundation stones like a held breath. The chimney still stood. Chimneys always stood; fire was a polite destroyer that way, it took the rooms and left the throat that had once breathed for them. Somebody had left flowers at the base of it, once, long enough ago that the petals had become a stain.

Cyrus stood at the curb with his hands in his jacket pockets and did not cry. He'd done his crying in a cell in 2014 and he considered the account closed.

"You're him," said a voice. "Aren't you."

An old woman was standing on the porch of the house next door, which had been the Adeyemi house, which meant the woman was probably Mrs. Adeyemi, which meant she had been watching him since the moment he parked, which meant — Cyrus had learned this much about small towns, and prison had confirmed it — that everything he did next was already being narrated to somebody.

"I'm sorry, ma'am?"

"Cyrus Thackeray." She said it the way you'd read a name off a headstone. "You've got her chin. Your mama's chin. I'd know it anywhere, and I'd know it double on a face that's been away."

"I live over in Harrow Creek now," he said, which was not a denial, because he was done with denials; he'd served his sentence for one already. "I do carpentry."

"I know what you do." She came down her porch steps with the unhurried authority of weather. "You know what I do? I watch that lot. Eleven years. And do you know who else watches that lot?"

"Ma'am—"

"Your mama watched it. From the home, whenever they'd drive her past. She'd go quiet — well, quieter — and she'd put her hand flat on the window glass, like this." Mrs. Adeyemi put her own hand flat against the air, and Cyrus felt it land somewhere under his ribs. "Flat, like she was feeling for heat. Eleven years, and then two years ago they stopped driving her past, and do you know why?"

"I expect you're going to tell me."

"Because she started saying a name when they did." Mrs. Adeyemi looked at him with eyes that had seen the fire and had not, he understood suddenly, looked away from anything since. "She started saying *Roland*. Just that. *Roland, Roland.* And the people from the home got nervous, and the visits stopped, and I have been an old woman with a phone for two years, Mr. Thackeray, waiting for somebody in your family to come stand on this curb so I could tell them what I saw."

The wind came down the street the way it always had, off the bend in the river, smelling of water and cold. Cyrus looked at the chimney. He thought about doors, and what his mother had said about them, and how you don't open one until you know what's on the other side.

"What did you see, Mrs. Adeyemi?"

She glanced up and down the street — a habit, he realized, eleven years old, a habit from the night of the fire — and when she spoke again her voice had dropped, and everything in Cyrus that had spent nine years learning to be still went very still indeed.

"First," she said, "you're going to want to sit down. And second — and I want you to hear this part before anything else, because I've been rehearsing it on that porch for two years — your mama didn't stop talking because her mind went soft. She stopped talking because she made a trade. And the thing she bought with it—" Mrs. Adeyemi's chin, Iris Thackeray's chin, lifted. "—was you."

* * *

He drove back to Harrow Creek with the windows down and the heater on, which made no sense and was exactly right. At a red light outside a gas station he caught himself humming. Three bars. The bridge of her song, the part where the boy in the ballad looks back at the window and the window is empty, and Cyrus had to pull over into the gas station lot and put his forehead on the steering wheel and let the light change behind him twice while an old pickup full of teenagers honked and he did not care.

Because here was the arithmetic, and he was a man who counted: his mother had gone silent to keep him alive. Tamsin had gone silent — Tamsin, who had stood in that police station at nineteen with the truth in their mouth and swallowed it — and Cyrus had spent nine years assuming the silence was cowardice, or worse, indifference. But Mrs. Adeyemi had said *trade*, and the word had gone into him like a chisel into a knot, and he could not stop turning it. What if the silence had never been empty? What if it had been full the whole time — full of a bargain, full of a price, full of a mother's hand flat against a window, feeling for heat?

It didn't forgive anybody. He wanted that on the record, in his own head, where he kept his records. It didn't forgive Tamsin, and it didn't forgive him for the letters he'd stopped answering in year seven out of pure self-protection, and it didn't forgive a world that let a woman buy her son's life with her own voice. But it reframed the lumber, and Cyrus was a carpenter, and a carpenter knows that half of every job is looking at the material you actually have instead of the material you ordered.

He got home after dark. There was a message on his answering machine — he kept an answering machine the way other men kept grudges — and he played it while he took off his boots.

"Cyrus." A pause long enough to hold a whole childhood. Tamsin's voice, lower than he remembered, rougher, but underneath it, unmistakably, the same voice that had said *Cyrus, I—* across a visitation table seven years ago. "It's me. I don't — I know you don't want to hear from me. I wouldn't blame you. But there's a song on the radio, and it's Mama's, and I think — I think somebody stole it, Cyrus, and I think I know who, and I think—" Another pause. A breath that sounded like it had been rehearsed and still hadn't gone to plan. "I'm going to be outside Mama's home Sunday morning. Nine o'clock. I'll be there whether you come or not. I'll be there either way."

A click. Then, because it was Tamsin, because it would always be Tamsin, the machine caught the last mutter before the phone went down: *"God. 'I'll be there either way.' Smooth, Thackeray. Real smooth."*

Cyrus stood in his kitchen in his socks and laughed — one short, cracked, involuntary note that surprised him so much he had to put a hand on the counter.

Then he looked at the machine for a long time, the way you look at a board before the first cut, knowing that everything after it follows from everything before it, and that there is no un-cutting a board.

Sunday was four days away.

He picked up a pencil — a stub, worn down, the same kind she had used — and wrote on a scrap of cherry plywood, in a carpenter's square hand: *Sunday. 9 a.m. Ask her why.* And underneath, after a while, after the coffee went cold: *And this time, listen to the answer.*

Outside, the wind came off the river, and somewhere forty miles away a chimney stood in a kept lot, and a song was loose in the world with the wrong name on it, and Cyrus Thackeray — who had been silent for nine years because silence was the sentence, and quiet for two more because quiet was the cure — began, very softly, to hum.
