## 11. The Archive in the Attic

The storage unit was not, technically, in an attic. It was in a corrugated steel maze called SafeKeep Self-Storage on the outskirts of Marrow Bend, wedged between a defunct bowling alley and a feed store that smelled like the inside of a horse's opinion. But the unit was upstairs — unit 2C, second floor, end of the corridor — and when Cyrus said, "Well, look at that. An attic," with the flat delivery of a man who had spent nine years learning not to feel things loudly, I let him have it.

We stood in front of the roll-up door like two people at a grave they weren't sure they were allowed to mourn at. Cyrus had the key. Of course he had the key. Iris had given it to him two years ago, in one of her lucid windows, along with a sentence that had apparently made no sense at the time: *"When the song comes back, go to 2C."*

"When the song comes back," Cyrus repeated now, turning the key over in his fingers. "She said that in March. I figured the dementia was talking. Songs don't come back. Songs aren't lost cats."

"And yet," I said.

"And yet." He unlocked the door and hauled it up, and the smell of the past rolled out to meet us — old cardboard, cedar blocks, the faint ghost of cigarette smoke that could only have come from my father's coat, which meant the coat was in there, which meant I was going to have to have a feeling about that eventually, but not yet. One feeling at a time. That was the house rule I'd lived by for nine years.

The unit was bigger than I expected. Iris had been paying for it for — I did the math and stopped doing the math, because the number was obscene, and it meant she'd been renting a steel box for decades to keep something safe from someone, and I had a sick, certain guess about who the someone was.

Inside: boxes. Dozens of them, labeled in her small, upright handwriting, the handwriting of a woman who'd once addressed envelopes for the county and never stopped treating every surface as an envelope. *KITCHEN — MISC. WINTER COATS. CYRUS — SCHOOL. TAMSIIN — SCHOOL.* (She'd spelled my name wrong on a box in 1994 and never corrected it, and I chose to find this funny rather than devastating, which is a skill, and I have a certificate.) And along the back wall, apart from the rest, stacked with a care that bordered on ceremony: seven cardboard file boxes, each one labeled with a year. 1971 through 1998.

"She kept everything," Cyrus said quietly.

"She kept *specific* everything," I said. "Look. No Christmas decorations. No dishes. This isn't a storage unit, it's an archive."

"An attic archive," Cyrus said, and I swear to God the man smiled.

* * *

We started with the oldest box, because Cyrus was methodical the way I was evasive — both coping mechanisms, just wearing different hats. Inside, wrapped in dish towels, were notebooks. Composition books, the cheap marbled kind, their covers soft as cloth from handling. The first one said, on the front, in Iris's hand: *Songs. Do not lend. Do not lose. — I.T.*

Cyrus opened it the way you'd open a letter from a doctor.

The pages were dense. Lyrics, mostly, but also chord charts in a shorthand she'd invented herself — little hieroglyphs for the shapes her fingers made, arrows for where the melody bent. And at the bottom of each finished song, a date and a signature, formal as a notary. *Iris Thackeray, 14 March 1972. Iris Thackeray, 2 November 1972.* Forty songs in that first box alone. I counted. I don't know why I counted. I think counting was easier than crying, and I have always been an excellent counter.

"This one," Cyrus said, and turned the book toward me.

The page read, at the top, in letters pressed so hard they'd torn through to the next sheet: *CYRUS'S LAMENT.* Below it, the lyrics. The whole song. The one I'd heard on the radio three weeks ago in another woman's voice, credited to a man named Dell Harrow, the one my mother had sung exactly once, in the kitchen, at night, and then said — and I could hear her saying it, that particular gentle iron — *"That one's not for the world, Tamsin. That one's for keeping."*

The date at the bottom: *Iris Thackeray, 9 August 1976.* The year before Cyrus was born. She'd written it for a child who didn't exist yet, a lullaby ahead of its lullabee, and then she'd spent his whole childhood singing it to him without ever telling him it was his.

Cyrus didn't say anything. He sat down on a box marked *WINTER COATS* and held the notebook like it was a bird that might startle, and I looked at the rafters of the storage facility and thought very hard about bowling alleys.

"Forty songs," I said finally, because someone had to say something and it wasn't going to be the emotional one. "Just in this box. There are six more boxes."

"She wrote every day," Cyrus said. "I forgot that. I forgot she wrote *every day.* After dinner she'd go to the kitchen table and I thought — I don't know what I thought. That she was doing the bills."

"She was doing the bills of the heart," I said, and he gave me a look that said *nine years away and you've gotten worse,* and he was right, and I loved him for the look, and I didn't tell him that either. One feeling at a time.

* * *

The contract was in the 1998 box.

We'd worked through three boxes by then, taking breaks Cyrus didn't need and I pretended not to need, and the afternoon light through the corridor's high window had gone the color of weak tea. The 1998 box was lighter than the others. Almost empty. One notebook — the last one, the songs stopping mid-year, mid-page, a verse abandoned at line six like someone had been called away from the table and never came back — and a single manila folder, and the folder was what made my hands go cold, because Iris Thackeray did not keep paperwork she didn't consider ammunition.

The folder said, on the tab: *KESTREL.*

Inside: a contract. *Song Catalog Assignment Agreement,* dated 14 June 1998, between Iris Thackeray of Marrow Bend and Kestrel Music Publishing, granting — I read it three times to make sure the words were doing what I thought they were doing — granting Kestrel Music "all rights, title, and interest, in perpetuity, throughout the universe" in the entirety of her catalog. Forty songs and counting. The universe. They'd gotten the universe for the price of a used sedan.

And at the bottom, her signature. *Iris Thackeray,* in flowing cursive.

"That's not her signature," Cyrus said.

"I know."

"Look at it. Mom printed her name like a schoolteacher. She signed the school forms, she signed my parole—" he stopped, recalibrated, went on. "She *printed.* She thought cursive was showing off. She used to say fancy handwriting was how they got you."

"They did get her," I said.

I held the page up to the light, as if the light were going to confess. The signature was beautiful. Looping, confident, the kind of signature that belonged to a woman who wrote *Songs. Do not lend* in upright block letters and dated everything like the world might contest it. It was a forgery done by someone talented, which was somehow worse than a bad one. A bad forgery is an insult. A good forgery is a plan.

And then I saw the second page, tucked behind the contract, and stopped breathing in the casual, practiced way I had, and then stopped breathing in the other way.

It was a photograph.

Me, at nineteen. Standing on the porch of the Marrow Bend house — the porch that burned four months later — squinting into the sun with that expression I remembered having, the one that was half smile and half *please don't perceive me.* Cyrus must have taken it. There was a thumb-shaped blur in the corner that was very him.

I turned it over.

On the back, in my mother's handwriting — the real one, the upright one, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — six words. No. Nine.

*Tell them. Or tell no one. But choose.*

* * *

Cyrus read it upside down before I could hand it to him, because he'd always been a shameless reader of other people's mail, and when he finished, the storage unit got very quiet, the way a room gets quiet when it understands it is holding something.

"When did she write this?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"Think. You're the one who remembers everything. You remember what everyone was wearing at every funeral since 1989."

"That's a gift and a curse, Cyrus."

"Think."

I thought. The photograph was from the summer I was nineteen. The fire was in October. The police station was in October, that fluorescent room where a detective named Brandt asked me, kindly, twice, whether I'd seen anything the night of the fire, and where I said no, twice, in a voice I barely recognized as mine, while Cyrus sat in the next room being seventeen and scared and already, somehow, decided upon. I'd told myself for nine years that I'd stayed silent because I was afraid. That was true. It had just never been the whole truth. The whole truth had a porch and a squint and a mother who watched her child choose, and wrote nine words on the back of a photograph, and put it in a folder, and kept it in a steel box for twenty-five years, waiting for the day the song came back.

"She knew," I said. "She knew what I did. Or didn't do. And she never — she never once said anything. Twenty-five years of Sunday dinners and she never once—"

"Because she was waiting for you to choose," Cyrus said. "That's the whole thing. That's her, all over. She wasn't going to push you off the diving board. She was just going to stand there at the edge of the pool with a towel."

"That's an insane metaphor."

"She used it on me when I was scared of the high dive. I was eight. It didn't help then either."

I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle, and I sat down on the box marked *CYRUS — SCHOOL* — which felt, in the moment, like sitting on the evidence — and I held the photograph and I did not do the thing where you compose yourself. I'd been composing myself for nine years. Four hundred and twelve postcards of composed. I let my face do what it wanted, which was apparently a lot.

Cyrus, to his enormous credit, didn't look away and didn't make it a moment. He just kept going through the folder, because grief is easier with your hands busy, and after a while he said, "Tamsin. Look at this," in a voice that had gone flat and careful, the voice he must have used reading verdicts.

Behind the photograph, folded in quarters, was a receipt. Dated 14 June 1998. The same day as the contract. Kestrel Music, payment to I. Thackeray: four thousand dollars. And beneath the receipt, a bank slip showing the deposit — and beneath *that,* in Iris's handwriting, a single annotation, dated three days later:

*Did not sign this. Did not take this money. Money returned by mail, certified, 17 June. They signed for it. R.K.'s man delivered the contract. Big fellow, gray car. He stood in the yard and looked at the house a long time before he knocked.*

I read it twice. Cyrus read it over my shoulder, and I felt him go still the way animals go still.

"She refused the money," he said. "She refused it and she *documented* it. Certified mail. She kept the proof that she never took a cent."

"She was building a file," I said. "The whole unit. It's not an attic. It's a case."

"Against Roland Kestrel."

"Against Roland Kestrel," I said, and the name sat in the corrugated air between us like a third person who'd let himself in.

We sat with that for a while. The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere below us, the feed store closed with a great rattling of gates, and the smell of the horse's opinion intensified.

"She knew what would happen if she pushed it," Cyrus said slowly. "The gray car. The man in the yard. She knew, and she filed everything anyway, and then she — what, sat on it for twenty-five years?"

"She sat on it for the same reason I sat on it," I said. "Because the last time this family told the truth, you went to prison for it."

He didn't say anything to that. He picked up the notebook with *CYRUS'S LAMENT* in it and held it against his chest, and I watched my brother — my actual brother, the one whose name I'd given to a song I didn't know was ours — decide something.

"We need a lawyer," he said. "A good one. One who isn't scared."

"Good luck. Kestrel owns half the lawyers in three counties."

"Then we need someone who's seen the inside of his machine and got out." He was quiet a second, and then, reluctantly, like the words cost him: "The songwriter. Harrow. The one who's been singing her song. If he bought it through Kestrel, he's got paperwork. Contracts, royalty statements, correspondence. He's either our best witness or he's in it up to his neck."

"He cried at his own gig, Cyrus. He sang it like it was breaking him."

"Guilty people cry too. I met several."

There was no arguing with nine years of prison sociology, so I didn't. I took the photograph out of the folder one more time and read the nine words on the back, and then, very deliberately, in front of my brother, I put it in my inside jacket pocket. Over the heart, like a badge, like a warrant.

"Choose," I said. "Fine. I'm choosing. But I'm doing it in order. First the lawyer, then the songwriter, then—" I looked at the seven boxes, the forty songs, the universe Kestrel had stolen for the price of a used sedan. "Then him."

Cyrus stood, and hauled the roll-up door down, and locked it, and for a moment we just stood in the corridor with the key in his fist, two middle-aged people holding a cardboard box of their mother's life between them like a bomb they'd agreed to carry together.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When we do this — whatever this turns into, court, newspapers, all of it — you don't smile your way through a single second of it. No charm. No composure. I've seen what you do when it gets hard, Tamsin. You make yourself pleasant until the danger passes."

"That's — okay, ouch."

"I'm serious."

"I know you're serious. That's what makes it ouch."

He almost smiled. It was, I'd have sworn, the first one in nine years that reached all the way up. "Come on," he said. "We'll start with the lawyer in the morning. Tonight—" he nodded at the box, at the notebooks, at the lullaby written before he was born — "tonight I want to read my mother's mail."

We carried it down the stairs together, the box heavy and light at once, and out into the parking lot where the evening had come on soft and purple, and neither of us noticed, then, the gray sedan parked across the road with its engine running — or if we did, we filed it away with everything else, in the box marked *DO NOT LEND. DO NOT LOSE,* which was getting fuller by the day, and which, very soon now, was going to have to be opened in front of strangers.
