## 3. What the Panes Remember

The tape measure sang back into its housing, and Dominic Ferro stood alone in the fenced lot with a number he didn't want: eleven panes cracked, three missing, the whole eastern wall bowed inward like a lung that had forgotten how to breathe.

He'd been measuring the glasshouse for six days. Officially, he was here for Hargrove & Finch, Estate Appraisers, to inventory the property before it went to auction — Lot 47, as the flyer called it, as if the place had never had a name, as if it hadn't been the only roof in the city that ever held him on purpose. Unofficially, he had spent six days waiting for a woman he'd last seen at fifteen, standing in this exact dirt, watching a hearse pull away from a curb that no longer existed.

He'd rehearsed the conversation a hundred times on the drive in. *I didn't have a choice. They were going to split us up anyway. I wrote you — I thought about writing you —* Every version collapsed under its own weight. So instead he measured glass, cataloged rust, and told himself the inventory was a kind of penance. You could count what you'd broken. It was harder to count what you'd abandoned.

The gate shrieked when she pushed it open.

He'd heard her coming before that — the particular rhythm of her walk, quick and clipped, like someone perpetually arriving late to her own life. Fifteen years, and he'd have known it in the dark. He turned around slowly, clipboard against his chest like a shield, and watched Ansel Ravensworth take in the scene: him, the tape measure, the laminated Hargrove & Finch badge, the surveyor's stakes with their little orange flags.

Her face did something complicated. Shock first, then something colder moving in underneath it, frost spreading across a window.

"You're the one appraising it," she said. Not a question.

"Inventorying," he said. "There's a difference."

"Is there." She came toward him across the lot, and he noticed she still wore her library lanyard, still had that look of someone who'd slept in a chair. "Because from where I'm standing, it looks like you're the man with the clipboard deciding what my mother's life is worth."

"Marisol wasn't my mother."

The words came out wrong, defensive, and he watched them land like a slap. Ansel stopped three feet from him. Up close she was thinner than the girl in his memory, sharper, but her eyes were the same — that gray that always seemed to be reading him, cataloging him, shelving him somewhere he couldn't reach.

"No," she said quietly. "She was mine. And you were the boy she fed anyway."

* * *

He should have let her walk away. Instead he heard himself say, "Do you want to see it?"

She looked at him like he'd offered her a knife, handle first.

"The interior," he said. "The door's not locked. It hasn't been locked in months. I've been — I go in. To work."

"Work." She almost laughed. "You call this work."

"I call it the only reason I came back."

That stopped her. He saw it stop her — the way her weight shifted back on her heels, the way her jaw set against whatever the sentence had started in her chest. She looked at the glasshouse door, hanging crooked on one hinge, and then at him, and he understood that she was deciding something, and that whatever she decided, he deserved the worse half of it.

"Fine," she said. "Show me."

* * *

The inside of the glasshouse had gone feral. Marisol had kept it like a cathedral — swept stone floors, herbs in clay pots along the south wall, a table under the skylight where she did everything: mending, arguing, feeding strays of every species including the human kind. Now the table was a skeleton of gray wood, and the fig tree she'd planted the year Dominic arrived had pushed through the rotted planter and grown sideways along the glass, its branches pressing against the panes like hands against a window.

Ansel stopped just inside the door. He watched her take it in — the weeds waist-high in the beds, the moss climbing the north wall, the fig tree gone monstrous — and he watched her grief change shape. It wasn't the building she was mourning. It was everything the building had held.

"She'd have hated this," Ansel said.

"She'd have loved it," Dominic said. "She'd have said the garden finally got to say what it wanted."

That earned him a look — not soft, exactly, but no longer a blade. He moved down the central path, pushing nettles aside, and she followed, and for a while the only sound was their footsteps and the tick of the fig branches against the glass.

"She kept your bed made," he said, before he could stop himself. "For the first two years. After you two moved out. Every morning she'd come in here and make it, like you were coming back."

"I didn't move out," Ansel said. "She died. I was nineteen. There was no one to stay for."

"There was me."

The words dropped into the overgrown silence like a stone into a well. They both listened for the splash. It took a long time coming.

"You were seventeen," she said. "You were a kid."

"I was old enough to say goodbye."

"Then why didn't you?"

He'd had six days to rehearse an answer and he gave her the true one, which he'd never rehearsed at all: "Because if I'd said it out loud, it would have been real. And I couldn't — " His voice caught on something. He pushed through it. "Marisol was the only person who ever kept me without being paid to. The state paid for the first three years. After that, she just — did it. And when she died, I kept waiting for someone to tell me the arrangement was over. Nobody did. So I decided it myself, before anyone else could."

"You decided to disappear."

"I decided to be gone before I was sent."

Ansel stood among the weeds with her arms folded, and for a moment she looked so much like Marisol — the same stubborn set of the shoulders, the same way of holding a hurt like a fact to be examined rather than felt — that he had to look away, at the fig tree, at the glass, anywhere.

"You know what I did the week after the funeral?" she said. "I came here every day. I sat at that table. I waited for you to come through that door. Not because I expected you to — because I couldn't make myself stop."

"I didn't know that."

"How would you? You didn't leave an address. You didn't leave a note. You left a hole in the shape of a person, Dominic, and I fell into it every single day."

* * *

He found it an hour later, or she did — neither of them would ever agree — in the corner bed where Marisol used to grow chamomile. A clay pot, overturned, its rim broken, and beneath it a flat stone with something scratched into the underside.

Ansel brushed the dirt away with her fingers, the way Marisol had taught them to handle anything old. The scratches weren't letters, exactly. They were shapes — the same shapes, Dominic realized with a jolt, that climbed the panes above the door in faded blue paint. The sentence. The one nobody could read. The one Ansel used to trace with her finger when she was small, asking Marisol what it said, and Marisol would only smile and say, *When you're both old enough to need it.*

"Both," Ansel whispered now, her finger on the stone. "She said *both*. I always thought I misremembered."

Dominic crouched beside her. The stone was cool under his hand, and the shapes were deliberate, patient, made by someone who'd had all the time in the world and no one to tell.

"She was leaving us a message," he said.

"She was leaving *you* a message," Ansel said. "I got the paint. You got the stone. She split it between us like — "

"Like everything else," he finished.

They stayed crouched there a moment too long, shoulder to shoulder in the ruins of the chamomile bed, and Dominic felt something in his chest that he had no inventory code for. Reluctant. Tender. Fifteen years late.

Then Ansel stood, and pocketed the stone, and looked at him with the frost back in her eyes — but thinner now, thawing at the edges.

"The appraisal," she said. "The demolition. Is any of it decided?"

He thought of the file in his car, the one with the demolition date circled in red, the one his boss wanted finalized by Friday. He thought of the word *both*, scratched under a stone, waiting fifteen years for two people to read it together.

"Not yet," he said. "But it will be soon. And Ansel — there's something in the estate paperwork you need to see. Something Marisol filed."

She went very still. "What kind of something?"

"A record," he said. "With your name on it."
