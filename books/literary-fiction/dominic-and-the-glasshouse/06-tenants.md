## 6. Tenants

He was waiting for me when I got off shift.

Not at the library — he'd have known better than that, after the way I'd walked out on him at the glasshouse. He was leaning against the brick wall across the street, under the streetlight that buzzed like it was deciding something, hands in the pockets of a coat that had been good once and had decided not to pretend anymore. Four a.m. The city had that rinsed, pre-dawn hush, and there he was in the middle of it like a word I'd been trying not to read.

I should have crossed to the other side. I'd had fifteen years of practice avoiding things; one more street wouldn't have killed me.

I crossed anyway.

"Ansel." My name in his mouth was different now. Deeper, worn at the edges, like a coin that had been through too many hands. "I figured you'd be getting off around now. You always liked the dead hours."

"You figured." I kept walking. He fell into step beside me, which was so Dominic it made my teeth ache — the boy who had never once waited to be invited anywhere, who simply arrived, who simply stayed, until the one time it mattered, when he didn't.

"Look, I know how it looked—"

"How it looked." I stopped under the next streetlight and turned to face him. Up close he had lines I didn't recognize and the same stubborn set to his jaw, the one Marisol used to say could crack walnuts. "You want to know how it looked, Dominic? It looked like a stranger in a good coat measuring up my mother's grave for salvage value."

"That's not—"

"Lot 47." I said it flat, the way the flyer said it. "Demolition pending. That's how it looked."

He exhaled through his nose. Somewhere down the block a delivery truck idled, backing up with its small mechanical complaint, and I was grateful for the sound because it meant the silence between us wasn't the only thing in the world.

"I'm not the one demolishing it," he said. "I told you. I was hired to inventory. That's all."

"Then quit."

He blinked. "What?"

"Quit. Walk away. If you're not the one doing it, prove it. You know how to leave. You've had practice."

The words came out sharper than I'd planned, and I watched them land, watched his face do something complicated — hurt folding itself into anger, anger folding itself into something older. He stepped back half a pace and laughed, but it wasn't a laugh, it was a wound wearing a laugh's clothing.

"There it is," he said. "Fifteen years, and that's the first real thing you've said to me."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"You've been polite at me since the glasshouse. Polite. Like I'm a patron with a question about the dewey decimal system. And now this." He pulled his hand through his hair, a gesture I remembered from when he was fourteen and Marisol's kitchen radio was broken and he couldn't fix it. "Fine. You want to say it? Say it. Say you think I abandoned you."

"You did."

The words were out before I could dress them up, and once they were out there was no taking them back, so I let them stand there in the cold air between us, ugly and true and mine.

"You did," I said again, quieter. "Marisol died on a Tuesday. Do you know that? A Tuesday in March. And by Friday you were gone. No note. No phone number. I came home from the funeral home and your room was empty. The bed stripped. You even took the poster, Dominic. The one with the planets. You took a *poster* and you left me a house that smelled like her and a silence I had to live in for fifteen years."

"Ansel—"

"I was seventeen." My voice cracked and I hated it, hated him for hearing it. "I was seventeen years old and the only person I had left in the world walked out without a goodbye, and I stood in your empty room and I thought — this is what I am. I'm the one people don't choose. Marisol took you in, she chose you, and then she died, and you chose the door. So don't stand there and tell me how it looked. I *saw* how it looked. I've been seeing it every day for fifteen years."

The truck pulled away. The streetlight buzzed. Dominic stood very still, and I realized with a strange, distant clarity that I'd been waiting to say all of that since I was seventeen, and that saying it hadn't fixed anything, hadn't even made it lighter — it had just cracked the ground open so we could both see what was buried underneath.

He was crying. Not dramatically — Dominic had never been dramatic, that had always been my department, the girl who read novels and felt everything at full volume. He just stood there and the tears came down his face the way rain comes down a window, quiet and unstoppable, and he didn't wipe them away.

"I was nineteen," he said.

"What?"

"I was nineteen years old, Ansel. She wasn't my mother. That's the thing nobody ever says out loud. She was *your* mother. I was the stray she brought home because I showed up half-frozen on her step and she couldn't not. And when she died, her sister — your aunt, the one who smelled like cigarettes and disapproval — sat me down in that kitchen and told me the estate was going to you. All of it. The house, the glasshouse, everything. And that I had no legal claim to any of it, and that she'd be watching to make sure I didn't try."

"That's not—" I started, and he talked over me, fifteen years of talking over him finally coming due.

"I had nothing. No family. No money. No claim. And I looked at you — seventeen, grieving, already so far inside yourself I don't think you heard a word anyone said that whole week — and I thought, if I stay, I'm a tenant. A charity case with no lease. Every day I'd be reminding you that the house wasn't mine, that she wasn't really mine, that I was the boy she was kind to, not the son she kept." His voice broke on *kept* and he pushed through it. "So I left before I could be asked to leave. That's the coward's version of dignity, and I've had fifteen years to know it, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and I hate that I'd do it again tomorrow."

The street was empty. Somewhere a bird was testing whether it was morning yet.

"You could have said goodbye," I whispered.

"To what?" And now his voice went hard, and the hard voice was worse than the crying, because the crying I could have forgiven. "To say goodbye I'd have had to admit I was leaving. And to admit I was leaving I'd have had to look at you. And Ansel — here's the thing I've never told anyone, not the therapists, not the three people on earth who know my last name. I couldn't look at you. Because you were the only person alive who knew what I'd lost, and looking at you meant losing it again, every single day, in your face."

I stood there with my hands in my pockets, seventeen and thirty-two at once, and the truth of it landed on me sideways: he hadn't left because I wasn't enough. He'd left because I was too much. Because I was the mirror of everything, and he couldn't afford the reflection.

"And you?" I said, because the wound wanted company. "Fifteen years, Dominic. You knew where the library was. You knew I'd never leave this city. You could have written. One letter. One postcard. You could have—"

"You never wrote either."

It came out fast and flat and it stopped me like a hand on the chest.

"You had my aunt's address," he said. "You had the whole apparatus of the state, Ansel — they find people, that's their whole job. You were the one with the house, the name, the claim. You could have looked for me. You could have written to the one place you knew I'd go — Marisol's sister knew people, the old neighborhood, you could have—" He shook his head. "You didn't. And I told myself it was because you were grieving. And then I told myself it was because you were angry. And then, somewhere around year five, I told myself the truth, which is that you didn't want to find me. Which was fine. Which was *fine*, because it meant I'd been right to go."

"That's not true."

"Isn't it?"

"I didn't write because—" And here it was, the thing I'd never said, the thing I'd folded up small and buried under fifteen years of shelving other people's stories. "Because I was afraid you'd write back."

He went still.

"I was afraid you'd write back and it would be a bill, or an apology, or a wedding invitation, or — anything. Anything that proved you'd built a life without me in it and were doing *fine*. Because as long as I never knew, I got to keep believing you were out there somewhere missing me. That was the only version I could live with. So I didn't look. I shelved books and I kept the glasshouse locked and I didn't look, and I told myself it was grief, but it was cowardice. Same as yours. Same coward's dignity, different door."

The sky was going grey at the edges. The streetlight above us clicked off, the way they do, surrendering to the dawn, and we stood in the new, honest light like two people who had just run out of places to hide.

"So that's it," Dominic said finally. His voice was wrecked and steady at the same time, the way voices get when they've passed through crying and come out the other side. "Fifteen years. Two cowards, one empty room, and a poster with the planets on it."

"I can't believe you took the poster."

"I couldn't sleep without it." He almost smiled. "Jupiter had this chip in the corner. I used to put my thumb on it and pretend it was a moon."

I laughed — a wet, cracked, terrible laugh, the first one in weeks — and then I wasn't laughing, I was crying, and then he was too, and we stood on the sidewalk in the grey morning holding nothing, having nothing, the glasshouse behind us and the day ahead of us and fifteen years of silence finally, finally broken open.

"We have to stop doing this," I said when I could speak. "The math. Who left who. It's a ledger with no bottom line."

"Then what do we do?"

"I don't know. But the glasshouse gets demolished in three weeks, and there's a sentence painted on those panes that nobody on earth can read, and Marisol wrote it, and I think—" I wiped my face with my sleeve. "I think it might be the only goodbye she ever got to leave either of us. And I'm not walking away from it twice."

Dominic looked at me for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat pocket and pulled out something folded, soft at the creases, and handed it to me.

It was the poster. The planets. Jupiter with its chipped corner.

"I didn't come back for the appraisal," he said. "The appraisal was the excuse. Ansel — I need to show you something in Marisol's ledger. I found it last week, and I've been trying to figure out how to tell you, because it changes everything. About her. About why she filed it. About *us*."
