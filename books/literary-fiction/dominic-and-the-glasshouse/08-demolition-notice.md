## 8. Demolition Notice

The email arrived at 6:47 a.m., which meant it had been composed the night before by someone who wanted Dominic to read it before he'd had coffee, before he'd had a chance to compose himself, before the day had hardened into anything he could defend.

*Re: Lot 47 — Ravensworth Property. Demolition date moved up. New date: the 14th. Final appraisal due Friday. No extensions.*

He read it three times in the stairwell of his building, phone held close to his face like a match he was trying to keep lit. The fourteenth. That was nine days out. Nine days from a month that had been promised, a month Ansel had been promised, a month they'd been using — quietly, greedily, without saying so aloud — to finish what Marisol had started with her painted sentence.

He thought about calling his supervisor, Halloran, and decided against it. Halloran didn't take calls before eight. Halloran took calls at eight exactly, and only then, and only if you were calling to confirm something he'd already decided.

Dominic climbed back into his apartment, which was still mostly boxes, because he'd never fully unpacked, because some part of him had understood from the beginning that this return was provisional, that cities let you come back but never let you stay on the old terms. He sat on the edge of the mattress and looked at the appraisal file spread across the floor — Lot 47, the glasshouse, every pane measured and photographed and assigned a salvage value. He had done meticulous work. It was the best inventory of his career, and he hated every page of it, because every page was a price tag on a place that had never once asked him for money.

His phone buzzed. Ansel.

*Did you see?*

She'd seen. Of course she'd seen. She probably subscribed to every municipal alert system in the city by now; she'd learned to read zoning notices the way other people read horoscopes.

*I saw,* he typed. *Coming to the house at noon. Don't do anything yet.*

*Define "anything."*

He almost smiled. *Just wait for me.*

*That's not a definition, Dominic.*

He put the phone face-down on the floor and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. Fifteen years ago he had left this woman — no, not left her; abandoned was the word she'd used, and abandonment was accurate even if it wasn't fair, even if fairness had nothing to do with a seventeen-year-old boy standing in a bus station with a duffel bag and a guardianship record he didn't know existed — and now he was going to have to choose between her again, except this time there was no bus station, no clean departure, no way to pretend the leaving was someone else's decision.

This time the choice was the job or the family. And the job had brought him back. That was the ugly arithmetic of it: without Ferro & Associates' client list, without the auctioneer's commission, he would never have had a reason to stand inside the glasshouse again, measuring light. The job had been the door. Now the door was closing on the thing he'd walked through it to find.

*

Halloran's office smelled like toner and ambition. There were two other appraisers' names on the door, both of them gone — one transferred to the coastal office, one fired — and Halloran kept the plate anyway, as if the names were trophies.

"You got my email," Halloran said. Not a question. He was a man who treated questions as concessions.

"The fourteenth is too soon. The structural survey alone—"

"Is done. You did it." Halloran leaned back, laced his fingers over his vest. He wore vests now; Dominic remembered when it had been ties. "Client wants speed. Client's paying for speed. You've been dragging your feet on this one for three weeks, Ferro, and I want to tell you why I've let it go, because I'm a generous man."

Dominic said nothing. Generosity, in Halloran's economy, was always a loan.

"Because you're good. Because your numbers hold up under audit. And because I assumed you were being thorough." He unfolded his hands. "But I got a call yesterday asking why the delay paperwork keeps getting *revised*. Why the environmental addendum was resubmitted twice. Why a man who finishes inventories in five days has taken twenty-one."

The room went very quiet. Outside the window, a crane swung its arm over the skyline with the patience of something that knew it would outlive everyone in the building.

"I revised them because they were incomplete," Dominic said.

"They were complete. You know how I know they were complete?" Halloran stood, came around the desk, perched on its edge — the posture of a man delivering bad news to a friend he doesn't have. "Because I read them myself. And then I had Ruiz read them. And Ruiz says they're the cleanest paperwork he's seen all year. So here's what's happening, Ferro. You finalize the appraisal by Friday at five, at the number the client's expecting, or you're off the lot and off the payroll. And I'll be honest with you, because I respect you: if you miss Friday, don't bother clearing out your desk slowly. Other firms talk. This city is smaller than it looks."

There it was. Not a threat, exactly. A weather report.

"And if I find discrepancies," Halloran added, turning back to his window, "in the final version — if the numbers move between now and Friday — I'll know why."

Dominic walked out with his badge feeling heavier than it had that morning, though it weighed the same. In the elevator he caught his reflection in the brushed steel, warped and doubled, and thought: *that's what she sees when she looks at me.* A man reflected imperfectly. A man who had already left once.

*

He went to the glasshouse instead of going home.

It was late afternoon, the hour when the panes did their best work, turning the overgrown interior into an aquarium of amber light. Weeds had pushed through the cracked floor tiles in green rivers. A bird had nested somewhere high in the iron ribs; he could hear it moving, a small industry of twigs and complaint. Ansel was already there, sitting on the old potting bench with her coat buttoned wrong, one buttonhole off so the whole garment hung skewed, and she hadn't fixed it, and somehow that undid him more than the demolition notice had.

"They moved it up," she said, without looking at him. "Nine days. Nine days, Dominic. The linguist was supposed to send her notes Thursday. The neighbor with the photographs — Mrs. Okafor — she can't come until next week. We're supposed to have a *month*."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned then, and her eyes had the flat shine of someone who had cried in private and intended to keep it that way. "Because I keep thinking there's something you're not telling me. You get this look. You had it when you told me about the guardianship ledger. You have it now."

He should have told her. That was the whole lesson of his life, the lesson he'd paid fifteen years of interest on: tell her. But telling her meant telling her that his job was the countdown clock, that his employer was the wrecking ball's accountant, that every day he'd bought her with revised paperwork had been stolen from a firm that was now watching him steal.

"Halloran gave me an ultimatum," he said. "Friday. Final numbers, their number, or I'm done. Blackballed, probably. He made sure I understood the secondary market for disgraced appraisers is small."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. A leaf detached itself somewhere above them and took its time falling, spinning, catching light.

"So quit," she said.

"It's not—" He laughed, but there was no humor in it, just air escaping. "You think I haven't run it? Quitting doesn't stop the demolition. They'll just hand the file to Ruiz, who'll finalize it in a day and feel good about himself. Me staying is the only thing slowing it down. Every revision I file buys us time. Except now they're watching, so the revisions are over, and Friday the file goes in finished whether I like it or not, and the fourteenth happens either way, and the only variable left is whether I'm employed when it does."

"Then we have nine days and no more tricks."

"We have nine days," he agreed. "And one trick."

She looked at him. Waiting. She had always been good at waiting; it was the thing he'd mistaken for patience when they were young, before he understood it was faith — that she waited because she believed people came back.

"There's a clause," he said slowly, working it out as he said it, which was dangerous, which was how he'd gotten into trouble his whole life, thinking out loud in front of people who remembered everything. "Historical review. If a property is flagged for cultural significance before the permit is finalized, the demolition auto-stays for sixty days pending review. It's rarely granted. It's almost never granted fast enough to matter. But filing it resets the clock while it's processed."

"Flagged by whom?"

"By anyone with standing." He met her eyes. "A preservation board. A historical society. Or — and this is the part Halloran will kill me for — anyone with access to the preliminary findings. Which show original nineteenth-century ironwork, intact period glazing, and a structure of documented local significance."

Ansel stood up from the potting bench very slowly, the way you stand when the ground might be thinner than it looks.

"Dominic. If you leak those findings, you won't just be fired. You'll be *finished*. He told you—"

"He told me a lot of things." He looked around the glasshouse — at the ribs of iron Marisol had scrubbed rust from with wire wool and vinegar, at the panes where her sentence still curved across the glass in that language belonging to no country, at the floor where two children had once slept in summer because it was cooler than the loft and Marisol had pretended not to notice. "I spent fifteen years believing I chose the job over you. That's the story I told myself in every city I worked in. But that's not true. I never chose anything. I just kept moving so I wouldn't have to. Well." He exhaled. "I'm choosing now."

"Don't decide like this. Not in one afternoon, not to fix—"

"It's not to fix anything, Ansel. It's not penance. Penance is just another way of keeping score, and I'm done keeping score." He pulled his phone out, opened the folder of preliminary findings, and looked at the attach field for a long moment. "Marisol took in strays. Every stray in six blocks, and she never asked a single one of them what they could do for her in return. I was the worst stray she had. The least grateful. So consider this me finally learning the family business."

"What family business?"

"Taking people in," he said. "Even when it costs."

He sent the file to the preservation board's public comment address, cc'ing the historical society, the council member whose district included the lot, and — after a moment's thought — Mrs. Okafor, who struck him as a woman who had been waiting forty years for a reason to make official phone calls.

The whoosh of the send was very small for such a large act. Somewhere across the city, Halloran's phone was probably already buzzing, and Ruiz was probably already reaching for his jacket, and the machinery of Ferro & Associates was grinding toward the destruction of Dominic Ferro's career with the same patient efficiency it brought to everything.

Ansel stared at him. "You didn't even hesitate."

"I hesitated for fifteen years. I'm out of practice at anything else."

She crossed the weedy floor and did something she hadn't done since they were children: she put her arms around him, briefly, hard, her chin against his shoulder, and then stepped back as if embarrassed by it, smoothing her wrongly-buttoned coat.

"Sixty days," she said. "If it's granted."

"If it's granted. And Halloran will fight it, and the client will fight it, and I'll be unemployed by Monday, so we should probably figure out what a former appraiser does for a living."

"You could shelve books," she said. "Night shift. The pay's terrible but the company's selective."

Outside, the light was failing, and the panes held the last of it the way they had always held everything — generously, without judgment, glowing. His phone buzzed. Then buzzed again. Then began ringing steadily, insistently, the sound of consequences arriving on schedule.

He looked at the screen. HALLORAN.

Then it stopped, and a text appeared:

*My office. 8 a.m. Bring your keys.*

Dominic turned the phone toward Ansel so she could read it. She read it, and something moved through her face — fear, guilt, and underneath both, unmistakably, hope.

"Eight a.m. tomorrow," she said. "And tonight—" she gestured upward, at the sentence curving across the darkening glass, at the words they had almost decoded, at the word that was hers and the word that was his, still waiting in the sequence like two hands not yet joined — "tonight we finish the translation. Whatever happens to you tomorrow, the sentence comes first."

Above them, in the iron ribs, the bird settled into its nest for the night, and the first streetlight beyond the fence flickered on, and somewhere in the city a printer was already producing the paperwork that would end Dominic Ferro's career — while inside the glasshouse, two strays of Marisol Ravensworth bent over a photograph of a painted sentence, racing the dawn.
