## 8. The Mother's Memory Goes Entirely

The bus to Fairhaven took forty minutes, and Ada spent all of them rehearsing nothing. That was the trick of it—she had learned over the years that preparation was a kind of lie, so she boarded with an empty mind and let the city scroll past the window like a film she'd seen too many times: the shuttered laundromat, the mural of a heron fading on the side of a credit union, the long gray seam of the river where it went industrial and forgot to be beautiful. She held her purse in her lap with both hands, the way she had as a girl riding to church, and she watched her reflection ghosted against the glass, superimposed on the passing streets, so that she seemed to be traveling inside herself.

Saturdays were visiting days. Not because the facility required it—Fairhaven required nothing but payment—but because Saturday was the day her mother had always baked bread, and some part of Ada believed, without evidence and against all instruction from the nurses, that rhythm outlasted memory. That if Ines could no longer hold a name in her mind, she might still hold a Saturday. That the body kept its own calendar when the mind's burned down.

The facility smelled of lavender and bleach, an argument between comfort and hygiene that neither side won. A nurse named Priya signed her in at the desk, and Ada gave her name the way she gave it everywhere—*Ada Crane*—and then, after the pause she could never quite close, added, "Ines Crane's daughter," as if the second phrase were the credential and the first merely a formality.

"She's having a good morning," Priya said. "She was singing earlier."

Ada's hand stopped on the pen. "Singing?"

"Humming, really. Some tune. She does that more lately." Priya smiled the professional smile of someone who found this charming rather than devastating. "It's nice. It seems to calm her."

Ada signed her name again, unnecessarily, a second time, because her first signature had come out wrong, the letters cramped and foreign.

* * *

Her mother was at the window in the sunroom, in the chair they'd learned to keep angled toward the light, wrapped in a cardigan the color of weak tea even though the radiator under the window clanked and breathed its dry heat into the room. The radiator. Ada saw it before she saw anything else—the old cast-iron unit, painted institutional white, ticking as it warmed—and something in her chest folded closed like a book.

"Mama," she said.

Ines turned. And for one unguarded moment her face did what it still sometimes did: it gathered itself around recognition, the way water gathers around a stone, and she said, "There you are," in a voice that made Ada feel nine years old and late for dinner.

"There I am," Ada agreed, and sat in the facing chair, and put her hand over her mother's, which was cool and light as a bird's bone.

They performed the liturgy. How are you, fine. Did you sleep, mostly. They've been taking us out to the garden when it doesn't rain, is the garden still there, yes Mama, the garden is still there. Ines's hands moved constantly now, smoothing the cardigan, patting the armrest, conducting some private music only she could hear. Her hair had gone entirely white and was thinning at the crown, and she wore it pinned up anyway, because vanity, thank God, was the last thing to go.

"You look tired," Ines said suddenly, sharply, with the old authority surfacing like a fish breaking water. "Are you sleeping? You work those nights."

"I sleep fine, Mama."

"You never slept. Even as a baby. Your father used to walk you up and down the hall at two in the morning and I'd hear him talking to you." She paused, and her eyes went vague, drifting toward the window, toward the heron-less sky. "He talked to you more than he talked to me."

"He talked to both of us."

"Did he?" said Ines, genuinely asking, and Ada understood that the conversation had already slipped its mooring and was drifting.

This was how visits went now. They were not conversations; they were weather systems, fronts moving through, moments of clarity arriving and departing without forecast. You learned to stop steering. You learned to say *yes, Mama* and *no, Mama* and to let the current take you where it would, because fighting it only frightened her, and frightened, she would grip Ada's wrist with sudden terrible strength and ask who she was, ask it with real fear, ask it as though a stranger had broken into the house of her dying mind.

Today the current ran toward the past, which was usual. The recent past had eroded completely—she asked about breakfast three times in ten minutes, remembered Priya not at all—but the deep past, the sedimentary past, rose up in her like things released from melting ice.

"The house on Delmore Street," she said. "The basement flooded once. Do you remember? Your father was down there all night with the shop vac."

"I remember."

"And the piano—" Ines's hands stilled. For a moment she was perfectly, unnaturally still, the way a room goes still when a glass is about to fall. "We should have sold the piano. It ruined the wood, sitting in that water."

"We didn't sell it, Mama."

"No," her mother said slowly, frowning, as though the piano itself were refusing to be remembered correctly. "No, we didn't. Because of—" She looked at Ada. Her eyes were clear and blue and utterly lost. "Because of the boy. The boy who played it."

Ada's throat closed.

"The boy," Ines repeated, softer now, questing. "There was a boy. Wasn't there a boy?"

And here it was, the moment that came every visit, wearing a different coat each time but always the same underneath: the moment when Dominic surfaced in his mother's mind as a shape without a name, a warmth without a story, and Ada stood at the edge of it holding everything she had never said.

"There was a boy," Ada said carefully.

"My son?" Ines asked. "Is he mine?"

"Yes, Mama. He's yours."

"What was his name?"

And Ada—who had spent two decades keeping that name behind her teeth like a stone, who had not spoken it at the funeral, who had not spoken it at the graveside, who had not written it in twenty years of unsent letters except in code, in initials, in *you know who I mean*—Ada opened her mouth.

What came out was: "He was your youngest."

Ines absorbed this. Nodded slowly. The window of her attention was already sliding shut; Ada could see it happening, the light moving off her mother's face. But before it closed, Ines began to hum.

Ada knew the melody before the third note. Of course she did. She knew it the way she knew her own pulse. It rose out of her mother unbidden, tuneless and wandering, losing its way and finding it again—four notes up, two down, the little turn at the end where Dominic had always lingered, teasing the resolution, making you wait for it. Their mother's version had no words. It didn't need them. It was the song stripped to its skeleton, the way a dementia patient might hum a lullaby from 1950, carried somewhere below language, below memory, in whatever cellar of the self remains when the house above has burned.

*Radiators.* Ines hummed 'Radiators' in a sunroom in Fairhaven while the radiator ticked beneath the window, and she did not know why, and she did not know what it was, and she did not know whose it was, and she hummed it the way rain falls—without intention, without origin, simply because it was in her and had nowhere else to go.

Ada sat very still. Her hand was still covering her mother's hand. She could feel the humming in it, faintly, a vibration like a wire under tension.

*Marty Fell,* said the voice on the radio, in her memory, in the dark of her car at dawn. *That was 'Radiators,' by Marty Fell.*

Her mother had the song in her body. Somewhere in the ruined architecture of Ines Crane's mind, in a corridor the disease had not yet reached or could not be bothered to flood, Dominic's song lived on, wordless, nameless, orphaned twice over—once by death, once by a stranger's mouth on the radio—and Ada realized with a sensation like vertigo that her mother was the last living thing on earth that carried the song without having learned it wrong.

"Mama," Ada heard herself say. Her own voice sounded far away, underwater. "Do you know that song?"

Ines hummed a few notes more, trailing off, frowning at the middle distance. Then she patted Ada's hand, twice, briskly, the gesture she'd used all Ada's childhood to mean *there, there, don't fuss.*

"It's a pretty one," Ines said. "I don't know where I got it. Isn't that funny? It just comes to me." She smiled, serene, unburdened. "It's like a gift. Something just comes to you, out of nowhere, and you don't know why."

Something just comes to you, out of nowhere, and you don't know why.

Ada thought of Marty Fell, fifty-something and fading, telling a radio host with warm sincerity that the song had come to him in a dream. *Out of nowhere.* The same sentence. The whole world saying the same sentence about her brother's song, everyone agreeing on the miracle and no one knowing the boy.

"Mama," she said again, and this time her voice cracked on the single syllable, and Ines looked at her with sudden alertness, with that piercing lucidity the afflicted sometimes produce at exactly the wrong moments, as though the mind, knowing it is leaving, wants one last look at what it's abandoning.

"What is it, sweetheart?"

And Ada almost told her. Everything. The radio, the stranger, the cassette she hadn't yet told anyone about—not even Ellis Vann, not yet, the tape sitting in her bag right now, Dominic's handwriting on the label, *Radiators — take 3, for Mom*, the words she had read forty times since finding it and still could not read aloud. She almost said: *Mama, the song you're humming, I know where it came from, I know whose it is, it's his, it's the boy's, it's your son's, his name was Dominic, his name was Dominic, his name was Dominic—*

Say it. Say it now, while she's looking at you. Say it while the window is open. What are you waiting for? What have you ever been waiting for?

"He wrote songs," Ada said.

Ines tilted her head. "Who did, sweetheart?"

"The boy. Your son. He wrote songs, and one of them was for you, and people are hearing it now, and they don't know his name, and I'm going to tell them. I'm going to fix it."

The sentences came out in a rush, half-confession, half-plea, and they were true, every word, and they were also nothing—they were a letter to no one—because Ines's face had already gone gentle and puzzled, the lucidity draining out of it like tide off a flat, and she said:

"That's nice, dear. Are you staying for lunch? They do a chicken thing on Saturdays. It's not bad."

* * *

Ada stayed for lunch. She helped her mother open the little packets of salt and pepper, cut the chicken thing into manageable pieces, deflected the question about whether she had children (asked four times, answered four times, each answer received as news). At one point Ines gripped her sleeve and said, "You're very kind. Do I know you?" and Ada said, "You know me, Mama," and Ines studied her face for a long time, searching it, and finally said, "You have sad eyes. My daughter has sad eyes too," and laughed at the coincidence.

Afterward they sat by the window again while the radiator ticked through its afternoon cycle, and Ines dozed in the light, chin dipping, hands quiet at last in her lap. Ada watched her and thought about the arithmetic of forgetting. Two decades ago, grief had taken Dominic whole. Last year it had taken her father's face. This year it was eating the middle years, the school years, the ordinary years, working outward from the wound like rot through fruit. Soon it would reach the boy at the piano. Perhaps it already had—perhaps the humming was all that remained, the song surviving its own author's author, a melody orphaned even in the mind that loved him first.

And Ada understood, sitting there in the lavender-and-bleach smell with her mother's cool hand under hers, that silence was not neutral. She had always told herself it was—that keeping quiet was keeping safe, that unsaid things caused no harm, that her invisibility hurt no one but herself. But the silence had a cost, and the cost compounded daily, like interest on a debt nobody was collecting. Every year she didn't speak the name, the world forgot a little more of him. Every year the song traveled farther from its source. Her mother was forgetting Dominic in real time, note by note, and Ada had chosen—had spent twenty years choosing—to let the forgetting happen unopposed, untestified, unwitnessed.

She had thought she was protecting herself. She saw now that she had simply been declining, year after year, to stand up in the courtroom of the world and say: *this happened, he existed, this was his.*

The cassette was in her bag, three feet away, in the outer pocket, against her hip. She could feel it there like a coal.

"Mama," she whispered, to the sleeping woman. "His name was Dominic. He loved you. He wrote you a song, and I'm going to make sure somebody knows."

Ines slept on. Outside the window, October was stripping the garden bare, one leaf at a time, patiently, thoroughly, the way it did every year, the way it would do again next year regardless of what anyone said or failed to say.

On the bus home, as the city scrolled past in reverse—the river, the mural, the dead laundromat—Ada took the cassette out of her bag and held it in both hands and read the label one more time. *Radiators — take 3, for Mom.* Dominic's handwriting, slanted and urgent, the letters of a nineteen-year-old who believed there would be time.

Then she turned it over, and on the back, in smaller letters, she found something she had missed—or refused to see—every previous time she'd held it: a phone number, seven digits, and beside it, in the same handwriting, a single word.

*Ellis.*
