## 15. Ines, Briefly Clear

The facility smelled of lavender and bleach, an argument between two kinds of forgetting—one that pretended to be kindness, one that admitted what it was. Ada signed the visitor log at 2:40 in the afternoon, a time she had chosen deliberately because it fell between lunch and the shuffleboard hour on the sun porch, when the corridors emptied and the staff retreated behind the nurses' station to drink bad coffee and speak in the low voices of people who had learned that most of their words went unheard anyway.

Her mother's room was at the end of the west wing, the last door before the window that looked out on a courtyard with a single birch tree and a bench no one sat on because the ground was always damp. Room 114. Ada knew the number the way she knew her own—without thinking, without wanting to.

She had been coming every Sunday for six years. Before that, twice a month. Before that, once, and then not for almost a year, in the raw period after the funeral home returned her mother's good coat to her, dry-cleaned, smelling of someone else's soap, and Ada had understood that there was no one left in the world who remembered Dominic except herself and a woman who was losing even that.

Six years of Sundays. And in all that time, her mother had never once looked at her and known her—not fully, not the way you know your own child, with recognition arriving whole like weather. There were fragments. There were moments when Ines Crane would study Ada's face with the intense, forensic patience of the very old and very lost, searching it for a name the way you search a drawer for keys, and sometimes she'd land on something adjacent—"You have kind hands," she said once, holding Ada's wrist, "are you the one who brings the books?"—and Ada would say yes, or say nothing, and the moment would pass through them both like light through water, touching everything, warming nothing.

Ada had gotten good at these visits. That was the shameful truth of it. She had developed a technique, a liturgy: she brought the same lemon drops from the shop on Delancey Street, she opened the blinds halfway so the room held light but not view, she read aloud from whatever novel she'd pulled from the returns cart that week—her mother had been a reader once, before, though now the sentences slid off her like rain off glass—and she stayed exactly fifty minutes, long enough to be a daughter, short enough not to become a witness to her own futility.

Today she had brought *The Collected Poems of Elizabeth Bishop*, because the week had been Bishop-shaped. Because Marty Fell had played his show at the Half-Light Club three nights ago and introduced "Radiators" by saying, into a hush you could hear the ice settling in, *This song was written by a man named Dominic Crane, who never got to record it, and I've been singing a ghost for thirty years.* Because Ellis Vann had aired the segment—the cassette, the story, Marty's voice cracking on the word *stolen*—and Ada had stood in the stacks at 3 a.m. listening to her brother's name travel across the sleepless city like a bird released indoors, battering against windows, finding the open sash at last.

Because the blog post had gone up yesterday calling her a fraud, and the library board had scheduled a meeting, and Dana Okafor had called twice, and Ada had spent the whole week being seen in ways she did not choose—and none of it, none of it, had made her brave enough for the thing she still owed.

So: Bishop. Lemon drops. Fifty minutes. The liturgy of avoidance, performed faithfully.

Ines was asleep when Ada came in, propped against her pillows at an angle that looked painful but apparently wasn't, her hands folded on the blanket like two pale birds that had given up flying. She had shrunk again since March. Her hair, white as paper, was thinning at the crown where the skin showed through, pink and vulnerable as a newborn's scalp. On the nightstand: a plastic cup of water, a hearing aid in its case, a photograph of a house Ada didn't recognize—or rather, recognized too well. It was the house on Fenwick Street, their house, the one they'd sold when Ada was twenty-three, and in the photograph the hydrangeas were blooming blue along the front walk, and standing on the porch steps, caught mid-laugh with his hand raised in a wave that would never finish, was a boy of seventeen with his father's jaw and his own impossible grin.

Dominic. The photograph had been there for years. The staff thought it was a grandson, maybe; someone had written *family* on the label in the chart. No one had ever asked Ada about it, and she had never offered, and the boy on the porch steps waved at the empty room all day and all night, at the nurses making their rounds, at the dark, at no one.

Ada set the lemon drops down beside the photograph. She opened the blinds halfway. She sat in the vinyl chair that groaned under her the way it always did, a small complaint she'd stopped hearing years ago, and she waited for her mother to wake, and while she waited she read Bishop silently to herself, the way you might rehearse lines for a play you dreaded performing.

*The art of losing isn't hard to master.*

Outside, the birch in the courtyard moved its leaves without wind, or with a wind too small to feel from here.

* * *

Her mother woke at ten past three, and for the first four minutes she was nobody's mother. She surfaced slowly, blinking at the ceiling, then at the window, then at Ada with the flat polite attention she gave to everyone—a hostess greeting a stranger at her own party. Ada performed the opening rites: hello, Mama, I brought lemon drops, I brought a book, shall I read to you. Ines nodded at intervals that may or may not have corresponded to the questions. Her eyes drifted to the photograph on the nightstand and away again, the way they always did, and Ada felt the old ache flex inside her chest, familiar as a scar.

Then Ada opened the book. And her mother said:

"You're the librarian."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't unusual—Ines often assigned Ada this identity, half-remembered from some conversation years ago, and Ada always accepted the role the way an actor accepts a cue. But then her mother went on, and her voice changed, gathered itself, came forward out of the fog with her whole face behind it, and Ada felt the air in the room go still.

"You work nights," Ines said. "I remember that. You used to come home at dawn with snow in your hair, and you'd stand in the kitchen eating cereal standing up, and you never told me anything." A pause. Her eyes were blue and clear and terrible. "You have her chin. Did anyone ever tell you that? You have my mother's chin."

"Mama."

"I know you." Ines pushed herself up against the pillows, and her hands were shaking, but her voice was not. "I know you. You're mine."

And Ada—who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of almost saying things, who could shelve a thousand books in silence, who could write letters she never sent and burn letters she couldn't send and keep the ones she couldn't burn in a shoebox under her bed like contraband—Ada felt every practiced sentence leave her at once. She was empty. She was nineteen again, standing in a funeral parlor with her throat locked. She was forty-three, letting a phone ring in a small apartment while her mother, somewhere across town, forgot which decade it was.

"Yes," Ada said. Her voice came out cracked. "Yes, Mama. I'm yours."

Ines smiled, and it was the smile from the photograph, from the porch, from the kitchen where a boy played piano badly and beautifully through the floorboards while their mother washed dishes and sang harmony off-key on purpose, to make him laugh. It was that smile, resurrected, aimed directly at Ada across six years of Sundays.

"The boy," Ines said.

Ada's heart stopped and restarted wrong.

"What boy, Mama?"

"You know." Impatience flickered across her face, the old impatience, the living woman's impatience, and it was so sudden and so real that Ada nearly laughed or wept or both. "Don't do that. Don't do the thing where you pretend. You always did that. Both of you. You'd break something and stand next to it looking innocent." She lifted one shaking hand and pointed—at the photograph, at the boy frozen mid-wave on the porch steps. "Him. My boy. He wrote a song."

The room tilted. Ada gripped the arms of the chair.

"In the basement," Ines said, and her voice dropped low, conspiratorial, the voice she'd used for secrets, for telling Ada where the Christmas presents were hidden, for saying *your father doesn't need to know how much this cost*. "On Miss Osgood's piano, God rest her, she'd have died if she knew. He wrote it down on the back of a phone bill. He said—" and here Ines closed her eyes, and her lips moved, retrieving, and Ada leaned forward without meaning to, leaned so far forward the chair creaked, "—he said, *it's for you, Mom. It's about the radiators. How they sing at night. You said nobody listens to them but me.*"

"He said that," Ada whispered. "Yes. He said that."

"Did he ever play it for anyone?" Ines opened her eyes, and they were sharp, urgent, frightened. "Because I can't find it. I look for it and I can't find it. The song. They took my things when I moved here, they said for safekeeping, and I can't find the song, and if I can't find it—" her breath hitched, and for a moment she was eighty-nine years old and drowning, "—then it's gone, isn't it? If nobody has it, it's like it never happened. Like he never happened."

And Ada Crane, keeper of uncatalogued holdings, guardian of a cassette labeled in her brother's handwriting that sat in a climate-controlled drawer eleven blocks away—Ada opened her mouth to tell her.

Everything. All of it. The radio, the stranger, the letter she burned and the letter she kept, the Half-Light Club, the confession, the broadcast, her brother's name going out over the airwaves at 3 a.m. while she stood in the poetry section crying among the returns. *He happened, Mama. He's happening right now. Strangers are learning his name. A man who stole him gave him back. I have his song, I found it in a box of unclaimed property, I've listened to it so many times the tape is wearing thin, and I'm going to record it myself even though I can't sing, and I'm sorry—I'm sorry about the phone call, I'm sorry about the funeral, I'm sorry I let you forget him alone, I love you, I never said it, I'm saying it now—*

"Mama," she said. "Listen. The song isn't lost. I found it. I have it. And last week it was on the radio, and the man who plays it told everyone whose it was, he said your son's name, he said *Dominic*, over and over, the whole city heard it, and there's more, there's so much more, I have to tell you all of it, I should have told you years ago—"

But the window was already closing.

She saw it happen. She had seen it happen a hundred times, the tide going out of her mother's face, the clarity draining like water through cupped hands—but never mid-sentence, never while Ada was finally, finally speaking. Ines's gaze slid off her. Off the room. Somewhere behind her eyes a door swung shut on its own weight, unhurried, indifferent, and the woman who had just demanded her son's song became a woman studying a stranger with kind hands.

"—and the radiators," Ines said pleasantly, to no one, to the ceiling, "sing at night. Did you know that? Nobody listens to them but me."

"Mama." Ada's voice broke on the word. "Mama, please. I'm not finished. Please, I only got to the middle—"

Ines turned to the window, to the birch moving its leaves, and hummed.

Four notes. Five. The melody rising out of her like smoke, unbidden, unowned, a thing her body had kept when her mind had surrendered everything else. She hummed "Radiators"—the verse, the turn, the little ascending phrase Dominic had stolen from the sound of their house at 2 a.m., pipes ticking, heat whispering through the walls—and she hummed it perfectly, note for note, and she did not know why.

Ada sat in the groaning chair and watched her mother's mouth shape a song she could no longer name, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the window did not open twice. That lucidity was not a door but a crack of light under one, and the light moved whether you followed it or not. That she had just spent the only clear minute her mother might have left talking about a radio broadcast instead of saying *I love you*—the sentence, the actual sentence, the one debt that no attribution, no co-credit, no broadcast could pay.

Fifty minutes. She stayed fifty minutes. She read Bishop aloud to a woman who heard weather. She put a lemon drop in her mother's palm and closed the fingers over it, and Ines said, "Thank you, dear, are you the one who brings the books?" and Ada said yes, yes, that's me, and walked out of Room 114 with her whole body shaking.

At the nurses' station she stopped. A young aide with kind eyes looked up.

"Mrs. Crane had a good day," the aide offered. "Really with us this afternoon. That doesn't happen much anymore."

"How much," Ada said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "How much longer does she have? Honestly. I need to plan something. I need—" *I need one more minute,* she did not say. *I need the window to open again. I need to be faster than the tide.*

The aide's kindness faltered into honesty. "Weeks," she said gently. "Maybe less. I'm sorry."

Ada signed the visitor log at 3:52. Outside, the afternoon was bright and ordinary, traffic moving, a man selling flowers from a cart on the corner, the world proceeding as if nothing irreplaceable were running out. She stood on the steps of the facility and dialed Ellis Vann's number from memory, and when he answered on the second ring, sleep-rough, wary, she said:

"It's Ada Crane. I need the recorder again. Tonight. And Ellis—" she pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the locked door behind it, "—I don't have weeks to get the words right. So I'm going to do it wrong. I'm going to do it tonight."
