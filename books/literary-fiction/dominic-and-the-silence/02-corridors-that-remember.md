## 2. Corridors That Remember

The mop bucket had a name. Someone — a janitor long since retired, or perhaps merely bored — had painted MARGARET on its side in flaking white letters, and Petra found he was grateful for it. It gave him something to say to her in the mornings. *Good morning, Margaret. Shall we?* And Margaret, being a bucket, asked nothing back.

He learned the practice wing the way a blind man learns a room: by touch, by echo, by the small betrayals of sound. The linoleum in the east corridor rang hollow over the old radiator pipes. The third-floor hallway had a draft that came from nowhere and went nowhere, whistling faintly through a window sealed sometime before Petra was born. The stairwell smelled of pencil shavings and floor wax and, underneath it all, something older — rosin, maybe, or just the memory of rosin, soaked so deep into the plaster that no amount of scrubbing would ever get it out.

He worked slowly. This was deliberate. A man who cleans quickly is a man with somewhere else to be, and Petra Vane had spent twenty years being nowhere in particular and had developed a strong preference for it.

* * *

The schedule Mr. Okonkwo handed him was typed on paper that had yellowed at the edges, as though it had been waiting for him in a drawer since the last century. *Practice Wing: Corridors 1–4, daily. Rooms 1–12, rotation.* Room 7 sat in the middle of the list like a stone in a shoe. He read past it three times before he let himself actually see it, then folded the schedule into quarters and put it in his breast pocket, where it lay against his heart like a warrant.

Not yet, he told himself. Not today.

There were twelve rooms in the old wing and eleven of them were just rooms. He could do eleven. He started with Room 3, which contained an upright piano with one dead key — the F above middle C, which meant that every student who practiced there learned to compose around the gap, building their scales with a hole in them, like dancers trained on a floor with a missing plank. Petra wiped the piano's lid and thought about how many hands had worn the varnish to a pale shadow in front of middle C. Two generations of fingers, polishing wood.

He emptied the wastebasket. In it: a coffee cup, three broken reeds (a clarinetist, then; wrong room, but the conservatory never respected boundaries), and a crumpled page of manuscript paper covered in a child's careful notation, crossed out, crossed out again, and finally stabbed through with a pencil so hard the point had snapped. Petra smoothed it out against his knee and looked at it for a long moment.

He remembered doing exactly that. Stabbing the page. The peculiar violence of it — not rage at the music, but rage at himself for failing to be worthy of it. He'd been nine the first time. Dominic had knelt beside him, picked up the pencil, and said, *Good. Now we know where you are. Let's find out where the music is.*

Petra put the page in his pocket instead of the bin. He couldn't have said why. It seemed rude to throw away someone's despair without asking.

* * *

By Thursday he had a system. Corridors first, while the students were still asleep or still pretending to be; rooms after ten, when the practicing started and he could work behind a wall of other people's sound. There was a particular pleasure in it, he discovered — cleaning while music happened elsewhere. The building hummed. Scales rose through the floors like heat. Somewhere below, a cellist worried the same four bars over and over, and Petra found himself conducting the mop handle in time until he caught himself and stopped, ears hot, grateful that Margaret kept his secrets.

He avoided the west end of the second corridor. This required no great artistry; the corridor simply ended there, at a door with a brass number screwed slightly crooked, and Petra's feet had apparently held a vote on the matter decades ago and never rescinded it. His body knew things his mind hadn't authorized. When he mopped toward that end of the hall, some quiet clerk inside him stamped the paperwork and turned him around at the water fountain, and off he went the other way, whistling — no, not whistling. He didn't whistle. But humming, almost. Something wordless and defensive.

The strange thing — the thing he kept noticing and kept refusing to notice — was that the corridor was shorter than he remembered. Everything was. The ceilings lower, the windows smaller, the grand staircase in the main hall reduced to something a doll might descend. Twenty years ago this building had been a cathedral. Now it was a school. He supposed that was what growing up meant, mostly: discovering that the cathedrals were schools all along, and the gods had been tired men in cardigans who needed their coffee.

Dominic had needed his coffee terribly. Three cups before noon, each one reheated twice because he'd forget it mid-lesson and wander off into some demonstration, playing Chopin with one hand and gesturing at the window with the other, saying things like *Do you hear how the light does that? That's rubato. The sun doesn't keep strict time either.*

Petra pushed the mop harder.

* * *

She appeared on Friday, materializing out of a practice room the way teenagers do — one moment the corridor was empty, the next there was a girl standing in it holding a violin case like a shield and looking at him with the frank, unembarrassed curiosity of someone who has not yet learned that questions have consequences.

"You're the new caretaker," she said. Not a question. An accusation, almost, as though he'd been keeping it from her personally.

"Janitor," Petra said. "Caretaker makes it sound like I take care of things."

"Don't you?"

He looked at her. She was perhaps seventeen, small and wiry, with hair that appeared to have been cut during an argument and a violin case covered in stickers — a planet, a cartoon frog, a faded logo from a summer festival he recognized and wished he didn't. Her bow tie — no, her collar — was inside out. There was rosin dust on her chin.

"The floors," he offered. "I take care of the floors."

"Ilsa," she said, sticking out the hand that wasn't holding the case. "Ilsa Beckett. You're new. I mean, obviously. I've memorized everyone here and you weren't in the inventory."

"The inventory," Petra repeated.

"Of people." She said it as though this were the most natural filing system in the world. "Faculty, staff, kitchen ladies, the scary registrar, the guy who fixes the radiators and hums. You're not any of them. So. New." She looked pleased with herself, and then, abruptly, businesslike. "Do you know anything about this building? Like, actual history? Because everyone says stuff but nobody agrees."

"What kind of stuff?"

"Like—" she shifted the case to her hip "—there's supposedly a room where something happened. Years ago. Before I was born. Marcus says a student smashed a piano. Priya says a teacher got fired. My teacher says nobody remembers and we should focus on our intonation." She wrinkled her nose. "Which is what she says about everything."

Petra's hands went very still on the mop handle. Margaret rocked gently in place, sloshing.

"Rooms remember things," he said carefully. "People don't. That's usually how it goes."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

Ilsa narrowed her eyes at him, and for one vertiginous second Petra saw it happen — saw the hunger come up in her face like weather, the same hungry look he'd once seen in his own mirror, the look of a young musician who has decided that the truth is a thing you can demand and receive if only you ask with enough force. He wanted to warn her. He had no idea what he'd be warning her about, or from whom, and that frightened him more than he cared to examine.

"Do you play?" she asked suddenly.

"No."

"You hold the mop like you do."

"It's a very musical mop."

She laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that gets students in trouble in corridors — and then a door opened somewhere down the hall and a voice called *Ilsa! Scales! Now!* and she rolled her eyes with the whole-body theatricality available only to the young, hoisted her case, and turned back.

"What's your name?" she demanded over her shoulder.

"Petra."

"Like the stones?"

"Like the stones."

"Huh." She considered this. "Okay, Petra-like-the-stones. If you ever want to tell me what happened in that room down there—" she pointed vaguely westward, and Petra's stomach dropped through several floors "—I'm a very good listener. Everyone says so. Well. Nobody says so. But I am."

And she was gone, into the room with the voice and the scales, leaving the corridor ringing.

* * *

That afternoon he cleaned the west end of the second corridor. Not Room 7 itself — his feet still filed their objection, and he let them win — but the stretch of hallway outside it, which he had been avoiding with increasing architectural precision all week. He mopped it slowly, properly, getting into the corners where the wax had built up in yellow strata like tree rings, each layer a year, each year a class of students who had walked this exact floor without knowing what had happened on it.

A boy passed him carrying a trumpet case and nodded, the way you nod at furniture. A faculty member hurried by reading a score, walking that particular bent-forward walk of someone hearing the music faster than they can play it. Nobody looked at Petra. Nobody looked at anybody. The building processed its children like a heart processes blood, endlessly, without sentiment, and Petra was just another corpuscle in the flow.

Except that the floor under his knees — he'd knelt to scrape gum, an indignity Margaret was spared — except that the floor was warm. Radiator pipes ran beneath the west corridor; he'd mapped them Monday. But this warmth felt different, felt specific, felt like the building remembering at him, and he sat back on his heels and let it, just for a moment, just alone in the empty hall with the light coming through the tall windows in long dusty blades.

*You're standing where I stood,* he thought. *Every day. You walk right past it.*

He didn't finish the thought. Finishing thoughts like that was how men ended up sitting on floors.

Instead he finished the corridor, wrung out Margaret, and wheeled her back toward the supply closet, passing the crooked brass 7 without turning his head. He was proud of that. He didn't turn his head. He looked straight ahead like a man with nothing behind him, and it was only later, emptying the bucket in the basement sink, that he noticed his hand had slipped into his pocket and was worrying the crumpled manuscript page — some child's crossed-out despair, stabbed through with a broken pencil — smoothing it, and smoothing it, and smoothing it, as though it were a letter he was waiting to be able to read.

The demolition notice went up on the staff board the next morning. He would see it at eight o'clock, between a flyer for a faculty recital and a lost-and-found listing for a black scarf, and everything he had arranged with himself — the slow work, the avoided corridor, the eleven rooms that were just rooms — would acquire a deadline.
