## 9. Letters, Middle Years

Petra had made a rule for himself: three letters a night. No more.

It wasn't discipline so much as self-preservation. He'd learned on the second evening — after the first letter and the one that followed it — that these pages did something to the architecture of a person. You went in thinking you were reading mail. You came out having been rearranged. Three letters was the dose his body could metabolize before sleep became a rumor instead of a practice.

He kept them in the order Dominic had written them, rubber-banded in bundles of ten like some accountant's idea of grief. The box smelled of dust and cedar and, faintly, improbably, of pencil shavings — the smell of the practice wing itself, as if the wood had exhaled into the paper over twenty years and given up trying to be anything else.

He read them at his kitchen table with the lamp angled away from the window, which was absurd, because no one was watching, but he'd spent two decades learning that habits of concealment outlive their reasons the way amputees feel weather in missing limbs.

The early letters had been about the rupture. These middle ones were about something worse: time.

* * *

The fourth letter began, *Petra — I have taken to writing to you the way other men take to talking to the sea. It is not correspondence if there is no reply, so let us call it what it is: I am practicing being unheard. I was, after all, your teacher. One should never ask a student to master anything one has not attempted oneself.*

Petra set the page down and laughed — an actual laugh, startled out of him, brief and cracked — and then found that the laugh had a floor that gave way, and beneath the floor was everything else.

That was the thing about Dominic's letters. They were funny. Nobody had warned him about that, least of all himself. In memory, Dominic Hale had been marble: the great man at the front of the studio, hands folded, listening to you ruin a Chopin etude with the patience of a geologist watching a glacier. But the man on the page was dry, wry, capable of describing the conservatory's new dean as "a man who believes music began in 1987 and peaked shortly thereafter." The humor didn't soften the letters. It sharpened them. It made them unmistakably alive, and every joke was a small fresh proof that this voice had gone on existing, year after year, in rooms Petra hadn't known existed, saying things meant for him that had been stopped at the door.

He read on.

*I saw a young man on the tram yesterday with your walk — that terrible impatient stride, as though the pavement were playing too slowly — and I nearly spoke to him. I did not, because he was perhaps nineteen and would have thought me mad, and because I have learned that grief makes one grab at strangers. But I mention it so you know: the city is full of false Petras now. I am learning to tell them from the original.*

*Original, if you are reading this: the lilac by the east wall bloomed. It blooms without your permission, which I have decided to find instructive.*

Petra pressed his thumb flat against the page, hard enough to whiten the nail, as if he could hold the sentence still long enough to live inside it.

* * *

By the seventh year, the letters changed key.

*I will tell you something I told no one at the time,* Dominic wrote, *because at the time it seemed like weakness, and I was raised by a generation of men who treated weakness the way organists treat a stuck key — you work around it and hope the audience doesn't notice.*

*My hands are going. Not dramatically — no tragedy, no falling chandelier. Just a slowing. A morning stiffness that takes longer to leave. Passages I could play asleep at forty now require negotiation. The doctors use words like "early" and "manageable," which are the two most dishonest words in medicine.*

*I tell you this because you were the last person I ever played for without calculation, and it seemed wrong for you to hear it from anyone else. Though of course you will not hear it at all, will you. You will hear it whenever you hear it, which may be never, which means I have just told my most guarded secret to a drawer.*

*The drawer is a good listener. It has your virtues: silence, attention, and an absolute refusal to interrupt with advice.*

Petra read that letter twice, then a third time, and somewhere in the third reading he understood that he was angry in a way he had never been angry in his life — not the hot anger of Room 7, not the shame-anger of the years after, but a cold, structural fury, the kind engineers feel when they discover the bridge they've been driving over was built without bolts.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it would not balance. Twenty years. Dozens of letters. And not one — not a single page in the box — bore any mark of return. No "returned to sender." No forwarding slip. Nothing. Letters do not simply vanish; institutions make them vanish. Someone had stood between this man and his student for two decades and called it administration.

He thought about Miriam Osei, the retired registrar, and the way she'd looked at him across her kitchen table — that careful, load-bearing silence she'd offered instead of answers. He put the thought down. Not yet. He wasn't ready to knock on that door until he knew exactly how much had been taken, and he was only beginning to count.

* * *

The tenth-year letters were written in a hand that had begun to lean.

Petra noticed it the way you notice a friend aging in photographs — suddenly, and then everywhere. The early letters marched across the page in that upright, severe script Dominic had used on exam comments, each stroke a small verdict. By the middle years the lines tilted eastward, the loops loosening, the pen pressing harder as if the hand were trying to hold the thought still by force.

*I have taken to dictating some of this to my neighbor's daughter, who types faster than I write and judges slower than I deserve. She thinks I am writing a memoir. I have not corrected her. There is a certain freedom in being misread by the young; I recommend it highly. You would be good at it. You always were.*

*I played for the first time in eight months last week. Schubert, the B-flat sonata, only the first movement, only for myself. My left hand betrayed me in the trills and I stopped and sat there in the quiet and do you know what I felt? Relief. Enormous, disgraceful relief. All those years I believed the music was the thing keeping me upright, and it turns out I can set it down and remain standing. Nobody tells you that. Nobody tells you that the thing you fear losing might be the thing holding you down.*

*I would have told you that, once. You'd have argued. God, how you'd have argued. You argued like other people breathe — automatically, and with more energy than the situation required.*

Petra had to stop. He got up, ran water into a glass he didn't drink, and stood at the sink looking at the dark window where his own face hung faintly against the night — older than the student in the letters, younger than the man he'd expected to be. Somewhere in the glass, for half a second, both faces occupied the same space.

"You'd have argued," he said aloud to the room, testing the words. His voice sounded borrowed.

* * *

Then came the intermediaries.

The thirteenth-year letter was different in kind — shorter, harder, written fast, the pen gouging the paper.

*Petra. I have done something desperate and I want it on record that I know it is desperate.*

*I hired a man. An investigator — a retired policeman who plays cello badly and charges moderately. I have asked him to find you. I don't know where you went. Vienna, someone said. Then Prague. Then nothing, which is its own address. I have spent years telling myself that your silence was chosen, that you built your life elsewhere and nailed the door shut, and that a decent man respects a nailed door.*

*I have decided I am not a decent man. I am an old one, and old men get one extravagance, and mine is going to be this: I am going to find you if it takes the rest of my money and whatever dignity remains, and I am going to stand wherever you are and say the things I should have said in Room 7, and then, if you wish it, I will turn around and never bother you again. That is the offer. Silence forever, purchased honestly, with the transaction conducted face to face like adults rather than through the post like ghosts.*

*If you are reading this, the man failed, or succeeded, or was intercepted — I genuinely no longer know which of those is possible in this building. Ask yourself, whoever you are, holding this: why does a school need to intercept mail? Schools exist to pass things along. That is the entire job.*

Petra sat very still.

The investigator. Of course there had been an investigator. And of course the trail had gone cold, because trails don't go cold on their own — they're chilled, deliberately, by institutions with filing systems and motives. He thought of Wren's smooth coffee-pouring courtesy, of the phrase *we want to honor the past appropriately*, of the way the word *appropriate* had done so much heavy lifting in that conversation it deserved hazard pay.

He flipped ahead through the bundles, no longer rationing himself, three-letter rule abandoned like a New Year's resolution in February. The fourteenth year. The fifteenth. Each letter referenced the search — *my man has traced you to a hotel in Leipzig, or a man using your name, which may be the same thing* — and then, in the sixteenth year:

*The investigator has resigned. He returned my retainer, which I respect, and refused to explain, which I don't. He said only: "Some doors are watched, maestro." I have thought about that sentence for eleven days. I have taught in this conservatory for thirty-one years and I am now afraid of its mailroom. Write that sentence again and look at it. This is what we have become — a place where an old man must hire a policeman to say goodbye to a boy, and even the policeman loses his nerve.*

Petra closed his eyes. Behind them, unbidden: the registrar's office as it had been in his student years — the brass grille, the hum of the pneumatic tube, Miriam Osei younger, efficient, kind-eyed, stamping things. *Some silences are load-bearing.* She had said it like a woman quoting herself.

* * *

The illness announced itself in the letters the way weather announces itself to sailors: gradually, then all at once.

*The word they used today was "progressive,"* Dominic wrote in the seventeenth year, *which is a strange comfort, since progress has been the family business. I have decided to treat my diagnosis the way I treated your generation's interpretations — with skepticism, close attention, and a refusal to be hurried.*

But the jokes thinned. The letters shortened. And then, in the eighteenth year, a letter arrived — reached Petra's table, rather — that was barely a page, the handwriting large and loose, a stranger's hand wearing Dominic's name.

*They tell me I should put my affairs in order. My affairs consist of a piano, a cat of poor character, and approximately eighteen years of unsent letters to you. The piano goes to the school. The cat goes to the neighbor. The letters go to the drawer, because the drawer has never once failed to receive them.*

*I am not writing to you tonight, Petra. I am writing past you — to the man you'll be when this box finds you, if it ever does. I hope he is kinder to himself than the boy was. I hope he still walks too fast. I hope somebody feeds him properly; you were always disgraceful about meals.*

*That's all. The hand tires. Even the anger tires, and I find I'm grateful — anger was always the least interesting thing either of us did.*

Petra read it four times. On the fourth reading he realized his cheeks were wet and had been for some time, and that the sound in the kitchen was his own breathing, ragged, untrained, the breathing of a man who had never learned that particular technique because no one had ever taught him that breathing was something you'd need to practice.

He let it happen. It seemed, finally, like the correct interpretation of the text.

* * *

Near midnight, he laid the letters out on the floor of his small apartment in chronological rows, all of them, dozens of pages making a paper timeline of a life conducted entirely in one direction. He walked along it slowly, hands behind his back like a museum visitor, and the curator in his head supplied dates and diagnoses and the slow westward drift of a handwriting surrendering its posture.

Twenty years of goodbye, stolen. Not lost — stolen. Filed, intercepted, routed, buried behind a wardrobe by someone who had decided that an institution's reputation outweighed a dying man's farewell, and who had then hidden the evidence in the one room scheduled for destruction, as if demolition were a form of shredding.

And the institution — the same institution, the same corridors, the same coffee poured by the same smooth hands — was now offering Petra Vane a symbolic role in a closing ceremony. A ribbon. A speech. A place at the podium while the wing came down.

They weren't honoring the past. They were scheduling its funeral before anyone could read the will.

Petra gathered the letters back into their bundles with exaggerated care, the way you handle a thing when roughness would mean admitting what it's doing to you. At the bottom of the box, beneath the final bundle, his fingers found something he'd missed before: a thin envelope, smaller than the rest, unstamped, addressed not to him but to *M. Osei, Registrar's Office — PERSONAL & CONFIDENTIAL* — in Dominic's tilting late hand.

He turned it over twice. He did not open it. Some doors, he was learning, you knocked on in person.

Miriam Osei had told him once that some silences were load-bearing. Fine. Tomorrow he intended to find out exactly what they were holding up — and what it would cost everyone, her included, when he pulled them out.
