## 8. Edna's Alphabet

The notebooks came home with me in a Bankers Box, the kind sold for storing tax records, which is what most lives are reduced to eventually: receipts for things you can no longer verify you owned.

I want to say I carried them into the house with reverence, set them on the kitchen table under a lamp, and began with the patience of a scholar. What actually happened is that I carried them in, set them down, ate the dinner Priya had left under foil — chicken, slightly overdone, the way she cooks when she's stopped expecting anyone to notice — and then told her I had some work to do in the study.

"Work," she said. Not a question. A word placed on the table between us like a coin someone suspects is counterfeit.

"The notebooks. I want to get a head start on the inventory before the appraiser comes."

She looked at the box. She looked at me. She has an accountant's gift for knowing when a ledger doesn't balance, and I watched her decide, visibly, not to audit me that night. "Okay," she said, and carried her plate to the sink, and the water ran a long time, longer than dishes require, which is a thing I noticed and filed and did nothing about.

* * *

Edna's cipher filled eleven notebooks, plus a loose sheaf of index cards and, inexplicably, the back pages of a 1974 seed catalog, where words sprouted in the margins among the tomato varieties like a second, stranger crop. I spread them across the study floor in chronological order, or what I guessed was chronological order, and sat down among them at nine in the evening with a legal pad and a feeling I would later recognize as the exact opposite of what I'd told Priya it was.

The language had no name, or if it did, the name was itself in the language, which is the sort of circular joke Edna would have enjoyed. She'd started it, as far as I could reconstruct, sometime in the late fifties — the earliest notebook was a composition book with a cover gone soft and gray as moleskin, its pages ruled in the wide-spaced lines of grade school. The first entries were simple substitution: ordinary English words written in a grid cipher, the kind a child might devise, A in one corner, B beside it, the whole alphabet folded into a box. *The cat sat on the mat* rendered as pure geometry.

But Edna hadn't stayed a child, and neither had the cipher.

By the second notebook the grid had broken open. She'd begun inventing words — real coinages, not substitutions — and here is the thing I understood only around two in the morning, sitting cross-legged on the floor with my spine making its opinions known: she wasn't encoding English. She was building a language. A small one, a private one, but a language, with roots and derivations, with words that leaned on other words the way beams lean on columns.

I know something about load paths. It's the first thing they teach you: every load has to travel somewhere, and your job is to make sure the path it travels doesn't end in something that fails. Edna had built a language where every word's load passed through another word, and the foundation words — the ones she'd coined first, the deep ones — carried everything built on top of them.

The final sentence in the last notebook, the one I'd found in the study, the one that had been sitting at the bottom of her life like a stone at the bottom of a creek, used those foundation words. The old ones. The ones from the composition book, from the beginning, from whatever year and whatever circumstance had made a woman decide she needed a place to say things that English couldn't be trusted to hold.

I didn't translate anything that first night. I want to be clear about that, because the version of this story I tell myself has me making steady progress, a professional man applying professional methods. What actually happened is I sat on the floor until three a.m. copying out grids, and then I went to bed and lay next to my sleeping wife composing sentences in a dead woman's private alphabet, and the ceiling above me was very white and very far away.

* * *

The language evolved in layers, and I learned to read its strata the way I read a core sample.

The fifties layer was substitution cipher — playful, almost coy, the secrecy itself the point. The early sixties layer introduced the first true coinages, and they clustered, I noticed, around a small set of concepts. There was a word that meant roughly *a room where two people have agreed to be honest*, and a word for *the hour after everyone else has gone to bed*, and a word I translated provisionally as *the particular silence of a house that is listening to you*. None of these were words English needed. All of them were words Edna apparently did.

The seventies layer got grammatical. She developed a tense that had no English equivalent — a construction marked by a small hook she drew over the verb — and it took me two nights to work out what it did. It wasn't past, present, or future. It was a tense for things that had happened and were still happening. *The summer we invented words* would take the hook. *My mother's hands kneading bread* would take the hook. Things that were over and refused to be.

I sat with that one a long time. I thought about my own marriage, about the way Priya and I had built a tense like that without meaning to — every finished argument still running underneath the current ones, every old disappointment hooked over its verb, still happening.

The eighties and nineties layers were mostly maintenance. She kept the language alive the way you keep a garden alive — not expanding it so much as tending it, using it, walking its rows. There were grocery lists in it. There was, in the 1974 seed catalog, a complete recipe for dill pickles, rendered in full, in a script so confident it barely needed the key. She'd written it for no one. Or for herself. Or for the language, which needed to be spoken to stay alive, and she was its only speaker, and she spoke to it daily the way you'd speak to a caged bird so it wouldn't forget its own song.

And the last notebook — the one from the final years, the one with the unfinished sentence — had almost nothing in it. A few dates. A few names of plants. And then, on the last written page, in handwriting that had thinned but not shaken, the sentence.

I had copied it onto an index card and taped the card above my desk, which is a thing people do with scripture, and I was aware of the resemblance and did it anyway.

* * *

Priya noticed on the fourth night.

"You're doing the thing," she said. She was standing in the study doorway in her robe, and the hallway clock behind her said 1:40, and I was on the floor again, surrounded by paper, and I suppose from where she stood it looked less like scholarship than like a man building a nest out of his grandmother's homework.

"What thing."

"The thing where you disappear into a problem because the problem can't disappoint you." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse. Heat would have meant she still expected the conversation to go somewhere. "Your bridges, your spreadsheets, your — " she gestured at the floor, at the seed catalog, at all of it — "whatever this is."

"It's the estate inventory," I said, and heard, as I said it, how the sentence had a hairline fracture running through it. "I told you. The appraiser comes next week."

"Mm." She leaned against the doorframe. In the lamplight her face was half-lit and half-dark, and I thought, with the clarity that arrives only at the worst times, that I could look at her forever and still somehow not see her. "Theo. I'm not angry. I want to say that first. I'm — " she searched for the word the way you search a drawer for a tool you own but haven't used in years — "I'm taking attendance. You're here but you're not here, and I'm noting it. That's all. Noting it."

"Okay," I said.

"Okay," she said, and stood there one second longer, giving me a doorway to walk through, and then she went to bed, and I stayed on the floor, and the doorway closed the way doorways do when nobody uses them — not with a sound, just with the room becoming one room again instead of two.

I want to record that I considered following her. I did consider it. The consideration took perhaps four seconds and then the composition book was open again and Edna's fifties-era grid was in front of me, and I told myself the lie that fluent liars tell, which is: *this is for her, this is for us, once I understand the sentence everything will be explainable, everything will balance.* I believed it the way you believe a bridge is sound because you've walked across it so many times without falling. Which is not evidence. That's just luck with a good posture.

* * *

Here is what I worked out by the end of the week, running on five hours of sleep a night and a diet of coffee and the chicken Priya left under foil, which I ate standing up like a raccoon:

The final sentence was old. Not old in its writing — she'd written it in the last years, the handwriting proved that — but old in its vocabulary. Every word in it came from the earliest strata, the composition book, the first coinages, the foundation layer. She had reached back sixty years to build it. It was as if she'd saved the original timbers all her life and finally, at the end, built the thing they were meant for.

Six words. I could isolate six words with reasonable confidence, because three of them appeared elsewhere in the corpus and I had their meanings cold.

Word one: a pronoun, first person. *I.* It appeared two hundred times; it was the most stable word in the language, the one thing Edna never revised.

Word two: a verb, and this one cost me most of a night. It appeared in the pickle recipe (*stay* the jars in a cool place — no, *keep* them, *let them remain*), in a 1963 entry about a storm (*the lights stayed out for two days*), and once, devastatingly, in a margin note beside a photograph I found pressed between pages: a young woman with dark hair and Edna's same stubborn chin, and beneath the photo, in cipher, the hooked tense, the tense of things over and still happening, and the verb *stay*. The photograph had no name on it. I'll come back to that, I told myself. I'll come back to everything.

Word three: a conjunction or a purpose marker — Edna used it to mean *in order that*, *so that*, the little hinge word that makes one clause exist for the sake of another. It appeared in sentences like *I planted beans so that we would have beans*, which is the least mysterious sentence ever constructed, and I was grateful to it for its dullness.

Word four: also a pronoun. Second person. *You.* Nearly as stable as *I*. The two of them appearing together in one sentence — *I*, *you* — in a language she'd built alone and spoken alone for sixty years, was doing something to my chest that I chose not to examine.

Word five: a modal, some flavor of *could* or *might* or *was able to*. The corpus had three near-matches and I couldn't yet tell them apart, and the difference mattered, the way the difference between a stress crack and a shrinkage crack matters — one is cosmetic, one is the whole deck letting go.

Word six: nothing. A word I could not find anywhere else in eleven notebooks, a seed catalog, and forty index cards. A word she had coined and used exactly once, at the end, in the sentence that mattered most. A hapax legomenon, the scholars call it — a word that occurs only once in a body of text, and which is therefore, practically speaking, untranslatable. You can't derive a meaning from a single occurrence. A single occurrence is just a shape. A single occurrence is a door with no hinges.

I sat on the floor of my study at two in the morning holding that fact the way you'd hold a piece of evidence you wish you hadn't found.

She had invented a word for this sentence and only this sentence. Which meant she had needed a word that her language — sixty years of it, a whole architecture of privacy and precision — did not contain. She'd had to build one more room onto the house at the very end.

* * *

On the sixth night I didn't sleep at all. I want to be honest about the mechanics of it, because compulsion is never the cinematic thing they show you, the wild-eyed man tearing his hair. Compulsion is quiet. Compulsion is *one more page*. It's the reasonable voice saying the grid on the next leaf might contain the modal verb's third variant, and the grid after that might contain a use of the purpose-marker in a construction that clarifies its scope, and the reasonable voice is right, that's the horror of it, it's always *technically* right, and so the night is spent one page at a time, and then it's four a.m., and then it's the birds, and Priya's alarm goes off at six and I hear her get up and pause outside the study door — I hear her pause, a full three seconds, a held breath of a pause — and then continue to the kitchen.

At seven she came in and stood over me. I was still on the floor. I had the composition book open on my knees like a kid doing homework he'd sworn was finished.

"Look at you," she said, and her voice wasn't angry, it was something past anger, some country beyond the border where anger runs out and what's left is a kind of weathered grief. "You look like you did the year your mother left. Do you know that? I've only seen the pictures, and you look exactly like the pictures."

"I'm close," I said. "Priya, I'm actually close. The sentence — most of it —"

"The sentence." She said it flatly, testing the word's load-bearing capacity, and finding it hollow. "The sentence is going to fix what, exactly? I genuinely want to know. You decode it, and then what? Does it put the house back? Does it put — " she stopped herself, and I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and the unfinished sentence sat between us, and I thought, with a lunatic's clarity, *Edna would have had a word for this. Edna built a whole language because English didn't have the words, and I am standing in an unfinished sentence right now and I don't have the word for it either.*

"I don't know," I said. "I think it names something. I think she gave something up for us, for me, and she wrote down what it was, and nobody ever read it, and I —" My voice did something unreliable. I steadied it. "I think she's the reason I know how to stay in a room without saying anything. I think I learned it from her. I'd like to know what it cost her."

Priya looked at me for a long moment. And then she did the thing that I will be turning over for the rest of my life, whatever else happens: she crouched down, picked up the nearest notebook — the seed catalog, the pickle recipe, the daily speech to the caged bird — and set it gently on top of the pile, the way you'd close a door on your way out of a room you've decided not to argue in.

"Come to bed," she said. "The notebooks will still be here tomorrow."

And the terrible thing, the thing I have to write down because this notebook of mine doesn't lie even when I do, is that I said, "In a minute," and she went, and I did not follow, and the minute lasted until sunrise.

* * *

It was the photograph that broke the week open.

Saturday morning, gray light, Priya gone to her sister's — she'd started doing that, small evacuations, and I had started not asking when she'd be back, and the two of us were conducting a whole marriage in the grammar of things unsaid. I was on the floor again, going back through the 1963 storm entry, trying to pin down the modal verb, when the photograph slipped from between the pages where I'd re-shelved it carelessly, and landed face up, and I looked at the young woman with Edna's chin, and I looked at the cipher beneath it — the hooked tense, the staying verb — and I did something I should have done on the first night.

I checked the date.

Not on the photograph; there wasn't one. On the notebook. The entry adjacent to the photograph, the storm entry, the one about lights staying out for two days. The composition book's pages weren't dated, but the storm was. I looked up the county records the way I look up everything, with the dumb persistence of a man who trusts databases more than his own memory, and the storm was June 1961, and I sat very still on the floor of my study while something rearranged itself in the dark behind my eyes.

1961. The year, I realized, that I had never once heard mentioned in Edna's house. Not in forty years of summers. There was a seam in her stories, a place where the fabric changed weave — before it, she talked about dances and her sister and a red coat; after it, she talked about us, about the garden, about bridges and weather and the price of things. A whole decade of her life had a load path that simply terminated. No column. No footing. The load went into the ground and vanished.

And the photograph — the young woman with the stubborn chin, unnamed, pressed between pages, captioned in a language built for saying what English couldn't be trusted to hold — the photograph was from before the seam.

I sat there until the light moved across the floor and climbed the far wall. Then I got my keys.

The county records office closed at noon on Saturdays, and I made it with eleven minutes to spare, and the clerk, a young man with the patient boredom of someone who serves the public, pulled the property files for Edna's parcel, and I went back through the deed history with my finger, and there it was, in the chain of title, in the flat bureaucratic type that doesn't know what it's carrying:

Two names on the deed, 1958 through 1961. *Edna V. Marsh* — and a second name I had never heard in my life, never seen on a Christmas card, never heard invoked at a funeral or a kitchen table, a name that had been removed from the record the way a cracked column gets removed from a load path: quietly, structurally, so the rest of the house could stand.

I stood in the records office holding the photocopy, and outside the Saturday traffic went by, and a woman laughed on the sidewalk, and the world continued in its cheerful indifference, and I understood two things at once, the way you understand a failure only after the inspection — never during, always after.

The first thing: Edna's language hadn't begun as a private diary code. It had begun, in 1958, as a language for two.

The second thing: whoever had erased the second name had not been able to erase the notebooks. Somewhere in that house — in the boxes I'd already carried home, in the rooms I hadn't opened, in the attic I had been avoiding because attics are where houses keep what they can't hold — there was a second half to this story. Maybe a second set of handwriting. Maybe letters. Maybe the reason the final sentence reached back to the oldest words, the words from the beginning, the words from the year there were two people to speak them.

I drove home with the photocopy on the passenger seat like a witness.

The house was dark. Priya still wasn't back. I stood in the kitchen in the gray afternoon light, and I looked at the attic hatch above the hallway, the cord hanging down like a question, and I got the stepladder.
