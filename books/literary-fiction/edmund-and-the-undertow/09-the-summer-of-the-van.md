## 9. The Summer of the Van

The summer I turned twelve, my parents bought a van.

It was a conversion van, the kind with wood-paneled sides and a mural of a seagull arcing over a sunset painted on the rear door, which my father called "freedom" and my mother called "the boat we're all going to drown in." It had a table that folded down into a bed, a cooler that didn't cool, and a ceiling so low that my father, who was six foot two and shrinking by then in ways that had nothing to do with his spine, had to travel through it bent at the waist like a man perpetually entering a low doorway he refused to acknowledge.

They bought it because they believed a family that drove together stayed together. This is a thing people believed in 1989, the way they once believed in bleeding as a cure. The idea was that if you put four people in a rolling living room and pointed it at national parks, whatever was rotting between the two adults would be outvoted by scenery. My mother said it would be "an adventure." My father said it would "get us out of the house." Neither of them said what they actually meant, which was that the house had become a place where every silence had a history and every conversation a body count, and maybe on the open road, with nothing behind them but mile markers, they could be people who hadn't yet done whatever it was they'd done.

I knew about the rot the way kids know about weather. Not the science of it, just the pressure in your ears before the storm.

What I remember most about that summer isn't the van. It's the porch at Edna's house, where the van was parked for six weeks while my parents took what they called "a trial run" to Door County without me, and Edna and I invented a language.

* * *

Edna's porch faced west, which meant that from roughly seven o'clock onward the whole world went the color of peach preserves, and anything you said on it sounded more important than it was. There were two rocking chairs and a swing that complained on its chains like an old woman being asked to get up, and a rail worn smooth in two places where hands had rested for fifty years. I used to think those smooth spots were from Edna and Grandpa, but Grandpa died when I was four, and when I asked her once whose hands made them, she looked at the rail for a long time and said, "Mine and somebody else's," and changed the subject with the skill of a magician palming a coin.

The language started as a joke. That's how all important things start, probably, including marriages.

We were shelling peas — Edna believed children should have their hands busy while their mouths worked, some farm-bred theory of hers — and I asked her why English didn't have a word for the feeling of the last day of summer vacation. You know the one. It's not sadness exactly. It's the specific ache of knowing something is ending while it's still happening, so you can't enjoy it, only mourn it slightly in advance, like eating dessert while reading the check.

"There should be a word for that," I said.

"Then make one," Edna said, not looking up from her peas. "English is just words somebody made up and got away with."

So I made one. *Vessaline*, I called it — I don't know where it came from; it arrived whole, the way words do when you're twelve and your brain is still soft enough to surprise itself. And Edna stopped shelling, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read then and have spent thirty years trying to translate, and said, "That's a good one. Write it down."

"Why?"

"Because if you don't write it down, it isn't real. A word that lives only in your head dies with you. A word on paper can outlive everybody who ever spoke it." She said this lightly, like she was talking about grocery lists, but something under it wasn't light at all. I heard the undertow even then. I just didn't know what it was pulling at.

She got a notebook — a marbled composition book, the black-and-white kind, with a rubber band around it to hold shut whatever lived inside — and she wrote *vessaline* on the first page in her small, upright hand, the handwriting of someone who'd learned penmanship when it was a moral discipline. Then she handed me the pencil and said, "Your word. Your entry. Define it."

And that was the beginning. That was the whole beginning, though neither of us knew it. Just a porch, a bowl of peas, and a woman deciding that a twelve-year-old's throwaway word deserved to be written down and kept.

* * *

By mid-July we had forty words. By August, ninety. We had rules, because Edna believed languages without rules were just noises, and the rules were hers and they were strict:

One: no word could be borrowed straight from English. If it already existed, we didn't need it. The language existed for what English refused to carry.

Two: every word needed a definition, and the definition had to be true. Not dictionary-true. Life-true. Edna would reject definitions on the grounds that they were "pretty but false," which I learned was the worst thing a sentence could be.

Three: the language was ours. Not mine, not hers — ours. She was very firm on this. "A secret shared by two people is a house," she said. "A secret kept by one person is a coffin." I laughed when she said it because it sounded funny, and she let me laugh, and only years later did I understand she hadn't been joking.

The words themselves came out of the summer like water out of a tap. We coined *harrowlight*, the gold slant of late afternoon that makes even a junkyard look forgiven. We coined *brack*, the taste left in your mouth after an argument you won. We coined *sennight-hush*, the quiet of a house where everyone is home but nobody is speaking, which Edna defined, in her own handwriting, as "the sound a family makes instead of talking." She wrote that one looking straight ahead, out at the yard, and I copied it into my half of the notebook without asking why, because I was learning already — twelve years old and learning — which questions cost too much to ask.

Because here's the thing I want to be honest about, since honesty is supposedly the point of all this: even that summer, even at its best, even in the peach-preserve light with the peas and the pencil, there was a fault line running under everything, and I stood on it daily without falling in only because I didn't know it was there.

The fault line was my parents' marriage, obviously. But it was also something else, something older, and the language itself was sitting right on top of it, and I didn't see that either.

* * *

Here's what I noticed without noticing: Edna never used our words outside the porch.

At dinner, when my mother called from the van trip to say they'd extended another week (a week! I was eleven-going-on-abandoned), and Edna said "we'll manage fine," I waited for her to say *vessaline*, or anything, some private word to soften it between us. She didn't. On the porch, the language bloomed. In the kitchen, in front of anyone else, it was dead as Latin. Once, when I slipped and used *harrowlight* in front of my aunt Carol, Edna cut in fast — "He means the evening light, Carol, the boy's a poet" — and steered us away so smoothly I almost missed it. Almost. But I was keeping a notebook now. I noticed things.

Another thing: sometimes, mid-word-coinage, Edna would go strange. We'd be working on a new entry and I'd suggest a root and she'd stop, pencil hovering, and look at the middle distance with an expression like a woman listening to a radio station that had gone off the air decades ago. Once I asked what she was thinking and she said, "Trying to remember if we ever used this sound," and I said, "Used it for what?" and she said, "For something," and wrote the word down in a way that closed the subject like a door.

And one more thing, the one that matters most, the one I've saved until now because I've spent my whole life saving it until now: there was a second rubber band on the notebook drawer.

The composition book we wrote in lived on the porch table all summer, fat and dog-eared, our shared scripture. But the drawer of Edna's desk — the one she opened only when she thought I wasn't watching, the one that stuck and required a particular lift-and-jiggle she'd perfected — held other notebooks. Older ones. Different covers. I saw them exactly twice. Once when she was getting stamps, once when a gust of wind blew the porch door open hard enough to rattle the desk, and both times she closed the drawer with a speed that told me everything except the content.

I was twelve. I assumed they were old word notebooks. Earlier drafts. Why wouldn't they be? The language had to come from somewhere.

I was right, and I was wrong in the way that only a child can be wrong — completely, and with total logical confidence. They were old word notebooks. They just weren't ours. They weren't even hers alone. Somewhere in that drawer was a handwriting that wasn't Edna's, paired with hers, entry by entry, two hands building a private tongue across a hundred pages — a language that had been invented once before, on some other porch, in some other light, by two people who needed words for things English refused to carry. And one of those two people had been erased so thoroughly that by 1989 there was no photograph of her on any wall, no name in any story, no trace anywhere except inside a locked drawer and, apparently, inside the deep grammar of a language a grandmother was now teaching her grandchild without teaching her where it came from.

But none of that I knew. What I knew in the summer of the van was this: the best hours of my life were happening on a west-facing porch with a woman who kept a locked drawer, and my parents were somewhere north of here failing to save their marriage with a seagull mural, and I had invented a word for the last day of summer vacation and it was going to come true in September whether I wanted it to or not.

* * *

The van came back on a Tuesday in late August, and I heard it before I saw it — that engine had a knock in it like a polite cough, always announcing bad news. I was on the porch with Edna, adding *lumen-brack* to the notebook, which we'd coined that afternoon for the shadow a lamp throws that's darker than the dark around it. (Edna's definition: "the proof that light has edges." Mine: "my parents' bedroom at 2 a.m." She let me keep mine.)

The van pulled into the drive and sat there ticking, and nobody got out for a full minute, which even at twelve I understood was a very long minute. When the doors finally opened, my mother came up the porch steps first, smiling the smile she'd been practicing in the visor mirror — I know because I saw her do it later, in the driveway, thinking no one was watching, rehearsing *everything's fine* like lines in a play she'd been recast into against her will. My father followed with the bags, moving carefully, the way men move when they've decided the marriage will survive and are daring it to disagree.

"How was it?" Edna asked, and my mother said, "Wonderful," and my father said, "Really something," and both of them said it in the same flat pitch, the same rhythm, like they'd coordinated it, like they'd rehearsed in the van during the long drive home, two people harmonizing on a song about how nothing happened.

Edna said, "Well. Supper's at six," and went inside, and my mother followed, and my father stood on the porch a moment looking at the notebook lying open on the table, at the columns of words in two handwritings, and something crossed his face — not jealousy exactly. Recognition. He knew a coded document when he saw one. He'd been married to my mother for fifteen years; their whole house ran on one.

"What's all this?" he said.

"A language," I said. "Me and Grandma made it up. For stuff English doesn't have words for."

My father looked at the page a long time. Then he smiled — and I want to be fair to him here, because he was not a monster, he was just a man standing on a fault line of his own — and he said, "That's nice, kiddo," in the voice adults use for things children make that they believe will be thrown away by winter. Paper boats. Crayon drawings. Languages.

Then he picked up the bags and went inside, and the screen door clapped shut behind him, and Edna came back out with a dish towel over her shoulder and sat down across from me and picked up the pencil.

"Now," she said. "Where were we."

Where were we. As if the van hadn't come back. As if the flat-voiced *wonderful* hadn't happened ten feet away. As if the whole detonation that was coming — and it came, God knows it came, that whole next year unspooling like a slow-motion film of a bridge failing, lawyers and custody schedules and my mother's new apartment that smelled like someone else's cigarettes, my father's new silence that smelled like nothing at all — as if none of it could touch the porch, because the porch had its own country now, with its own laws and its own tongue, and citizenship was limited to two.

We coined three more words that evening. I don't remember what they were. I remember the light going from peach to ash, and the crickets starting up, and Edna writing in her small upright hand, and the rubber band waiting in the drawer below us like a heartbeat under a floor.

Years later — much later, after the auction notice, after the notebooks, after everything — I would understand what she was doing that summer. Not the whole of it. But the shape of it. She was building me an ark. She could see the flood coming; she'd survived one herself, decades ago, a flood with a name that got sanded off the family record, and she knew you cannot stop a flood, you can only build above the waterline. So she built a language. A sealed room inside ordinary speech where a kid could store the unsayable — the last days, the flat voices, the darker-than-dark shadows — until he was old enough to open it.

A secret shared by two people is a house. She told me that herself.

I just didn't ask, that summer, who had helped her build the first one — or why the deed to it had been burned.

The pencil moved. The light failed. Down in the drawer, behind the jiggle-lock, the older notebooks waited in the dark, holding half of every word we thought we were inventing, and I sat on the porch of that house feeling, for the last time in my childhood, completely safe — not knowing that safety was the one thing in the world Edna's language had no word for, because she had never once, in her entire life, been able to afford it.
