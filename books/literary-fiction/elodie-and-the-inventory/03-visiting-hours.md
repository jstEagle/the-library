## 3. Visiting Hours

The bus to Fernwood ran every forty minutes on Sundays and I had learned to time my grief to its schedule. That was the trick nobody told you about a parent in care: the visiting became a route, and routes forgive you for not thinking about where they lead.

I sat in the third row with my hands around a paper cup of vending-machine coffee and watched the city do its slow Sunday undressing — shop grates half-lowered, a man hosing down the pavement outside a bakery, light coming off the wet stone like something spilled. Twelve years of this route. First to the house on Delmore Street when she still lived there, then to the assisted apartment on Kessler, then, four years ago now, to Fernwood, with its sign shaped like a leaf and its lobby that smelled of lavender oil laid over something older and less negotiable.

I knew what I would find because I always knew: Odile Iversen at the window table in the sunroom, cards dealt out in front of her in a patience she no longer remembered how to finish. She played the first half of the game beautifully and then lost the thread somewhere around the seventh card, and the staff let the layouts sit there all afternoon like small abandoned cities.

She looked up when I came in. This was the part that never stopped being a held breath — the two seconds before she decided who I was.

"Wren," she said.

Sunday's coin came up right. I let the breath go and kissed her cheek, which was cool and papery and smelled of the rosewater lotion Thea brought every month.

"You're early," she said. "Your father used to be late. You were always the punctual one."

"I got it from somewhere," I said, and sat, and began gathering her cards into a deck while she watched me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere — fondness, maybe, or the polite attention of someone being visited by a man she'd been assured was family.

* * *

We played gin rummy because it was the one game her hands still knew without consulting her. Her fingers were astonishing, actually. That was the thing the disease hadn't gotten to yet, or maybe would never get to — the knuckles moved over the cards with a fluency that belonged to some earlier, better-organized version of her, a woman who'd dealt blackjack in a smoky room in 1968 to pay for a semester of art school she never finished. Watching her shuffle was watching a message arrive from someone who could no longer send one.

"Threes," she said, laying them down. "You never watch my threes."

"I'm watching your threes."

"You're watching the window." She was right. There was a magpie on the sill, doing its insolent head-tilt. "Your father watched windows too. He said it was research." She laughed, a short dry sound. "He was a liar about half of everything but he was honest about the birds."

"Mom."

"Mm?"

Nothing. I drew a card. The silence between us had furniture in it now, after twelve years — armchairs, side tables, whole rooms of things we didn't say, arranged so carefully you could move through the house of it without bumping anything. I was a librarian. I was good at quiet. I had made quiet into a career, a craft, a kind of armor you could wear in public.

"Teddy called," she said suddenly.

My hand stopped over the deck. "Who?"

"Teddy. From the harbor. He wanted to know if your father was coming to the union meeting Thursday." She frowned at her cards. "I told him I didn't know. He's been dead eleven years and he still won't tell anyone his schedule."

There it was. The tide going out and coming back in, sometimes within a single sentence. I had learned not to correct her. Thea had read the literature; correcting caused distress, the pamphlets said, caused the person to feel the ground open under them. So we lived inside her weather. We were sailors in our own mother.

"He was always bad with schedules," I said.

"Terrible." She beamed at me, grateful, and for a moment I couldn't tell whether she was grateful because I'd agreed or because some part of her, deep under the flood line, knew exactly what I'd just done and loved me for doing it. That second possibility was worse. I drank my terrible coffee rather than look at it directly.

* * *

Thea arrived at three, as she always did, carrying a bag of oranges and a paperback from the hospital gift shop that Odile would never read. My sister had our mother's dark hair and none of her softness; Thea had been sanded down by years of managing this decline into something efficient and hard-edged, and she wore her competence like a uniform she resented having to put on.

"There's my girl," Odile said, meaning it, knowing it, and Thea kissed her and took the chair across from me, and for twenty minutes the three of us performed the Sunday play: rummy, oranges peeled in long spirals, the magpie, the weather, a story about a nurse named Priya that Odile told twice and we laughed at both times.

It was during the second telling that Thea looked at me across the table, and I saw that today the performance was going to get reviewed.

* * *

She walked me out. That was how she did it — never in front of Mom, never making a scene in the lavender-smelling rooms, but standing between me and the exit with her arms crossed, so that leaving required passing through her.

"Walk me to the bus stop," she said. It wasn't a request.

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold and cold. We walked the length of the parking lot without speaking, gravel crunching, and I counted the steps the way I counted everything, because counting was a way of being present without arriving.

"The letters," she said.

"What about them?"

"Don't do that." Her voice stayed level, which was how I knew it was bad. Thea only went quiet when she was furious; loud meant she'd win, quiet meant she'd already decided to burn something down. "Don't give me the reference-desk voice. I found them, Wren. In the box in the garage. The box marked *books — keep*, behind the Christmas decorations, which, by the way, is a hell of a place to hide twelve years of correspondence."

My stomach dropped through the pavement. "Those are private."

"They're addressed to Mom."

"They were never sent."

"That's my point!" She stopped walking. A woman pushing a stroller gave us a wide berth. "That's exactly my point. They were never sent. Do you understand what I read last night? I sat in that garage until two in the morning reading letters my brother wrote to our mother — beautiful ones, Wren, genuinely beautiful, you should hear yourself when you think no one's listening — telling her everything. What she gave you. What you're afraid of losing. That you love her. That you've loved her every day since Dad died and you can't say it to her face so you write it down and shelve it in a garage like a returned book nobody's going to check out."

"I don't know what you want me to say."

"I want you to say it to *her*. That's the entire thing. That's the whole ask. It's so small it fits in a thimble." She was crying now, angrily, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like the tears were an administrative error. "Do you know what she asked me last month? She asked if you were happy. That's it. That's what she worries about at four in the morning, when the night staff can't settle her. Whether you're happy. And I have to sit there and lie and say yes, of course, he's great, he loves his library — while I know there's a box in the garage full of proof that her son loves her more than anything on earth and has never once let her hear it."

"She knows," I said. "She knows I—"

"How?" The word cracked. "How does she know? Through what? Through the weather? Through gin rummy? Wren, she's *losing time*. Every month there's less of her. You keep waiting for — what? The right moment? Some perfect afternoon when the light hits right and you finally say the thing? There is no right moment. There's just the next one, and the next one, and each one has less of her in it than the last."

A car pulled past us into the lot. Somewhere inside the building, faintly, someone was playing a piano badly, the same eight bars over and over, patient as erosion.

"It's not that simple," I said.

"It's exactly that simple. That's what makes it unbearable." She stepped closer. Up close I could see she had Dad's eyes, gray-green, the ones I'd inherited nothing of. "You know what I think? I think you're not afraid of saying it. I think you're afraid that once you say it, it becomes real, and real things can be taken away. You've been holding the door shut on that room for twelve years and calling it privacy."

I didn't answer. There was nothing in me that could answer, because she was right, and being right about someone is the cruelest thing a sister can do.

The bus came. It sighed down at the curb between us, doors opening, warm and lit and full of strangers going home. I stood there with one foot in the air.

"Next Sunday," Thea said. "Say one true thing to her. Out loud. One. If you can't manage that, don't bother coming, because I can't keep doing both parts of this family by myself."

The doors waited. I got on. Through the window I watched her stand in the gold light with her arms wrapped around herself, getting smaller, and I pressed my forehead briefly against the glass like a kid, like a coward, like a man drafting another letter in his head already, already reaching for the shelf where the unsent things live.

* * *

Back at the library that night, the regulars came through in their usual order. Marcus with the engineering journals. Dolores, who read romances with the spines cracked like they'd personally wronged her. The kid who slept in the philosophy section and never took books, only warmth.

At two a.m., between shelving runs, I stood at the returns cart with a novel somebody had left a receipt in, using it as a bookmark, and I thought about boxes marked *books — keep*. About how everything I felt had a filing system. About Thea's face in the gold light, and the piano in the sunroom playing its eight patient bars, and my mother asking the ceiling at four in the morning whether I was happy.

I took out a slip of paper. Old habit. My pen hovered.

And then I put it back in my pocket, unmarked, because for the first time in twelve years the blank page felt less like refuge than like evidence.

Across the reading room, the clock ticked toward closing, and the door opened — though I hadn't heard the bell, and no one, as far as I could see, had come in.
