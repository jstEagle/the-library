## 10. The Song About Leaving

November came down on the city like a lid. The library's radiators clanked through the small hours, and the insomniacs arrived in scarves, carrying their private weathers with them — the man who read only obituaries of people he'd never met, the nurse who came off shifts smelling of hand sanitizer and slept upright in the periodicals section for exactly forty minutes before her alarm. Wren knew their rhythms the way he knew the Dewey decimal system: by heart, without ever having decided to learn them.

Mireille arrived at eleven, which was late even for her. She stood inside the entrance longer than usual, letting the heat find her, unwinding her scarf one loop at a time like someone opening a letter she wasn't sure she wanted to read.

"You look tired," he said.

"I am tired." She said it plainly, without apology, the way she said most things. "Tired is honest. Come. We work tonight, but not from the book."

They had a system now, the two of them. A corner table by the biography stacks where the light was warm and the foot traffic thinned after midnight. Her notebook, his transcriptions, the growing stack of pages that constituted — as far as either of them knew — the only written record of Lissune grammar in existence. He'd begun to think of it as *the book*, though it had no title and no order yet, just accumulation, sediment, the language settling into paper grain by grain.

But tonight she left the notebook in her bag.

"Tonight," she said, "I teach you songs."

* * *

She explained it the way she explained everything — sideways first, then straight on.

"In Lissune, when someone leaves, you don't say a word to them. Not at the door, not at the station, not at the grave. You sing. There are five songs. Each one is sung at a different kind of leaving, and each one says something different about coming back."

"Coming back," Wren repeated. "Even at a grave?"

"*Especially* at a grave." She held up one finger. "You are thinking of goodbye. Goodbye means the door closes. Lissune has no such word. The closest thing is *selu en varre* — until the memory returns. It means: I will carry you in my remembering, and you will be present as long as I remember you, so really neither of us has gone anywhere."

"That's not true, though," he said. "People go."

"People go," she agreed. "The song doesn't argue with facts. The song argues with loneliness. Sit up. We begin with the first song."

It was called *Orra*, and it was sung when someone left for the day — to market, to work, to the houses of neighbors. She sang it once through before teaching him a single line, and Wren felt something in his chest shift position, some furniture of feeling moved an inch and never moved back.

Her voice was not beautiful in any conservatory sense. It was thin at the top and gravelly at the bottom, a voice that had been used hard for eighty-some years. But she sang the way she spoke Lissune — as if every sound were a room she was walking him through, pointing out where the light fell.

"The words mean, roughly: *Go, and take the morning with you; leave me the evening, which is yours anyway.*" She smiled. "It sounds small. It is not small. It is the whole philosophy. You give the person the part of the day they need, and you keep what remains, and this is how love works without becoming a cage."

He wrote down the transliteration. His phonetics were getting better; three months of lessons had trained his ear to hear the difference between sounds English flattened into sameness. But the meaning — he wrote the meaning twice, crossed it out, wrote it again.

"Second song," Mireille said. "*Venni orra.* Sung when someone leaves for a season. Harvest, war, school, sea voyages. This one has a verse that must be improvised by the singer — you name what you will miss, specifically, out loud, in front of everyone. There is no general missing allowed. You cannot say *I will miss you*. You must say *I will miss the way you burn the rice and blame the pot.*"

Wren laughed. Then stopped, because she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere.

"What?"

"Nothing. Sing the verse. Improvise. Name something specific you would miss."

"About whom?"

"Anyone. Everyone. Practice."

He sat there for a long moment. The library hummed around them — radiator, fluorescent buzz, the distant elevator. He thought of Odile's hands, how they still moved automatically when music played, conducting something no one else could hear. He thought of the smell of her kitchen in the house on Delmore Street, cardamom and toast, a smell that existed nowhere else on earth and that he had never once told her he loved.

"I would miss," he said slowly, testing it, "the way my mother hums when she thinks no one is listening. She hums wrong notes on purpose. Or maybe not on purpose. I've never asked."

Mireille nodded once, satisfied, and did not say *good* or *well done*. She simply moved to the third song, and Wren understood that in her country of origin, praise was also a word you didn't use — you just proceeded, and proceeding was the praise.

* * *

The third song took two weeks.

*Sellu vane* was sung at long departures — emigration, exile, the leaving that might not reverse. It was the longest of the five, seven verses, and the melody kept resolving somewhere unexpected, a minor turn at the end of each phrase that made the ear lean forward, waiting for a homecoming the song refused to promise.

"Why does it do that?" Wren asked one night, playing back his own recording of her singing it, wincing at his transcription errors. "The melody keeps almost-ending."

"Because the leaver is listening," Mireille said. "The song is built so the person going away hears, in every verse, that the ones staying behind have not finished. The melody does not close because the relationship does not close. Even if the ship never returns. Even if the letters stop." She tapped the table. "My mother sang this to my brother in 1959. He went to Marseille and died there in 1971 and she sang it again at his grave, all seven verses, and then she said, *now it is finished,* and it was finished, but it was finished properly. That is the whole art. Things end. Ending well is a skill. Most people die without ever learning it, and their children spend fifty years humming the unresolved phrase."

Wren's pen had stopped moving.

"Mireille," he said. "Why are you teaching me these? I'm not going anywhere."

She looked at him for a long time. Outside, a delivery truck hissed past on the wet street. Somewhere in the stacks, the obituary man coughed his small nightly cough.

"No," she said finally. "You're not going anywhere. That has always been your problem, I think." She closed the notebook gently. "Next week, the fourth song. Bring your voice. It is bad, but it is honest, and honesty carries a tune better than talent."

* * *

He didn't sleep much after that. He lay in his apartment above the dry cleaner listening to the recordings, her thin sure voice moving through the farewell cycle, and he turned over what she'd said, the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath.

She was eighty-four. She lost words sometimes now — small ones, mostly, the names of tools, the second syllable of familiar streets. She'd covered it well, but he'd started keeping a private tally, and the tally was growing. And he himself was the last student. The last. Which meant the cycle of five songs existed, after tonight, in exactly two heads on the planet, and one of those heads was already misplacing things.

She wasn't teaching him the songs because he might leave.

She was teaching him the songs because *she* was.

The realization arrived without drama, the way the worst realizations do — quietly, at 3 a.m., fully formed, wearing its own evidence. Every lesson had been a rehearsal. The grammar of staying, the greetings, the improvisations of missing — she had been building him a toolkit for a specific job, and the job was losing her, and doing it properly, with all seven verses, with the melody unresolved until it could resolve honestly.

He got up and made tea he didn't drink and thought about Odile, who had called him a stranger on Tuesday and smiled at him the way you smile at strangers, politely, from a distance, across a border that used to be a kitchen. Two women, two declines, two curricula. One of them was teaching him a language. The other had taught him silence. He was a slow student in both classrooms.

At 4 a.m. he opened his laptop and drafted a letter to his mother — the old ritual, the unsent thing:

*Mom. Today I learned a song that isn't allowed to end. I don't know how to explain it except that I think someone has been trying to teach me how to say things to you, and I've been failing the course for twelve years, and I'm afraid the final exam is coming whether I've studied or not—*

He stopped. Saved it to the folder with the others. Two hundred and eleven documents now. He knew the number because Thea had once accused him of counting everything except what mattered.

* * *

The fourth song, when it came, broke him a little.

*Enna sellu* was sung at the deathbed. She warned him first — sat him down, poured the tea she brought in a thermos now, looked at him over the rim of the cup.

"This one, you will want to stop me halfway. Don't. Let it be ugly. It is supposed to be ugly at first. Grief that comes out pretty is grief that hasn't finished its work."

Then she sang it, all nine verses, and it was ugly and gorgeous at once, a melody that climbed and failed and climbed again, and the words — he caught maybe half of them, translating frantically in the margins — were addressed by the dying person to the ones staying. *Do not hold my hands too tight. Do not make your faces into weather I must manage. Tell me the ordinary news. I want to leave in the middle of life, not at the edge of it.*

When she finished, the library was very quiet. The nurse in periodicals had come over without either of them noticing and was standing at the end of the aisle, holding her phone against her chest, eyes bright.

"Sorry," she whispered, and retreated.

Mireille hadn't broken stride. Now she set down the thermos and looked at Wren with terrible gentleness.

"You understand now," she said. Not a question.

"I understand the songs."

"You understand what they are *for*." She reached across the table and put her hand flat on his transcription pages, on months of his handwriting, his careful phonetics, his marginal translations. "A language dies the way a person dies, Wren. Slowly, then all at once, and mostly alone unless someone insists on being in the room. I have spent two years insisting. You are the room." She withdrew her hand. "Next week we finish. The fifth song. It is short. They save the shortest for last, because by then, the singer and the listener both know everything the long ones were trying to say."

He walked her to the door. She wound her scarf back on, loop by loop, sealing herself against the cold.

"Wren." She paused at the threshold, breath fogging. "Your mother. Does she still hum?"

He blinked. "How did you—"

"You mentioned it once. In a verse you improvised." She smiled, small and sly. "Specific missing. It stays in the memory. That is what it is *for*." She stepped out into the November dark. "Sing the fourth song to her sometime. Not the words — she wouldn't know them. Just the shape of it. Ugly is fine. Ugly is honest."

He watched her cross the street under the sodium lamp, small and upright, a woman carrying an entire civilization in a canvas bag, and he understood with sudden, nauseating clarity that the fifth song — the short one, the last one — was not for the language at all.

It was for him. It had always been going to be for him.

That night he checked his phone and found a message from Thea, sent at 11:47 p.m., four words long, no punctuation:

**Mom found a letter.**

He stared at it until the screen dimmed, then dimmed it again himself, and sat in the dark of the night library with the farewell cycle open on the table in front of him, five songs, none of them meaning goodbye forever, all of them meaning *soon*.
