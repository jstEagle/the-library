## 21. The Last Fluent Speaker

She died on the fourth of March, a Tuesday, which was wrong in every way that mattered. Tuesdays were lesson days. The world should have had the decency to rearrange itself around that.

I had been with her since nine that morning. Simone had gone to the pharmacy to argue about a prescription, and Mireille had wanted to work — she always wanted to work now, with the urgency of someone packing a suitcase while the taxi idles outside. We were deep into the archive by then: four hundred and eleven recordings, most under an hour, some stretching past two as she chased a word through her own memory like a woman following a scent through a house she'd lived in for ninety years.

That morning we were doing verbs of weather. Lissune had fourteen words for rain and I could produce maybe six from memory without her prompting, and she was drilling me the way she used to when her mind was sharper — *again, again, say it again until your mouth believes it* — and I was saying them, *selu, selu, selu*, the word for rain that falls so fine it doesn't wet the skin but enters it anyway, and she was pleased, I remember she was pleased, she did the thing with her chin where she lifted it half an inch and that was applause.

Then she said, "Now the one for rain that comes after a long dry time. The rain people wait for. You know this one."

And I didn't. And I said, "I don't."

And she opened her mouth to give it to me.

The recorder was running. It's still running in my memory, though of course that's not how memory works; that's how grief works, turning everything into tape. She drew breath — I heard it, that small intake, the sound of a door opening — and then nothing came out. Not silence exactly. Something before silence. Her mouth stayed open on the shape of a word that had no sound yet, the way a hand stays open on the shape of a thing it meant to catch.

"Madame Okonkwo-Bass," I said, because even at the end I called her that, formal as a citation, and she would have corrected me if she could have, *Wren, we are past citations* —

Simone came back to find me holding her hand and the recorder still going, forty minutes of nothing but room-tone and my own breathing and once, terribly, my voice saying her name over and over like a man calling across water.

* * *

They let me sit with her for a while afterward, in that small front room with its two lamps and its shelves of cassette tapes from three decades of teaching, tapes in languages mostly dead or dying, labeled in her careful hand. I sat in the chair where I'd sat through two winters of lessons and looked at the space where she'd been and understood, in the dumb animal way you understand such things, that the last fluent speaker of Lissune was no longer fluent in anything, not even air.

There is a phrase in Lissune for this. Of course there is. There is a phrase for everything, because Mireille made sure of it, because for sixty years she had been building levees against exactly this moment, word by word, student by student, and the water came anyway.

The phrase is *alu mennei*. It means, roughly, *the room where the voice was*. You say it when someone dies. You don't say goodbye — there is no goodbye — you say *until the memory returns*, and if the person is dead, you say it anyway, because in Lissune the dead are not gone, they are simply not-yet-remembered, and remembering them is a job, an obligation, a daily labor, like tending a fire.

I said it out loud to the empty chair. My Lissune accent is still bad. She told me once it sounded like "a Frenchman learning Norwegian underwater." I said it anyway. *Alu mennei.* The room where the voice was.

Then I stopped the recorder, finally, and saw the file length: 411 hours, 22 minutes, and 6 seconds, the last six seconds being the shape of a word with no sound in it.

* * *

Simone and I sat in her mother's kitchen that evening drinking tea neither of us wanted. She looked ten years older than she had in October, when she'd arrived with movers and certainty. Grief had done what argument couldn't: it had made us colleagues.

"He finished it," she said. "Whatever he was trying to teach you. He got almost all the way through."

"The rain after drought." I shook my head. "I never learned it. Nobody knows it now. It died in her mouth."

Simone turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "You know what I keep thinking about? That lullaby. In January, when I sat in. I hadn't heard it since I was seven. I thought I'd invented it — I swear to God, Wren, I thought it was something I'd dreamed, because who sings a song like that, where does a song like that come from?" Her voice caught. "It came from her. It came from all of them. Every woman back to whoever spoke it first."

"She'd have liked that you remembered it."

"She'd have hated that I needed the recording to get it right." Simone smiled, wet-eyed. "But she'd have taken it. She was practical underneath all that ceremony. Don't tell anyone I said that."

"I'm putting it in the archive notes."

"Don't you dare."

We sat a while longer. Outside, March was doing its indecisive work, snow then rain then a light too clean for winter. Somewhere down the block a kid was practicing trumpet badly, the same eight bars over and over, getting slightly less bad each time, and I thought: that's it, that's the whole thing, that's transmission, somebody playing after somebody else has stopped.

* * *

The completion of the archive took me five weeks. This was the work I had promised her in February, when her lapses had grown long enough that she'd looked at me one afternoon with polite blankness and asked if I was the young man from the library, and I had said yes, and she had said, "Good. Then write everything down. A language that lives in one head is a guest; a language that lives on paper is family."

So I wrote everything down.

Four hundred eleven hours of audio, digitized and backed up three ways. Two hundred pages of grammar, compiled from her lessons and her own notebooks, which Simone found in a trunk — exercises she'd written for students in 1968, 1979, 1991, each generation of learners smaller than the last, the handwriting growing more urgent. A lexicon of just over nine thousand words, which sounds like a lot until you learn that English has closer to a million, and then it sounds like a candle in a cathedral, and then you remember that a candle is not nothing, a candle is exactly the right amount of light for reading by.

The unfinished word I left unfinished. I marked it in the transcript as she left it in the air: a bracket, a gap, a note in my hand — *rain after long drought; speaker's final word; untransmitted.* Thea, when she read that page, said it was the most honest sentence in the whole archive. I think she meant it kindly. I've decided to take it kindly.

What I could not do alone, I did with help. The insomniacs helped.

You have to understand about the night library: it collects people the way a tide pool collects what the sea leaves behind. The man who reads only maritime disasters. The nurse coming off shifts who sits in periodicals and doesn't read, just sits. The teenager who does homework at 3 a.m. because home is loud. They had watched me carry boxes of tapes in all winter. They had listened to Lissune leaking through the staff-room door while I transcribed — Mireille's voice, singing, counting, correcting, laughing at my accent. One night the nurse, whose name is Dolores and who has worked nights so long she says dawn gives her a headache, knocked on the door frame and said, "That lady on the tapes. She the one who taught you?"

"Yes."

"She sounds like she wouldn't take any shit."

"She would not have taken any shit whatsoever."

Dolores nodded, satisfied, and went back to her magazines. But after that they started asking. What does that word mean. Say the rain ones again. The teenager — Priya, she's called — started leaving her homework to listen, headphones on, eyes closed, and one night she said, "It sounds like water talking," and I wrote that down too, because Mireille would have wanted it in the record: *described by a stranger as water talking, which is correct.*

* * *

Donating it was Simone's idea, actually, and I want that on the record because our history started with her assuming I was a con man preying on her mother, and fairness matters, especially between people who have hurt each other accidentally.

"It shouldn't live in your apartment," she said. We were at her mother's kitchen table again, sorting the trunk. "You'll keep it safe, obviously, you're — whatever you are, meticulous, obsessive, fine. But an archive in one apartment is one fire away from nothing. Where did she want it?"

"She never said. She said archives were 'where languages go to be embalmed.'"

"And then she spent her last year making one."

"Because I begged her."

"Because you begged her," Simone agreed. "So. Where do languages go to stay alive?"

I thought about it for three weeks, which surprised no one who knows me. I thought about university collections, where it would sit in climate-controlled dark and be consulted by two dissertations and forgotten. I thought about the national archive, where it would be catalogued correctly and completely and never played. I thought about Mireille shelving books in her stories, putting things back where they belong instead of forward.

Then I thought about Dolores and her magazines. About Priya at 3 a.m. About the maritime-disaster man, who one night in February had asked me, apropos of nothing, whether the farewell songs were available, because his wife had died in November and he'd been listening to the fourth one on his commute and it was, he said, the first thing since that had felt like it was aimed at him personally.

Open shelves. That was the answer. Not special collections, not behind glass. The regular collection, at the main branch, catalogued like any other book, borrowable by anyone with a card, which is to say anyone at all — the sleepless, the grieving, the curious, the bored teenager killing time until the buses start. A language doesn't survive by being protected. It survives by being in the way.

The library director signed off in eleven minutes. She was newer than me, younger than me, and she said, "This is the best thing that's happened here in years, and I include the renovation," and I said thank you, and then I stood in the stacks for a while holding the first box, unable to put it down, because putting it down meant it wasn't mine anymore, and it hadn't been mine for a long time, and I still couldn't.

I shelved it myself. Call number PJ-something-or-other, a number that belongs to no established family of languages, a number the cataloguers invented for Lissune alone, which means somewhere in the great ordered system of human knowledge there is now a shelf with one language on it, waiting.

On the spine of the finding aid I wrote, in the space where libraries sometimes put a dedication: *For Mireille Okonkwo-Bass, who counted her words. Until the memory returns.*

* * *

Thea came to the opening — there wasn't an opening, there was no ceremony, Mireille would have risen from whatever afterlife she occupies to mock me — but Thea came on the Tuesday night in April when the tapes went public, and so did Simone, and so did Dolores, off-shift for once, blinking in the fluorescent dark like a creature from deeper water.

Priya checked out the first item. The lullaby. She brought it to the desk at 2 a.m. and said, "Is it okay if I copy it? My little sister can't sleep lately. Maybe water talking helps."

"Take it," I said. "That's what it's for."

Thea watched this happen with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she's worn since we were children, the one that means *I see you and I'm deciding whether to say it.* She said it later, in the parking lot, in the cold.

"You know what I keep thinking? Twelve years of letters. Twelve years, Wren. And it took a dying language to teach you to talk." She wasn't cruel. That's the worst part. She was just accurate. "But you talked. You got there. Mom knew your face at the end and you filled it up with everything. So I'm not complaining. I'm just — noting the irony for the record."

"Noted," I said.

"And you're going to keep the study circle going. The one at the library. The strangers."

"Six of them now. Seven if Priya's sister comes."

"Lissune has no future tense," Thea said. "So how do you say next week's class?"

"*Mennei selu*," I said. "The not-yet-remembered. You say it like it already happened, somewhere, in a place you can't get to yet. Like memory running ahead instead of behind."

Thea looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and real, breath smoking in the cold. "God help those seven strangers. They have no idea what they signed up for."

"No," I said. "Neither did I."

Inside, through the glass, I could see the night floor lit up against the dark, and on the open shelves, halfway down the row, the small bright spines of the finding aids, and beyond them the city doing its never-quite-sleeping, and somewhere in it, I knew without checking, someone was awake and would come in tonight, the way people do, looking for nothing in particular, and run their finger along the shelf, and stop.

The recorder is in my bag. It's always in my bag now. Old habit, or new one — I've stopped asking which. The study circle meets Thursdays at ten. First word I ever learned, first word I'll teach them: the greeting that means *you are arrived*, past tense, as if the arriving were already a gift received.

Seven strangers. One candle. Rain after a long dry time, still unnamed, still falling somewhere in the space between her last breath and whoever says the word next.

I unlocked the doors and turned on the lights and waited for the night to bring them in.
