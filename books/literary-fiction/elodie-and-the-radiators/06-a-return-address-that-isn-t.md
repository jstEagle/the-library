## 6. A Return Address That Isn't

The return stamp said *The Radiators, 14 Marine Parade, Saltmere-on-Sea* — and then, beneath it, in smaller type that had faded to the colour of weak tea: *No Letters Forwarded.*

I sat with it for a long time on my mother's kitchen floor, forty-one envelopes fanned around me like a hand of cards I'd been dealt too late. No letters forwarded. It was practically a motto. Practically an epitaph. My mother had spent twenty years writing to a woman who had instructed the post office, in effect, to pretend she didn't exist, and my mother had kept writing anyway, and had kept every rejection, filed them behind a wardrobe like love letters from a war she'd lost.

Saltmere-on-Sea. I looked it up on my phone, which felt obscene, like using a fire hose to water a houseplant. Two hours by train, one change at Ashford. A town whose Wikipedia entry was four sentences long and contained the phrase "notable for its shingle beach." Notable for its shingle beach. Somewhere out there was a woman who had been notable for my mother.

Here is what I knew about myself by day nine of cataloguing a dead woman's house: I was good at tracing things. I had spent eleven years as a probate researcher, which is a polite way of saying I found people who didn't want to be found and told them they'd inherited money they usually didn't want either. I could find anyone. I had found a man in Perth who'd changed his name twice and his continent once. I had found heirs hiding inside monasteries. The work had taught me one iron law of human nature: nobody truly disappears. They just stop answering, and the world, being lazy, takes the hint.

Elodie Marchetti had not disappeared. She had returned forty-one letters with a stamp that gave her address away like a blush. Either she hadn't thought of that, or — and this possibility sat in my chest like a swallowed stone — she hadn't minded being findable. She had only minded being reached.

I booked a train for Thursday because Wednesday felt too eager and Friday felt like cowardice with better lighting.

* * *

Saltmere-on-Sea announces itself the way a shy person enters a party: apologetically, sideways, hoping you'll leave before it has to make conversation. The train station had one platform and a vending machine that ate coins as a hobby. The high street ran downhill toward the sea with the resigned air of a thing that had stopped expecting anything good to happen at the bottom of it. There was a chippy, two charity shops, a pharmacy with a faded photograph of the Queen in the window that predated decimal currency, and a seafront promenade whose railings had been painted so many times they were more paint than metal.

It was October. The town had the specific melancholy of English seaside places off-season, when the arcades are shuttered and the ice cream kiosks wear padlocks like little silver wounds, and the gulls wheel overhead looking personally offended by the absence of chips. I walked down Marine Parade with the wind pushing me along like it had somewhere better for me to be, reading numbers.

Number 14 was not what I expected, and also exactly what I expected, which is the trick good buildings play on you.

It was tall and narrow and grey, three storeys of Victorian stucco gone soft at the edges, the way old faces go soft, with a bay window on each floor and a front door painted a blue so deep it was nearly black. The name over the door wasn't on a sign but stencilled directly onto the wood in white letters that someone had refreshed recently enough to matter: THE RADIATORS. Below it, smaller, in different handwriting — newer, less official: *Residents Only. Well. Residents and People Who Knock Politely.*

There was a garden in front, or rather there had been a garden once and now there was a riot — rosemary gone woody and enormous, a fig tree leaning against the wall like a drunk against a lamppost, nasturtiums staging a coup across the path. Someone had planted all of this decades ago and then simply refused to impose order on it, and the plants, given their freedom, had produced something wilder and more alive than any tended garden on the street.

And there was heat. That's what stopped me on the pavement, hand half-raised toward the bell. From an open upstairs window came a low mechanical hum, the sound of radiators working, and beneath it, faintly, someone playing a piano badly and without shame. The whole house exhaled warmth into the cold street like a body breathing.

I stood there longer than I meant to. Long enough that a man coming out of number 12 gave me the look English people give you when you've committed the crime of existing conspicuously.

My mother's letters had called their flat's broken heating "the radiators" — clanking, unpredictable, beloved. And here was a boarding house named after heating, on a street where the sea did its grey business thirty yards away, and inside it someone was playing piano like the piano owed them money. I pressed the bell and heard it ring somewhere deep in the house, and then nothing happened, which I would later learn was standard procedure.

I knocked. The door opened on the second knock, and that's how I met Dev.

He was maybe twenty-five, wearing a jumper with a hole at one elbow and paint on the other sleeve, holding a spanner in one hand and a mug in the other, and he looked at me with the frank, unhurried assessment of someone who answers doors for a living and has learned that the door is just the first question.

"Hello," he said. "Are you here about the room?"

"The room?"

"We've got one going. Third floor, small, faces the back. If you're here about the room."

"I'm not here about the room," I said, and then, because lying to him felt immediately and strangely expensive: "Or — I don't know what I'm here about. Is Elodie Marchetti here?"

I have interrupted funerals with inheritance news. I have watched grown men cry over shares in a quarry. I know what faces do. And Dev's face did something very small and very fast — a flicker, a door closing one inch — before settling into careful blankness, and I want to be honest: I almost missed it. He covered well. But I'd spent eleven years watching people decide whether to tell me the truth, and I knew the micro-expression of a man recalibrating.

"Marchetti," he repeated, tasting the name. "Don't know it."

"She used to live here. Nineteen eighty-eight, onwards. She may go by another name now."

"Right." He drank from his mug, buying time. Steam came off it. "Who's asking?"

"Tamsin Galloway."

Another flicker. Faster this time. So he *did* know the name Galloway — or the surname meant something, or my face did, and I made a note of that and filed it under *later*, because the alternative was to grab him by the spanner and shake the truth out, and I was raised by a woman who taught me that the fastest way to lose information is to demand it.

"My mother grew up with her," I said. "They were close. I've got letters. Returned ones." I heard my own voice do the thing it does when I'm trying to sound casual about something that has eaten me alive for nine days. "Forty-one of them."

Dev looked past me, down the street, as if the answer might be walking toward us and he could flag it down instead of giving it himself. The wind came off the sea and pushed my hair across my face and he waited, politely, until I'd pushed it back, which gave him time to decide whatever he decided.

"Look," he said finally, lowering his voice, though there was no one within fifty yards except a gull with opinions. "I'm the caretaker. I fix boilers and unblock drains and I put the bins out. What I don't do is answer questions about the old days. Not because I'm hiding anything." He paused. "Well. Also because I'm hiding something. But mostly because the residents—" he tipped his head toward the house, from which the bad piano had stopped, and I noticed the stopping, noted the stopping, "—they came here to get away from questions. Every single one of them. That's sort of the admission criteria. You trade your old life for a room and no one asks what you traded. House rule. Older than me. Older than most of them."

"And Elodie Marchetti is one of them."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't say it isn't true, either."

He smiled despite himself — a quick, crooked smile, gone fast, like a match struck in wind. "You're a lawyer or something?"

"Probate researcher. I find people."

"Yeah," he said, with feeling. "See, that's exactly the kind of person this house is built to survive." He set the mug on the windowsill behind him, and I understood I was being gently managed toward the end of the conversation. "I'll tell you what I can tell you. This house has been here since before the war and it's been called The Radiators since about nineteen seventy-something, and it runs itself, more or less, and we take people who need taking. That's it. That's the tour."

"That's not a tour, that's a brochure."

"It's a very honest brochure." He picked the mug back up. Over his shoulder, through the open door, I could see a hallway: worn runner carpet, coats on pegs, a noticeboard thick with layered paper like sedimentary rock, and — absurdly, wonderfully — a radiator in the hall painted gloss white, gleaming, clearly polished, clearly loved, standing there like a shrine. "If you're staying in town, the Anchor does rooms. Tell them Dev sent you, they'll charge you less and gossip about you more."

"Dev," I said, as he began to close the door. "One question. Just one. Not about the old days. About right now. Is she happy?"

The door stopped. He stood in the gap for a moment, and something moved through his face that wasn't calculation at all — something younger and sadder, the look of a young man carrying other people's secrets and finding them heavier than his own.

"That's not one question," he said quietly. "That's about six, wearing a coat." And then, before closing the door the rest of the way: "She walks. Mornings. When the tide's out. Everyone knows it, nobody says it. That's not an answer, that's just weather."

The door clicked shut. I stood on the pavement of Marine Parade with the wind flattening my coat against me and the smell of salt and fig leaves and, faintly, from somewhere inside, boiler heat, and I felt the thing I'd been carrying since the wardrobe split cleanly in two: half of it was hope, warm and stupid and enormous — *she's alive, she walks the tide line, she plays piano badly in a house named after the sound my mother's heart must have made* — and the other half was a splinter, thin and bright and already working its way in.

Because Dev had known the name Galloway. Because the piano had stopped when I rang. Because a house built on not-asking had a caretaker who answered doors with a spanner in his hand and a rehearsed face, and rehearsed faces are maintained by communities, and communities maintain them for reasons.

I walked down to the Anchor, took a room with a view of a wall, and unpacked the letters onto the bed like a dealer laying out evidence. Forty-one envelopes. Forty-one stamps. *The Radiators, 14 Marine Parade.* She had never changed the return address. Twenty years, and the stamp stayed the same — which meant either she never left, or she left and came back, or — and here the splinter turned — she wanted the letters to come home even while refusing to let them in. A return address isn't an invitation. But it isn't nothing, either. It's a door left unlocked in a house where everyone pretends the locks are decorative.

Outside my window the sea did its grey business. Down the road, faintly, the piano started again — worse than before, gloriously worse, someone playing with total commitment and no talent whatsoever, and I lay on the creaking bed and listened to it and thought: *that is the sound of a place where people are allowed to be bad at things.*

Tomorrow I would go back. Tomorrow I would knock politely, per the stencil, and offer something useful, because houses like that don't open for questions but they might open for a leaking pipe, and I had fixed a radiator exactly once, in a flat in Leeds, badly, but I remembered the principle.

Tonight I read letter twelve again, the one about the invented holiday — *Happy Founders' Day, E., founder of everything worth founding* — and I fell asleep with it on my chest like a hot water bottle, and dreamed of a hallway with a polished white radiator in it, gleaming like a shrine, and woke at dawn with the splinter still in and the hope somehow larger anyway.

That's the thing about splinters. They only hurt when you press on the wood.

I intended to press.
