## 9. Letters 12 Through 19

By the fourth night I had developed a ritual, which is what grief does when it can't find a church: it builds one out of whatever's lying around. Mine consisted of a kettle, the good lamp dragged in from my mother's study, and the shoebox open on the bed like a patient on an operating table. I would make tea I didn't drink, put on a record I didn't listen to — her record, Billie Holiday, because it seemed only fair that she should have to hear what I was hearing — and I would read.

Letter twelve arrived on a Tuesday, according to the postmark. It began:

*Dear E.,*

*Happy Founding Day. Four years today since you named the radiators and thereby saved us both from dying of dignity. I have bought us a cake. It is a lemon cake and it is terrible and I ate your half so you wouldn't have to suffer.*

I sat with that for a long time. *Founding Day.* There was no such holiday. I checked — not that night, but later, obsessively, in the way I was beginning to do everything — and there is no Founding Day anywhere in any calendar of any nation, religious or civic. Which meant she had invented it. My mother, who once told me birthdays were "an administrative event," who signed my cards *M* without love or x or even a full stop, had invented a holiday. For two people. In a flat with broken heating.

The letter went on to describe, in detail I can only call loving, the repair of the kitchen window latch. There was a diagram. My mother drew diagrams. She had never drawn me a diagram of anything, though God knows I'd asked — how to bleed a radiator, how to unstick a zip, how to be less afraid of telephones. The diagram in letter twelve was annotated in her small upright hand: *you hold the frame like this, idiot, while I lever.* The word *idiot* had been crossed out and rewritten as *darling*, and then crossed out again, and left crossed out, which told me more than either word alone.

* * *

Here is what I learned about codes, reading letters written by people who were hiding in plain sight: they don't hide by saying less. They hide by saying things that look like nothing. A grocery list is a grocery list until you understand that no one writes *the good butter, the expensive kind, we've earned it* to a person they are merely renting a flat with. No one invents holidays over a window latch. No one signs off *Yours in domestic conspiracy, T.G.* unless the conspiracy is the whole point.

Tamsin Galloway. She signed herself T.G., and I am Tamsin Galloway, and I had gone twenty-nine years without knowing where my name came from, assuming it came from nowhere, from duty, from a grandmother I'd never met. But there it was in letter thirteen, dated March 1987:

*You keep calling me T. like I'm a train station. Say the whole thing. You know I like it when you say the whole thing.*

And Elodie, apparently, had said the whole thing. Repeatedly. Enough that my mother — who hated her own name, who introduced herself as Ms. Galloway to doctors and neighbors and, I now understand, to her own daughter — had let this woman wear it smooth like a stone in a pocket.

I started keeping notes. This is the sort of thing that would have embarrassed me in week one; by week two I had a system. Index cards. Cross-references. My mother's house filling up around me with my own handwriting for the first time, as if the cataloging had finally found its proper subject. Not the furniture. Her.

Letter fourteen was almost entirely practical — boiler pressure, a dispute with the landlord, a recipe copied out for *the soup you liked, the one with the smoked paprika, don't skimp* — and then, at the bottom, in a different ink, added later, clearly after posting had been considered and postponed:

*P.S. Mrs. Anwar asked if we were sisters. I said yes. She looked at me the way people look at liars they approve of.*

* * *

I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the only inheritance she left me that I actually want. What I felt was jealousy. Not the polite kind — the animal kind. I read these letters the way you'd read correspondence between your partner and someone else, except the partner was my mother and the someone else was a stranger and the betrayal was that of my entire childhood. Every warm sentence was evidence. Every joke was an exhibit. Somewhere in a flat in 1987 my mother was funny — genuinely funny, the kind of funny that makes a person write *I laughed until the woman upstairs banged on the floor* — and she had packed that woman away somewhere before I was born and raised me with the box empty.

Or not empty. That's the thing I kept circling. Raised me with the box *behind the wardrobe*, which is worse, which means she knew exactly what she had and chose the wall over the shelf.

Letter fifteen was the first one that frightened me. Not because of what it said but because of what it assumed:

*I've been looking at cottages. Don't laugh. There's one outside Wells, needs everything doing to it, roof like a colander, but there's a garden and the sea at the end of the lane and a room that could be yours for your paints. When it's safe. When it's safe we'll go and I will never answer another envelope in my life.*

When it's safe. Three words, and underneath them their whole inverted architecture: it wasn't safe. It was 1988 and two women sharing a name and a holiday and a signature like *yours in domestic conspiracy* needed a cottage at the end of a lane and silence between them and the sea. I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the shopkeeper in her old neighborhood told me about the father arriving *like weather*. Two years before my birth, these letters begin. One year before my birth, they're planning escapes. And then —

Then the dates kept coming and the letters kept coming, right through 1988, right through the spring of 1989, and I lay awake doing the cruelest math of my life: I was already a possibility during some of them. Maybe already a fact. My mother was writing *when it's safe we'll go* to Elodie Marchetti while carrying, or about to carry, or having just decided to carry, me.

Unless the deciding came later. Unless the letters explain it. I turned to letter sixteen hungry for the turn, the pivot, the moment the plan dies — and found instead:

*The landlady at the cottage place wrote back. She wants references, which is hilarious, references for what, our character? Fine. Character reference: E. Marchetti has lived with me four years and has never once let me carry the heavy end. That should suffice.*

They were still planning. In the spring of 1989, months before I was born, they were still planning. The cottage, the sea at the end of the lane, the room for the paints. I got up and walked around the house at two in the morning touching walls, which is a thing I do now, and I thought: I was the thing that made it unsafe. Or I was the excuse. Or I was the lifeboat. I didn't know which, and each version rearranged the furniture of my self-regard differently, and none of the arrangements were comfortable.

* * *

Letter seventeen was short. Letter eighteen was shorter. Something had happened between them and the pages had gone careful, the way a voice goes careful in a hospital corridor. Seventeen said:

*I know what you heard. It isn't what you think, or it is, or it's neither. Come home and I'll say it properly, out loud, the way you're always telling me to say things.*

Eighteen said:

*I'm not apologizing for being frightened. I'm apologizing for making you stand outside in it.*

No context. Twenty years of context, withheld. I began to understand that these letters were not going to hand me the story. They were going to hand me the shape of the story and let me freeze to death inside it.

Letter nineteen was the last one before the gap, and I read it four times, and each time I noticed something new, the way you do with a room you're about to leave. It was long. It was warm again — the fight, whatever it was, apparently survived. It contained a list titled *Things To Do Before Wells*, itemized in her tidy hand:

1. *Tell the landlord (E. will cry, bring biscuits)*
2. *Sell the good table (we never use it, we eat standing at the counter like animals)*
3. *Tell my mother (no date set, possibly never, see also: cowardice)*
4. *Buy paint for the little room (E.'s choice, obviously)*

And then, at the bottom, beneath the list, a paragraph that had been written and heavily struck through and rewritten, the ink layered like sediment:

~~*There's something I have to tell you and I've been putting it off because once I say it the cottage stops being a plan and becomes a decision and decisions are*~~

~~*Elodie, before anything else, you should know that I*~~

*Tell you what. I'll say it when I see you. Some things want a face. Save me the chair by the window.*

Save me the chair by the window. She ended it there. Warm, whole, forward-looking, a woman with a list and a plan and a chair by a window in a cottage outside Wells — and then the postmark, October 1989, six weeks before I was born.

And then nothing.

Not a pause. Nothing. Letter nineteen to letter twenty-one, a gap of eleven weeks, and in that gap I was born, and in that gap — presumably — everything happened. The father arriving like weather. The choice. Whatever my mother paid and whatever Elodie was made to pay alongside her.

Because here is the thing about the shoebox. I counted the letters the first night: forty-one. I counted them again on the fourth night, because counting is what I do instead of sleeping: forty envelopes addressed to E. Marchetti, forty stamped RETURN TO SENDER in that brutal rubber-stamp red, plus one loose sheet — the draft, the unfinished one, *Elodie, I chose, and here is what it cost,* breaking off mid-sentence like a cliff edge.

Forty envelopes. Letters one through eleven, then twelve through nineteen, then twenty-one through forty-one.

Twenty was missing.

I went through the box three times. I shook it. I held it upside down over the bed like a man shaking a piggy bank, and what fell out was a single torn stub — the top corner of an envelope, the size of a stamp, bearing three words in my mother's handwriting:

*Elodie — please —*

That was all. *Elodie — please —* and then the tear, ragged, hurried, done by hand, not by time. Someone had torn that letter open and torn it apart, or torn it in half and lost half, or torn it deliberately and kept the piece that hurt least. Or hurt most. I sat on the floor of my mother's bedroom at four in the morning holding a fragment of paper the size of a matchbook, and I understood that I was holding the center of everything. Whatever was in letter twenty was the reason for the wardrobe wall, for the forty returned envelopes, for the draft that couldn't finish its sentence, for the locked door I'd spent a week hating a dead woman for being.

She wrote *please*. My mother, who never asked anyone for anything, who treated need like a debt — she begged. And whatever she begged for, the answer came back in the only language Elodie ever spoke again: silence, return to sender, return to sender, return to sender, thirty-one times, for twenty years.

I put the stub in my breast pocket, which is insane, it's paper, it weighs nothing, but I wanted it against my heartbeat anyway, the way you'd carry a key to a door you haven't found yet.

Because that was the other thing I understood, lying on the floor among the index cards and the cold tea: the letters had a return address. Every single envelope carried it, stamped in faded blue ink, a seaside town I'd never once heard my mother mention. She wrote to a ghost for twenty years and put a real address on every envelope, and either she hoped someday the ghost would write back, or she was telling me — leaving the trail lit behind her like a fuse — that if I ever found this box, I would know where the rest of the story lived.

In the morning I looked it up. Four hours' drive. A boarding house called The Radiators, still taking guests, according to a listing older than I am.

I packed a bag.
