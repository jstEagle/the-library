## 10. The Father's File

My father lived in a care home called Beechwood Grange, which sounded like a hotel where the trees had been given equal billing with the guests. It sat on a hill outside a market town I'd never visited, forty minutes by two buses and a stretch of walking that gave me time to rehearse and abandon eleven different opening lines.

I hadn't seen him in six years. The last occasion was my thirtieth birthday, which he'd attended for ninety minutes, drunk two glasses of warm white wine, complimented my flat's radiators — *your mother always said they were temperamental* — and left before the cake. Before that, the intervals stretched longer: Christmas cards with his handwriting getting smaller, like he was writing himself out of the frame. We were not estranged in the dramatic sense. There had been no shouting, no slammed doors, no scene at the funeral worth reporting. We were estranged the way Britain is estranged from Europe: technically still adjacent, functionally speaking different languages, both pretending the arrangement was mutual.

He had my eyes. That was the thing nobody warned you about estrangement — you carry the other person around on your own face, so every mirror is a small reunion.

The letter I brought was number fourteen. I chose it from the box on the train, turning them over like tarot cards, looking for one that would do its work without me having to say anything at all. Number fourteen was dated March 1987 and began:

*Dear E.,*

*You will be pleased to know I have finally learned to drive. You will be less pleased to learn that I stalled at the roundabout four times and a man in a van shouted something at me that I have decided to interpret as encouragement. I thought of you when the engine died — you always said I could kill anything with enough patience.*

*I have been thinking about the cottage. The one with the blue door. I looked it up and it rents by the week and there is a stove that works and a garden that doesn't. Say the word and I'll book it for June. Say the word.*

*Yours, entirely and inconveniently,*
*M.*

It was the most naked of the early letters. It said *say the word* twice, as if once hadn't been brave enough. And it was signed with an initial only — M. — as if even on paper, even to Elodie, my mother couldn't quite claim her whole name.

I put it in my coat pocket, over my heart, which felt melodramatic until I noticed I'd done it and then felt embarrassed about the melodrama and kept it there anyway.

* * *

Beechwood Grange smelled of lavender and floor polish and, underneath, faintly and honestly, of age. A receptionist with a lanyard and infinite patience checked me against a list. "Mr. Galloway doesn't get many visitors," she said, and then seemed to hear how that sounded and added, "He'll be thrilled."

"He might not be," I said. "We're not close."

She smiled the smile of someone who has heard every possible configuration of family damage and found none of it disqualifying. "Room 11. Second left past the conservatory. He likes the window open but he'll deny being cold."

My father was sitting in an armchair by the window, reading a paperback held at arm's length, and for a moment — this is the humiliating part — for a moment I felt the old reflexive lift of a child coming home. Six years collapsed. He looked smaller than memory, but the same: the same iron-grey hair combed with unnecessary precision, the same cardigan buttoned wrong at the bottom, the same way of holding a book as though it owed him money.

He looked up. There was a pause of perhaps three seconds in which I watched him decide who I was — not recognize me, exactly, but assemble me from available parts.

"Tamsin," he said. Not a question. An identification, filed correctly.

"Hello, Dad."

"You've got your mother's chin."

"I've got your eyes."

"So you have." He closed the book on his thumb. "Well. Sit down, then. Don't hover."

There it was. Twenty minutes into a six-year silence and we were already doing the thing where affection was expressed exclusively through furniture management. I sat on the visitor chair. Between us stood a small table with a glass of water, a crossword half-finished in biro, and a photograph of me aged seven that I had no memory of giving him. It was the only photo in the room. I stared at it while he stared at me, and we conducted our entire reunion through the medium of a child neither of us had become.

"You look well," he said.

"I look tired."

"That too." He almost smiled. My father's smiles were like solar eclipses — documented, rare, never to be viewed directly. "The funeral was decent. Good turnout."

"You were at the back."

"I'm good at the back." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Your mother would have appreciated the hymns. She pretended not to care about that sort of thing, but she did."

"Dad." I took the letter out of my pocket. My hands were steadier than I expected. "I found something. At the house. And I need you to tell me what you know."

He looked at the envelope — my mother's handwriting, *E. Marchetti*, the franked stamp, RETURN TO SENDER struck through in someone else's ink — and something happened to his face. It didn't crumple. It *drained*. Like watching a tide go out of a man.

"Where did you get that?"

"Behind the wardrobe. There were forty-one of them. All returned unopened."

"Forty-one." He repeated it the way you'd repeat a diagnosis. Then he held out his hand. "Give it here."

* * *

I want to record accurately what happened next, because I have turned it over so many times since that the edges have gone soft, and accuracy is all I have left of it.

He read the letter. All of it. His lips moved slightly on the second paragraph — *say the word* — and when he reached the signature his hand began to shake, just the hand, the rest of him very still, a tremor traveling up from the paper like current finding earth.

Then he wept.

Not sobbing. Nothing so usable as sobbing. Tears simply arrived and ran down his face and he made no move to stop them and no sound at all, staring at the page, and I sat across from him with my heart going like a trapped bird and understood that I was watching a man read a letter he had already read — or written, or witnessed, or paid for — thirty-five years ago, and that whatever it cost him then was being charged again now, at interest.

"Dad—"

"Don't." One word, wet and quiet. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, briskly, the way you'd deal with rain rather than grief. He folded the letter along its original creases with terrible care and set it on the table between us, face down, like a card he refused to play.

"Explain," I said. "Please. I've spent three weeks inside her silence. I've earned an explanation."

"You've earned nothing," he said, "and neither did I," and then, because my father could be cruel only accidentally, he saw what it did to my face and closed his eyes. "That came out wrong. Sit still. Let me think."

He thought. Outside the window, a magpie walked along the sill with the confidence of a landlord. Somewhere down the corridor a trolley rattled and a woman laughed at something a carer said, and the ordinary sounds of the living went on around us while my father sat with his eyes shut and his hands flat on his knees like a man bracing for turbulence.

"She wrote those letters for two years," he said at last. "Before you. During. After. I knew about some of them. Not all. She hid them better than she hid anything else, and she was very good at hiding things — you'll have noticed."

"I've noticed nothing else."

"No." He opened his eyes. "No, I suppose you haven't, and that's the point, isn't it. That was the design."

"What design? Whose?"

"Mine and hers. Mostly hers. I was—" He stopped. Started again. My father was not an articulate man; he was a man who had spent a life saying less than he meant and had gotten fluent at it. "I was the arrangement, Tamsin. That's what I was. I knew that going in. Your mother told me plainly, which I respected. She said, *I can give you a respectable life and a child and I will be a good wife, but there is a door in me that stays shut, and you don't get to ask what's behind it.* And I said yes. Because I wanted her. Because wanting her felt like the biggest thing that would ever happen to me, and I was twenty-six and stupid, and I thought love was a thing you could bank."

"And Elodie?"

His jaw worked. "Elodie was the door."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got that won't hurt you worse than you're already hurt, and believe me, girl, I've done the arithmetic on this more times than you've had hot dinners." He leaned forward. For a second the old authority came back — the man who had signed things, arranged things, driven my mother to appointments and stood outside doors. "You found the letters. Fine. But listen to me. Whatever story you're building out of them — and you are building one, I can see it in your face, you've got her face when she was building something — whatever it is, it's missing the middle of it. The middle isn't in the letters. The middle is why they all came back stamped."

"Because Elodie sent them back."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because answering them would have killed her." He said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report weather. "Or ruined her. Or dragged her back under. Pick your verb. I never got the full account either — that was the other term of the arrangement. I drove her to the station once. Once. To see Elodie off. Your mother sat in the car and didn't go in. She asked me to post the last letter instead of her, because she couldn't make her hand do it, and I posted it, and it came back, and after that there weren't any more letters. Just the draft. You found the draft too, I expect."

The room tilted slightly. "How do you know about the draft?"

"Because I'm the one who found it first." He almost laughed, and it was the bleakest sound I'd ever heard him make. "Nineteen ninety-four. She'd left it in her work bag and I read it standing in the kitchen and it stopped mid-sentence and I put it back and never said a word, because what was there to say? *Dear wife, I've read your unfinished letter to the woman you loved, shall I make tea?*" He shook his head slowly. "Your mother practiced that sentence her whole life, Tamsin. Practiced it and never performed it. Some people die mid-rehearsal."

I was crying now myself, quietly, without drama, which felt like the family style. "Why wouldn't she let herself say it? What was so catastrophic about one sentence?"

And my father looked at me for a long time — looked at me with his own eyes, the ones I carried around on my face — and I watched him weigh something, and I watched the scales tip toward mercy, and then tip back, and then settle somewhere in between, which was where my father had lived his entire life.

"Ask the house," he said. "The boarding house. The Radiators, she called it. If anyone can finish that sentence, it's the people she sent your mother's silence back to." He picked up the letter, held it a moment, and handed it back to me with both hands, formally, like returning a weapon. "But take this one thing from me, because it's the only thing I've got that's true all the way down."

He gripped my wrist. His hand was lighter than I remembered, papery, but the grip was still his.

"Your mother paid so you wouldn't have to."

"Paid what?" I said. "Paid *whom*? Dad, that's not — you can't just—"

But he had already let go of my wrist and picked up his crossword, and something in the set of his shoulders said the audience was over. Not cruelty. Exhaustion. A man who had held a door shut for thirty-five years and had just, for ten minutes, taken his weight off it, and found his arms shaking.

"The middle," he said, to the crossword. "Get the middle. Then come back and hate me properly, if there's any left over."

* * *

I walked down through the grounds of Beechwood Grange with the letter in my pocket again, over my heart, and the magpie followed me along the wall for fifty yards as if it expected payment.

Complicit, hostage, or both. I turned it over on the long walk to the bus stop. He had married knowing; he had posted the final letter himself; he had read the draft and folded it back into her life without a word. Complicit, certainly. But complicity requires a beneficiary, and I could not find his anywhere. He'd gotten a wife who was kind to him and absent from him in the same gesture, a daughter who inherited the locked door, and thirty-five years of knowing precisely what he'd traded for and never once being allowed to discuss the exchange rate. Hostage, then. Or maybe there was a third category my vocabulary didn't cover yet: the people who stand outside the room while the decision gets made, and spend the rest of their lives as furniture in someone else's secret.

At the bus stop I took out the letter and read it again. *Say the word. Say the word.* She'd said it twice, and the word had never come, and the envelope had come back, and somewhere in that silence I had been conceived, or born, or named — the timeline still wouldn't sit straight in my head, and I was beginning to suspect it never would, that the arithmetic of it was designed not to balance.

The bus came. I got on. In my pocket, my phone buzzed: a message from Dev, the caretaker at The Radiators, sent late the night before and only now surfacing from a dead zone on the coast road.

*You should know the house talked about you last night. Not everyone's pleased you're asking questions. But Mrs. O says — and I quote — "if the girl wants the middle of the story, tell her Room 4 has been waiting thirty-five years, it can wait till Thursday."*

Thursday. Three days away.

I pressed the phone against my chest, right over letter fourteen, and watched the hedges flick past, and understood that I had just been given an appointment with a room.

And somewhere behind a wardrobe in a sold house, or in a cupboard door in a boarding house by the sea, a photograph of me aged seven months was taped where someone could see it every day — and I was about to find out whether I'd been the reason for the silence, or the only excuse anyone could bear to give it.
