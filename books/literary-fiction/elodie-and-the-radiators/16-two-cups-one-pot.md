## 16. Two Cups, One Pot

I have made tea for strangers for fifty years, and I can tell you this: you learn everything about a person from how they hold the cup.

The girl — Tamsin, though I refused to use the name out loud for the first hour — held hers with both hands wrapped around it, not drinking, just warming herself on it, as if the tea were a small animal she'd rescued and wasn't sure she was allowed to keep. Margaret used to do that. Exactly that. I looked at those hands and felt thirty-five years arrive all at once, like a tide coming in over ground you'd convinced yourself was solid.

"You have her way of standing in doorways," I'd said to her on the shingle, because it was true and because I needed to say something before my legs gave out. She had stood there at the edge of my daily walk with her shoulders squared against the wind and her weight on her back foot, half in, half out, the posture of a woman who has spent her whole life waiting to be invited in or told to leave. Margaret stood like that. In doorways, in conversations, in our bed, in the whole of her own life — always positioned at the threshold, never once committing to the room.

Now she was in my kitchen. The daughter-shaped proof of everything I had spent three and a half decades not thinking about, sitting at my table with her mother's hands around my cup, and Mrs. Okonkwo had retreated to the parlour with the tact of a woman who has lived communally long enough to know which silences are furniture and which are weather.

"I'm not going to ask you anything," Tamsin said. "Not yet. I just — will you tell me? Whatever you want to tell me."

Which is, I'll grant her, the only correct opening move. Everyone else who had come to The Radiators asking about the old days wanted a story they could carry away, a souvenir, something to file. She was offering me the pen.

So I made a second cup. One pot, two cups — that was always the ritual, even when we were poor enough that the tea bags got a second dunking, even when we were so young that poverty seemed like a personality rather than a condition. One pot, two cups. You don't pour for someone unless you intend to sit with them. That was the whole theology of it, really, though neither of us knew to call it theology back then. We called it Tuesday.

* * *

Her name was Margaret Galloway and she arrived at the flat on Calder Street in October 1984 with one suitcase, a coat that had been good five years earlier, and the expression of someone who has already read the last page of the book and decided to keep going anyway. I was twenty-three and subletting a room from a couple who fought exclusively through notes left on the fridge. The landlady said the other room was taken. It wasn't. Margaret took it.

She was not beautiful in the way that gets written about. She was beautiful in the way that gets *noticed* — by shopkeepers, by bus drivers, by other women in queues, all of them doing that small double-take and then pretending they hadn't. She had a laugh that started as a cough, like the laugh itself was embarrassed to exist. And she was funny. God, she was funny, in the dry, sideways, self-deprecating register that people mistake for sadness until they realise it's actually armour worn so long it's grown into the skin.

We became friends the way fires become fires. No decision anywhere in it. Within a month we had a system: she cooked, I burned things, we ate standing up at the counter because the table was buried under her sewing and my books. Within two months we had named the radiators.

You should understand about the radiators. The flat's heating was a Victorian joke — cast-iron monsters under every window that clanked and hissed and gurgled like a ship's engine room and produced heat in roughly the proportion that a candle produces daylight. Margaret knocked on one the first cold night and said, "What do we call them? They're clearly alive." I said they sounded like a jazz band tuning up. She said no — they were a band, an actual band, and each one had a name, and by midnight we'd christened all seven. The big one in the front room was Duke. The temperamental one in the bathroom that scalded you or froze you depending on its mood was Miles. There was a little one in the hall that only ever produced a faint warm breath, and she called him Chet Baker, "because he's beautiful and he barely plays."

That was the kind of thing we did. We invented holidays — St. Swithin's Eve Observed, the Feast of the First Frost, National Put the Clocks Forward Illegally Day, which we celebrated by being an hour early to everything for one glorious confusing Sunday. We had codes in the grocery lists. *Milk, bread, the usual, and something for Sunday* meant she'd had a bad week at work and wanted the good biscuits and no questions. *Eggs (6)* versus *eggs (dozen)* was a message system about whether we were speaking freely or carefully. I could write you a dictionary of that flat. Two women in three rooms, and we built a language dense enough to live inside.

People ask — people asked, eventually, the ones who guessed — when did you know. And the honest answer is that there was no knowing because there was no before. I have tried to find the seam in it, the moment friendship turned, and there isn't one. It was like trying to remember when you learned your own name. Somewhere in the second winter she fell asleep against my shoulder during a radio play and neither of us moved for two hours because moving would have required acknowledging what stillness meant, and after that it was simply true, the way the radiators were simply loud. We didn't announce it. We didn't even say it, not for months. Saying it would have made it a thing in the world, and the world — we both understood this without discussing it — had opinions about things like us.

But inside the flat, inside the language, we were married. I want to be precise about that word because I know what it costs. Married. Not playing house, not experimenting, not two lonely women making do. Married. I knew where she liked to be touched and where she couldn't bear it. She knew that I cried at the shipping forecast and pretended it was the onions. We had a plan — we always had plans, plans were our love language — and the plan was a cottage. A specific cottage; we'd seen it in an estate agent's window in Whitby, grey stone, blue door, a garden gone wild. "When it's safe," she said, and I said, "When it's safe," and we both knew safe might be a country that didn't exist yet, and we kept the clipping anyway, in the tin with the ticket stubs. Room 4 still has the tin. I checked, the day after she died. I checked before I let myself feel anything else. That tells you something about me, probably.

* * *

Then there was a man.

I want to tell this part properly, because I imagine you've had thirty-five years of versions, most of them wrong, and because the girl across the table from me has his jaw.

His name was Richard — no, you'd know him by another name, the one on your birth certificate, but I knew him as Richard and Richard he stays. He was a colleague of Margaret's, decent, dull in the way of men who have never been required to be interesting, and he pursued her with the patient confidence of someone who has never heard the word no delivered at full volume. And here is the part I need you to sit with, Tamsin, because your whole life sits on top of it:

He wasn't a villain. I have spent decades wishing he were. A villain would make the arithmetic clean. But he was just a man, kind in a generic way, ambitious in a small way, who saw a beautiful woman and wanted the ordinary life with her that the world kept insisting was the only real one. And Margaret — Margaret was tired. That's the ugly truth underneath everything. She was tired of the locked doors and the lowered voices and the landlord's innuendo and her mother's silence and the constant low hum of danger that came with loving me in 1987. She was tired of *when it's safe*. Safe was being offered to her on a plate, with a ring on it, and all it cost was —

Well. All it cost was me.

The ultimatum wasn't dramatic. People expect a scene, shouting, a slammed door. What actually happened was a Tuesday evening in March, and she said, "He's asked me. Properly asked me." And I said, "And?" And she stood in the doorway — that doorway, that exact stance your daughter has, I wasn't lying to her on the beach — and she said, "Elodie, I can't do this for another twenty years. I can't spend my whole life in rooms." And I said, "So leave the room." And she said, "You know that's not what I mean."

And I did know. That was the worst of it. I knew exactly what she meant, and some traitorous exhausted part of me understood it, and understanding it made me furious, and being furious made me ashamed, and I have carried all three of those things in the same pocket ever since. She chose survival. I have called it betrayal for thirty-five years and I have called it survival for thirty-five years and both words fit and neither one comforts.

She married him in September. I did not go. I stood outside the church — yes, I went that far, I walked forty miles of my own stupidity to stand across the road from a church in the rain like a madwoman — and I watched her come out in white, and she looked happy, is the terrible thing, or she looked like a woman performing happiness well enough to pass, and at that distance I couldn't tell the difference, and I have never stopped wondering whether she could.

* * *

Now the letters. Because I know about the letters — Mrs. Okonkwo told me, after the council finally voted to let you upstairs, and I sat in my room and shook for an hour, and then I got angry, and then I got curious, and curiosity won, the way it always does, curse it.

Forty-one letters. She wrote me forty-one letters and I returned every single one unopened, and you have read them, and you have earned the truth of them, so here it is:

I returned them because answering them would have killed me.

Not metaphorically. Let me be plain, since everyone in this house has learned to be plain the hard way. If I had opened letter one — *Dear E., I saw a dog today that looked like Duke* — if I had opened that letter and seen her handwriting, I would have written back. And if I had written back, I would have been in it again, up to my neck, the secret meetings and the coded lists and the looking-over-shoulder, except now she'd be a married woman and I'd be the other woman, the dirty little secret, the thing that could end her marriage and her job and her standing with one exposed envelope. And worse — worse — I would have been the reason she was living half a life. I would have watched her choose the cage every day and call it a home, and I would have had to smile through the bars.

I couldn't survive that twice. I had already survived losing her once, and I knew the weight of it to the ounce, and I knew I could not carry it again while also carrying the guilt of being the weight. So I sent them back. Unopened. Return to sender. Forty-one times I performed a cruelty so complete that it must have looked like hatred, and it was the opposite of hatred, it was the only shape my love could take that didn't destroy us both. I buried her alive so that she could stay dead to me, because a dead Margaret I could survive. A half-alive one, writing to me from inside a marriage, needing me, apologising to me — no. No. I am a strong woman, Tamsin, I have built a house out of other people's broken pieces and kept it standing through winters that should have finished it, and I am telling you that would have broken me past mending.

And then you were born.

They sent me one photograph. One, smuggled through her sister, years later — a baby on a blanket, furious red face, fists like a boxer's. I taped it inside the cupboard door in Room 4 where nobody would see it, and I looked at it every morning for years, and I will tell you what I felt, since you've doubtless been torturing yourself with theories: I felt relief. Enormous, shameful relief. Because that photograph was the proof that her choice had bought something. That the arithmetic had come out with a number in it. She had traded me for a child, and the child was real and breathing and furious, and I thought: good. Good. Then it wasn't for nothing. Then let her keep her bargain, and let me keep my grief, and let the letters rot in whatever drawer she hid them in.

I stopped writing back — I had never written back — but more than that, I stopped *almost* writing back. Do you understand the discipline that takes? For years my hand would start toward paper and I would look at the cupboard door and stop. You were my reason, child. Not my wound. My reason. I need you to hear that, even if you don't believe it yet.

Do I absolve her? No. She broke something in me that never healed right, and I have earned the right to say so, and I say it to her grave whenever I visit it, which is more often than I admit to anyone here. Do I condemn her? No. She was drowning and she grabbed the nearest boat, and the boat was a man, and I was open water. Anyone who tells you they'd have chosen differently is either lying or hasn't been drowning.

* * *

The tea had gone cold somewhere in the middle of all that. Hers too — she hadn't drunk a drop, just held it, warming her hands on her inheritance.

She was crying without any noise, which is also Margaret, who cried like a person defusing a bomb. I pushed the biscuit tin toward her, because grief runs on sugar whether it admits it or not, and because I needed a moment where neither of us had to be the strong one.

"She never said it," Tamsin said finally. Her voice was wrecked and steady at once, which is a trick I recognised. "In the letters. Twenty years of them and she circles it and circles it and never says it. And there's a draft — an unsent one — it starts *Elodie, I chose, and here is what it cost*, and it stops. Mid-sentence. It just stops."

I closed my eyes. Thirty-five years, and the sentence still had its hand around my throat.

"Will you finish it?" she asked. "For her. Read it aloud — the draft — and finish the sentence. However she would have said it. You knew her voice better than anyone alive."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Downstairs someone laughed; the house does that, keeps living while you're coming apart, that's its whole philosophy.

"It's been a long time since I let myself hear her voice," I said.

"I know," said Margaret Galloway's daughter, holding out the unfinished page with both hands, the way you offer something to a bird. "Me neither."

I took the paper.
