## 17. The Sentence

I had rehearsed the request all the way up the tide road, and then, standing in Elodie Marchetti's kitchen with two cups of tea going cold between us, I lost it entirely. What came out instead was: "Do you still have the draft?"

She didn't pretend not to know which draft. She had known since the moment I'd said my mother's name on the shingle yesterday — maybe before that, maybe since the photo of a baby taped inside a cupboard door. Some silences are so long that when they finally break, they don't break loudly. They just open, like a door someone has been leaning against for thirty-five years.

"The one she never sent," I said. "The reply. It stops in the middle of a sentence."

"I know where it stops." Elodie turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, a small precise gesture, like adjusting an instrument. "I've known for thirty-five years where it stops."

"You've read it?"

"Margaret showed it to me." My mother's name, in this stranger's mouth, was *Margaret*, and hearing it was like seeing her in colour for the first time. "In 1990. She came here once. One time. She stood in that doorway" — she nodded at the kitchen door, and something went through me, because I had been told I had her way of standing in doorways, and here was the original frame — "and she put it on the table and asked me to burn it. And I said no. And she said, *then at least don't answer it.*"

"And you didn't."

"And I didn't." She looked at her tea as if it might be holding its breath too. "Forty-one letters, Tamsin. Every one of them came back to me unopened. Do you know what that is? It's a conversation with a wall. But a wall you built yourself, brick by brick, because you love what's on the other side of it too much to knock."

I thought about saying something. I had a whole speech somewhere in my chest, assembled over weeks of reading her name in my mother's handwriting — *Dear E.,* over and over, *Dear E.,* like a prayer said to a saint who never answered. But Elodie raised one hand, slightly, and the speech sat back down.

"She's dead," Elodie said. "Three months now. Mrs. Okonkwo told me, and then Dev told me, and then I yelled at both of them for telling me, and then I sat in Room 4 all night with the radiator clanking like a dying animal." She looked up. "You want me to read it aloud. The unfinished sentence. You want me to finish it."

"How did you—"

"Because you've got her eyes and none of her patience, and you've been circling the request since you sat down like a moth around a bulb it doesn't trust." She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile I'd seen on her, and it rearranged her whole face into someone I felt I'd met before — in a photograph, in a letter, in the margins of my mother's life. "Where is it?"

I took it out of my coat pocket. Of course I'd brought it. I had carried it for eleven days like a splinter working toward the surface: four lines in my mother's hand, the fourth stopping mid-word, mid-thought, mid-life.

*Elodie, I chose, and here is what it cost.*

And then nothing. Thirty-five years of nothing, folded into an envelope that smelled of another decade.

Elodie didn't reach for it immediately. She wiped her palms on her trousers first — an old habit, I'd learn later, from years of painting and plastering and fixing other people's radiators, always cleaning her hands before touching anything that mattered. Then she unfolded the page and flattened it against the table with both hands, the way you'd calm a frightened animal.

"She wrote this," she said quietly, "the week after your christening. I know because she mentions the christening gown, and then stops. She couldn't get past the gown. Isn't that something? A woman can walk away from everything, but a christening gown will finish her off."

"Read it," I said. My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Please. Out loud. I've read it forty times and it's just — ink. It's my mother's handwriting saying half a thing. I need to hear it finished. Even if you're guessing. *Especially* if you're guessing."

Elodie looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the tide was doing whatever the tide does when nobody's watching — I could hear it, that long slow exhale against the shingle, the sound I would come to think of as the house breathing.

"If I finish it," she said, "it becomes true. That's the danger of sentences. You can unsay a thought. You can't unsay a sentence spoken aloud in a kitchen with the witness present." She tapped the page. "Are you prepared for it to be true?"

"No," I said honestly.

"Good," she said. "Neither was she."

* * *

She read the first three lines in her own voice, low and even, and it was strange how little they needed her — my mother's words survived being read by a stranger, the way a tune survives being hummed badly. But when she reached the fourth line, she stopped. Her thumb rested under the final word — *cost* — the way you'd rest your hand on a door you weren't sure you wanted to open.

Then she closed her eyes.

And when she spoke again, it wasn't her voice. I don't mean she did an impression. I mean something came through her that had been waiting thirty-five years for a mouth — my mother's cadence, my mother's particular way of arriving at the hard part of a sentence only after circling it twice, the dryness that was never really dryness, the warmth that was never quite allowed to be warm.

"*Elodie, I chose, and here is what it cost.*" A breath. "*I chose him because he was safe, and I chose safety because I was a coward, and I called it protecting you because cowards always do. I chose you both, and lost you both, and I am sorry.*"

The kitchen went absolutely still. Even the radiator, that old iron thing ticking away in the corner like the house's stubborn heart, seemed to hold its note.

Elodie opened her eyes. They were wet, but she didn't wipe them, didn't acknowledge them, let them sit there like weather.

"That's it," she said, in her own voice again. "That's the sentence. Or near enough. I've had thirty-five years to imagine the ending, and every version I ever wrote ended the same way, because Margaret Galloway never in her life started a confession she intended to leave short." She slid the page across the table toward me. "There. Now it's true. Now neither of us can unsay it."

I picked up the paper. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me — grief is humiliating enough without tremors. The words were still hers, still stopped where they'd always stopped. But the air above them had changed. Something had crossed it.

"It changes everything," I said. "And it fixes nothing."

Elodie barked a laugh — short, surprised, real. "Oh, you *are* hers. Yes. Exactly that. Welcome to the family business, Tamsin: we trade in things that change everything and fix nothing. It's the only honest currency there is."

"Why didn't she send it?" I asked, though I'd sworn I wouldn't ask, though I knew the answer, though I'd reconstructed it myself from a torn stub and a draft and a father weeping in a care home. "Why write it and stop?"

"Because finishing it meant meaning it," Elodie said. "And meaning it meant living with it. Your mother could do almost anything except complete a sentence that would have required her to become a different person." She stood, took both cups, and poured fresh tea without asking, and I understood this was not avoidance; it was liturgy. "But here's what I'll tell you, since you've earned it and since I'm old and tired of guarding doors. She didn't stop writing because she stopped loving me. She stopped because loving me out loud would have cost you. And she decided — wrongly, probably, but she decided — that you were worth more than I was."

"And was she right?"

Elodie set the cup down in front of me and looked at me straight, and I saw that behind the sharpness was thirty-five years of having asked herself the same question at three in the morning.

"No," she said. "And yes. And it doesn't matter, because it's done, and the dead don't take corrections." She sat back down. "What matters is what you do with the sentence now. She left it half-finished. You're the one holding the pen."

I looked down at the draft. At the space after *cost*, the white gap where the rest should have been, thirty-five years wide.

"I want to finish it," I heard myself say. "Not her way. Mine. And I want to send it — to the house, to everyone, to whoever opens the mail. She owed you forty-one letters. The least I can do is deliver the forty-second."

Elodie studied me for a long time. Then she pushed the sugar bowl aside, took a clean sheet of paper from the drawer — I noticed the drawer held paper, pens, envelopes, as if the house itself had been waiting decades for correspondence to resume — and slid it toward me along with a pen.

"Well then," she said. "Start writing. And Tamsin—" She paused, and the almost-smile came back, softer this time. "Finish the sentence. Whatever else you do. In this house, we've had enough of people who stop halfway."

I put the pen to the paper, and outside the tide turned, and somewhere upstairs a radiator knocked twice, like a knock at a door that had finally, after all these years, been answered.
