## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

Bram Eastwick could write an apology that made a grown man weep into his oatmeal, and he did so most mornings before nine.

This particular Tuesday it was for a Mr. Delacroix, who had forgotten his wedding anniversary for the third consecutive year and now required not merely forgiveness but something closer to canonization. Bram drafted the letter in longhand first—clients trusted the tremor of a pen the way they never trusted a keyboard—then typed it up, printed it on cream stock, and sealed it with the wax stamp he kept for occasions requiring gravitas.

*What I failed to see is that love is not a monument but a garden,* he wrote, *and I have been admiring the fence.*

Mr. Delacroix would send it. Mrs. Delacroix would cry. They would renew their vows in Napa by spring. Bram billed four hundred dollars and felt nothing whatsoever, which was, he had come to understand, the entire point of the profession. He was a man who sold other people's feelings at retail while keeping his own in a warehouse somewhere without a loading dock.

His office occupied the front room of a third-floor walkup on Corvair Street, chosen for its proximity to the post office and its total absence of view. The desk faced a wall. On the wall hung a single framed item: a certificate of appreciation from the Grief Literacy Society of Greater Ashford, which he had won for a eulogy delivered on behalf of a client who couldn't bring herself to speak at her own mother's funeral. Bram had attended too, standing at the back, mouthing along. It was the closest he came to public speaking, and even then his lips moved silently, like a man practicing a language he'd been exiled from.

At 11:15 he performed the day's central ritual.

The ritual went like this: he took from the drawer a postcard—one of forty remaining in a box he'd bought in bulk a decade ago, each bearing a photograph of a lighthouse he had never visited—and wrote a message in his smallest, most controlled hand. Today's read: *The man across the hall got a parrot. It says "hello" in three voices, all of them wrong.* Then he addressed it to 14 Marrow Lane, Apartment B, affixed a stamp, and walked it down three flights and two blocks to the blue mailbox on the corner of Vetch.

The mail carrier collected from that box at noon. By one o'clock, Bram's words were en route to a burned lot.

He had been sending postcards to 14 Marrow Lane for nineteen years, eight months, and some days he counted precisely. The house had gone up in flames when he was twenty-two; the city had razed what remained when he was twenty-four; the lot had sat vacant ever since, growing weeds with real ambition. The postal service, in its infinite bureaucratic mercy, had never returned a single card. Somewhere in a dead-letter facility in Atlanta, presumably, there was a crate containing two decades of Bram Eastwick's correspondence—a crate that constituted, if anyone ever audited it, the most complete record of his inner life. He found this thought comforting rather than humiliating, which told him most of what he needed to know about himself.

He did not write to his sister. That was the arrangement, though no one had arranged it. Odile had stopped writing first—or he had stopped answering first; the sequence had been disputed internally for years, litigated nightly at 3 a.m. in a courtroom of his skull where he always lost. The last thing she'd said to him, standing in the parking lot of the Redding Temporary Housing Center with everything they owned in two trash bags, was: *"You always know the right words, Bram. It's the only thing you're a coward about."*

He had proven her right with great consistency since.

* * *

The legal notice arrived on Thursday, folded inside an envelope from the City of Ashford Department of Asset Recovery, which sounded less like a government office than a support group for people who had lost things.

Bram almost discarded it as junk. The city sent him notices regularly—street sweeping, water quality, a referendum about roundabouts—and he recycled them with the serene efficiency of a man who had opted out of civic life the way other people opt out of organ donation. But this one had his name spelled correctly, which unsettled him more than any error would have.

*NOTICE OF PUBLIC AUCTION,* it began. *Pursuant to Ordinance 44-C, the following properties, held in receivership...*

He read the address twice. Three times. The fourth time he read it aloud, because sometimes his mouth could do what his eyes refused.

"Fourteen Marrow Lane."

The lot. The weeds. The chain-link fence with its tasteful NO TRESPASSING sign, installed by someone with a sense of humor he'd never appreciated. Nineteen years of postcards sitting in a dead-letter facility in Atlanta, and now the ground itself was going under the hammer.

Bram sat down on the kitchen chair—he worked at the kitchen table when the letters were difficult, and this qualified—and read the notice through properly. Auction date: the twenty-sixth. Venue: Halloway & Finch Auctioneers, Pier Road. Minimum bid: $18,000. Sold as-is, where-is, cash or certified funds.

As-is. Where-is. As if a place could be anything else.

He noticed, distantly, that his hands were doing something unusual, which was shaking. He watched them with clinical interest, the way he might watch a stranger's hands. He was, professionally speaking, an expert in the physical symptoms of emotion. He had described trembling hands for widowers, clenched jaws for estranged brothers, a catch in the throat for every bridegroom who'd ever hired him. He knew the vocabulary of feeling the way a travel agent knows Paris.

It occurred to him, sitting there with the notice spread flat beneath his palms, that he had never once used the vocabulary on himself.

* * *

That evening he broke routine, which for Bram Eastwick was roughly equivalent to a lighthouse changing its flash pattern. He did not eat his scheduled dinner. Instead he walked to Vetch Street and stood in front of the blue mailbox for a long time, holding a postcard he hadn't written yet.

A woman walking a terrier gave him a look. People often gave Bram looks; he was a tall, still man who stood in places longer than was normal, wearing the expression of someone listening to music no one else could hear. He smiled at her—the smile, the one clients paid for, the one that ended conversations—and she hurried on.

The smile was his best work. He'd built it at twenty-two, in the weeks after the fire, when everyone at the temporary housing center kept asking how he was holding up and he discovered that if you smiled in a certain way, people stopped asking. It was a masterpiece of engineering: warm enough to reassure, closed enough to discourage follow-up questions. He had worn it so long it had fused to his face, and somewhere underneath it, presumably, was the face he'd been born with.

He looked down at the blank postcard. The lighthouse stared out at its painted sea.

For nineteen years the messages had been easy—small domestic reports, weather, the parrot, the price of oranges—because they were addressed to a place, not a person. Places don't ask questions. Places don't say *you always know the right words.* A burned lot was the perfect correspondent: it received everything and demanded nothing.

But tonight the notice sat folded in his jacket pocket like a stone, and the lot was no longer a lot. It was a property with a parcel number, entering the market, acquiring paperwork, accumulating history again. Someone would bid on it. Someone would build on it. And every postcard he'd ever sent would be buried under whatever came next, unclaimed, unread, a life's correspondence filed under *undeliverable.*

Bram uncapped his pen.

*Dear Marrow Lane,* he started, and then stopped, because for the first time in nineteen years the lie of the address offended him. There was no one at Marrow Lane. There had been no one at Marrow Lane since the night of the fire, since—

Since the night he'd run.

He wrote instead, in handwriting that wobbled like a drunk's: *I'm sorry. I'm coming back. — B.*

Then he hesitated over the address line, pen hovering, and finally—because the notice was in his pocket, because the auction date was circled in his mind, because a man cannot mail a letter to a decision he hasn't made—he wrote the return address clearly in the corner, legible for the first time in two decades:

*Bram Eastwick, 3rd Fl., 77 Corvair St.*

He dropped it in the slot and heard it land on top of the day's collection with a small, final sound, like a coin going into a machine that dispenses consequences.

Walking home, he passed the community board outside the pharmacy, where flyers layered like sediment. A new one had gone up that afternoon, glossy against the faded ones: a photograph of a smiling man in a good suit, arm around a ribbon-cutting scissors. **COUNCILMAN REEVE VOSS: PROUD TO ANNOUNCE THE MARROW STREET REVITALIZATION INITIATIVE.** Beneath the photo, in bold type: *A New Future for an Old Neighborhood.*

Bram stood in front of the flyer until the streetlights buzzed on.

He knew the name. Of course he knew the name. Everyone in Ashford knew Reeve Voss—the slumlord turned benefactor, the man whose name was on the library wing and the youth center and half the renovated storefronts downtown. What Bram knew, privately, in the locked room of himself, was something else: that Voss had owned the building next door to 14 Marrow Lane. That the insurance settlement had arrived with suspicious speed. That his mother had once said, laughing, *that man would burn his own grandmother for the zoning variance*—and then, six months later, wasn't laughing anymore, because nobody was.

He had never said any of this aloud. Not to the fire investigators. Not to the caseworkers. Not to Odile, who had asked him, once, in the parking lot, with the trash bags at their feet: *Did you see anything, Bram? Did you see who?*

And he had smiled. The smile. The one that ends conversations.

She had looked at him for a long moment—longer than anyone had ever looked at him—and then picked up both bags, hers and his, and carried them inside alone.

Bram turned away from the flyer. His heart was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional. Tomorrow he would call the auction house and register as a bidder, and the clerk would ask for a name, and he would give his own, out loud, to a stranger, for the first time in years.

Somewhere across town, presumably, boxes were being packed. Someone at Halloway & Finch was cataloguing the contents of a storage unit marked MARROW LANE—contents that should not exist, because everything had burned, and yet.

He didn't know that yet. He only knew that the lighthouse postcard was gone, swallowed into the city's machinery, carrying his return address toward a future he could not yet imagine—and that for the first time in nineteen years, Bram Eastwick wanted to be found.
