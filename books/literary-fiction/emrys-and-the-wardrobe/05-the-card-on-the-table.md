## 5. The Card on the Table

The card sat on my kitchen table for three days before I did anything about it.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only discipline I have left. Three days. It lay where Daniel Ferro—or whatever his name truly was—had set it down at the reception, face up beside the finger sandwiches and the funeral flowers Margot had ordered in white because our mother had once said she disliked lilies. The card was cream-colored, heavy stock, the kind of paper that announces its own expense without shouting. No name printed on it. No telephone number. Only a single line of handwriting in an alphabet I didn't recognize, slanting letters with little flags and hooks, dense as birds on a wire.

For three days I walked past it. I made coffee. I ate toast I could not taste. I read the obituaries in the paper out of a habit so old it had become geological. And each time I passed the table, the card looked at me the way certain objects learn to look at you—not accusingly, nothing so dramatic, but with the patient attention of something that knows it will win.

Margot had watched me pocket it at the reception. I'd seen her see it. She'd said nothing, which from Margot was a sentence in itself, a whole paragraph of nothing, and I had spent thirty years learning to read her silences the way other people read weather. This one said: *leave it*. Or possibly: *not here*. Or possibly: *please*.

But here is what I have come to understand about myself, late and reluctantly: I am a man who reviews restaurants he cannot taste. My entire professional life is built on the gap between what is presented to me and what I can actually receive. I describe saffron and smoked paprika and the bright acid of a good verjus, and I do it beautifully—I have won awards for it—and none of it passes through me. It goes from memory to page without ever touching the living nerve. So when a stranger hands me a sentence in a dead family's language and walks away into the rain, some part of me recognizes the arrangement immediately. Here is a text. Here is a flavor everyone else can perceive and I cannot. And here is the old, familiar hunger to fake my way through until the faking becomes real.

On the fourth morning I photographed the card.

I used the same phone I use to document plates—the amuse-bouche, the intermezzo, the deconstructed anything—and there was something obscene about that continuity, laying the funeral card down on the same marble slab where I photograph quenelles, adjusting the light, tapping the shutter twice to be safe. The image came out sharp. The script stared up at me from the screen, and this time, enlarged, I could see that the hand was old. Not shaky, exactly, but economical in the way of someone who learned to write when ink was rationed by the drop. Every stroke earned its place.

I sent it to three people.

This required some research, I'll admit. I knew no linguists. I know food writers, chefs, maître d's, one very charming sommelier who cries when he discusses Riesling. But the internet is a great leveler of ignorance, and within an hour I had assembled a shortlist: three academics whose names surfaced repeatedly in connection with minority languages, endangered scripts, the small stubborn tongues that survive in valleys and on islands while empires wash over them like tides.

The first was a Professor Edmund Hale at Cambridge, whose specialty was listed as Romance philology. His reply came within two hours, brisk as a bus schedule:

*Mr. Amberdale — Thank you for your enquiry. I regret I cannot assist. The script appears to be a regional variant outside my area of competence. Might I suggest contacting a specialist in Alpine linguistics? Yours sincerely, E. Hale.*

*Appears to be.* Even his refusal contained information. A regional variant. Alpine. I held onto those words like a man holding onto a railing.

The second was Dr. Sofia Renner-Marchetti in Zurich. She took a full day, and her reply was longer, warmer, and somehow more unsettling:

*Dear Mr. Amberdale, What an intriguing image. I must decline to speculate, however, as I make it a policy never to authenticate or interpret documents whose provenance is unknown to me. If you can tell me where this writing comes from—who wrote it, when, and under what circumstances—I would be glad to reconsider. With best wishes, S. Renner-Marchetti.*

Provenance. Under what circumstances. I read the email four times. There was a carefulness in it that went beyond academic caution. It was the care of someone stepping around something on the floor—a word like *provenance*, deployed where a simpler person would have written *context*. As if the sentence itself were a known object. As if it had a file somewhere, a history, a shape that scholars had agreed not to touch without gloves.

And then there was the third.

Professor Ilse Brandt. Innsbruck. Her university page listed her title in both German and Ladin, which meant nothing to me then and means everything to me now. Her photograph showed a woman in her sixties with cropped grey hair and the unimpressed expression of someone who has spent forty years listening to amateurs mispronounce things.

I sent her the same email I'd sent the others. The photograph of the card. A brief note: *Found among my late mother's effects. Can you identify the language? Any assistance appreciated. Quentin Amberdale.*

Then I waited.

Waiting, I have discovered, is a country I visit more often than I'd like. I waited through lunch, which was a bowl of soup I chose for its temperature rather than its flavor, since temperature is the last sense to abandon you. I waited through the afternoon, during which I drafted four hundred words of a review of a bistro in Marylebone where the duck was, according to my notes, "excellent"—a word I typed while looking at the duck's photograph and feeling precisely nothing, the way a man might describe a stranger's wedding from the car park. I waited through dinner, through the blue hour when the city goes quiet enough to hear its own plumbing, through the first hour of sleep and the second.

At ten minutes past midnight, my phone lit up on the nightstand.

I should say something about how it felt, seeing her name there. It didn't feel like an answer. It felt like a door in a house you thought you knew—one you've walked past a thousand times—which has suddenly swung open onto a room you've never seen. The message was four words long.

*Where did you get this?*

No greeting. No signature. No question mark, even—though the absence of the question mark somehow made it worse, made it less a question than a demand wearing a question's clothes. I sat up in bed with the phone in both hands like a man holding a bird that might fly apart.

I typed: *It was given to me. At my mother's funeral. By a man I don't know.*

I watched the screen. The three dots appeared, vanished, appeared again. Whoever Ilse Brandt was, she was awake at half past midnight in Innsbruck, typing and deleting, typing and deleting. Finally:

*Given. To you. At a funeral.*

*I understand how that sounds,* I wrote.

*Do you?* came the reply, and then, before I could formulate an answer: *Mr. Amberdale. Is the original in your possession?*

*Yes. A card. Handwritten.*

*Don't lose it. Don't show it to anyone else. And don't—* The dots churned for a long time. *There are things you should understand before we speak further. Are you able to talk tomorrow? Properly. By phone.*

*What things?*

Another long pause. When her answer came, I read it standing at the window with the city laid out below me, all those lit windows stacked in the dark like cells in a honeycomb, each one containing somebody's version of a life.

*Because that sentence is not a conversation. It is structured like testimony. And testimony, Mr. Amberdale, always has two sides—the one who speaks it, and the one it was spoken against. I need to know which side you are on.*

I stood there for a long time after the screen dimmed. Testimony. The word sat in me like a stone dropped into a well whose bottom I couldn't hear. Somewhere below, eventually, a splash—but distant, deferred, belonging to a depth I hadn't yet measured.

I went back to the kitchen and looked at the card again under the overhead light. Cream stock. Old hand. Those hooked, flagged letters crowded together like people sheltering from weather. I had photographed it, catalogued it, dispatched it across Europe to strangers, and still I could not read a single character of it. It was mine and utterly foreign, the way my own face is foreign in photographs, the way my own name—Amberdale, a name chosen out of a catalogue of respectable names sometime before I was born—has always sounded slightly like someone else's.

*Which side are you on.*

I thought about Daniel Ferro's face at the reception. Gaunt, courteous, rain-wet. He had said *Verroney* the way you'd hand someone a live coal—quickly, watching to see if they'd flinch. Margot hadn't flinched. That was the thing I kept returning to, the detail my mind circled like a tongue returning to a broken tooth: Margot hadn't flinched. She had gone smooth and pleasant and empty, which is what she does instead of flinching, and I had known her for fifty-eight years and only now asked myself what that smoothness was built over.

I took another photograph of the card. Then another, with different lighting, raking the light sideways across the paper so the pen-strokes threw tiny shadows and the writing stood up off the surface like terrain. I zoomed in until the pixels broke apart. I zoomed out until the card was just a pale rectangle on dark wood, a door seen from the wrong side.

Somewhere in the middle of the night—it must have been three, maybe four—I found myself at the table with a notebook, copying the sentence by hand. Character by character, doing my poor best with shapes I didn't understand, the way a child copies Chinese characters off a takeout menu. And as I copied, something happened that I can only describe as recognition running ahead of understanding. My hand knew things. The rhythm of the strokes, the way certain letters leaned into the next, the cadence of the line as it moved left to right and then, halfway through, seemed to gather itself and slow. It was like hearing a song in a language you don't speak and finding your foot keeping time anyway.

When I finished, I had a page of clumsy mimicry, and beneath it, without quite deciding to, I had written a single English word:

*Testimony.*

Underneath that, after a while: *Whose?*

The morning came up grey over the rooftops. I made coffee I couldn't taste and drank it anyway, for the warmth, for the ritual, for the small lie of normalcy it told. On the table the card lay where it had lain for five days now, patient as a creditor. My phone sat beside it, dark, holding Ilse Brandt's question the way a drawer holds a knife.

At nine o'clock, Margot called. I watched her name pulse on the screen—Margot, my sister, keeper of logistics, mistress of the smooth surface—and I thought about provenance, about circumstances, about which side anyone was on.

I let it ring out.

Then I picked up the card, put it in my inside pocket, over the heart, where a man carries the things he hasn't yet decided to confess to, and I dialed Innsbruck.

She answered on the second ring, before I could speak, as if she'd been sitting with the phone in her hand all night, as indeed perhaps she had.

"Before you say anything," Ilse Brandt said, in English flattened by decades of lecture halls into something precise and faintly musical, "tell me your father's name."

"His name," I said, "was Aldous Amberdale."

The silence on the line lasted long enough for me to hear her breathing change.

"No," she said finally. "Try again."
