## 27. The Last Witness Fails

The call came on a Tuesday, which felt wrong. Catastrophes, Quentin had always believed, should arrive on Fridays, at night, in bad weather—should announce themselves with the decorum of their own weight. Instead the telephone rang at eleven in the morning while he was standing at his kitchen counter buttering toast he would not taste, and Daniel Ferro's voice came through the speaker without its usual architecture of courtesy.

"They've moved her to palliative care." A pause, and then, as if the words had to be pried loose one at a time: "The doctors say days. Maybe a week. She's stopped eating. She asks—" His voice caught, and Quentin heard, with a strange vertigo, the sound of a man forgetting to perform composure. "She asks whether the boy has learned to speak yet."

Quentin set down the knife. "The boy?"

"You. You're the boy. You've always been the boy. The last Verroney child, the one who might still do what the old one couldn't." A breath, unsteady. "I'm sorry. I don't mean to— I'm not threatening you. I want you to know that I know the difference. But I have spent forty years being polite about this, Quentin, and politeness is what we had instead of a country, and I am telling you now that I have run out of it."

Outside the window, a delivery van idled at the curb, its driver eating a sandwich with the unselfconscious appetite of the living. Quentin watched him and felt the old familiar distance open between himself and the world—the sense of standing behind glass while everything on the other side continued without him. But this time the glass had a crack in it, and through the crack came a sound he recognized: grief, unprocessed, in someone else's voice, saying the things his own voice had never learned to say.

"Tell me what she needs," Quentin said.

"She needs the sentence. Read aloud. By a Verroney. In the valley dialect, then in English, so she can finally hear what Aldous wrote instead of imagining it for sixty years." Daniel's voice dropped. "She has imagined it ten thousand ways. Cruel ways, mostly. She has done him the cruelty of imagining, because no one ever gave her the truth to hold instead. Do you understand what that does to a person? To spend a lifetime filling a silence with your own worst guesses?"

Quentin did understand. He had spent thirty years filling the silence of his own mouth with descriptions of food he could not taste, a whole career built on the architecture of a wound he refused to name. He understood it so well that his throat tightened, and for a moment neither man spoke, and the delivery van pulled away, and the kitchen was very quiet.

"Come to the office," Daniel said finally, the courtesy reassembling itself piece by piece, like a man putting on a coat. "Or I'll come to you. Or we can meet anywhere. I don't care about the where anymore. I used to care about the where. I used to think if we did this properly, in the right room, with the right witnesses, it would mean something. Now I think the room doesn't matter. Only that she's alive when it happens."

* * *

He came to Quentin's apartment that evening, which was itself a surrender—Daniel Ferro had never once crossed the threshold of Quentin's private life, had kept their meetings in lobbies and offices and the neutral territory of cafés, as though the matter were a negotiation between institutions. Now he stood in the doorway with rain on his shoulders and his cardigan buttoned wrong, one hole below the other, and Quentin saw that he had dressed in the dark or in haste or in the particular blindness of a man whose mind has left his body behind.

"She's in St. Vincent's," Daniel said, sitting without being invited, on the edge of the chair, as if the chair were a station and he a man waiting for a train. "Palliative ward, fourth floor. The nurses are kind. They let me sit with her even though I'm not family. I told them I was family. It's the first lie I've told in years and it isn't even a lie."

Quentin poured him a whiskey he didn't want and took one himself he didn't taste. They sat across from each other in the room that still felt rented, and the rain worked at the windows, and Daniel held his glass without drinking, turning it slowly, the way a man turns an object when the object is standing in for everything he cannot say.

"I was twelve the first time I understood what she carried," he said. "She took me to the cemetery in the valley—this was before she came to live with us, when she still refused to leave, and we visited every summer. There's a memorial stone there. No bodies. Just names. My great-grandparents, my great-aunt, my great-uncle, all deported in March of 1945, all dead within the year. And she stood in front of that stone and she didn't cry. That's what I remember most. Twelve years old, waiting for the crying, and it never came. She just read the names, out loud, in order, the way you'd read a shopping list. And then she said, 'Now you know them,' and we went and had lunch."

He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and dry.

"It took me thirty years to understand that the reading *was* the crying. That some people's grief comes out as liturgy. That she had turned the unbearable into a procedure so she could survive performing it, and that the procedure had one missing step, one line left unsaid, and she has been waiting for that line the way you wait for a train that is very, very late but has not been cancelled. And now—" His voice did the thing again, the fracture, the man forgetting to perform. "Now they're telling me it's not late anymore. Now they're telling me it's not coming."

Quentin set down his glass. "I'm working on the translation. Brandt and I are close. There's a question of—"

"I know about the question. The two words." Daniel waved it away with a hand that trembled slightly. "I've known about the two words since I was a boy. My grandmother taught them to me herself, without knowing she was teaching me. She'd use one for the neighbors who informed, and one for the priest who looked away, and she never used either one for Aldous Verroney. That was the thing I couldn't understand as a child. She had a word for everyone. Everyone except him. She kept him in the silence, and I thought the silence was hatred, and it took me until I was a grown man to understand it was the opposite. You don't keep a person in the silence unless you can't stop thinking about them."

He stood, abruptly, and went to the window, and stood looking out at the rain with his back to Quentin, and Quentin watched him and felt something turn over in his chest—the recognition, unwelcome and total, of his own grief standing in another man's body. This was what he had been for thirty years: a person keeping a wound in the silence because the silence was the only shape the love could take. Toby, drowned at eleven, and Quentin's tongue turned to chalk, and no one ever said the name at dinner again, and the family called it moving on, and it was liturgy, it was procedure, it was a sentence left unfinished for a lifetime.

"Daniel," he said. "When my brother died, I stopped tasting food. Same day. Doctors never found a cause."

Daniel turned. The rain-light made him look older, and also younger, stripped of the careful gauntness he wore like armor.

"My mother started setting a place for him at the table. For years. My father would take the plate away after she went to bed, and wash it, and put it back in the cupboard, and the next night she'd set it again, and neither of them ever said a word about it. That was our house. Everything important happened in silence and was called something else." Quentin heard his own voice as if from a distance, steady, factual, and underneath it something moving that he had no intention of examining yet. "So I understand the procedure. And I understand what it costs to be the one waiting for someone else to finish a sentence."

"Then you know why I can't be polite anymore." Daniel came back to the chair but did not sit. He stood over it, gripping its back, and the courtesy was gone from his face entirely now, and what remained was rawer and more honest and much harder to look at. "I have spent my whole life being reasonable about this. Reasonable, patient, grateful for small concessions. And I am telling you that if she dies with the sentence unread, I will not survive it the way she survived the camps. She survived because there was a step left. There was one thing still owed to her, one line still to be read, and a person can live on that. I have watched her live on it for sixty years like a plant growing toward a window. And if the window goes dark—" He stopped. His knuckles were white on the chair. "I'm not threatening you. I need you to hear that. I'm begging you. I don't know how to do it gracefully, so I'm just doing it. I'm begging."

* * *

After Daniel left, Quentin sat for a long time in the dark apartment with the whiskey he couldn't taste and the notebook open on the table, and he did not read it. He looked at it the way you look at a locked door when you have finally decided to open it and are gathering the strength for the handle.

The sentence waited at the bottom of the last written page, mid-stroke, abandoned in 1946 or 1947 by a man who could not finish it either. Aldous had left it hanging. Daniel's grandmother had waited for it. And Quentin understood, sitting in the dark with the rain against the glass, that the sentence had never been a confession to be delivered. It was a debt, and debts of this kind do not expire; they only change hands, passing from the debtor to the debtor's children, accruing interest in silence, until someone in the line of the owing stands up in the line of the owed and says the words out loud.

His telephone lit on the table. Ilse Brandt.

"I've been thinking about the verb," she said, without greeting. She always began mid-thought; it was one of the things he had come to trust about her. "The one in the final sentence. I told you there were two words for betrayal in this dialect. I've been through the valley grammar again, and the parish records you photographed, and I want to say something before you decide, because I think you're about to decide too fast. Everyone does, with testimony. They want it to mean one thing. But Aldous wrote the way accountants write, Quentin. Every word chosen because the other word was worse. You need to hear the old woman's voice before you choose. There's a recording—Daniel mentioned his family made tapes of her testimony in the nineties. Do you understand? You may not have to choose at all. She may have already chosen for him."

Quentin closed his eyes. "Ilse. She's dying. Days."

A silence on the line, and then, quietly, the scholar's voice giving way to something plainer: "Then you have your answer about which word he used. The one that leaves the door open. He wrote it to be finished, Quentin. Testimony like this—written mid-sentence, kept in a tin, carried across a border of silence for sixty years—it was never written to be read. It was written to be *completed*. By someone else. In someone else's voice. That's what the form is telling you."

After she hung up, Quentin sat with the notebook and read the final line again, the sentence that broke off mid-word, and this time he read it aloud, alone, in the dark, in his own voice, in English, haltingly, feeling for the shape of it the way a tongue feels for a tooth that is no longer there.

His voice held until the third word. Then it did what it always did—the failure, the closing, the chalk—and he sat in the silence after it, listening to the rain, and understood that the clock was no longer metaphorical. Somewhere across the city, in a ward that smelled of lavender and antiseptic, an old woman was growing quiet the way tide goes out, and the only thing that could reach her now was a boy's voice, saying an old man's words, in a language that had nearly died with everyone who spoke it.

He picked up the telephone and called Daniel.

"I need the tapes," Quentin said. "Her testimony. All of them. Tonight, if you can."

There was a pause, and then Daniel's voice came back stripped of everything—courtesy, caution, the last scaffolding of the reasonable man—leaving only the bare beam underneath.

"I'll drive them to you now. Quentin—" The pause again, longer. "Thank you isn't the right word. There isn't a right word. There never has been. That's rather the whole problem, isn't it?"

Quentin looked at the notebook, at the sentence waiting at the bottom of the page like a held breath.

"No," he said. "But there's a right sentence. And I'm going to find it."
