## 38. First Taste

The trattoria was on a side street in Soho that smelled of drains and garlic in equal measure, the kind of place where the menu was laminated and the wine came in glasses with the restaurant's name printed slightly crooked. Quentin had passed it perhaps two hundred times in the years he'd lived within walking distance, and had never once gone in, because reviewing it would have been pointless—there was nothing there to say anything about—and because eating had long since stopped being something he did for pleasure. It was maintenance. Fueling. A chore performed three times a day with the enthusiasm most men brought to flossing.

He went in now because it was raining, because he had been walking for two hours without deciding to walk, and because the word *TRATTORIA* over the door promised nothing, and he found himself lately drawn to things that promised nothing.

The room was narrow and warm. Six tables, red-checked cloths, a television mounted above the till playing football with the sound off. A woman of about sixty ran the floor alone; she moved with the unhurried authority of someone who had been doing this so long that the work had become a form of breathing. She looked at him the way such women look at all solitary men—not with curiosity, not with pity, just with an efficient assessment of how much bread to bring.

"Table for one?"

"For one."

She seated him against the wall beneath a framed photograph of some coastal town he didn't recognize, handed him a menu he didn't open, and set down a small plate, a napkin rolled around cutlery, and a bottle of olive oil and a bottle of vinegar standing together like an old married couple.

"Bread's coming," she said. "You want the menu or shall I tell you what we've got?"

"Tell me."

"Pasta e fagioli. Rigatoni with sausage and broccoli rabe. Sea bream if you trust me, which you shouldn't, it came in yesterday." She said it without smiling, daring him to object.

"The soup," he said. "And whatever else you'd give someone who isn't going to complain."

That earned him half a smile. "Everyone complains. That's what they come for." And she went off toward the kitchen without writing anything down.

Quentin sat with his hands flat on the tablecloth and watched the rain run down the window beside him. Three days ago he had read the sentence aloud at a bedside in a hospital ward that smelled of lilies and disinfectant, and an old woman named Lucia Ferro had opened her eyes, looked at him—or through him, at the boy his father had been, at the winter of 1944, at sixty years of waiting compressed into a single syllable—and said, *Finally.* Then she had closed her eyes again and slept, and two days later she had died in her sleep, quietly, between one breath and the next, as if the sentence had been the last thing holding her upright and once it was spoken she could finally put the weight down.

Daniel had called him from the corridor outside her room. He hadn't said *she's gone* or any of the phrases people use. He had said: "It's done. Thank you." And then, after a silence long enough that Quentin had checked whether the call had dropped: "I don't know what I'm supposed to do now. I've been doing this my whole life. The waiting. I don't know who I am without it."

"You're free," Quentin had said, and heard how inadequate it sounded, and Daniel had laughed—a short, startled sound, almost a cough—and said, "Yes. God help us both," and hung up.

Since then Quentin had slept badly, eaten worse, and walked the city in long undirected loops like a dog looking for a gate left open. Margot had sent a single text—*I heard. Are you all right?*—and he had answered *No* and then *Yes* and then nothing, and she had let it stand, which was its own kind of mercy, the first uncomplicated thing she had given him in forty years.

The bread arrived. It was still warm, which surprised him; he had expected the bread of places like this to be an afterthought, but the woman set down four thick slices with a crust that crackled faintly as it cooled, and poured oil into the shallow dish beside them—oil the color of hay, green-gold at the edges where the light caught it.

"Eat before it goes cold," she said, and disappeared.

He tore a piece of bread. Dipped it. Brought it to his mouth.

For twenty-two years, this moment had been the same. The mechanics of chewing, the texture registering somewhere below thought—the grit of crust, the collapse of crumb—and then nothing. A blank where flavor should have been, the way a missing tooth leaves a gap the tongue keeps returning to. He had learned to eat anyway, the way an amputee learns to walk, compensating, improvising, building a life around the absence until the absence became architecture. He had built a whole career on the absence, God help him. Twenty years of describing flavors he could only reconstruct, a forensic scientist of other people's pleasure, testifying about a crime scene he couldn't enter.

The bread touched his tongue.

And the world tilted.

It was the oil first—that was what he would remember afterward, trying to explain it to Ilse Brandt weeks later on a phone call neither of them wanted to end. Not the bread, not the salt, but the oil: a green bitterness at the back of the throat, peppery, alive, followed by something round and golden that he had no vocabulary for because he had spent twenty years borrowing everyone else's. It wasn't a flavor arriving. It was a country reopening its borders. It was light coming into a house that had been shuttered so long the family had rearranged their lives around the dark.

Quentin put the bread down. His hand was shaking.

He picked it up again and ate it, slowly, deliberately, the way you might touch a scar to confirm it's real. Salt next—he asked the woman for salt, though the bread needed none, and she brought it without comment, and he touched a wet fingertip to it and pressed it to his tongue and the taste bloomed sharp and mineral and ancient, the oldest taste there is, the taste of blood and sea and tears, and something in his chest cracked along a line he hadn't known was there.

Because grief arrived with it. That was the thing no one had warned him about, or rather, the thing one doctor had half-promised, years ago, in a beige office in Bloomsbury, tapping his pen against the referral letter: *"Sometimes, Mr. Amberdale, when the underlying condition resolves—when whatever the mind has been protecting itself against is finally faced—the senses return. Though I should say"—and here the doctor had looked at him over his glasses with a clinical kindness that Quentin had resented for two decades—"that what comes back may not be pleasant. Taste is memory's closest neighbor. If the memory is grief, the taste will be grief."*

He had walked out of that office thinking: *Then I'll never taste anything, because I will never face it.* And he had kept that promise for twenty-two years, right up until a gaunt man with a card and a sentence had stood in his mother's living room and called him by a name he didn't know he owned.

Now the grief came up through the flavor like water rising through floorboards. Every taste was doubled: the oil and the winters his father never spoke of; the salt and Toby's name, which no one in the family had said aloud since the funeral except Margot, once, drunk, at Christmas, before catching herself like a woman stepping back from a ledge. The food tasted of everything it had cost. He sat in the cheap trattoria with the football flickering silently above the till and wept into his plate.

Not elegantly. Not the way he imagined weeping ought to look—composed, literary, a single tear acceptable to witnesses. He sobbed, shoulders hitching, into the pasta e fagioli when it came, into the steam rising off it, into the beans and the rosemary and the thread of tomato, each spoonful another blow of sweetness and salt and sorrow, and the woman behind the counter saw him and did nothing, which was the greatest kindness available to him just then. She refilled his water glass. She turned the television down a notch, though it was already silent. She let a grown man grieve alone at table six under a photograph of a town on the Ligurian coast, and when he finally surfaced, wiping his face with the rolled napkin, she brought him more bread without being asked and said only:

"It's good, isn't it? My mother's recipe. From before."

"From before what?" His voice was wrecked.

"Before we came here. Before everything." She shrugged, the shrug of a woman whose family history was too large for a Tuesday evening. "Everything tastes better from before. That's not nostalgia. That's just true."

He ate the whole bowl. He ate the second helping she insisted on. He drank a glass of the crooked-labeled wine and tasted it—actually tasted it, dark cherry and dust and something tannic that gripped the inside of his cheeks—and he laughed out loud at the sheer outrageousness of it, at the fact that this had been available to him all along, locked behind a door he himself had bricked, in a cellar he himself had refused to enter, and the laugh came out wet and broken and turned into more crying, and the woman brought him a coffee he hadn't ordered and told him, "On the house. You've had a day."

"I've had a life," he said.

"Haven't we all," she said, and took away the plates.

* * *

He walked home through the rain without hurrying. The city was soft-edged and shining, and he kept stopping—at shop windows, at the mouth of alleys, under awnings—to catalog what he could smell, because smell had come back with taste, riding in like a passenger. Wet stone. Diesel. The yeast-warm exhale of a bakery preparing tomorrow's dough. A florist's bucket of eucalyptus, sharp enough to sting. For twenty-two years these had been wallpaper. Now they were sentences, and he stood reading them one by one like a man released from prison touching the letters on street signs.

His apartment, when he reached it, looked different. Smaller, actually—the rooms he had furnished with such careful neutrality, the beige competence of a man staging a life rather than living one. He had always told himself the minimalism was discipline. Standing in the doorway with rain in his hair and the taste of rosemary still ghosting his palate, he recognized it for what it was: a rented room. A tenant's furnishings. Nothing chosen, everything provisional, because a man who cannot taste cannot commit to a color.

He thought about the phrase Daniel had used in the office, months ago, in that terrible gentle voice: *Money is how the Verroneys stayed owners instead of tenants.* Quentin had flinched at it then. Now, drying his hair with a towel in his own hallway, he understood it differently. His father had bought ownership with a betrayal and paid for it in silence for the rest of his life. Margot had maintained the property with anonymous checks and iron discretion. And Quentin—Quentin had been a tenant in his own inheritance, subletting a life his father's guilt had furnished, never once asking what the deed actually said.

No longer. The translation was finished—Ilse had signed off on the last pages ten days ago, and the Ferros' consent had come through Daniel in a single email containing no punctuation except a period, which Quentin had come to understand was how Daniel said *thank you*. The book would exist. Under his byline. Under both his names. Whatever came of it—Margot's lawyers, the journalists already circling, the literary world's appetite for scandal dressed as conscience—it would exist, and it would be true, and truth, he was learning, tastes like salt: necessary, corrosive, the beginning of every broth.

He made himself tea, mostly as an experiment, and stood at the kitchen window drinking it while the rain thinned to mist. Darjeeling. He could taste the Darjeeling—the muscatel note he had read about a thousand times in tasting notes written by men he'd secretly envied, the floral top note, the astringent finish that dried the mouth and somehow made you want more. He laughed again, alone, at the window, a mad quiet laugh, and let the tea go cold in his hand because he couldn't stop tasting it even as it cooled, the flavor changing as it died, sweetening strangely at the last, the way stories do.

Toby would have liked this, he thought. Toby, who at nine had eaten a raw chili on a dare and cried and then eaten another one. Toby, who drowned in a river in July while Quentin, fourteen and responsible, was supposed to be watching. The original wound. The one under all the others. He had spent twenty-two years believing his tongue had died with his brother, that God or his own mind had amputated flavor as punishment, and the punishment had seemed fitting, even right: a critic who could not taste, a brother who could not save. But it hadn't been punishment. It had been anesthesia. His mind had numbed the limb before the amputation, and the amputation had never actually happened—all those years, the tongue had been there, waiting, packed in ice, faithful as a dog.

What had woken it wasn't courage. He wanted to be honest about that, at least with himself. It wasn't the reading, or the redemption, or any of the words the journalists would reach for. It was the ledger. Sitting in Ilse Brandt's office in Cambridge with the notebook open between them, reading his father's account of March 1945 in a dialect that sounded like water over stones, Quentin had felt something shift in his chest—a door unsealing—and by evening he could taste the coffee in Ilse's cups, appallingly, overwhelmingly, and had gripped the arm of his chair while she watched him with the careful face of a woman who has seen students weep over documents before. Grief had entered through the flavor, exactly as the beige-office doctor promised. Or flavor had entered through the grief. By then the two were indistinguishable, and he suspected they always had been, for everyone, and that the rest of the world simply hadn't noticed, the way you don't notice breathing until it stops.

He washed his cup. Dried it. Put it away. Small domestic liturgies, each one now accompanied by sensation—the lemon scent of the washing-up liquid nearly knocking him sideways, absurdly moving, a smell he would remember for the rest of his life and be unable to explain to anyone why.

Before bed, he opened the notebook one more time. It lived on his desk now, under glass Margot didn't know about yet, though she would soon enough. He didn't read the ledger entry. He had read it enough for one lifetime; it would keep; it was patient the way the dead are patient. Instead he turned to the last page, where his father's handwriting stopped mid-sentence—the sentence, the one on the card, the one Lucia Ferro had waited sixty years to hear—and he looked at the final unfinished line the way you look at a parent's face in an old photograph, searching it for permission.

Tomorrow he would meet Daniel at the solicitor's office to sign the papers transferring the notebook's custody. Tomorrow Margot's lawyer would send whatever letter Margot's lawyer sends. Tomorrow the world would begin deciding what Aldous Verroney Amberdale had been—monster, victim, child, all three—and Quentin would have no power to stop it and no wish to.

But tonight, tonight he had tasted bread and salt and oil and grief, and they had all been the same thing, and he had wept in public and been left alone, and somewhere across the city Daniel Ferro was spending his first night in sixty years' worth of inherited waiting without anything left to wait for, and Quentin raised the cold cup of tea to the dark window, to the reflection of a man with rain-damp hair and red eyes, and said quietly, in a language he was only beginning to learn:

"To Matteo. To Lucia. To the boy in the ledger, who was fourteen." A pause. "To Dad—who used the wrong word, and knew it, and wrote it down anyway."

He drank the cold tea. It tasted of tannin and endings and, faintly, unbelievably, of morning.

His phone buzzed on the desk. A message from an unknown number, and beneath the number, a photograph: a headstone in a village churchyard, weathered granite, the old inscription still legible—ALDOUS AMBERDALE, BELOVED FATHER—and someone had already begun chiseling beneath it. Fresh cuts in the stone. Letters taking shape.

The message had no text. Only the photograph, and after a moment, a second one: a close-up of the new carving, half-finished, the mason's tools still resting against the granite in the frame.

Someone had gotten there first.
