## 3. The Name Nobody Uses

Her aunt found her on the third morning, kneeling in the hallway with a radiator key in one hand and a rag going brown in the other.

"You've been asking questions," Marta said.

Lior didn't stand. She kept her thumb against the bleed valve, feeling the small tremor of air and water negotiating their old argument. "I've been listening."

"That's worse." Marta's shadow fell across the radiator, across the brass fittings Lior had polished the day before, across the faint watermark on the wallpaper where a picture had hung for fifty years and been taken down only last week by an estate lawyer with cotton gloves. "Your grandmother paid for this house to be quiet. Do you understand what I'm telling you? She *paid* for it."

The valve gave its hiss — that same unfinished-sentence hiss Lior had catalogued her first night back, the sound like a woman drawing breath to say something and then deciding, again, against it. Water came then, clear at first, then rust-bright, and with it — Lior would swear to this until she died — a smell of coal smoke and platform grit, of a winter train station forty years gone.

"Sit down, Marta."

"I will not sit down in my mother's house while you—"

"Then stand," Lior said, "and tell me who Emrys is."

The silence that followed was not the family's usual silence. Their silence was upholstered, deliberate, a thing maintained like the good furniture — dusted, covered, never used. This was different. This was the silence of a dam being asked, politely, to hold.

Marta sat. Not on the chair but on the edge of it, perched, as if the truth might be over quickly if she didn't commit her full weight.

"He had a brother," she said finally. "Your grandmother. Before any of us, before your mother even. A brother named—" and here her mouth did something complicated, a door opening onto a room that had been bricked over so long the bricks had grown into the frame. "He had a name we don't use."

"The one Emrys said. At the graveside."

"At the graveside." Marta laughed, or made the shape of a laugh without putting anything in it. "In front of the minister. In front of the cousins from Toronto."

"Tell me."

And Marta told her, in the voice people use for things they learned secondhand and have carried so long they've forgotten they weren't there: that in the darkest year — the year Lior's history lessons had covered in gray blocks and euphemism, the year the family did not discuss because discussing required words and words required breath and breath was not guaranteed — the brother had been given a choice. Everyone was given a choice then; that was the obscenity of it, that it was framed as a choice. Sign the paper, take the new name, keep the shop, keep the street address, keep your life. Or refuse, and lose all of it, and go.

"He signed nothing," Marta said. "He stood up in a room full of men who were signing, and he said his own name out loud. His real one. And then he walked to the station and took the last train east, and my mother watched him go from behind a pillar because she wasn't allowed to walk him there. She was fourteen." Marta's hands were folded now, white-knuckled, a posture Lior recognized from the funeral, from every family gathering of her childhood — the posture of a woman holding a lid down from the inside. "She never spoke his name again. Not once. Sixty years. When he died — over there, years later, married, a son — she sent nothing. When the letters came, she burned them unread. I know because I burned some of them for her, near the end, when her hands shook too badly to hold the matches."

"She read them first?"

"Some of them." Marta looked at the radiator as if it had spoken. "I think she read all of them. I think that was the punishment she chose for herself. To read every word and answer none."

* * *

Emrys had taken a room at the Anchor Inn because it was the only place in town with a ground-floor bed, and when Lior came to him that evening he was propped against pillows with his coat still on, as though he expected to be thrown out at any moment and wanted to be dressed for it.

"You're the piano tuner," he said. "The granddaughter."

"I tune houses, mostly. Pianos when they ask."

Something moved through his face — amusement trying to find purchase on exhaustion and failing. He was thinner than he'd seemed at the graveside, or perhaps grief had simply finished its work early. There was a hospital bracelet under his left sleeve. He saw her notice and pulled the cuff down with the unhurried dignity of a man who has stopped pretending.

"They gave me four months in March," he said. "March was optimistic. My father died believing his sister would write. One letter. One word. Even a word of anger — he'd have taken anger. Anger is a kind of claiming." He turned his head toward the window, where the town's few lights were coming on in the rain. "I came here to do what he couldn't. To stand in front of her house and say the name and see if anyone answered."

"She's dead three weeks."

"I know when she's dead." Gently. "I've known since the obituary appeared under the other name. The traded one. I clipped it. I carry it in my wallet like a ticket." He did take out the wallet then, and unfolded a newsprint rectangle soft as cloth from handling, and Lior saw her grandmother's face, younger than any photograph in the parlor, smiling under a name that had been purchased like a coat to hide the old clothes beneath.

"What do you want?" Lior asked. "From us. From the estate. Money? There's money, there'll be—"

"Stop." He said it kindly, but it landed like a hand on a struck bell. "I don't want money. Money is how you pay strangers. I want—" He searched, and Lior waited, and outside the rain thickened against the glass. "When my father was dying he talked about her constantly. Not bitterly. He talked about a girl who could whistle two notes at once, who hid plums in her pockets, who once — this was his favorite story — once stood between him and their father's belt and dared him to strike through her. He wanted to know if she remembered being that girl. If anything of her survived the trade. And I thought: someone has to go and find out. Someone has to knock on the door of the silence and ask it, politely, to open."

"And no one answered."

"No one answered." He closed his eyes. "So now I'm asking you. Not for the name to be restored — I know better than to ask a house to unburn its papers. Just — acknowledged. Once. By blood. That he existed. That she knew he existed. That the silence was a choice and not an erasure so complete even the stones forgot."

Lior sat with that. She was good at sitting with things; it was her whole profession, sitting with instruments until they trusted her enough to show what was wrong.

"There are letters," she said. "Unburned ones. I found them Tuesday, inside the parlor radiator casing. Wrapped in oilcloth. She hid them in the heating system, Emrys. The one place in the house that speaks."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he began, very quietly, to cry — not the crying of grief exactly, or not only that, but the crying of a man hearing, after a lifetime of static, a signal he'd begun to believe was never transmitted at all.

* * *

Marta burned the rest on Thursday.

Lior came downstairs to the smell of smoke that wasn't the boiler's and found her aunt in the yard behind the kitchen, feeding pages into a galvanized bucket, one at a time, with the terrible ceremony of the devout. The wind took the ash sideways into the hedge.

"You went to him." Marta didn't turn around. "You went to him and you showed him—"

"He already knew about the letters. He's known about them longer than either of us has been alive."

"He gets *nothing*." Now she turned, and her face was her mother's face, the same jaw set the same way, and Lior understood suddenly that this was inheritance too — that the silence had bred in the family like a trait, passing down through the women like eye color. "Do you know what that name cost us? What it cost *her*, carrying it alone, refusing it alone, so the rest of us could grow up safe and stupid and unmarked? And now you want to dig it up and wave it around because a dying man makes you sad—"

"Because he's *family*," Lior said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, and hated it, and let it. "Because he knocked on the door of the silence, Marta, and everyone in this family has spent sixty years pretending the door isn't there."

Behind them, inside the house, something went off like a rifle.

They ran in together. Water sheeted down the parlor wall; the great cast-iron radiator under the window had split along its seam, a black gash vomiting steam and scald, and the pressure of it drove them both back into the hall coughing. The wrapped letters — the oilcloth bundle Lior had left on the windowsill — were soaked, ink blooming gray-blue across the envelopes like bruises surfacing.

Marta stood in the doorway, dripping, watching sixty years of correspondence drown on the sill, and something in her went out — not surrender, Lior thought, but exhaustion finally reaching the end of its fuel.

"I can't hold it anymore," Marta said, very quietly. "I never could. It was always her holding it. I just helped."

The radiator hissed, guttered, and went quiet — quieter than it had ever been, quiet as a held breath.

Lior looked at the soaked letters, at the burst iron, at her aunt's ruined face, and understood with a clarity that felt less like thought than like temperature: the house was done waiting. The goodbye her grandmother never spoke, the goodbye Emrys had crossed a sea to collect — it was not going to come from the dead, and it was not going to come from Marta, whose voice had been conscripted into the silence so long it had forgotten its own register.

It would have to come from her. The tuner. The listener. The only one left willing to hear.

That night she laid the letters out on towels to dry, one by one, and read the first line of the topmost page, in faded ink, in a young man's careful hand:

*Sister — if you are reading this, then somewhere in that house of yours, something finally leaked.*
