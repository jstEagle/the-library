## 6. The Auctioneer's Terms

The card had been waiting for Coral at the library's front desk for two days before she opened it. It sat in the interoffice mail tray like something that had died there — heavy cream stock, no stamp, hand-delivered, her name letterpressed in ink the color of dried violets. *Coral Crane. Cataloger. Night Division.* No title of address, which she found herself reading as either respect or insult, depending on the hour.

She opened it at 2:47 a.m. on a Tuesday, standing between the 800s and the 900s with a cart of misshelved biographies at her hip.

*Ms. Crane — Vesper Hale requests the pleasure of your consultation regarding the Marlowe estate. Thursday, eleven o'clock. The Hale Rooms, 19 Corvus Street. Compensation commensurate with your discretion.*

The last sentence was the one she read four times. Not the money — she'd barely registered the money — but *discretion*, sitting there in the middle of a business card like a stone in a shoe. Discretion from whom? About what? The house was public record now. The auction notices had gone up on the lampposts along Delancey like weather warnings. There was nothing left in the Marlowe house to be discreet about except the things Coral herself had carried out of it, and she had carried out exactly one thing, wrapped in a scarf, hidden in the bottom drawer of her desk behind a false panel she'd built at twenty-three out of spite and carpentry classes.

*Compensation commensurate with your discretion.* As if the card knew.

She put it back in the envelope. She shelved the biographies. She thought about how the fluorescent light at that end of the 900s made everyone's hands look like they belonged to someone older, and she looked at her own hands for a long time, turning them over, checking for signs of the person she was about to become.

* * *

The Hale Rooms occupied the ground floor of a former bank on Corvus Street, and they had kept the vault. Coral knew this because Vesper Hale told her, twice, in the first five minutes, in the tone of a woman who had learned that the vault was the single most effective thing she could say to a certain kind of person. The marble floors had been polished back to their 1920s arrogance. The teller cages were gone, replaced by glass cases, and inside the cases were objects arranged with the loving precision of a reliquary: a fountain pen that had belonged to a dead poet, its nib worn to one side by the pressure of a hand Coral could almost see; a child's shoe, single, bronzed; a stack of unsent letters tied with grosgrain ribbon, the envelopes yellowed to the color of weak tea.

Coral stood in the doorway a moment too long, and Vesper noticed. Vesper noticed everything; you could feel it happening, a faint pressure against the skin, like standing near a radio antenna.

"You're looking at the letters," Vesper said. She was smaller than Coral had expected, and older — silver at the temples, cut short, a face that had been handsome in a sharp way and had decided, somewhere in her fifties, to stop softening it. She wore charcoal gray and a watch that probably cost more than Coral's annual salary and had the specific unostentatiousness of money that had stopped needing to prove anything. "Everyone looks at the letters. The pen people pretend to look at the pen. The shoe is a litmus test — if someone's eyes go to the shoe first, I know they're a sentimentalist, and I adjust my prices accordingly."

"Which am I?"

Vesper smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, warm at exactly the temperature required. "You looked at the letters. But you looked at the vault door first. That's a cataloger. You're inventorying my inventory." She turned, heels clicking a rhythm on the marble that the room seemed to have been built to accommodate. "Come. Coffee? I have coffee that will ruin you for the library's machine. I've heard about the library's machine."

"I like the library's machine."

"I know," Vesper said, without turning around. "That's why I said it."

* * *

The consultation happened at a table that had clearly been a banker's desk in a former life, oak the color of dark honey, big enough to land a plane on. Vesper spread the auction catalog's working draft across it — loose pages, some still in proof form, photographs of the Marlowe house's contents arranged in the order Vesper had decided they would die: furniture first, then the smaller domestic things, then the paper.

Coral had cataloged perhaps four hundred of those objects herself over the past two weeks. She had written descriptions in the flat, faithful voice her profession demanded — *walnut writing desk, c. 1968, water damage to lower left drawer; set of twelve dinner plates, transferware, two chipped* — and she had done it the way she did everything, which was thoroughly, and with a grief she kept in a separate room and visited only after hours. Seeing her own descriptions now, typeset, with estimated values beside them, was like seeing her childhood wearing a price tag. The desk where her mother had written grocery lists and, once, a letter — *writing desk, c. 1968, water damage* — was estimated at four hundred dollars. Coral looked at that number for a long time. Four hundred dollars. The desk had held her mother's elbows. It had held the weight of her mother leaning on it to look out the window at two children playing a game in the street that no one else could see was a game.

"You've done remarkable work," Vesper said. She said it the way she did everything — precisely, and with an implication folded inside like a letter inside an envelope. "The provenance notes especially. Most catalogers write *provenance: estate of*. You've written *provenance: purchased by Eleanor Crane, 1971, from the Delancey hardware store, receipt retained*. Do you know what that does to a lot's value?"

"It makes it honest."

"It makes it *expensive*," Vesper said, and laughed — a real laugh, Coral was surprised to note, low and unguarded, there and gone like a bird. "Honesty is the most valuable thing in this room, Ms. Crane. Everything else is furniture." She slid a page across the desk. It was a contract, or the skeleton of one, with figures in it that made Coral's eyes do the thing eyes do when they encounter numbers with too many digits — a little stutter, a refusal, a second look. "The estate's executors have engaged me to authenticate the literary materials. Letters, manuscripts, marginalia, anything in the house with a hand on it. The appraisers can value a desk. They cannot value a *sentence*. That requires someone who understands what sentences are worth, and to whom."

"I'm a cataloger, not an authenticator."

"You're both, and you know it, and the distinction is a courtesy we extend to people who need to feel like they have a profession rather than a gift." Vesper folded her hands. Her rings were plain gold, old, the kind of jewelry that had been worn by someone else first. "I want you to authenticate the Marlowe papers. All of them. At your own pace, in your own manner, with your name printed in the catalog as consulting cataloger — which, I'll note, will do more for your career than ten years of nights in the stacks. And in exchange—" she tapped the figure on the page, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal "—this."

Coral didn't look at the number again. She looked at Vesper's hands instead, at the way they rested on the contract without claiming it, and thought: *she has done this before. Many times. She knows exactly how long to let the number sit there before speaking, and she knows that I am currently not breathing correctly, and she is letting me not breathe because the not-breathing is part of the sale.*

"What papers, specifically?"

"Correspondence. Household documents. Anything of literary or historical interest." A pause, small as a coin slipping into a pocket. "And the letter."

The word landed in the room and sat there.

Coral kept her face the way she kept it at 3 a.m. when the man who came in every Thursday to read the same book about lighthouses fell asleep at the same table — neutral, attentive, a face that gave nothing back. Ten years of loving Felix had trained her for this. You learned to hold a sentence behind your teeth without letting it show in your jaw.

"What letter?" she said.

Vesper's eyes did something Coral had never quite seen before — they warmed and sharpened at the same time, like a lens finding focus. "The letter found behind the baseboard in the upstairs bedroom. Dated — as far as the preliminary assessment goes — some twenty years ago. Partially composed in a private or constructed script. The executors are calling it an anomaly. I'm calling it the centerpiece." She leaned back. "You didn't know I knew. I can see that. Which means you found it, and you told no one, and you've been carrying it around in your head for — how long? Two weeks? Three?" She didn't wait for an answer. "I don't care, Ms. Crane. I want to be clear about that. I'm not offended. I'm not suspicious. I'm *delighted*. It means the letter matters to someone, and things that matter to someone are the only things I can sell."

The vault door was visible from where Coral sat, through the interior glass, its great wheel handle dull as a moon. She thought about the scarf-wrapped bundle in her desk drawer, forty blocks away, and felt the specific vertigo of being seen through a wall.

"It's my mother's handwriting," Coral said. It came out quieter than she intended. She hadn't planned to say it at all.

"Yes," Vesper said, and here her voice changed — softened, or put on softness, Coral couldn't tell, and the not-being-able-to-tell was itself a kind of alarm. "I know. Eleanor Crane. I did my research; I always do. I'm sorry for your loss — I know it was some years ago now, which makes the sympathy complicated, so let me say it differently: the house is full of her, isn't it. I've walked it. She's in the wallpaper choices. She's in the way the garden was allowed to go a little wild in the back, which I read as a woman who ran out of time for tidiness but couldn't bring herself to cut things down." She paused. "The letter is hers. The script is, I believe, a children's invented language — there's precedent in the literature, twin languages, sibling languages, and yours is documented in the neighborhood, if you can call two children a literature. The executors want it authenticated. The buyers want it *storied*. A letter in a secret language, written by a woman the neighborhood remembers, found hidden in a wall — Ms. Crane, do you know what that letter is worth?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the beauty of it. It's worth whatever the two loneliest people in the bidding room decide it's worth, and the lonelier they are, the higher it goes." Vesper said this without a flicker of irony, without a flicker of anything, and Coral understood that she was in the presence of a person who had made a complete philosophy out of this and lived inside it comfortably, the way some people lived inside religion.

"And if I authenticate it, it gets sold. To a stranger. To someone who will put it in a glass case like—" she gestured at the teller cages, the bronzed shoe, the unsent letters "—like that."

"Like that," Vesper agreed. "Or in a drawer. Or in a fire, eventually, because most things end in fire or landfill and the ones that end in glass cases are the lucky ones." She turned a page of the contract toward Coral. "But that's the transaction. Here's the part I suspect you actually care about." She tapped a clause near the bottom. "The consulting cataloger retains the right of first annotation. Whatever you determine about the letter's contents, authorship, and meaning goes into the catalog under your name, in your words, unedited. You can't stop the sale. But you can *frame* it. You can make sure that whatever the world knows about that letter, it knows it because you told them." She sat back. "Ownership is one kind of love, Ms. Crane. But it's not the only one. Some people love by keeping. Some people love by *telling*. I've built a life on the assumption that the second kind will settle for the first if the price is right, and I have rarely been wrong."

The coffee arrived — a silent assistant, a tray, cups with no saucer-rattle — and Coral drank hers because her hands needed something to betray besides her voice. It was, as promised, ruinous. She hated it a little.

"I'll need to see the letter again," Coral said. "To authenticate it properly. The original."

"Of course."

"It's in the executors' custody?"

"It's in my custody as of this morning," Vesper said. "The executors signed the transfer at nine. It's in the vault." She let that sit. The vault. The great wheel handle. The room where things went to be kept, which was a kind of love, which was a kind of death, which was both at once and Vesper Hale had never seen a reason to distinguish them.

Coral set down her cup. The porcelain made a small, definite sound against the oak, and in the quiet of the former bank it rang like a decision.

"I'll take the work," she said. "The authentication. The annotation clause. But I have a condition of my own."

Vesper's eyebrows rose a millimeter — genuine interest, or the performance of it, and Coral was beginning to understand that with this woman the distinction might not exist. "Go on."

"Before it's cataloged. Before it's photographed. Before it's *storied* — I want time with it. Alone. To translate it. Fully." Coral heard her own voice say the word *fully* and felt the floor of the sentence open under it, because *fully* was a promise she didn't yet know how to keep, and both of them knew the script in that letter was one only two living people could read, and only one of them was sitting at this table.

Vesper studied her for a moment. Then she smiled — the calibrated smile, warm to exactly the required temperature — and turned the contract around, and slid a pen across the oak, a fountain pen with a nib worn to one side by someone else's hand.

"Time," Vesper said, "is the one thing I always have in stock."

Coral took the pen. It was heavier than it looked. Everything in this room was heavier than it looked, she thought — the marble, the vault, the number on the page, the word *discretion* on a violet-inked card — and she signed her name in ink the color of dried violets, and the pen wrote smoothly, and she thought: *my mother's desk is worth four hundred dollars, and I have just been paid more than that to decide what her last words mean.*

Walking out past the glass cases, she made herself look at the unsent letters, the ones tied with grosgrain ribbon, the ones no one had ever claimed. They were beautiful in the way that held things are beautiful. She did not look at the bronzed shoe.

Outside, Corvus Street was doing its gray noon business, and the 47 bus went by at the corner, its reroute notice pasted to the shelter glass like a scar. Six weeks, it said. Six weeks, and then the neighborhood would have to learn to walk.

Coral stood on the curb a moment longer than she needed to. Then she turned toward the library, toward the night shift, toward the false drawer and the scarf-wrapped bundle that was now, by contract, a copy of something in a vault — and she walked the way she always walked, thoroughly, with a grief kept in a separate room, and the separate room had just gotten one door heavier.
