## 34. Rehearsal

The bus was empty the way a church is empty at three in the morning — not vacant but waiting, holding its breath for something it had been built to receive.

Coral climbed the steps of the 47 at the depot on Halloway Street and felt the familiar give of the worn rubber treads under her boots, the faint diesel-and-cold-metal smell that she had catalogued years ago without meaning to, the way she catalogued everything: *night bus, interior, 11:40 p.m., one driver, two passengers, zero witnesses.* The driver was a woman named Odette who had been driving this route since before Coral was born, or so Felix claimed, and who looked at them now through the wide rearview mirror with the expression of someone who had watched a thousand small dramas play out between her stops and had long ago stopped being surprised by any of them.

"Rehearsal?" Odette said. Just the word. She knew. Of course she knew — the whole route knew by now, the way water knows about a stone dropped into it. News of the auction, of the letter, of what Coral had done in that gilded room full of velvet ropes and numbered paddles, had traveled the neighborhood faster than the bus itself ever had.

"Rehearsal," Coral confirmed.

Odette nodded once and pulled out onto the empty avenue, and the city slid past the windows like a film run at half speed — shuttered bakeries, the laundromat with its single fluorescent light burning blue over folded towels no one would collect until morning, the pawnshop window where a saxophone leaned against a display of costume jewelry like a man waiting for a friend who wasn't coming.

Felix was already sitting in the third row from the back, their row, the row they'd claimed as children on the Saturday trips to the central library when his mother used to hand them each a quarter for the fare and say, *Spend it wisely, spend it together.* He had the letter's copy — his transcription, made from memory, in his own careful hand — folded into quarters in his jacket pocket, and he patted it now the way you'd pat a nervous animal.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. So we do this."

"We do this."

He looked at her across the aisle, and in the sodium light that strobed through the windows at intervals — dark, gold, dark, gold — his face kept disappearing and reappearing, which felt right, which felt like the whole last month condensed into a lighting effect. "From the top," he said. "Like we're actually standing up in front of all those people. Like Vesper Hale is watching us with her little gavel and her little smile."

"She doesn't use a gavel," Coral said. "She uses a bell. A small brass bell. It's worse somehow."

"It's absolutely worse." He unfolded the pages. His hands were steady; she noticed that, and envied it. Her own hands had been unsteady for weeks now, ever since the reading, ever since the room fractured and the magnate withdrew and Vesper's composure broke like ice going out from a riverbank. They had won — if winning could be said to describe what happened when truth detonated in a public place and everyone stood around afterward blinking in the smoke. The auction had collapsed into a community sale. The press had come and gone. Vesper had resigned amid the slow leak of her suppression deals, each one surfacing like a body in a thawing lake. And through all of it, through every interview and every headline and every neighbor stopping Coral outside the diner to shake her hand, there had been this other thing — the unsaid thing, riding along beside her like a passenger who never got off.

*Say it,* she told herself, as she had told herself for ten years. *Tonight. On the bus. Where it belongs.*

Not yet. First the rehearsal. First duty, then truth — the old order, the inherited order, the order she had sworn in chapter after chapter of her own life to break.

"Go ahead," she said aloud. "Read your part."

* * *

Felix read.

He read standing up, because he said he wanted to practice it the way they'd have to do it, and he braced one hand on the grab rail as the bus swayed, and his voice filled the empty vehicle with his mother's words — no, with Coral's mother's words, transcribed through his childhood memory, which meant they arrived in the world carrying both women inside them like nested dolls.

*"I am writing this in our language because paper can be seized but a country of two cannot,"* he read. *"Because what I did, I did in daylight, and daylight has never needed translation. But why I did it — that lives in the language only the children speak, and I am leaving it to them to decide whether it should be spoken again."*

The bus took the long curve past the shuttered textile mill, and the wheels sang against the rails embedded in the street, a sound Coral had heard ten thousand times and had never once thought she'd miss until the reroute notice went up.

*"They will tell you Marlowe stole the money,"* Felix continued, and here his voice changed — flattened, went careful, the way ground goes careful over an old minefield. *"Let them tell it. I know whose signature moved the funds, and I know why I let the story stand. A family of three is easier to save than a reputation, and reputations grow back. Families don't always. I chose the family. I would choose it again. I paid in the currency I had, which was my name, my health, and eventually everything else, and I want you two to know —"* 

He stopped.

Coral looked up. "You're fine. That's the hard part and you got through it."

"That's not the hard part," Felix said quietly. He sat down across from her, the pages loose in his hands. "The hard part is the next line. The line she wrote and then crossed out. You've seen it — the strikethrough, the one she didn't finish translating even for us."

"I've seen it."

"In the original language it says—" and then he said it, the invented words falling from his mouth with the ease of thirty years' fluency, syllables they had built themselves out of playground phonetics and secret grammar, a country of two that had admitted exactly one citizen apiece and no others. And then he translated it, and the translation was: *"What I could not give you, I tried to give each other."*

Silence. The bus hummed. Somewhere near the front, Odette cleared her throat very softly, a librarian's cough, granting them privacy in the only room she controlled.

"She wrote that," Coral said, "and then she stopped. Mid-sentence. The rest of the page is blank except for the date."

"Do you think she couldn't finish it?"

"I think she finished it," Coral said slowly, "and then decided we should have to. I think that was the rule she found in the margins — the one about sentences that can't be taken back. She wrote the sentence that can't be taken back, and then she left the taking-back impossible by leaving the saying to us." She laughed, a small broken sound. "Ten years I've been angry at her for dying. Tonight I'm angry at her for teaching me so well."

"Teaching you what?"

"How to leave the important thing unfinished." She hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way water comes through a crack — finding the weakness, following it down. She looked at the window fast, at the dark street, at anything.

Felix didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was strange — not angry, not gentle, but *attentive*, the voice of a man who has spent a lifetime listening to a language only two people speak and has just heard a new word coined in real time. "Coral."

"Hm?"

"What were you about to say? Before the window."

"Nothing. I was—"

"You were about to say something true." He said it without accusation, which was somehow more devastating than accusation. "I've been noticing. For weeks. Every time we get close to it — the thing that isn't the letter, isn't the auction, isn't any of this — you get this look, like you're standing at a door, and then the phone rings, or the bus stops, or you remember some paperwork, and the door closes. I used to think it was me. That I was the thing behind the door and you were keeping it shut on purpose." He turned the pages over in his hands, not reading them. "But I watched you in that auction room. I watched you open a door in front of two hundred people that you'd kept shut for twenty-five years. So it's not that you can't open doors. It's that there's one door you still won't."

The bus stopped for a red light on an empty street. Nobody ran the light at midnight on Halloway Avenue; there was no one to run it for. The light held them anyway, obedient to a logic larger than need, and Coral understood suddenly that she was living inside that logic — stopping for signals no one else could see, obeying rules written by a dead woman and a frightened child, rehearsing and rehearsing and never performing.

"Felix," she said.

"Yeah."

And then the bus lurched forward, the light gone green, and the momentum of it — the small physical fact of a vehicle resuming its route — knocked the sentence sideways in her mouth, and what came out instead was: "We should run it again from the middle section. Your mother's part. You keep rushing the list of names."

Something flickered across his face. Not surprise. Recognition — the recognition of a pattern witnessed, finally, from the outside. She saw him see it. She saw him decide, visibly, deliberately, not to push. Not tonight. Not mid-rehearsal. He folded the pages along their worn creases and said, "Yeah. Okay. From the names."

But he had noticed. That was the difference. For ten years the pattern had operated in the dark, invisible even to her, duty arriving exactly on schedule to interrupt every approach of truth. Now it had been seen, named almost, held up to the sodium light. A pattern witnessed is a pattern beginning to die. She could feel it dying in her chest even as she hid behind the rehearsal one more time — feel the door straining against hinges she herself had installed.

*Next time,* she promised herself, and hated how many times she had promised that, and promised it anyway.

* * *

They ran it twice more. By the third pass Felix had stopped rushing the names, and Coral had stopped flinching at her mother's confession, and somewhere around the curve past the reservoir the rehearsal stopped being rehearsal and became something closer to mourning — the two of them speaking a dead woman's sentences into an empty bus while the city she'd died protecting slept around them, unaware, ungrateful, forgiven anyway.

"This is good," Felix said afterward. They were at the back now, both of them, shoulder to shoulder in their old row, the letter copy resting on the seat between them like a sleeping child. "This is actually good. Tomorrow night we do it in front of people and it'll hold."

"It'll hold," Coral agreed.

"And then—" He paused. She felt him choose his words, felt the care of it, and braced. "And then there's the other thing. The thing that isn't the letter."

"The auction's over, Felix. There's nothing left to translate publicly."

"I'm not talking about the auction." He was looking straight ahead through the windshield's far reflection, at the road unwinding toward the depot, toward the end of the line. "I'm talking about the sentence you keep almost saying. I told you — I've been counting. Four times tonight alone. Once on the fire escape last spring, before the phone call. Once at the library, the day we finished the translation, when you looked at me and started to say my name and then said 'the letter' instead." He turned to face her then, and his eyes in the dark bus were steady and sad and impossibly kind. "I'm not asking you to say it. I want you to know that. Whatever it is, whenever it's ready, or never — I'm not going anywhere, and I'm not asking. I just needed you to know that I see the door. Because I think — I think maybe you've spent your whole life believing that if nobody sees you hesitate, then the hesitation doesn't count. Doesn't cost anything. And it does count, Coral. It's costing you right now. I can hear it."

The bus pulled into the depot. The engine dropped to idle. Outside, the first gray suggestion of dawn was touching the tops of the buildings — not the blue hour yet, but its antechamber, its waiting room.

Odette called back softly, "Last stop, you two. Unless you want to ride it again."

Coral looked at Felix. Felix looked at Coral. And the strangest thing happened, the thing she would remember later as the moment the pattern finally broke: she opened her mouth to say the safe thing — *no, thanks, we're done* — and instead what came out was, "One more loop."

"One more loop," Felix echoed, and something passed between them that was not the confession, not yet, but was the ground the confession would stand on — level, cleared, load-bearing.

Odette smiled in the mirror and pulled out of the depot, and the 47 carried them back into the dark toward the blue hour, the letter folded between them, the unsaid sentence riding in Coral's chest like a second heartbeat, louder now, closer to the surface, patient the way a tide is patient.

Tomorrow night, the reading. Tomorrow night, the room full of neighbors and reporters and whatever remained of Vesper Hale's ambitions. But tonight — one more loop, and the city sliding past the glass, and Felix's shoulder warm against hers, and the door standing open at last, waiting only for her to walk through it.
