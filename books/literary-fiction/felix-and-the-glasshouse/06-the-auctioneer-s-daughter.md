## 6. The Auctioneer's Daughter

The auction house sat on Corvane's main street the way old dogs sit on porches: without ambition, without apology, certain of its right to be there. Calloway & Sons, the sign said, though everyone in town knew there had never been sons, only June, and that the ampersand was doing more work than any child ever had.

Mara had walked past it twice already since arriving, cataloguing it the way they catalogued everything — width of the storefront, condition of the brick, the hairline settlement crack running diagonally from the second-floor window down through the painted letters *EST. 1961*. Structures told you their histories if you knew how to listen. This one was saying: *I have been leaning a long time, and no one has minded.*

Inside smelled of dust and lemon oil and something older underneath — paper, mostly, the particular sweetness of paper that had been folded and refolded for decades. Auction houses were archives that happened to sell things. Every object on every shelf had been loved once, or at least owned, which was sometimes the same thing and sometimes the cruelest possible distance from it.

"You're the Iversen girl."

Mara turned. A woman stood in the doorway to the back office, wiping her hands on a rag, and for a moment Mara's whole nervous system did what it always did when someone used that word — *girl* — about them: a small flinch, quickly converted into stillness.

Not a girl. Forty-one years old, hands scarred faintly at the knuckles from bridge cable work, hair gone grey early at the temples. But the woman said it with such easy certainty that arguing would have required an explanation, and explanations were not something Mara carried anymore.

"I'm sorry," the woman said, and tilted her head, and something shifted in her face like light moving across water. "You're not a girl. You're — God. You're Mara. Mara Iversen."

And then Mara saw it, the way you suddenly see the figure in the magic-eye poster: June Calloway, who had been eleven the summer Mara was sixteen, who had trailed after them through Ruth's glasshouse asking questions the way younger children do, relentless and adoring and utterly without malice. June, who had freckles then and a gap between her front teeth, who had once spent an entire afternoon trying to convince Mara that she could hear the fig trees growing if she pressed her ear flat against the trunk.

June Calloway, who now stood five-foot-six in boots, with laugh lines carved deep around eyes the color of wet slate, holding a rag like her father used to hold his gavel.

"June," Mara said. Their voice came out strange, half-strangled by eleven years of not being anyone's summer memory.

"You look exactly the same," June said, and then laughed at herself. "No. That's a lie. You look like someone who inspects bridges."

"How could you possibly—"

"Your hands." June nodded at them. "My father sold off the equipment from the county yard two years ago. I catalogued every piece. There was a torque wrench with wear marks just like yours — calluses here, here." She touched her own palm, demonstrating. "You spend your life gripping steel. It writes itself into you."

Mara looked down at their own hands as though seeing them for the first time. This was the thing people never understood about inspection work — that it wasn't abstract, wasn't numbers floating in space. It was the body against the structure, day after day, year after year. The work left records in places no report ever reached.

"You remember me," Mara said. Not a question. An accusation they hadn't meant to make.

"Every summer for six years, your grandmother let me come over and 'help' in the glasshouse, which meant I got underfoot and broke a clay pot in 1994 and blamed the cat." June set the rag down. "There was no cat, Mara. I've carried that confession for thirty years. I hope you can find it in yourself to forgive me."

It was absurd. It was a joke about a clay pot, offered in a dusty auction house between two middle-aged strangers who had once been children in the same humid green room full of fig trees. And Mara felt their throat tighten anyway, because no one had spoken to them about that time, that place, in the ordinary past tense of shared memory. Dev didn't talk about it. Mara didn't talk about it. The glasshouse existed between them like a body neither would look at directly.

But June remembered the cat that didn't exist.

"There were figs," Mara heard themselves say. "That summer. Ruth let you take some home in a shoebox."

"She wrapped each one in newspaper." June's voice had gone quieter. "I ate them on my porch and cried, and I didn't know why. My mother thought I was sick." She paused. "Do you know why a person would cry eating a perfect fig?"

Mara knew. Mara had known for thirty years and built a career on not saying so. Some things are so precisely good that they show you the exact dimensions of everything else you've lost.

* * *

They ended up at the diner two doors down, because June declared it so ("I'm not letting you leave this town again without at least one cup of coffee in you, that's how I lost track of Dev, I'm not repeating the mistake"), and because Mara found, to their surprise, that they didn't want to refuse.

June ordered for both of them without asking, which should have been irritating and instead felt like being handled by someone who knew the machinery. Coffee, pie, the special. "Trust me," she said. "I've eaten here four times a week since 2009. I know what's safe."

The diner was half empty at two in the afternoon. A ceiling fan turned with the lazy skepticism of a mechanism that had outlived its belief in its own purpose. Through the window, Mara could see the corner of Alder Street, or where Alder Street began, anyway — the rest disappeared behind the hardware store, but Mara's mind completed the map without permission: three blocks up, the chain-link fence, the clouded panes catching whatever light the afternoon had to give.

"So," June said, wrapping both hands around her mug. "You're here for the auction."

"I'm here."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have."

June studied them over the rim of her cup. She had her father's way of looking at people — appraising, patient, willing to wait out silence — but where Walt Calloway's gaze had always made Mara feel like merchandise, June's felt more like reading. Like she was checking for structural damage and finding plenty of it.

"My dad ran the estate sale when your grandmother went into the home," June said. "Twenty years ago. Half of Corvane walked through that house and bought her spoons." She said it without cruelty, just fact, the way weather is fact. "He kept the inventory file, though. He keeps inventory files on everything. Man's got files on estates that were settled before I was born. Won't throw anything away. Says provenance is the only honest thing left in this business."

Mara turned their coffee mug a quarter rotation on the formica. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked why I'm telling you this, which means you want to know, which means my instinct was right." June smiled — quick, crooked, nothing like the practiced smile Mara deployed to end conversations. June's smile started conversations. It was exhausting to be near. "There's a file on Alder Street. Thick one. Photographs, appraisal notes, condition reports. And—" she paused, and Mara watched her decide something, watched the decision move through her face like load shifting through a truss, "—there's a note in my dad's hand about papers. Papers your grandmother left in the potting shed. He catalogued them, sealed them back up, and they're in the file."

The fan turned. Somewhere in the kitchen, something sizzled and was ignored.

"Papers," Mara repeated.

"Ruth's handwriting, from what I understand. My dad mentioned it once, years ago, when he'd had a few. Said the family lawyer told him to keep it separate from anything public-facing. Said it wasn't auction material." June shrugged, and the shrug was too casual, and both of them knew it was too casual. "Estates are full of stuff like that. Letters nobody sent. Instructions nobody followed. I open boxes that are basically graveyards of unsaid things."

Mara's chest did something complicated. They took a breath and let it out slow, the way they'd been trained to do on high structures when the wind picked up — steady, measured, giving the fear nothing to push against.

"What kind of instructions?"

"I don't know. I haven't read them." June met their eyes. "I could have. The file's been in my office for six months while we prep the sale. But it didn't feel right, going through another woman's private papers just because they ended up in my building." She turned her mug too, mirroring Mara without seeming to notice. "Although I'll admit the temptation has been significant."

"Why tell me at all? You don't owe me anything. We were kids thirty years ago."

June looked out the window toward Alder Street, and her expression did something Mara couldn't parse — grief and guilt and affection all compressed into a single unreadable line, like a fracture too fine to measure without instruments.

"Because when I was eleven, your grandmother taught me how to prune a fig tree," she said. "She put the shears in my hand and said, *Cut clean, cut close, and never apologize to the tree.* I've thought about that sentence probably ten thousand times since. When I took over the business. When my dad got sick. When I divorced." She laughed softly. "I run an auction house, Mara. I sell other people's lives by the lot. And every single day I think: cut clean, cut close, don't apologize. And every single day I wonder if Ruth ever managed to follow her own advice, or if she just taught it to children because she couldn't practice it herself."

The coffee had gone cold. Mara drank it anyway.

"The walkthrough," June said, straightening. "I can get you into the glasshouse before the sale. Private. Tomorrow morning, before my staff arrives. No buyers, no clipboard, no gavel. Just you and the building." She hesitated. "And me, if you want. Or not. Your call."

Mara thought about the chain-link fence, the cataract panes, the way Dev had stood measuring the lot with a tape measure like a man taking vital signs of something already dead. They thought about walking through those doors alone, and about walking through them with someone who remembered the cat that didn't exist.

"With you," Mara said. "Tomorrow."

"Seven a.m. I'll bring keys and terrible vending machine coffee." June stood, pulled cash from her pocket, and dropped it on the table before Mara could protest. "This one's on the house. Call it provenance."

At the door, she paused with her hand on the frame.

"Mara. The papers in that file — I didn't tell you to hurt you. I told you because I've watched my father sit on things for twenty years out of some idea of protection, and I've watched what that protection costs the people it's supposedly for." Her voice was gentle and absolutely unyielding. "Whatever's in those letters, whoever they were meant for — I think they were meant to be found. Things that are meant to stay buried don't end up in potting sheds with labels on them."

"Labels," Mara said. "What label?"

June's face did the thing again — the flicker, the decision, the load shifting.

"'For whichever child comes back,'" she said quietly. "That's what my dad wrote in the margin. *For whichever child comes back.*"

Then she pushed open the door, and the bell above it rang, and she was gone into the bright cold street, leaving Mara alone at the table with two cups of dead coffee and a sentence that weighed more than any girder they had ever rated.

Outside, the wind had picked up. Mara could feel it even through the glass — a low pressure moving in off the valley, the kind that made old structures groan at night, the kind that preceded weather.

They sat for a long time, listening to the fan turn, thinking about sealed envelopes and unsent letters and a grandmother who had cut clean and cut close and never apologized to the tree.

Thinking: *whichever child comes back.*

Thinking: *both of us came back.*

Neither of us was supposed to.
