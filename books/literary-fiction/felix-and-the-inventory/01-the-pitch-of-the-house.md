## 1. The Pitch of the House

The house told you things before the family did. That was the first lesson Odile Montrose had learned at Verrecchia, seven years ago, on a raw October morning when she'd carried her tuning kit up the gravel drive and stood for a full minute in the courtyard listening to the place clear its throat.

Every house has a voice. Most people never hear it because they're too busy filling it with their own — televisions, arguments, the small percussive theater of daily life. But a house left to itself, or nearly itself, will speak in its native tongue: water moving through pipes with an opinion about pressure, beams settling by fractions of millimeters as the sun crossed from one side of the roof to the other, radiators ticking out their slow morse code of heat and cooling. The Verrecchia house was old enough and proud enough to have a great deal to say, and it said it constantly, in a register just below human hearing, the way some dogs can hear a whistle that would drive a man mad if he could catch it.

Odile could catch it. She had never told anyone this, because telling anyone would have required explaining herself, and she had built her entire livelihood on the principle that a person who explains herself is a person who can be argued with.

She arrived at nine, as she always did on the second Thursday of the month, and let herself in through the side door with the key Marthe Verrecchia had given her the first year and never asked back. The key was heavy, brass gone soft at the edges from decades of hands, and it turned with a sound like a polite cough.

"Good morning," said the hallway, in the voice of cold marble and a draft from under the study door.

"Good morning," Odile replied, because she was not a barbarian, even to houses.

* * *

The grand piano lived in the music room at the east end of the ground floor, a Bösendorfer, black lacquer gone slightly amber at the edges where sunlight had been leaning against it for sixty years. It was a beautiful instrument and a difficult one, temperamental in the way of expensive things that have been loved inconsistently — adored at Christmas, neglected in March, played badly by guests who wanted to be seen playing it more than they wanted to play it.

Odile set down her case, opened it, and did what she always did first, which was nothing. She sat on the piano bench and listened to the room.

This was the part of the work no conservatory taught. You had to learn a room before you could tune a piano in it, because a piano is never alone; it is in conversation with everything around it. Hard floors sharpen it. Heavy drapes muffle it. A high ceiling gives it ambitions it cannot fulfill. The Verrecchia music room had a parquet floor that added a bright edge to every note, two tall windows dressed in velvet the color of dried blood, and a ceiling high enough that the sound went up and came back down a half-beat late, like a guest who arrives after the soup.

And underneath all of it — beneath the piano's voice, beneath the room's voice — the house's own low commentary continued. This morning it was saying something about the boiler. She could hear it through the wall: a faint rhythmic knock, irregular, like a heart with a murmur. Third time this month. Someone ought to bleed the radiators on the east side before winter made it worse.

She would mention it. She mentioned such things once a season, carefully, folded into conversation like a pill hidden in cheese. *The east rooms run cold, Madame Verrecchia, have you noticed? Old systems, they get air in them.* And Marthe would nod and say thank you, Odile, we'll have someone look at it, and someone would not look at it, and next visit Odile would fold the observation into the cheese again. This was the dance. It had steps. She knew them all.

She uncapped the felt strip and began to mute the strings, working methodically across the tenor section, and let her mind do what it always did while her hands worked: read the house like a page.

* * *

You could tell a family's whole story through a house, if you paid attention. Not the story they told — the other one, the true one, written in maintenance and neglect.

The Verrecchia house said: *we were once richer than we are now, and we have agreed not to discuss it.* It said this in the third-floor bedrooms, closed since before Odile's time, their furniture sheeted, their radiators drained. It said it in the kitchen garden, reduced each year by a hedge-row, then another, surrendered back to grass with the quiet dignity of territory ceded in a war nobody declared. It said it most clearly in the roof, which had been patched rather than replaced, competently but without love, the way you patch a coat you intend to keep wearing until it disintegrates.

But the house also said: *we are still here.* The silver was polished. The floors were waxed. The portraits in the long gallery — stern Verrecchias receding into the past like a line of dominoes — were dusted weekly by Mrs. Ferro, who had cleaned for the family for thirty years and communicated primarily in sighs of varying pitch.

And the house said one thing more, something Odile had only recently begun to hear clearly, the way you finally make out a word in a song you've known for years. It said it in the west wing, in the way the corridor there was always a degree colder than the rest of the house, in the way the doors along it stayed shut, in the wardrobe — a great dark walnut wardrobe in the corner bedroom — that sat an inch away from the wall instead of flush against it, as though the wall behind it were a person nobody wished to face.

Houses keep secrets the same way families do: by arranging furniture around them.

* * *

At half past ten, footsteps on the stairs announced the day's first performance.

It was Cato, the youngest, twenty-four and perpetually rumpled, coming down in search of coffee with his hair doing something architectural and unintended. He passed the open door of the music room, saw Odile bent over the open piano, and performed the small startle that everyone in the family performed upon encountering her, as though she were a piece of furniture that had learned to sit upright.

"Oh — morning, Odile." He hovered. He always hovered. "Is it — are we flat again?"

"The Bösendorfer drifts sharp in dry weather and flat in wet," Odile said. "October is transitional."

"Right. Transitional." He nodded as though she'd said something profound, which was his habit, and retreated toward the kitchen, and Odile noted — because noting was her profession, in a sense, though her invoices said otherwise — that he hadn't slept, that he'd been smoking in his room again (the smell clung to him like a guilty conscience), and that he'd said *we* flat, including himself in the piano's condition, which was either accidental or the most honest sentence spoken in that house all week.

The middle child, Tomas, was not home. He lived two hours away and came for occasions: anniversaries, birthdays, the annual accounting of what remained. His absence registered in the house the way a missing tooth registers in a mouth — the family spoke around the gap. Odile had tuned through three of his visits now and could report that Tomas Verrecchia walked through his childhood home like a man walking through a museum of himself: interested, respectful, and quietly relieved to be leaving before closing time.

Marthe appeared at eleven, as punctual as a train and roughly as warm. She stood in the doorway of the music room holding a cup of tea she would not drink, wearing gray wool and the expression of a woman reviewing a ledger.

"Odile."

"Madame Verrecchia."

"You're on the treble already?"

"Almost. Another hour for the upper section. The pins in the top octave are getting loose — nothing urgent, but I'd like to replace two of them next visit, if that's agreeable."

"Of course." A pause, calibrated. "Mother so valued your work."

*Valued.* Past tense, worn smooth by eleven months of use. The matriarch, Elena Verrecchia, had been dead nearly a year now, and the family still spoke of her exclusively in the past tense, as though the tense itself were a form of mourning, or perhaps a form of containment — keeping her safely on the far side of the grammatical border where the living couldn't be troubled by her.

"She trusted me with it," Odile said, which was true, and neutral, and cost nothing.

"Mm." Marthe's eyes moved over the room — over the piano, over Odile, over the felt strips laid out with surgical neatness — performing an inventory she probably didn't know she was performing. Then, with the abruptness of a door closing gently: "Lunch will be at one. Mrs. Ferro sets a plate in the kitchen for you, if you'd like."

"Thank you."

And she was gone, wool and ledger, leaving behind the faintest scent of lily-of-the-valley soap and the strong impression of having been measured and found acceptable, provisionally, pending further review.

Odile returned to the treble strings. Her hands were steady. Her heart, which had done a small complicated thing during that pause — the pause in which Marthe's gaze had rested on her a half-second longer than courtesy required — settled back into its usual rhythm.

Being needed without being seen. That was the arrangement, and it was a good one, and she had paid dearly to learn how good it was. Seven years of Thursdays. A key of her own. A plate set out in the kitchen, because even the invisible must eat, and Mrs. Ferro, who was invisible too, always put the good bread near her end of the table without comment, one invisible woman acknowledging another across the visible ones' heads.

* * *

She broke for lunch at one, as summoned by no one and expected by everyone, and ate in the kitchen while Mrs. Ferro chopped onions with the violence of a woman who had opinions she was not paid to share.

"The east radiator's knocking again," Odile said.

"I know." Chop. Chop. "I told them in September. I'm told it will be seen to." The words *I'm told* were delivered with the flat precision of a guillotine blade being oiled.

"It'll crack if it freezes."

"I know that too." Mrs. Ferro looked up, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other across the kitchen table like members of a resistance cell confirming a shared cipher. "How is the piano?"

"Transitional."

"Hm." Which meant: *that's what I thought.* Mrs. Ferro went back to her onions. Odile finished her soup. Between them they had exchanged approximately four sentences and an entire treaty.

On her way back through the hall, she paused — not deliberately, her feet simply stopped, the way they sometimes did — and stood in the long gallery among the dominoes of dead Verrecchias. Afternoon light came through the tall windows and lay across the parquet in long gold bars, and the house spoke its afternoon language: beams ticking overhead as the sun warmed the south roof, the boiler murmuring its grievance, somewhere upstairs a door easing on its hinges with a sound like a sigh held too long.

There was a portrait at the end of the gallery that had always struck her as odd. Not odd in subject — another stern ancestor, another chair, another dog at the feet — but odd in placement. It hung slightly apart from the others, on the return wall by the window, where the light hit it badly, where a visitor's eye would slide past it without catching. As though it had been moved there. As though the family had arranged the room — arranged the *light* — so that this particular face would go unwitnessed.

She had noticed it years ago and filed it away, the way she filed away the cold west corridor and the wardrobe standing its careful inch off the wall. Noticing was free. Speaking was not. She had learned the exchange rate young, and she had paid in full, once, in another house with another family, and the debt had taken years to clear. Never again. She tuned pianos. What the houses said between notes was not her business.

Still, standing there in the gold light, she found her eyes going back to the displaced portrait. A man, younger than the others. Something in the mouth — the others' mouths were set like locks, but this one's mouth had a slight asymmetry, almost a smile, almost a question. The plaque beneath it bore a name half-worn: she had to lean close to read it, and even then the letters resisted.

*Aurelio.*

A name she had never heard spoken in seven years of Thursdays. Not at dinner-tables glimpsed through doors, not in Marthe's clipped instructions, not in Cato's rambling, not in Mrs. Ferro's sighs, which were fluent in everything else.

"Aurelio," she said aloud, very quietly, testing the weight of it in the house's air.

Above her, a beam ticked. Down the hall, the boiler knocked twice and went silent, as if listening.

Odile straightened, adjusted her case on her shoulder, and went back to the piano, where forty strings waited in the treble for her to bring them to pitch. She worked until five, and the house talked to her the whole time, and she answered in the only language she allowed herself — A440, steady, true — and if her eyes drifted once, twice, toward the east wall and the wardrobe-shaped shadow of silence in the corner bedroom above, well.

Even instruments drift. Even October is transitional.
