## 13. Letter Twenty-Nine: What Was Taken

**Rosalind**

The letters had an order to them now. Felix had insisted on it — laid them out across his hotel room floor in a grid of four by ten, one corner weighted with the ice bucket because the desk had run out of coasters and Felix refused to put a glass on history.

"Twenty-nine," he said that night, holding it up to the lamp like a jeweler assessing a stone. "This one's different."

"How?"

"It's longer. And she pressed harder." He turned it over. "You can see the pen went through the paper in places. She was angry when she wrote this. Or frightened. With Edith those were always the same weather."

We had developed a ritual over the six weeks since the wardrobe: he read the Hush aloud in his halting, boyhood-accented way, and I wrote down what I heard, and then we argued about what we'd written until one of us laughed or one of us left the room. It was not a method any linguist would endorse. It worked anyway. That was the thing about a language built for two people — you didn't need to be fluent in it. You only needed to be fluent in each other, and grief, it turned out, was a kind of fluency.

"Read it," I said.

Felix smoothed the page flat against his knee. Outside, the city did its insomniac hum — sirens somewhere near the ring road, a delivery truck beeping backwards into some other person's life at two in the morning.

"'Dear Vera,'" he began, and stopped. "It starts *Dear Vera*. The others mostly start with just her name, or nothing at all. This one's formal. Like a business letter." He looked up at me. "Like a confession."

I remember thinking, even then, even before the words came, that the room had gone slightly too quiet, the way it does before a lift arrives.

**Felix**

I want to write down exactly what the letter said, because I have learned in these weeks that memory is a thief with excellent taste, and if I don't record it while my hands still shake in the right places, I'll smooth it over later. So:

*Dear Vera.*

*I am going to write this once and I am going to write it properly, which means I am going to write it in our language so that no one can take it from me, not the solicitors, not Mother, not him. You taught me that a language can be a locked room. I am locking the door behind me now, and you are the only key, and I know you will never turn it, and I deserve that.*

*I was pregnant in March. You know whose. You were there when I found out, in the orchard, and you held my hands and said we would tell them together, and I let you believe that, and that is the first theft. But it is not the last.*

*I told them the baby was mine and that I would keep it alone. I let everyone believe what they already believed about me — that I was careless, that I was the sister who got into trouble — because it was easier than telling them what you had done for me, which was everything, which was more than everything. You had saved your money for three years. For the adoption. For the family in the north who had written back twice. You gave me the envelope, Vera. You put it in my hands at the bus station and you said, "For whatever you decide," and I decided to spend it on a lie.*

*I told him the child was mine and no one else's, and I watched relief move through him like weather, and I let it. Because if the child was mine, then I was not the girl who couldn't manage, and you were not the sister who had given away her whole future for me, and nobody had to look at what we'd actually done.*

*They took you away after that. Not literally. Worse. They took you away politely, with casseroles and concern, sent you to Aunt Ruth's "for a rest," and by the time you came back I had made myself into the story they preferred, and you had become the cautionary tale, and neither of us could say one word of it aloud because the whole architecture would come down.*

*I kept the money. I spent it on the flat, on the pram, on the years. I named her something you would recognize and then pretended I hadn't.*

*Every letter I have written you since has been interest on that debt. This one is the principal.*

*I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking you to know. There is a difference and I only understood it last week, standing in a kitchen, watching her eat toast.*

*Her name is Rosalind. She has your laugh. I hear it every day and every day it costs me.*

*Yours in the locked room,*
*Edith*

That was the letter. I've checked my transcription four times and it holds.

I read it aloud to Rosalind because that was the ritual, and because I am, apparently, a coward of a very specific kind — the kind who hides inside procedure. If I follow the steps, I tell myself, I am not responsible for what the steps produce.

I got as far as *"Her name is Rosalind"* before I understood what I was reading.

**Rosalind**

There is a moment — I have since learned it has a name in trauma literature, though knowing the name is like knowing the Latin for a flower growing out of a crack in your skull — there is a moment when the floor does not literally give way but your relationship to floors changes permanently. You understand, viscerally, that all your life you have been walking on something that was never load-bearing. Someone just told you it was, and you believed them, because why wouldn't you? Floors are the first thing we're taught to trust.

Felix's voice did something strange on the word *Rosalind*. It didn't break. It went flat, the way voices do when the body has decided the news is too heavy to carry with inflection.

He kept reading. *She has your laugh.* He kept reading. *Every day it costs me.*

And I sat on the edge of a hotel bed in a room that smelled of carpet shampoo and someone else's cigarettes, and I thought, with the strange administrative calm that arrives in emergencies: *the adoption. Vera's adoption. The money. The baby.*

Then the calm finished its paperwork and left, and the floor went.

Because here is what I knew, in the way you know things before you know them: I had been adopted. Or rather — I had been *told*, at nineteen, in a car park, by my mother, in eleven sentences, that I had been "chosen," and the word had been doing so much work in that sentence that I'd never examined the foundations under it. Chosen. Like a puppy. Like a paint color. I had built forty-some years on the word *chosen* and never once asked: chosen *from what? From whom?*

Vera. The woman in the letters. The woman my mother wrote to for fifty years and never mailed a single envelope to. The woman whose laugh I supposedly had.

"Felix," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to the old man who reads dictionaries at the Meridian — dry, borrowed, secondhand. "Say the next part again."

"I don't think—"

"The part about the name. Say it again."

He looked at me the way you look at someone standing on ice you've already heard crack. "*I named her something you would recognize,*" he recited, quietly, "and then pretended I hadn't."

"My mother named me Rosalind," I said. "But that wasn't — Felix, that wasn't her name for me. That was Vera's. She gave me Vera's—" I stopped, because the sentence had arrived somewhere I wasn't ready to stand.

The laugh. I have been told my whole life that I laugh like no one in the family. My father used to say it almost accusingly, as if I'd acquired it somewhere disreputable. *Where did you get that laugh?* And my mother would go quiet in a particular way, a way I had filed for decades under *embarrassment*, and which was actually, I now understood, *grief*, wearing embarrassment's coat.

I laughed. Right there, in that beige room, I laughed — short, awful, involuntary — and Felix flinched as if I'd struck him, and I realized he was hearing it too. Whatever Edith heard every day. Whatever cost her.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, God. It's true. The laugh is true."

**Felix**

There is a version of this scene where I comfort her. I have imagined that version many times since, lying awake in the hotel bed, and in it I say wise things and hold her hands and the moment lands softly. In the actual room, I did none of that. I sat very still on the floor with twenty-eight decoded letters around my knees like fallen leaves, and I thought about a boy of nine being carried out of his grandmother's house during a row he wasn't supposed to hear, and how the row had been about money, and how his mother had gripped his wrist too hard and said, *Never ask about Vera. Never say her name in this house.*

I had obeyed for fifty years. Obedience, I have come to believe, is just loyalty that hasn't been audited.

"Rosalind," I said finally, "you need to breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe."

"Fine. Then don't. But do it anyway."

That got half a sound out of her — not quite a laugh, more the ghost of one, and somehow that was worse, because it proved the letter right in real time. She pressed both hands over her mouth.

"She stole me," Rosalind said through her fingers. "Not — no. She didn't steal *me*. She stole Vera's—" She stood up, sat down, stood again, pacing the narrow strip between the bed and the window like a sentence that couldn't find its verb. "Vera was adopting a baby. Vera had a *family lined up*. And Edith took it. Took the money, took the chance, took the — and then raised the evidence. Raised it. Fed it toast. Watched it laugh."

"You're not evidence."

"Aren't I?" She turned on me and her eyes were wet and furious and I recognized the fury because it was Edith's, exactly Edith's, the same storm I'd walked out on nine years ago. "What am I, Felix? A daughter? A debt repayment? A living apology she never posted?"

"I don't know," I said, because lying to her seemed suddenly obscene. "I genuinely don't know. But I know what you're not. You're not the lie. The lie is Edith's. You're just the person who's been carrying it without knowing, which — God, Rosalind, that might be worse than anything in the letter."

She stopped pacing. Something in her face closed, the way shop shutters close — not angry, just done for the night.

"I need to go home," she said.

"It's three in the morning."

"I live above a laundrette, Felix. Home is where the machines are. It's fine." She gathered her coat, and then, at the door, with her hand on the handle, she said the thing I will keep: "Don't translate any more tonight. Promise me. Some of these letters should be opened in daylight."

I promised. I broke the promise within the hour, but that comes later, and later belongs to another chapter of this mess.

**Rosalind**

I walked home through the sleeping-adjacent city at four a.m., and the streets did their usual trick of seeming like a stage set between performances. A fox regarded me outside the bakery with the professional indifference of a colleague. The laundrette below my flat was running its overnight cycle, and through the window the machines tumbled their loads in slow, hypnotic somersaults, dozens of strangers' sheets turning over and over, never getting anywhere.

I climbed the stairs and did not turn on the light. I sat on the floor by the window with my knees up, watching the dryers spin.

Here is what I could not stop doing: inventorying myself. Every memory came up for revaluation, like stock after a currency collapse. The word *chosen*. The car park. My mother's face when I laughed. The way she'd once, only once, called me by a different name in her sleep — I'd assumed it was a dead friend, a schoolgirl, someone from the lost country of her youth. *Vera, Vera, put it down.* I had thought: put what down? Now I knew. Put down the envelope. Put down the guilt. Put down the sister.

I had spent my whole adult life feeling like a tenant in my own existence — month to month, no roots permitted, ready to be given notice. I'd blamed temperament. I'd blamed the city, the insomnia, the century. It had never occurred to me that the feeling might be *accurate*. That I was, in the most literal sense available to a human being, living in someone else's arrangement. A sublet. A life let furnished, by a woman who'd taken it from her sister and never once changed the locks.

The dryers turned. Somewhere below, a sock escaped its drum and lay abandoned on the folding table, small and orphaned and absurd, and I started crying — not elegantly, not cathartically, just leaking, steadily, the way old buildings leak, from everywhere at once.

My phone buzzed on the floor beside me. Felix.

*Did you get home ok*

I typed: *yes.* Then deleted it. Typed: *no.* Deleted that too. Finally I wrote:

*She has your laugh. Did you feel that land? Because it landed.*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared.

*Rosalind,* he wrote. *There's something I have to tell you. About why I left. It's not what you think.*

I stared at the screen until the words blurred. Below me the machines spun. Above me, nowhere, my mother — whoever she had been, whichever of her names applied — was silent in the ground, keeping, as ever, the only appointment she'd never missed.

I did not answer Felix. I put the phone face-down on the floorboards, and I sat in the dark above the laundrette, in the flat I had rented for eleven years without once calling it mine, and I waited for morning the way you wait for a verdict.

Whatever Felix had done, whatever he was about to confess — I could feel it waiting too, patient as a letter behind a wardrobe.
