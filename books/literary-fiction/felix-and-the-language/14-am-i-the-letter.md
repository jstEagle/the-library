## 14. Am I the Letter?

The morning after the floor gave way beneath her, Rosalind Thackeray did the only sensible thing available to a woman who had just discovered she was a plot twist: she went to bed.

Not her bed, at first. She tried the sofa, then the armchair, then the floor beside the radiator, which hummed like a small apologetic animal. None of them held her. Finally she climbed into bed with her shoes still on, because taking them off would have meant admitting she intended to stay, and she did not intend to stay anywhere. She intended, if pressed, to hover.

She slept until four in the afternoon.

When she woke, the light through the curtain had gone the color of weak tea, and for one merciful second she was nobody, just a body in a warm room above a laundrette, with the dryers turning below like patient planets. Then the second ended, and she was Rosalind, who was also, apparently, a letter. Addressed to a man named — she still didn't know his name. The father. The one Edith had kept by claiming Vera's baby as her own. *Her* baby. *Her* self.

She lay there and thought about parcels.

She thought about how, at the Meridian Library, misdelivered items were the small comedy of her week. The man who kept receiving catalogs for a dentist in Leeds. The woman whose reserved novel arrived with someone else's name inked inside the cover, so that she read the whole book under an alias, reluctantly returning to her own life at the desk. Rosalind had always found this funny. She had stamped RETURN TO SENDER on things with the serene confidence of a person who knew where she herself belonged.

Well. She thought about the stamp. She thought about herself, fifty years in transit, delivered to the wrong address, signed for by the wrong woman, and no one — not the sender, not the carrier, not the recipient — ever filing a complaint.

She got up. She took her shoes off. That was the whole of what she accomplished before dark.

* * *

The days that followed had a texture she recognized from her worst winters, but deeper, as though someone had taken the familiar fabric of her loneliness and turned it inside out to show the seams.

She did not go to work. She called the library on the third day and told Dev she had flu, and Dev — bless him, Dev who alphabetized the returns cart by *vibe* — said, "Oh no, is it the bad flu or the lying-down flu?" and she said, "The lying-down one," which was at least honest, and he said, "Take what you need, Rosalind. The dictionaries will wait. Mr. Okafor asked after you, by the way."

She said she'd be back soon. She meant it the way you mean *we should get coffee sometime* — as a sound shaped like a promise, with nothing inside it.

She did not answer Felix. He called twice a day, morning and evening, punctual as a tide. She watched the phone light up on the kitchen counter and felt the old tenant-feeling settle over her: *this is not my crisis, this is a crisis I am storing for someone else.* Except it was hers. That was the whole horror. For the first time in her life, the thing in the flat was hers, and she wanted to hand it back to a landlord who did not exist.

She ate toast standing up. She ate cereal over the sink. She ate, one memorable night at three a.m., a cold baked potato with her hands, standing at the window watching the laundrette's sign buzz and gutter — LAUNDRETTE, with the E long dead, so it read LAUNDR TT, which she had always privately found very funny and which now struck her as a message from the universe: even the building couldn't finish its own word.

She slept through the days like something buried. The sun would come up and she would pull the duvet over her head with the indignation of a woman being personally photographed. The city went about its business below — buses sighing at the corner, the bakery queue forming at seven, someone always, always practicing the trumpet badly around nine — and she lay in the dark of her rented rooms listening to all of it the way you listen to a party in the flat upstairs: present, excluded, oddly comforted.

* * *

On the fifth day, she tried to read.

This was the true measure of the damage. Rosalind had read through grief, through flu, through the great heartbreak of her twenties, through a fire alarm at four a.m. in a hotel in York. Reading was her load-bearing wall. She picked up the novel on her nightstand — a fat, kind, well-reviewed thing about a family with secrets, which, she now realized, she had chosen with the unconscious instinct of a woman who enjoyed other people's secrets at a safe remove — and she read the same paragraph four times.

The paragraph was about a woman discovering her mother had lied to her.

She closed the book and set it down as if it were hot.

Because here was the thing she kept circling, the way her tongue kept returning to a chipped tooth: it wasn't the theft. It was the *addressing*. Somewhere in the last week, in the margins of the letters, in Felix's careful voice as he translated, she had begun to think of herself not as a person but as a piece of mail. Edith had written her into being with a false return address. Everything Rosalind had built on top of that — the night shifts, the small flat, the carefully curated solitude she'd been calling peace for forty years — was furniture arranged in a house she'd been delivered to by mistake.

She thought about Vera. That was the worst of it. Somewhere two towns over was a woman who had been *sender* and *intended recipient* both, and Rosalind could not stop constructing her: Vera at twenty-three, coming home to an empty nursery that had been painted, Vera growing old in a town Edith never visited, Vera asking — as Felix had learned from the care home's brochure, that absurd detail — *did she ever write?*

Rosalind lay in the dark and thought: *I am the thing she was asking about. I am the answer to a question she's been asking for fifty years, and I am lying in bed above a laundrette eating a potato.*

She laughed. It came out wrong, a single bark, and the flat was so quiet afterward that she apologized out loud to no one.

* * *

Felix stopped calling twice a day around day six and started calling once, in the evening, and leaving voicemails that were small masterpieces of brotherly restraint.

"Rosalind. It's Felix. It's Tuesday. I've been re-reading Letter Twenty-Nine, or trying to — I can't do it alone, which is, I think, the point of it. No pressure. The noodles place on Marlow Street does that thing with the chili oil you liked. That's all. Bye."

She played that one three times. She noticed she had liked the chili oil thing, that Felix had remembered, that *liked* was a word with a brother attached to it now, and then she noticed she was doing the thing where a feeling arrives and she immediately files it in the archive instead of answering the door. *Tenant behavior,* she thought. *You don't answer the door in a building you don't own.*

On day seven she dreamt of the wardrobe.

In the dream, she was small, and the wardrobe in Edith's flat was enormous, taller than the ceiling should allow, and its doors stood open on a darkness that was not empty but *addressed* — she understood this in the dream-way, without words — a darkness with her name on it, in Hush, in the looping script that meant, depending on the stroke, either *beloved* or *borrowed*. She woke at noon with the word still behind her eyes and lay there translating it back and forth until the two meanings wore smooth and became the same word, which was somehow the truest thing that had happened all week.

* * *

On the ninth day, the laundrette flooded.

Not dramatically. A hose behind machine four gave up in the night, and Rosalind woke at two in the afternoon to a knock — the landlord's son, Kofi, apologetic, holding a wet-dry vacuum like a weapon he'd been issued reluctantly. Water had crept along the shared wall; her hallway carpet had a dark border, like the flat was being framed for a portrait.

"Sorry about this," Kofi said. "Dad says a week, maybe two, till it's properly dry. You can stay, obviously, it's not dangerous, it's just — damp."

"It's fine," Rosalind said, and then, because the week had hollowed out whatever filter she usually kept between her brain and her mouth: "Everything's damp, really, if you think about it."

Kofi looked at her the way people look at a vending machine that has spoken. "Right. Well. We'll bring a dehumidifier up."

The dehumidifier arrived that evening, a squat white machine that roared like a small devoted dragon and began, immediately, to fill its tray with water drawn out of her walls. Rosalind sat on the floor of the hallway watching it, and something about the sight — the machine patiently pulling the invisible out of the structure, tray by tray, hour by hour — made her eyes sting.

She was still sitting there when Felix knocked.

She knew it was him before she opened the door. Nobody else knocked like that: two knocks, a pause, a third knock slightly softer, as if the third one was doing the emotional labor of the first two. She stood in her hallway with the dragon roaring and the damp border framing her and considered not answering, and then considered that she was forty-one years old and had spent nine days being a parcel, and parcels don't get to decide when they're collected, but people do.

She opened the door.

Felix stood there holding a bag of takeaway — she could smell the chili oil, which was either a coincidence or the most manipulative act of brotherly love in recorded history — and he looked at her the way you look at a house you're worried has structural problems.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey," she said.

"You look like you've been sleeping in your shoes."

"I've been sleeping in everything." She stepped back to let him in, and it occurred to her, as he crossed the threshold and stood dripping rain onto the hallway carpet, that this was the first time in nine days she had voluntarily been in a room with another person, and that the flat — the damp, rented, misdelivered flat — felt, for one unsteady second, less like an address and more like a home with someone else in it.

* * *

They ate the noodles on the floor of the sitting room, because the table was covered in letters, and Felix talked about nothing for a while — the chili oil, a pigeon that had been following him, the care home's brochure, which described its facilities as "homely" and its activities as "varied," two words that between them promised absolutely nothing. Rosalind ate and listened and felt herself being slowly, gently re-inflated, like a flat tire attached to a very patient pump.

Finally he set down his carton and said, "I'm not going to push. But I want to say one thing, and then I'll shut up."

She braced.

"You've spent nine days in here deciding what you are," Felix said. "Whether you're hers, or hers, or nobody's, or a lie with a birthday." He turned his chopsticks over in his fingers, not looking at her, which was his way of looking at her. "And I get it. I do. But I keep thinking about what you told me the old man at the library said about translation. That you're not finished when there's nothing left to add."

Rosalind went still.

"You're not finished when there's nothing left to *take away*," she said quietly. "That was Okafor. That was — " She stopped. Because she had been doing the opposite, hadn't she. Nine days of subtraction. Taking away the work, the brother, the light, the name. Stripping herself down to the sentence she couldn't bear, as if grief were an editing job and the goal was to cut the manuscript to its single unbearable line.

Felix watched her arrive there. He was good at that; he'd always been good at waiting for her to catch up to herself.

"The letter's not finished," she said. "The sentence. Edith's sentence. It doesn't resolve — you said so, we both felt it — because it isn't done being written. And I've been sitting in the dark trying to read an unfinished sentence and blaming myself for the ink." She looked at her brother, at his tired, kind, guilty, loyal face, and something in her chest unclenched by a single degree. "Vera's still alive, Felix."

"I know where you're going."

"She's the only other reader. She's the only person alive who can finish it." Rosalind stood up, and her knees cracked, and the dehumidifier roared on, pulling the damp out of the walls one tray at a time, and she heard herself say the thing she had been circling for nine days without ever once saying: "We have to go. Not because I'm the letter. Because the sentence isn't finished, and she's still waiting to hear it."

Felix nodded slowly. Then he reached into his jacket pocket and took out an envelope — old, soft-cornered, the paper gone the color of oatmeal — and held it out to her with a strange, careful formality, like a man surrendering a weapon.

"There's one thing I didn't tell you," he said. "When I went back through the box. Letter Thirty was in there too. It's addressed to both of us."
