## 16. The Untranslatable Sentence, Attempted

My sister has a way of knocking that I have come to understand as a form of punctuation. Three sharp raps — a colon, announcing that something is about to follow. Two soft ones — a comma, meaning she's only checking I'm still alive in there. And the single knock, the one she used that Tuesday evening in October, is a full stop. It means: I have decided something. Open the door and be part of it.

I opened the door. She was holding the shoebox against her chest the way you hold a cat that has recently forgiven you and might un-forgive you at any moment.

"Letter Twenty-Nine," she said. "We're doing it properly tonight."

"Properly," I said. "As opposed to the other eleven attempts."

"The other eleven attempts were reconnaissance. This is the campaign."

Rosalind had inherited from our mother not the language of Hush — that she'd been deliberately kept from, a fact that had curdled in all of us differently — but the language of Edith's metaphors, which was its own dialect and nearly as impenetrable. I stepped aside and let her in.

My flat, in those weeks, had stopped being a place I was staying and started being a place where we worked. There was a table by the window that had previously served as a surface for keys I kept losing and mail I kept not opening. Now it was covered in Rosalind's index cards, my old fountain pen, two desk lamps angled like interrogators, and a thermos of coffee so strong it had opinions. The letters themselves lived in the shoebox, but Letter Twenty-Nine lived in a plastic sleeve, because we had learned to be careful the hard way — the way you learn most things, by nearly destroying the thing you love at two in the morning.

"Before we start," Rosalind said, sitting down and pulling her cardigan around her like armor she'd grown fond of, "I want to say something."

This alarmed me. In my experience, when Rosalind announces she wants to say something, what follows is either an apology or an accusation, and I had recently received a full ration of both.

"I've been thinking about what you told me," she said. "About the thing you took the blame for. When I was nineteen."

I kept my hands busy with the thermos. Pouring coffee is an excellent activity when your chest is doing something you'd rather not discuss.

"You don't have to say anything," I said. "It was a long time ago."

"That's exactly the problem. Everyone in this family treats everything like it was a long time ago. Mum wrote forty-one letters over fifty years and treated them like they were yesterday." She turned the sleeve over in her hands. "So. Thank you. For what you did. And I'm sorry. For what it cost you."

The coffee went into the cup. Some of it went onto the table. I mopped it with my sleeve, which gave me time to arrange my face into something that wasn't whatever it had been about to be.

"Well," I said. "Now we've both said a thing. We can never do it again. It'll ruin us."

"Agreed." She almost smiled. "Pass the letter."

* * *

I want to describe what it is like to translate a language that only two people ever spoke, one of whom is dead, and to do it with the only other person alive who carries any of it in his mouth. It is like reconstructing a bridge from memory while standing on it. Every word we recovered was a plank; every gap was a long look down.

Hush, for those who were never eight years old in a Norfolk orchard with a sister who refused to speak English on principle, was not a cipher. Ciphers substitute. Hush *behaved*. It had moods. It borrowed shamelessly — from the Latin our grandfather muttered over his garden, from radio plays, from a word Edith once misheard on a bus and refused to ever correct because she liked her version better. It had a grammar that made sense only if you accepted its one founding law: nothing in Hush could be said by one person alone. Certain constructions — the vows, the apologies, the futures — required two speakers, one beginning, one answering, like a call-and-response hymn.

Edith and Vera had built a language whose deepest rooms could only be entered in company.

I had forgotten that. Or rather, I had filed it under childhood, under things that were true once and were not required to remain true. Rosalind, who had never known Hush at all, understood it faster than I did, the way someone who has never owned a clock can still tell you the time by the light.

We had been at it for two hours when we hit the sentence.

You know the one. Every family has a sentence like it, even if it isn't written down. The thing that was said or not said at the funeral, the hospital, the kitchen table in 1994. Ours happened to be written down, in green ink, at the bottom of Letter Twenty-Nine, and it looked like this — or as close as I can render it in these poor borrowed letters:

*Velm-ah, soth renn ila, kest duvarren —*

And then it stopped. Not trailed off. Stopped, the way a door stops when it meets a frame.

"It's grammatically complete," Rosalind said, for perhaps the ninth time, tapping the card where she'd transcribed it. "You said so yourself."

"It is grammatically complete," I agreed, for the ninth time, because agreement was cheaper than the alternative. "The verb is in the paired future. The subject is the shared first person — the *we* that only exists in Hush. The final word is an imperative. It's a sentence that asks to be answered."

"So why can't I read it?"

"Because," I said, and then I stopped, because the answer had arrived all at once, the way answers do, wearing no clothes and no explanation, simply *there*, the way my sister was suddenly *there* in my flat, in my life, after nine years of a silence we had both maintained with the diligence of monks.

"Felix?"

"Read it with me," I said.

She looked at me the way she used to look at me when I suggested she climb down from a roof. "With you how?"

"Out loud. Together. The paired future isn't a tense, Ros — it's an instruction. It can't be *read* by one person because it isn't *in* one person. Edith built the whole language on it. The vows, the futures, the — " I was standing now, I realized, and the desk lamp was making a halo of my shadow on the wall, and I did not care. "The sentence isn't unfinished. It's *unstaffed*. It's been waiting for two speakers for fifty years."

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. Then she said, with the particular dryness she deployed when she was terrified and wanted to be thought merely skeptical: "You're telling me our mother encoded a duet into a letter she never mailed, addressed to a woman she never spoke to again, and the duet has been sitting in a shoebox waiting for — what? Two random descendants to turn up and do karaoke with her grief?"

"I'm telling you it's worse than that," I said. "I think she wrote it *for* us. I think she knew, eventually, that the letter would be found by someone who wasn't Vera. I think the last sentence was always addressed to whoever came next."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the laundrette's dryer turned over with its patient, planetary sound.

"That's an awful lot to infer from one verb," she said.

"It is," I admitted. "But consider the alternative. Consider that she wrote a sentence she knew couldn't be finished, and then died. That's cruelty. That's Edith leaving us a locked door with the key melted into the handle. Whereas if she wrote a sentence that could only be finished by two people who had inherited the quarrel — " I spread my hands. "That's a will. That's a testament. That's an instruction: *you two, together, or not at all.*"

Rosalind looked down at the letter. When she spoke again, the dryness had gone out of her voice and left something younger behind it.

"She spent her whole life not saying things," she said. "And then she built a sentence that made saying it *impossible to do alone*. So that no Thackeray could ever again keep a silence by themselves." She laughed — a small, cracked sound, half wonder. "That's the most Mum thing I've ever heard. That's *exactly* what she'd do. Weaponize grammar against her own children's loneliness."

"She'd have made a decent novelist," I said.

"Terrible. She'd have made everyone cry and refused to explain why."

* * *

We did not read it that night. I want to be honest about that, because the version of this story where we clasp hands over the shoebox and the sentence resolves in a shower of light is a version I'd like very much to be true, and it isn't. What happened instead was that we stood on opposite sides of the table like a couple about to dance for the first time since a wedding, and Rosalind read the first half alone — *Velm-ah, soth renn ila* — and the sounds came out of her mouth in Edith's cadence without her permission, and she stopped, and pressed her knuckles to her lips, and said, "God. It's her voice. It's actually her voice in my mouth," and I said, "That's how it works, that's the whole trick of it," and my own voice did something embarrassing, and we agreed, mutually and with great dignity, that it was late and we should sleep on it.

I did not sleep on it. I lay on my back in the dark with the sentence turning over in me like a stone in a riverbed, wearing itself smooth, and I thought about all the paired futures Edith and Vera had built when they were girls. *We will* — but only ever *we*, and only ever with the other one answering. A language that made loneliness grammatically impossible, invented by two children who could not have known they were building a lifeboat. And then one of them stole the other's whole future — my existence, my breath, my first cry in a hospital where Vera was not — and the lifeboat sat empty for fifty years, and Edith rowed out to it every year and wrote one more letter and came back.

At four in the morning I got up and wrote on an index card, in English, the closest thing to a translation I could manage without Rosalind:

*We, who were two and then were not, will —*

And I looked at the dash, and I understood that the dash was not Edith's failure. The dash was the door. The imperative at the end — *kest duvarren* — was not a word. It was a held-out hand.

* * *

We tried again on Thursday, after Rosalind finished her shift, and this time we did it standing, because sitting felt wrong, because the sentence had the posture of a thing meant to be spoken upright, at a graveside or a threshold.

"Rules," Rosalind said. She had made rules. Of course she had made rules; she was a librarian. "No stopping once we start. No explaining. If it works, it works, and if it doesn't, we never speak of it, like the incident with the trampoline."

"The incident with the trampoline is the reason you have that scar."

"The incident with the trampoline is *closed*, Felix. Begin."

I took the first half. She took the answer. We had worked out the division the night before, over the phone, at an hour when neither of us would admit to being awake, and the strange thing — the thing I keep returning to, the thing I suspect I will be returning to when I am an old man telling this story badly to someone who didn't ask — is that the division was obvious. There was no negotiation. The sentence itself told us who said what. It had been waiting so long to be spoken that it had opinions about the casting.

I breathed in. The green ink seemed very dark in the lamplight, dark as orchard shade.

"*Velm-ah,*" I said. "*Soth renn ila.*"

*We, who were two and then were not —*

And Rosalind, who had never spoken Hush in her life until this autumn, who had been kept out of it the way you keep a child out of a room where something is being grieved, answered. Her accent was imperfect. Her vowels bent where they should have held. None of it mattered. The language took her in the way a current takes a swimmer — not gently, but completely.

"*— soth renn ila, kest duvarren —*"

*— will, if the other speaks, be —*

And there it stopped. The frame. The door meeting its jamb. The final word, the one Edith never wrote, the one that was not in the letter because it was never Edith's to give.

We stood there in the humming lamplight with the unfinished sentence between us, and it did not feel like failure. That was the revelation, the one that rearranged me. It felt like a held breath. It felt like the last note of a chord that refuses to resolve until the whole room leans in. Edith had not left the sentence broken. She had left it *open* — open the way you leave a door open when you are watching from the kitchen to see whether the child will walk through it, whether they will come to you, whether they will choose the thing you cannot ask for.

"She couldn't write the last word," Rosalind said quietly. "Because it wasn't hers to write. It was Vera's."

"Or," I said, "because the last word can only exist once it's spoken to her. To her face. By us. The sentence isn't finished in the writing. It's finished in the delivering."

Rosalind looked at the shoebox. Then at me. And I watched her arrive at it — I watched the decision move through her like weather — and I knew what she was going to say before she said it, because we were, after all, our mother's children, and the paired future had finally found its speakers.

"We go to Vera," she said. "Both of us. And we read it to her. And we let her finish it."

"There's a care home two towns over and a woman who may or may not want to see us."

"There's a sentence that's been waiting fifty years," Rosalind said, "and I am not going to be the Thackeray who leaves it on read."

I laughed — really laughed, for the first time in what felt like years, the kind that surprises you by coming out of your own mouth — and she tried to look disapproving and failed within a second and a half, and for one moment we were not the damaged survivors of Edith's silences but simply a brother and sister at a table covered in index cards, planning a road trip.

Then she went still, the way she does, the full-stop stillness.

"Felix," she said. "There's one problem."

"Name it."

"We don't actually know what the last word is. We can't rehearse an ending we don't have. What if she asks us what we think it should be? What if she *makes* us say it?"

I looked at the green ink. I looked at my sister, who had spent her whole life as a tenant of other people's silences and was, I could see it now, standing on the threshold of owning the whole building.

"Then we say the truest thing we have," I said, "and we let her correct our translation. It's what she'd do. It's what they'd both do."

Rosalind picked up the letter, slid it back into its sleeve, and held it a moment longer than necessary.

"Two towns over," she said. "Saturday. I'll drive."

And that is how, three days later, I came to be sitting in my sister's car on a gray morning with a shoebox on my knees and fifty years of unsaid words in my lap, watching the city thin out into hedgerows, rehearsing a sentence neither of us could finish, toward a woman who had been asking every visitor the same question for years — *did she ever write?* — and who had no idea that the answer was finally, at long last, on its way.
