## 4. Ilse at the Bus Stop

The funeral had been three days ago and I still hadn't unpacked my suitcase. This was not grief, exactly—or it was grief in its most practical costume, the kind that looks like indecision about socks. My mother's house sat behind me on Alder Row with its windows dark, holding everything she'd owned and none of the explanation I wanted from any of it.

What I did instead was what I always do when a text refuses to yield: I went looking for the other witness.

Ilse. Gray hair cut short as a verdict, a coat too good for this town, hands that had held out toward my mother's coffin as if reaching across sixty years of water. *Varga*, she'd said, and I'd said *Kavanagh* like slamming a door, and she'd said *names come back* like someone who had all the time in the world and intended to use mine.

I asked around, which in a town this size means I stood in the bakery for eleven minutes and was told everything by a woman named Petra who remembered me primarily as "the Kavanagh girl who could read before the priest." The stranger—foreign accent, polite, paid cash—had been seen twice: once at the war memorial, once asking after bus schedules.

Bus schedules.

There is one bus line left that runs into the old quarter of town, where the streets narrow and the houses lean together like conspirators. Route 12. I had ridden it every day of my childhood, standing on the worn rubber of the back step because sitting felt like surrender, watching the town unspool backward into the neighborhood where my grandmother Marta had lived until I was nine. Next month, according to the notice stapled to the shelter's plexiglass, Route 12 would be rerouted along the ring road. The old terminus—the little loop by the shuttered bathhouse where drivers used to drink coffee from thermoses and pretend not to see us skipping fares—would close. *Due to declining ridership and infrastructure renewal.* The town had found a way to stop going there without ever admitting it had stopped.

So of course that was where Ilse would be. If you want to find where a family came from, you follow the last road that remembers.

* * *

The terminus at four in the afternoon in late October is a study in abandonment done tastefully. The shelter's plexiglass had gone amber with age, so the whole world through it looked preserved in resin. A bench. A timetable behind glass, sun-bleached except for the rerouting notice, which was crisp and white and aggressive, like a tooth in an old mouth. Beyond the loop, the street ran downhill between facades—some restored, some bricked over, one entire row wearing scaffolding like a bandage—and at the bottom, just visible, the green copper roof of the bathhouse, oxidized to the color of old patience.

She was already there. Of course she was. Ilse sat on the bench with her ankles crossed and a canvas bag beside her, reading the timetable the way scholars read fragments—patiently, expecting gaps.

"You're following me," she said, without looking up.

"I'm waiting for a bus."

"Are you."

"There's a schedule." I gestured at the glass. "It's public infrastructure."

"Mm." She looked up then, and there was no triumph in her face, which somehow made it worse. Just a kind of level attention, the look of someone who has decided not to be in a hurry with you. "The next one is at ten past four. It goes to the old quarter and comes back empty. I checked yesterday. Nobody rides it anymore except," she glanced at me, "apparently, people who are waiting for buses."

I sat down at the far end of the bench, leaving a gap wide enough to land a small aircraft in. "You said something at the funeral."

"I said several things. Most of them were condolences."

"You called us Varga."

"I did." She folded her hands. "And you corrected me so quickly. Like catching a plate before it falls. Five years I've been coming to understand your family, Mrs.—Kavanagh—and you defend the name faster than you defend the dead woman lying right there."

"My mother," I said, "was a Kavanagh her whole life."

"No," Ilse said gently, "she was a Kavanagh for fifty years. Before that she was a Varga for nine. Children don't get to choose what gets erased first."

The 4:10 arrived. It was one of those single-bendy buses built for crowds that no longer existed, and it pulled into the loop with the sigh of a machine performing a duty it no longer believed in. The driver—a young man with the resigned posture of someone who had drawn the short route—opened the door and looked at us both with mild surprise, the way zookeepers look when an exhibit starts moving.

We got on. I want to be clear that I had decided, rationally, to get on, for reasons related to my mother's house and unfinished business and absolutely nothing to do with the word *Varga* still hanging in the cold air between two women on a bench. Ilse paid for both of us before I could object, dropping coins into the tray with the efficiency of someone who had studied the fare system from abroad.

"Two?" the driver said.

"She's with me," Ilse said, and sat down across from me, facing backward, which struck me as either an accident or a philosophy.

* * *

The bus went downhill. That was the whole journey, more or less—downhill, through streets whose names I realized I had never once spoken aloud as an adult. Vaszary Street. The lane behind the tannery, which no longer smelled of anything but rain. The corner where Fekete's shop had been, now a phone repair place with a hand-lettered sign promising FAST SCREENS.

"You're staring," Ilse said.

"I'm looking out the window."

"At what?"

"Streets."

"Whose streets?"

I turned from the window. Up close, in the flat light of the bus, she looked tired in a way that wasn't about sleep. There were lines around her eyes that seemed carved rather than earned, the kind of face that had spent decades holding an expression and finally set it down.

"Why does it matter to you," I said, "where we lived?"

"Where *they* lived," she corrected. "Your grandmother and mine. Same house, if the records are right. Same courtyard, same well, same winter." She smoothed her coat over her knees. "I'm here for six days. I flew eleven hours. My husband thinks I've come to visit a colleague in Prague, which is technically adjacent to the truth. And tomorrow morning I will stand in front of a building or a parking lot or whatever they've put where number fourteen used to be, and I will have seen where they lived, and then I can go home."

"And then what?"

"And then nothing." She said it simply, without self-pity, which was somehow the most unsettling available option. "That's the part nobody warns you about, chasing a story down to its root. You dig all the way to the bottom and there's just—dirt. Ordinary dirt. But you can't *not* know it. Can you?"

I could think of several things I had successfully not known for five years and told her so with my silence.

The bus jolted over the tram tracks—disused since before I was born, but the town had never paved them over; they lay in the asphalt like healed fractures. Outside, the neighborhood assembled itself piece by piece: the school with its new plastic windows set into old stone, the courtyard entrances with their iron gates painted municipal green, the bathhouse sliding past with its boarded windows and its roof gone fully green, a patient oxidizing heart.

"That one," Ilse said suddenly, leaning forward. "The bathhouse. Anna—" she caught herself, and I watched her do it, watched the name go back behind her teeth like a coin returned to a pocket. "My grandmother swam there. In the thirties. She said the water came up from a spring under the hill and it was warm even in February, and that your grandmother—"

She stopped.

"Go on," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended.

"I don't actually know how the sentence ends," Ilse admitted. "That's why I'm here. My grandmother told stories the way you'd deal cards facedown. *Marta and I*, and then nothing. Every photograph from before the war, she kept in a biscuit tin, and every photograph had the same two girls in it, and whenever I asked who the other girl was, she'd say *a cousin*, in a voice that closed like a shop gate." She looked at me. "You know that voice. Don't tell me you don't. Your grandmother taught it to you herself—I saw it at the graveside. You wear it like a coat."

The bus slowed for the terminus loop. Nobody else got on or off. The driver drank from a thermos with the serenity of a man who had long ago stopped expecting his route to mean anything.

"I'm getting off here," I said, though I had nowhere to be.

"So am I," said Ilse.

* * *

The loop by the bathhouse smelled of wet leaves and cold stone. We stood on the pavement while the bus turned around—it took a full minute, the whole length of the loop, a small ceremonial orbit—and pulled away uphill, leaving us the only living things on the street besides a crow with strong opinions.

Number fourteen was gone. In its place: a gravel lot behind a chain-link fence, weeds coming up through the gravel in defiant rows, and a sign announcing FUTURE PHASE II RESIDENTIAL DEVELOPMENT with the optimism of documents that will outlive their authors.

Ilse stood at the fence for a long time. Her hand came up and rested on the chain-link, fingers spread, and I understood—with reluctance, with actual physical reluctance, like swallowing something too large—that she was touching a wall that had been inside a house that had been inside her family, and that some circuit was completing somewhere in her that had been open for seventy years.

"They lived on the second floor," she said quietly. "Anna's room faced the well. Marta's faced the street. They could signal each other with lamps if one of them was awake and the other wasn't supposed to be." She turned to me. "Do you know that? Did anyone tell you that?"

"No."

"Did anyone tell you *anything*?"

"My grandmother taught me words," I said. "Foods. Songs. How to curse beautifully, which I'm told is rare in a child of seven. She didn't teach me history. History was—" I searched for it. "History was the thing that happened to other families. Ours began in 1953, apparently, with good honest work and no past whatsoever."

Ilse laughed. It startled both of us. It was a real laugh, brief and rusty, and it cracked her carved face open into something younger.

"*No past whatsoever,*" she repeated. "God. Mine was the opposite. Nothing *but* past. I grew up inside a grievance like a weather system. Anna died when I was twenty-two and I inherited a biscuit tin and a grudge, and I have carried both around Europe for forty years, and I could not tell you, honestly, which weighed more." She looked at the gravel lot again. "And then three months ago I found a record. A census page. Two sisters, same house, same parents, and one of them—"

"Don't," I said.

"—one of them grew up to become your—"

"I said don't." My voice came out harder than the moment deserved, and I heard it happen, heard myself reach for the shop-gate voice, the closing voice, the inheritance. "You don't get to come here with eleven hours of flight and a biscuit tin and rearrange my dead to fit yours. Whatever you think you found, whatever story you've built across the water—you don't know this family."

"No," Ilse agreed. "I don't. That's rather the point. Neither, I suspect, do you."

A wind came down the hill, pushing leaves along the pavement in little delegations. Somewhere above us a window opened and a radio escaped briefly—a weather report, cheerful about rain—then shut again.

"The photographs," I said, before I could stop myself. "In the tin box. Under the floorboards of my mother's house. There are two girls. Identical dresses. Nineteen thirty-eight, written on the back in pencil."

Ilse went very still. Stillness, I was beginning to learn, ran in both our lines.

"Identical dresses," she repeated.

"Sewn from the same cloth. Someone wrote on the back—my grandmother's handwriting, I think, though I only half-read the script. It's the old way of writing. I can make out maybe half the letters anymore." I heard myself continuing, helpless, the silence developing a leak. "There are letters too. Dozens. Sealed. In a script I can't properly read, and I am, for what it's worth, a professional translator, which makes it worse, not better."

"What does the writing on the photograph say?" Ilse asked. "The nineteen thirty-eight one. Can you read that much?"

I could. I had read it forty times since finding the box, standing in my mother's kitchen at two in the morning with the floorboard still open behind me. Two words. A name and a word.

"*Marta es Anna*," I said. "Marta and Anna. And underneath, smaller: *ketto testver*. Two sisters."

The crow said something rude. Down at the end of the street, the copper roof of the bathhouse held the last of the light.

"My grandmother's name was Anna," Ilse said, very quietly, in the tone of a person setting down something heavy after forty years and discovering their arms remember how to be empty.

"I know," I said. "I worked that out on the bench."

Neither of us moved. The rerouting notice fluttered in its frame across the street, white and crisp, counting down.

"There's a letter in the box," I heard myself say, "that I can almost read. Almost. Maybe a third. And it's addressed to Marta, and it's signed A., and it's dated 1947, and I have been standing in my mother's kitchen for three nights trying to decide whether to burn it or—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "Whether to keep pretending I don't need help."

Ilse looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached into her canvas bag and produced, of all things, a magnifying glass, a notebook, and a pencil sharpened at both ends, and laid them on top of the chain-link fence post like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"I have six days," she said. "A return ticket, a hotel by the station, and a husband in Prague-adjacent ignorance. And I have spent forty years reading the handwriting of a woman who wrote her whole life in code." She nodded at the direction of my mother's house, uphill, behind us, holding its floorboards and its tin and its sealed mouths. "Show me the letters, Sebastian Kavanagh. Or should I say—"

"Don't push it," I said.

Her mouth twitched. "Varga."

The word landed in the cold air between us and, for the first time since the graveside, I didn't catch it. I let it fall. It sounded, I noticed with something like vertigo, like a key turning in a lock I had been told was decorative.

We walked uphill together, two women who shared nothing but a surname and a silence, toward a house with an open floor in it. Behind us the loop stood empty, waiting for a bus that would run nine more times before the town forgot the way.
