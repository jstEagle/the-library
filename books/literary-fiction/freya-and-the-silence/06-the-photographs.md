## 6. The Photographs

The café on Kossuth Square had been a bank when Sebastian was a child, and before that—according to her mother, who claimed to remember everything and remembered selectively—a bathhouse where the town's midwives soaked their tired feet. Now it was a café with mismatched chairs and a menu laminated in three languages, none of them the one Sebastian had grown up speaking. She chose it because it was neutral ground. Neutral in the way that a demilitarized zone is neutral.

Ilse was already there when Sebastian arrived, ten minutes early herself, seated at a table by the window with her hands folded around a cup of tea she hadn't touched. She looked up, and for a moment neither woman moved. Then Ilse gestured at the chair across from her with the small, formal politeness of someone raised by people who believed manners were load-bearing.

"You came," Ilse said.

"You sound surprised."

"I am. I'd have given it fifty-fifty." She said it without rancor, almost cheerfully, and something in Sebastian's chest loosened half a degree. There was, she was discovering, something restful about a person who admitted to odds.

Sebastian sat. A waiter materialized and hovered until she ordered coffee she didn't want, because refusing felt like leaving the table before the game had started. Outside, the square did its slow Tuesday business: pigeons, a man selling chestnuts from a cart, the clock on the town hall running four minutes fast as it had run four minutes fast for her entire life, so that everyone in this town had been gently lied to about time for eighty years.

"May I see them?" Ilse asked.

Sebastian had brought the photographs in a padded envelope, the kind her mother used for important documents, which meant the envelope itself smelled faintly of the house—of dust and lavender and the particular staleness of rooms closed against winter. She laid the photographs out on the table between them like a card hand, face down first, an instinct she didn't examine.

"I found forty-one," she said. "In the tin box. Under the floorboards in my mother's bedroom, if you can believe it. Under the *floorboards*. Like we were hiding from the Gestapo instead of from our own history."

"People hide from both," Ilse said quietly. "Often at once."

Sebastian turned over the first photograph.

It was the one she'd stared at longest the night before, propped against the lamp on her hotel nightstand while the town outside went dark window by window. Two girls, standing side by side on what appeared to be a porch—the railing behind them painted white, though time had eaten the paint down to gray bones. They were perhaps eight years old. Identical dresses: dark cotton, white collars, a row of small buttons each, the kind of dress that got handed down or sewn twice from the same pattern because fabric cost money and sisters were the same size. Their hair was braided in identical crowns. Even their expressions were near-identical—not smiling, not solemn, but wearing the particular guarded patience of children made to stand still for a camera, back when a photograph was an event you dressed for and endured.

One girl stood very straight, chin lifted, eyes fixed slightly above the lens as if looking past the photographer toward something more interesting. The other leaned a fraction toward her sister, shoulder almost touching shoulder, her gaze aimed directly into the camera with an intensity that crossed eighty-six years and landed somewhere under Sebastian's ribs.

"That's my grandmother," Sebastian said, touching the straight-backed girl. "Marta."

"And the other one—" Ilse's voice did something complicated, a word starting and being rebuilt midway, "—that's Anna. My grandmother. Anna Varga."

The name sat on the table between the coffee cups. Sebastian let it sit. She had spent five years practicing silence; she could hold a silence now the way a mason holds a level, perfectly, indefinitely. But this silence had a different texture. It wasn't emptiness. It was full of the word *Varga*, ringing.

"They look alike," Sebastian said finally. It was inadequate and she knew it.

"They were mistaken for twins all their lives," Ilse said. "They weren't. Fourteen months apart. Anna was younger." She reached out but didn't touch the photograph; her finger traced a line in the air above it, following the braid. "My grandmother kept a copy of this same picture. In a frame on her dresser, in Vienna, for sixty years. When I was a child I thought the two girls were her and her friend. She never said otherwise. She never said anything about it at all, actually. If you asked, she'd turn the frame face-down and change the subject to whether you'd eaten."

"Mine turned the subject to whether I'd *practiced*," Sebastian said, and heard the laugh escape before she could vet it—short, surprised out of her. "The old language. She taught me. Words for weather, words for bread, words for scolding. And there was exactly one word forbidden."

"Let me guess." Ilse's eyes came up. "She forbade the family name."

The coffee arrived. The waiter set it down, registered the spread of old photographs, the two women leaning over them like conspirators, and retreated with the discretion of a man who had seen many things on Kossuth Square and judged none of them.

"How did you know?" Sebastian asked.

"Because mine did too. Except inverted." Ilse wrapped both hands around her teacup again, though it must have gone cold long ago. "Anna never spoke Marta's name either. Not once in my childhood. When my mother mentioned her aunt—the one who survived, the one who went west and married and became somebody else entirely—Anna would leave the room. Not dramatically. She'd just... be elsewhere. You'd turn around and the chair was empty and you could hear water running in the kitchen, though nothing needed washing." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, warm and wounded at once. "I grew up thinking my grandmother was afraid of plumbing."

Sebastian laughed again—she hadn't laughed this much in a single morning since before her father died—and then felt guilty for it, and then decided, deliberately, not to. Guilt could wait its turn. The queue for guilt in her chest was already long.

* * *

They worked through the photographs in order, or what they guessed was order, laying them out across the table until the waiter began giving them the look reserved for customers colonizing territory. The pictures told a story in fragments, the way stories from that part of the world always did: whole years missing, whole people missing, a wedding where the groom's face had been scratched out with a pin by some ancestor with strong feelings, a christening, a harvest, a funeral where every adult wore black and every child looked bewildered by the formality of grief.

"This is 1936," Ilse said, turning a photograph to read the pencil inscription on the back. Her handwriting-reading was better than Sebastian's; the old script slanted and doubled letters in ways that defeated Sebastian's eye, though she could feel the meanings hovering just beyond reach, like fish visible under ice. "*Ágnes és a lányok*—Ágnes and the girls. That would be their mother. Your great-grandmother."

"I have no idea what happened to Ágnes," Sebastian admitted. "I don't even know her name existed until right now."

"Neither did I, honestly. My mother has a family Bible with pages torn out. Torn *out*, cleanly, with a ruler, I'd swear. Somebody curated us." Ilse laid the photograph beside another one, matching faces. "Here—same girl, taller. This is 1938, look at the date on the back. The last summer. After this they're teenagers, and after that..."

"After that is the war," Sebastian said.

"After that is the war."

There was a gap in the record then, and both women felt it the way you feel a missing stair in the dark. Photographs resumed in 1947—one girl, older, thinner, standing alone in front of a house Sebastian recognized with a jolt: it was the house on Veres Street, the one Route 12 still climbed toward, or had climbed toward, until next month. Her grandmother stood on those steps at maybe nineteen, and she was not looking above the camera anymore. She was looking directly into it, and her expression was the one Sebastian knew from mirrors on bad days: the face of a person who has agreed to continue.

"There's no Anna," Sebastian said slowly. She flipped through the remaining photographs, faster now, then forced herself to slow down and check properly, because rushing was how you missed things and she had built a career on not missing things. "Not in any of them. Not after '38."

"No."

"She's just—gone. From the record. From the pictures. From everything."

"Yes," Ilse said. "That's rather the shape of it, isn't it."

Sebastian set the photographs down. Her hands were steady—that was the translator's training, the performer's discipline, the body keeping its composure while the interior rearranged itself—but she felt a vertigo open beneath her, gentle and enormous, like standing at the edge of a stairwell in a building you thought you knew. Because here was the thing she couldn't stop noticing: she had loved Marta. Loved her with the whole uncomplicated devotion of a child for the one adult who gave her a secret language, who taught her that words could be held close like warm stones. And that woman had apparently amputated an entire sister from existence—no photographs, no name spoken, no grave visited, no story told—and had raised Sebastian inside the clean room of that erasure, and Sebastian had never once, in all her childhood, thought to ask: *Grandmother, who else lives in this language? Who else did these dresses belong to?*

"We inherit secrets like furniture," she said aloud. "You grow up around them so long you stop seeing them. You put your cup down on them."

"My mother inherited bitterness instead," Ilse said. "Also furniture, technically. Also hard to move."

* * *

By late afternoon they had arranged the photographs into a rough chronology across three pushed-together tables, and the waiter had stopped pretending they might order lunch, and the light through the window had gone the color of weak honey. Sebastian had learned things she didn't yet know what to do with: that the girls' father had been a railway clerk; that the family had kept goats, improbably, on a street where goats were frowned upon; that Anna, according to a 1939 school certificate, had won a prize for recitation. Poetry. The sister who vanished had won a prize for poetry, and Sebastian sat with that fact for a long moment, turning it over, feeling its weight.

"What happened to her?" she asked at last. "After. Do you know?"

"I know what my grandmother knew, which is to say almost nothing and all of it wrong-shaped." Ilse gathered her own small stack of photographs—she had brought three, brought across an ocean, carried in a wallet worn soft as cloth. "Anna was taken east in '44. Or hid, and was taken. Or fled, and was taken. The versions disagree. What's certain is that she survived—there's a Red Cross record, 1947, a query filed by Anna Varga looking for relatives. And there's..." Ilse hesitated, and Sebastian watched her decide to say the thing, watched the decision happen in real time, a woman choosing truth over comfort with the weary efficiency of long practice. "There are letters. Letters Anna wrote to Marta. After the war. For years. My mother has them. Or had them—I have them now, in my suitcase at the hotel, because I couldn't make myself leave them home and I couldn't make myself tell you about them yesterday."

Sebastian went very still.

Letters. Postwar. Unanswered—she knew they were unanswered the way you know a wound is yours, instantly, without evidence, because she had grown up inside the silence of a woman who kept a locked tin box under her daughter's floorboards and taught her granddaughter every beautiful word except the ones that mattered.

"I have letters too," Sebastian said. Her voice came out lower than intended. "In the box. I can't read most of them. The script defeats me. I've been telling myself I'll find a scholar, an archivist, someone official, anyone except—" She stopped.

"Except me," Ilse said.

"Except the one person whose grandmother wrote them."

The clock on the town hall struck six, four minutes fast, lying to the whole square as usual. Somewhere behind them the espresso machine hissed. On the table between them lay two girls in identical dresses, standing shoulder to shoulder in the last summer anyone photographed them together, looking out at their granddaughters with expressions neither woman could fully read and neither could stop trying to.

Ilse reached into her bag and drew out an envelope, old, soft-cornered, addressed in a slanting hand. She set it beside the 1938 photograph, next to the girl who leaned toward her sister.

"Then we read them together," Ilse said. "All of them. Whatever they say."

Sebastian looked at the envelope, and felt the vertigo tilt again—not falling this time, she realized, but leaning. The way the younger girl leaned, in the photograph. Toward.

"Tomorrow," Sebastian said. "My hotel. I'll bring the box."

And she was already dreading it, and already unable to wait.
