## 1. Expansion Joints

The Harrow Bridge woke before Beatrix did, the way it always did, groaning in its sleep as the first delivery trucks came off the ring road and pressed their weight into its spine. He had been listening to that sound for twenty years—longer than his marriage had lasted in any meaningful sense, though he would never have said so aloud—and he knew its vocabulary the way other men knew their wives' breathing. A low complaint from the north abutment meant cold settling into the concrete overnight. A higher note, almost musical, near the third pier, meant the bearing pads were doing what they were designed to do: yielding, absorbing, forgiving.

Forgiveness was the bridge's whole profession, when you thought about it. Everything built across a gap is an argument for forgiveness.

Beatrix Winterbourne stood at the base of the eastern stairwell at 6:40 in the morning with a thermos of coffee he wouldn't drink until it went cold and a canvas bag holding his caliper, his feeler gauges, his clipboard, and a pencil worn down to a stub because he refused, on principle he could no longer articulate, to switch to pens. The morning was the color of dishwater, October pressing its grey thumb down on the river valley, and the water below moved with the slow brown patience of a river that had outlasted every argument ever conducted on its banks.

He climbed.

The stairs were his alone at this hour. The maintenance crews didn't start until seven, the joggers later than that, and the gulls kept their own counsel. His boots rang on the steel treads—a sound he had stopped hearing years ago, the way you stop hearing your own name—and by the time he reached the deck level his breath was coming in white plumes and his shoulders had loosened into the particular set they took only here, forty meters above the water, where the world reduced itself to tolerances.

He began at Joint One, as he always did, kneeling on the rubber mat he'd brought himself because the city wouldn't authorize the expense and because kneeling directly on cold steel at his age cost him a full day of complaining knees. The expansion joint ran across the full width of the carriageway like a seam in a garment, a finger-width gap between two sections of deck, filled with a neoprene sealant that was supposed to compress in summer heat and stretch in winter cold and ask nothing of anyone in between.

He set the caliper against the joint's northern edge and read it.

Fourteen point six millimeters.

He wrote it down. The date, the joint number, the reading, the ambient temperature—eleven degrees Celsius, taken from the gauge clipped inside his jacket pocket, because steel lied about temperature and he had learned never to trust a structure's own account of itself.

*Steel lies,* he thought, and then, without meaning to, without wanting to: *So do husbands.*

The thought arrived the way these thoughts always arrived—not as a revelation but as a kind of weather, moving through him and out again, leaving nothing changed behind it. Twenty years of readings lived in the filing cabinets of his office, in green ledger books going back to his first year with the firm, and if you laid them end to end you could watch the joints of the Harrow Bridge drift, millimeter by millimeter, season by season, the whole structure easing apart at a rate so slow no single day could detect it. Two millimeters a year, some joints. Three, if the winter was cruel. Nothing you'd notice standing on the deck. Everything you'd notice after twenty years.

Marguerite had once asked him—he couldn't remember when, some anniversary or other, the wine half-finished, her hands folded around her glass in that way she had, like a woman holding something small and alive—what he actually *did* all day. And he had tried to explain expansion joints to her, the way a bridge is not one thing but many things held in a controlled disagreement, and she had listened with her head tilted, and then she had said:

"So you measure how far apart things have grown."

And he had laughed, because it was funny, and she had not laughed, and the moment had closed over them like water over a dropped stone, and neither of them had gone back for it.

That was nineteen years ago. Freya had been seven. She'd been at the table drawing, that night—she used to draw everything, bridges and dogs and the neighbor's car and, once, inexplicably, a wardrobe with a light coming out of it—and she had looked up when her mother said it, with the terrible directness of children, and said, "Daddy measures gaps?"

"I measure whether the gaps are safe," he'd told her.

"And if they're not safe?"

"Then we fix them."

She had nodded, satisfied, and gone back to her drawing, and Beatrix had felt—for just a moment, a moment he had spent two decades trying not to remember—the sensation of being seen through, the way x-rays see through skin to the honest architecture beneath. Children did that. Children asked what the sentence underneath the sentence was. Then they grew up and learned to stop asking, and the family became a structure with all its joints sealed over, load-bearing silence everywhere you looked, and everyone called it stability.

He moved to Joint Two.

The caliper opened and closed in his hands with a small machined precision that he loved without embarrassment. It was the only object in his life that did exactly what it was told. You set it against a gap and it told you the truth of the gap, in increments of a tenth of a millimeter, and it never softened the number, never rounded it toward kindness, never said *it's fine, don't worry, I'm sure it means nothing.* Fourteen point nine. He wrote it down. The joint had gained three-tenths since spring. Within tolerance. Everything within tolerance. The word *tolerance* sat in his mouth like a coin.

Below him the river carried a barge downstream, low in the water, its wake unrolling in long grey V's. A man on the deck of the barge smoked a cigarette and looked at nothing. Beatrix watched him pass under the bridge and out the far side and thought: *that man has no idea anyone is above him, measuring.* That was the arrangement. The invisible men stood on top of the visible world and kept it from falling, and in exchange the visible world agreed never to look up.

He had written a song about that once.

The thought surfaced and he pushed it down with the practiced motion of a man who had been pushing down the same thought for twenty years, smooth as closing a drawer, and for a moment—one bad moment—he let himself hear the first four bars of it anyway, the melody that came to him on this bridge in another October, humming through his teeth while the caliper hung forgotten in his hand, the melody he had carried home that night and written into a notebook and then hidden in a place where no reasonable person would ever—

He checked Joint Three instead.

* * *

By eight the joggers had started, and by nine the maintenance crew, and Beatrix worked the western span with the methodical rhythm of a man who had turned routine into a kind of anesthesia. Kneel. Measure. Record. Move. Kneel. Measure. Record. The crew foreman, a broad-shouldered woman named Okafor who had worked the Harrow for eleven years and trusted Beatrix more than she trusted her own instruments, waved to him from the access road and shouted something about the coffee truck, and he raised a hand without processing the words, and she laughed and went on.

At Joint Nine he found something.

It was nothing—or it was the beginning of nothing, which was worse. A hairline in the deck concrete radiating from the joint's anchor point, fine as a hair pulled from a dark head, running maybe eighty millimeters before it vanished into the texture of the aggregate. He crouched over it for a long time. Other inspectors would have logged it as surface crazing, cosmetic, the kind of thing concrete did the way skin wrinkled. But Beatrix had spent twenty years learning the difference between a crack that was tired and a crack that was lying, and this one had a direction. This one was pointed at something.

He measured it. Width: zero point four millimeters. Length: eighty-two. He photographed it twice, once with the scale card beside it, once without, and wrote the location coordinates in his ledger with the stub of his pencil, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

"You find something?" Okafor, beside him now, coffee in each hand, offering one. He took it. It was already lukewarm. He drank it anyway.

"Possibly," he said.

"Possibly." She snorted. "You're the only man I know who uses that word to mean yes."

"It means the data isn't complete."

"The data's never complete, Beatrix. That's why they pay us. Somebody's got to make the call." She looked at the hairline herself, tilting her head the way Marguerite tilted hers, and he felt the resemblance land somewhere below his ribs. "Looks like crazing to me."

"Maybe," he said.

But he marked the joint with orange survey paint, a small neat X, and in the ledger, in the column reserved for follow-up, he wrote: *monitor. re-inspect 30 days.*

Thirty days. He had been monitoring things for thirty days at a time for twenty years. Monitor the joint. Re-inspect in spring. Watch the drift, log the drift, file the drift in a green book that nobody read, and tell yourself that watching closely was the same as acting, that measuring a distance was the same as crossing it.

Freya used to stand on this bridge with him, when she was small, palms flat on the railing, and ask him questions he couldn't answer. Not engineering questions—those he could answer in his sleep. The other kind. *Why does the bridge move if it's not alive? Why do they build it so it can't stay still? Isn't it scared?* And he had given her the textbook answers, thermal expansion, load distribution, the physics of allowance, and she had accepted them the way children accept answers, provisionally, with the real question banked somewhere behind the eyes for later.

Later had come. Later was eight months of telephone silence, living across the river in a flat he had never seen, working nights at a bar whose name he knew only from a delivery receipt Marguerite had found in the recycling. Eight months. He had measured that too, helplessly, the way he measured everything—eight months, roughly two hundred and forty days, a drift of some kind that no instrument in his canvas bag could read.

He finished the western span at noon, ate the sandwich Marguerite had left wrapped on the counter—mustard, no butter, exactly as he liked, a fidelity expressed entirely through condiments—and started the underside inspection at one o'clock, harnessed and clipped to the gantry rail, lowering himself into the cathedral of steel beneath the deck.

This was the part of the bridge no one saw. The public knew the Harrow as its silhouette, its graceful arc against the sky, the postcard view. Underneath was the truth of it: rivets and gusset plates and cross-bracing, decades of pigeon droppings and rust bloom and old repair welds, the whole secret skeleton doing its work in the dark. He moved along the lower chord with his flashlight, tapping plates with his inspection hammer, listening. Sound steel rang clean. Compromised steel sounded dull, apologetic, a note that died too soon.

Everything down here sounded fine today. He was almost disappointed. There was a comfort in finding damage—it gave the day a shape, gave his hands a job beyond recording the slow, legal, sanctioned drift of a structure coming apart at a rate no one could feel.

Halfway along the third pier he stopped, hung in his harness above the brown water, and let himself think the thought he'd been outrunning since dawn.

Someone had opened the wardrobe.

Not literally—he hadn't been home yet, hadn't seen—but he knew it the way he knew a dull note in steel, by vibration, by the wrongness in the morning's frequency. Three days ago the song had played on the radio. *Winter Light.* His melody, note for note, the exact voicing, even the mistake in the second verse where he'd written a B natural where the harmony wanted a B flat and had loved the error too much to fix it. On the radio. Credited to a man named Callum Reyes.

There was exactly one copy of that song outside his own head. It lived on a reel-to-reel tape in a cardboard box, in a wardrobe, in Freya's room, in a house where the door of that room had stayed closed for four years like a joint sealed over with neoprene.

Either Marguerite had finally opened it, or Freya had come home.

Or someone else had. Someone who belonged to Freya's new life across the river, some man named Callum Reyes who sang Beatrix's confession in his own voice on the drive-time slot of a regional station while Beatrix knelt on a bridge measuring how far apart things had grown.

His hands, hanging in the harness, were perfectly steady. That was the horror of it, he thought. Twenty years of practice had made his hands steady under any conditions whatsoever. Grief, terror, joy—if joy still came—they registered nowhere in the instruments. He was a man calibrated to within a tenth of a millimeter, and there was no unit for this.

He finished the underside inspection. He packed his bag. At the top of the eastern stairwell he paused, as he always did, with one hand on the cold railing, and looked back down the length of the bridge—the deck stretching away into the grey afternoon, the joints ticking softly as the temperature shifted, the whole patient structure allowing itself its millimeters, its lawful drift, its slow divergence from the shape it was drawn in.

*Two millimeters a year,* he thought. *Twenty years. Do the arithmetic.*

He didn't. He knew the answer the way you know a scar.

Then he walked down the stairs, past Joint One, past the orange X he'd painted at Joint Nine, and out into the parking gravel where his van sat waiting, and drove home through streets he had traveled ten thousand times, toward a house he had measured for twenty years without once stepping inside its true dimensions—toward the closed door at the end of the upstairs hall, and the wardrobe behind it, and whatever he was going to find, or fail to find, when he finally opened it tonight.
