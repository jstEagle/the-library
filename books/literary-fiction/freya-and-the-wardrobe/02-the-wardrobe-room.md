## 2. The Wardrobe Room

The house told him before he was through the door. Beatrix Pemberton had learned to read a house the way he read a bridge—the small telltales, the deflections invisible to anyone not trained to look. Marguerite's coat was gone from its hook. Her keys were gone from the ceramic bowl by the telephone, the bowl Freya had made in the fourth grade, lopsided and glazed the color of pond water, which Marguerite had refused to replace for twenty-two years and would refuse for twenty-two more. The kitchen smelled of the coffee she'd drunk standing up, alone, at six-forty in the morning, and there was a note on the counter in her small, ruled hand—the hand of a woman who had once wanted to be an architect and had settled for making lists.

*At Celia's. Back Sunday. There's soup.*

He stood in the kitchen holding the note the way he held a load calculation: carefully, with both hands, as though it might bear more weight than it appeared to.

*At Celia's. Back Sunday.* Four times this month. He did not keep a ledger the way Marguerite did—she had her accounts, he knew, though he'd never seen them, the way he knew the river was rising under the bridge without ever putting a gauge in it—but he kept a count of a different kind. Nights. Absences measured in the dust on the passenger side of her car, in the second toothbrush drying untouched in the cup, in the way the house had gone quiet in a register he could only describe as structural. Not silence, exactly. Silence was an absence of sound. This was an absence of something that had once been sound and was now only the space where it had been.

He put the note down. He washed his coffee mug, though he didn't want coffee. This was the ritual: come home, find the house empty, perform the small domestic pantomime of a man who lived there, and then—when the pantomime was done, when the mug was dry and the lights were on and the house had been made to look inhabited—go upstairs.

He went upstairs.

* * *

Freya's room was at the end of the hall, past the bathroom with its warped door, past the linen closet where Marguerite kept the towels folded in thirds the way her mother had. The door was white paint over older white paint over older white paint still, and it stuck at the bottom the way it had always stuck, and Beatrix lifted it slightly as he pushed it open, a motion so worn into his body that he did it without thinking, the way he still reached for the light switch in a house he'd sold eleven years ago.

The room was exactly as she had left it. That was the lie he told himself. In truth it was exactly as *he* had left it, the week after she drove across the river with everything she owned in a hatchback and did not look back, and he had come up here with a dust cloth and stood in the doorway for forty minutes and then gone back downstairs and made a sandwich he did not eat.

The bed was made with the quilt Marguerite's mother had sewn, faded now to the color of weak tea. The desk still had the pencil sharpener bolted to its corner. On the windowsill, a row of glass animals—a horse with a chipped ear, a whale, a fox—caught the late light and threw it back in small, patient colors. The wallpaper was yellow roses, chosen by Marguerite when Freya was expected, cheerful and relentless, the pattern of a woman who believed rooms could be made happy by force.

And against the far wall, under the window, stood the wardrobe.

It had come with the house, an old pine thing too heavy to move, with a cornice like the top of a tomb and two doors that opened with a sound like a held breath being let go. They had never been able to give it away. Marguerite had wanted to paint it white; Beatrix had said the wood was good; and so it had stayed, dark and stubborn, and at some point in the years when Freya was small it had stopped being furniture and become something else. A place. His place. The one room inside the room.

He crossed the carpet—still the blue carpet with the faded constellation pattern, Orion worn to almost nothing where her feet had landed every morning—and stood in front of the wardrobe with his hand on the left door, and did what he always did, which was nothing, for a while. Just stood. A man in a doorway of his own making.

Twenty years. He had been coming to this room for twenty years, and every time there was a moment—a long one, sometimes a minute, sometimes five—when he did not open it. When he stood with his hand flat against the pine and felt the coolness of it and thought: *today I will take it downstairs. Today I will put the tapes on the kitchen table and say to Marguerite, this is what I was doing all those evenings in the garage, this is why I learned the mixing board, this is the thing I could not say so I sang it instead into a microphone in a room where no one could hear.* And every time, the moment passed, the way a load passes through a structure into the earth, distributed, dispersed, harmless. He was very good at distributing loads.

He opened the doors.

* * *

The smell came first, the way it always did: pine resin and old paper and the faint chemical sweetness of magnetic tape, a smell that was, for Beatrix, the smell of the person he might have been. Inside, the wardrobe had been fitted out with a carpenter's care—his own care, one winter of Sundays with a jigsaw and a hand plane, work he'd done in the garage and carried up in pieces so Marguerite wouldn't ask. Shelves on the left. On the right, a deep drawer he'd built to hold the reel-to-reel tapes, each one in its box, each box labeled in his small ruled hand—*Winter Light, take 3*; *For M., unfinished*; *Lullaby (don't)*—the dates running backward through time like tree rings, 1999 at the front and 1987 at the back, the year Freya was born, the year everything began.

He took the front box out. *Winter Light, take 3.* He knew what was on it the way he knew the load rating of the Harrow Bridge span: absolutely, uselessly, without needing to check. Four minutes and eleven seconds. One voice, one guitar, one mistake in the second verse that he'd kept because the mistake was true and the rest of it was only pretty. He had written it in the hospital corridor the night Freya came into the world, three weeks early, blue at first, then pink, then screaming, and he had stood in the corridor holding his own hands and understood, with a clarity that had frightened him, that he was never going to be able to tell anyone what he felt, that the feeling was too large for his mouth, that it would come out wrong or come out small, and so he had done the only thing his hands knew how to do besides measuring: he had written it down. And then he had hidden it. He had hidden it the way you hide anything precious and shameful, in the one place no one would look, because the one place no one would look was the room of a child who had no interest in her father's secrets.

She had been interested once. That was the thing that sat in his chest now, heavy as a span bearing down. She had been seven, maybe eight, and he had come up here one evening and found her sitting on the floor of the open wardrobe with a tape box in her lap, turning it over, reading the label, and she had looked up at him with Marguerite's gray eyes and said, *Daddy, what's Winter Light?* And he had said—God, he had said—*Old paperwork. Work thing.* And she had nodded, seven years old and already learning the family language, already learning that some doors in this house opened onto nothing, and she had put the box back on the shelf with both hands, carefully, the way he handled evidence, and closed the doors, and never asked again.

He had told himself for years that she'd forgotten. He told himself a lot of things.

Beatrix sat down on the edge of the bed—the bed where his daughter had slept for eighteen years, the quilt cool under his hand—and held the tape box on his knees. Outside the window the light was going amber and then copper, the way it did in October, and a car went past on the street below with its radio audible for a moment, some pop song, a woman's voice, and was gone.

He talked to her. That was the other part of the ritual, the part he would have denied under oath. He talked to the tapes, or to the room, or to the girl in the photographs on the desk—Freya at nine with a gap in her teeth, Freya at sixteen scowling at a chemistry book, Freya at twenty-two on graduation day, standing slightly apart from the family in the photo, the way you stand when you're already halfway out a door.

"You'd laugh," he said to the empty room. "You'd laugh at me, sitting here."

The room said nothing. Rooms are good at that. He'd spent twenty years in rooms that said nothing and had come to find it restful, which was perhaps the whole problem.

He opened the box. The reel sat inside in its plastic cradle, brown tape wound tight and even, and he turned it over in his hands without taking it out, feeling the weight of it, four minutes and eleven seconds of the truest thing he had ever made, sealed in a box in a wardrobe in a room where nobody came.

* * *

He did not know how long he sat there. Long enough that the copper light went gray. Long enough that his knees complained when he finally stood, and he heard himself make the small sound Marguerite made, the same involuntary syllable, and had to stand still for a moment with that.

That was the thing about twenty years with a person. You became them in the places where you'd failed to become yourself.

He put the tape back on the shelf. He was closing the doors—his hand already on the left one, the ritual nearly complete, the almost-said almost unsaid again, safely reinterred for another week or another month—when he stopped.

Something was wrong.

He stood very still and let the room tell him. This was his actual skill, the thing he was paid for: to stand in front of a structure and feel the discrepancy, the millimeter that shouldn't be there. The wardrobe doors. He looked at them. He looked at the gap between them, where they met, and his stomach performed a slow, cold revolution, because the gap was wrong. Not by much. A millimeter, maybe two. The left door sat proud of the right, and he knew—he *knew*, the way he knew the sound of his own car in the driveway—that he had left them flush. He always left them flush. He had oiled the hinges himself; the doors closed with a precision he took a small, secret pride in, one of the only precise things in his imprecise life.

Someone had opened the wardrobe. Recently. And closed it carelessly—or in a hurry, or with shaking hands.

He pulled the doors open and went through the shelves with the caliper-hands of his trade, checking, counting. The tapes were all there. The notebooks were all there, the stack of them on the lower shelf, forty-odd school exercise books in different colors, the whole written history of his silence. He counted them twice. Nothing missing. Nothing moved, as far as he could tell, except—

Except the front box. *Winter Light, take 3.* He had put it back at a slight angle; he could see the angle of it in his memory, could see his own hand setting it down an hour ago. It sat square now. Squared by another hand.

He stood in the blue-carpeted room with the glass animals going dark on the windowsill and felt the vertigo come up through the floor like vibration through a deck. Marguerite was at Celia's. Marguerite had been at Celia's four times this month, and before that twice, and the house had been empty on other days too, days he'd been on the bridge with his calipers, measuring tolerances while someone else moved through his house with their hands on his secrets.

Or it was Freya. Freya, who had a key nobody had asked to be returned. Freya, who hadn't called in— he did the arithmetic and did not like the number. Freya, who had stood in this room at seven years old and asked *Daddy, what's Winter Light* and been handed a lie and had, apparently, never let go of it.

He closed the wardrobe doors, carefully, flush, the way they were meant to sit, and stood with his palm flat against the pine.

"Who's been in here," he said to the room, quietly, and the room, being a room, said nothing at all.

Downstairs, the telephone began to ring.
