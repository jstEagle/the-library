## 5. The First Letter

Three days. That was how long the tin sat on the kitchen table before Astrid could touch it.

She had carried it home from Alder Row like a relic from a war she'd fought alone—wrapped in her cardigan, held against her sternum on the train, so that if anyone had asked what was in the box she could have said *my heartbeat* and been nearly accurate. She'd set it down and then not touched it. It sat there through three mornings of coffee, two dinners Tomas ate in silence across from her, one night when she woke at 3 a.m. and stood in the kitchen doorway just looking at it, the way you look at a dog you suspect is dying.

The tin was biscuit-colored once, she could tell. Now it was the color of tea left too long, with a rusted seam along the lid and a dent in one corner shaped like a thumbprint. On the top, half worn away, a picture of windmills. Holland, maybe. Or a fantasy of Holland printed in a factory in Sheffield. Her mother had never been to Holland. Her mother had wanted to go everywhere and gone to work.

Astrid made coffee she didn't drink. She washed a cup that was already clean. She dried her hands twice.

Then she sat down, worked her thumbnail under the lid's rusted lip, and opened it.

The smell came first: smoke, yes, but underneath it something older and sweeter—paper aging, lavender gone to dust, the ghost of the drawer where the tin must have lived for years. Inside, letters. Not postcards. Letters, folded into thirds, each one addressed to her in handwriting she knew better than her own face: *Miss A. Voss*, in Halina's slanted, urgent script, the letters leaning east like they were always about to set off somewhere. Each envelope stamped. None of them franked. No postmark anywhere. A stack of months, sealed and undelivered, going back into the dark like tree rings.

Astrid counted them without meaning to. Her fingers moved through the stack the way they moved through coins. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. She stopped counting when her hands started shaking, which was at thirty-four, which meant nothing yet except that there were more than three years' worth, which meant—

Which meant her mother had written to her. Monthly. Maybe more. For years after the fire. After the silence. After Astrid walked out of the hospital corridor and did not turn around, and told herself for twelve years that she had vanished cleanly, completely, the way smoke vanishes—that she had been, in her mother's life, an absence, a closed door, a daughter who simply stopped existing.

She had built her whole self on that belief. The belief had load-bearing walls.

And here was a tin full of proof that the walls had been holding up nothing. That behind them, all this time, her mother had been writing into the same silence Astrid wrote into—except Halina hadn't mailed hers either. Hadn't known the address. Or had known it and chosen, month after month, to write anyway and seal anyway and keep anyway, a correspondence conducted entirely inside a biscuit tin behind a wardrobe.

Two women writing to each other across a burned house, both too proud or too frightened or too broken to put a stamp to it.

Astrid picked up the first envelope. The paper had gone soft at the corners, furred like a moth's wing. The date on the back, in her mother's hand, because Halina dated everything, even eggs in the fridge: *14 October.*

Twelve years ago. Six weeks after the fire.

Her name looked up at her from the envelope. *Miss A. Voss.* Not *my darling*. Not *Astrid*. Miss. As if her mother were also practicing distance, also keeping a ritual, also obeying rules Astrid had thought were hers alone.

She opened it.

* * *

*My Astrid —*

*I don't know where to send this so I am sending it nowhere, which is to say I am putting it in the tin your father kept his receipts in, God rest him, he would laugh to see it used for love letters instead of tax. Though these are not love letters. Or they are. I don't know what you call a letter to someone who used to be your whole address.*

*It is raining today. The kind of rain that comes sideways off the estuary and gets under the door. Mrs. Okonkwo brought me soup and stayed two hours talking about her son in Manchester, and I let her talk because her voice is better than my own thoughts, which are mostly furniture. Where is the table. Where are the chairs. Where are the four chairs we ate at every night while you grew two inches a year and hated the cabbage.*

*The insurance people came again. They ask questions I don't have answers for. I tell them what I know, which is very little, and they write it down as though little were enough.*

*I hope wherever you are it isn't raining. I hope you found a room with a good window. You always needed a window. You would sit on your bed and look out at the street like the street owed you something, and maybe it did.*

*Eat properly. Wear a coat. I am not asking you anything in this letter. I decided that is the rule: no questions until you answer one. So this is only weather, and soup, and chairs.*

*Mama*

*Astrid read it standing up. Then she read it sitting down. Then she read it a third time with her finger under the lines like a child learning, because some part of her could not believe that language still worked, that words could be arranged in this order by this person, that her mother's voice—which she had not heard aloud in twelve years, which lived in her memory flattened and grainy like an old recording—could come back this whole, this particular, this annoying.*

*Wear a coat.*

Twelve years of telling herself stories about what her mother must think of her—the cold verdicts, the disappointment, the door closed forever—and the truth was a woman writing *eat properly* into a biscuit tin and sealing it with a kiss she had no way to deliver.

Astrid noticed she was crying only when a drop hit the envelope and she snatched it away, horrified, blotting the tear with her sleeve as though the letter were a wound and salt would make it worse. The paper was already smoke-stained at the edges. What harm could one more mark do. But she blotted it anyway. Some things you protect past all reason.

She took the next envelope from the stack. Dated *9 November.* Same year.

* * *

*My Astrid —*

*Colder now. The radiators clank like the house is complaining, and I suppose it has cause. There is a gap under the back door where the fox comes in at night. I've started leaving bread out for it, which the neighbors will say is madness, feeding vermin, but it seems wrong to shut everything out just because things went wrong. Something got burned out of me this year, and what's left is softer than I'd like. Softness is not a virtue in this country. Here softness is what happens to fruit.*

*I got a job at the care home on Fenwick Road. Mornings only. Old men who call me by their daughters' names and old women who hold my hand and tell me their secrets like I'm a priest. One of them, Mrs. Brandt, was a seamstress. She says my hands are a seamstress's hands. I said they're a cleaner's hands and she said those are often the same thing.*

*I don't say any of this to make you feel sorry for me. I say it because I promised myself I would write to you as if you might read it, and if you might read it then you should get the truth, and the truth is that I am managing. Managing is a word I learned from the English and I use it now more than my own language.*

*No questions. Still the rule. Though I will admit the rule is getting heavy, like carrying water in my mouth.*

*There is a bird that sits on the wire outside the kitchen. I have decided it is a wagtail but I don't actually know birds. Your father knew birds. He'd have named it in a second. I miss being married to a man who could name things.*

*Be well. Be warm. Be somewhere with a good window.*

*Mama*

* * *

Astrid lost track of time. The light in the kitchen went from grey to gold to blue-grey again, and she didn't get up to switch on the lamp, and she didn't hear Tomas come home, and when his key turned in the lock she flinched like a swimmer touched by a stranger in dark water.

"You're sitting in the dark," he said.

"I'm reading."

"I can see that." He hung up his coat. She heard him decide not to ask. She heard the decision cost him something. "There's leftover curry."

"Later."

He stood in the doorway a moment longer. In the dimness she felt his gaze move from her face to the tin to the envelopes spread across the table like a dealt hand, and she watched—not proudly, not with any pleasure—as he understood that whatever this was, it predated him, belonged to a country he didn't hold citizenship in, and that she was not going to explain. He said, "Okay," softly, the way you say okay to a door closing, and went to heat the curry alone, and the microwave hummed, and Astrid turned back to her mother's voice.

*February. My Astrid —*

*Snow today, real snow, the first since the fire. The garden looks forgiven. Everything does, under snow. I stood at the window a long time with my coffee getting cold, watching it come down over the empty lot next door, and I thought: this is what forgetting looks like from above. Gentle. Covering. Not cruel, just thorough.*

*I am not forgetting. I want that written somewhere, even if the somewhere is a tin. I am not forgetting, and I am not forgiving either, and I am not blaming, and I don't know which of those is the hardest to stop doing.*

*You turned twenty-six last month. I baked nothing. I want you to know I baked nothing, and that the nothing sat in the kitchen all day like a guest.*

*Work is work. Mrs. Brandt died in January, quietly, mid-sentence almost, and they let me sit with her afterward because I asked. Her family came and cried in the corridor and I thought: families are just people who haven't finished hurting each other yet.*

*That's not bitter. Or it is. I can't always tell anymore.*

*The rule stands. No questions. But Astrid, I'll tell you a secret the rule allows: I have started dreaming in English. My own mother would grieve to hear it. Language leaves you the way children leave you—one day they're speaking and one day they're speaking to someone else.*

*Keep the coat on.*

*Mama*

* * *

By midnight Astrid had read eleven letters. She had made a system without deciding to: read one, breathe, walk to the window, look out at her own street—at the lit windows opposite, strangers eating dinner in pools of yellow light—then come back and read another. The letters were becoming a place she visited. Her mother's voice was rebuilding itself in her chest room by room: the slight formality, the English learned late and worn carefully, the Polish gravity underneath it all, the jokes that arrived deadpan and stayed.

And underneath everything, the vertigo. That was the only word for it. Vertigo—the ground proving false long after you'd built your life on it.

Because Astrid had a story. She had told it to herself so many times it had worn smooth as a pebble: *I disappeared. I was a ghost to her. She mourned me or she cursed me, probably both, and then she forgot me, and my absence was total, and therefore my silence cost nothing, and therefore I was free.* Every choice she'd made for twelve years—the flat with the bad view she never complained about, the job she never advanced in, the relationships conducted at a depth of exactly one meter, including, she admitted it now, staring at the letters, including Tomas—every one of them rested on that pebble-smooth story. *I am invisible. Invisibility is safety. What she doesn't know can't be betrayed, and what I don't say can't be a lie.*

Thirty-four letters. Thirty-four months minimum. And the stack wasn't done; the tin was more than half full.

Her mother had written monthly for eleven years. Eleven years of weather and soup and wagtails and dead seamstresses and unbaked birthday cakes, addressed, stamped, sealed, and hidden behind a wardrobe in a house that had no roof. Writing to a daughter who might as well have been dead, except that dead daughters don't need coats.

Astrid pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw sparks.

The cruelty of it was the tenderness. If the letters had been angry she could have survived them—anger was a currency she understood, she'd been paying in it for years, self-hatred compounding monthly like a debt. But this. *I am not forgetting, and I am not forgiving either, and I am not blaming.* This was a woman doing the arithmetic of love in the dark and refusing to round the numbers. This was her mother, alive, specific, funny—*carrying water in my mouth*—her mother who had wanted to go to Holland and gone to Fenwick Road instead, holding her hand out month after month toward a daughter who had spent twelve years believing the hand wasn't there.

Or worse: believing she didn't deserve it.

Astrid got up and drank water straight from the tap, gasping a little, as if she'd been underwater. In the window's reflection she saw the kitchen behind her: the tin, the papers, the lamp she'd left off. Her own face, pale, floating above it all like something seen through ice.

One letter left unread tonight. Number twelve. She carried it back to the table, and before opening it she noticed something she should have noticed sooner: the dates weren't perfectly monthly anymore. There was a gap—October, November, then nothing until February. Three missing months. Winter of the second year. And the February letter's paper was different, thinner, cheaper, as if bought in haste.

She slit it open with her thumbnail.

*March. My Astrid —*

*I missed two months. I'm not going to explain why except to say that grief is not a river, it is weather, and some winters it snows sideways and you cannot go out in it. I'm out in it again now. The doctor says my heart is fine, which I take as a professional opinion and not a compliment.*

*I almost broke the rule tonight. I had the pen on the paper and the question was right there: WHERE ARE YOU. Two words. I wrote them and looked at them and they looked back at me like animals. Then I crossed them out so hard the pen went through, and here is what I learned: the question doesn't die when you cross it out. It just goes underground. It's down there now, under every line of every letter, growing.*

*Someday I will ask you something. I can feel it coming like a tooth coming in. When I do, I hope you understand it won't be a question really. It will be a door.*

*Until then: weather, soup, chairs. The fox had kits. Four of them, under the shed. I watch them at dusk. Your father would have named them.*

*Mama*

Astrid sat for a long time with the letter flat under her palms, feeling its cheap thin paper, its crossed-out question pressing up against her skin like a heartbeat through a wall.

Then she looked at the tin—at the twenty-odd envelopes still waiting, the years still unopened, the sentence her mother had not yet written—and understood that she was not reading letters. She was walking into a house room by room, and somewhere ahead, in a room she couldn't see yet, something was waiting that she had fled from once already.

She reached for the thirteenth envelope.
