## 5. The Printer's Address

The return address was on the ninth letter, and Ada had missed it twice before he saw it. That was the thing about reading a dead woman's handwriting—you skimmed the way you skimmed anything familiar, trusting your eye to know what it already knew. *Miriam Ashgrove, 14 Corben Lane* was an address he'd lived inside for eighteen years; his gaze slid over it the way water slides over oiled stone. But on the ninth letter, folded differently, the ink had feathered where her pen had pressed too hard, and beneath her name, in smaller script, almost apologetic: *c/o T. Varga, Printer, 3 Weir Street, Marlow Bend.*

Marlow Bend. He said it out loud in the stairwell of her empty flat, testing it, and the word *Bend* did something to him—suggested a turning, a river deciding against its course.

He went home that night and told Nell he had a work conference. Two days, Leeds. She looked at him across the kitchen table with an expression he couldn't parse—was it belief? Was she granting him the lie the way you grant a child a coin they've clearly dropped?—and said, "Fine," and turned back to her marking. He stood there a moment longer than made sense, waiting for something he couldn't name, then took his bag upstairs and packed it badly, forgetting his razor, forgetting socks, packing three shirts when one would have done. His hands were doing the packing. The rest of him was already on a train.

* * *

The train followed the river for the last hour, and Ada watched it the way he watched everything now—with an inspector's eye, cataloguing. The water was high and brown-green, carrying branches, carrying the memory of rain from hills he couldn't see. Bridges crossed it at intervals: a stone arch so old its parapet had been rebuilt in brick, a steel truss painted the grey of weathered skin, a footbridge sagging like a hammock left out all winter. He rated them without meaning to. Sound. Fair. Poor. It was how his mind rested itself, laying load paths over the world.

But today the habit kept breaking down, because every bridge he looked at raised a question he couldn't answer with instruments. Had his mother ever crossed this one? Had she stood at a window in Marlow Bend and watched a man set type, and thought about trains?

Forty-one letters. He'd read thirty-three now, rationing himself like a man stretching rations, though he didn't know what he was saving them for. The Miriam in the letters was a person he had never met. She was funny in a way that made him laugh alone on a train, drawing looks—a woman who described a government censor as "a man who reads other people's mail for a living and has never once received any," who called Tomas's printing press "your iron mistress" and claimed to be jealous of it. She wrote about hiding pamphlets in a pram she didn't own yet, borrowed for the purpose, pushing it through checkpoints with a face "so innocent I frighten myself." She wrote love the way young people write it: as appetite, as argument, as a game both players were winning.

And she wrote, always, in the corner of the first page, a small number. 1. 2. 3. Up to 41. As if she were keeping count not of the letters but of something else—as if each one were a rung, or a day marked off a wall.

The woman who raised him had written shopping lists in the same hand. *Milk. Bread. Batteries.* Her handwriting had been tidy to the point of anonymity, every letter the same height, as if she'd trained herself into uniformity. Now he understood: she had. The girl who scrawled *come at blue hour, the light on the water makes even the police look romantic* had spent fifty years pressing herself flat, letter by letter, until nothing rose above the line.

He got off at Marlow Bend with his badly packed bag and stood on the platform breathing air that tasted of river and diesel and cut grass, and felt the thrill come up in him like fever. This was the town. These were the streets her words had been posted toward, whether or not they ever arrived. He was walking inside the geography of her secret, and every ordinary thing—the chip shop, the church spire, a dog tied outside a bakery—seemed charged, complicit, lit from within.

* * *

Weir Street ran downhill toward the water, which explained the name, and the print shop was exactly where a man would want it: low-ceilinged, wide-windowed, close enough to the river to hear it working. Except it wasn't a print shop anymore. The window held a chalkboard—*FLAT WHITE / SOUP OF THE DAY: LEEK & POTATO / CARD ONLY*—and through the glass Ada could see exposed brick, hanging plants, a counter of pale wood where once, presumably, a compositor's stone had stood.

He stopped across the street, under a plane tree shedding its bark in camouflage patches, and experienced the specific disappointment of the man who arrives too late everywhere. Of course it was a café. Everything became a café eventually. History was renovated, whitewashed, fitted with pendant lighting and a card machine. He thought of his mother's flat, stripped to its walls, the wardrobe already sold or bound for the skip, and grief arrived sideways—not for Tomas, whom he'd never known, but for the shop, for the idea that somewhere things stayed put long enough to be found.

Then he noticed the press.

It stood in the far corner behind a rope barrier, visible through the window: a cast-iron hand press, wheel and platen, painted black, polished by touch. A museum piece. An ornament. But real—he knew machines, knew the difference between a thing maintained and a thing displayed, and this one had the look of maintenance. Someone oiled it. Someone, occasionally, perhaps, still pulled a lever just to feel it answer.

Ada crossed the street before he decided to. The bell above the door gave a small bright note, absurdly cheerful, and the smell inside was coffee and warm wood and, underneath it, faintly, ink—that ghost of oil and solvent that never fully leaves a room where ink lived for decades. He recognized it without knowing how. Some knowledge comes down in the blood like a trade.

The owner was a woman in her sixties, silver-cropped hair, reading glasses pushed up into it, wiping down the espresso machine with the tenderness people usually reserve for horses. She glanced up. "Sit anywhere, love, I'll be with you."

"I'm not—" He'd rehearsed nothing. He'd imagined walking in and the story simply leaving him, whole, but standing here with steam hissing and students hunched over laptops, the story stuck in his throat like a swallowed stone. "Could I ask you something? About the building?"

She finished her wipe-down, unhurried, and gave him the look of a woman who'd answered this question many times. "The press isn't for sale."

"No. I—" He laughed, unexpectedly, and the laugh loosened something. "No. My mother used to correspond with someone here. A printer. In the seventies. I found some letters." He heard how it sounded—like a scam, like a film—and hurried on. "His name might have been Tomas Varga."

The cloth stopped moving.

It was a small thing, the stopping. Anyone else wouldn't have seen it. But Ada had spent twenty years watching structures for the millimeter of movement that precedes failure, and he saw it: the stillness that means load has transferred somewhere unexpected.

"Tomas," she said. Not a question. A weighing.

"He set type here. I think. Before—" Before what, he didn't know. Before everything.

She hung the cloth on the machine's rail and came around the counter, and up close he saw that her eyes were quick and kind and assessing all at once. "You'd better sit down," she said. "I'll make you a coffee. On the house. You look like a man who's been holding his breath since the train station."

"How did you know I came by train?"

"You've got the walk," she said. "Platform legs. Everyone gets it coming here—the river does something to people, slows them down before they're ready."

She made two coffees, which he hadn't asked for, and brought them to the table by the window, and sat down opposite him without asking, which told him she'd been waiting years for someone to come in and ask about Tomas, and had despaired, and hadn't quite.

"I bought this place in '94," she said. "From a property company that had let it rot for ten years. There were type sorts still in the cracks of the floorboards. Little metal letters. I used to find them for months—an *e* here, an ampersand there, like the building was spelling something out slowly." She smiled. "I kept them in a jar. Sold the jar to a man from the university eventually, but I kept the ampersand. Seemed rude, somehow, selling that one."

"The man who worked here," Ada said. "Tomas."

"Tomas the type-setter, we called him. Though I only know that secondhand—I was a girl in the next town when he had the shop. But my aunt knew him. Everyone knew him, in the way you know someone the authorities also know, which is to say you knew him and pretended you didn't." She wrapped her hands around her own cup. "He printed things. The wrong things, according to some. Pamphlets, samizdat nonsense, whatever the government of the week had banned that month. My aunt said he had the fastest hands she'd ever seen—could set a page while talking to you, never looking down, like a pianist."

Ada's chest hurt. He realized he was gripping the cup too hard and made himself stop. "Did he have family?"

The pause this time was longer, and he watched her arrive at a decision in it—watched her decide to trust him, or decide that at sixty-something she was past the age of caution, or both. "A son," she said. "Daniel. He teaches at the comprehensive up the hill. Still lives here—grew up in the flat above this very shop, back when it was a flat and not a café with pretensions." She gestured at the hanging plants with dry affection. "His father died—God, it must be fifteen years ago now. Heart. In here, actually, or near enough. They say he came down to check on the building when it was empty, and the neighbors found him on the step the next morning, sitting up, like he'd just paused to rest." She shook her head. "Died looking at the door of the place he loved. There are worse doors."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. He drank the coffee and barely tasted it. "Does Daniel—" He stopped. Started again. "Is he—does he look like his father?"

"Oh, everyone says so. Same hands, apparently. Big hands, clever hands." She studied Ada then, really studied him, and he felt the inspection turn—felt himself become the structure under examination, his face read for stress lines, deflections, cracks. "You said your mother corresponded with him."

"Yes."

"In the seventies."

"Yes."

"And you've come all this way because—" She left it open, gently, the way you leave a gate open for an animal you don't want to startle.

Because, Ada thought. Because forty-one letters. Because a woman who chose batteries and milk over the whole burning vocabulary of her younger self. Because a man died on a step looking at a door. Because I am possibly standing in front of the only other person alive who inherited this silence, and I don't know if I'm here to claim him or to warn him or simply to stand near the source of the sound.

"My mother died recently," he said. "I found letters she never sent. All of them addressed to him. None of them posted." He heard his own voice go flat and careful, the inspector's voice, and hated it. "I wanted to see the place they came from. Or would have come from. If she'd had the—"

He didn't finish. The owner reached across the table and touched the back of his hand, briefly, once, the way you steady something.

"There's a son," she repeated softly. "Daniel Varga. Teaches history, would you believe. Up at St. Brendan's. He comes in Thursdays, sometimes, for a flat white and a slice of the lemon cake, sits right there where you're sitting, and reads old newspapers like other men read novels." She withdrew her hand. "If you wanted to leave a message, I could—"

"No," Ada said, too fast. Then, more carefully: "No. Thank you. I need to—" Think. Breathe. Find out what I'm made of before I test it against his.

He stood, and thanked her, and asked her name—Halina, she said, Halina Osei, and the name landed strangely, another life carried across another border, this town full of people who had arrived from elsewhere and built cafés over ruins—and walked to the door. At the threshold he stopped, because through the window, down the hill, along the river path, a man was walking away from the school with a satchel over one shoulder and a stack of exercise books under his arm, and he was tall, and he moved with a loose easy stride, and Ada could not see his hands.

Halina came and stood beside him and followed his gaze, and said nothing, which was its own kindness.

"That's him?" Ada asked.

"That's him."

Ada watched Daniel Varga reach the corner and turn, out of sight, toward whatever street held whatever life—a wife or no wife, children or none, a father's grave and a father's press and forty-one letters' worth of a woman named M. whom he had never heard of and who had, without meaning to, without ever posting a single page, helped raise him too. Every value Ada had was hers. Every caution. Every measured sentence. He was walking around inside her compromise, wearing it.

The thrill that had carried him off the train curdled as he stood there, cooling in his chest, turning into its honest twin: fear. Not of Daniel. Of the arithmetic. If the man in the letters was half of what Miriam wrote, then the woman who raised Ada had walked away from that, and the question he'd been carrying since the stairwell—*why*—was about to be answered by a stranger with his mother's laugh, maybe, or his mother's eyes, and answers, Ada knew professionally, were load-bearing. You didn't remove a wall to see what was behind it unless you were prepared for the building to move.

"Thursdays," Halina said behind him. "In case that's useful. And love—" he turned; she was already back at her machine, not looking at him, "—whatever you're going to do, do it kindly. That boy lost his father twice. Once to the prison, once to the heart attack. He doesn't know he's missing anything else. Think hard about whether finding out is a gift."

Ada pushed open the door. The bell rang its bright stupid note. Outside, the river went on bending, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream except the things that sank.

He had two days. He had told Nell two days, and somewhere in his jacket pocket was a phone he hadn't checked since Leeds, and in his head was a list of thirty-three letters and eight unread, and down the hill, in a classroom smelling of whiteboard markers, a man with clever hands was teaching children about causes and effects.

Ada started walking. Not toward the school. Away from it—toward the river, toward the bench by the weir, toward anywhere his legs would take him while the rest of him caught up. He'd buy bread, he thought absurdly. He'd find a market. He'd invent errands until dark.

He had one full day before Thursday.
