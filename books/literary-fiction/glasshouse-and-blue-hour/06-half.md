## 6. Half

The market square in Vellbrück smelled of wet stone and frying onions, and Ada stood at its edge for forty minutes before he admitted to himself what he was doing, which was nothing.

He had told himself the journey was reconnaissance. That was the word he'd used on the train, silently, watching the river widen as the line followed it down from the hills—*reconnaissance*, a good inspector's word, a word that made cowardice sound like method. You reconnoitered a structure before you committed a crew to it. You looked for the defects first. You did not walk onto a span until you understood what it was carrying.

But a man was not a viaduct, and Daniel Havel was not a defect, and Ada had been standing beside a fruit stall with two overripe pears in his hand for so long that the vendor had begun to watch him with the particular patience reserved for the unhinged.

He bought the pears. It gave him something to do with his hands.

* * *

He had found the address the way he found everything—by reading the record twice and trusting the second reading. The café owner, a broad woman named Marta with flour on her forearms, had spoken of Tomas the type-setter with the easy affection people reserve for the dead, and then she had said the sentence Ada had both hoped for and dreaded: *his boy still teaches at the school up the hill. Comes in Thursdays for the rye bread. You can't miss him—he's got his father's hands.*

Ada had missed him three times already this morning, or rather, he had failed to confirm him, because every man between forty and sixty who entered the square became, for one lurching second, a half-brother, and then resolved into a stranger, and Ada felt each resolution as a small reprieve he was ashamed to be grateful for.

He had walked the square's perimeter four times now. He knew its geometry the way he knew a deck plan: the fountain off-center, its basin cracked and patched with a resin that had yellowed; the arcade on the north side with its sagging lintel—he'd caught himself assessing it, noting the spalled concrete at the third column, the rust weep-staining below—because his mind went to structures the way other men's minds went to weather. The school was up the hill behind the linden trees; he could see its roof, a low grey hip of tiles, and he had already imagined, in humiliating detail, the walk up there, the corridor, the smell of chalk and floor wax, a man in a classroom doorway turning around.

*Hello. I'm the son of the woman who didn't come.*

He had rehearsed it on the train in four registers. Plain, apologetic, bureaucratic, and—this one only once, on the far side of the tunnel, when the dark pressed against the window—desperate. None of them survived daylight. In daylight they all sounded like what they were: the opening moves of a man asking a stranger to give him back something that had never been his.

The pears were going soft in his jacket pocket. He could feel them against his ribs like a small failing organ.

* * *

He bought bread because Marta had said Thursdays, and rye, and because a man standing in a market square for two hours needed a pretext, and bread was the most innocent one available. The baker's stall was at the square's east corner, under a striped awning faded to the color of old bandage, and Ada joined the queue behind a woman with a pram and a dog on a braided lead, and when his turn came he pointed at a dark round loaf and said, *that one,* and the baker wrapped it and Ada paid and then stood there, holding it, with no plan for it whatsoever.

He did not eat bread as a rule. Nell bought it; Nell managed the household's carbohydrates the way she managed everything, competently and without discussion. This loaf was not for eating. It was a prop. He understood that with perfect clarity and continued to hold it anyway, because the alternative was to hold nothing, and a man holding nothing in a market square is a man waiting for someone, and a man waiting for someone in this square, on this Thursday, was a man who had made a decision.

The loaf grew warm against his palm. Somewhere behind him the fountain ran over its cracked lip in a thin silver thread, and the sound of it was so like the sound of water finding a path through a hairline fracture—*weeping*, they called it in his trade, when water traced a flaw and announced it to the world—that Ada had to close his eyes for a moment.

His mother had wept like that. Not openly—never openly. She had wept the way concrete weeps: a slow staining at the edge of things, a darkening you only noticed after years, when the damage had already gone structural. He had found her once, he was eleven, standing at the kitchen window with her back to him and her hand pressed flat against the glass, and when he'd said *Mum?* she had turned around with her face already reassembled, and said, *the condensation, love,* and he had believed her, because children believe the load path they're given.

She had taught him caution as if it were a virtue. Look twice. Sign nothing. Love the things you can carry. He had thought, for fifty years, that this was wisdom. Standing in the square with a loaf of bread he would never eat, he understood it for what it had been: a woman teaching her child the walls she'd built to survive inside, and calling the walls a house.

* * *

He saw him at twenty past eleven.

There was no mistaking it, once he saw it—the way you cannot unsee a crack once the dye has penetrated. A man came down the hill road with a canvas bag slung across his body and a stack of exercise books under one arm, and he was ordinary in every respect, medium height, greying at the temples, a corduroy jacket with the elbows gone soft—and his hands were wrong. Too expressive. They moved while he walked, conducting some private argument with the air, and Ada knew those hands. He had seen them forty years ago, folded around a teacup, turning pages, smoothing a tablecloth flat with a motion like a benediction. He had seen them in a photograph behind a false panel in a wardrobe, resting on a young woman's shoulder while she laughed at something outside the frame.

His father's hands. His mother's laugh. And Ada standing in the middle with the arithmetic of it: *half.*

That was the word that had come to him on the train and would not leave. Not *brother*. *Half.* Because what did they share, he and this man? A father who had died—Marta had said it gently, *he waited for her, you know, in his way, all his life*—and a mother who had chosen, and a silence the size of a decade. Half a man. Half a history. Ada had spent two days in this town telling himself he had come for the truth, and now the truth was crossing the square in a corduroy jacket, and every cell in Ada's body was voting to leave.

He turned toward the arcade. It was not a decision; that was the terrible part. It was a reflex, load finding the path of least resistance, and he was three columns deep into the arcade's shadow before he caught himself doing it—doing *her*—and stopped so abruptly that a woman with a shopping trolley swore at him.

He stood between the columns with his bread and his pears and his heart going like a jackhammer on bad bearings, and he watched Daniel Havel buy rye bread.

It was unbearable how ordinary it was. The baker knew him—there was a nod, a few words, the baker reaching for the dark loaf without being asked, the loaf already set aside, wrapped, waiting. A man known in his town. A man who came on Thursdays. Daniel paid, and said something that made the baker laugh, and the laugh—Ada felt it in his sternum—had a shape he recognized. Not his mother's. Something else. Something he had no archive for and therefore no defense against.

Daniel stood at the stall's edge and ate the end of the loaf right there, tearing it, chewing, looking out over the square at nothing in particular, at the fountain, at the pigeons, at the ordinary machinery of a Thursday—and Ada, forty meters away in the dark of the arcade, understood that he was looking at a man who had been loved in the open. Who had a father's name on his documents and a father's grave to visit and a town that saved his bread. And who had still, somehow, lost the same woman Ada had lost—lost her without ever having had her, which was perhaps the greater loss, or perhaps only the differently shaped one.

*He waited for her, in his way, all his life.*

* * *

Ada made three attempts.

The first was logistical: he would simply walk across the square, and what he'd say would arrive on its own, the way words did at work, in the incident reports he was known for, plain and exact. He got as far as the fountain. The cracked basin was being refilled by a man in waders, and the water was coming back into it, climbing the stain-lines of forty summers, and Ada stood watching the water rise over the old marks and could not make his feet go the last twenty meters. He told himself it was because he hadn't prepared. He knew, in the honest room at the back of himself, that no preparation would ever be sufficient, that he had come here to do a thing that could not be done carefully, and careful was the only way he knew how to enter any room.

The second attempt was through the pears. He would offer the pears. He had bought them, after all; a gift was a door; people took gifts from strangers every day. He got the pears out of his jacket and held them and looked at them—bruised, overripe, one of them weeping a dark sweetness at its shoulder where it had pressed against his ribs all morning—and he understood that he was about to walk up to a grieving stranger and hand him fruit his father's other son had carried around a market square for two hours like a talisman, and he put them back. The bruise spread under his hand.

The third attempt he did not even begin. He simply stood at the square's edge with his loaf and watched Daniel cross toward the hill road, exercise books under his arm, hands talking to the air, and he let him go. He stood there and let him go and watched him diminish up the hill under the lindens until the trees took him, and the whole time some voice in him—his mother's voice, or the voice she had installed in him, which amounted to the same thing—said, in her tidy, reasonable, surviving tone: *Another day. You've come this far. There's no hurry. Better to do it properly.*

There was always no hurry. There had been no hurry for fifty years. His mother had died with forty-one letters in a shoebox and every one of them unsent, and here was her son, in her town—no, in *his* town, in *Tomas's* town—holding bread he wouldn't eat, and the letters were still unsent. He was the shoebox. He had carried her across the country and now he stood in a square doing exactly what she had done at a train station fifty years ago, which was nothing, which was turning around, which was surviving.

* * *

He sat on the fountain's rim—on the dry part, away from the man in waders—and ate nothing and drank nothing and conducted, in the honest room at the back of himself, the examination he had been avoiding since the stairwell of his mother's flat.

*Coward,* the examination began, and he did not object to the term, because objecting was another form of caution. But then the examiner in him, the inspector, the man who wrote reports that ruined friendships, insisted on precision, and precision said: *not cowardice exactly. Inheritance.* Cowardice was a failure of nerve in the moment. What he had was a whole architecture of avoidance, load-bearing, well-maintained, built by a woman who had needed it to live and never told him it was temporary scaffolding, never told him it was anything at all. He had grown up inside it. He had married inside it—God, Nell, Nell who asked him questions across the kitchen table and received his load-bearing silences and had stopped, at some point he could not date, asking at all. What had he given her? A house with no doors. And she had lived in it anyway, all these years, and he had called that peace.

The fountain man finished and packed his hoses away and the water settled and began, very quietly, to find the crack again. Ada watched the thin thread of it trace the old path down the basin's outer face, patient, exact, going where the flaw told it to go.

He thought: *I will go up the hill tomorrow. Friday. I'll prepare. I'll write it down, what to say, I'll—*

He heard his mother say *better to do it properly* and recognized, finally, the sentence for what it was: the sound a life makes when it is being postponed one day at a time.

He stood. The loaf sat beside him on the rim where he'd set it down, and he looked at it for a long moment, this absurd prop, this bread bought for a meeting he had failed to have, and then—because he could not throw it away, because throwing it away would be one more small unsent thing—he picked it up and carried it.

The square was emptying toward lunch. The awnings snapped in a wind coming down off the hills, and the light had gone the flat silver of early afternoon, and Ada walked the perimeter one more time, not reconnaissance this time but something like a circuit, a man walking the deck of his own failure and tapping it, listening for the hollow places.

At the east corner, the baker was folding down his awning. Their eyes met, and Ada had a sudden vertiginous certainty—the kind he usually only trusted in structures—that the baker had watched him all morning. That the whole square had watched him. That somewhere between the fountain and the fruit stall, a stranger carrying bread and pears and his father's face—Marta must have told him, *you can't miss him, he's got his father's hands*, and hands were not the only thing that ran in families—had been noted, and puzzled over, and perhaps already mentioned, up the hill, over dinner: *there was a man in the square today, Thursday, watching the school road, holding a loaf he never ate.*

Ada stopped at the corner of the arcade. His heart was doing its bad-bearing hammer again.

Because that was the thing about cracks. You could stand at a distance and call it caution, call it method, call it another day, but a crack does not care what you call it. It opens at its own rate. And somewhere up the hill behind the lindens, in a flat that smelled of exercise books and rye bread, a man with his father's hands might already be holding the other end of the fracture—might already be wondering who in the world had come to stand in his square and look at him like a man reading a defect report on his own life.

Tomorrow, Ada told himself, and heard, underneath it, the water finding the crack.

He turned up the hill road after all—just to walk it, just to see the school from the outside, just to stand beneath the lindens where the trees had taken Daniel—and as he came around the last bend, the schoolyard gate opened, and Daniel Havel came out alone, locking it behind him, and looked directly down the hill at the man standing there with a loaf of bread in his hands.
