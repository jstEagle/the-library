## 8. The Rerouting Notice

The notice went up on a Tuesday, though I didn't see it until Thursday, because that was the shape of my life: everything arrived two days late, filtered through someone else's urgency.

I was walking to Mirela's house from the Delmore Street stop, the 47 having deposited me at the corner of Ash and Ninth as it had done for six weeks now, twice a week, always the same driver, a heavyset man with a beard like wire wool who never spoke and whom I had come to regard as a kind of colleague. We were both in the business of moving through rooms without disturbing them.

The notice was taped to the inside of the bus shelter's plexiglass, at eye level, laminated against weather, which meant the city had planned this for months and only now deigned to tell us. It had the municipal blue header, the municipal font, the municipal tone of an apology that isn't one.

**PUBLIC NOTICE — SERVICE CHANGE EFFECTIVE [DATE]**

**ROUTE 47 WILL BE REROUTED TO SERVE THE NEW TRANSIT CORRIDOR ON VANDERMEER AVENUE. STOPS ALONG DELMORE STREET AND ASH STREET WILL BE DISCONTINUED.**

I stood there longer than the notice deserved. That's what I remember most—not reading it, but standing inside the shelter while rain needled the roof, reading it again, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something less final. *Discontinued.* Not closed. Not removed. Discontinued, the way you discontinue a product, a fabric pattern, a shade of paint. As if the stops had been a line of dishware.

Three months. The notice gave them three months, which is the amount of time cities believe grief takes.

I should say something here about why a bus route mattered to me, because on paper it shouldn't have. I owned a car once, a gray hatchback I sold after my father died because I couldn't stand the way it held his smell in the upholstery—coffee and WD-40 and something sweeter underneath, the smell of a man who kept his tools clean because they were the only things he loved out loud. After that I took buses everywhere, and I told people it was practical, and it was practical, but that wasn't the reason. The reason was that a bus is the only public room where you're allowed to be silent without explanation. Everyone on the 47 was silent together, all those strangers agreeing, twice a day, not to know each other. It was my church.

And now the church was being moved across town to serve a corridor named after a developer.

I photographed the notice with my phone, which felt absurd, forensic, like photographing a dying relative's hands. Then I walked the rest of the way to Delmore Street with the words sitting in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed whole.

* * *

Mirela was in the kitchen when I let myself in—I had a key now; she'd pressed it into my hand in week three with the offhand authority of someone handing you a fork—and she was doing something to a pot of tea that involved more ceremony than the word *tea* can hold. She'd taken to wearing a shawl indoors even in mild weather, a dark red thing with fraying tassels, and she looked up at me over her glasses the way she always did, as if I were a piece of mail she'd been expecting.

"You're late," she said.

"The bus—" I started, and stopped, because I didn't want to say it yet. Saying it would make it real, and I had spent fifty-one years perfecting the art of not making things real. "Traffic," I said instead, which was a lie so small it barely qualified.

She narrowed her eyes. Mirela could hear lies the way I heard flat notes—not as moral failures but as frequencies, wrongnesses in the air. "Sit," she said. "We'll do water words today. You still confuse *sella* and *sellem*, and it embarrasses us both."

*Sella* was the river. *Sellem* was the memory of the river—the river as carried in a mind, in a mouth, after the river itself was gone. In English these are the same word, or rather English doesn't bother distinguishing, because English assumes the thing and the memory of the thing will stay together, will keep each other company indefinitely. Vessic made no such assumption. Vessic had watched a valley fill with water and had learned, the hard way, that the world divides into what remains and what must now be carried.

I sat. I took out my notebook, the cheap spiral one I'd bought at the drugstore, its pages already softening at the edges from handling. Mirela poured the tea, and we began.

But I was bad that day. My ear—which had never failed me, which was the one instrument I trusted absolutely—kept sliding off the sounds. *Sella* came out as *sellem*. *Sellem* came out as nothing at all, just air shaped vaguely like a word. Mirela corrected me with increasing terseness, and I felt the old shame rise, the stutter-era shame, the feeling of my own mouth being a locked door with me locked outside it.

Finally she set down her cup with a click that ended the lesson.

"Something happened," she said. "On your walk. You've been carrying it since you came through the door. You think I can't hear it? This house has thin walls and I have thick ears."

I almost lied again. I want to be honest about that—the lie was right there, ready, smooth from use. *Nothing. Just work. A difficult client.* And then I thought about what she'd said to me in week one, when I'd asked her why she wanted to learn piano tuning at her age, and she'd said: *Because everything that goes quiet dies a little, and I am tired of supervising deaths.*

I put my phone on the table between us and opened the photograph.

She read it the way she read everything—slowly, her lips moving, first in silence and then aloud. Her lips moved because Vessic had been, before the dam, before the dispersal, before sixty years of speaking it only to herself, a language of the mouth above all else, and she believed a word wasn't fully alive until it passed through breath.

"Route forty-seven," she read. "Rerouted. Discontinued." She said the last word twice, tasting it. Then she looked up. "Three months."

"Three months," I said.

She nodded slowly, and I expected anger—I wanted her to be angry, actually, because anger is a manageable emotion, anger has handles—but what came instead was something quieter and much worse. She folded her glasses and set them down. She looked out the kitchen window at her backyard, where a pear tree grew crooked against the fence, planted, she'd told me once, by the previous owner, a Serbian stonemason whose name nobody on the street remembered anymore.

"This street," she said, "has been dying for sixty years. Slowly. One shop, one family, one bus at a time. I have watched it the way you watch a tide go out—you cannot stop it, you can only mark where the water was." She touched the phone screen, the blue municipal header, gently, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it hurts. "But a bus is different. A shop closes, that is an ending. A bus reroutes—that is a sentence being cut in half. Everything east of the cut has to find another way to mean what it meant."

I didn't fully understand her then. I understand her better now, and I understand that understanding her better has cost me something I won't get back.

"Read it to me," she said suddenly.

"What?"

"The notice. Read it aloud. In your voice." She pushed the phone back across the table. "I want to hear how it sounds coming out of a person who lives here."

So I read it. The municipal header, the effective date, the corridor on Vandermeer Avenue, the discontinued stops. My voice did its professional thing, the tuner's voice, level and unbothered, and hearing myself deliver the death sentence of my own bus route in that voice made me feel like a coroner reading his own autopsy.

Mirela listened with her eyes closed. When I finished she was quiet for a long moment.

"Now I'll read it," she said. "In Vessic. So you can hear the difference between how a city says goodbye and how a person does."

"There's no Vessic word for 'route,'" I pointed out. "You told me. There's no word for 'transit' or 'corridor' or—"

"No," she agreed. "There isn't. There were no buses in Ves. There was the river, and there were feet, and there was the road along the valley that everyone called *koru im*—the long going. So I will translate it the way translation actually works, which is not finding matching words but finding matching wounds."

And she read it.

I cannot reproduce what she said. This is the failure I keep running into, writing this down: I have Casper Sterling's ear, the ear that can hear a string go false from three rooms away, and I cannot give you Mirela's Vessic. The sounds don't survive transcription. But I can tell you the shape of it. Where the English notice was square—headered, dated, boxed—her version was long and low, sentences that unspooled like water finding its level, and certain words recurred, words I knew by then: *sellem*, the remembered river; *vesh*, the word for home-that-is-gone (not lost—gone, deliberately gone, gone by decision); and *koru im*, the long going, which she used for the bus route, folding the 47 into the same word her village had used for the road along the drowned valley, so that the bus and the river-road became one road, one going, interrupted twice.

She read it twice. She had said she would, and she did, and the second time was slower, and somewhere in the second reading her voice changed—it dropped into a register I hadn't heard from her, lower, almost conversational, the register of someone telling a neighbor something true over a fence—and the house changed around her. I swear this. The radiators, which clanked their grief-clatter at odd hours, went silent. The refrigerator hummed softer. Even the rain on the kitchen window seemed to fall in her rhythm rather than its own. The house was listening. The house, which I had spent six weeks learning to hear, was leaning toward her voice the way I leaned toward a piano's soundboard.

And I wept.

Not elegantly. Not the single dignified tear of literary fiction. I sat at Mirela Voss's kitchen table with my cold tea and my spiral notebook and I cried like a man whose face had finally come unstuck, tears running freely, my breath doing the hitching thing I hadn't let it do since I was nine years old and my father had put his hand on my shoulder and said, *Enough of that now,* and I had learned, permanently, that enough was the correct amount of crying for any occasion, including none.

I didn't know why I was crying. That's the part I need you to believe, because it matters. I understood every word she used that I knew. I understood the structure, roughly. I understood it was a bus notice. Nothing in the content explained the response. If she had read the same information in English with the same cadence, I'd have nodded somberly and made a note.

But it wasn't the information. It was the carrying. Every word in her mouth arrived already grieving—grieving the bus, yes, but also the road under the reservoir, the brother on the last boat, the sixty years of talking to herself in a language with no second mouth. The notice in Vessic wasn't about a route change. It was every ending she'd ever survived, arriving in the present tense, and my body—my stupid, loyal, long-suppressed body—recognized the tune before my mind did, the way you recognize a song from childhood before you can name it, and it answered.

"I'm sorry," I managed. "I don't know why—"

"Don't apologize." She reached across the table and put her hand over mine, and her hand was light and dry and warm, a bird's hand, a hand that had hung wash and pulled nets and written letters that were never answered. "Crying at the right moment is fluency. Most people cry at the wrong moments or never. You just conjugated correctly for the first time."

I laughed through the tears, which is a sound I hadn't made since childhood either, and she let me sit there until it passed, drinking her tea, watching me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

When I could speak again, I said, "What did you say? At the end. The last part, the second time through. It sounded different from the rest."

She considered me for a while. Rain ticked the window. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, like a fist on a door, like someone asking to be let in.

"It was a question," she said finally. "The notice asks a question, if you translate it honestly. Cities pretend notices are statements, but they're questions. *Will anyone object? Will anyone come? Does anyone still need this?*" She turned the phone face-down on the table, a small decisive gesture, closing the subject like a book. "In Vessic, the question comes out as: *when the going stops, who carries the going?*"

"And what's the answer?"

"The answer," Mirela said, gathering our cups, standing, moving to the sink with the unhurried economy of a woman who had washed dishes in four kitchens across seven decades, "is whatever happens in the next three months. That's all an answer ever is. Time plus a person."

She ran the tap. Steam rose. I sat at her table with my wet face drying in the warm kitchen air, and I thought about the notice taped inside the shelter, laminated, official, already more permanent than the thing it announced the end of.

Three months. Ninety days of the 47. I found myself counting without meaning to, the way you count strings on a piano, and I thought: I should write to the transit authority. I should organize something. I should—

But even as the thoughts formed, I felt the old machinery engaging, the smile machinery, the invisibility machinery, the part of me that had spent five decades ensuring that Casper Sterling would never be the person standing up in a room full of strangers saying *this matters*. That person got noticed. That person got answers. That person had to have a voice steady enough to survive being heard, and mine had been built, deliberately, out of exactly the opposite materials.

"Casper," Mirela said, without turning from the sink. "You've gone quiet in the particular way you go quiet when you've decided in advance that you won't do the thing you're thinking about doing."

"I haven't decided anything."

"Mm." She shut off the tap. "Then decide faster. Water rises whether or not anyone votes on it. I can tell you that with authority." She dried her hands and looked at me, and behind the glasses her eyes were the gray-brown of the reservoir I wouldn't see for another fourteen chapters of my life, though neither of us knew that. "Come. Enough grief for one morning. Today you learn the verbs for leaving. Vessic has eleven of them, and you are going to hate every single one."

I picked up my notebook. Outside, far off, an engine changed gears—the 47, maybe, or its replacement already rehearsing on Vandermeer Avenue—and the house settled around us, holding its breath, keeping what it knew.

What I didn't know—what I couldn't have known as I bent over my notebook and wrote *eleven verbs for leaving* in my small careful handwriting—was that in three months' time I would be standing in a fluorescent conference room in front of a microphone, in front of strangers, in front of a panel with nameplates, saying words in a dead woman's language with my childhood stutter breaking the sentences open like ice.

That night I dreamed of water rising over a road, and on the road, impossibly, a city bus, its lights on, its doors open, waiting.
