## 9. Not-Yet-Kin

I have not had a guest in this body's nine years of living on Delmore Street, and before that, in the other house, in the other life, I learned early what guests do. They take. They carry your words out of your house like water in their hands, and they spill some on the way, and what reaches the other shore is never what you gave them. So I stopped giving. A language with one mouth is a grave with a door painted on it, but at least the grave is quiet.

And yet here I am, on a Thursday in October, climbing the stairs of a building on Corvane Avenue because a piano tuner with a smile like a locked door said, *you could come see the metronome sometime*, and I said, *Thursday*, before I could think better of it.

He did not expect me to say Thursday. I watched the word land on him like a bird landing on a wire. He almost took it back. His mouth opened and closed the way mouths do when a person is deciding between two sentences, one true and one smooth. I have watched mouths my whole life. In Vessic we had a word for the pause before the smooth sentence — *vessa* — the same word we used for the still water before the dam. The pause where the truth is still underwater.

He chose the true one. He stepped aside and let me in.

* * *

The apartment is small and clean in the way of rooms that are cleaned so they will not have to be felt. I stand in the doorway a moment longer than politeness requires, and he notices, and he does not know why, and I do not tell him. I am cataloguing. This is what old women do and what linguists do and what I have always done: I read a room the way he reads a piano, by what it holds and what it refuses to hold.

The metronome sits on a shelf above the radiator, brass and black, wound tight, though nothing in this apartment is making music. It is not running. A metronome that is not running but is wound — that is a man who has prepared himself for a discipline he does not practice. I say nothing. I put my coat on the hook he offers, and the hook is at the height of a taller man, and I understand that everything in this room was arranged by a person who expected to be someone else.

There are books, of course. Tuning manuals, acoustics, a dictionary of piano technology with a cracked spine. And one book that does not belong: a slim novel with a library sticker half-peeled from its cover. He sees me look at it and his ears go pink. A man who steals a library book rather than return a thing he loves. I file this away with the metronome.

The radiator under the window ticks as it cools, and I put my palm flat against it without thinking, the way I have put my palm against every wall of every house since I was a girl in Ves, and Casper watches me do it and does not laugh, and does not ask why. This is the whole reason I chose him, though I did not know it until this moment. Most people would laugh. Most people would ask. He only listens to the radiator with me, the way two people listen to a third person talking in the next room.

* * *

He makes tea. The kitchen is a corridor with a stove in it, and I hear him moving in there the way I hear everything now, with the too-loud ears of the nearly deaf and the too-keen ears of the nearly dead, which are the same ears. A cup set down too hard. A spoon that stirs longer than tea requires. He is nervous. Good. A student should be nervous. Nervousness is the body admitting that something matters.

I use his nervousness. While the kettle shouts, I look.

On the table by the window: a stack of envelopes, stamped, addressed, sealed, and unsent. Nine, ten, twelve of them. I do not touch them — I have manners, whatever the linguists will tell you — but I read the top one's address upside down from where I stand, because my eyes still work in two directions like a bird's. A name in another city. A woman's name. The handwriting starts strong and gets smaller toward the bottom of the address, the way handwriting does when the writer is arguing with himself about whether to write at all.

Twelve sealed letters is not a correspondence. Twelve sealed letters is a conversation with a door.

In Ves we had no word for *letter*. We had *koru* — what is carried — and we had *im* — what is carried toward someone. A letter unsent was not a thing we needed a word for, because in Ves you walked to the person's door and said it or you didn't. The dam gave us all our unsent letters at once, in 1958, a whole valley's worth of things carried toward people who were scattered like seed across three provinces. I have written four thousand unsent letters in my life. I know the weight of the stack on his table. It is the weight of a held breath.

He comes back with two cups and sees where I am looking and does not explain, and I do not ask, and we sit down with the whole silent conversation between us like a third guest.

* * *

The photo is on the bookshelf, wedged between the acoustics texts where it will not have to be looked at directly, the way you wedge a splinter. A young woman, squinting into sun, one hand raised not quite waving. Behind her, a porch rail. I know porch rails. I grew up leaning on one.

"Your mother?" I ask, in the tone I would use to ask about the weather, because the tone is the whole trick. Ask a heavy question lightly and people answer before they know they've decided to.

He looks at the photo the way you look at a word in a language you used to speak. "Yes," he says. Then, because I say nothing, because I have learned that my silence is a crowbar: "She died. A while ago."

"A while," I say. "In the forgotten tense."

He looks at me sharply, and I let him. He knows by now — three weeks of lessons, water words and door words, his careful mouth mangling my vowels so earnestly it hurts me somewhere under the ribs — he knows that Vessic verbs remember. Seen. Told. Forgotten. Three tenses for the past, because my people understood that the past is not one country.

"Forgotten," he says slowly. "You mean — things we've lost."

"I mean things you have decided not to see." I set down my tea. "In Vessic, if your mother died and you never once said her death aloud, you would speak of her in the forgotten form. Not because she is gone from your heart. Because she is gone from your mouth. There is a difference. English does not know the difference. English thinks the heart and the mouth are the same room."

He is quiet for a long time. The radiator ticks. Somewhere below us a door closes and the building carries it up the stairwell like a throat swallowing.

"She's in the forgotten form," he says at last. "Isn't she. In me."

"Only you can conjugate yourself," I say. "That is the cruelty and the mercy of it."

* * *

I should explain something about being the last speaker of a language, since no one who is not one can explain it and everyone who is one is dead or is me.

It is not loneliness. Loneliness is a room with the door closed. What I have is a room with no door at all, no walls, a room the size of a flooded valley, and every word I speak goes out over the water and comes back to me unchanged, because there is no other shore. For sixty years I have spoken Vessic to radiators and stairwells and the inside of my own skull, and the language has stayed alive the way a fire stays alive in a sealed stove — consuming its own air, getting smaller, getting stranger, until what burns is not wood but the memory of wood.

A language needs two mouths. I told him that the first week and he thought it was a proverb. It is not a proverb. It is an engineering fact, like a piano needing a soundboard. The strings are nothing without the wood that carries them.

And I chose him — I have not told him this and I may never tell him, but he is in my house reading my dead brother's handwriting, so he may as well know it here, in the privacy of my own head — I chose him not because he was kind, though he is, in the sideways way of people who hide it. I chose him because when I watched him tune my piano, I saw a man listening to a house. Not a piano. A house. He had his ear to the soundboard and his eyes half-closed and he was hearing the whole building breathe through the instrument, the pipes and the floors and the old bones of the walls, and I thought: *there is a man who already speaks to things that cannot answer. He is halfway to Vessic and doesn't know it.*

The last speakers of dying languages cannot afford to choose well. We choose who is near. But sometimes who is near is right, and you thank whatever drowned valley made you for the luck.

* * *

He catches me looking at the metronome.

"You can start it, if you want," he says. "It doesn't — I don't use it much anymore."

"Why is it wound, then?"

The question goes into him like a needle finding the place. I watch him decide, again, between the true sentence and the smooth one. I have begun to think of these moments as *vessa* — the still water. I am learning to wait on the shore of him.

"I used to practice four hours a day," he says. "Not piano. Listening. My teacher said a tuner's ear is a discipline, like a monk's. You wind the metronome and you sit and you listen to the room until you can hear what's wrong with it." He turns the cup in his hands. "I haven't sat with it in years. But I wind it. In case."

"In case of what?"

He almost smiles — not the locked-door smile, the other one, the one that is starting to happen around me without his permission. "In case I'm still that person, somewhere under the newer ones."

I get up, because old women may do what they like, and I touch the metronome's brass face with one finger. Cold. Kept. A relic he tends. In Ves we kept our grandmother's ladle on the wall for forty years after the kitchen it hung in was under forty feet of water. You wind what you cannot play. You keep what you cannot use. This is not sentiment. This is grammar. This is the forgotten tense, hanging on a wall, ticking in reserve.

"Play it," I say. "For me. Now."

He looks at me the way my brother looked at me when I told him to say goodbye to the house before we got in the truck. As if I have asked him to do something both simple and unbearable.

So he winds it — it is already wound, he only touches it, the way you touch a sleeping animal — and he sits down in the chair facing the shelf, and the metronome begins, *tick, tick, tick*, and he closes his eyes and listens to his own apartment as if it were a room he had never heard before. I watch his face while he does it. Something unclenches between his eyebrows. Something in his shoulders comes down half an inch, like a house settling.

I do not know what he hears. That is his. But I know what it costs him to hear it, and I know what it will give him, because I sat in a sealed stove of a language for sixty years and I know what it is to finally let the air in.

* * *

Before I leave, he walks me to the stairs, and at the door he does the thing I have been waiting three weeks for him to do. He hesitates. The pause before the smooth sentence. *Vessa.*

"Mrs. Voss," he says. "Mirela." My name in his mouth is still crooked — the *r* too hard, the vowels too flat, a foreigner's weather in my name — but he says it, and it comes out of his mouth and crosses the air between us and lands on me from outside for the first time in nine years. A word of mine, come back to me changed. Come back to me at all.

"Why did you really come tonight?" I ask him. "You invited me. Why?"

He looks past me, down the stairwell, at the place where his answer is hiding.

"Because you're the only person," he says, "who talks to things that don't answer. And I wanted to be in a room with that. For an hour."

It is not the whole truth. There is more under the water — there is always more under the water, a valley's worth — but it is a true sentence, and true sentences in this man's mouth are so rare they ring like struck glass.

"Next Thursday," I say. "Bring nothing. I am going to teach you one sentence. Only one. And I will not tell you what it means."

He blinks. "How can I learn a sentence if I don't know what it means?"

"How can you carry water," I say, "if you keep opening your hands to look at it?"

I go down the stairs slowly, holding the rail, and behind me I hear the door close, and then — I stop on the landing to be sure — I hear the metronome, still ticking. He has not stopped it. He is sitting in there with it, listening to his own life, and I am on the stairs with a sentence in my mouth like a stone from the bottom of the valley, and Thursday is six days away, and I am not sure, anymore, whether I am teaching him or asking him.

*Shal velessa koru im.* I do not say it. Not yet. But I carry it toward him, the way my brother's last letter was carried toward a house that is under the water now, and I climb down into the October dark knowing that whatever happens Thursday, the language has already done what languages do.

It has found its second mouth. Now I only have to decide what to feed it.
