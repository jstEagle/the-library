## 1. Postcards to Ash

The postcards came in packs of twenty from the stationery shop on Fell Street, the kind of shop that still sold fountain pen ink and rubber bands by weight, and every Sunday evening Ansel Galloway sat at whatever table her current room afforded and wrote one.

She had been doing this for eleven years. She did not think of it as a ritual. Rituals were for people who believed something was listening.

*Dear Mum,* she wrote, because the address was for her mother even if the house was gone, *the light here comes through the window at an angle that makes everything look like it's waiting. I cleared a house in Marston this week — a man who collected model ships. Forty-one of them. His daughter wanted them gone by Friday.*

She paused. The kettle in the shared kitchen down the hall ticked as it cooled. Outside, someone's television murmured through the wall, a laugh track rising and falling like weather.

*I hope wherever you are has a window like this one. I hope the light is kind there.*

She signed it the way she always did — just *A*, no surname, as though a full signature might commit her to something — and propped it against the lamp to dry. In the morning she would post it to 14 Calder Row, a house that had been ash and foundation since she was nine years old. The Royal Mail would return it, eventually, stamped with some bureaucratic apology, and she would add it to the shoebox under her bed, which held forty-one returned postcards, then forty-two, an archive of letters addressed to nowhere, delivered faithfully to nowhere, year after year.

She had never once asked herself why she did it. That was the trick of it. You could survive almost anything if you didn't ask yourself why you were doing it.

* * *

House-clearing was a trade that suited her the way a locked door suits a woman with no keys: perfectly, and with a certain grim symmetry. She emptied the homes of the dead and the departed, catalogued what could be sold, tipped what couldn't, and left each property swept and hollow, ready for its next life. She owned nothing that wouldn't fit in her car — a ten-year-old hatchback with a rusting wheel arch — and she owed nothing to anyone, which she considered the closest thing to wealth a person could hold.

The work itself was neither morbid nor tender, or so she told clients. It was logistics. A house was a container; a death was a change of inventory. She had learned to lift a dead man's coat off its hook without feeling anything, to bag up forty years of birthday cards without reading them, to carry out a child's cot without letting her hands remember anything.

But houses talked. That was the part nobody warned you about. Not in words — in weights. Every room had a gravity shaped by the lives that had pooled in it, and after twenty years in the trade Ansel could walk into a bungalow and know within minutes whether the marriage had been happy, whether the children had visited, whether the man who died had spent his last decade sleeping in the front room because the stairs frightened him. Houses kept accounts. People just stopped reading them.

The Marsh commission came on a Tuesday, in the form of a solicitor's letter on heavy cream paper.

*Dear Ms Galloway,* it began. *We act for the estate of the late Edith Marsh, deceased 3rd March last. We understand from your reputation that you undertake complete clearances with discretion. The property is 9 Latchmere Crescent. We require the house emptied by the end of the month. There is no family. The contents are to be valued, sold where practicable, and the remainder disposed of. One further matter: the deceased was, we believe, somewhat reclusive. Should you discover correspondence or documents of a personal nature, we ask that you set them aside unopened for our review.*

Ansel read the letter twice, standing in the doorway of her rented room with rain starting against the window. Reclusive. No family. Unopened correspondence. It was, she thought, the most honest brief she'd ever been given: here is a woman who kept things, and here is your instruction not to look.

She wrote back that afternoon accepting the job. Her hand hesitated only once, over the date — end of the month, four weeks, plenty of time — and then she signed, folded the letter, and posted it before she could reconsider.

That night she slept badly, which was unusual. She slept in rented rooms the way other people slept in airports: lightly, with one ear open, her possessions packed in such a way that she could be gone in eleven minutes. She had lived this way since she was nineteen — rooms above shops, rooms in shared houses, a caravan one winter in Whitby that she still thought of fondly because it had been hers for exactly as long as she paid for it. Never own anything. Never owe anyone. If you travel light enough, nothing can burn.

She had never formulated it as a rule. Rules were things you chose. This had chosen her, the way a scar chooses its shape.

* * *

9 Latchmere Crescent stood at the quiet end of a street of semis, behind a hedge grown wild and leggy, its gate hanging at the angle gates hang when no hand has lifted them in years. The house was brick, two-storeyed, with a bay window fogged by decades of grime and a front garden gone to grass and dandelion clocks. On the doorstep, wedged in the letterbox, were three catalogues furred with damp.

Ansel let herself in with the key the solicitor had posted and stood a moment in the hall, doing what she always did first: listening.

The house said: *careful*. It said: *drawn curtains, dust sheets, a woman who moved through her own rooms like a visitor.* The air was cool and smelled of lavender polish laid over something older — paper, maybe, or dried flowers, or simply time. The carpets were patterned roses, worn pale along one path from the kitchen to the stairs, as though Edith Marsh had walked the same line for forty years, a tram route through her own life.

Ansel opened the curtains room by room and let the light in. Dust rose in the shafts and turned slowly, like something deciding whether to settle.

She worked the ground floor first, methodically, top to bottom, left to right — her order imposed on the house's disorder, which was how she liked it. The kitchen held tins arranged by type, labels facing out, dates checked and marked in pencil on the lids. The sitting room held a television under a cloth, a sewing basket with a half-mended pillowcase still pinned to its frame, and photographs on the mantelpiece: all landscapes. Seaside towns, harbour walls, a promenade in winter. Not one face in any frame.

"People," Ansel said to the empty room, "who photograph empty places."

The room did not answer, but it seemed to agree.

By Thursday she had the ground floor boxed and listed. The house grew more talkative as it emptied, the way houses do — every removal uncovering another layer, another stratum of the life. Under the sofa cushions: a bus ticket dated 1998 and a single earring. Behind the sideboard: a child's marble, blue glass with a twist of white at its heart, which Ansel held in her palm for longer than she meant to before dropping it into the keep-box. She didn't examine why. Some things you kept without asking permission from yourself.

Upstairs there were two bedrooms and a bathroom. The smaller bedroom had been a sewing room, then clearly not a sewing room, then something between: a chair by the window facing the street, positioned — Ansel noted it with professional interest — to see the pavement outside number 7 opposite, or perhaps the corner beyond it. A watching room. A woman who photographed empty places and kept a chair aimed at the street.

The main bedroom was next, and Ansel saved it for last, the way she always did. Bedrooms were the vaults. Bedrooms told you what a person actually was, beneath the public rooms' performance.

Edith Marsh's bedroom was spare. A single bed, made tight, hospital corners. A wardrobe — old, dark oak, heavier than it looked. A dressing table with a lace runner and a jewellery box that proved, when opened, to contain buttons. A Bible on the nightstand with a ribbon marker at Psalms. And on the walls, framed this time, more photographs: the same seaside town, over and over, across decades. The same stretch of promenade, the same railings, the sea changing mood behind them. Winter, summer, storm, calm. A town watched for fifty years and never left.

Ansel stood in the middle of the room with her notebook open and felt the particular vertigo she sometimes got in the homes of the solitary — the sense of a whole human weather system that had blown itself out with no one to report it. Edith Marsh had eaten meals alone in that kitchen for decades. Had sewn that pillowcase for no one. Had sat in that chair by the window, watching a street that never once produced a visitor for her.

It was none of Ansel's business. That was the phrase she used, internally, like a hand laid flat on a table: *none of my business.* She had built a life out of that phrase. Other people's griefs passed through her hands and she let them go, unbent, unstained. It was the cleanest way to live. It was also, if she was honest in the hour past midnight when honesty came calling, the loneliest.

She started on the wardrobe that Friday morning.

It took the screwdriver twenty minutes and both her knees complaining to get the old thing eased away from the wall, and when it swung free on the stripped floorboards Ansel stepped into the gap behind it to sweep — and stopped.

The wall there was wrong. She saw it immediately, the way you see a face in a crowd that shouldn't be there: a rectangle of plaster slightly paler than the rest, edges too straight, and at knee height, a seam. Not a repair. A cavity. Someone had built a false patch over a hollow in the wall and then, for years, had stood a wardrobe against it the way you'd stand a bookcase in front of a door you'd decided never to open again.

Ansel crouched. The plaster patch came away with a dull crack, easier than it should have, as if it had been waiting.

Behind it, wrapped in a plastic bread bag gone brittle with age, was a shoebox.

She drew it out and set it on the bed. It weighed more than a shoebox should. Through the clouded plastic she could see paper — envelopes, dozens of them, packed tight — and string tied round in a bow that someone had knotted carefully, deliberately, the knot of a person who expected the box to stay shut.

Ansel Galloway had emptied two hundred and six houses in her working life. She had found love letters in water tanks, money in hymnals, ashes in biscuit tins, a wedding dress folded into a suitcase under floorboards. Nothing in any of those houses had ever made her hands go cold.

She untied the string anyway. Later — she would tell herself later — but her fingers were already working the knot, and the box was already open, and the top envelope was already in her hand.

Dozens of envelopes. All addressed in the same careful, sloping hand. All addressed to *Mrs E. Marsh, 9 Latchmere Crescent*. All unopened.

And all signed, in the bottom corner of each envelope where senders write their names so the postman knows where returns should go, in handwriting that Ansel Galloway knew better than her own — the handwriting she had inherited, the handwriting on forty-one returned postcards stacked in a shoebox under her bed in a rented room eleven miles away —

*Mrs M. Galloway.*

Her mother's name. Her mother's hand. Dozens of letters, over years, to a woman called Edith Marsh whom Marion Galloway had never once mentioned in her entire speaking life.

The earliest envelope was dated three weeks before the fire.

Ansel sat down on the dead woman's bed, in the dead woman's room, in the house she was hired to empty, and held the box on her knees while the dust turned slowly in the light, and the past — her finished, swept-out, properly disposed-of past — quietly stopped being finished.
