## 12. The Condemned Houses

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which felt like the council's idea of mercy—midweek, when most people were too tired to fight. By Wednesday it had been photographed, printed, and pinned to the door of the harbor café with four rusting drawing pins and one furious annotation in blue biro: *They can demolish the houses. They cannot demolish us.* The handwriting was Marisol's. I recognized the aggressive little loop on the *d*.

I read the notice twice, standing in the café doorway with a coffee going cold in my hand.

**SALTMERE BAY DISTRICT COUNCIL — NOTICE OF FINAL DEMOLITION**
*Lower Town structures 1–34 inclusive. Works to commence Monday, 14th October. All former residents must remove personal effects by Friday.*

Thirty-four structures. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. Number eleven had been ours—the Galloway house, if a house could be said to belong to anyone after ten years of salt rot and squatters' pigeons. My mother's house. The kitchen where she'd taught me to gut mackerel. The bedroom where I'd sung her a song at fourteen and then spent a decade pretending I hadn't.

"Personal effects," I said aloud, to no one. "As if we left luggage."

"You did leave luggage." Marisol appeared at my elbow with the pot, refilling my cup unasked, the way she'd been doing for three weeks now, as if I were a plant that needed regular watering and occasional turning toward the light. "Everyone did. That's what the lower town is now—a lost property office for people who couldn't come back."

"That's bleak."

"That's Vessic." She said it lightly, but the word landed heavy, because lately everything in that language landed heavy, like stones dropped into shallow water. She nodded at the notice. "We're having a festival."

"A festival."

"A farewell festival. For the houses. Before they come down." She wiped her hands on her apron, and I noticed—because I was learning to notice again—that her fingers were ink-stained at the edges, as though she'd been writing something long by hand. "The last night of the lower town. Everyone who ever lived there comes back, walks the streets one more time, sings the old songs, eats too much, cries into the fish stew. Then Monday the machines come and it's memory."

"And the council agreed to this?"

"The council agreed to nothing. We're doing it anyway. There's a difference between permission and inevitability, Theo. You of all people should know that."

I should have laughed. Instead something in my chest tightened, because she'd said *you of all people*, and I still didn't know what I was a person *of*, exactly. A translator who hadn't translated anything real in weeks. A daughter of a town that was scheduled for demolition. A woman whose mother's house was about to be fed into a crusher along with thirty-three others.

"When?" I asked.

"Saturday night. Sundown onward." Her eyes were bright and slightly terrifying, the way they got when she'd already decided something and was merely informing you of it. "There's a program. Songs, stories, the walking of the streets. Aunt Ivet is opening it—she insists, she says she buried two husbands and a fishing fleet, she can bury a street." Marisol paused, and I saw the request coming the way you see a wave coming: too late to move. "We need someone to translate the program. Into English. So the outsiders can follow—the journalists, the couple from the heritage board, Daniel if he—" She caught herself. "So everyone can understand."

"I don't—"

"It's mostly short things. Song titles. A blessing. Ivet's opening words, maybe five sentences. You've been sitting with her every afternoon for two weeks. You know more than you think you do."

That was the trap, of course. That was precisely the trap. Because I *had* been sitting with Ivet every afternoon, notebook open, catching fragments of her testimony like a man catching rain in his mouth—and some of it had lodged where I couldn't dig it out. Words rising in me unbidden at odd hours. *Denna* for the hush before wind. *Sorrela* for a grief you carry like a passenger rather than a wound. But knowing words wasn't the same as being able to stand in front of a crowd and string them together while they watched your mouth move and waited to see if the island would accept you back.

"Marisol," I said carefully, "I translate *from* Vessic. On paper. With a dictionary and unlimited time and nobody looking at me."

"So translate on paper. Read it out. Nobody's asking you to orate."

"You're asking me to stand up in front of the entire village and be the person who carries the language across water, which is—Ivet's phrase, not mine, and it has weight, Marisol, it has *teeth*—and I haven't spoken a full sentence aloud since I was fourteen years old except to a grave and a dying woman, and both times I cried, and Saturday there will be three hundred people and a heritage board and probably a drone."

Marisol looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the tide was coming in over the old seawall, patient as paperwork.

"All right," she said finally, and to my surprise she let it go. "Think about it. The program isn't printed till Friday."

But her voice had a particular gentleness in it that I distrusted immediately, the gentleness of someone who has already seen the ending of a story you're still arguing about the middle of.

* * *

I walked down into the lower town that afternoon, which I had been avoiding with the discipline of a woman avoiding a diagnosis.

It was worse than I remembered and better than I feared, which is the specific cruelty of childhood places. The street I'd lived on was still cobbled, still sloping toward the water, but the houses wore their condemnation like tags on livestock—orange plastic sheeting over the windows, spray-painted numbers, the word **UNSAFE** stenciled at knee height on every door as if the buildings themselves had misbehaved. Gulls had colonized number nineteen. Someone's garden had gone entirely to sea thrift and bindweed, and it was, obscenely, beautiful.

Number eleven stood with its door half off its hinges, hanging at an angle like a jaw that wouldn't close.

I stood in front of it for a long time. A woman passing with shopping bags—Mrs. Trelawney, I thought, older, wider, unmistakably herself—stopped beside me and followed my gaze up to the sagging porch.

"Galloway girl," she said. Not a question. In Saltmere Bay, you remained whoever you had been at twelve, indefinitely, like a fly in amber.

"Hello, Mrs. Trelawney."

"They'll knock it flat Monday." She said it the way you report weather. Then, in Vessic, soft and almost shy, as if offering something breakable: *"Kessa venni torra?"* — are you staying through the end?

And here was the thing I could not explain to anyone who had not spent ten years amputating a language from their own mouth: I understood her perfectly. Every syllable arrived whole. It was only the answering that had rotted out of me, the production line closed, the machinery sold for scrap. I stood there with the sentence glowing inside me like swallowed light and produced, after a silence long enough to embarrass us both:

"Yes. I'm—yes."

Mrs. Trelawney patted my arm with a hand like a warm brick and moved on, and I hated myself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional.

* * *

That evening I found Ivet in her chair by the window of Marisol's back room, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, watching the light go off the bay like a lamp being carried away down a corridor.

She didn't turn when I came in. She never wasted motion on arrivals; you existed when you sat down.

*"Tella,"* she said. Sit.

I sat. I opened the notebook. We worked—or rather, she spoke and I chased, for an hour, her voice moving through the testimony we'd been traversing all week: the winter of the great storm, the year the herring left, the names of boats. My pen kept pace badly. Twice she stopped mid-sentence and looked at me with those pale, unhurried eyes until I found the word she'd been waiting for me to find, and handed it to her, and she continued as if nothing had happened, which was its own kind of pedagogy.

At the end, she closed her hands in her lap and said, in English, deliberately, so there could be no mistaking:

"Saturday. You will say the words for them. The program."

"Marisol told you."

"Marisol tells me everything. It is her punishment for being born loud." A dry click that might have been laughter. "You will stand up. You will speak."

"Ivet, I can't. What if I get it wrong? What if I stand up there and the language comes out broken and everyone hears exactly how far I fell?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions, which was total.

"What if you do?" she said.

"That's not comforting."

"No. Comfort is for the well." She leaned forward, shawl slipping, and gripped my wrist with a strength that startled me, ninety-one years old and built of anchor chain. "Theo. Listen. When the storm took the fleet in 'sixty-eight, my father stood on this same shore and called the names of the drowned into the water. He got some of the names wrong. Grief had shaken them loose. Do you know what the village said afterward?" She waited. I shook my head. "Nothing. They said nothing. Because a wrong name called into the sea is worth ten right names kept in the throat." Her grip tightened. "The language does not need you perfect. It needs you *present*. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference."

My eyes were burning. I looked at the window, at the dark water, anywhere else.

"I'll think about it," I whispered, which was what I always said, which was the sentence I had used for ten years to mean *no* while sounding like *maybe*.

Ivet released my wrist. She settled back into her chair and pulled the shawl up, and just before I reached the door she said one more thing, quietly, in Vessic, and I understood it perfectly and answered nothing at all:

*"Vessa kenna dora."* The hiding place is also a room you live in.

* * *

I didn't sleep. At two in the morning I sat at the rented room's narrow desk with the festival program draft Marisol had pressed on me—handwritten, smelling of woodsmoke and ambition—and I translated the first line.

*The Last Night of the Lower Town.*

Easy. English to English, nearly. The second line was a song title: *"Orra venni, orra kell."* One tide comes, one tide goes. I wrote it out beneath, crossed it out, wrote it again. Fine. Fine. The third line was Ivet's opening, transcribed in Marisol's careful hand, five sentences of formal, old-fashioned Vessic with constructions I half-recognized from my mother's prayers, and I sat there with my pen hovering and felt the panic arrive on schedule, punctual as the demolition crews would be.

Because here is what nobody tells you about losing a language: it doesn't leave all at once. It leaves the way a shoreline erodes. First the outer words go—the ones you rarely need. Then the middle ones. And what remains is not fluency but a kind of interior coastline, beautiful and unstable, that you can walk at low tide and drown in at high. I could *hear* Ivet's five sentences. I could feel their shape against the inside of my skull like a face pressed behind glass. But to lift them out—to turn them into English in front of three hundred people, to be the vessel, the carrier, the one whose mouth the language passed through—

I put down the pen.

I picked it up. I put it down again.

At three o'clock I did the thing I am least proud of, which is saying a great deal: I folded the program closed, tucked it under the notebook, and lay down on the bed with my back to the desk, as if the papers might forget me if I forgot them first.

Retreat, my mother would have called it, with her fisherman's contempt for tactical language. *You can retreat all the way home, Theo, but then where are you?*

Where indeed. Home was scheduled for demolition on Monday.

* * *

In the morning Marisol found me at the harbor wall, drinking coffee I didn't want and watching the holdouts string bunting between the condemned lampposts—actual bunting, orange and white, defiantly festive, hung between notices of structural failure. Somewhere down the street someone was rehearsing a fiddle tune with more enthusiasm than accuracy.

"Well?" she said.

"I can't do it, Mari."

She didn't argue. That was the worst part. She just stood beside me, forearms on the wall, looking out at the water where the old town's reflection wavered and broke and reassembled itself, imperfect, every second.

"You know what I did for ten years?" she said at last. "Every night, I sang your song. Alone. In a kitchen. To nobody. And every single time, I thought: this is ridiculous, she's not coming back, the song doesn't even know it's hers anymore. And I sang it anyway. Do you know why?"

"Because you're stubborn."

"Because a thing sung is a thing kept. Even badly. Especially badly." She turned and looked at me, and there was no anger in her face, which was somehow more devastating than anger. "Nobody's asking you to be fluent, Theo. We're asking you to be there. There's a difference, and—"

"—and it's the whole difference. Yes. Ivet quoted you."

"She quoted *me*? I said it first?"

"You've clearly been coordinating."

"We're a village. Coordination is all we have left." She pushed off the wall and brushed biscuit crumbs from her apron, businesslike again, the door held open one more hour. "Program prints tomorrow noon. If you change your mind, you know where I am. And Theo—if you don't—that's allowed too. But then Saturday you sit in the front row, and you listen, and you let the language find you instead of you finding it. Either way, you're in the room."

She walked off toward the bunting, calling something to the fiddler, laughing at the answer.

I stayed at the wall a long time. Below me the tide slid in over the old seawall, covering stone that had stood for two hundred years and would stand, presumably, until Monday's machines decided otherwise. In my pocket, the folded program pressed against my hip like a small, patient accusation.

Five sentences. Ivet's five sentences. I could hear them still, glowing behind the glass.

I took out the paper, unfolded it, and read the third line again. And again. My lips moved around the first word without sound—just the shape of it, the muscle memory stirring like a leg that had been asleep.

Then footsteps behind me on the cobbles, quick and light, and Marisol's voice, breathless, pitched wrong—not festival-bright but tight, urgent, afraid:

"Theo. Come quickly. It's Ivet."
