## 13. Words That Don't Come Back

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it keeps a calendar. Mine ran ten years late, and now that it had finally arrived, it wanted everything at once—back pay, interest, the whole outstanding balance of a decade. I had wept over a cassette tape like a reasonable person. I had said four broken words of Vessic to a ninety-one-year-old woman and felt something in my chest unclick like a rusted latch. I had, against all evidence, begun to believe the worst was over.

The worst, as it turned out, was a blank page.

I sat in my rented room above the chandlery with Ivet's testimony spread across the bed—the recordings Marisol had made on her phone, the transcriptions Ivet had insisted on dictating in Vessic because, she said, "the words come out different in English, and I want the true ones first"—and I tried to translate the first sentence, and the language simply wasn't there.

Not diminished. Not rusty. *Gone.* The way a tide goes out and takes the sand with it.

The first sentence was about herring. *Thal vennu, thal vennu, imma ketha sorrela*—and I knew *thal* was fish, or possibly the sea, or possibly both, because Vessic had never seen much point in separating the fish from the water they swam in, and I knew *imma* meant *when*, and the rest was fog. I had spoken this language before I spoke English. I had dreamed in it. And now I sat with a sentence about herring like a man holding a key that no longer fit any lock in the house.

I tried the second sentence. Worse. I tried the third and caught myself reaching for the Vessic word for *winter* and finding only *winter*, sitting there in my head, smug and monolingual, like a landlord who'd changed the locks.

"You're joking," I said aloud, to no one, in English, because apparently that was all I had.

I had translated four novels from Portuguese. I had a working knowledge of Catalan strong enough to argue with a waiter in Barcelona about whether the fish was fresh (it was not). I had once rendered a Chilean poet's entire collection about the disappearance of his brother into English and made a grown editor cry at a launch party. Words were supposed to be the one thing I could do. Words were the room I lived in. And the one language that had ever been *mine*—not learned, not acquired, but poured into me before I could consent—had packed itself up and left no forwarding address.

I did what any professional would do. I made a list.

*Words I have lost, a partial inventory:*

*The word for the gap between the sea and the sky at dusk. (I used to know it. I used to point at it.)*
*The word for a wave that has already decided to break.*
*The word my mother used for the space under the stairs, which was also the word she used for the space inside grief, which was also, I think, the word for the inside of a shell.*
*The word for "enough." There is no English "enough" that means what that one meant.*

I stared at the list. The list stared back. Somewhere below my window, the sea did its tireless arithmetic against the seawall, and I thought: *there is probably a word for that sound, too, and I don't have it, and my mother knew it, and she is under six feet of clifftop turf with the whole vocabulary of a people, and I am up here with a notebook and a pen running out of ink.*

The rage came on slowly, the way weather comes. It started as heat in my ears and became a full atmospheric event. Ten years. Ten years I had told myself the language was safe, banked, sleeping in some cold storage of the self, retrievable on demand like a coat from a wardrobe. Ten years of silence, and I had called it *keeping*. And now, when it finally mattered—when a woman older than the seawall had handed me the last living testimony of an entire way of being human and said *carry it across water*—I opened the wardrobe and found it empty except for one wire hanger, swinging.

I thought about the relatives at the funeral. *Shameful island noise.* I thought about how quickly I had agreed with them, how fast I'd been to translate myself into something a London dinner party could digest. I thought about Daniel saying *you're so articulate when you let yourself be* and how I had glowed at that, *articulate*, as if articulateness were a virtue and not just a smaller cage with better lighting.

And then I did the thing I am not proud of.

I took the notebook to the little electric heater in the corner of the room, the one with the exposed coil that the landlady had assured me was "perfectly safe, mostly," and I held the notebook above it. The cover began to warm. The pages—my catalog of losses, my inventory of everything I'd surrendered—curled slightly at the corner nearest the coil, and I thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy: *if I can't have the words, I won't have the record of not having them either.*

That's the thing about rage. It doesn't want to fix anything. It wants to destroy the evidence.

The corner of the page browned. I smelled it—that dry, sweet, terrible smell of paper beginning to go—and some small sane clerk in the back office of my brain filed a complaint, and I pulled the notebook back, and I sat down on the floor with it against my chest, and I did not cry, because apparently I had used up my ration on the cassette, and instead I just breathed like a woman who has narrowly talked herself out of a very stupid crime.

That was when someone knocked.

* * *

Ivet did not wait for me to answer the door, because Ivet was ninety-one and had stopped waiting for doors decades ago. She came in on Marisol's arm, took one look at me—on the floor, notebook clutched to my sternum, the heater still ticking with residual heat, the smell of scorched paper in the air—and said something in Vessic that I understood perfectly and could not have translated for a thousand pounds.

It was the word my mother used when I'd done something foolish and been caught. The word that meant, roughly, *well, look at this disaster, and she's mine.*

"Sit down, Aunt Ivet," Marisol said, easing her into the room's one decent chair. Marisol looked at me, at the heater, at the notebook. She didn't ask. That's a thing about Marisol: she never asks a question she already knows the answer to, and she knows the answer to nearly everything, because she stayed, and staying teaches you things that leaving never will.

"I can't do it," I said. English, flat, defeated. "The testimony. I sat down with the first sentence and it's—it's gone. It's not rusty, it's not slow, it's *gone*. There's a sentence about herring and I know it's beautiful and I can't tell you why. I know the shape of the language and none of the contents. I've been standing in this house my whole life and someone's come in the night and taken every piece of furniture and left the rooms exactly the same size."

Ivet listened to all of this with her head tilted, the way she did, like a heron deciding whether you were worth the strike. Then she said, in slow, deliberate English—she used English the way she used her stick, only when the terrain required it:

"Show me the herring."

I played it. Her own voice, recorded three winters ago, saying the sentence about the herring boats going out at first light, and I watched her face while I listened to a sentence I could feel the beauty of and not touch, like glass over a thing in a museum. When it finished she was quiet a moment.

"Say it," she said.

"I can't."

"Say what you have."

"*Thal vennu,*" I said, and stopped, because the next part was a white wall. "*Imma—*" and stopped again. "That's it. That's everything. Two and a half words out of a whole sentence about the most important thing that ever happened to this village."

Ivet nodded slowly, as if I had confirmed something she'd suspected for a long time. Then she said, in Vessic, and I understood every word, which was its own small cruelty:

*You are counting what is missing from the cupboard. You have not once looked at what is still in it.*

"I don't understand what's still in it," I said. "That's the whole problem. I open my mouth and—"

"You hid it." She said it in English, and the English was accented and exact. "You did not lose it. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. A thing that is lost is gone from you. A thing that is hidden is *in* you, and you have put a door over it, and you have been standing in front of the door for ten years calling the door a wall."

I opened my mouth to argue—I had a whole argument loaded, ten years of evidence, the blank page, the herring, the smug monolingual *winter* sitting in my head like a squatter—and she raised one hand, one finger, and I stopped, because you stopped, when Ivet raised a finger. Everyone stopped. Probably the tide would have stopped, if asked.

"When the soldiers' language came to the school," she said, "the children who fought it, who spoke Vessic in the yard, they were beaten, and they were beaten worse the next time, and some of them lost the language by force, and it broke something in them that never healed. But the clever ones—" and here something moved across her face that was not quite a smile, "—the clever ones learned to hide it. They spoke English in the classroom and Vessic under the blankets. They sang the songs only in their heads. They put the language somewhere the school couldn't reach. Do you know what we called that?"

I shook my head.

"We called it *kessa vennu*. The keeping-under. And we did not think of it as losing. We thought of it as burying seed. You do not bury seed because you are finished with it. You bury it because there is a winter, and you are waiting for the winter to end."

"Ten years is a long winter," I said.

"It is," she agreed. "And you are not dead, and the seed is not dead, or you would not be sitting on that floor smelling of burnt paper looking like a woman whose house has been robbed. A robbed woman knows what she's lost. You—" and she pointed the finger at me again, gently this time, "—you know what you *have*, you just refuse to open the cupboards because the last time you opened them, someone told you the contents were shameful."

"That's not—" I started, and then stopped, because it was exactly that, and had been exactly that since a churchyard in 2013 when a woman in a good coat had said *island noise* and I had folded the whole language up small enough to fit in a pocket and then forgotten which pocket.

"Hiding is also keeping," Ivet said. "Say it."

"That's ridiculous—"

"Say it."

"Hiding is also keeping," I said, in English, and then—without deciding to, without any permission from the committee in my head that usually approved such transactions—I said it in Vessic. *Kessa imma kessa.* The words came out rough and half-remembered and possibly grammatically wrong, and they came out, and the room did not end, and Ivet's heron-tilted head inclined a single degree, which from Ivet was a standing ovation.

"Again," she said.

* * *

We worked for two hours. Not translating—there was no translating to be done yet—but excavating. Ivet would say a word, and I would reach for it, and half the time I'd come up with nothing, and she'd say the word again, slower, and somewhere around the fourth or fifth repetition something would surface—not from memory exactly, memory being too orderly a word for it, but from somewhere deeper and less respectable, like fish rising to bread thrown on water. The word for the gap between sea and sky at dusk: *sorrela*, which was also, I discovered with a jolt, in the herring sentence, which meant the first line of Ivet's testimony was not about fish at all, it was about that hour, that light, the boats going out into the place where the water and the sky couldn't be told apart. I had been holding a poem and calling it a failure.

"Your mother had the best *sorrela* of anyone," Ivet said, apropos of nothing, watching me write. "She would stand at the wall at that hour and just look at it. I used to tell her, Maren, the fish won't wait. She'd say, Ivet, the fish are the least of it."

I wrote *sorrela* in the notebook, over the scorch mark, because it seemed right that the word for the gap between sea and sky should sit exactly where I'd tried to burn the record of my losses. Some words you earn by destruction.

By the time Marisol came back up with tea—strong, over-sweet, the way the lower town made it—I had eleven words. Eleven words out of, I don't know, ten thousand. It was the worst ratio of any translation project of my career, and I have never in my life been so pleased with a day's work.

Ivet let Marisol settle the tea, and then she said, in English, in that stick-on-rocky-ground way: "Tomorrow, you will go to the cemetery."

It wasn't a question. Ivet didn't traffic in questions.

"The tide's been taking the wall," Marisol said quietly, not looking at me. "Your mother's row is next, maybe. The council won't move the stones. They say it's not cost-effective." A pause with a whole argument inside it. "People have been going up to say goodbye to their people before the sea does it for them. It's a thing we do. It's an old thing."

I looked at my notebook. Eleven words. One of them the word for the hour when the sea and sky stop being separate, which was, I was beginning to understand, the hour my mother had loved best and the hour the cemetery faced and the hour, presumably, when goodbyes were meant to be said in this village, because of course it was, because Vessic had a word for it, because Vessic had had a word for everything and I had buried the whole vocabulary like seed and called the burying a betrayal.

"Say goodbye properly," Ivet said. Not gently. Ivet was not a gentle woman; she was a true one, which is harder and lasts longer. "Not the English goodbye, which is a door closing. The Vessic one, which is a boat going out. You will know the difference when you say it."

"And if the words don't come?" I asked.

Ivet drank her tea. She looked out the window at the light doing its evening work on the bay, at the condemned houses below with their windows like closed eyes, at the seawall holding, holding, holding.

"Then you will stand at her stone," she said, "with your empty mouth, and you will open it anyway. The seed knows the way up. It always has. It's only waiting for you to stop standing on the ground."

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. I sat with the notebook and the recordings and I made a new list, and this one I titled differently.

*Words I have kept, a partial inventory:*

*The word for the gap between the sea and the sky at dusk.*
*The word for hiding that is also keeping.*
*The word my mother used when I was a disaster and hers.*
*The beginning of a sentence about herring, which is not about herring.*

Four entries. The page looked almost empty. But I found, somewhere around three in the morning, that I had stopped reading it as a record of what was missing, and started reading it as a planting schedule.

Below my window, the tide came in against the seawall, patient as a creditor, and I thought about the clifftop, and the row of stones the council had written off, and my mother under the turf with her *sorrela* and her shell-word for the inside of grief, and I understood that tomorrow I was going to have to stand at that stone and open my mouth with no idea what would come out of it.

I was, I realized, more frightened of that than I had been of the blank page.

The blank page, at least, couldn't hear me.
