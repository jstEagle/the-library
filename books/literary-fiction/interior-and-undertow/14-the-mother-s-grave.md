## 14. The Mother's Grave

The cemetery sat on the cliff above the old town like a congregation that had gotten out just in time.

I'd forgotten that about Saltmere Bay—how the dead got the best views. The living were packed down in the valley with the damp and the fish smell and the slow catastrophe of the tide, while the dead were arranged in neat rows on the headland, facing the water they'd all spent their lives pretending not to fear. My mother was up there somewhere, third row from the seaward wall, if my memory of the funeral still worked, which it mostly didn't. Grief had edited that day heavily. What remained was weather: grey sky, grey sea, a lot of black coats, and Aunt Ivet's voice going on and on in Vessic while I stood there understanding every word and saying none of them.

Ten years later I climbed the cliff path alone, in October, with the wind doing its best to remove me from the narrative.

I had told myself this was research. That word had done a lot of heavy lifting since I arrived. *Research* was what I called it when I walked past the condemned houses on Marine Row. *Research* when I sat in the harbor café for two hours nursing one coffee and watching Marisol through the window, memorizing the way she moved, older now, broader in the shoulders, her hair gone from black to something like iron. *Research* when I lay awake in my rented room above the chandlery listening to the sea work at the seawall like a patient burglar.

But you can't do research on your mother's grave. There's no methodology for it. No footnote where a footnote should be.

The path was worse than I remembered—or rather, it was exactly as bad as I remembered, which meant ten years of erosion had happened to it too. Sheep-cropped turf, then gorse, then the long exposed shoulder of the headland where the wind came off the Atlantic with nothing to stop it and no interest in being stopped. I walked with my head down and my hands in my pockets and thought, as I always thought on this path, about the day we carried her up it. Four men and a box. The whole village behind them, because in Saltmere Bay you didn't decide whether to attend a funeral; you decided only how early to arrive so you wouldn't be seen arriving late.

I found her stone by process of elimination. The third row from the seaward wall had thinned since I'd been gone—not the graves themselves but the stones, several of them leaning now, one lying flat as if it had simply given up mid-sentence. And hers—

Hers.

*MERREN EASTWICK*, said the granite. Below that, in English, the dates. Below that, the inscription my father's family had chosen, over whatever objections my mother would have raised if she'd been consulted, which she hadn't been, being recently deceased: *AT REST*.

At rest. As if she'd been tired. As if the whole problem—the language, the island, the marriage, me—had been a matter of insufficient napping.

The stone was weathered in the way things weather here, which is not gently. The salt had gotten into the letters, blurring them, so that *EASTWICK* looked soft-edged, half-dissolved, a word the sea was already negotiating to take back. Lichen had colonized the lower left corner in a map of nowhere. Someone had left flowers once—a plastic bunch, faded to the color of weak tea, wedged into the vase at the base—but not recently. Not for years, probably. My father hadn't come back after he left, and I hadn't come back at all, and apparently that arithmetic was simple enough even for granite.

"Hello," I said, in English, because that was the language I used for impossible situations. Job interviews. Apologies. This.

The wind took the word and did something dismissive with it.

I crouched down, knees cracking in a way that suggested I was no longer fourteen either, and touched the stone. It was cold, obviously. Granite is always cold; that's the deal. But there's cold and there's cold, and this was the kind that seemed to have an opinion about you.

I had prepared things to say. That was the humiliating part—I'd actually prepared them, sitting in my rented room the night before with the notebook open, drafting remarks for a conversation with a rock. In English, of course. Because when I reached for Vessic these days, English got there first, standing in the doorway with its arms crossed like a bouncer who'd learned my face.

*I'm sorry I stayed away.* Drafted. Discarded—it sounded like a resignation letter.

*I've been translating someone's testimony.* True, but what was I expecting, approval? A posthumous pat on the head?

*You should see what they've done to the lower town.* She'd seen it coming. Everyone had seen it coming; that was the island's whole personality. Seeing it coming and staying anyway.

I knelt there in the wind with my drafts and my silence, and the sea went on below, working at the seawall, patient as a creditor.

Here is what nobody tells you about burying a language: it doesn't feel like burial. It feels like tidying. You put a word away because someone laughed at it—you put away *shen* and *vessa* and the whole soft grammar of home—and you tell yourself it's temporary, it's practical, you'll need the shelf space later. And then one day you're thirty-four years old in a clifftop cemetery and you reach for the shelf and find it empty, dusted, arranged, and you understand that the tidying was the whole event. The burial happened in installments. You attended none of them.

My mother had sung to me in Vessic before I could walk. She'd scolded me in it, teased me in it, counted stairs in it, sworn at the weather in it. It was the sound of her thinking. And I had let relatives—aunts by marriage, women with voices like slammed car doors—call it shameful island noise, and I had said nothing, and then I had said less than nothing, and then I had said nothing in a second language, fluently, for a decade.

Except once. Once, at the end, in the hospital room that smelled of antiseptic and cut flowers going over, I had sung her the lullaby I'd made myself, out of pieces of the old songs, words I half understood stitched onto a tune I'd invented on the walk over. Sung it once, badly, quietly, into the space above her pillow while machines kept her time for her. Her eyes had opened. She hadn't spoken—there wasn't anything left to speak with—but her mouth had moved around the edges of the chorus, following, and I have spent ten years telling myself that was imagination, that dying women don't lip-sync, that I invented the whole thing afterward because I needed it.

And then a radio host in London had played that same lullaby on a Tuesday night and called it a folk song from Saltmere Bay, and the ground under that story had opened up, and here I was.

Marisol had kept singing it. Ten years of singing it, alone, into a cassette recorder, harmonizing with a ghost girl's voice. I still didn't fully know what to do with that. There was a version of me that wanted to be furious, and a version that wanted to kneel to her too, and they were taking turns.

The wind shifted. Below me the old town showed its teeth—roofless houses, the seawall dark with weed, the demolition notices flapping on doors like the world's most bureaucratic autumn. Tomorrow or the next day the council vans would come. The festival was Saturday. Ivet's testimony sat in my notebook, forty pages of it, translated and untranslated, waiting for me to be equal to it.

And I understood, kneeling there, that all of it funneled down to this patch of lichen and cold stone. Everything else was rehearsal.

"You'd laugh at me," I said to the stone. "You'd say—" and I reached for the phrase, her phrase, the one she used when I was being precious about my homework, my pronunciation, my carefully correct little life—

English offered: *you'd say I was being ridiculous.*

Vessic stood further back, arms folded, letting me come to it.

"*Nellu vash,*" I tried. You'd laugh. It came out wrong—I could hear it come out wrong, the stress on the wrong syllable, the vowel flattened by ten years of London vowels, *nellu* sounding like something you'd order in a café. A gull screamed overhead, possibly in correction.

But it was out. It was in the air. The wind took it the way it had taken "hello," and the difference was invisible from outside, but I felt it go through me like a swallowed coal.

I stayed crouched there, heart going, absurdly frightened, as if the sentence might turn around and bite. As if some authority might arrive to fine me for speaking without a license. Nobody came. The sheep continued their program of ecological vandalism along the far slope. The sea kept its own counsel.

"*Merren.*" Her name, in the island way, not the English way—two beats, the second falling. Saying it felt like stepping on a stair that wasn't where your foot expected. "*Merren. Nessa veth i shen.*"

*I am here. I brought the grief.*

That last part wasn't idiomatic. I knew it wasn't idiomatic even as I said it—*nessa veth* was closer to *I carry* than *I bring*, and *shen* was bigger than grief, it was the word for the specific sorrow of things that leave, tides, birds, people, words. My mother had used it about the herring fleet. I was using it about myself. The grammar groaned a little under the weight.

It didn't matter. That was the thing I hadn't known, couldn't have known from inside the silence: it doesn't have to be right. It has to be said. A language doesn't live in correctness; it lives in mouths, in air, in the disgraceful imperfect present tense of someone trying. Ivet had told me that, in her way, without saying it—by refusing English, by making me stumble toward her across the broken ground of my own childhood vocabulary until we met somewhere in the middle, both of us limping, both of us understood.

"*Shen veth i nellu vash,*" I said again, getting it more wrong the second time, laughing at myself getting it wrong, and hearing—in the laugh, in the flat vowels, in the ruined beautiful grammar—her voice underneath mine like a watermark.

I talked to her for a long time after that. I won't reproduce it; most of it was incoherent, and some of it was in English anyway, because grief is bilingual whether you like it or not. I told her about the testimony, about Ivet choosing English for her children and calling it salvation, and watched my mother's whole life rearrange itself around that fact like furniture after a flood. I told her about Marisol and the tape. I told her about Daniel, briefly, in the way you tell a dead parent about a relationship you already know is ending—carefully, in past tense, testing the fit.

I told her I was sorry. In both languages. Twice each. It sounded inadequate in both, which was somehow comforting; at least I was failing equally.

The light changed. It does that here in October without warning—one moment the sea is pewter, the next it's burning, the sun finding a gap in the cloud and setting the whole bay alight, the condemned houses glowing gold like something worth saving. I turned and looked at it properly for the first time since I'd arrived. The lower town. My house among them, the blue door surely rotted off its hinges by now, the window I'd climbed out of at fifteen still holding its shape against the sky.

They were going to knock it down. All of it. And the village had decided—Marisol had decided, Ivet had decided, the holdouts and the returners and the ones who never left—that before it went, it would be sung to. That you don't let a place die unattended. That farewell is a skill, and skills must be practiced.

I had declined to translate the festival program. I'd heard myself say no and watched my own mouth do it, the old reflex, fluent self-erasure, and I'd hated it even as the word left me.

Kneeling there, with the light burning and my mother's name dissolving letter by letter under my hand, I found I was no longer available for that particular lie.

"I'll do it," I said aloud—to her, to the stone, to the sea, to whoever was taking minutes. "The program. The translation. All of it." My voice cracked on *translation*, which felt accurate. "I'll stand up in front of everyone and read words I'm not sure of, in a language I broke, and if I get it wrong they can correct me. That's the job. That's the whole job."

The wind pushed at me, meaninglessly, or meaning everything.

I pressed my palm flat against the granite one more time—cold, opinionated, hers—and stood up. My knees objected. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, I could hear the chandlery radio, and beneath it, thinner, someone practicing a song. I knew the song. Of course I knew the song. It had my voice in it.

I started down the path, and halfway to the gate I stopped, turned back, and gave the cemetery one last sentence, full volume, into the wind, halting and wrong and entirely mine:

"*Nessa kella, Merren. Shen veth. I kessa vorth.*"

*I'm going now. The grief comes with me. I'll carry it across.*

Then I walked down toward the lights coming on in the condemned houses, and the almost-saying was over, and the saying had begun—and at the bottom of the cliff path, waiting by the gate with her coat collar up and her eyes red-rimmed, stood Marisol, and behind her, unmistakable even at distance, even in the failing light, the small fierce figure of Aunt Ivet, leaning on her stick like the last word of a sentence the island refused to finish.
