## 15. Festival Preparations

### Marisol

You can tell what a village believes by what it cooks when it expects to lose something. Saltmere Bay, facing the demolition of the lower town, has decided to believe in bread. Not a loaf of it, not a symbolic crust passed hand to hand for the cameras — bread the way my grandmother made it, forty loaves at a time, in an oven older than the seawall, because if we are going to mourn the houses we might as well do it with flour under our fingernails.

I have been up since four. The oven behind the chandlery takes three hours to heat and another hour to stop sulking, and Aunt Ivet says — said, before her stroke, and now says again in her slow, careful way, each word set down like a cup on a saucer — that bread baked in grief comes out flat unless you argue with someone while you knead it.

So we argue. It is practically liturgy.

"It's a funeral," says Denzil Prowse, who has not agreed with anyone since 1987 and considers this his contribution to civic life. "You don't put bunting on a coffin."

"You put flowers," I say, punching dough. "Bunting is flowers for buildings."

"Bunting is English," he says, with the satisfaction of a man landing a blow he's been practicing all week. "Vessic doesn't even have a word for it."

"Vessic didn't have a word for *television* either, Denzil, and you've got one in every room."

He glowers. The dough rises anyway. That is the thing about arguments here — they are less about winning than about keeping the fire going, and everyone knows their part.

Theo arrives at seven, carrying her sewing box like a shield. She has been coming to things for two weeks now — the planning meetings, the banner-cutting, yesterday the argument about whether the festival should end at sundown or after dark — and she still stands slightly outside every circle, as though waiting to be told the entry fee. Nobody tells her. That's how it works here. You're in when you stop asking.

She holds up the first finished banner and I forget, briefly, how to breathe.

It's the word for *return* — *senva* — stitched in blue thread on white cloth, and she has gotten the second vowel wrong. Not badly wrong. Wrong the way someone is wrong who learned a language from the inside and then locked the door on it for ten years; the accent of the body survives even when the memory doesn't.

"The second one curves down," I say gently. "*Senva.* Like water going back out."

She looks at the letter. Then at me. Then, unexpectedly, she laughs — a short, startled sound, like a bird hitting a window and discovering it was open all along.

"Like water going back out," she repeats, and unpicks the stitch without shame, which is more than Denzil has managed in thirty-eight years.

By noon there are nine banners strung across the chandlery yard, snapping in the wind off the bay. *Senva.* *Kessa* — home, the kind you carry. The word for the pause between waves. The word for a name spoken so it can be let go. People come out of the condemned houses to look at them, arms folded, saying nothing, and I watch them read their own language hanging above a street the council has already erased from its maps, and I think: resurrection, then. Whatever Denzil says.

Though I notice he takes photographs of the banners when he thinks no one is watching. On his television-room telephone.

### Theo

I have sewn exactly twice in my adult life: a button onto Daniel's coat, and a hem I abandoned halfway through in favor of safety pins. So it is absurd that I am now responsible for eleven meters of Vessic orthography, and yet here I am, in Marisol's kitchen, threading needles with the concentration of a bomb-disposal officer, because it turns out that stitching a word letter by letter is the only way I can bear to write it.

On paper, my hand shakes. In thread, it doesn't. There is something about the slowness — the fact that each letter costs me ninety seconds and a stabbed thumb — that makes honesty affordable. I cannot rush a banner. I cannot soften it into palatable English. If I put *kessa* on cloth, *kessa* stays *kessa*, curved vowels and all, flapping over a street scheduled for destruction, saying what it says.

Marisol corrects me constantly and I have stopped minding. This is new information about myself. In London, correction felt like eviction. Here it feels like being handed the right key after years of jiggling the wrong one in the lock.

"The word for *festival*," I say, working the needle through the fabric. "Is it the mourning word or the—"

"There isn't a separate one." She's grating beetroot for something that will stain everything within a five-meter radius. "Same word. *Lenna.* Gathering. The old people used to say you find out which kind it was afterward."

"That seems administratively irresponsible."

"That seems very Daniel of you," she says, not looking up, and I drop a stitch and pretend I didn't.

We don't talk about him much. He calls in the evenings and I take the calls on the harbor wall where the signal is bad enough to be an excuse, and afterwards I stand there watching the water and translating the conversation into itself — *I miss you* meaning *come back*, meaning *be smaller*, meaning *the version of you I keep in London*. Then I go back inside and sew.

In the afternoon the children arrive. This was Marisol's doing — she has been collecting them all week from the new school up the hill, promising them paint and sugar, and they descend on the kitchen like weather. There are six of them, ranging from a girl of about ten named Petra who looks at me with unnerving directness, to twins who spend most of the visit trying to eat the thread.

Petra picks up one of my practice scraps — the failed *senva*, the vowel curving the wrong way — and studies it.

"This is wrong," she announces.

"I know."

"My nan says you're the one who left."

The kitchen goes quiet in the particular way kitchens go quiet when six children are listening. Marisol keeps grating. I keep sewing, because if I stop my hands will do something embarrassing.

"She's right," I say. "I did leave."

"Why?"

Because relatives called it shameful island noise. Because I was fourteen and wanted to be from nowhere. Because grief is heavy and English is light and I chose light. None of these are answers for a ten-year-old.

"Because I forgot how," I say finally. "And I'm learning again. That's why the letters are wrong."

Petra considers this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she takes the scrap, turns it over, and draws the correct vowel on the back in biro, curving down, like water going back out.

"There," she says. "Now it's right."

I look at the biro correction on the back of my failure and feel something shift in my chest that I refuse, for the moment, to examine.

### Denzil

Nobody asked me to write anything down but somebody has to, so it's me, same as the flood committee, same as the mooring register. I'm keeping the list of what the festival needs, and I'll tell you what it needs: it needs to stop growing.

Monday it was songs and bread. Tuesday someone added dancing, which we haven't done since the Queen's jubilee and did badly then. Wednesday Marisol wants lanterns floated out past the seawall, which is against approximately four maritime regulations and common sense besides. And now the Callaghans' boy is talking about a bonfire on the shingle, and Mrs. Tregowan has decided the whole thing must begin with a procession from the church down to the water, in order, oldest first, which means Denzil Prowse at the front of it because I am the oldest and I resent the implication.

But here's what I'll admit, since nobody reads this but me: when I walked through the lower town this morning and saw those banners — blue thread on white, our mother-tongue hung out like washing over streets the council calls uninhabitable — I stood there longer than a man should stand in the wind. My father spoke Vessic to his boats. Never to us. Said it wasn't a language for the house. I believed him for sixty years, the way you believe a seawall.

It's a funeral, I've been saying. But I caught myself humming one of the old songs while I wrote the list, and I'll tell you something about funerals: the dead don't hum back.

So. Resurrection, possibly. I've amended the minutes.

### Mrs. Tregowan

Everyone assumes that because I am eighty-three and bossy I must be against the festival, and everyone is wrong, which is my favorite position to occupy. I am *for* it. I simply intend for it to be done properly, which in Saltmere Bay is a full-time occupation requiring vigilance, stamina, and a certain cruelty with seating arrangements.

The question everyone keeps asking — mourning or resurrection? — strikes me as a question asked by people with nothing useful to do. Of course it is both. Everything worth doing here is both. We bury our dead in ground that floods; we christen boats with salt water; we sing at weddings in the same minor key we sing at gravesides. Any fool could have told the council that a town is not a location. A town is a habit performed by enough people at once.

My contribution is the food, and I have organized it along strict generational lines: the old women cook the dishes nobody younger remembers, the middle ones carry things, and the young ones are to be stationed at tables and required to ask questions. Asking is the whole trick. My mother taught me that. A recipe written down is a corpse in a drawer. A recipe asked for is alive and annoyed at you, which is better.

Today I supervised the salting of fish in the condemned kitchen of the old Harrow house, which the council says is unsafe, and which is unsafe, and which we used anyway, because some ovens you don't bake in, you just stand near. Young Theo came — the translator, Marisol's sister in every way that counts and no way that paperwork recognizes — and stood in the doorway with that look she has, the one like a woman reading a menu in a language she almost speaks.

I put a knife in her hand and pointed at onions. You don't ask permission in my kitchen; you grieve or you chop.

She chopped. Badly at first, then better, and somewhere around the third onion she started humming — that song, the lullaby, the one half this village has heard Marisol sing for ten years without knowing whose it was. She didn't seem to know she was doing it. The other women heard it and went quiet in the good way, the way you go quiet when a fire catches.

Mourning or resurrection. Hm. Ask the onions.

### Theo

Evening, and the banners are done: twelve of them, strung and re-strung until Denzil pronounced them "adequate," which Marisol informs me is the highest honor he confers on anything short of his own opinions. The words curve across the condemned street like a sentence someone is speaking slowly, giving the sea time to listen.

*Senva. Kessa. Lenna.* Return. Home-carried. Gathering-that-is-mourning-or-resurrection-depending-on-afterward.

I sit on the harbor steps with the practice scraps in my lap — my failures, corrected in biro by a ten-year-old, kept now like evidence. Petra's vowel stares up at me, curving down, like water going back out. Like a tide. Like a translation traveling the only direction that matters.

Tomorrow we finish the lanterns. The day after, the procession, oldest first, Denzil leading us into whatever this turns out to have been. I have been asked — Marisol asked, carefully, the way you offer food to a stray — whether I will read the program aloud in Vessic, both languages, standing in front of everyone. I said I'd think about it. What I am actually doing is rehearsing it silently against the sound of the water, testing each word for cracks.

Most of them hold. Some of them don't. I am learning the difference between a word I've lost and a word I'm hiding, and it is slower work than sewing.

Down the beach, Marisol is showing the children how to weight the lanterns, and their voices carry — English, mostly, but threaded now with the other thing, the small hard bright words they're picking up like shells. One of them sings a snatch of the lullaby, wrong notes and all, and the others laugh and join in, worse.

I listen. I don't correct them.

Above the street, the banners snap in the wind, and I am still sitting there, still mouthing the program to the tide, when headlights swing down the hill road — too many of them, moving too slowly, in convoy — and stop at the top of the lane, engines idling, facing the lower town.
