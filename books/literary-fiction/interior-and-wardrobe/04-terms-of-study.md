## 4. Terms of Study

I returned to the cottage on a Tuesday because Tuesdays felt contractual. Mondays were for beginnings, and I did not want this to be a beginning; Fridays were for endings, and I refused to think about what it might become. Tuesday was the day of the middle of things, and I intended to remain firmly in the middle—of a project, of a commission, of a professional arrangement with clear deliverables.

I had prepared documents.

Not metaphorical documents. Actual documents, printed at an internet café in the village because my laptop battery had died on the coast road and the cottage had no Wi-Fi, which Halvard referred to as "the quiet" as though it were a feature he'd personally installed. The documents were three pages long. They covered hours, fees, cancellation terms, intellectual property, data protection, and a clause about recording permissions that I had drafted at one in the morning with the particular feverishness of a woman building a wall out of A4 paper.

Three pages. I had even brought a pen with a satisfying click.

Halvard met me at the door holding a colander full of mussels.

"Good," he said, looking at the folder under my arm. "You've brought the paperwork. We'll need something to prop the cupboard door shut."

"It's a contract," I said.

"It's a very tidy object," he agreed, and shuffled through to the kitchen, leaving me to close the door behind myself, which I did, carefully, like someone entering a church or a trap.

* * *

The kitchen, I was learning, was not a room in Halvard's house so much as the house's governing body. The sitting room existed, presumably, in some ceremonial capacity, but everything that mattered happened at the kitchen table: the eating, the talking, the listening to tapes, the slow ceremonial dismantling of a loaf of dark bread that he baked himself and defended with the fervor of a man discussing borders.

He set the colander in the sink and gestured at the table. "Sit. We'll talk terms over food. That's how it's done."

"Over food," I repeated, sitting anyway, because refusing would have required a kind of energy I hadn't budgeted for.

"In Vessic there's a saying." He lowered himself into his chair with the sound of a ship settling at anchor. "*Nessa vellu, kessa vellu.* Bread shared, words shared. It doesn't translate well. Something is lost about the bread."

"Everything is lost about the bread when you translate it," I said. "That's the job."

He looked at me for a moment with an expression I couldn't file—somewhere between amusement and diagnosis—and then began sorting mussels into two bowls: open ones and ones that had decided against participating. "You'll want tea."

"I'll want to discuss the structure of our sessions."

"You'll want tea first. Structure goes down better with tea. This is also in the old way." He filled the kettle without waiting for my answer, which I was beginning to understand was his general method with the world.

I opened my folder while his back was turned. "Page one covers scheduling. I propose three sessions per week, two hours each, fixed times. Fixed times matter. In my experience, flexible arrangements drift."

"Drift," he said, tasting the word. "You say that like it's a bad thing. Tides drift. That's rather their whole profession."

"Tides have somewhere to go. Flexible arrangements just dissolve."

"Mm." He set a bowl of mussels in front of me, then the tea, then a jar of honey with a spoon standing up in it like a flag planted in conquered territory. "Read me your page one."

I read it. I had written it to be read aloud—I always did, testing sentences against the ear the way a joiner tests a shelf against the level—and it sounded, in that kitchen, absurdly formal, like reading a lease agreement to a lighthouse. *The student shall arrive punctually. Sessions shall commence at nine o'clock and conclude at eleven. Breaks shall be limited to ten minutes.*

"Conclude at eleven," Halvard repeated. "And what happens at eleven? Do I turn back into a pumpkin?"

"At eleven the session ends and we both return to our respective days."

"My day," he said, "is this kitchen. My other day is also this kitchen. You're proposing to schedule the tide out of the sea."

"The tide comes back," I said. "That's the point of tides."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, more the shadow a smile leaves when it passes through. He sat down opposite me, wiped his hands on a towel that had clearly been through several wars, and folded them.

"All right. Let's do it properly, since you've gone to the trouble. Tell me your terms, all of them, and I'll tell you mine, and then we'll see whose survive the night."

* * *

So I read him all three pages.

I read him the fee schedule—he waved that away immediately ("You'll take no fee. You'll take the tapes, eventually, if you earn them, and you'll take lunch")—and I read him the confidentiality clause, and the clause about recordings, and the clause, which I had labored over, stating that either party could terminate the arrangement with seven days' written notice, no explanation required.

"No explanation required," he said softly, when I reached it.

"That's standard."

"For whom?"

"For everyone. It protects both parties. Explanations become negotiations, negotiations become—" I stopped, hearing where the sentence was going. "It's cleaner."

"Cleaner." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn, considering it. "In 1974 I recorded a woman who used words like yours. Cleaner. Standard. Terms. She was translating for the council by then, doing their letters, their notices. Beautiful English. She told me once that she kept her Vessic in a box in her chest, locked, and the key thrown into the harbor, and that this was not sadness but hygiene." He looked up at me. "She was your grandmother."

The kitchen went very still. Outside, a gull made its opinion known and thought better of it.

"I know," I said finally. "You played me her voice. On Saturday."

"I did. And you went white as the crockery and asked me to stop the tape, and then you came back on Tuesday with three pages of terms and a clause about explanations not being required." He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Ulyana. I'm ninety-one years old. I don't have time to pretend I haven't noticed things. But I do have time to wait for you to notice them yourself. So." He unfolded his hands. "Your terms. Which of them are about protecting the work, and which are about protecting you from it?"

"That's not a fair question."

"No," he agreed cheerfully. "The good ones never are. More tea?"

* * *

We negotiated for two hours anyway, because I am who I am, and because part of me suspected—if I'm honest now, though I wasn't honest then—that the negotiation itself was the lesson. Every term I proposed, he met not with refusal but with a counter-offer delivered in Vessic first, then English, so that I had to hear the shape of the thing twice before I understood it once.

Hours: he conceded fixed start times but not end times. "A story ends when it ends. You don't cut a fish to fit the pan."

Fees: he won absolutely. No money would pass between us. When I argued that unpaid arrangements created obligation, and obligation created resentment, he laughed—a real laugh, rusty, like a gate opening after winter—and said, "Child, obligation is the only reason languages survive. Nobody learns their mother's tongue out of enthusiasm. They learn it because they owe it."

Boundaries: here he grew quiet. I had written a clause stating that lessons would concern language only—the grammar, the vocabulary, the archive—and would not extend to personal history, family matters, or, I had written with great care, *the circumstances of prior acquaintance between the instructor and the student's family.*

He read that sentence three times. Then he took off his glasses, polished them on the war-torn towel, and put them back on.

"You want to learn a language," he said, "without learning anything."

"A language is a system. Systems can be studied neutrally."

"Can they." He wasn't mocking me. That was the terrible thing. He sounded genuinely curious, the way a doctor sounds asking where it hurts. "Then study this one neutrally. Here's your first word." He held up the honey spoon. "*Vessa.* Honey. Easy. Now hold out your hand."

"Why?"

"Because in Vessic you don't say *vessa*. You say *vessa maro*—honey-given. There is no word for honey that isn't honey being handed to someone. The word contains the hand." He waited. "Hold out your hand, Ulyana."

I want to record, for accuracy, that I hesitated. I want to record that some part of me stood at the border checkpoint of myself and demanded papers, purpose, duration of stay. And I want to record that I held out my hand anyway, and he laid the spoon across my palm, and said "*Vessa maro*," and the word arrived in me whole, with the weight of the spoon and the stickiness of the honey and the warmth of another person's kitchen in it, and I understood that I had already lost the argument about neutrality, possibly before I entered the house.

"Terms," I said, putting honey in my tea to have something to do with my face. "Revised terms. Six weeks. Starting Thursday. Grammar in the mornings, as I proposed. Your stories in the afternoons, since you'll tell them whether I schedule them or not. And at the end of six weeks, I write up what I've learned—for the archive, properly catalogued—and we assess whether to continue."

"Assess," he said, delighted. "Like a bridge inspection."

"Exactly like a bridge inspection."

"And if the bridge holds?"

"Then we discuss further phases."

"Further phases." He shook his head slowly, savoring it. "Thirty years I've watched this language die, and now it has a project manager." But he said it gently, and he reached across the table and tapped page three of my contract, the termination clause, the one about explanations not required. "Six weeks, then. But strike this one."

"It's standard practice—"

"Strike it, and I'll agree to everything else. Six weeks, no fee, mornings and afternoons, assessments, phases, the lot. But nobody walks out of these lessons without saying why. Not you, not me. If you leave, you'll tell me why you're leaving, and I'll have to stand here and bear it. That's the old way too." He refilled my cup. "Words cost something here. That's the whole curriculum, really. Everything else is vocabulary."

I looked at the clause. I had drafted it at one in the morning precisely because I'd known, even then, that I might need a door with no questions asked. And here was an old man asking me to weld it shut on the first day.

"Seven days' notice instead?" I offered.

"Two days," he countered. "Long enough to mean it, short enough to be honest."

"Three."

"Done." He spat in his palm and held it out, and when I stared at him, he grinned like a boy of eighty. "Kidding. We shake hands. Although the spit version does bind you in the old law. Best not to find out how."

* * *

I walked back along the coast road in the late afternoon light, the signed contract—or the struck-through contract, which is perhaps its own genre—in my bag, telling myself the story I had prepared: that this was research. Fieldwork. A rare opportunity to document a moribund language from its final native speaker, six weeks, bounded, professional, useful. I was a translator. Translation was what I did with dangerous things: I took them apart in one language and reassembled them safely in another, and nothing ever bled through.

The tide was out. Below the seawall, the sand lay exposed in long wet sheets, gleaming, and beyond it the water stood far off, patient, keeping its own counsel. There was a word for this, I realized—Halvard had mentioned it on Saturday, almost in passing, the word for the space between tide and shore, the ground that belongs to neither and both—and I found I had already forgotten it, and that forgetting it felt less like a gap than like a door I'd heard close somewhere in a large house.

Six weeks, I told myself. Forty-two days. Bounded. Professional.

At the top of the lane I stopped, because ahead of me, tacked to the village shop's noticeboard between a lost cat and a ceilidh announcement, was a printed sheet: *FOR SALE BY AUCTION — HARBOUR COTTAGE & CONTENTS — VIEWING SATURDAYS.* And beneath the auctioneer's blurb about "extensive private archive, sold as seen," a smaller line, in different ink, added by hand:

*Enquiries to the family solicitor. All tapes must go.*

I stood there longer than a person merely reading a noticeboard stands anywhere. Sold as seen. All tapes must go. Somewhere behind that small line of ink was a filing cabinet, and somewhere in that cabinet, I now understood with a cold sliding feeling, were more voices than Halvard's—voices labeled in a maiden name I knew, voices from years I'd been told contained nothing.

I bought milk I didn't need, because the shopkeeper was watching, and walked back to my rented room with the noticeboard's small print repeating itself all the way home.

Saturday was viewing day. I had four days to decide whether I was a researcher with a contract—or something else entirely.
