## 5. Borrowed Words

Halvard Ness had taught the language to four people in his life, and he had learned something different from each of them. From his mother he learned that Vessic was not a skill but a temperature—you were either warm in it or you were not. From his wife, Astrid, who never learned more than a hundred words but used every one of them correctly at dinner parties to watch Englishmen flinch, he learned that a language could be a weapon, which was not its best use but was sometimes its most honest one. From the student who left—her name was Marged, and he did not let himself think of her often, only daily—he learned that you could give someone everything and they could still carry it out of your house like luggage they intended to abandon at the station.

And from Ulyana Fairweather, in the first week of their lessons, he learned that a language could be buried alive.

He noticed it on the second morning, before the tea had finished steeping. He had asked her—casually, the way you drop bait through ice—what she would call him, if she called him anything other than *Mr. Ness*. And she said, without thinking, without any permission from the part of herself that guarded doors: *"Vessu."*

The word for an elder who teaches you. Not teacher exactly. Closer to *the one whose hands you borrow*. It came out of her whole, unbroken, pronounced with the soft double-s that took English speakers three years to manage, and then she flinched.

It was a small flinch. Her shoulders drew up half an inch; her eyes went to the window as if someone outside had called her name. But Halvard had spent ninety-one years watching people's bodies betray what their mouths denied, and he knew what he had seen. The word had arrived home and she had been frightened of it.

"You know that word," he said.

"I don't," she said, in her translator's voice, the one that filed things under work. "I must have picked it up. Children absorb—"

"Ulyana." He let the name sit between them like a stone placed on a table. "You said it right. The *ss* is the hardest sound we have. Your tongue knew where to go."

She wrapped both hands around her teacup as though the cup might be subpoenaed later and she wanted no fingerprints on it. "Muscle memory," she said. "From before I can remember."

"Before you can remember is where all of us keep our best things."

He did not push further. That was the first rule of teaching a buried language, though he had never written it down because he had never had a student long enough to need rules: you do not dig. You wait for the tide to bring things up on its own, and when it does, you do not grab. You stand beside the person and say, *look, there, do you see it*, and you hope to God they look before the water takes it back.

* * *

He began, without quite admitting it to himself, to keep a catalogue. Not of the language—the language he kept in his head, 412 hours of tape notwithstanding, in the old-fashioned way, the way his grandmother had kept hers, in the wet archive behind the eyes. No, what he catalogued was Ulyana's losses. It felt diagnostic rather than prying, the way a doctor notes where the ache sits. Every evening after she left—he insisted she leave by seven, go back to her rented room above the chandlery, sleep like a person instead of a scholar—he sat at his kitchen table with a school exercise book from 1963 and wrote down what the day had shown him was missing.

Day three: kinship terms. He had asked her how she would introduce him to her partner, this Marcus, if Marcus were there. She said, *I'd say, this is Halvard.* And he asked, yes, but what are you to each other? Student and teacher? And she frowned and said, *I suppose so,* and he watched her reach inside herself for the Vessic word for that bond—*sennavek*, the one-who-carries-your-words-forward—and watched her hand come back empty. Not empty, he corrected himself in the exercise book that night. Full of something she wouldn't lift out. Because thirty seconds later, making tea, she hummed two bars of a tune and stopped mid-note as if she'd walked into a door she couldn't see, and he knew the tune. Everyone from the village knew the tune. It was the counting song mothers sang while braiding hair, the one that went up to ten and then started lying about the numbers just to make children laugh. His mother had sung it. Half the tapes in his front room held some version of it. Ulyana had it in her, note-perfect, and no idea what it was or where it came from, and when he told her—a little cruelly, perhaps, though he preferred to think of it as honestly—that it was a lullaby, she set down her cup very carefully and said, "Is it," in a voice like a lid closing.

Day five: the sea words. This was the one that hurt him, and he was a man who had arranged his whole life to be hurt as little as possible, which is to say he had stayed in one house for seventy years and let the world do its leaving elsewhere. There is a word in Vessic—*vaelmira*—for the space between the tide and the shore. Not the beach. Not the shallows. The moving strip itself, the place that belongs to neither element, that is sea twice a day and land twice a day and never either. The old people said a person could live their whole life in the vaelmira and never once touch ground or water, and whether they meant it as warning or invitation depended entirely on the speaker and, usually, on how much they'd had to drink.

He asked her to translate it. This was fair game; translation was her trade, and he wanted to see her work. She considered it with real professional attention, head tilted, and offered: "Intertidal zone?"

He laughed until his ribs complained. Then he saw her face and stopped laughing, mostly.

"It's not a zone," he said. "A zone is where they make you take your shoes off at the airport. Vaelmira is—" and here he found himself, at ninety-one, reaching, which annoyed him. "Vaelmira is where the limpets panic. It's where my father stood every morning of his life deciding whether the day belonged to the boat or the net. It's the width of one indecision. You have a word for it. I've heard you say it."

"I haven't."

"You will. Probably in your sleep. Probably complaining about me."

But she didn't laugh, and he noted that too, in the exercise book, in pencil, because some entries you want to be able to erase.

* * *

By the end of the first week he had filled nine pages, and the shape of the thing was clear enough that it kept him awake, staring at the ceiling his father had plastered, listening to the house tick. What Ulyana had lost was not vocabulary. Vocabulary you could teach from a book, and God knew he'd made the books. What she had lost was the *permission*. Somewhere between her grandmother's kitchen and whatever polished rooms she worked in now, someone had told her that these words were not hers to say, and she had believed it so thoroughly that when they surfaced anyway—whole, correct, warm—they frightened her, like finding a stranger's coat in your wardrobe that fits you perfectly.

He thought about her grandmother more than was comfortable. Maren. He had known her when she was Maren Ostrand and quick as a gull, before the leaving, before whatever happened in that family that turned a house full of speakers into a house full of silence. He was not a stupid man, whatever the village doctor said about his heart, and he had known since the letter why he'd written it, and since the first morning why he'd chosen her. But knowing a thing and being ready to say a thing were, in Vessic as in everything, two different words entirely.

On the eighth day, the tide brought something up.

They were working on verbs—not grammar, he'd promised her no grammar before noon, but she had broken her own rule, arriving at six with her notebook already open, hungry in a way that pleased him so much he had to hide it behind grumbling—and she was struggling with the past tense, which in Vessic is a wicked business, three forms depending on whether the thing remembered still touches you. And she said, trying to construct a sentence about her childhood, "I—when I was small, I—" and stopped, and her face did the thing faces do when a word arrives uninvited.

"*Skjelda*," she said quietly. Testing it.

"That's right," he said, keeping his voice level the way you keep your voice level near a bird you want to stay on the fence. "That's the past that still touches you. What were you going to say?"

She looked at her notebook. She looked at the window, where the harbour was doing its grey morning business. And then she said, in Vessic, slowly, each word set down like a foot on ice: "*Ek skjelda vaelmira bo.*"

*I lived in the space between tide and shore.*

Her accent was imperfect. Her grammar was a child's grammar. Halvard Ness sat very still in his own kitchen and felt something move through his chest that had nothing to do with the heart the doctor worried about, or possibly everything.

"That sentence," he said, when he trusted his voice, "is sixty years old. You've been carrying it since you were nine."

"Don't be absurd. I constructed it just now, from your lesson—"

"You put the verb first. We don't teach that until month three. You put it first because that's how a child says it, how a child *said* it, in this kitchen, probably, in this village, before—" He stopped himself. Before whatever happened. Before the severance. He was not ready to say it and she was nowhere near ready to hear it, and there were, thank God, another five weeks.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, in English, in her flat professional voice, "What does *bo* do in that position? Grammatically?"

And he understood that she would rather dissect the sentence than feel it, and he loved her a little for that too, because it was exactly what he had done for seventy years with the tapes and the notebooks and the careful cataloguing of a dying thing, filing grief under scholarship, and he knew precisely how much protection it bought and precisely how little.

"It holds the door open," he said. "In that tense, *bo* means the living isn't finished. Even if you've gone. Even if the place has changed hands. The sentence stays true."

She wrote it down. Of course she wrote it down. But he saw that her hand shook slightly, and he saw, when she thought he wasn't looking, that she touched the page afterward, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a scar to check it's still yours.

That evening, after she'd gone, he opened the exercise book and wrote beneath the day's entry a single line, and then sat looking at it for a long while:

*Not lost. Locked. Someone had the key and used it.*

He knew who. He suspected she was beginning to suspect too. And downstairs in the front room, on the shelf, in the dark, Tape 41 waited with the patience of all recorded things, holding a young woman's laughter and an argument about grief and, underneath it, a voice older still, saying plainly what the family had spent sixty years pretending was weather.

Tomorrow, he thought. Or the day after. The tide brings things up on its own schedule, and you cannot hurry it, and you must not grab.

But he was ninety-one, and the buyer's solicitor had begun writing letters, and he found, setting the exercise book aside, that his patience—which had lasted seventy years—was beginning to run out at exactly the speed of his heart.
