## 9. Aunt Vera's Telephone Voice

The telephone rang at half past seven in the evening, which was how Ulyana knew it was Vera before she even looked at the screen. Nobody else in her life called at half past seven. Marcus texted. Colleagues emailed with subject lines like *Quick question* that contained nothing quick and no questions. Her mother, when she had still been alive to call, had favored the long silence of the letter. But Vera telephoned the way other people went to church: regularly, dutifully, and with a certain grim satisfaction in having done it properly.

Ulyana set down her pen — she'd been working through Halvard's tape log, cataloguing by candlelight because the cottage electricity had a temperamental relationship with lamps — and picked up.

"Aunt Vera."

"Ulyana." A pause, the length of a held breath. "You sound odd."

"I'm in a drafty house on the coast."

"You're always in a drafty house somewhere." This was not quite true, but it was true enough that Ulyana let it stand. Vera continued before she could confirm or deny anything. "I'm ringing because of the Ness house. The sale."

Ulyana sat up straighter. Outside Halvard's kitchen window, the tide was coming in over the flats, making its low patient sound, like someone turning pages very slowly.

"What about it?"

"It's moving faster than anyone expected. Mrs. Trelawney down the post office says the buyer's solicitor has already been out twice this month. Twice! For a house that's been emptying itself for years, suddenly everyone's in a hurry." Vera's voice had the particular briskness she deployed when delivering news she had rehearsed. "And there's something else. I've seen the estate papers."

"Have you," said Ulyana carefully.

"Naturally I've seen them. I'm on the parish council; things come through me whether I want them or not." A small cough. "Your name isn't in them, if that's what you're wondering. But his is."

"Whose?"

"Don't be clever, Ulyana. You know whose. Halvard Ness. He's named in the papers as — well, as a party of interest, whatever that means. Something about the contents. The recordings, I expect." Another pause, longer this time, and when Vera spoke again her voice had dropped into a register Ulyana recognized from childhood: the flat, careful tone her aunt used whenever the family's history came too near the surface, like a woman walking on ice she knew had cracked once before. "I didn't know you were still in contact with him."

And there it was. The lid pressing down from inside.

"We've only just—" Ulyana began, then stopped herself. She thought about lying. She was, professionally speaking, an excellent liar; translation was lying with a clear conscience, saying in one language what another language wanted said. But something in her — some new, unsteady thing that had grown in six weeks of grammar at dawn and stories at dusk — did not want to lie to Vera, of all people. Not tonight.

"I'm studying with him," she said. "Vessic. He's teaching me."

The silence on the line was so complete that Ulyana checked the screen to see if the call had dropped. It hadn't. She could hear Vera breathing, slow and deliberate, the way she breathed when she was counting to some number larger than ten.

"Studying," Vera said at last. The word came out flattened, pressed between boards. "Vessic."

"Yes."

"With Halvard Ness."

"Yes."

Another silence. Then, in the flattest voice Ulyana had ever heard her use — flatter than at her mother's funeral, flatter than the day the family lawyer read the will — Vera said: "I see. Well. That's your business, I suppose."

It was the *I suppose* that did it. Ulyana had spent thirty years reading voices the way other people read faces, and she heard everything folded into those two small words: not indifference but terror, not disapproval but something older and more frightened, a door being shut quickly before anything could get out. Or in.

"Aunt Vera—"

"How is he?" Vera asked abruptly. "His health. He must be what, ninety now?"

"Ninety-one. He's—" Ulyana thought of Halvard climbing his stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, refusing help with a wave that managed to be both gracious and imperious. "He's stubborn. He walks to the shore every morning. He eats more butter than any cardiologist would permit."

A sound came down the line that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "That sounds like him," Vera said, and then caught herself, as though the words had escaped without permission. "I mean — one hears things. Village talk."

"One hears things," Ulyana repeated gently.

"Don't take that tone with me, Ulyana Fairweather. I've known you since you were in nappies and you were a sly little thing even then." But there was no heat in it, and underneath the briskness Ulyana could hear something else, something that sounded almost like longing, which made no sense at all, and every sense.

"Why does it bother you?" Ulyana asked. "That I'm learning."

"It doesn't bother me."

"It bothers you enough to go flat. You've gone flat, Aunt Vera. You do it when Grandmother used to mention the old days, you do it when anyone says the word Vessic within earshot of you. You're doing it right now. I can hear it. It's like listening to a door close."

"That is quite enough—"

"He plays tapes," Ulyana said, and watched, in imagination, the effect land. "Recordings. From the seventies. There are hundreds of hours of them. Interviews, songs, stories. People from the village. People who are dead now, speaking in their own voices, in their own words." She paused. "There are tapes labeled with our name, Aunt Vera."

This time the silence had a different quality entirely. Not counted breaths but a kind of free fall. When Vera finally spoke, her voice had lost all its scaffolding.

"You haven't listened to them."

"Not yet."

"Don't." The word came out sharp, immediate, unguarded — and then, hearing itself, Vera pulled back behind the flatness again, but too late. "Some things are better left where they were put. That's all I'll say. Some things were put away for reasons, and the reasons haven't expired just because everyone who made them is dead."

"Whose reasons?"

"Goodnight, Ulyana."

"Aunt Vera—"

"The sale completes soon. Faster than expected, like I said. Whatever arrangement you have with that man, it will end when the house changes hands, and perhaps that's for the best. Come home for a visit sometime. Properly home, not — wherever you are. Bring nothing. Ask nothing." A breath. "I make a passable gooseberry fool these days. Your grandmother's recipe."

*Your grandmother's recipe.* Said in English, every word of it, and yet Ulyana heard beneath it, unmistakably, a word in another tongue — the word Halvard had taught her in week two, the verb that meant both *to remain* and *to be kept*. Vera had never spoken Vessic to her in thirty years. But she had just handed her granddaughter's recipe across the telephone line like a message passed under a door.

"I'll come," Ulyana said quietly. "Soon."

"Hm," said Vera, which in the dialect of their family meant *yes*, and rang off.

* * *

Ulyana sat in Halvard's kitchen for a long while after, the phone dark in her hand, the tide filling the window frame inch by inch.

She should have felt victorious. She had, after all, gotten further in one phone call than in thirty years of Christmas cards: she had located the wound, confirmed it was tended rather than healed, and extracted a promise of gooseberry fool. Instead she felt the way she imagined archaeologists felt standing at the edge of a newly opened chamber — thrilled and appalled in equal measure, aware that the air rushing out had been sealed in for a reason.

*Don't listen to them.*

Too late, Aunt Vera. She had already listened to one minute of Tape 41's neighbors, and she would listen to Tape 41 itself soon, tonight perhaps, because a voice going flat down a telephone line was its own kind of evidence, and Ulyana had built a career on following a word to its root no matter what it cost.

From the hallway came the shuffle of Halvard's slippers, and then Halvard himself, in his cardigan the color of wet slate, pausing in the doorway with two mugs and an expression of studied innocence that fooled no one.

"You were eavesdropping," Ulyana said.

"I was making tea in my own kitchen," he said, setting the mugs down. "The kettle announces things. One can't help overhearing announcements." He lowered himself into his chair, wincing slightly, and looked at her over the rim of his mug with eyes that had seen ninety-one years and missed very little of them. "Your aunt," he said. "Vera. How is she?"

"You know her."

"I knew her mother better." He said it mildly, terribly, the way he said most true things, and then, before Ulyana could decide whether to press him, he nodded toward the shelf of tapes, the hundreds of spines in their careful handwriting. "Tomorrow we do the subjunctive," he said. "Tonight, I think, you should eat something. Grief and grammar both go better on a full stomach."

"I'm not grieving."

"No," he agreed placidly. "Not yet."

Outside, the tide reached whatever it had been reaching for all evening, and began, patiently, to withdraw. And upstairs in the archive room, Tape 41 waited on its shelf with her grandmother's maiden name inked along its spine, patient as the sea, keeping what it kept.
