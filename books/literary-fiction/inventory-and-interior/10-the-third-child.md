## 10. The Third Child

The pie plate sat between them on Ruthie's kitchen table like a small moon, scraped clean, a crescent of cherry filling dried at its rim. Coral had stopped hearing the words somewhere around *boat owner* and had not started again until Ruthie said, "You were there too, honey. You know that, don't you?"

Three kids at the quarry.

Coral had driven back to the motel with the windows down even though the air off the water was cold enough to make her eyes run, because she could not bear the sealed quiet of the car, the way it held her breath for her. She parked under the flickering sign at the Sandpiper Motel—VACANCY with the V dead so it read only ACANCY, which felt accurate—and sat in the lot while the engine ticked itself cool and the gulls argued over something in the dumpster and the whole ordinary machinery of the town went on around her, indifferent, as it had gone on in August of 1989 while her brother lay facedown in green water.

She tried to remember the quarry.

This was the thing she kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: she tried, genuinely tried, and found nothing. Not a blurred nothing, not a faded photograph of a nothing, but a clean absence, a page removed from a book so skillfully that the binding didn't even creak. She remembered that summer in pieces—the smell of cut grass on Salt Row, the popsicle stains on the porch boards, Danny teaching her gin rummy with a deck missing the queen of hearts, which he claimed made the game more interesting and which she now understood had simply been a lost card. She remembered being sixteen. She remembered being alive in a way she had never been again, that specific summer aliveness, salt hair and sunburnt shoulders and the enormous unearned confidence of it.

And then August ended, and Danny was dead, and her memory resumed on the other side of the funeral like a film spliced by a careless editor. There was a gap. She had always assumed the gap was grief—that grief had simply eaten those weeks, the way it eats things, indiscriminately, the way mold eats a wall from the inside out where you can't see it.

But grief did not eat selectively. Grief would have taken the gin rummy too.

She sat in the parking lot until the light went from pewter to violet, and then she went inside and sat on the bed with the bedspread the color of old mustard, and she took out the coroner's report and read it again, though she no longer needed to. *Contusions consistent with blunt force trauma to the left temporal region.* She took out her phone and looked at the photographs she'd taken of Marianne's diary pages, the ones from August 1989, the handwriting going from careful to frantic to a kind of terrible calm, and she read the entry from the fourteenth again:

*He says he doesn't remember. I have told him never to say otherwise. C. saw everything and remembers nothing, thank God, thank God.*

*C. saw everything and remembers nothing.*

Her own initial. Her mother's handwriting. Thank God, thank God, as if Coral's emptied mind were a mercy bestowed rather than a theft committed—as if someone had reached into her sixteen-year-old skull and taken the quarry out of it, and this was cause for gratitude.

She set the phone face-down on the bedspread. The room smelled of mildew and industrial lemon. Somewhere down the hall a television laughed at something, canned laughter, a hundred people laughing on cue at nothing.

*You were there too, honey.*

She had said, in Ruthie's kitchen, with the pie untouched before her: *I don't remember being there.* And Ruthie had looked at her with an expression Coral couldn't parse then and could parse now, replaying it—pity, yes, but also a kind of confirmation, the look of a woman watching a lock turn over exactly as expected. Ruthie had said, gently, "No. I expect you don't."

* * *

She did not sleep. At some point past two she gave up on the pretense and drove, because driving was what her body knew how to do when her mind refused to hold still, and her body chose the road out past the shuttered cannery and up through the pines toward the quarry without consulting her, the way a dog chooses the route to a house where it once lived.

The gate was chained, as it had been chained for thirty years, ever since the county declared it a hazard and then, having declared it, did nothing else. She left the car on the gravel shoulder and walked the last quarter mile the way they used to walk it, Danny ahead, always ahead, his swimmer's shoulders rolling, Theo trailing behind with his book bag he insisted on bringing everywhere, and herself last, carrying the towels, because she was the youngest and the youngest carried the towels. That she remembered. That came back to her with the smell of pine pitch and hot dust attached, vivid as a postcard.

It was the quarry itself that was empty.

She stood at the edge where the rock shelf dropped away into water gone dark with evening, and she looked at the place where her brother had died, and she felt—nothing. Not numbness exactly. Numbness implies something beneath it, frozen and waiting. This was more like standing in a room where furniture had been and finding only rectangles of unfaded wallpaper. Something had been here. Something had been here and involved her, and the wallpaper remembered the shape of it better than she did.

The water below was black and patient. A bird—some night thing—called once from the far bank and got no answer.

*If I was there,* she said aloud, to the water, to the boy under it, *what did I see?*

The question fell straight down the rock face and did not echo. Quarries don't echo; the stone eats sound. She thought: even the acoustics here are built for keeping things.

She stayed until her hands were cold and the first mosquitoes found her, and then she walked back to the car, and in the rearview mirror her own face looked like a stranger's face wearing her expression, and she understood that this was not a figure of speech anymore. It was the actual condition she was in. She had spent fifteen years making herself invisible, perfecting invisibility like a craft, reshelving books at 3 a.m., declining invitations, telling no one anything—and she had believed it was temperament, shyness, preference. Now she wondered if it was architecture. If the whole shape of her life had been built around a hole the way a tree grows around a fence wire, and everyone who loved her had known about the wire except her.

*The witness her family arranged itself around.* She didn't have those words yet. They would come later, from Dr. Okafor's mouth, in a carpeted office that smelled of chamomile. What she had that night was simpler and worse: the image of her mother writing *thank God, thank God*, and the understanding that the God being thanked had done something to her, or let something be done, and that every person who had ever told her she was fine, she was just quiet, she'd get over it someday, had been reading from the same script.

* * *

Dr. Okafor's office was above the pharmacy on Delancey Street, up a staircase that complained underfoot, in a suite that had probably been a dentist's in another decade. The diploma on the wall said Lagos, then Boston, then a date recent enough that Coral understood she was one of the few patients this man had chosen rather than inherited. He was younger than she expected, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-shot hair and a way of listening that made silence feel like participation rather than failure.

"I'm not staying," Coral said, when she'd finished—she had talked for forty minutes, which surprised her, the whole thing pouring out in the flat inventory voice she used for cataloguing: the house, the auction, the diaries, the report, the bruising, Ruthie, the boat owner, the three kids, the empty quarry, the entry with her own initial in it. "I fly back Sunday. I just need—I need someone to tell me if this is possible."

"Tell you what is possible?"

"That a person can—" She stopped. Started again. "That a person can lose six weeks. Whole weeks. Not misplace them. Lose them like they were never issued."

Dr. Okafor took his glasses down out of his hair and put them on, which seemed to be his version of settling in. "Before I answer," he said, "I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer honestly, even if the answer is ugly. When you imagine recovering those weeks—actually recovering them, seeing what happened at that quarry—what do you feel?"

Coral had prepared for questions about symptoms. About sleep, appetite, flashbacks. This question caught her under the ribs.

"Terror," she said. And then, because the room was quiet and honest-seeming: "Worse than terror. Terror has a future tense. This is—" She searched. "It's like standing outside a door in my own house and knowing something of mine is behind it and being afraid to open it because whatever's in there has been in there for thirty years, alone, and it might not be—it might not be what I left there. It might have changed. It might be angry."

Dr. Okafor nodded slowly, and wrote nothing, which she appreciated. Everyone else in her life wrote things down.

"What you're describing has a name," he said. "Dissociative amnesia. It's rare in its dramatic forms and common in its quiet ones. The mind, under unbearable stress, doesn't always fail to record. Sometimes it records perfectly and then walls the recording off—not deletes it, walls it. The material isn't gone. It's sequestered. And the walling-off can persist for decades if the environment never makes it safe to take the wall down." He folded his hands. "Your mother's diary suggests your family understood, consciously or not, that the safest place for you to keep that summer was inside a locked room. And they built their lives around the lock."

"The witness," Coral said. The words came out of her sounding borrowed, secondhand, like she'd read them in someone else's novel. "That's what I am. I'm the witness. Everything—all of it, the moving away, the silence, the way nobody ever talked about him, the way my mother used to change the subject when Danny came up like she was swatting a fly—"

"Gently," Dr. Okafor said.

"No. No, I want to say it while I can." Her hands had begun to shake, and she watched them shake with a strange clinical interest, as though they belonged to a client. "They arranged themselves around me. Around the hole in me. My mother paid a man to keep quiet about what I saw. My brother—Theo—left the state at eighteen and never came back for funerals, weddings, anything. Fifteen years I've been shelving other people's lives at night because mine had a room in it I wasn't allowed to enter, and I called that a career. I called it a personality." She laughed, one dry note with nothing under it. "I've been living in the anteroom of my own life for thirty years, and the rent was free because the family was paying it."

Dr. Okafor let the silence sit for a moment, then leaned forward. "I want to offer you two cautions, and then one thing that may actually help. First caution: recovered memories are real, and recovered memories are also fragile. What comes back may come back incomplete, contaminated by everything you've learned since, and you will not be able to tell which parts are the quarry and which parts are the diaries. Hold whatever returns loosely. Second caution: the mind walled that room off for a reason, and forcing the door is how people get hurt. It will open when it opens, or it won't, and either way you survive it—you already have survived it; that's literally what the amnesia means."

"And the helpful thing?"

"You asked if it's possible, to lose weeks like they were never issued." He took off his glasses. Without them his face was suddenly older, and kinder. "It's possible. But I'll tell you what I've seen, in twenty years of this work. People like you—people with a sequestered room—they usually aren't waiting for the contents. Most of them, if you press them, admit they're waiting for permission. Someone to stand at the door and say: whatever is in there, you didn't put it there, and you won't have to carry it alone." He smiled slightly. "Your mother thanked God you remembered nothing. I'd suggest the opposite prayer. Remembering nothing was never the same as being innocent of it. You've been carrying that room your whole life. You've just been carrying it blind."

* * *

She walked down Delancey Street in the late-morning light with the town doing its small business around her—the hardware store, the bait shop, the church with its sign announcing a potluck—and for the first time since she'd arrived she saw the ledger Ruthie had talked about, the town's own architecture of silence, everywhere at once. The woman watering petunias outside the realty office had been at the funeral. The old man mending a crab pot on the corner had been on the volunteer fire crew that pulled Danny out. Every face she passed had a page in the book, and hers was the only page written in invisible ink, and everyone had agreed, all these years, not to hold it up to the light.

At the motel she packed slowly, folding each item the way she shelved books, spine aligned, edges flush, order imposed on chaos. Her flight was Sunday. The auction preview was Saturday. In the bottom of her bag, wrapped in a sweater, were scans of eleven years of her mother's handwriting, and somewhere in those pages—she was certain of it now, certain the way you're certain of weather coming—was the name of the person who had been in the water with Danny. Three kids at the quarry. One dead, one silenced, one erased. Which meant the third child was either her or Theo, and she had spent her whole life being the one who remembered nothing, which left—

She stopped folding.

Stood very still in the mustard-colored room with her half-packed bag open on the bed, and let the arithmetic finish itself, cold and simple as a catalog number.

There were three kids at the quarry. She was one. Danny was two. And Theo—who had wanted the house sold fast and clean, no ceremony; who had gone still, very still, when she mentioned the drawer; who had left the state at eighteen and built a life with no room in it for Salt Row—Theo was three.

It didn't prove anything. She heard Dr. Okafor's caution, held it loosely. Bruises were not confessions. Absence was not guilt. But the shape of it rose in her mind anyway, complete, the way the outline of the furniture rises from the unfaded wallpaper: her brother at fourteen, her brother who taught her cards too, her brother whose briskness she had mistaken for grief management, arranging himself around the same locked room she'd been arranged around—except he hadn't been arranged around it. He had built it.

Her phone buzzed on the nightstand. An email. Subject line: **Auction Preview — Saturday 10 AM — Lot List Attached.**

Below it, a text from Theo, sent an hour ago, the punctuation crisp as always:

*Flying in Friday. Let's coordinate before the preview. Don't want any surprises at the table.*

She looked at the word *surprises* for a long time.

Then she opened the diary scans, found the entry dated August 12th, 1989—the day after Danny drowned, the ink pressed hard enough to tear the paper—and began, line by line, to read what her mother had written about the third child.
