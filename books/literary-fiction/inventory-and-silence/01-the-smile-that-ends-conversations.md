## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

The Halbrook Branch Library closed to the public at nine, but for the eleven people who came after — the ones with the laminated night-shift cards issued by the county's sleeplessness outreach program — it stayed open until six in the morning, and Wren Reyes was its entire staff.

They had worked the night shift for ten years, and in that time they had perfected a smile. It was not a warm smile. Warmth invited follow-up questions, and follow-up questions were how a shift went from quiet to complicated. Wren's smile was architectural: it acknowledged a person, held them at a comfortable distance, and gently suggested an exit, all in under two seconds. Coworkers from the day shift called it *the customer service face*. Wren thought of it more accurately as a door that looked like a welcome mat.

At 11:40 p.m., Marcus Osei arrived, as he always did, forty minutes early, carrying his binder of lottery tickets going back six years.

"Evening, Wren."

"Evening, Marcus. Your table's open."

It was always his table — the one by the microfilm room, under the vent that hummed at exactly the pitch of a refrigerator, which Marcus claimed helped him think. He sat, opened the binder, and began arranging losing tickets by date and game type, a taxonomy only he understood. Twice a year he asked Wren to print something off the public computer, usually a horoscope archive, and twice a year Wren printed it without commentary, because commentary was a door opening.

"You look tired," Marcus said tonight.

"I'm fine." The smile. "Let me know if you need anything."

He nodded, satisfied, and returned to 2019. This was the whole art of it: give people just enough acknowledgment that they didn't need to escalate. Loneliness, Wren had learned, was mostly a demand for witness, and witness could be rationed like anything else.

* * *

The supervisor, Dolores Kaminski, did not work nights. She worked days, and she left notes. Tonight's note was waiting on the desk when Wren clocked in at ten, folded once, on official letterhead, which meant she'd typed it during business hours and thought about it for at least an afternoon.

*Wren —*

*A patron from Tuesday called to say you were "efficient but cold." I know nights are different. But we're a community branch, and community means warmth. Try asking people how they're doing. Try remembering their names.*

*I know you remember their names. That's not the same as using them.*

*D.*

Wren read it twice, folded it along the existing crease, and filed it in the drawer where the other notes lived — fourteen months of them now, a paper stratum of Dolores's ongoing campaign to make Wren into someone who chatted. Wren did use names. That was the trick of it. Using a person's name made them feel known; feeling known made them stop trying to be known; stopping was what they actually wanted at three in the morning, even if they didn't know it. Everyone who came to the night library was running from something — a bed where a spouse had died, an apartment where a baby used to cry, a head that would not stop rehearsing arguments with people long gone. What they needed was a room where nothing was required of them. Wren provided the room. Dolores wanted a hostess.

At midnight the overheads dimmed to half, per the energy schedule, and the library became its true self: a cavern of sleeping stacks, green exit lights, the tick of the radiators. Wren walked the floor, reshelving the day shift's abandoned carts, and performed the small maintenance of the night — straightening chairs, wiping down the study tables, checking the bathroom for the things people left behind out of exhaustion or intention. Tonight there was a single glove, leather, good quality, left palm-up on the sink like an offering. Wren put it in lost and found, where it would sit for ninety days and then go to the donation bin, unclaimed, like most things.

* * *

At 1:15, Priya Nair came in with her laptop and her thermos and took her seat at the far carrel, the one facing the wall, and did not look up. Priya was writing something — a dissertation, a novel, a resignation letter; Wren had never asked — and she came five nights a week and spoke perhaps twenty words total. She was, in this way, Wren's favorite patron, though favorite was a word Wren would have denied under oath. There was no maintenance required. Priya had already learned the grammar of the place: presence without performance.

The hard ones were the new ones. At 2:03, the front scanner chimed and a man in his thirties came in wearing a hospital bracelet he hadn't bothered to remove, still smelling faintly of antiseptic, and stood in the lobby looking around with the expression of someone who has been discharged into a world with no instructions attached.

"Can I help you?" Wren said, from the desk, before he could drift.

"They said — the nurse said there's a place. For people who can't—"

"Night cards. Do you have one?"

"No. She gave me a number to call but—" He showed Wren his phone, which displayed a screenshot of a county webpage with a phone number circled in red. The office closed at five.

Wren could have told him to come back tomorrow. Tomorrow was the correct procedural answer. Instead Wren pulled up the form, printed it, slid it across the desk with a pen. "Fill this out. I'll issue a temporary card tonight and the permanent one will mail to you. There's coffee in the back, decaf only after two — house rule, not mine. Bathrooms are to your left. Quiet hours start at four, but honestly nobody enforces them against sleeping."

The man stared at the form. Then at Wren. His eyes were doing the thing eyes do when they've been crying in a place where crying isn't allowed.

"Thank you," he said. "I don't — my wife's in there. ICU. They keep saying *stable*, but stable isn't—"

"I hope she goes home soon," Wren said, and deployed the smile, and turned to the returns cart, and the man filled out his form and found his chair and the moment ended, cleanly, the way Wren ended all moments: gently, completely, with no loose threads for anyone to pull.

Except that walking the cart back through the 300s, Wren heard the sentence again. *Stable isn't.* Stable isn't what? Safe? Over? Enough? The man had been holding a sentence he couldn't finish, and Wren had handed him a pen instead of an ear, and this was the job, this was the talent, this was the thing Dolores kept mistaking for a deficiency: Wren could end any conversation without breaking it. A surgeon of small talk. Ten years of practice. If there were a certification, Wren would have framed it.

So why did the unfinished sentence follow them around the building for the next hour like a cat that had decided whose door it lived at?

* * *

At 3:30, Ruth Okonkwo — no relation to anyone, retired schoolteacher, insomniac by vocation rather than affliction — arrived for her usual hour of large-print mysteries and unsolicited opinions about them.

"Wren. You've got a face on you."

"I always have a face."

"Not this one. This one's got weather in it." Ruth settled into the armchair by the periodicals, which she considered hers by right of seniority. "Man came in earlier. Hospital bracelet. I saw him leave."

"He left at three."

"Did you talk to him?"

"I issued him a card."

Ruth looked at Wren over her glasses with the particular patience of a woman who had spent thirty-five years catching students in the act of answering a different question than the one asked. "That's not what I asked."

"It's what happened."

"Mm." She opened her mystery, read a page, closed it. "You know what my mother used to say? She said some people keep their houses so tidy because they're afraid of what's in the drawers." She tapped the armrest. "You run a very tidy conversation, Wren Reyes."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't a compliment."

"It was accurate, though."

Ruth laughed — a real laugh, brief, surprised out of her — and shook her head and went back to her book, and Wren went back to the desk, and both of them pretended the exchange had been nothing, which is a kind of intimacy too, maybe the only kind Wren had ever been fluent in.

* * *

Four o'clock. Quiet hours. The building exhaled. Marcus slept in his chair with his head tipped back, mouth open, the binder zipped shut beside him like a finished argument. Priya typed with the focused silence of someone defusing something. The hospital-bracelet man had fallen asleep at his table too, cheek on his forearm, and Wren stood over him for a moment with the blanket from the lost-and-found bin — the one kept for exactly this — and laid it over his shoulders without waking him. He didn't stir. In the morning he would return the blanket folded, or he wouldn't, and either way Wren would never learn his name, and this was fine. This was the design.

Wren sat at the desk and did the inventory. It was a nightly ritual, half habit, half superstition: count the patrons, check the exits, log the incidents (tonight: zero), note supplies (thermal receipt paper low; request Monday). Eleven years ago, when Wren first took this shift, the inventory had taken twenty minutes. Now it took four, because Wren had memorized the building the way other people memorize prayers.

Then there was nothing to do, which was the dangerous part. Between four and six the library belonged to the radiators and the exit signs, and Wren was alone with the fact that the shift was the life. That the deflections had compounded into a decade. That Dolores's notes were right in the way notes from cheerful people are right — technically, uselessly right. *Try asking people how they're doing.* Wren knew how everyone was doing. That had never once been the problem.

The problem was that Wren could not remember the last time anyone had asked *them*.

Mother, obviously. But Delia Reyes communicated in scheduled Sunday calls of eleven minutes' duration, calls conducted with the emotional range of a voicemail greeting, and even those had grown shorter since March, since whatever it was that had tightened around Delia's voice lately, some new knot Wren couldn't name and hadn't been permitted to ask about. Asking about Delia's interior life was like asking the locked box of photographs in the hall closet to explain itself. You didn't. You grew up understanding that certain drawers stayed shut, and you built your own house with the same architecture, tidy, nothing in the drawers, and you called it competence, and mostly, at 4 a.m., you almost believed it.

Almost.

Wren logged off the incident report, checked the doors, dimmed the desk lamp. Outside the tall front windows, Halbrook Avenue lay blue and empty under the streetlights, and the first gray suggestion of dawn was still two hours off.

And then the scanner chimed.

Wren glanced at the clock — 5:12, fifteen minutes before close — and looked up expecting Marcus's ride or an early commuter, and saw instead, standing just inside the doors, an elderly woman Wren had never seen before. Small, straight-backed, dressed with a precision that seemed imported from another decade: wool coat, scarf knotted at the throat, a canvas tote bag held in both hands like something being carried across a border. Her hair was white and cut short and practical. Her eyes moved over the library — the sleeping men, the carrels, the desk — with the unhurried attention of someone taking a census.

She did not look confused. That was the first strange thing. New patrons at 5 a.m. were always confused; confusion was practically the dress code. This woman looked like she had come to a place she had already decided about.

Wren rose. "Good morning. We close at six, but you're welcome to—"

"I know," the woman said. Her voice was low and even, an accent Wren couldn't place, vowels shaped slightly differently than English wanted them. "I'm not here for books."

"All right."

"I've been watching this building for three weeks." She said it without apology, the way you'd state the weather. "I sit across the street, at the bus shelter, when the buses aren't running. I watch who comes in and who goes out. You let a man sleep under a blanket and you didn't wake him to tell him about it. You speak to everyone and you tell no one anything." She set the tote bag on the desk between them, carefully, both hands. "I need to learn something, Mr.—"

"Wren."

"*Wren.*" She tried the name once, testing its weight, and something in her face flickered — recognition, almost, or its ghost. "My name is Ilsa Vane. And I am going to die soon, probably within the year, and before I do, I need to teach one person everything I know. I've been watching to see if this building had anyone in it worth teaching." She looked up at Wren, and her eyes were dry and steady and terribly awake for five in the morning. "I believe it does. I'd like to begin tonight, if you'll stay a few minutes past closing."

Wren reached, automatically, for the smile — the door that looked like a welcome mat, the gentle ending, ten years of practice ready to deploy — and found, for the first time in memory, that it wouldn't come.
