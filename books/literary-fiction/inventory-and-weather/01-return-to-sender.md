## 1. Return to Sender

The postcards arrived at the house every Tuesday, which was a lie Silas told themselves, because the house had been gone fifteen years and the postcards went nowhere at all. But the writing of them happened on Tuesdays. That much was true. That much had held.

Silas sat at the kitchen table of a fourth-floor walk-up on Delancey Street with the window open to the sound of a city that had never heard of Harrow Bend, and they uncapped the good pen—the one with the nib worn to a slant that matched their hand—and they wrote, in the small cursive they'd once been praised for in the fourth grade, postcard number four thousand three hundred and eighty-one.

*Dear 114 Meridian Road,*

That was how they always began. Not *Dear Mother*, not *Dear Father*, not *Dear Elias*. The address was the addressee. It was the only honest way to do it, because the house was the thing that had received them, held them, and then, on the night of October 3rd, stopped existing—and everything Silas had to say was to that. To the absence with a zip code.

The postcards themselves were a particular brand, bought in boxes of fifty from a stationery store on Bleecker that smelled of leather and dust and had a cat named after a poet. The image on the front, in this box, was a painting of a lighthouse at dusk, the beam a smear of yellow against a violet sky. Silas had not chosen it. The store had been out of everything else. It didn't matter. The picture was for whoever might one day read the card; the writing was for the house.

Today's card, Silas decided, would be about weather. The weather was the safe subject, the one they retreated to the way other people retreated to talk of sports or traffic. Fifteen years of weather. Fifteen years of *it rained today* and *the heat broke at last* and *first frost, I could see my breath on the fire escape*. Fifteen years of circling, in small talk, the one large thing.

*Dear 114 Meridian Road,*

*It rained all night and the gutters on Delancey sound like a creek. I bought a coat. You would laugh at how much I paid for it. I have been thinking about the porch swing, how the chains sang in wind. I have been thinking about—*

The pen stopped. The nib rested in the wet of the last letter, a small dark bloom spreading into the fiber of the card.

Silas set the pen down with the care of someone disarming something.

There it was, then. The edge of it. *I have been thinking about—* and after the dash, the whole country of unsaid things, the one Silas had been walking the border of for fifteen years like a man pacing a fence line at dusk, never crossing, never quite going home, never quite turning away. *I have been thinking about the night of the fire. I have been thinking about whether you knew. I have been thinking about whether Elias—* No. Not that name. Not on a Tuesday. Not before coffee had gone cold in its cup, which was the signal, the agreed-upon internal protocol, that the writing was over for the day.

Silas picked up the pen again and finished the sentence the way they always finished such sentences, with a swerve, a feint, a small act of literary cowardice they were honest enough to recognize as such.

*I have been thinking about whether it will snow this year. Probably not. It never does until it does.*

They signed it the way they signed all of them: with an initial only. *—S.* A name was a door, and Silas had spent fifteen years keeping doors closed. The postcards went into the drawer of the side table, the drawer that held fifteen years of them, bundled in rubber bands that had perished and been replaced, the earliest ones faded to the color of weak tea. Four thousand three hundred and eighty-one postcards, and not one of them mailed. The stamps were still on the kitchen windowsill, a roll of them bought in a moment of weakness in 2011 and never affixed, because to mail a postcard to a burned house was to admit something Silas could not yet admit, and to not mail it was to keep the whole ritual suspended in amber, ongoing, unfinished, alive.

The ritual was the point. Silas understood this the way you understand a limp: not as a choice but as an accommodation, the body's own negotiation with what happened to it. Every Tuesday, the pen, the card, the almost-saying. Every Tuesday, the drawer. It was not a correspondence. It was a vigil.

The coffee had gone cold. Silas drank it anyway, standing at the window, watching a delivery truck double-park and a woman yell from a third-floor window with a joy that seemed, to Silas, almost aggressive. Below, the city went about its enormous indifferent business. Somewhere a jackhammer started and stopped. Somewhere a dog was being told it was a good dog. The world, Silas thought, is full of people saying things to each other, actual things, out loud, with their whole chests, and here I am, forty-one years old, writing weather reports to a foundation slab two thousand miles away.

They did not think of it as grief. Grief was a word for people who had something to grieve, and what Silas had was more like a debt, or a sentence, or a room in the house of themselves they had locked at fifteen and never reopened, though they passed it daily, though they could hear, sometimes, something moving inside it.

* * *

The legal notice came on Thursday, which was wrong in itself, because nothing came on Thursdays. Thursdays were for the electric bill and nothing else. Silas stood in the lobby of the building in socks, having run down four flights when the buzzer went, and signed for an envelope that was heavy, cream-colored, and stamped with the kind of firm name that cost four hundred dollars an hour to think up: *Hollis, Prather & Vane, Attorneys at Law, Executors and Trustees*.

Silas carried it upstairs without opening it, which was itself a ritual, the ritual of the unopened thing. They made tea. They sat. They held the envelope under the lamp and turned it over twice. The return address meant nothing and everything: a firm in the state capital, four hours' drive from Harrow Bend. Nobody in the capital had reason to write to Silas Delacroix. Nobody in Harrow Bend had Silas's address at all—Silas had made sure of that, fifteen years of careful unlisting, of PO boxes, of the small daily labor of being findable by no one.

The name on the envelope was spelled correctly. That was the detail that made Silas's hands go cold. *Silas Delacroix*. Not a guess, not a misdirection. Someone had looked it up. Someone had gone looking.

They opened it with a butter knife, sliding the blade under the flap the way their father used to open the electric bill, and hated themselves for the memory, and sat with the hatred a moment, and read.

*RE: Estate of Raymond Delacroix, deceased — Notice of Judicial Sale of Real Property*

*You are hereby notified that the property located at 114 Meridian Road, Harrow Bend, consisting of 2.3 acres more or less, including the residential structure and all appurtenances thereon, will be offered at public auction on the 14th day of June at the county courthouse...*

Silas read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves on the third reading, which was disappointing, because on the second reading Silas had been certain they'd misunderstood.

The property at 114 Meridian Road. The lot. The burned foundation and the blackened chimney stub that the fire had left standing like a sentry nobody relieved. And—the notice said *including the residential structure*—the other house. The little house behind the big one, the one that had been their grandmother's, the one that hadn't burned because it stood at the far end of the property behind the stand of pines, the house nobody had lived in since Silas was nine, the house with the green door and the porch that sagged on its left side and the smell of camphor and old paper that Silas could summon now, instantly, completely, as if they'd never left.

The house was being sold. The house with everything still in it.

Because that was the thing Silas had never been able to explain to anyone, the thing they'd never tried to explain to anyone: when Silas left Harrow Bend at fifteen, two weeks after the fire, shipped off to an aunt in a state with better weather and worse memories, the property had been sealed. Their father was dead—dead in the fire, the only one the fire took, the fire that took the house and the study and the porch swing and the father and, in a way that no one in Harrow Bend ever said out loud but everyone understood, the boy Elias too, because a boy who runs away the same night his father dies and is never seen again is a boy the town quietly files under *dead* and stops asking about.

The property had gone into some kind of trust, some legal holding pattern. Silas had been a minor. There had been lawyers Silas never met, papers signed by the aunt, a monthly remittance that paid for the storage unit Silas never visited. And then Silas turned eighteen and the remittance stopped and Silas, with the particular genius for avoidance that would define the next twenty-three years, simply never asked what happened to the land. It existed in the mind the way the postcards did: suspended, ongoing, neither kept nor lost.

Now it was being lost. On the 14th of June. At public auction.

Silas set the notice down on the table, next to the drawer of postcards, and looked at the two objects together—the fifteen years of almost-saying and the bureaucratic end of everything—and felt something that was not quite dread and not quite grief and not quite relief, though it had the shape of all three, like a key that fits three different locks badly.

* * *

That night Silas did not sleep. This was not unusual; Silas had a relationship with sleep that could best be described as estranged, two parties who occasionally shared a bed out of habit and parted before dawn with mutual disappointment. But this night the not-sleeping had a texture, a direction. Silas lay on their back under the ceiling fan and watched its slow rotation and let the mind do what the mind did on such nights, which was to walk the property.

It always walked the same route. Up the gravel drive, past the mailbox with the bent flag. Along the side of the house, where the lilac grew against the foundation and smelled so heavy in June that you could get drunk on it. Around back, past the clothesline, to the pines. Through the pines, where the light went green and the needles were soft underfoot, to the little house with the green door. In these nocturnal walks the fire had never happened. The house stood whole. Sometimes Elias was on the porch of the little house, reading, one ankle over his knee, and sometimes he looked up, and sometimes—this was the part Silas could never control, the part that made the nights unbearable—he looked up and said, *Where have you been?* in the flat, unimpressed tone of a brother who has been waiting exactly long enough to be annoyed, and Silas would wake with wet eyes and a heart going like a trapped bird and lie there until the fan's rotation made them dizzy.

Tonight the walk went wrong from the start. The mailbox was new, steel, municipal. The lilac was gone. And the little house stood with its green door open, swinging, and inside it strangers' hands were lifting out the grandmother's dishes, the camphor chest, the boxes of photographs, and appraising them, writing numbers on tags, and Silas ran through the pines—needles sharp as wire now, ground hard as pavement—shouting something they couldn't hear themselves say, and the strangers turned, and they had no faces, only the smooth blank of people in dreams, and one of them held up the cedar box—

Silas sat up in the dark.

The cedar box.

They hadn't thought of the cedar box in years. It had lived on the top shelf of the little house's closet, behind the winter coats, and it was the one thing in that house that had been neither grandmother's nor father's. It was Elias's. Elias had kept it locked—had kept it locked even from Silas, which had been, at twelve and thirteen and fourteen, an offense Silas had catalogued with the wounded outrage of the young: *what could he possibly have that he can't show me, we tell each other everything, we're supposed to tell each other everything.* A small brass lock. A key Elias wore on a string around his neck, under his shirt, and would touch sometimes when he was thinking, the way other boys touched a cross.

The box had been in the little house. The little house had been sealed with everything in it. Which meant the box was still there, behind the winter coats, on the top shelf, unless—

Unless the inventory had already been taken. Unless strangers had already gone through the little house the way strangers went through everything, lifting and tagging and pricing, and had found the box, and had broken it open, because strangers do not respect locks, they respect *value*, and a locked box is an invitation.

Silas got up. The floor was cold. In the kitchen they turned on the light over the stove, the dim one, the one that made everything look like a memory of itself, and read the legal notice again, properly this time, all the fine print, and there on the second page found the sentence that would end the first life of Silas Delacroix and begin the second:

*An inventory of the personal property included in the sale has been prepared and is available to interested parties upon written request.*

* * *

Friday morning, Silas wrote the letter. It took four attempts. The first was too formal, the second too desperate, the third abandoned mid-sentence when the pen pressed through the paper. The fourth was three lines long and said only that Silas Delacroix was an interested party and requested a copy of the inventory of personal property at 114 Meridian Road, and gave the Delancey Street address, and signed the full name for the first time in fifteen years—signed it and then sat looking at it, *Silas Delacroix*, the letters unfamiliar as a stranger's, the name of a person who did not exist anymore except on legal documents and in the memory of a town that remembered wrong.

They mailed it from the post office on Essex, the old one with the brass doors, and watched it disappear into the slot, and felt the particular vertigo of having set something in motion that cannot be recalled.

That evening, at the table, Silas wrote postcard number four thousand three hundred and eighty-two. The pen hovered. For a long time the card stayed blank, the lighthouse burning its futile beam into the violet dusk, and Silas sat with the blankness the way you sit with a person who has just told you something enormous and is waiting to see what you are.

Then they wrote:

*Dear 114 Meridian Road,*

*They are going to sell you. I found out today. I don't know what happens to a house that gets sold twice, once to fire and once to strangers. I don't know what happens to the things inside you. I am trying not to care. You know I have never been any good at that.*

*The lilac by the kitchen window—does it still come up? Nobody to smell it. What a waste.*

*—S.*

Silas read it over. Then, for the first time in fifteen years, they did something new with the finished card. They did not put it in the drawer.

They stood at the window instead, holding it, while the city made its noise below and the light went the color of the lighthouse's beam, and they thought about the 14th of June, and about the county courthouse, and about the distance between Delancey Street and Harrow Bend—two thousand miles, four days' drive, fifteen years—and about the fact that a person can keep a door locked from the inside for a very long time, but a door is a door, and somewhere, always, there is a key.

They put the postcard in the drawer after all. But they did not rubber-band it with the others. They left it loose on top, the way you leave a bag half-packed by the door.

In the drawer, under the bundles, under fifteen years of weather reports and porch swings and *the heat broke at last*, the almost-said things lay in the dark like seeds. Silas turned off the stove light. Somewhere in a county four hours from the capital, a lawyer's clerk was putting a copy of an inventory into an envelope, and somewhere in that inventory, in a line not yet read, a cedar box was waiting to be named.
