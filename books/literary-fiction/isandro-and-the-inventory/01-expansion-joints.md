## 1. Expansion Joints

A bridge is honest in a way a marriage is not. That is the whole reason I took this job, though I'd never say it out loud at a dinner party, if we still went to dinner parties, which we don't.

Bridges tell you exactly how much they've moved and when. You put a gauge against the joint and it gives you a number — 4 millimeters of seasonal travel, 11 of creep, 2 of something you'll have to think about later. The bridge doesn't pretend the gap isn't there. It doesn't paper over the crack with a smile and ask if you want tea. An expansion joint exists so that two enormous pieces of concrete can sit next to each other for fifty years without grinding each other to dust. There's a lesson in that, presumably, but I've never been able to apply it at home.

My name is Petra Almeida. Twenty-two years inspecting structures for the municipal engineering office, which means I have spent more waking hours with bridges than with my husband, and I cannot honestly say which relationship has deteriorated faster. The bridges at least get scheduled maintenance.

* * *

That Tuesday I was on the Vasco span, the one over the freight cut, doing the quarterly walk with my feeler gauges and my clipboard and my coffee going cold in its cup on the parapet where I always set it, in violation of about three safety bulletins. Morning fog coming off the water in slow gray ropes. Gulls arguing about something. The usual congregation of joggers who resent having to share a sidewalk with a woman kneeling in an orange vest.

The joint at pier four had opened to nine millimeters, up from seven last quarter. Not alarming. Not nothing. I wrote the number down, then wrote it again because my handwriting had gone small and cramped, the way it does when I'm thinking about something other than what I'm writing.

Isandro smiled at me across the kitchen table that morning. I want to be precise about this smile, because precision is the only language I'm fluent in anymore. It was the smile he deploys when I ask him how his day went, or whether he slept, or anything else that might require a second sentence from him. It begins at the corners of his mouth, arrives fully formed, and ends the conversation like a well-placed wedge. Engineers use wedges too. A good wedge can lift forty tons. Isandro's smile has lifted him out of every conversation we've had since roughly 2019, and I have stopped counting the years precisely because counting them made me want to put my fist through drywall.

"How did you sleep?" I asked.

He smiled. Wedge. Done.

I could have said something. I want to be clear that I know that. I am not a passive instrument; I am a licensed professional with opinions and a torque wrench. But there is a particular cowardice that looks like tact from the inside, and I have gotten very comfortable in it. You let the nine millimeters go to ten. You note it in the log. You schedule a follow-up inspection you never quite perform. And meanwhile the structure keeps moving, imperceptibly, daily, until one day someone asks you how a thing got this bad and you produce a folder of measurements proving you knew all along.

"Nine," I said out loud, to no one, tapping the gauge.

A jogger slowed down. "Sorry?"

"The joint," I said. "Never mind."

She ran on. The gulls resumed their litigation. I drank my cold coffee and thought about how Isandro used to talk — actually talk, in paragraphs, with digressions, back when we were young enough to mistake fluency for intimacy. He'd told me once, early, before the silence calcified, that he came from a riverside neighborhood nobody put on postcards. He'd said it the way you'd mention a debt: quickly, and never again. I built our life on top of that omission the way you build a roadbed over soft soil — fine for years, until it isn't.

* * *

The work order came through at two in the afternoon, while I was under the Vasco span with a flashlight in my teeth, looking at hairline cracking in a bearing shelf that probably meant nothing and possibly meant everything.

My phone buzzed against my hip. I climbed down, spat the flashlight into my palm, and read the dispatch.

**WO-4417. Pedestrian Bridge, Ribeira do Sul district. Condition assessment prior to demolition. Structural report required within 30 days.**

Ribeira do Sul.

I stood there under the span with rust sifting down onto my clipboard, and the name went through me like a current through wet concrete. Because I knew the district — not from maps, though yes, also from maps, I have read every map of this city the way other people read novels — but from that single sentence Isandro gave me twenty-some years ago, and from the way his face closed afterward like a door with a broken latch. The riverside neighborhood. The one he left. The one he does not discuss, does not drive through, would not even look at when the news showed flooding there four winters ago. I remember that specifically: flood footage on the television, water brown and muscular between the houses, and Isandro getting up to wash dishes that were already washed.

And now here was the district in my queue, assigned by a scheduling algorithm that does not know my husband's history, does not know my marriage, does not care. Just a bridge number, a deadline, a checkbox marked *demolition pending*.

I should request reassignment. That's the professional move. Conflict of interest, or adjacent to one, or at minimum a conflict of marital peace, which is a scarce resource in my household, rationed like wartime sugar.

I read the work order again. Footbridge, steel through-truss, 1930s construction, connects the Ribeira do Sul neighborhood across the river to the bus terminal — the last direct link, according to the notes, since the ferry stopped running in the nineties. Slated for demolition as part of some redevelopment package. My job would be to document its condition thoroughly enough to justify taking it down.

There are two kinds of inspections. The kind where you find out what's wrong, and the kind where you're sent to confirm what's already been decided. I've done both. The second kind pays the same and costs more.

I typed my acceptance into the system before I finished thinking about it. *Almeida, P. Assigned. Site visit Thursday.*

Then I sat on the bumper of the truck for a while, watching the freight line, feeling the particular guilt of a person who has just made a decision she intends to conceal. Because that was the plan, wasn't it. I wasn't going to tell him. I was going to spend my Thursdays in the neighborhood he fled, measuring a bridge he won't name, and come home smelling of river air and say nothing, and he would deploy the smile, and I would deploy mine, and the two of them would meet over the dinner table like expansion joints — designed, I suppose, to keep us from grinding each other to dust, and doing the opposite.

Here is what nobody tells you about tolerances: they exist because movement is inevitable. Nothing stays fixed. Steel grows in summer heat and shrinks in winter cold, and if you forbid it room to move, it finds the room anyway — it cracks, it buckles, it tears itself loose from its anchors. Every joint I've ever measured is a confession that somebody, somewhere, understood that things change and decided to plan for it rather than pretend otherwise.

We never planned for it, Isandro and I. We just stopped moving. Two rigid masses butted against each other, no gap allowed, no give, and I have spent twenty years documenting the stress fractures in everything except the obvious place.

* * *

Thursday morning I drove out to the Ribeira do Sul district with the windows down, which I never do, because the truck's air conditioning works fine and I am a creature of habit. But something about crossing into streets whose names I half-recognized from Isandro's one abandoned sentence made me want the air on my face, as if I were sneaking in rather than driving in, as if the district itself might notice me and report back.

It was poorer than the postcards would have made it, if anyone had made postcards. Low houses the color of faded laundry, laundry actual and unfaded strung between them, a shuttered market with graffiti like scar tissue, and everywhere the sound of the river underneath everything, patient, going about its business. Old men at a café table watched my municipal truck pass with expressions I couldn't read — or chose not to read, which amounts to the same thing.

The footbridge appeared at the end of the street the way bridges do: suddenly, and as if it had always been waiting. Steel through-truss, green paint gone chalky, rivets in rows like buttons on an old coat. It crossed the river in a single shallow arc, and on the far side I could see the bus terminal, and a bus idling there, and I understood without being told that this bridge was how the neighborhood reached the rest of its own city.

I parked. Signed in. Took out my clipboard.

And then I saw her — a small elderly woman standing at the near abutment, holding a camera pointed at the truss, photographing what any inspector would recognize as cracks. She wore a cardigan the color of strong tea and an expression of tremendous concentration, and as I approached she lifted a small recorder to her mouth and spoke into it — a few low, liquid syllables in a language I did not know, though something in the rhythm of it snagged on me like a burr, like a word heard once in another life.

She lowered the recorder and looked at me. Looked at my orange vest, my clipboard, my face.

"You're the one who measures," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the structural engineer, yes."

"And what do you measure?"

"Gaps," I said. "Movement. How much things have shifted."

She nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she'd long suspected about engineers, or about the world. Then she looked at me again — really looked, the way you'd read a gauge — and said:

"You have the listening face."

I opened my mouth to deflect, and found, to my considerable annoyance, that I didn't know yet what I was going to say.
