## 6. Vocabulary of a Marriage

I had a system, of course. I always have a system.

Tuesdays and Thursdays I told Isandro the inspection office was running late shifts — which was even true, in the way that a foundation is true: technically load-bearing, mostly buried. I'd drive back across town to the shuttered market, climb the stairs that smelled of camphor and old rain, and sit at Dona Célia's table while she taught me a language the way other people hand you contraband — quickly, in small quantities, with a glance at the door.

She taught through objects. That was her method and she defended it like a doctrine. A chipped cup became *kavira*. A bus token, green and worn smooth, became *solme* — which she said meant both *coin* and *permission*, and she looked at me over her glasses when she said it, as if the word were a mirror and she wanted to see what I did with my face.

"You have the listening face," she told me again, third lesson, "but you also have the hiding mouth. In my language we have a word for that too."

"Of course you do," I said.

"*Saveth.* It means a person who keeps their words in a drawer." She tapped the table. "The drawer rusts, Petra. Then the words are stuck. Then the person is stuck."

I wrote it down. I write everything down; it's how I pretend the world is measurable.

What I did not write down — what I kept in no drawer at all but in my mouth, practicing in the car with the windows up like a teenager rehearsing an apology — was the growing suspicion that this language was not merely a language. That it was a specific language. That the way Célia rolled her r's, the way she said *Isandro's river* without ever saying Isandro, the way she once stopped mid-lesson to look at my wedding ring and say, softly, "His grandmother sat where you are sitting" — that these were not coincidences. Coincidences don't have grammar.

I went home that night and opened the box in the closet that Isandro pretends is full of tax papers. It is full of tax papers and, underneath them, three photographs and a funeral card. In the photographs a woman stands on the footbridge, small and straight-backed, holding a boy by the hand. The boy is looking at the camera with the expression Isandro wears when someone asks him about his childhood: polite, closed, a door painted on a wall.

On the back, in faded ink, in a script I couldn't read but could now half-sound-out, was a sentence. I sounded it out in the closet with the light off, which is a stupid thing to do and I did it anyway, twice.

Then I did what any reasonable person in a calcified marriage does with a discovered language. I hid it better, and I practiced in the shower, and I told myself I was doing it for Célia, for the archive, for posterity — for anything except the true reason, which was that I wanted to walk into my own kitchen one evening and say a word my husband had buried, and watch his face.

I want to be clear that I knew this was a terrible idea. I'm a bridge inspector. I assess things for a living. I knew exactly what I was doing and I rated its load capacity as marginal and I proceeded.

* * *

He caught me on a Sunday. Of course he did. Sundays are when our marriage does its most accurate modeling.

I was at the counter chopping onions for a soup we would eat in silence, and I was murmuring. I didn't even know I was doing it — that's the danger of a language that lives in your mouth instead of on paper. It comes out when your guard is down. I was saying the word over and over, working the vowels the way Célia had shown me, pressing my thumb into the cutting board on the stressed syllable like a joint under load.

"*Emena. Emena. Emena.*"

The knife stopped. Not because I stopped it.

I turned and Isandro was standing in the kitchen doorway with his coat still on, keys in his hand, and his face had gone through every stage of a structural failure in the space of two seconds: hairline crack, propagation, collapse.

"Where did you hear that word."

It wasn't a question. Questions go up at the end. His voice went down, into the ground, the way voices do when they're bracing.

"The bridge," I said. "The old woman on the bridge. Célia. She's the last speaker, Isandro. She's recording everything before they demolish it, and she asked for a student, and I—"

"You what." He set the keys down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you'd otherwise throw. "You've been going there. How long?"

"Three weeks."

"Three *weeks*." He laughed, one syllable, no humor in it, a sound like a bolt shearing. "You've been taking lessons. In *her* language. Behind my back. In my own kitchen you've been— what were you even saying? What were you saying just now?"

I could have lied. I have a hiding mouth; Célia diagnosed it herself. But something in me — the same thing that makes me write down the exact millimeter a joint has shifted rather than round it to something comfortable — decided that we had rounded enough.

"*Emena,*" I said. "It means stay."

The kitchen went very quiet. The refrigerator hummed its idiot hum. Isandro stood there with his coat on like a man who has arrived at a funeral for someone who is still standing in the room.

"Say it again," he said, and his voice had changed, dropped its armor, and that was worse. "Please."

"*Emena.*"

He closed his eyes. "My grandmother used to say it at the door. Every time I left. Every single time. She'd put her hand on my head and say *emena*, and I'd say I'd be back Sunday, and she'd say it again, *emena*, and she didn't mean Sunday." He opened his eyes. "She meant stay. She meant don't become one of them. She meant don't forget." His jaw worked. "And the last time, I didn't even turn around. I just waved. I waved at her like she was a neighbor."

"Isandro—"

"No." He held up a hand. "No. Because here's my question, Petra, and I want an actual answer. How long have you known? Not the word. *Him.* The boy in the photographs. The neighborhood. How long have you known where I'm from and said nothing, while you took the bus over there and learned the words I spent forty years burying? How long have you been *digging*?"

"Digging." I put the knife down because holding a knife during this conversation seemed statistically unwise. "You think I've been digging?"

"What else do you call it? Secret lessons. Secret visits. You've been excavating my—"

"Your what? Your childhood? Your *language*?" And then it was out of me, twenty years of it, in full sentences, which in our marriage was unprecedented — we were a couple of fragments, a couple of well-managed clauses, and suddenly I was speaking in whole paragraphs and couldn't stop. "You never told me! In twenty-two years you never once said, my grandmother spoke a language that's dying, or, I grew up across that bridge, or, here is a single fact about the place that made me! I had to learn your grandmother's name from a funeral card in a box you pretend is tax papers! You didn't bury a childhood, Isandro, you buried a *person*, and then you married me and handed me a floor plan with a room missing and acted confused when I noticed the draft!"

"That room was locked for a reason."

"Locked rooms are still rooms! They still lose heat! I've been living next to a locked room for two decades and you want to know why I went through the door the first time it was opened for me?" My voice cracked and I hated it and kept going. "Because I'm married to a man who smiles at me the way he smiles at telemarketers, who ends conversations with that smile like it's a door closing, who has perfected the art of being present and gone at the same time — and then an old woman on a bridge looked at me and said *you have the listening face*, and it was the first time in years anyone had suggested I was capable of hearing anything, including myself!"

"So this is about you."

"This is about *us*," I said, "which is a thing we have never once had a sentence about, so forgive me if I'm clumsy with the grammar."

He stared at me. And here is what I will remember, what I already knew I'd remember even as it was happening: he didn't leave. His coat was on. His keys were three feet away. The door was right there, the exit he'd used his whole life, the Sunday wave — and he didn't take it. He took off his coat instead, slowly, like a man dismantling something, and hung it on the chair.

"She begged me to stay," he said, finally. Quietly. The fury gone out of him and left only the thing underneath, which was worse and truer. "Not just with the word. With everything. And I told her the city had opportunities. I said *opportunities* to a woman who was offering me everything she had. And then the thing happened with my uncle, the shame, the reason I really left, and I let everyone believe I left for a salary. I let *myself* believe it. It was cleaner." He looked at his hands. "You want to know what broke us, Petra? Nothing broke us. Nothing ever broke us. We just never built anything. I never built anything. I've been standing at the door of every room in my life, waving."

The onions sat there, unwept-over, doing their work on me anyway.

"I'm not done with the lessons," I said. It wasn't a negotiation. "Célia is eighty-six years old and she is the last one, Isandro, and when that bridge comes down the bus stops coming, and when the bus stops coming she might as well be on an island. I'm not going to stop learning her language because it's also your wound. I'm sorry. I know that's — I know it's trespass. But some things are worth trespassing for."

He was quiet a long time. Then he said, in a voice I had never heard him use in our kitchen, in our marriage, in our entire careful, millimeter-measured life — a voice with an accent in it, an old one, a river one:

"*Emena* doesn't just mean stay." He swallowed. "It means stay *and I'll tell you everything*. It's a compound. The whole language is full of them. Words that only work if there's another person there to complete them."

"That's the most you've said to me in a year."

"I know." He almost smiled — not the door-closing smile, the other one, the one I'd only seen in the photographs, on the boy on the bridge. "There's a tape," he said. "In her archive, if she still has it. My grandmother made recordings. Before she died. And Petra — there are things on it I have spent my whole life making sure no one would ever hear."

"And now?"

He looked at the window, toward the river, toward the bridge he hadn't crossed in forty years.

"Now I think someone's about to hear them whether I like it or not," he said. "Because I got a letter yesterday, from a law firm, on behalf of something called the Meridian Heritage Initiative. They're claiming Célia's archive is abandoned property." He finally took off his shoes, which somehow felt like the largest act of the entire day. "They spelled her name wrong."
