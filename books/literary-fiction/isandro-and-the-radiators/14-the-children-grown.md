## 14. The Children, Grown

The nurse's name was Wren Okafor now. It had been Wren Delacroix in the Radiator House records, and before that — in the one photograph Sable had found tucked behind Juniper's letters, a girl of perhaps nine squinting into summer sun with her arm slung around a smaller boy's shoulders — it had been nothing anyone wrote down at all.

Sable found her the way they had learned to find everything in this town: sideways, through people who owed other people small kindnesses. A woman at the county clinic who remembered a nurse who never took holidays. A pharmacist who said she was the only person he'd ever seen check a dosage three times and then apologize to the bottle. She worked days at St. Ambrose now, after eleven years of nights, and she lived alone in a narrow brick house on Calder Street with a garden so precisely tended it looked less like pleasure than penance.

Sable waited until her shift ended, because it seemed important not to ambush her in the place where she saved lives. They stood instead by the low iron gate as the evening went amber around them, and Sable held up the photograph — the girl with her arm around the boy — and watched Wren Okafor's face do something complicated and quick, like weather crossing water.

"You're the letter one," she said. Not a question. "The demolition. Everyone's talking about it."

"I'm trying to find out what happened to Tam."

"Don't." The word came out flat and final, a door closing. Then, softer, almost pleading: "Please don't."

"Three beds were accounted for that night. Two bodies were named. He was erased from every record I can find, and someone went to a great deal of trouble to make sure of it. That's not grief, Ms. Okafor. That's paperwork."

Wren looked down the street both ways, though no car had passed in ten minutes, and Sable understood suddenly that this was habit — that this woman had been checking sightlines since she was small enough to need to.

"You don't understand what it was like," she said. "In there. You think you do, because you've read the letters. But those letters are the *good* parts. Those letters are what we were allowed to hope in." Her hands, nurse's hands, capable and steady, had begun to tremble. "Corwin Ashe gave us beds when nobody else would take us. He gave us shoes. He came at Christmas with oranges in our stockings, real oranges, and Halcyon would stand in the doorway with her arms crossed and her face like a closed book, and we thought she was ungrateful. We thought — God help us — we thought *she* was the cruel one, because she wouldn't smile at him."

"And what was she actually doing?"

"Standing between us," Wren said simply. "Every time she could. And when she couldn't—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again her voice had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere clinical and far away, the voice Sable imagined she used to deliver news to families. "There was a room off the study. You had to go through the study to get to it. Most of us never saw inside. Tam did. Tam went in there more than once, and he'd come out and not talk for two days, and Halcyon would sit with him all night on the landing outside his door like a sentry, and none of us knew why, and we didn't ask, because asking questions in that house was like touching a stove to see if it was hot. You only did it once."

"The pact," Sable said. "Halcyon told me about it. Or about its shape. She said the children needed the story closed."

"The pact wasn't spoken. That's what made it hold. Nobody ever sat us down and said *we will never tell*. It just — condensed. Like frost on glass. You could see your breath make it, everyone's breath together, and after a while you couldn't see through it anymore, and you stopped wanting to." Wren's eyes came up and fixed on Sable with terrible steadiness. "I became a nurse because in that house I learned exactly how much damage a body can hide and keep functioning. Do you know what happens if you open all of this now? Juniper's mother is still alive. Ede Lark is still alive. Half this town built its good opinion of itself on Corwin Ashe's money, and the other half built its forgiveness of Halcyon Marsh on believing she was dead. You dig him up, you dig up all of us. Every one of us who kept quiet. Every one of us who let her burn in name so we wouldn't have to burn in fact."

"She didn't let herself. She chose it. There's a difference, and I think you know it, because you've spent thirty years choosing things for other people too."

For a moment something moved under Wren's composure — rage, or its exhausted cousin — and then it settled, and she was only tired, standing in her immaculate garden in her scrubs with the light dying behind her.

"I'm begging you," she said. "As one person who writes hard words to another: stop. Some doors are load-bearing. You pull them out and the whole house comes down on whoever's still inside."

She went up her walk without looking back, and at the door she paused, hand on the frame, and said — not turning, not raising her voice, but clearly enough that the words crossed the garden like something thrown:

"He liked them small. Remember that, whatever else you decide. He liked them small, and Tam was the smallest."

Then the door shut, and the porch light came on, and Sable stood alone on Calder Street holding a photograph of two children who had believed an orange in a stocking was love.

* * *

The prison was four hours north, past country that flattened out into fields the color of old dishwater, and getting in took six weeks, two denied requests, and a letter Sable wrote in their professional hand explaining — carefully, truthfully, omitting only what needed omitting — that they were compiling a historical account of the Radiator House fire and wished to speak with a former resident.

Dell Marchetti had been Dell Ferro then. In the records he was the boy who survived; in the town's memory he was the delinquent who'd been in and out of trouble since; in the fire report he was Resident #4, age fourteen, treated for smoke inhalation and released to state custody within the week. He was fifty-one years old now and serving the ninth year of an eighteen-year sentence for arson himself, and when Sable read that detail in his file they had to put the page down and walk to the window and breathe for a while, because the symmetry was too neat to be accident and too cruel to be anything else.

The visiting room smelled of floor wax and pencil shavings. Dell Marchetti came in small — that was the first thing. A big man gone compact, folded into himself the way furniture folds for storage, with forearms mapped in faded ink and eyes that catalogued exits before they catalogued faces. He sat across the table and looked at Sable for a long moment.

"You're the one digging," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Wren called me. Told me to expect you."

"She asked me to stop."

"Wren asks everybody to stop. Wren's been asking the world to stop since she was nine years old." He almost smiled. It didn't take. "Did she tell you about the oranges?"

"She did."

"Yeah." He looked down at his hands, turned them over as if checking them for something. "You want to know the sickest part? I can still smell them. Thirty-eight years, and some mornings I wake up and I can smell Christmas in that house, clove and peel, and for about four seconds I'm happy. Four seconds. Then I remember what the oranges were *for*, and I'm nine again, and I'm grateful, and being grateful is the worst thing I have ever felt in my life."

"Dell—"

"You want the key." He said it plainly, no drama in it, and Sable felt the floor of the conversation tilt. "Don't look surprised. Wren told me what you found at the house. What you're finding. And I've had eighteen years in here to decide what I'd do if somebody ever came asking with the truth in their pocket." He leaned forward, and up close Sable could see the boy in him still, the fourteen-year-old released to state custody, the child the system had processed like mail. "Here's what I know that you don't. The night of the fire, Halcyon got us out through the kitchen. Me, Wren, two others. Juniper went back in — or was sent back in, we never knew which, and that not-knowing is its own kind of life sentence. And Tam—" His jaw worked. "Tam wasn't in the house that night. Hasn't been for weeks. Halcyon knew where he was. She wouldn't say. She went back in for the ledgers, not for him, because there was no him to go back for, and I have carried that sentence around in my chest for thirty-eight years wondering if I could have—"

He stopped. Breathed. A guard drifted closer and drifted away again.

"There's a storage unit," Dell said, quieter now. "Fairview Self-Storage, unit 114, out past the quarry road. Rented under a dead man's name in 1991 and paid forward twenty years by somebody who never missed a month. After that it rolls over automatic. Nobody's opened it since it was locked." He reached across the table, and his hand — scarred knuckles, a wedding band worn on a chain because rings weren't allowed — pressed something small and cold into Sable's palm. "Juniper gave it to me the week before the fire. Said, *if anything happens, you hold this and you don't give it to anybody unless they come asking about Tam by name.*" His eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "Thirty-eight years, nobody came asking about Tam by name. Everybody wanted to ask about the fire. About Ashe. About poor dead Halcyon. Nobody — not one reporter, not one cop, not one bleeding-heart historian — ever said his name."

Sable's fingers closed around the key. Brass, small-toothed, warm from his hand.

"Why me?" they asked. "Why now?"

Dell Marchetti sat back, and for a moment the folded-up man unfolded, and Sable saw the boy from the photograph, arm slung around a smaller boy's shoulders, squinting into summer.

"Because you write to the dead," he said. "Nine years of postcards to a burned house. Anybody who does that isn't looking for a story. They're looking for somebody." He stood, and the guard came for him, and at the door he turned, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, meant for Sable alone across the wax-smelling room:

"Unit 114. And Voss — whatever's in there, it proves Tam was alive *after* Ashe says he died. Read that again. Alive. After. Which means somewhere out there, maybe, if the world has any mercy left in it at all—" He couldn't finish it. He shook his head once, hard, like a man clearing water from his ears, and let the guard take his arm.

"Ask about him by name," he said from the doorway. "That's all any of us ever wanted. Somebody to ask about him by name."

The door closed. Sable sat alone in the visiting room with a brass key in their fist and the word *alive* ringing in them like a struck bell, and outside the wire-fenced windows the dishwater fields went gold toward evening, and four hours south, past the quarry road, a steel door with a number on it waited in the dark to give up what it had been keeping.
