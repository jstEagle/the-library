## 12. Cousins at the Door

The morning after the burial, Lior woke to the sound of the house being judged.

It was a particular sound, one they remembered from childhood visits—the creak of a floorboard evaluated, the click of a cupboard opened and found wanting, the low murmur of relatives conducting an audit of the dead woman's housekeeping. Lior lay in the narrow bed of their mother's guest room and listened to it the way one listens to weather: inevitable, impersonal, and likely to leave damage.

Downstairs, someone was moving the good plates.

They found six of them in the kitchen: Aunt Marisol at the head of the table with her hands folded like a woman at a tribunal, cousin Rina stacking saucers with unnecessary force, and two more cousins whose names arrived a half-second late to Lior's memory—Pilar, the tall one, and little Jun, who was forty now and would be little Jun at his own funeral. Tomás stood by the window with his back to the room, which in this family was a form of speech in itself.

"There she is," said Rina, in the old language, and Lior felt the words land in a part of them that English never reached. *She.* Not *they.* In the mother tongue there had never been a neutral place to stand, and the family had decided long ago what Lior was.

"Good morning," Lior said in English, out of pure spite, and poured coffee.

"We have been talking," Marisol began.

"Have you." Lior sat down. "I heard the plates."

Rina flushed. Pilar made a small sound that might have been a laugh, quickly strangled. Marisol lifted one hand and the room went still, the way it always had—she had been doing this since before Lior was born, governing kitchens with a single raised palm.

"What you did at the cemetery," Marisol said, "was not grief. Grief makes mistakes. What you did was a *choice.* You opened the gate for that man in front of the priest, in front of the whole town, in front of your mother's grave, and you cannot tell me you did not know what it would cost."

"I didn't know what it would cost," Lior said. "That's rather the point of choices. If you know the cost, it's arithmetic."

"Don't be clever with me, child. I buried a sister this week. I am not in the mood for clever."

Lior looked at her—really looked, for the first time since the wake. Marisol was seventy-three and had the kind of face that weather had argued with for decades and mostly lost. Her hands were folded so tightly that the knuckles had gone the color of the sea in January. She was furious, yes. But underneath the fury, unmistakable to anyone who had spent a childhood being governed by that palm, was fear.

"You're not angry," Lior said quietly. "You're scared."

"I am *furious.*"

"Both, then. But the fury is the fence around the scared part. I grew up inside that fence, Tía. I know how it's built."

For a moment Marisol's composure wavered, and Lior thought—foolishly, hopefully—that something true was about to be said at this table. Then Rina slammed a saucer down and the moment was paved over.

"He stood at the *fence,*" Rina said. "That was the agreement. The back row, the fence, the whole humiliating arrangement, and your mother would have wanted—"

"Would she?" Lior asked. "Which of you asked her?"

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and temperature. Pilar set down the cup she'd been drying and looked at the floor, and Lior understood that Pilar, at least, had heard the question land somewhere she didn't want it.

"He destroyed this family," Marisol said, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "He informed on his own father. Your grandfather went into the water fleeing the coast guard because of what Isandro did, and this family went hungry for three years, and your mother never once—*never once*—spoke his name again. Forty years, Lior. Forty years of silence. And you think a week of your curiosity entitles you to overturn it."

"I think," Lior said, "that forty years is a very long time to keep a story if the story is true. True stories get told. It's the false ones that need guarding."

"That is *filth.* That is—your mother is not three days in the ground and you sit in her kitchen and call her a liar."

"I called her no such thing." Lior's voice stayed low. They were surprised at themselves; the city Lior would already have stood up, gathered coat and keys, made some elegant retreat. This Lior stayed seated. "I said the story might be false. Those are different people, Tía. The mother I knew told me the truth even when it cost her. If she lied about her brother, she had a reason, and I'd like to know it before I bury it with her."

Marisol stood. The chair scraped. For one suspended second Lior thought she might strike them, and thought, with a strange calm, that they would deserve it less than she feared.

"Get out of my sister's kitchen," Marisol said, "and let us clean it properly."

"It's my kitchen now."

"It was never going to be your kitchen. You left. You left and you lost the language and you came back a stranger, and now you want to be an *heir.* You want the house and the name and the right to decide, and you want it all without having paid for it. Belonging isn't inherited, child. It's *kept.* And you didn't keep it."

The words went in exactly where she aimed them. Lior felt the old wound open—the tenant's wound, the one from the city, from every sublet and every contract and every year of living in other people's grammar. Marisol had always known where the soft places were. That was what made her the family's spine and its whip both.

"Maybe," Lior said, standing slowly, "but I'm the only one at this table asking questions instead of guarding answers. So you'll forgive me if I don't hand you the keys to the silence just because you've been holding them longest."

They walked out. Behind them, the good plates resumed their clinking, louder than before, the way people whistle past a graveyard.

* * *

Lior spent the middle of the day in the mother's garden, doing the only diplomacy available to them: pulling weeds. The garden had gone feral in the last year of the illness, and the rosemary had staged a successful coup over the eastern bed. Their hands worked while their mind replayed the morning, and it was in the garden, dirt to the wrist, that the grief finally landed—late, sideways, as grief does when it has had to queue behind everything else.

Their mother was dead. Their mother was dead and the last conversation they'd had was about a parking space. Their mother had written letters to a brother for forty years and mailed none of them, and Lior would never now ask her why, and the why was sitting in a tin box on the dresser like an egg that might still hatch.

"You'll ruin your back like that."

Tomás stood at the gate. He was forty-one, built like the fishing boats he no longer worked on—broad, weathered, retired early from the sea and not entirely reconciled to it. He came into the garden without invitation, which in this family constituted intimacy, and crouched beside the rosemary bed.

"Your aunt is going to make your life hell," he said.

"She's been making my life hell since I was six. It's practically a family tradition. I'd miss it."

Tomás almost smiled. He pulled a weed, examined its roots with the seriousness of a man interrogating a witness. "I told her not to come this morning. I told her, let the kid breathe. But you know Marisol. Grief and command are the same muscle for her."

"She thinks I'm desecrating the dead."

"She thinks she's protecting the dead. It's different." He sat back on his heels. "Lior. What I came to say—"

He stopped. A gull went over, screaming its one opinion. Tomás watched it go the way men watch things they'd rather follow.

"What you said at the table," he said finally. "About true stories getting told. It got under my collar, because it's true, and I hate that it's true, and I've been walking around since the burial with a splinter in my head."

"Tell me."

"My father—" He stopped again. Started differently. "You know the story. Everyone knows the story. The night of the storm, October '79, Isandro goes to the coast guard, the patrol goes out after your grandfather's boat, the storm takes him. Drowned fleeing arrest. That's the story. Marisol was twenty-six, she was in the room when the telegram came, she's kept that story like a relic ever since. Forty years, polished every year."

"Yes."

"And I believe her. I want that said first. My aunt is many things, but she doesn't lie about the dead." He pulled another weed, harder than necessary, roots and all. "But I was fourteen that year, and I worked the harbor that summer, mending nets for old Ferro, and I remember things. Little things. The kind of things a boy remembers because they're strange and then forgets because they're strange."

Lior's hands went still in the dirt.

"The telegram gave the date," Tomás said. "October ninth. The storm was the eighth into the ninth—everyone remembers that storm, it took the roof off the church hall. And the story is that the coast guard went out on the ninth and your grandfather's boat went down that night, fleeing. That's the story. That's what's carved in everyone's hearts if nowhere else, because there's no grave, there's just the date."

"Tomás."

"I saw him." The words came out flat, almost annoyed, as if they'd been sitting in his chest for forty years and he'd finally gotten tired of carrying them. "Not on the ninth. On the *tenth.* The morning after. I was at Ferro's shed, early, before school, and there was a boat going out past the breakwater in that flat gray calm they get after a storm, and a man at the tiller with his collar up. And he raised his hand. Just—raised it. The way you do. And I raised mine back, because I was fourteen and you raise your hand back, it's the harbor, everyone waves at everyone. And then Ferro came out and told me to get inside and mind the nets, and I did, because I was fourteen."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere below, the tide was doing its patient work against the sea wall.

"You're sure it was him," Lior said. Not a question. Their voice had gone strange.

"I'm sure it was your grandfather's coat," Tomás said. "The oilskin, the yellow one with the torn cuff. He wore it every day of his life, everyone knew it, it was practically the town's coat. And I'm sure of the wave. Lior, I waved at a dead man and he waved back, and I was fourteen, and then Marisol told me he'd drowned the night before, and what was I going to say? That I'd waved at a ghost? In *this* family? With my aunt in her black dress keeping the relic polished?" He shook his head. "You put it away. You put it away the way you put away everything in this town. In a box. In a tin box."

Lior stood. Dirt fell from their hands. The morning, which had been about plates and accusations, was suddenly about something with a keel and a tiller.

"The tenth," Lior said. "The morning of the tenth."

"Flat calm. I've thought about that calm my whole life, how the sea goes quiet after it kills something, like it's embarrassed." Tomás finally looked up at them, and his face was the face of a man setting down a load he'd forgotten was heavy. "I'm not saying Marisol lied. I'm saying Marisol *believes* the ninth, and I *know* the tenth, and both of us can't be right. And if the tenth is right, then your grandfather didn't drown fleeing the coast guard at all. He got out. He got out *after,* in the calm, in the yellow coat, and he waved goodbye to a fourteen-year-old boy who waved back."

"Who else knows?"

"Nobody. Ferro, maybe—he's dead now. And you, as of five minutes ago, because your aunt lit such a fire under me this morning that the splinter finally worked its way out." He stood, brushed off his knees. "I'm not taking sides. I want that understood. Marisol raised me half my life and I love her and I'm not going to stand in her kitchen and call her a liar about her own father. But you asked your questions at the table, and it seemed to me somebody ought to hand you the answer they've been guarding, even if it's only half an answer."

"Tomás." Lior's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Why the yellow coat? Why do I know the yellow coat?"

Tomás frowned. "It was famous. The torn left cuff. He patched it with sailcloth and never replaced it, said the coat had earned its keep."

"No." Lior pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. Something was moving behind them, deep, the way a word moves when it's about to surface after years underwater—a memory, not a word this time, or a word and a memory braided together, rising. "No, I know it from somewhere else. I *saw* it. I was—how old would I have been—"

"Lior—"

"The coat. The night boat. I *remember* a night boat, I've always remembered a night boat and I thought I invented it, I thought it was a dream I made up because everyone talked about drowning—" They stopped. Breathed. The memory was there, just below the surface of their mind, and it would not come up yet, and they understood with terrible clarity that this was the bargain working, the price Mrs. Okafor had named: language returns as memory leaves, and some memories only surface when a word buys them passage.

Tomás was watching them with an expression that had moved past discomfort into something like awe. "The night boat," he repeated.

"I need to see Mrs. Okafor," Lior said. "Today. Now."

"She's ninety-two. She naps at four."

"Then I'll be there at three."

Tomás hesitated at the gate, one hand on the latch. He looked back at the house—at the kitchen window where Rina's silhouette was still stacking the dead woman's dishes—and then at Lior, and something in his face settled, the way a boat settles when its cargo is finally lashed down.

"For what it's worth," he said, "when you opened that gate yesterday, half of us wanted to walk out. And the other half wanted to walk in and didn't, because the ones in front didn't." He opened the latch. "I'm telling you about the tenth because somebody in this family has to start saying the things they only say in gardens."

He went. Lior stood alone in the rosemary, hands still dirty, heart going like a gull in a gale, and above the sea wall the tide kept up its patient, ancient work—taking a little, leaving a little, keeping everything.
