## 13. The Harbor Records

The municipal archive of Puerto Viejo occupied the ground floor of what had once been the customs house, a building so old it seemed to have given up on standing upright and was now merely leaning against the hill for support. Inside, the air smelled of salt-rot and old paper, which is to say it smelled exactly like my mother's desk drawers, and I felt an absurd pang of homesickness for a room I'd never been in before.

The archivist was a small man named Señor Bautista who wore his reading glasses on a chain around his neck like a religious medal. He looked up when we entered, took in Isandro—the boatyard hands, the forearms, the face weathered into something between leather and driftwood—and made a judgment I could see land behind his eyes.

"Records," Isandro said pleasantly. "Coast guard logs. Nineteen seventy-nine."

"Which month?"

"October. The night of the storm." Isandro's voice did not change at all, but I felt the room tilt slightly, the way a deck tilts when a swell comes under you that you didn't see coming.

Bautista pulled the glasses down onto his nose with one finger, a gesture of enormous ceremony. "Storm of October '79," he repeated. "You'll want Volume Forty-one. Third shelf, back wall. Bring me the request slip first."

There was no request slip. There was a pad of them, yellowed, on the counter, and a pen attached to the counter by a string so short you had to write hunched over like a conspirator. I filled one out in careful block letters—*Ishikawa, Lior. Purpose: family research*—and while I wrote, Bautista watched us both with the mild, bottomless patience of a man who has spent forty years watching people come looking for things they were not prepared to find.

"People come in here," he said, as he led us back between the shelves, "looking for their grandfathers' fishing licenses, their mothers' birth certificates, proof their uncle stole land from somebody. They always think the paper will settle something." He stopped at the third shelf and ran his finger along the spines without looking at them. "Paper doesn't settle anything. Paper just makes it official."

"That's what we're hoping for," I said.

"No," he said, pulling down a ledger bound in cracked green canvas, thick as a Bible and nearly as heavy. "Nobody hopes for that. They hope for the opposite, and then they get this." He set it on the reading table with a thump that raised dust motes into the shaft of light from the high window, where they hung and turned like tiny confused fish. "Table only. No pens near the book. Pencils are in the cup. You sign out when you leave."

He shuffled back to his counter. Isandro looked at the ledger the way a man looks at a door he has been walking toward for forty years.

"You want to do the honors?" I asked.

He shook his head slowly. "You read. I'll watch your face."

* * *

The coast guard log was not dramatic. That was the first thing that struck me—that whatever we were about to find or fail to find, it lived in a document written by tired men in bad handwriting, recording weather and tonnage and engine trouble in the flat administrative language of people whose job it was to notice things and write them down and go home to dinner.

October 1979 began unremarkably. Fishing vessels in and out. A citation for an unlit navigation buoy. On the fourth, a note that the lighthouse keeper's dog had bitten a customs officer, which Bautista would later tell us was the most exciting entry in any volume that year.

Isandro stood beside me, very still. Every few pages he would say, quietly, "Slower," or "Again," though I don't think he was following the words—I think he was listening for the date underneath them, the way you can hear a wave coming before it breaks.

Then the twelfth of October.

I turned the page and stopped.

The right-hand corner of the leaf had been torn away—not ripped in haste, but cut, or torn along a fold, carefully, the way you'd remove a coupon or a photograph from an album. What remained was a stub of paper maybe two centimeters wide, and on the stub, in faded violet ink, the tail end of a sentence:

*—wind veering SSW, seas building, all harbor craft advised—*

And then nothing. The next legible entry began on the following page, dated the fourteenth of October, two days later, in a different hand entirely, as if whoever had been keeping the log had simply... stopped, and someone else had picked up the pen after a silence.

"Two days," Isandro said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like sound heard through a hull. "The storm was one night. One night, and they lost two days of record."

I touched the torn edge with one fingertip. The paper there was soft, furred, older-feeling than the rest of the page—as if the wound had been handled, worried at, over many years. Or maybe I was imagining that. Maybe I wanted the evidence to have a history, a biography, the way I wanted everything in this town to mean more than it did.

"Señor Bautista," I called.

He appeared with the unhurried gait of a man who had known, since the moment we walked in, exactly why we had come.

"The log for October 1979," I said. "There's a page missing."

"There is," he agreed.

"Do you know what happened to it?"

Bautista took off his glasses entirely, which I understood now was a gesture of great seriousness, the archival equivalent of removing one's hat. He looked at the torn stub for a long moment.

"I have been the archivist here for thirty-one years," he said. "Before me there was Señora Ferreiro, may she rest, who kept the records for twenty-six years. And before her, a series of gentlemen whose primary qualifications were being related to the mayor." He folded his hands. "In thirty-one years, I have found three pages missing from these volumes. One concerns a land dispute involving the church. One concerns a shipment that certain families prefer not to discuss. And one—" he tapped the stub gently with one finger, "—is this."

"Who tore it out?"

"Ah." He put his glasses back on, and the world narrowed politely to the size of his face. "That question I cannot answer. But I can tell you what I know, which is different, and possibly more useful."

We waited. Even the dust motes seemed to wait.

"When a page is removed from a volume in my care, I am required to file a report of damage. When I inherited this collection, I performed a full condition survey—it took me two years; you may imagine my Christmases—and I noted this tear then. The paper edges were already soft. Already handled. Which means the page was removed not recently, but long ago. Within a few years of the date itself, I would estimate. While the ink was still young enough to matter to somebody."

"So someone in the harbor office," Isandro said slowly. "Someone with access."

"Someone with access, yes. And someone with a reason." Bautista smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was the smile of a man handing you a stone when you'd asked for water. "I will tell you one more thing, because you have signed the slip and you have been polite and because you look like people who will keep digging whether I help you or not, and it is better for the records if you dig here, where I can watch. In nineteen eighty-two, the harbor master—a man named Elías Duarte—retired early. Very early. He was fifty-one years old, in perfect health, and he left his post with a pension that required special authorization from the provincial office. Special authorization," he repeated, savoring it, "is a phrase that appears in files when someone important wants something inconvenient to go away."

"You think Duarte took the page," I said.

"I think nothing. I am an archivist; thinking is above my pay grade." He gathered his glasses chain in his hand. "But I will tell you that Elías Duarte's daughter married into a family from up the coast. A fishing family. Good people, hardworking. The Varelas."

The name went through the room like cold water through a hull.

My grandmother had been a Varela. My mother's mother. Marisol's mother.

Isandro had gone very pale beneath the weathering, the way wood goes pale when you sand through the surface layer. "Duarte's daughter married a Varela," he said.

"In nineteen seventy-eight," Bautista confirmed. "A year before your storm. Small town, señor. Everyone is everyone's cousin eventually. It is our chief industry, after fish."

* * *

Outside, the light had gone hard and white, the kind of coastal noon that flattens shadows and makes everything look exposed. We walked down toward the harbor without deciding to; our feet simply went there, the way water goes downhill.

Isandro bought coffee from the kiosk on the seawall and handed me a cup without asking how I took it, which turned out to be correct, which unsettled me more than anything else that morning.

"My mother's family," he said, staring at the water. "Duarte. The harbor master. Tío Rafael used to play cards with him every Thursday. I sat on the man's knee when I was six years old. He let me steer his launch once, in the channel, just for a minute." He laughed, a short, terrible sound. "I reported Papá to the coast guard, and the coast guard was run by Papá's card partner, and the whole time I thought I was speaking to the law. To something clean. I was speaking to family."

"Maybe he still did his duty," I said. "Maybe he logged it and—"

"And then tore it out." Isandro turned to me, and his eyes were wet and furious and frightened all at once. "Lior. Think. If Duarte had logged my report—if there was a record that I, Isandro, son of Emilio, had gone to the authorities about the smuggling boats—then everything the family believed about that night changes. Everything. It wasn't betrayal whispered in the dark. It was testimony, on file, signed. My name would be in that book next to the truth."

"And someone tore it out."

"And someone tore it out." He gripped the seawall, knuckles whitening. "Do you understand what that means? For forty years I have believed I destroyed this family. Marisol believes it. Your mother—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Your mother believed it. But if the page existed, and someone who loved us took it—"

"Then the silence wasn't an accident," I finished. "It was maintained. By hand. By someone close enough to the harbor office to get at the log, and close enough to us to know what the page would do."

We stood there while the fishing boats knocked against their moorings, patient as heartbeats. Somewhere down the quay a radio played a song my mother must have danced to, and I found I knew every word of it, and hated that I knew it, and loved it too.

"Marisol," Isandro said at last.

"She was nineteen," I said. "She couldn't have gotten into the harbor office."

"Not her. Not then." He shook his head slowly. "But Lior—who taught Marisol that the past was a dangerous animal? Who decided, year after year, what this family would remember and what it wouldn't? Someone started this silence. Someone built it, plank by plank, like a hull. And a person who can build a thing like that—" he looked out at the gray-green water, "—a person like that doesn't stop at one page."

My phone buzzed in my pocket. I almost didn't check it. But it was Tomás, my cousin, and the message was short, and I read it twice because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense the first time.

*Lior. I've been thinking about what you asked. About the timeline. Call me. There's something about Tía Carmen's funeral — the dates don't work. Nobody wants to hear this, but the dates don't work.*

Below it, a second message, sent a minute later:

*I checked the parish register. I shouldn't have. Come to the house. Please come alone.*

I showed Isandro the screen. He read it, and read it again, and when he looked up, something had shifted in his face—not hope exactly, but its dangerous cousin, the thing that lives next door to hope and borrows its tools.

"Alone," he said. "He said alone."

"He did."

Isandro nodded, and pressed the coffee cup into my hand, and managed something that was almost a smile. "Go, then. But Lior—" He hesitated, and for a moment he looked every one of his sixty-some years, a man standing at the edge of a record with a page torn out of it. "Whatever Tomás found. Whatever it says. Remember that the people who tore that page out of the book did it because they loved someone. Keep that in your hand when you open the door. You'll need something to hold on to."

I walked up the hill toward Tomás's house with the phone warm in my pocket and the torn stub of October 12th printed on the inside of my eyelids, and behind me the harbor kept knocking its boats against the moorings, keeping its own count, keeping its own silence, patient as paper.
