## 18. The Family Table

I spent the whole morning setting the table wrong.

Not wrong, exactly—wrong by the standards of my mother, who had opinions about table settings the way other people had opinions about weather. The forks went on the left, she used to say, because the right hand belongs to God and the left to the work. I had no idea what that meant when I was seven. I have no idea what it means now. But I put the forks on the left, all the same, and when I caught myself doing it I had to stand very still in the kitchen for a while, one hand pressed flat against the refrigerator, until the feeling passed through me like weather.

Twelve places. I had set twelve places, and then I stood back and looked at them and thought: twelve is a lie. Because one of the twelve was set for a man half the family would rather see drowned twice than seated at their mother's table. And another was set for a woman who was not coming, and another for a cousin who had already texted me the word *shameful* at six in the morning, unpunctuated, like a stone through a window.

I set twelve places anyway. My mother had twelve plates in the good cabinet, blue-rimmed, chipped at the edges, and I had washed every one of them by hand because the dishwasher was a thing she never trusted. The plates came out of the water looking older than they were, the blue worn to a soft gray at the rims, like a coastline retreating.

Isandro arrived at four o'clock, two hours early, holding a fish.

"I didn't know what to bring," he said, standing on the step like a man at a border checkpoint. "So I brought something I could catch myself. It seemed—" He stopped. Started again. "It seemed honest."

The fish was a sea bream, silver-bellied, still stiff with the cold of the boat. I took it in both hands and it was heavier than I expected, the way everything in this town was heavier than I expected.

"It's perfect," I said. "Come in. Help me with the chairs. There are always more chairs than there are people who want to sit in them."

"That's true of most things," he said, and came inside, and stood for a long moment in the hallway with his cap in his hands, looking at the photographs on the wall as if checking whether he had been removed from these too.

He hadn't. My mother had taken down every photograph of him, but she had never taken down the photographs that merely *contained* him—a wedding, a christening, a beach day where he stood at the far edge of the frame, squinting, half out of focus, a man perpetually about to leave the picture. There he was. Forty years in the corner of every family memory, cropped by nobody's hand but time's.

Isandro looked at the beach photograph for a long time. "I remember that day," he said quietly. "The wind came up and we lost the umbrella. Your mother chased it half the length of the beach."

"She never told me that story."

"She wouldn't have. It wasn't a story yet. It was just a Tuesday." He hung his cap on the hook by the door, in the place where caps had always hung, and went to find the chairs.

* * *

The first to arrive was Tomás, which surprised no one and relieved everyone, because Tomás has the useful gift of a man who walks into a room and makes it ordinary again. He brought a case of beer and a bag of oranges and he kissed me on both cheeks and said, "You know I'm supposed to hate you right now, yes? Marisol has a list. I'm on it. You're on it. There is a laminated aspect to this list."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because I read the letter." He set the oranges on the counter and would not look at me. "The one you translated. The first one. You sent it to me Tuesday and I have read it eleven times and I hate you a little for sending it, because now I can't un-know what's in it." He finally looked up. His eyes were red at the rims. "She wrote to him every year, Lior. Every year, and she never sent one, and she never told *anyone*, and she let us all carry the hatred for her like it was a family heirloom." He laughed, wet and unhappy. "We polished it. We passed it down. I was teaching it to my daughter."

The oven ticked. Somewhere down the street a dog was losing an argument with a bicycle.

"There's still time to un-teach it," I said.

"Yes," said Tomás. "That is what I am doing here. Un-teaching. Pass me a knife, I'll cut the oranges."

* * *

They came in ones and twos after that, the way people come to a funeral or a storm. My cousin Elena with her husband and her two boys, who immediately colonized the hallway and had to be peeled off the coat hooks. Old Raimundo, who had fished with my grandfather and who kissed my hand like it was 1975. The Ferreiras from two doors down, who were not family at all but had decided forty years ago that they were, and no one had ever had the energy to dispute it.

And then, at seven o'clock exactly, the door opened and Marisol came in.

I had invited her. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger Mrs. Okafor keeps of this town's silences: I wrote her a note in my careful, childish handwriting, in the old language, and I delivered it myself, and I stood on her step while she read it, and she read it twice, and then she folded it along its crease and put it in her apron pocket and said, "I will think about it," which in Marisol's language meant she had already decided and was going to make me wait out of principle.

Now she stood in the doorway in her good black dress, holding a dish covered with a cloth, and the room did the thing rooms do when the weather changes—everyone kept talking, but the talking got thinner.

She looked at the table. Twelve places. She looked at the place at the far end, where I had put the good chair, the one with the arms, the one that had been my grandfather's.

"That's his seat," she said. Not to me. To the room. "That was Papa's seat."

"And now it's Isandro's," I said. "Because someone should sit in it, and he's the one who came back."

Marisol stood there for a long moment. I watched her decide—not whether to stay, I think, but how much of herself to bring across the threshold. Then she walked to the kitchen, set her dish on the counter, lifted the cloth. It was her rice, the one with the saffron and the little clams, the dish she had made for every family occasion since before I was born, the dish that meant *this is still a family, whatever else it is*.

"Your mother never could make this right," she said, to no one, to the clams. "Too much lemon. Every year, too much lemon." And she took the wooden spoon from the drawer where it had always lived and began, without being asked, without anyone daring to ask, to finish the sauce.

* * *

We ate.

I will not pretend it was easy. There were long stretches where the only sound was cutlery and the sea, and Elena's husband told a story about a parking garage that died in the telling, and someone refilled glasses that did not need refilling. Isandro sat at the far end in my grandfather's chair with his hands in his lap like a schoolboy, and every time he reached for the bread Marisol's spoon paused for half a second in the pot.

But there were also moments. Raimundo told a story about a storm in 1968 and Isandro, before he could stop himself, added the detail about the lighthouse keeper's radio, and Raimundo pointed his fork at him and said, "That's right, that's exactly right, nobody else remembers the radio," and the two old men looked at each other across forty years of nothing and something in the room loosened, a knot giving way.

Elena's younger boy, who is seven and therefore the only honest person present, climbed down from his chair, walked the entire length of the table under the supervision of no one, and stood next to Isandro.

"Are you the ghost?" he asked.

"Elena—" his mother began, stricken.

"No," Isandro said. He crouched down to the boy's height. "I'm the uncle. I was just away for a long time."

"Like the umbrella story?"

Isandro looked up at me, and I realized the boy had been told the beach story at some point, by somebody, and had kept it the way children keep things, without filing it under grief or guilt, just *story*, warm in the pocket.

"Exactly like the umbrella story," Isandro said gravely. "Sometimes things blow away. Sometimes you chase them. Sometimes you get them back."

The boy considered this, nodded once, the nod of a man concluding a business meeting, and climbed back into his chair.

* * *

After the plates were cleared—Marisol would not let anyone else touch them, and Isandro dried, standing beside her at the sink in a silence that was not comfortable but was not war—I went to the cabinet and took down the letters.

I had translated only the first one fully. Mrs. Okafor and I had worked through it over three evenings, and each word had cost me something, and I had paid, and I would pay again tonight, reading it aloud. I had not told anyone that part. Some prices you pay privately.

I stood at the head of the table, which was not my place and never would be, but it was where the light was, and I waited for the room to quiet, which took longer than it should have, because quiet is a thing families resist the way water resists a shape.

"This is my mother's letter," I said. "Written to her brother. Never sent. I'm going to read it in her language first, and then in ours. I'm not going to explain it or defend it. It doesn't need me for that."

I read.

My voice shook on the first line and steadied after that, the way a boat steadies once it's past the breakwater. The old language moved through the room like a current—Raimundo closed his eyes; Marisol's hands went still on the dish towel; Tomás stared at the table as if the words were written there. It is a language built for this, I thought, for saying the hard thing slowly, with all its weight attached. Every noun carried its whole history. Every verb remembered its ancestors.

Then I read it again in the language we share, and it was smaller, the way a translated thing is always smaller, but it was whole:

*My brother. Another year, and I still have not sent this. The lemon tree you planted by the back door has finally died and I have not replaced it, because replacing it would mean deciding whose fault the death was, and I am tired of deciding things like that. I was not angry at you for as long as they think. I was angry at the sea, and at Papa, and at the shape of a story that needed a villain, and you were the only one who stayed in reach. I am sorry I let them use you that way. I am sorry I needed you to be the villain so I could go on being the good daughter. Come home. Not for me. Come home so the children have somewhere true to stand. Your sister, who kept your chair.*

I looked up.

Marisol was crying. Not quietly—there was nothing quiet about it, nothing performed; it was the crying of a woman who had carried a dish for forty years and had just been told the dish was empty the whole time. Isandro had not moved. He sat in his father's chair with his eyes on the table and his jaw set, and one tear got past him, and he let it, and did not wipe it away, as if wiping it away would be one more erasure and he was done being erased.

Tomás stood up first. He crossed the room and put his hand on the back of Isandro's chair—not on Isandro, on the chair, which was as much as he could manage tonight, and everyone understood that, and it counted.

Elena stood too, and took her husband's sleeve, and her face was doing several things at once. "I can't," she said to me, not unkindly. "Not tonight. Maybe not— I don't know. Papa's grave is out there, Lior. I can't sit at a table where—" She stopped. She kissed my cheek. She gathered her boys, and the older one protested, because he had been enjoying the oranges, and she said, "We'll come back," and I believed her, and I also knew that *we'll come back* is a sentence that families say and do not always mean, and I let her go without making her mean it.

The Ferreiras stayed. Raimundo stayed. Tomás stayed, and refilled the glasses, and told the umbrella story back to Isandro with the ending changed—"and she caught it, and she hit your uncle Rui with it, and that's why we don't speak to the Rui branch of this family, and that part is true"—and the room, against all odds, against all the laminated lists, laughed.

Marisol dried the last plate and put it in the cabinet, blue rim facing out, and then she stood with her back to us and said, to the cabinet: "Next time, someone else makes the rice. Too much lemon runs in this family." And she put on her coat, and at the door she paused, and did not turn around, and said, in the old language, one word—the word for *stay*—and left.

Isandro and I stood in the kitchen among the wreckage of twelve places.

"Half stayed," he said.

"Half stayed."

"That's more than I asked for. I asked for the back row of a cemetery."

"You asked for the back row," I said, "because you thought that was all you deserved. My mother spent forty years telling you otherwise in letters she was too ashamed to send. I'm just the mailman." I untied the apron my mother had left on its hook, the one with the faded lemons on it, and hung it back up. "There's one more letter, you know. The last one. I haven't translated it yet. Mrs. Okafor says it's the longest."

Isandro went still.

"And," I said, because the night was nearly over and I wanted to say it while the room was still warm, "there's something else. Something I remembered. Something about a night boat, and a date that doesn't fit. I need to tell you, and I need you to sit down for it, and I need you to understand that everything we buried this week—the drowning, the betrayal, all of it—may have been buried over the wrong body."

The clock in the hallway, which my mother had never once let stop, ticked on into the silence.
