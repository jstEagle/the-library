## 4. Inventory of Almosts

The library kept an accession book for everything. Every item that entered the collection was logged: date, provenance, condition, shelf. Theo had filled in thousands of those lines in her seven years on the night shift, her handwriting small and level, her dates always correct. There was a comfort in it. Nothing in the building arrived without being named, and nothing was named without being assigned a place where it could be found again.

It occurred to her, on the third of March, somewhere between the last insomniac leaving and the first gray light pressing at the tall windows, that she had never once applied this discipline to herself.

She was at the reference desk with a cup of tea going cold beside her and the accession ledger open to a blank page, and instead of logging the morning's donations — two boxes of water-damaged local histories from the estate sale on Corvus Street — she found herself drawing a faint ruled column down the margin of a sheet of scrap paper and titling it, in her smallest handwriting: *Items withheld from collection.*

Because that was what they were. She had been accessioning her silences for seven years, cataloging them with the same care she gave to anything else that entered her keeping, and she had never once written them down. They lived in the same part of her where she kept the layout of the stacks and the birthdays of the regulars and the exact pitch of Isandro's laugh when it came through two aisles of cartography — the part of her that was, functionally, the library itself.

She began, because it was nearly four a.m. and the building hummed in that register that made honesty feel like housekeeping.

*Item the first. Provenance: the staff canteen, December, seven years ago. Condition: interrupted. Description: a dinner.*

It had not even been a dinner, really. It had been the end of a shift that had run long because the heating had failed and they'd stayed to keep the pipes from freezing, and afterward they had gone to the Vietnamese place on the corner that stayed open for cab drivers and night nurses, and they had sat across from each other with pho going lukewarm between them, and Isandro had said, *Theo, there's something I've been meaning to* — and the kitchen had caught fire. Not the building. A pan, a towel, thirty seconds of shouting and a fire extinguisher and a waiter crying, and by the time the smoke cleared Isandro was laughing and wiping his eyes and the sentence was gone, dissolved like sugar in broth, and neither of them went looking for it. She had paid for both meals. She remembered that. She remembered thinking, with a clarity that still embarrassed her: *the universe has opinions, and I will respect them.*

*Item the second. Provenance: the bus stop outside St. Anselm's, April of the following year. Condition: water-damaged. Description: one umbrella, shared.*

It had rained like the sky was settling a grudge, and his umbrella — a black thing, elderly, one rib bent — had covered her more than him, which was his way of doing things, always angling the shelter toward someone else and pretending he hadn't noticed the rain on his own shoulder. They had stood closer than colleagues stand. She could still reconstruct the exact distance: eleven inches, maybe twelve, measured not in inches but in the warmth coming off his coat sleeve. And she had turned her face to him to say it — she had *turned her face*, she had committed to the geometry of it — and at that precise moment the 4A had hissed up to the curb with its lights full on, and the moment had been illuminated, exposed, and had scuttled off into the dark like something with too many legs. He had said, *This is you,* and she had said, *Yes,* and the umbrella had closed between them like a wing folding.

*Item the third. Provenance: the east stairwell, two years ago this autumn. Condition: deceased on arrival. Description: one sentence, seven words, never completed.*

She knew the seven words. She had known them for two years. They were good words; she had tested them against other sentences in other languages, in the dictionaries she shelved, and they held up. But the stairwell had that acoustic — concrete, a stairwell's dead echo — and her voice had come out of her mouth sounding like a stranger's, and Isandro had looked up from the middle of the flight, and she had watched him *wait*, and his waiting had been so patient, so unguarded, so exactly the face she had loved for five years by then, that she had lost her nerve at the last step and converted the sentence mid-air into: *—going to need you to sign the delivery slip for the microfilm cabinet.*

He had signed the delivery slip. He had signed it beautifully. And she had carried the seven words back down the stairwell and shelved them in the dark aisle of herself where the others lived, and she had logged the transaction, mentally, with a steady hand.

*Item the fourth. Provenance: the map room, last spring. Condition: misfiled. Description: a compliment, delivered as a bibliographic note.*

He had found a 1911 harbor chart with soundings marked in a surveyor's hand so fine it looked like embroidery, and he had brought it to her desk and laid it flat and said nothing, just turned it so she could see, because he knew she would understand, and she had understood, and what she had said was: *The ink's iron gall. It'll outlive us.* And what she had meant was: *I will love you longer than this paper lasts, and this paper has outlived everyone who ever touched it.* He had smiled and said, *Everything here outlives us, that's the whole point of the place,* and rolled the chart back into its tube, and the real sentence had gone into the margin of her ledger, uncatalogued, uncirculated, reference only.

Theo looked at her list. Four items. Four accession records for a collection of one. She almost laughed, there in the humming dark, and the laugh turned into something else on the way up and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until it passed.

The trouble with working nights in a library was that you became an archivist of your own life whether you wanted to or not. You learned what preservation meant. You learned that the things you loved most were the things you handled least — no sunlight, no fingerprints, no circulation. You kept them mint. You kept them unread. And you told yourself this was devotion, when any honest cataloger would have flagged it immediately as *deaccession pending*, as an item the institution was quietly planning to lose.

She was still holding the pen over the list when the freight elevator shuddered to life at the far end of the basement level, and she knew the weight of it, the particular groan of the cage carrying more than books. She knew his footsteps too. Seven years will teach you a man's footsteps the way it will teach you a tide table: by heart, and uselessly.

Isandro came around the shelving end of the reference desk with his coat already on, which was wrong — his shift didn't end for an hour and a half — and a folder under his arm, and the particular stillness of a man carrying news he has rehearsed.

"You're here early," Theo said, which was absurd, since she was always there, since she was the fixed point and he was the one who moved, but her mouth had its own accession rules and never circulated anything true before dawn.

"I couldn't sleep." He set the folder on the desk, squared it to the edge with two fingers — she watched him do it, that small compulsive courtesy of his, aligning things, and hated how well she knew it — and then, instead of opening it, he looked at the scrap paper in front of her. "What's that?"

"Accession notes." She turned it over, casually, a sleight of hand she'd have caught in any patron. "Estate donation from Corvus Street. Water damage."

"Right." He nodded at the overturned page as if he believed her, and maybe he did; that was the cruelty of it, that her discipline was good enough to be believed. Then he opened the folder anyway, and slid a single sheet across the desk to her, and the sheet had a letterhead she recognized, because she cataloged correspondence from that letterhead twice a year: the Institute of Cartographic Heritage, in Lisbon.

"They've shortlisted me," Isandro said. "For the maritime collection post. The interview is — " he checked, though she could see he didn't need to check, that he knew the date the way she knew tide tables — "the nineteenth. In person."

The clock above the periodicals said 4:17. Theo looked at the letterhead, at the embossed compass rose, at his name typed beneath a line that said *We are pleased to inform you* — and she performed, with seven years of practice, the entire sequence. The eyebrows. The small genuine-seeming smile, which was genuine, which was the horror of it, because she *was* glad for him, gladness and dread arrived in the same envelope the way they always did, sharing postage. "Isandro. That's — the Almeida Collection? The one you wrote your thesis on?"

"The one I wrote my thesis on." He laughed, a short exhale of a laugh. "They quoted my own footnotes back to me in the letter. I nearly framed it."

"You should frame it."

"Theo." He said her name the way he said it when she was deflecting, which was often, which was a service she provided. "Say something real."

*Item the fifth,* she thought, with the ledger open in her mind's eye, pen poised. *Provenance: the reference desk, 4:17 a.m. Condition: withheld. Description: everything.*

"Say something real," he said again, softer, and for one vertiginous second the stairwell opened up inside her, the concrete acoustics, the seven words standing at attention — and then the automatic lights in the biography stacks clicked off on their timer, that soft pneumatic sigh of a building going back to sleep, and the moment passed through the room like the 4A through rain, and she said:

"I'm proud of you. And the collection will fall apart without you, so I hope you fail the interview."

He laughed — the real one, the one that came through two aisles of cartography — and some of the stillness went out of his shoulders, and she understood that her joke had been received exactly as designed: as affection, as nothing, as a colleague being a colleague. Panic, dressed in professionalism, walking out the front door waving.

"When did you decide to apply?" she asked, and was proud of how steady it came out.

"Months ago." He took the letter back, refolded it along its existing crease. "I almost didn't send it. I sat with the envelope for a week." He looked at the dark windows, at the city beyond them still burning its lights against the dark, the city that never quite slept and lately seemed determined never to sleep at all. "It felt like something was ending. I don't know. You spend enough nights in this building, you start reading the room."

"The room is fine," Theo said. "The room is a listed structure. It can't end."

But they both looked at it then — at the reading lamps burning low over the empty tables, at the night wing with its green shades half-drawn, at the whole dim kingdom they had tended together for seven years — and neither of them said what had been said in the staff meeting, what was in the minutes she had cataloged herself: *phase two of the Harrow Group acquisition, review of night services, proposed conversion of underutilized floor space.* The developers moved through the city the way water moves through a wall: no single dramatic breach, just a spreading stain, a softening, a slow giving way. The hours that belonged to no one were being bought, one by one, and priced by the square foot.

"If I get it," Isandro said, and stopped, and Theo watched him convert his own unfinished sentence into something safer, the way she'd taught herself to do, the way apparently everyone did, the whole species fluent in the grammar of the almost. "If I get it, I'd want — " He looked at her. Eleven inches of desk between them. "I'd want you to help me pack the things I'd want to take. You know what's worth keeping better than anyone."

*Item the sixth,* she thought. *Provenance: the reference desk. Condition: misheard on purpose. Description: an invitation to help him leave.*

"I'd be honored," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she logged it, and the pen in her mind did not shake either, because that was the whole discipline, that was the seven-year craft of it: you could be drowning as long as the paperwork stayed dry.

He stayed another twenty minutes. They went through the Corvus Street donation together, because that was what they did, that was the liturgy — he lifted each water-stained volume and she called out the catalog details and their hands met twice over the same ruined book and neither of them mentioned it, and the second time, over a parish register from 1938 with its pages fused into a single gray brick, his hand stayed on hers a half-second longer than the book required, and neither of them mentioned that either.

*Item the seventh,* she thought. *Provenance: the donation table. Condition: fused. Description: two hands, one register, four seconds.*

At 5:40 he went down to the freight elevator with his coat collar up, and the cage groaned under his weight, and Theo stood at the reference desk and looked down at her scrap paper, at the four items in her small level handwriting, and beneath them, without deciding to, she wrote a fifth line:

*Item the fifth. Provenance: Lisbon, pending. Condition: —*

She couldn't fill in the condition field. She sat there while the sky went the color of dishwater and the first day bus sighed past the windows, and the field stayed blank, because there was no controlled vocabulary for it, no term in the thesaurus for *the thing you love is about to be relocated to another country and you have catalogued your cowardice so thoroughly that you no longer know where the original is shelved.*

Then she folded the paper in four and put it in her breast pocket, where it lay against her sternum like something awaiting a stamp.

She told herself she had a month. The interview was the nineteenth; decisions would take weeks after that; there was time, there was always time, the night shift ran on time the way the sea ran on tide. She shelved the morning's returns. She logged the Corvus Street donation in the real ledger, date correct, handwriting level. And it was only when she came to the last box, the one from the basement, the one the flood had reached — only when she lifted out the warped lid of the tin and saw the envelopes inside, their edges furred with damp, their ink blooming like things drowned and resurrected — that she remembered the wardrobe, and the letters, and Adaeze, and understood that the building had been trying to tell her something for weeks, and that she, like everyone, had been too busy practicing her almosts to read the room.

She turned the first envelope over. The address had been written twice, crossed out once. The stamp was uncancelled. Seventy-some years in the dark, and it had never once been posted.

Theo stood in the gray light with a dead woman's undelivered letter in her hand, and her heart did the thing it did in stairwells.

She went to find Isandro.
