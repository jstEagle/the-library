## 9. The Interview

The map room is the coldest room in the library and the only one where the light behaves. It comes down in long amber columns through the clerestory windows, catching the dust that never settles so much as it drifts, patient as plankton, and it turns the flat files and rolled vellum into something like a chapel. Theo has always thought that if the building has a heart, it is here — not because maps are beautiful, though they are, but because a map is a promise someone made about a place, and promises age better than most things.

She is rehousing the harbor charts when Isandro comes in.

She knows it's him before she hears him. Seven years will do that: she can read his footsteps the way sailors read weather, the particular unhurried weight of them, the half-second pause at the threshold that he always makes, as if asking the room's permission. She keeps her eyes on the 1934 survey of the eastern quay, on the soundings penciled in a dead cartographer's careful hand, and she says, without turning, "If you're here to tell me you shelved the coastal atlases under geography again, I've already forgiven you."

"I'm here to tell you something else."

Something in his voice — a flatness, a held breath — makes her hands stop moving. She sets the chart down with exaggerated care, aligning its edge with the table's, because her hands need a job or they will betray her, and turns around.

He is standing by the window with the light behind him, which means she cannot see his face properly, only the outline of him, the way his jacket hangs slightly wrong because he's been pulling at the collar. He is holding an envelope. She recognizes the letterhead before he says anything — the deep navy of the Meridian Institute, the embossed compass rose she once spent an entire evening looking up online while he was at lunch, back in year three, when she still believed curiosity about a person could be satisfied the way hunger is, completely and then forgotten.

"You got it," she says.

"I got it." He laughs, short and disbelieving, and steps out of the light so she can see him whole. He looks the way he looked the day they pulled the tin box from behind the wardrobe — lit from inside and frightened of it. "Two years' fellowship. The maritime collection in Lisbon. Original charts, Theo. Sixteenth-century portolans that have never been digitized, never even *catalogued* properly. There's a chart there from before anyone drew this harbor correctly — before anyone knew where the sandbars were, how many ships they ate." He stops himself, embarrassed by his own velocity, the way he always does. "I found out Tuesday. I wanted to— I waited."

"You waited," she repeats. Her voice comes out level. She is distantly proud of it, the way you're proud of a colleague who performs well under pressure, except the colleague is you.

"Until today. Until—" He gestures vaguely, taking in the map room, the dust, the amber light, her. "I don't know. Until it felt real enough to say out loud."

*Say it out loud.* Theo thinks, absurdly, of the stairwell in year two, the sentence that died there between floors, and of all the other sentences since, each one escorted to the door of her mouth and turned away politely, thank you, not tonight, we're closing. Seven years of practice, and here is the examination she never studied for: not how to say the thing, but how to stand in a cold bright room and watch him hold a future that does not include her, and be gracious about it.

"When do you leave?" she asks.

"January. The fifteenth." He winces as if the date were a physical thing he'd dropped on her foot. "It's soon. I know it's soon. They want me there for the winter acquisition cycle, and there's a conservator I'm supposed to apprentice with, a woman named Beatriz Anselmo who worked on the—"

"You'll be good at it," Theo says.

And there it is — the thing she does, the thing she has done for seven years, flawlessly, the way a night librarian keeps the hours no one wants: she gives him exactly what he needs. Not what she needs. What he needs is permission, enthusiasm, a friend who celebrates him cleanly, without sediment. So she smiles, and the smile is real, that's the terrible part; some traitor part of her genuinely is glad for him, glad the world has finally noticed what she noticed in year one, that Isandro Voss reads water-damaged paper the way other people read faces, that he deserves Lisbon and portolans and a life larger than a basement that floods.

"It's wonderful," she says, and means it, and hates meaning it, and means hating it, and all of these happen in the space between one dust mote and the next drifting down through the amber column between them.

Isandro watches her face.

She knows he's watching because he goes quiet in the specific way he goes quiet when he's reading something — she's seen him do it over the letters, over Adaeze's looping hand, over the tide tables in the reference room. He reads people the way he reads documents: slowly, twice, looking for the watermark. And Theo feels her face doing its work, the careful work, the seven-year apprenticeship showing itself: the smile calibrated to joy-without-claim, the eyes kept soft but not wet, the small forward tilt of the head that says *tell me more, I'm safe, I ask nothing.*

"Say something else," he says quietly.

"What?"

"Anything else. You said 'you'll be good at it' and 'it's wonderful' and both times you sounded like the announcements. Like the recording." He shifts his weight. "Like the voice that tells us the building closes at six."

"The building *does* close at six," Theo says. "For everyone else."

"Theo."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Say my name like it's a question you already know the answer to." She turns back to the harbor chart, smooths a corner that doesn't need smoothing. The 1934 surveyor has drawn the undertow in fine hatched lines off the eastern pier, a warning to any vessel foolish enough to cut close. *Dangerous in all conditions,* the margin note says. *Especially at slack water, when it cannot be seen.* "I'm happy for you," she says to the map. "I am. That's the truest thing in this room right now, and the room contains four hundred years of very true documents."

Behind her, silence. Then the sound of him exhaling, long, through his nose — the sound of a man deciding, consciously, to accept a gift wrapped in the wrong paper.

"Okay," he says. "Okay. Thank you." A pause. "You'll write to me? Actual letters. I'll be surrounded by correspondence from dead admirals; I'd like at least one correspondent who's alive."

"Email exists."

"Email isn't the same and you know it. You're the one who told me a letter is a room you build for someone and wait inside." He says it lightly, teasing, quoting her back to herself, and the words go through her like a needle finding the old scar. Year four. The umbrella. She had said it on the steps outside the library, rain coming down in gray ropes, both of them under one inadequate umbrella, and she had meant it as a key, had watched his face for the lock to turn, and he had smiled and said *that's beautiful* and stepped out into the rain to test whether it was still raining, which it was, and the moment closed over them like water.

"A letter is a room you build for someone and wait inside," she agrees now, evenly. "Fine. I'll write. Someone has to tell you when the basement floods again."

"It won't flood. They're fixing the pipe."

"They said that last time."

* * *

They end up sitting on the floor between the flat files, which is against regulations and which they have done perhaps thirty times in seven years, always for reasons that seemed sufficient at the time: a fire drill, a power outage, a letter from Adaeze that needed to be read sitting down. Now there is no reason except that standing up had become unbearable for both of them, though neither says so.

Isandro turns the envelope over in his hands. "Can I tell you the strange part?"

"You're going to regardless."

"The strange part is that I almost didn't apply. Months ago. Before the letters, even." He picks at the envelope's corner. "I wrote the application and then I put it in a drawer for three weeks. And I couldn't figure out why. On paper it was everything — the collection, the city, the stipend. And every morning I'd open the drawer and look at it and feel like I was—" He searches. "Like I was leaving in the middle of a sentence."

Theo's chest does something complicated and silent.

"And then the wardrobe came apart," he goes on, "and we found her letters, and I thought — oh. Oh, that's why. Because she stayed. Because Adaeze had a whole ocean of reasons to leave and she folded them up and put them in a tin and lived fifty years next door to the unsaid thing." He looks at her. In the amber light his eyes are nearly the color of the varnish on the oldest charts. "So I decided I wasn't going to be her. I decided the drawer was cowardice dressed up as loyalty. I sent the application the same week."

"That's—" Theo begins, and has to stop, and restart. "That's the healthiest thing anyone has ever said in this building."

"Isn't it?" He grins, briefly, and then the grin fades into something more careful. "Except now that it's real, I keep thinking about the tin box. Forty-one letters and none of them delivered. And I think: what if the problem was never that she didn't leave? What if the problem was that she never *said* anything either way? She just — performed. Married well. Kept the fire burning in the right window at the right hour like it was a job." He shakes his head. "Duty as a performance. I don't want that. Whatever I do, I don't want to perform my life."

"No," Theo says. "Neither do I."

The words come out with more weight than she intends, and she feels them land — sees them land, in the small stillness of his shoulders — and then, because she is who she is, she stands up briskly and brushes off her trousers and becomes, visibly, a librarian again.

"Well," she says. "Lisbon. You'll need a heavier coat than you own, and you'll need to learn that their coffee is not optional, it's structural. And you'll need—" Her voice catches, one hairline fracture, mended so fast anyone else would have missed it. Anyone else. "You'll need someone to water whatever survives in your office, which will be nothing, because you kill everything, which is fine, plants are a scam."

"See, *this*," Isandro says, standing too, laughing, and something in the laugh is relieved and something in it is wounded, and Theo cannot tell which part belongs to whom. "This is the Theo I'll miss."

*Will miss.* Future tense. She holds the phrase at arm's length, examines it like an accession record: condition, fair; provenance, unclear; estimated value, incalculable; disposition, pending.

"When do you tell the others?" she asks.

"Monday. There's a staff meeting." He hesitates at the door of the map room, the light full on him now, and for a moment he looks like he might say something else — she watches the sentence form behind his eyes, watches it approach the door of his mouth the way she has watched her own sentences approach hers for seven years, and she braces for it, actually braces, one hand finding the edge of the flat file behind her.

"I'm glad it was you I told first," he says instead.

"Of course it was," Theo says. "Who else would sit on the floor with you?"

He laughs and goes, his footsteps fading down the corridor toward the daylight shift, and Theo stays alone in the map room among the promises people made about places, the coastlines surveyed and the soundings taken and the dangerous waters marked in fine hatched lines — *dangerous in all conditions, especially when it cannot be seen* — and she stands there until the dust finishes one of its slow descents through the amber column, and then another, and then she takes out her phone and opens the search bar and types, before she can talk herself out of it:

*Adaeze Voss, married name, 1948.*

Because if Adaeze stayed and performed and survived fifty years beside the unsaid thing, Theo needs to know what that cost, itemized. She needs the ledger. She needs to know whether the woman in the letters got to be happy, or whether happiness was just another word for a fire kept burning in the right window at the right hour, for no one, forever.

The search spins. Results begin to load.
