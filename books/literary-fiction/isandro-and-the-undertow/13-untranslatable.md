## 13. Untranslatable

The library at three in the morning is a held breath.

Theo knew this the way she knew the weight of the date stamp, the particular complaint of the third-floor radiator, the exact number of steps from the circulation desk to the map room — forty-one, which she had never once said aloud to Isandro because it seemed like the kind of thing a person only counts when they are counting something else. The building at this hour was hers in all the ways that mattered and none of the ways that counted. The security lights burned their low amber. The elevators slept with their doors open like patient mouths. Somewhere above her, the city went on not sleeping either, but elsewhere, in towers she had no keys to.

She had brought the letters up from the basement against every regulation she was sworn to uphold, and she had done it the way she did everything that mattered: carefully, invisibly, with a folder labeled *PENDING ACCESSIONS — DO NOT SHelve* laid over them like a shroud. Adaeze's handwriting traveled under her arm through the stacks, forty-one water-stained ghosts riding an elevator built for cartographers and insomniacs, and Theo felt each one of them pressing against her sternum like small cold hands.

At the reading table by the north window she spread them out in chronological order, which was also, she had come to understand, the order of a woman's heart being dismantled piece by piece and reassembled wrong. The early letters were long, exuberant, full of the harbor and the market and the specific blue of certain evenings. The middle ones shortened. The last ones were brief as telegrams, as if Adaeze had learned that love, unreciprocated by circumstance, eventually learns economy.

And then there was the one written partly in Igbo — Adaeze's mother's tongue, the language she had reportedly saved for anger and prayer and, apparently, for this: a single paragraph set down in a script that leaned hard to the right, as though even on paper the words were trying to run toward Tomas across the water.

Theo had studied Igbo for two years in graduate school, badly and then adequately and then, during one strange winter when she'd had nothing but time and a broken engagement behind her, well. She could read it the way you read a river you've swum before — knowing where the currents were, respecting the deep places. She had been translating the letters for Isandro in careful installments, giving him his grandmother back a page at a time, rationing the past like wartime bread.

But this paragraph she had not translated. Not for him. Not for herself. She had copied it out onto a sheet of legal pad paper three nights ago, sat down with her best dictionary and her old grammar notes, and then — nothing. The pen had hovered. It was hovering now.

*Ndị mmadụ na-ekwu na mmiri na-ewere ihe niile anyị na-enye ya.*

That much was easy. *People say the water takes everything we give it.* A proverb-shaped opening, the kind of sentence elders used to begin arguments they intended to win. Theo wrote it out in English beneath the original, her hand steady, her professional self fully online. Translation was a job. She was good at jobs.

The next line took longer. There was a word she had to look up twice, *mbọ*, effort or striving or the act of wading in, depending on context, and context here was doing something complicated. *Ana m agbalị ịgụ gị n'ime mmiri dị ka m gụrụ akwụkwọ ozi gị* — *I try to read you in the water the way I read your letters.* Beautiful. Devastating. Translatable. She wrote it down and felt the familiar ache of admiring someone dead, which was a feeling she was getting alarmingly good at.

Then came the sentence.

It began simply enough. *Tomas, ọ dịghị ihe m ga-ekwu n'asụsụ ha* — *Tomas, there is nothing I can say in their language* — and Theo's pen stopped so abruptly it left a small crater in the legal pad, a period punctuating a sentence that hadn't ended.

Because she understood what came next. That was the problem. She had understood it the first night, standing over the tin box with Isandro's shoulder warm near hers, and she had closed the folder and said, *This part's damaged, water got to it,* which was a lie so smooth she had almost believed it herself. The ink was fine. The water had gotten to nothing. What had gotten to it was recognition — the ugly, total kind, the kind that finds you out.

*Tomas, there is nothing I can say in their language, so I will say it in mine, where my mother cannot hear it and your wife will never read it:*

And then the sentence. Seven words in Igbo. Simple words. A child could parse them. Theo had parsed them in under four seconds, standing in the flooded basement with her sleeves rolled up and her heart going like a trapped bird, and in those four seconds she had watched seven years of her own discipline stand up and walk out of her body, leaving her holding a dead woman's confession like a mirror.

She knew the sentence. She had known it since she was twenty-four years old, sitting on the floor of a kitchen in another city, saying it to someone else first.

* * *

Her name had been Marguerite. Everyone called her Mags, except Theo, who called her Marguerite precisely because nobody else did, because that was the year Theo had learned that loving someone meant noticing the name under the nickname. They had met in a seminar on West African oral literature, of all things — two women huddled in the back row over shared notes, and within a month they were inseparable in the way that only happens once or twice in a life, finishing each other's citations, sharing a flat with paper-thin walls, trading coats and secrets and Sunday mornings.

Theo had loved her the way you love a co-conspirator, which is to say completely, and without, for a long time, examining the grammar of it. Love between women could hide inside friendship the way contraband hides inside legitimate cargo. Nobody checks. You don't check yourself. You tell yourself the warmth is ordinary, the jealousy is loyalty, the way your whole chest reorganizes itself around her footsteps in the hall is just — closeness. Just what friends are.

Until the night of the storm, the one that knocked the power out across half the city, when they sat on the kitchen floor by candlelight eating cereal because the stove was electric and neither of them could cook anyway, and Marguerite was laughing with her head tipped back and the candle made her throat gold, and Theo felt the sentence rise in her like floodwater.

She said it. That was the thing people never understood later, when she told the story as a joke about bad timing. She actually said it. She didn't choke, didn't deflect, didn't perform the elegant retreat she would later perfect into an art form. Twenty-four-year-old Theo Okafor looked at her best friend in the candlelight and said, plainly, in the plainest words she owned: *I'm in love with you. I have been for a year. You don't have to do anything with that. I just couldn't keep carrying it alone.*

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were waiting.

Marguerite's face did something Theo had never seen it do before and would spend years learning to name: it closed. Not with cruelty. With fear. With the particular panic of a person who has just discovered the floor of their life is thinner than they thought. She said, gently, terribly, *Oh, Theo. No. God, no — I mean, I love you, obviously, but not — please don't make this a thing.*

*A thing.* Her love, a thing. An object suddenly in the room that everyone had to step around.

What happened next was not a scene. Scenes require drama, raised voices, a slammed door. What happened next was worse: nothing dramatic at all. Marguerite was kind. Marguerite was careful. Marguerite started washing her cereal bowl immediately, standing at the sink with her back turned, and the conversation continued in a normal register about the seminar and the leaking window and whether they should buy a fan before summer, and both of them pretended with such flawless competence that the sentence Theo had spoken seemed to dissolve into the air, unclaimed, unreceipted.

But the flat changed temperature. You cannot unsay a thing; you can only agree to pretend it was never said, and pretending is labor, and labor shows. Within two months Marguerite had found a sublet across town with a friend from her department. Within six they saw each other at conferences and exchanged the warm, hollow greetings of the formerly close. Within a year Theo could count their conversations per annum on one hand, and each one cost her a day of recovery.

The lesson she drew from it — and she drew it fast, the way you yank a hand off a hot stove — was not *love is dangerous*. It was more precise than that, more useful, more damning: *the sentence is the weapon.* The feeling was survivable. The feeling had been survivable for a year, a warm stone she carried everywhere. It was the saying that detonated. Words, once released, could not be recalled, could not be reclassified, could not be folded back into the friendly dark. Say it and you lose the person. Keep it and you keep everything — the shared shifts, the umbrella, the laugh across the reading room, the whole architecture of almost.

So she built the discipline. Seven years of it, practiced daily like scales. She learned to convert the sentence into behavior: remembering how he took his tea, holding the elevator, laughing exactly one beat after he did so it wouldn't look like obedience. She learned to let the feeling crest and pass through her like weather, untouched, unnamed. People praised her composure. Her supervisor called her *unflappable* at a staff review and Theo had smiled and thought: *you have no idea what it costs to be flapped and flapless simultaneously, every working hour, forever.*

And now a dead woman was holding up the same sentence in a different alphabet, saying: *I carried it too. I carried it until the man who should have heard it drowned rather than hear it, and then I carried it fifty more years in a language my husband didn't speak, and I put it in a box behind a wardrobe because some sentences refuse to die quietly and must be entombed alive.*

Adaeze had married well. Theo knew that already from Isandro's family stories — the respectable marriage, the children, the long widowhood in a house full of photographs where no lighthouse keeper appeared. Adaeze had done the math Theo was still doing and arrived at the answer Theo feared was correct: survival means silence, silence means keeping, keeping means losing anyway, just slowly, with interest.

Unless. Unless the sentence, said aloud, was not a grenade but a key. Unless Marguerite's kitchen was not a prophecy but a single data point, and Theo had spent seven years generalizing from a sample size of one frightened woman by candlelight.

* * *

The pen hovered.

Outside the north window the city performed its three a.m. pantomime: a delivery truck idling below, its reverse-beep counting off seconds like a metronome for cowards; a lit window across the street where someone sat up too late with their own unsayable things, probably; the harbor invisible but present, a mile east, holding whatever it held.

Theo looked at the seven words. She had written them out phonetically in the margin days ago, in her own cramped hand, as if transcription were harmless, as if the Latin alphabet could domesticate them. *Ọ dị m n'obi ịbụ onye ị na-ehi ụra na ya ruo mgbe ikpeazụ.* Even rendered phonetically they refused to be inert. She could feel them in her mouth without speaking them, the way you can feel a stair in the dark.

*I want to be the one you fall asleep beside until the end.*

No. That wasn't quite — she tried again, silently, the translator's hopeless second draft. *My heart wants to lie down next to yours until the last day.* Closer. Igbo didn't say *I want* the way English did; it located the wanting in the chest, in the *obi*, the heart as a physical place with weather. Adaeze hadn't said *I desire you* or even *I love you*. She had said something more architectural: *my heart wants to build its sleeping beside your sleeping, until the end of days.* A whole life proposed in the grammar of rest.

And underneath it, in smaller script, squeezed against the margin as if ashamed even of its own size, Adaeze had added one more line, and this was the part that had made Theo sit down on a crate in the flooded basement and breathe until Isandro asked if she was all right:

*Ma ọ bụrụ na ị gụrụ nke a, pịa m n'obi ma sị m okwu ọ bụla, ma ọ bụghị nke a.*

*If you are reading this, hold my heart and say anything at all — anything but this.*

Anything but this. Because Adaeze too had known the arithmetic. Because Adaeze had written the sentence and then, in the same ink, begged the reader never to make her live in the world where it had been said. The letter was a door bolted from the inside. Tomas had drowned before he could knock.

Or — Theo's pen moved at last, writing the English in her smallest hand — or Adaeze had wanted him to knock. Had mailed the letter knowing the sea was between them, trusting the sea to do what her courage couldn't. Some confessions are designed to arrive too late. It lets you have said it without ever having to survive the saying.

Theo understood this with the intimacy of a twin.

She looked at her translation notes — three pages now, the paragraph nearly complete, only the final sentence left in the original with its phonetic ghost beside it — and she thought about Isandro reading them. He wouldn't, of course. He didn't go through her things; he was a man of scrupulous boundaries, boundaries she had helped maintain for seven years like a gardener pruning anything that grew toward the light. And yet the thought arrived anyway, unwelcome and precise: *these pages are lying on a table in a public building, and the sentence is right there, and anyone who reads Igbo could—*

Nobody in this library read Igbo. She had checked, months ago, casually, catastrophically. She was the only one. The sentence was safe. The sentence would stay swallowed, passed from Adaeze to her like a relay baton of cowardice, carried to the grave, buried behind another wardrobe—

The delivery truck outside pulled away. In the new quiet, Theo became aware of a sound she had been half-hearing for several minutes without processing it: the freight elevator, humming. Descending? Ascending? At three-forty in the morning, when the only other soul in the building was the security guard dozing over his crossword in the lobby?

She gathered the letters quickly, sliding them back into their folder, and was reaching for her translation notes when she heard, distinctly, footsteps on the mezzanine stairs. Unhurried. Familiar. A gait she could identify blindfolded from forty paces, having catalogued it for seven years among her private holdings.

Isandro came around the end of the stacks with his coat already on and a cardboard box under one arm, and he stopped when he saw her, and his eyes went — briefly, a half-second, a duration she would replay for weeks — to the table. To the legal pad. To the pages of her handwriting fanned there in the amber light, faceup, legible, complete.

"Theo," he said. "I couldn't sleep either. I was going to—" He paused. Something moved behind his face, some decision being weighed on scales she couldn't see. "I was going to ask you something."

He set the box down on the table, directly beside her open notes, and did not move his hand away from it.
