## 18. The Going-Away Gathering

They held it in the map room because the reading room had already been stripped for the surveyors. Someone — Priya, I think, though she denied it with the particular vigor of the guilty — had strung a strand of fairy lights along the atlas cases, and the little bulbs made the old maps glow like things underwater: coastlines rising and sinking in amber, empires dissolving at their edges. Half the shelves were boxed. The other half wore dust sheets like mourners.

I arrived early, as I always did, to do the work that makes a party look effortless. I moved the trolley of oversize folios into the corridor. I found forty-one plastic cups and counted them twice, because counting is what my hands do when my mind is elsewhere. I set out the biscuits on a cartography drawer lined with butcher paper — Mercator's projection under the shortbread, which felt like an honest metaphor for the evening ahead.

In my cardigan pocket, folded into eighths, was a single sheet of paper.

I had written it at 3 a.m. three nights before, in the staff room, with the kettle ticking as it cooled. One sentence, translated from Adaeze's dialect as faithfully as I could make it, and beneath it, in smaller handwriting, the admission I had not yet said aloud to anyone living: *This is mine too. It has been mine for seven years.* The paper had been in my pocket so long now that it had taken the shape of my hip, softened at the creases, gone slightly translucent where my thumb worried the fold. I touched it the way you touch a stone you've decided to carry across a river — checking, always checking, that it was still there.

The stone metaphor was Isandro's, actually, though he didn't know he'd lent it to me. Years ago, shelving geology, he'd held up a river-smoothed pebble from some monograph's plate and said, "You know why they're smooth? Because water argues with them every single day and never stops." I had thought then, as I thought now: everything I love arrives to me secondhand, through him, annotated by him.

* * *

People came in waves, the way people do when the occasion is goodbye and everyone wants to arrive after the awkwardness has been absorbed by someone else.

First the night shift, naturally — we are a tribe of the unsociable hours. Priya from periodicals, who had known Isandro since his probation year and greeted him by punching his arm. Old Marek from acquisitions, who brought a bottle of plum brandy in a bottle that had once held dish soap, on the theory that no one would steal it from the staff fridge, which was true for eleven years until tonight. The twins from circulation, Devi and Anouk, who moved through the world as a single organism with two opinions.

Then the day people, blinking and slightly wrong-looking under the lights, like creatures dragged up from depth. And then, inevitably, the administration.

Gerald Voss arrived at seven-forty precisely, in a charcoal coat that cost more than my annual book budget, carrying a bottle of wine with a label in French and an expression of magnanimity that I had learned, over the past months, to read the way a sailor reads weather. Gerald was the supervisor who had signed off on the modernization months before any of us were told. He smiled with all of his teeth. His smiles had the specific warmth of heat lamps over food no one intends to eat.

"Theo." He took both my hands in his, which I permitted for exactly one and a half seconds. "What a turnout. You've done wonders with the space."

"It's the map room," I said. "It does itself."

"That's the spirit. Stewardship." He said the word the way developers say *heritage* — meaning the opposite. He drifted off toward the brandy, working the room, shaking hands, memorizing faces for later use or later disposal. I watched him pause at the atlas case, tilt his head at the fairy lights, and murmur something to the man beside him — the magnate's representative, the one with the lanyard and the tablet, who had been coming to the library for weeks now measuring our darkness in lumens and square feet. The representative laughed at whatever Gerald said. Neither of them looked at the maps.

Isandro arrived last, which was unlike him, and I understood immediately why: he had dressed as though for a court appearance. Grey suit, no tie, the top button open in a concession to humanity. He stood in the doorway of the map room for a moment, taking in the fairy lights and the dust sheets and the faces, and I saw him do the thing he does — the slow inventory, the cataloguing of a room the way he catalogs a shelf, everything in its place, everything accounted for.

His eyes found me across the room. They always find me; that is the one reliable law of my universe. Seven years of practice had taught us both how to look at each other in public: briefly, warmly, with nothing in it that could be quoted back. But tonight something slipped in the mechanism. Tonight his gaze caught on mine and stayed half a second too long, and I felt the folded paper in my pocket go heavy as ballast.

*Say something ordinary,* I instructed myself. *Say: nice suit. Say: the brandy is dish soap.*

"You came," I said, which was unforgivably stupid, and he smiled, and the smile undid me more thoroughly than any cleverness could have.

"Where else," he said, and let it hang there, unfinished, the way he lets everything hang, and went to greet Marek.

* * *

The toasts began at eight and deteriorated on schedule.

Marek went first, raising the dish-soap bottle: "To Isandro, who reshelved my misprints without reporting me for six years, which in this institution constitutes sainthood." Laughter. Isandro raised his cup — the brandy was already in it, which explained the sudden rosiness of several cheeks — and said, "Your misprints taught me everything I know about forgiveness."

Devi spoke for circulation: a story about the flood of '19, when Isandro had carried wet folios up four flights two at a time until three in the morning, saying nothing, complaining less. "He treats books," Devi concluded, "the way the rest of us only claim to." Anouk added, "And he alphabetizes his feelings, which is why none of us have ever seen one." More laughter. Isandro laughed too, easily, and I watched him laugh and thought: *that's the performance. That's my performance. We are the same animal, he and I, trained in the same discipline of the almost-said.* It should have comforted me. It only made the paper in my pocket heavier.

Priya cried a little, which surprised everyone including Priya, and covered it by accusing Isandro of stealing her stapler in 2019, and he solemnly returned a stapler he had apparently purchased for this purpose, and there was more laughter, and the fairy lights trembled whenever the radiator shuddered, and outside the tall windows the city went on selling its last quiet hours block by block.

Then Gerald tapped his glass with a pen — a pen, of course, gold-capped, the kind that signs things — and the room arranged itself into the shape of listening.

"I'll keep it brief," said Gerald, who had never once kept anything brief, "because I know we all want to get back to the celebration." He beamed at Isandro. "Seven years. Remarkable. In that time I've watched you grow from a promising young shelver into—" a small pause, a connoisseur's pause, "—a genuinely institutional figure. The kind of colleague an organization builds around."

*I know,* I thought. *That's why you're letting the building fall on him.*

"When the opportunity abroad came through," Gerald continued, "I confess I wasn't surprised. Talent like Isandro's was never going to stay in a basement forever. And frankly—" here he spread his hands, the very portrait of candor, "—it's a sign of health, isn't it? An institution that produces people the wider world wants. We should be proud. This place gave him his foundation, and foundations," he said, with a glance around the half-boxed room that lasted exactly long enough, "are meant to be built upon."

The representative with the lanyard nodded along, thumbing his tablet. Somewhere in that device, I knew, was a floor plan of the night wing rendered in cheerful blue, labeled *PHASE ONE*, with our reading room drawn as a rendering: no patrons, no dust, no hours that belong to no one. Just light, and glass, and a nameplate not yet engraved.

Isandro thanked him. That was the worst part — he thanked him, gracefully, with a joke about the basement flooding having prepared him for any climate, and the room laughed, and Gerald laughed loudest, and I stood by the atlas cases holding my cup of tea I wasn't drinking and performed, flawlessly, the face of a colleague at a farewell party.

Inside, where the translation lived, something turned over like a page in wind.

* * *

Later — nine-something, the party gone soft and warm, Marek asleep sitting up with his dignity intact and his bottle empty — Isandro found me at the window.

Not approached. Found. There's a difference; seven years teaches you the difference. He simply ended up beside me the way water ends up level, looking out at the harbor district, where the cranes stood against the sodium sky like patient herons.

"Gerald called it a foundation," he said.

"He calls everything a foundation. It's the only architectural term he knows."

"He signed the papers in March." Not a question. I looked at him. "Priya told me," he said. "Last week. I haven't—" He stopped. Recatalogued. "I haven't decided what to do about it."

"What is there to do?"

"I don't know. Something. Nothing. Sign nothing, say less — isn't that what we've been doing?" He said *we*, and I felt it land somewhere below the ribs. Then, quieter, watching the cranes: "Adaeze married well, you know. After Tomas. Lived fifty more years and never said his name to anyone. I keep thinking about that. What it costs, surviving something. Whether the silence is a refuge or a debt."

"Both," I said. "That's the trap of it. Refuge and debt are the same object viewed from different decades."

He turned to look at me then, fully, and the party noise receded the way it does in the seconds before a wave breaks. "You've been carrying something all week," he said. "Since the letters. Since the translation. You hold your pocket when you think no one's watching. Like a talisman. Or a weight."

My hand was, in fact, on my pocket. I removed it with enormous dignity, which fooled neither of us.

"It's nothing," I said.

"All right," he said, and let it go, and that was the discipline, that was the art we had perfected together over seven years — the graceful acceptance of each other's silences, the courtesy of not asking twice. He lifted his cup toward the window, toward the cranes, toward the drowned keeper and the signal fire and the whole submerged country of things unsaid between us. "To foundations," he said wryly.

"To foundations," I said, and drank, and hated myself gently, completely, in the way that has become almost tender over the years.

* * *

By ten the party was thinning. The twins left arm in arm, arguing about whether Isandro's new office would have a window. Priya hugged him for a full minute and pretended afterward that she hadn't. Marek woke, delivered a final toast in a language no one present spoke, and was steered into a taxi. The fairy lights stayed on; nobody wanted to be the one to switch them off.

And still I stood among the dust-sheeted shelves with the paper in my pocket, and still I did not move.

Here is what I want recorded, whoever finds these words, whoever inherits this account the way Isandro inherited Adaeze's tin box: it was not cowardice that held me there. Cowardice would have been simpler. It was arithmetic. I stood doing the sum I had been avoiding for seven years — the sum of what is lost if I speak and what is lost if I don't — and both columns came out ruinous, and the equation refused to balance, and so I stood, holding the stone, at the edge of the river.

He was across the room, saying goodbye to the last of the day staff, laughing at something, his hand resting on a box marked *ATLASES — Q–Z*. Tomorrow he would fly out. Monday the surveyors would come for the night wing. The tide of the world was going out in every direction at once, and the two of us stood on the flats watching it, polite as strangers, fluent in each other's silences, masters of a language whose only rule was that nothing in it may be finished.

Adaeze waited fifty years and mailed one letter too many and one letter too late, and the sea returned it unopened, and I had sworn — alone, at 3 a.m., to a cooling kettle — that I would not be her. That undelivered words do not die; they wait. That the debt comes due eventually, with interest, and the interest is a whole life.

Across the room, Isandro looked up from the goodbyes and found me again. Held. Half a second too long. Two seconds. Three.

The room emptied. The fairy lights burned. My hand went into my pocket, and closed around the folded page, and did not let go.
