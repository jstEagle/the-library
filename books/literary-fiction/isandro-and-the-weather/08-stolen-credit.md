## 8. Stolen Credit

The summer of 1972 smelled like cut grass and somebody else's money, and I want it on the record — my record, the one nobody pressed — that I knew the difference between those two smells before I ever signed anything.

The letter came on a Tuesday. I remember because Tuesdays were when Marcus washed the car and complained about it, and he was halfway through hosing down the hood of our father's Buick when I came out of the house holding an envelope with a return address in the city and my name spelled almost right. *Isandro Vey.* Almost right was more than most people managed. They wanted to call me Sandy, or Alex, or once, memorably, *Anders*, as if I were a Scandinavian exchange student who'd wandered into the wrong decade.

"Who's writing to you from the city?" Marcus asked, not looking up from the windshield. He had that tone he got when he suspected the world was about to hand me something and then take it back, which was his way of loving me: pre-emptive grief.

"Somebody from Meridian Records," I said, and even saying it out loud felt like showing off, so I said it again, quieter, just to feel it in my mouth.

He shut off the hose. That was how I knew it mattered. Marcus did not stop mid-chore for anything short of weather or women.

* * *

Mr. Halloran — that was the producer's name, Gerald Halloran, a man built like a filing cabinet who wore his tie loose the way other men wore their regrets — drove out to see us himself, which he made sure we understood was unusual. He sat at our kitchen table drinking coffee my mother kept refilling out of pure nerves, and he listened to the demo tape all the way through without talking, which I later learned was his one genuine talent. Listening just long enough to know what you'd give up for him.

"That's the girl singing?" he said, when the tape hissed into silence. Edith's voice hung in the room a second longer than she had, the way it always did.

"Edith," I said. "Edith Thornbury."

"And you wrote."

"We wrote," I said.

Halloran nodded slowly, the nod of a man confirming inventory. "Well. Here's the situation." And there it was, the phrase every musician learns to dread, the little trapdoor under the word *situation*. "Meridian wants the songs. Both sides of what you sent, plus whatever else you've got in that notebook of yours. We're talking real distribution. Radio play. This is not some garage operation." He smiled at the garage across the yard as if it were a charming child. "But there are complications."

My mother, bless her, chose that moment to discover the oven needed checking, and left us alone with him, which I have thought about since. What she heard. What she pretended not to hear.

"The complication is me," I said.

Halloran looked at me with something that wasn't quite pity. Pity would have been kinder. It was more like appraisal — the look you give a chair you're deciding whether to keep. "The complication is the business," he said. "Edith's father has been in touch. Mr. Thornbury is prepared to fund the session costs personally. Studio time, pressing, promotion. That kind of backing is worth more than any label advance at this stage." He turned his coffee cup a quarter-turn, a man aligning things. "In exchange, the record comes out under her name. Solo artist. The songs are work-for-hire — you'd be compensated for your studio time, standard rate, and there'd be a line in the credits for arrangement assistance."

"Arrangement assistance," I repeated.

"It's a real credit," he said. "It's in small print, but it's there."

I want to be fair to Halloran. I have spent fifty years being fair to Halloran, mostly against my will, in the courtroom of three a.m. He didn't invent the terms. He was a messenger with good shoes. But I remember thinking, in that kitchen, with the smell of my mother's coffee and the sound of the hose still dripping outside: *this man has already decided I'm furniture, and he's waiting for me to agree to be furniture politely.*

"I wrote those songs," I said.

"You wrote half of them," Halloran said. "And the girl's father says the other half too. Says he's got her notebooks." He spread his hands, apologetic, a priest absolving himself in advance. "Look, son. In this business, a name on a credit is worth exactly what someone will pay you for it. Right now, that's studio time and a line of small print. Take it, get your foot in the door, write the next batch under your own name. Everybody starts somewhere."

"Everybody starts somewhere," I said. "Just not everybody starts erased."

He didn't flinch. Filing cabinets don't. He finished his coffee, thanked my mother for the pie she hadn't offered, and told me Meridian would need an answer by Friday.

* * *

Marcus found me in the garage after dark, sitting where Edith and I had written half of everything, on the old couch with the springs that announced you like a town crier. He didn't sit so much as lower himself onto the arm of it, which was his way of leaving room for an exit.

"Heard about the offer," he said.

"Offer," I said. "That's generous."

"It's money, Iso."

"It's forty dollars a session and my name in letters too small to read."

"It's a record," Marcus said, and his voice did the thing it did when he was trying to be the older brother instead of the mocker, the thing that was worse somehow, gentler, more dangerous. "Iso. Listen to me. You know what happens to boys like us who wait for the perfect deal? Nothing happens to them. That's what happens. They wait, and they get older, and the songs stay in the notebook, and one day you're thirty years old playing bar mitzvahs in a town that never learned your name anyway." He leaned forward. "You think anybody in this country is going to hear that song and check the fine print? They're going to hear *her voice*. Her voice is going to carry your melody into kitchens and cars all over the state, and nobody — *nobody* — can take back a melody. You'll have written a song people know. That's immortality, brother. Small print can't touch it."

I hated him a little, right then, because he was right in the way that mattered to survival and wrong in the way that mattered to everything else, and I couldn't yet tell which way the scale tipped.

"They'll take the next ones too," I said. "You don't understand how these people work. You give them one song as work-for-hire, they own the style. They own the next ten before you've written them."

"So write different songs."

"These are the songs," I said, and my voice cracked on it, embarrassing, sixteen years old and cracking over chords. "These are the ones, Marcus. You heard the tape. You told me yourself it was the best thing you ever heard come out of this garage, and now you're telling me to hand it over with a bow on it."

"I'm telling you to survive," he said. "There's a difference between a name and a life. Daddy's been cutting hair for thirty years and nobody knows his name either, and we ate every winter." He stood up, brushed off his pants, and put his hand on my shoulder, heavy as a verdict. "Sign the paper, Iso. Marry the girl if she'll have you. Write new songs. Don't let pride eat the whole meal."

After he left I sat in the dark with the springs creaking under me and tried to imagine a version of myself who signed. He seemed reasonable. He seemed survivable. He also seemed like a stranger wearing my face, and I couldn't make myself love him.

* * *

Edith came by the next evening, through the gap in the hedge the way she always did, carrying two bottles of pop and that walk of hers that pretended the whole neighborhood wasn't watching. Her father had forbidden the door, but doors were never really the issue with Edith; she treated prohibitions like weather, something to dress around.

We sat on the garage floor with the couch behind us and the notebook open between us, and for a while we did what we always did, which was finish each other's lines. She'd hummed a verse at me for three weeks that wouldn't resolve, and I finally heard where it wanted to go, and we stitched it together in twenty minutes the way we did, laughing, her knee against mine, the whole future so obvious it barely needed saying. That was the last whole song. I didn't know it then. Nobody ever knows the last one until much later, when they count.

Then she went quiet, the particular quiet she got when her father's voice was running underneath her thoughts like groundwater.

"He came to the house," she said. "Halloran. Before he saw you."

"I figured."

"Daddy offered him the money before Halloran even asked." She turned the pop bottle in her hands, around and around. "He's been planning this since the tape. Since before the tape. He heard us through the window in June, Iso. He's known all summer what we sound like together, and what he heard was an asset."

"Edith—"

"He says it's protection." She said it fast, the way you say a thing you've been rehearsing until it stops sounding like what it is. "He says a solo girl singer is a clean story. He says a mixed act, a boy with no family money and no — he says the labels won't touch it, and even if they do, he says—" She stopped. Around and around went the bottle. "He says he's doing it for me."

"What do you say?"

She looked at me then, and I want to describe it accurately, because for years afterward I misremembered it as weakness, and it took me most of a life to see it was something worse than weakness. She looked at me the way you look at a house you love that you already know is flooding.

"I say I'm scared," she said. "I say I'm eighteen years old and my father holds every dollar I've ever touched and every wall I've ever lived inside, and you're asking me to bet all of it on a garage and a boy with a guitar." Her voice steadied in a way that frightened me. "And I say I hate myself for doing the arithmetic."

"Don't do the arithmetic," I said. "That's the whole point of us. We were supposed to be the thing that doesn't do the arithmetic."

"That's easy for you to say. You'd only lose the songs." She caught herself, and I watched her catch herself, watched the apology arrive and get swallowed. "That's not — Iso, that came out wrong."

"No," I said. "It came out true. That's the problem with it."

We sat there while the light went out of the gaps in the garage boards, and neither of us played anything, which had maybe never happened in that room before. Music had always been the place we could say things. Now the unsayable thing sat between us and the guitars stayed in their cases like witnesses refusing to testify.

Finally she said, "If I sign it. If I do what he wants. Would you still — would you hate me?"

And here is the answer I gave, and I have audited it for fifty years and cannot improve on it or forgive it: I said, "I could never hate you."

Which was a lie, or a prophecy, depending on how you file it. Because the truth was I didn't know. The truth was I had never been hated-adjacent by anyone I loved before, and I assumed love was load-bearing enough to hold whatever she put on it. The truth was I should have said: *don't sign it, and if you sign it, yes, some part of me will die in that room and haunt you politely forever.* Instead I handed her absolution in advance, gift-wrapped, no conditions, and she carried it out through the hedge like a receipt for something she hadn't purchased yet.

* * *

Friday I went to Halloran's hotel with the contract in my jacket and Marcus's voice in one ear and my own stubbornness in the other, and I read every line twice, including the small print, especially the small print, the line that said *arrangement assistance: I. Vey* in type you'd need a jeweler's loupe to love. Edith sat beside me at the little table with her hands folded, wearing the church dress her father liked, and Halloran hovered with a pen laid out between us like a surgeon's instrument.

I asked for one change. Just one. Not my name on the label. Not royalties. Just: *words and music by E. Thornbury and I. Vey,* in the same size font, on the sleeve, where a human being might someday read it.

Halloran looked genuinely pained. "Mr. Thornbury's terms were explicit."

"I'm not asking Mr. Thornbury. I'm asking you."

"And I'm telling you the funding walks if the terms change." He slid the pen toward me, then, seeing my face, angled it toward Edith instead, smooth as dealing cards. "Miss Thornbury. It's your name on the marquee. Your call."

And she looked at the pen. I watched her look at the pen for what felt like the length of a whole song, and I understood, in the floor-dropping way you understand certain things, that she was going to pick it up. That some part of her had picked it up already, days ago, in a kitchen, in a study, in the groundwater of her father's voice. That the wavering I'd been reading as a woman choosing between two futures was actually a woman rehearsing one of them until it felt inevitable.

"Iso," she said softly, "we can still — after this, we can still write together, nothing has to change between—"

I stood up. The chair made a noise. Somewhere in the hotel a television laughed at its own joke.

"Don't sign it," I said. "That's my last thing to say to you that isn't angry. Don't sign it."

She didn't sign it that day. That's the detail I clung to for decades, the splinter of *she didn't, not that day, not in front of me* — I built whole rooms in my head out of that splinter. Halloran closed his folder and said the offer stood until the end of the month, and we rode home in silence in Marcus's borrowed car, and Edith's shoulder touched mine the entire way, warm and deliberate, a person holding onto a railing while deciding whether to jump or climb back inside.

The end of the month came. I never saw her sign anything. That was the trick of it, the cruelty I only untangled years later: I never got a scene. No confrontation, no tearful confession, no villain twirling the pen. There was just a Friday when Halloran stopped calling, and a record that appeared that fall with her name on the sleeve and my melodies inside it like organs in a stolen body, and a girl at the hedge gap who couldn't meet my eyes, and a silence that opened between us the width of the rest of our lives.

What I didn't know — what I couldn't have known, standing in that ruined summer with my fists full of pride — was that she had written it all down. Every word of it. That somewhere in a house I would never be invited into again, behind a piano, a confession was already addressed to me and sealed with fear, waiting fifty years for a pair of hands brave enough to open the panel.

Her hands weren't brave enough. Mine weren't either, for a long time.

But hands were coming. Hands I hadn't met yet, with her eyes and none of her fear.
