## 18. The Garage

### Silas

The storm arrived the way my mother used to announce guests — early, without apology, and with an air of having been expected all along. By four o'clock the light had gone the color of old pewter, and by five the first fat drops were hitting the windows of the music room like someone testing the glass for weaknesses. I had the tin box open on the piano bench, the letters fanned out in the order I'd reconstructed them, and the reel-to-reel sitting on top of everything like a paperweight made of grief.

I was, I'll admit, talking to the house. Not to my mother — I'd given up on her as an interlocutor somewhere around the attic — but to the house itself, the way you talk to a dog or a car or a therapist you can't afford. "Well," I said, "he's not coming."

The house, being a house, said nothing, which is more than most people manage.

He'd left the mailbox letter in my mailbox three days ago. Not a reply — Isandro Vey did not reply, apparently, he *responded*, the way weather responds to pressure — but a single sheet, no salutation, no signature, just a sentence in a hand that shook like a man holding a railing: *The garage is still standing, then.*

That was it. That was the whole letter. I'd read it maybe sixty times, which, I understand, is the same number of times he'd read my half of Edith's confession, so at least we were even in our obsessions. And I'd understood it the way you understand a knock at the door — not as a statement but as an invitation, or possibly a dare. The garage. Where they'd written the songs. He wanted to see it, or he wanted me to see him see it, or he simply wanted, at eighty-one, to stand in the last room where anyone had ever believed he was going to be somebody.

So I'd spent the afternoon doing something I hadn't done since childhood: cleaning the garage. Not organizing — cleaning. Sweeping out forty years of leaves and wasp nests and the desiccated corpse of a badminton set nobody had ever played with. I'd dragged the two folding chairs from the porch out there, set them facing each other in the middle of the concrete, and then felt so absurd about the arrangement that I turned one of them to face the wall, as if he might prefer not to be watched.

The rain picked up. Through the small window over the workbench, the yard went silver and then gray and then the color of a photograph left in the sun too long. I sat in the chair facing the wall and listened to the roof do its drumming impression and thought about how strange it was that I was nervous. Not scared, exactly. Nervous the way you're nervous before a recital — the feeling that something you've practiced in private is about to be performed in public, and the public is the one person alive who will know if you play it wrong.

Headlights swept the driveway at six-forty. A car I didn't recognize — a sedan the color of a wet dime, driven with the extreme caution of a man who had decided some years ago that dying in a crash would be an undignified way to waste a perfectly good grudge. The engine cut. The wipers stopped mid-arc. And then a long silence, long enough that I actually got up and opened the garage door myself, into the rain and the smell of it, and there he was, standing beside the car with an umbrella he hadn't opened, looking at the garage the way a man looks at a grave he isn't sure is the right one.

He was smaller than the photographs. That was the first thing. In the pictures from the tin box he was all shoulders and dark hair and that grin Edith had captured mid-laugh, the grin of a boy who has never once been told no by anyone whose opinion mattered. The man in my driveway was thin as a coat hanger, white-haired, in a rain jacket that had outlived several presidents. But when he looked up at me, the eyes were the same eyes, and they went through me like a nail through soft wood.

"You're her," he said. Not a question. "You look like her when she was angry."

"Hello to you too."

"You look like her when she was angry," he repeated, as if I'd disputed it, "and she was angry more often than she let anyone see." He looked past me into the garage. "You cleaned it."

"There was a badminton set. It had to be done."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not a smile, but the place where a smile had been amputated. He walked past me, slowly, one hand trailing along the doorframe like a man checking a wound for tenderness, and stood in the middle of the concrete floor, in the exact spot where I'd put the chairs, and looked at the workbench, and the window, and the water stain on the ceiling shaped like nothing in particular, and I watched eighty-one years rearrange themselves on his face.

"It was bigger," he said.

"Everything was."

### Isandro

The garage was smaller and the roof leaked in the same corner and someone had painted the floor, or sealed it, or done something to it that made it look less like a floor and more like a stage, and none of it mattered, because the second I stepped inside I was nineteen years old and it was ninety degrees and Edith was sitting on an overturned paint bucket with a legal pad on her knees, saying, *no, no, it goes up there, listen —* and going up there, and me following her with the guitar, and the two of us climbing into a key like kids climbing a tree we'd been told not to climb.

Fifty-three years. I had not let myself think about this room in fifty-three years, and now here it was, smaller, and here I was, older, and here was her daughter standing in the doorway with her mother's angry face and her mother's habit of waiting for other people to speak first.

"You cleaned it," I said again, because the first time hadn't taken.

"I told you. The badminton set."

"Edith hated badminton."

"Then why—"

"Because her father bought it," I said. "He bought everything. That was the whole problem, if you want the short version." I lowered myself into one of the folding chairs — she'd set out two, which meant she'd expected me, which meant she'd read the letter the way I'd meant it, which meant something I wasn't ready to look at directly. The chair was cold and my knees announced the descent the way they always did now, loudly, like a couple of old men complaining about the government. "Sit down, Silas. I didn't drive two hours in a storm to stand."

She sat. She had her mother's way of sitting, too — spine straight, hands quiet, the whole body arranged like a letter that hadn't been opened yet.

"Ask me," I said. "You drove to my shop. You left the letter. You've been asking everyone in this town about me, I hear things, this town is a telephone with a roof. So ask me what you came to ask."

She was quiet for a while. The rain filled the silence, which was considerate of it. Then: "Did you love her?"

I laughed. I couldn't help it — it came up out of me like water out of a cracked pipe. "That's not the question. You've had the letter a week. You know I loved her. The letter says it in every line, she wrote it down, she kept it, she kept *me*, folded up behind the piano for fifty years like a receipt for something she never paid for. Ask me the real question."

"Fine." Her chin came up — Edith again, the same chin, the same angle of defiance deployed at the exact moment of maximum vulnerability. "Why did you send the tape? Three weeks before she died. Why then, after fifty years? Why not let it go?"

"Because I was dying," I said, "and I found out that letting go is a thing people say to make the dying feel like they've accomplished something." I looked at the workbench, at the window, at the gray light coming through it. "I had the tape in a drawer for fifty years. Her voice and mine. I couldn't play it. I couldn't throw it out. And then the doctor sat me down in March and used the word *progressive* four times, and I went home and I played it, and I sat on the floor of my shop like a fool and listened to a nineteen-year-old girl sing a song we wrote in this room, and I thought: she is still alive. She is out there, somewhere, and this song is in a drawer, and when I die it goes in the trash with everything else, and then nobody ever knew. Not even her daughter. Her own daughter doesn't know her mother could *do* this."

"She knew," Silas said quietly. "I found the reel. In the attic. Labeled in her handwriting."

That stopped me. I had not expected that. I had expected the tape to be a stranger to this house — mailed out into the world like a message in a bottle, received by a radio man, heard by strangers, never by the one person it was about. But of course Edith had kept a copy. Edith kept everything. That was the whole architecture of the woman: she kept everything and gave back nothing.

"She listened to it," Silas said. "There are wear marks on the box. She played it enough to wear the label."

"Then she heard it," I said, "and she still never said a word. Fifty years, and she had my voice in her attic and my name on nothing and my letters behind her piano, and she let the world believe those songs were hers." I heard my own voice go hard, the old hardness, Marcus used to say I could sharpen my voice on a stone. "You want to know what your mother cost me? Sit there. I'll tell you what she cost me.

"She cost me the songs. Nine of them. Nine songs, Silas, and I wrote every note of them, and her name went on the paperwork because her father sat across a desk from a nineteen-year-old boy and explained, in the voice of a man discussing the weather, that no record company in 1972 was pressing records with a name like mine on the label. And she signed. She stood in the hallway outside that office — I saw her through the glass, I have seen her through that glass every day for fifty years — and she signed.

"She cost me Marcus. My brother stopped speaking to me for six years because I wouldn't sign, because I chose pride over a paycheck, because in his arithmetic a stolen credit was still a credit and I was a fool with a guitar and a grudge. And when he finally came around, when he finally understood, it was at our mother's funeral, and we'd lost the years in between.

"She cost me the road south. I was supposed to go to the city with her in September of '72. We had it planned on a legal pad in this garage. Instead I went alone, and I came back alone, and I opened a guitar shop two hours from the woman who taught me that a song is a room two people build, and I lived above it for fifty years like a man camping in the ruins of his own life.

"And she cost me—" I stopped. The rain came down. Somewhere a gutter was overflowing, doing its work badly. "She cost me the version of myself that existed before I learned what it costs to trust somebody. That's the invoice. That's what she cost. I've been reading it for fifty years and the total doesn't change."

### Silas

The rain hammered the roof and Isandro Vey sat in a folding chair in my mother's garage and read me the bill, and I let him, because he had earned every line of it, and because some part of me had driven two hours to a shuttered shop precisely to hear it read.

But he'd had fifty years to rehearse his grief, and I'd had three weeks to rehearse mine, and when he finally stopped — when the last number of the invoice had been read out into the wet air — I found that mine was not smaller. It had just never been itemized.

"You want to know what her silence cost me," I said.

He looked at me then, really looked, and I saw him register that I wasn't going to apologize on her behalf, that I hadn't come to stand proxy at her trial.

"I got the mother who hummed," I said. "That's how I think of her now — the mother who hummed. She hummed in the kitchen, that song, every night of my childhood, and when I asked her what it was she said it was hers. *It's mine, baby. Mama made it up.* And I believed her, because you believe your mother, that's the whole deal, that's the contract you sign at birth without reading it. And every night she hummed a stolen song over the dinner dishes and let me believe it was hers, and I built a childhood on top of that belief the way you build a house on a foundation you've never inspected.

"She never played music. Did you know that? A woman who could write — who could *co-write* — nine songs, and she never touched the piano in that music room. Not once. I used to think she'd just gotten tired of it. Now I know she couldn't go near it. It was the scene of the crime. She turned the whole music room into a locked exhibit and she lived in the rest of the house like a woman living next to a fire she'd started.

"And she never told me. Not when I was a kid, not when I grew up, not when she was dying. Dale and I sat with her in the hospice bed and she held our hands and told us she loved us and she did not say one word about the tin box behind the piano, about you, about the songs, about the fact that her entire interior life had been amputated in 1972 and she'd carried the stump around for fifty years and never once let her children see it.

"You know what I got from her? I got the smile. The one that ends conversations. I've been using it my whole adult life — at work, at funerals, at family dinners with Dale. I learned it from her. It's a door she taught me to close, and I didn't even know it was a door until three weeks ago, because a door you've never seen open just looks like a wall.

"She cost me her. That's what it comes down to. I never met her. I met the woman who was left after she locked everything true about herself behind a piano, and I loved that woman, and I mourned that woman, and now I find out she was a room with a hidden panel, and the person in the photographs — laughing, in that garage, with you — I never got to meet her at all. She cost me my own mother, Isandro. And she did it to protect me, supposedly. That's the part I can't get past. She wasn't protecting me. She was protecting the version of herself I was allowed to have. She was keeping the exhibit clean."

The rain came harder. A gust hit the garage door and it shuddered in its track like something alive. Isandro sat with his hands folded on his knee, an old man's hands, spotted and knuckled, and he didn't interrupt, and he didn't defend her, and I realized that was its own kind of answer — that he had spent fifty years prosecuting Edith Vey née Thornbury and he was not going to waste my testimony on her defense.

"I'm not asking you to forgive her," I said. "I want to be clear about that. I'm not here to hand you her apology. She didn't write one. The letter — the half letter, the one I left in your mailbox — that's as close as she ever came, and she didn't even send it. I'm not going to do her apologizing for her. It would be one more thing she got credit for."

Something flickered across his face — the amputated smile again, or its ghost.

"Then what are you here for?" he said.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said in three weeks. "I think I came to hear the invoice read. And to read you mine. And to find out if two people who were robbed by the same woman can stand in the same garage without one of them catching fire."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the storm leaned on the house, and the light through the small window went from gray to the color of a bruise, and somewhere down the street a transformer thumped and half the block went dark and came back.

"I can't forgive her," he said finally. "I want to be clear about that."

"Understood."

"I've tried. Forty times I read that letter, and forty times I got to the part where she says she was afraid, and forty times I thought: I was afraid too. I was nineteen and her father had money and lawyers and the whole town behind him, and I was afraid every single day, and I didn't sign. Fear isn't an explanation, Silas. It's a condition. Everybody has it. It's what you do while you're afraid that you're answerable for."

"I know."

"And I can't forgive you, either," he went on, "which you didn't ask, but I'll say it, because you're her daughter and you've got her face and her chin and her way of sitting there like a letter waiting to be opened, and somebody should say it to your face so you don't spend fifty years wondering: *I can't look at you without seeing the house that beat me.* It's not fair. You didn't sign anything. But fairness left this story in 1972 and it hasn't been back."

"I know that too."

He nodded slowly, and looked around the garage one more time — at the workbench, at the window, at the corner where the roof had always leaked — and when he spoke again his voice had changed, dropped its armor somewhere without my seeing him take it off.

"But I'll tell you something," he said. "I sat in that chair and I read you the invoice and I meant every line. And then you sat there and read me yours, and I heard it, and here's the thing that's going to keep me up tonight: her silence cost you your mother. And her signature cost me mine. And neither one of us gets the years back, and the songs are still in the world, and the world still thinks they're hers." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet, and furious, and clear. "So the question isn't forgiveness. Forgiveness is between me and a dead woman and I'll settle it or I won't. The question is what happens Friday."

The rain eased, just slightly, the way a held breath eases.

"Friday," I said.

"The closing. Your brother signed the papers. The developer. The house goes, the music room goes, this garage—" he gestured at the walls, at the fifty-three years soaked into them, "—goes. A bulldozer writes the last word." He leaned forward, and the old man's hands gripped the old man's knees. "Unless the estate gets rewritten. Unless the house and the royalties and the name on those songs go where they should have gone in 1972. Unless somebody in this family decides that justice isn't a feeling, it's a transfer of title."

My heart was doing something unprofessional.

"You've been talking to my lawyer," I said.

"I've been talking to nobody," said Isandro Vey. "I've been talking to the rain. But you've got three days, Silas, and a signature, and I am too old and too proud and too damned tired to ask you for it. So I'm going to get in my car and drive two hours home in this storm like a fool, and you're going to sit in that chair and decide what your mother's house is worth — the money, or the truth." He stood, slowly, knees complaining, and paused at the door with the rain silvering down behind him. "And whatever you decide — I want you to know the girl in the photographs loved this garage. It was the only room in this town where her father's word wasn't law. It was the only room where she was free."

He opened his umbrella at last — the gesture of a man conceding, at the very end, that the weather was not going to negotiate — and walked to his car through the downpour, and I stood in the doorway of the garage where they had written the songs, and watched the wet dime of his car pull away, and then I went inside, past the unsigned contract on the kitchen table, and picked up the phone, and dialed the lawyer before I could talk myself out of it.

He answered on the second ring. "Ms. Thornbury."

"Mr. Adeyemi," I said. "I need to rewrite an estate. And I need it done by Friday."
