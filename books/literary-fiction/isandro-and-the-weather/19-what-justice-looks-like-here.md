## 19. What Justice Looks Like Here

The morning after the storm, the house smelled like every house smells after a storm: wet wood, cold dust, and the faint metallic tang of a place that has been reminded it is temporary. I stood in the kitchen with a cup of coffee I had no intention of drinking and thought about justice.

Not in the way you're supposed to think about it — scales, blindfolds, the solemn machinery of courts. I'd spent three weeks now inside my mother's silences, and I could tell you with authority that no courtroom on earth was built for this. What judge would hear the case? *Your Honor, the deceased stole a song fifty years ago by letting her father's fear do her signing for her, and then she hummed the evidence to herself over soup for four decades.* The gavel would come down out of sheer embarrassment.

No contract restores anything. A contract is just paper that remembers what people agreed to before they changed their minds. An apology — well. I'd watched Isandro stand in the ruined garage the night before, rain still ticking off the collapsed roof behind him, and tell me plainly what my mother cost him: the name on the songs, the years, the city he never reached because there was no money to reach it with. And I told him what her silence cost me: a childhood of humming along to a song she claimed as hers alone, a daughter's love extended to a stranger wearing my mother's face. Neither of us forgave anybody. Forgiveness wasn't on the table. It hadn't even been invited.

But standing there at my mother's counter, watching the light come up gray over the yard where a garage used to be whole, I understood something with the clean, cold clarity of a number finally balancing: the only justice available here was redistribution. Not restoration — you can't restore fifty years, can't un-erase a man from his own music. But redistribution. The house. The royalties Edith had quietly collected, decade after decade, while Isandro fixed radios or guitars or whatever a man does for forty years when his name was struck from his own work. The name itself — the credit, the truth of who wrote what, spoken aloud where it counted.

My mother had hoarded. Fear hoards; that's all fear knows how to do. And everything she hoarded was still sitting here, in this house, in these accounts, in these rights, like furniture she never let anyone sit on.

I picked up the phone and called Ruth Adeyemi, the estate lawyer, before I could talk myself out of it. Cowardice is most dangerous in the ten minutes after breakfast.

* * *

"Silas." Ruth's voice had the careful warmth of a woman who bills by the hour and has learned to dread clients who call before nine. "How are you holding up?"

"I need to rewrite the estate."

A pause long enough to contain an entire career's worth of professional caution. "The closing is Friday. Dale signed the preliminary papers yesterday — I assume you know that."

"I know that. I'm asking if we can change what happens instead."

Another pause. "Walk me through it," she said, and I heard her pull a legal pad toward her, the soft squeak of it, and something in me steadied. There is a particular comfort in the sound of someone preparing to write down your madness.

So I walked her through it. The house to Isandro Vey, or to him and his granddaughter Lila together — I'd leave the structure to her. The song rights, which I had confirmed last week sat in a catalog Edith's father had set up in 1972 and which had paid out, modestly but relentlessly, into an account my mother checked like a pulse, to Isandro. Retroactively where possible, forward where not. My share of the liquid assets split between me and Dale, minus whatever Dale needed, which was more than he'd said, which I already knew.

"You understand," Ruth said, when I finished, "that Dale can contest this. He's a beneficiary. If he objects—"

"He won't object."

"You can't know that."

"I can," I said, "because tonight I'm going to show him the tape."

"The tape."

"There's a recording," I said, "of my mother and Isandro Vey singing together in 1971. On it, you can hear the moment she decides to be afraid. You can hear her say, in her own voice, *I can't lose everything for a song.*" I looked out at the yard. "Ruth, I've been listening to it for two days. I keep waiting for it to stop being devastating. It doesn't. It just gets more specific."

She was quiet for a moment. Then, in a voice stripped of billing rates: "My father lost his family's land in Lagos to a cousin and a forged signature. He spent thirty years saying the law couldn't give it back, only decide who got to keep pretending." A breath. "Send me the numbers on the royalties. I'll draft both versions — the sale, and yours. You'll have them Thursday morning."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," she said. "Convince your brother. That's the hard part. Paperwork always agrees with whoever's holding the pen."

* * *

Dale arrived at six, which meant he'd driven straight from work, which meant he was wearing his anger the way he wore his tie — knotted tight, slightly crooked, strangling him a little and letting him pretend otherwise.

He came in carrying a folder of spreadsheets like a shield, and I made him coffee anyway, because he was my brother and because grief makes us stupid in opposite directions. His stupidity was arithmetic. Mine was apparently real estate.

"They moved the closing to eleven a.m. Friday," he said, sitting down across from me at the table where our mother's unsigned contract had once lain like a sleeping animal. "The developer's lawyer called twice today. Twice, Silas. They want this done."

"I know."

"So we sign."

"Dale." I turned my cup slowly on the table. "Before you say another word, I need you to listen to something."

His jaw did the thing it does — the tightening, the little muscle jumping near the ear, the Thornbury inheritance neither of us asked for. "If this is more of the ghost-hunting—"

"It's Mom's voice."

That stopped him. Grief is a door and every one of us has our own handle; his was her voice, because he'd been the one home evenings, the one she cooked for, the one she hummed over. I saw the handle turn behind his eyes.

I set my phone on the table between us and pressed play.

The reel-to-reel hiss first, that oceanic sound, the room of 1971 breathing. Then a guitar, young and sure of itself. Then two voices — and I watched my brother's face as he recognized hers, our mother at twenty-three, a woman neither of us had ever met, singing a song we both knew from a kitchen we both grew up in. His coffee cup froze halfway to his mouth.

"That's—" he started.

"Wait."

They sang the verse. They sang it again, braided together, and even through a phone speaker and fifty years you could hear what they were to each other, the way voices agree without negotiating. Then the guitar stumbled. Stopped. And a man's voice, low, amused, a little hurt: *"Edie. We were going to send that one."*

And then our mother. Younger than I had ever been allowed to imagine her. *"I can't lose everything for a song, Isandro. You don't understand what my father—"* A rustle, the microphone bumped, and then silence, the tape running on into its own empty future.

I stopped it there.

Dale sat very still. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone strange and flat, the voice of a man doing math he didn't want to do. "That's the song. The one she—" He stopped. Started again. "She hummed that. My whole life. She told me she made it up."

"She told everyone that. She told the radio station that, probably, if they ever asked. And meanwhile there's a man two hours away whose name isn't on any of it, and an account collecting his money every quarter for fifty years."

"What account?"

I slid Ruth's preliminary summary across the table. Royalties. Dates. Amounts. Small numbers, relentless, a heartbeat monitored for half a century. Dale read it the way he reads everything — lips moving slightly, pen appearing from nowhere, margins annotated — and I watched the arithmetic do to him what it does: convert feeling into figures he could hold without dropping.

"This is..." He put the pen down. Picked it up. "This is a lot of quarters of nothing."

"It's his life's work, Dale. It's the only proof he wrote it."

"And you want to give him the house too." Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's coat.

"I want to give him what's his. The house is the closest thing to *his* that we're holding. She wrote those songs in that garage. She hid his letters behind her piano for fifty years, Dale. Behind the piano. Like a wound she kept in the parlor."

He stood up. Walked to the window. Our mother's kitchen window, where she'd stood a thousand evenings deciding what to hide from us next. "Maya's consult is Tuesday," he said, to the glass. "The good specialist. Insurance covers maybe a third." His shoulders, under the shirt I suddenly realized was his best one, began to shake — not crying, exactly, but the tectonic version, the thing underneath trying to surface. "Do you know what it's like to sit in a waiting room doing division? Your daughter's spine divided by your bank account? I signed those papers because I couldn't—"

"I know," I said.

"You don't—"

"I know, Dale. I found the bills. In her desk, third week. You think I've been stalling the sale for fun?" I stood and crossed to him, and for a moment we were just two people at a window in a dead woman's kitchen, which is its own kind of prayer. "You don't have to be the villain and I don't have to be the saint. That's the deal. Here's the actual deal: Ruth is drafting it so your share comes out first. All of it. Enough for the consult and then some. The house and the rights go to Isandro. The money goes to Maya's spine. Nobody contests anything, because contesting means lawyers, and lawyers mean months, and months mean—" I didn't finish. He knew the end of that sentence better than I did.

He was quiet a long time. Outside, the neighborhood did its evening sounds — Mrs. Okonkwo's wind chimes, a dog, a car slowing for the stop sign nobody respected.

"You really believe he deserves it," Dale said finally. Not hostile. Testing the load-bearing wall.

"I believe he was robbed," I said. "And I believe Mom spent fifty years paying herself hush money out of his royalties, and calling it a life. And I believe we are the executors of that, whether we like it or not." I took a breath. "Justice isn't a verdict, Dale. Nobody's coming down from a mountain with a ruling. It's just — a choice somebody makes with their hands. This is ours to make."

He looked at the spreadsheet again. At the phone with the tape still on it. At the window, maybe, where her reflection would have been, if reflections kept.

"If he says no," Dale said. "If he takes it and it doesn't fix anything and he dies angry anyway — then what was it for?"

"Then it was still true," I said. "That's the only thing I've got. It was true, and we put it back where it belonged."

Dale nodded, slow, the nod of a man agreeing with a verdict he intends to appeal to himself privately for years. "Draft it," he said. "But I'm reading every line."

"You'd better," I said. "It's the most interesting thing Mom ever made us sign."

Almost — almost — he smiled.

* * *

Thursday morning, Ruth's drafts arrived, both versions side by side like twins raised apart. I read mine at the kitchen table with the tin box open beside me, the photographs of the laughing strangers fanned out like witnesses. The language was dry and beautiful the way only legal language can be — *all right, title, and interest* — and every clause landed in me like a small stone dropped into deep water.

At noon I drove the executed copy to Ruth's office, and at four I mailed a single page to a shuttered guitar shop two hours away, care of the man who lived above it. One paragraph. No apology — he'd earned the right to refuse one. Just the facts: Friday, eleven a.m., the county records office, and what would be waiting there if he chose to come. And at the bottom, because I couldn't help myself, because I am my mother's daughter after all and we keep things:

*P.S. She kept the letters behind the piano. All of them. Even the torn one. Especially, I think, the torn one.*

Then I went home to a house that no longer belonged, in any way that mattered, to me — and waited to learn whether a locked door two hours away would open.
