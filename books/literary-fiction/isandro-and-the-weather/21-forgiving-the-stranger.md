## 21. Forgiving the Stranger

The house, emptied of everything but its own echoes, had developed a new voice. I noticed it on my last morning: a hollowness in the hallway, a longer reverb in the kitchen, as if the rooms were practicing for their next owner. Houses do that. They rehearse. My mother's house — *the* house, I was trying to stop calling it hers, though forty years of habit doesn't surrender to three weeks of truth — stood stripped and swept and waiting, and it sounded like a stranger clearing her throat.

I had one box left. Not metaphorically. Dale had taken the truckload of furniture to the liquidator on Tuesday, and the estate sale had scattered the rest across half the county like ashes, and what remained fit inside a single cardboard box I'd labeled, with the flat humor that has gotten me through every funeral and breakup since 1998, **MISCELLANEOUS / SENTIMENTAL**.

Inside it: the tin box from behind the piano. The reel-to-reel tape from the attic. A hairbrush I couldn't bring myself to sell or throw away, because throwing away a dead woman's hairbrush felt like a line even I wasn't ready to cross. And Edith's good scissors, which cut fabric like a promise kept.

I sat on the floor of the empty music room — the piano gone now, sold to a church choir director who'd wept a little over it, which I chose to take as an omen in my favor — and I looked at that box for a long time.

Here is what nobody tells you about grief, or maybe they tell you and you don't listen because you're busy thinking about casseroles: it isn't a wave. Waves imply rhythm, implies relief between sets. Grief at week four is more like weather. It just sits on you. Some days it's fog, some days it's a low gray ceiling you could touch if you reached up, and once in a while — this morning, for instance — there's a break in it, a strip of blue so ordinary it almost offends you.

The strip of blue this morning was the realization that I was going to miss her. Not the woman in the letters. Not the girl laughing in the photographs with the dark-haired boy. Her. Edith Thornbury, née Aldous, who burned toast on Sundays and hummed a stolen song while she did it, who said *wear a coat* as though it were a complete philosophy of love, who signed her name to another person's music because she was afraid of her father and afraid of poverty and afraid, most of all, I think, of the size of her own wanting.

I was going to miss her, and I still didn't forgive her, and I had finally made peace with the fact that both of those things were true at once and would remain true, probably, until one of them quietly won.

* * *

I'd been carrying around a legal pad for two weeks with notes toward a letter I never intended to send. That sounds neurotic. It is neurotic. But there's a long tradition of letters to the dead — I'd read three books about mourning in the weeks since the funeral, skimming, the way I skim everything now, patience being another casualty — and all of them agreed: write it anyway. The dead are excellent readers. They never interrupt, never argue, never say *well, actually, Silas, you've misunderstood your own childhood.*

So I took out the legal pad, crossed out my grocery list (*eggs, batteries, call dentist*), and started fresh on a clean page.

*Dear Mother,* I wrote, and then immediately hated it. Too formal. She'd have laughed. *Dear Mom* was worse — saccharine, and also a lie by omission, since "Mom" was a word I'd retired sometime around adolescence in favor of nothing at all. We were a family that communicated through the passive voice. *The dishes need doing. The thermostat's been changed.* Love, translated into logistics.

I tried again.

*Mother. Edith. You.*

That was better. That was honest. For twenty years she'd been "Mother" when I was angry and "Edith" when I was talking about her to strangers and "you" only in my head, where the real conversations happened.

*I'm writing this in the music room,* I wrote. *Or what was the music room. There's a rectangle of less-faded carpet where the piano stood, like a tan line. The house looks embarrassed without it.*

Good. Wry. Keep going before the wryness curdles into avoidance, which is the family specialty.

*I found the letters. I found the tape. I know about Isandro Vey, and I know what you did, and I want you to know that I know, because I think part of why you hid those letters behind the piano was that some part of you wanted them found. Wanted *me* to find them. You couldn't say any of it out loud — God knows I come by my silence honestly — but you built a door into the wall and then spent fifty years not opening it, and you left the key where a stubborn child would eventually look.*

I stopped there. My eyes had gone hot, which annoyed me. I'd budgeted for composure today; the schedule was box, letter, lockbox of documents to the lawyer, drive away by two. Tears were not on the agenda. But grief doesn't read agendas. Grief reads the room and then ignores it.

I let myself cry for exactly as long as it took to notice I was crying, which is my mother's method, inherited intact, and then I blew my nose on a paper towel from the roll I'd left on the windowsill and kept writing.

*Here's what I understand now, and I want credit for understanding it, because it took me a month and two hundred miles of driving and one locked door and one deacon with a selective memory:*

*You were twenty-two years old and your father owned you the way men of his generation owned daughters — completely, and with paperwork. He told you signing those papers was the price of staying, and staying was the price of survival, and survival was the thing he'd raised you to value above everything, including yourself. Especially yourself. He didn't threaten you with violence. He threatened you with erasure, which is slower and works better.*

*And Isandro — Isandro offered you the opposite. He offered you a life with no paperwork at all. Two voices and a guitar and a road. It sounds like freedom. It was also poverty, and uncertainty, and a man whose pride was load-bearing, and you were twenty-two and afraid, and fear makes small choices look enormous and enormous choices look impossible.*

*I'm not saying it was fine. It wasn't fine. You let him be erased. You took the credit and the royalties and you cashed those checks for fifty years, and every quarter, when that envelope came, you must have felt it — the little stone in the shoe of your conscience — and you hummed his song anyway, in the kitchen, thinking no one heard. Except I heard. I grew up inside that song like it was wallpaper. You gave me the theft as a lullaby, Mother. That's either the most selfish thing you ever did or the most honest, and I've decided I don't have to choose.*

I looked at that paragraph for a long time. It was harsher than I'd planned and truer than I'd expected, and I decided to leave it. This was not going to be an absolution. I'd learned that much from Isandro, standing outside his door while his voice came through the wood like something carried on bad weather: *Your mother had fifty years.* He wasn't wrong. Forgiveness on a deadline isn't forgiveness; it's capitulation with better lighting.

But here was the thing I'd figured out somewhere on the drive back from Marcus Vey's, somewhere past the county line with the tape hissing in the player and my hands steady on the wheel in a way that frightened me slightly: I didn't have to absolve her to understand her. Understanding was enough. Understanding might be the whole job.

*Because here's what else I know,* I wrote. *You stayed. You stayed in this town, in this house, under his roof and then alone in it, and you raised two children on a bookkeeper's salary and a reputation you guarded like a flame in wind. You never remarried. You never played the song for anyone but me. And when the radio finally played it — his recording, his name in the credits where it always should have been — you were three weeks dead, and I have chosen to believe that's not a coincidence. I've chosen to believe you waited until it was safe. Until no one could ask you to account for it. You did the right thing the way you did everything: late, silently, and with the door locked.*

*That's not forgiveness. I want to be clear about that, in case you're reading this from wherever mothers go when they die — I suspect it's a waiting room with better chairs. This is not forgiveness. This is me setting down the weight long enough to describe it accurately. You were afraid. You were small in the ways your father made you small. And you loved me — I know this now, I finally believe it — in the only language you were ever taught, which was provision without explanation. Coats worn. Toast burned. A song hummed over the stove like a confession neither of us knew how to translate.*

*You loved me badly and completely, and I will spend the rest of my life deciding daily what to do with that sentence, and I'm okay with that. Dale isn't, yet. Dale thinks love should arrive itemized. Give him time. He cries at dog food commercials; he'll come around.*

I laughed a little at that, wetly, alone in an empty room, which is the traditional venue.

*One more thing. The house goes to Isandro on Friday. Dale agreed — it took the tape and a bottle of the cheap scotch you hid behind the flour canister, because of course you hid scotch behind the flour, of course you did — and I watched him sign the amendment with the face of a man donating a kidney to someone who once keyed his car. He'll be glad someday. So will I. Probably. The royalties go to him too, and Lila — his granddaughter, the one who shows up whether he asks or not, which is apparently genetic on both sides of this whole mess.*

*I'm keeping almost nothing. The scissors. The tape. The song, which was never yours to keep and never mine either, and which I intend to learn properly, out loud, where people can hear me do it badly. You'd hate that. You'd say, Silas, people will *look* at you. Yes, Mother. That's rather the point.*

I ran out of steam there, or maybe out of courage — same engine, different fuel — and sat looking at the page, at my handwriting getting looser as it went, the way handwriting does when the person holding the pen stops performing.

Then I wrote the ending. Or what I thought was the ending.

*I don't forgive you. I understand you. I'm told these are different things, and I believe it the way I believe in tectonic plates — abstractly, and with total conviction. Maybe forgiveness comes later. Maybe it comes never. Maybe it turns out that understanding is what forgiveness looks like when both parties are too stubborn to say so, and if that's true, then we forgave each other years ago, over dishes, in the passive voice, and neither of us ever knew.*

*Wear a coat wherever you are. It's probably cold.*

*— Silas*

* * *

I copied it onto clean paper — the legal pad version had a grocery list bleeding through from the previous page, *eggs, batteries, call dentist*, and somehow that felt wrong for a document intended to outlast me — and then I sat with the finished letter on my knee and faced the practical question: what does one *do* with a letter to the dead?

Mail it? To where? The lawyer's office, addressed to *Estate of Edith Thornbury, c/o The Great Silence*? Burn it, ceremonially, in the sink, like a teenager ending a friendship? Keep it, in the MISCELLANEOUS/SENTIMENTAL box, where it would become somebody else's archaeological find in another fifty years — my niece, probably, or Lila, digging through my affairs the way I'd dug through my mother's, finding my neat cursive and my unsent grievances and drawing her own conclusions about the women in this family and our complicated relationship with envelopes?

No. None of those.

I looked around the empty music room instead. The rectangle of unfaded carpet. The window with its view of the ruined garage, which Isandro had stood in during the storm and where he'd told me, plainly, what my mother cost him, and where I'd told him what her silence cost me, and where neither of us had forgiven anything, and where — this was the strange part — we had both, walking back through the rain to our separate cars, seemed lighter. Truth without absolution. It's a real product. I recommend it, with reservations.

The room was going to belong to strangers soon. Isandro, or Lila, or whoever they became in this house — a music room again, I hoped, a room with a piano in it and a rectangle of sun on the floor. Whoever came next deserved to know what had happened here. Not the whole story — the whole story was mine and Isandro's and Marcus's and Dale's, contested territory, memory with four claimants — but a piece of it. A true piece. Left the way my mother had left hers: hidden in plain sight, behind nothing, waiting for a stubborn person to look.

There was no loose panel in this room. No piano to hide things behind anymore. So I did the opposite of what she'd done.

I walked to the center of the room, set the letter down on the bare floorboards, and weighed down its corners with four things from the MISCELLANEOUS/SENTIMENTAL box: a guitar pick I'd found taped inside the reel-to-reel case, Isandro's, worn smooth as a river stone; one of Edith's hairpins; the torn corner of the half letter, matched and mended now, kept as a bookmark keeps a place; and a single sheet of paper on which I'd written, in block capitals, so there could be no mistaking it:

**THIS ROOM HELD TWO VOICES ONCE. THE SONG WAS WRITTEN BY ISANDRO VEY AND SUNG BY EDITH ALDOUS. SHE TOOK THE CREDIT. HE KEPT THE MUSIC. EVERYONE SURVIVED. NOBODY WAS FINE. IF YOU FOUND THIS, SING SOMETHING.**

It was too much. It was melodramatic. My mother would have hated it, and Isandro would pretend to hate it, and Lila would photograph it and post it somewhere, and all of that felt exactly right. Grief, I was learning, is allowed to be ordinary — the dishes, the boxes, the drive home — but memory needs a little theater. Memory is the one production in this family that has always run long.

I left the letter on the floor, weighted, facing up, and I closed the door of the music room behind me, and I did not look back, because I am my mother's daughter and sentimentality is best practiced in moderation.

* * *

The last box went into the passenger seat of my car, seatbelted in, because I have boundaries about cargo safety and none whatsoever about dignity. I walked the house one final time with the keys in my hand. Kitchen, where the contract had sat unsigned for a week while I learned to lie to a realtor with a smile that, unlike my professional one, actually worked. Hallway, where the echo had already moved on to its next voice. Living room, where the piano's ghost held its rectangle of light. Front door, which I locked out of habit and then unlocked, because it wasn't mine to lock anymore.

On the porch, my phone buzzed. I checked it with the particular dread of a woman expecting another email from the developer's attorney.

It wasn't the attorney. It was a number I didn't know, and a text:

*This is Lila Vey. Granddad says you sing. He says it badly, but that you sing anyway. He's doing a small thing at the community hall Saturday — first time out in thirty years. He won't ask you himself, obviously, because he's ninety percent pride and ten percent guitar strings. Come. He's playing YOUR song. Well. His song. You know what I mean.*

I stood on the porch of the house I no longer owned, in the cold, without a coat, and I read it twice, and then a third time, and the weather above the trees did something complicated and bright.

*Obviously,* I typed back.
