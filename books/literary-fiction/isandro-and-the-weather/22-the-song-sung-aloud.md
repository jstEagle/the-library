## 22. The Song, Sung Aloud

The invitation came on a postcard, which felt right. Lila Vey had a sense of occasion that ran toward the analog — she'd been the one to digitize the reel-to-reel tape, but only after insisting we listen to it once the old way, the needle hissing like rain on a tin roof, Edith's voice rising out of 1971 as if she'd never left the room.

*You're invited,* the postcard said, in handwriting that slanted forward like it was in a hurry to get somewhere. *Isandro is playing. The Unitarian hall on Delmore Street. Saturday, seven o'clock. He says come. He won't say why, which means he wants you there and is constitutionally incapable of asking.*

I read it three times at my kitchen table — my kitchen table now, in an apartment with walls thin enough to carry my neighbor's arguments about fantasy football — and then I put it in the drawer where I kept things I wasn't ready to deal with: the funeral guest book, Dale's spare house key, a birthday card from Edith I'd found unopened in her desk, dated four years ago, because apparently even her love had needed a buffer of silence.

Then I took it back out.

*He says come.*

Three weeks earlier, I would have told you Isandro Vey was incapable of saying *come* to anyone. He was a man who communicated through doors — through them closed, mostly, and once, memorably, through one opened just wide enough for a hand to pass out a half-torn letter and take nothing else. The night of the storm, standing in the ruined garage where he and my mother had written songs before either of them could legally drink, he had spoken plainly, and plainness had cost him visibly, like a man paying cash for something he'd always bought on credit. But he hadn't asked me for anything, and he hadn't offered anything either. What he'd done was tell the truth, which turned out to be its own category of transaction.

Now this postcard. Now *he says come.*

Dale called on Thursday to ask if I was going.

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because I already knew.

"He's ninety-one, Silas." Dale's voice did the thing it did when he was converting feeling into logistics — flattening it, weighing it, filing it under *manageable*. "Ninety-one-year-olds don't do encores."

"Maybe he knows something we don't."

"Everybody knows something we don't. That's the family motto." A pause. I heard him breathing, and underneath the breathing, faintly, the sound of Priya's daughter — my niece, Amara — watching something loud and bright in another room. The surgery consult had gone well, or well enough; the word *manageable* had made its way into the vocabulary, and Dale wore it like a man wearing a coat borrowed from a bigger man. "I'll be there Saturday," he said. "Front row. Somebody has to represent the Thornbury side of the ledger."

"You're coming to a concert?"

"I'm coming to watch you cry," he said, "in a controlled environment. It's what brothers are for."

And that, more than the postcard, decided me.

* * *

The Unitarian hall on Delmore Street was the kind of room that had hosted fifty years of bake sales and memorial services and one memorable production of *Our Town* in which the Stage Manager had been played by a woman who refused to memorize lines and simply held the script at chest height all night. Folding chairs. A piano nobody trusted. Acoustics that forgave everything.

Lila had set up maybe sixty chairs. By ten past seven, people were standing along the walls.

I recognized some of them. Mrs. Okonkwo, from next door to Edith's house, wearing a hat that suggested church had been relocated. The DJ from the radio station, off duty, in a jacket that said he'd tried. Two women I didn't know who turned out, later, to have driven up from the postmark town, where Isandro's guitar shop had been a landmark the way lighthouses are landmarks — useless, beloved, assumed permanent until someone tells you otherwise. And scattered through the crowd, faces I couldn't place but that shared a certain look: the look of people who had heard a song on the radio with no name attached and had come anyway, drawn by the specific gravity of a melody that seemed to remember them.

Lila found me before the lights went down. She was twenty-six and carried herself like someone who had recently won an argument with a much older opponent and was still surprised by the victory.

"He's nervous," she said. "He won't admit it. He's been tuning that guitar for forty minutes and it was in tune twenty minutes ago."

"That tracks."

"He asked if you came." She looked at me with her grandfather's dark eyes and none of his defenses. "I said yes. He said 'hm,' which in Vey means 'thank God.'"

"How is he?"

"Smaller," she said, without flinching. "Since Friday. Like he put down something heavy and hasn't figured out how to walk straight yet." She touched my arm, briefly, the way you touch a doorframe going around a corner. "Thank you. For the house. For — all of it. He'll never say it right, so I'm saying it wrong, but thank you."

"It wasn't charity," I said. "It was arithmetic. Fifty years of royalties Edith collected on songs she didn't write. The house was built on the same math. I just balanced the books."

Lila smiled. "You sound like him."

"I've been told I sound like her. It evens out."

The lights went down.

* * *

He walked out slowly, and the room did the kind thing rooms do for the very old, which is to hold its breath so the footsteps can be heard. He was smaller than he'd been in the garage during the storm — or maybe the garage had been generous, full of shadows and memory, giving everyone their best dimensions. Here under the worklights he was a narrow old man in a clean shirt, holding a guitar with a worn spot on its lower bout shaped exactly like the place a forearm rests across fifty years of playing.

He sat. He adjusted the microphone down, down, further down, until Lila came out and fixed it, and the small domestic comedy of it broke the room open into laughter, and he let the laughter happen, and I understood that this too was craft.

"My name," he said, "is Isandro Vey. Some of you know that name from a radio station that plays songs nobody asked for." Laughter again, warmer. "Fifty years ago I wrote a song with a girl named Edith Thornbury, in a garage on Corliss Avenue that smelled like motor oil and cut grass. She wrote half of it. Maybe more than half. For fifty years, the world knew it by other names, other hands, and she let it stand that way, and I let myself believe I didn't care, and both of us were lying, and the song waited. Songs are patient. They can afford to be. It's people who run out of time."

He looked out at us, and for a moment his eyes found mine — third row, aisle seat, exactly where a man who says *come* without saying it would know to look — and something passed between us that was not forgiveness, because we had agreed, in the garage, in the rain-wind, that forgiveness was not ours to hand each other. It was simpler than that. It was recognition. Two people who had loved the same impossible woman, from opposite ends of her silence.

"This song has no name," he said. "We never gave it one. We were going to name it together, when we got to the city, when we had a record, when we had a life. We ran out of *when*. So tonight I'm doing the naming alone, which is the last unfair thing I'll do to her." He settled his hand on the strings. "It's called 'Edith's Weather.' Because she used to say you can't argue with weather, you can only dress for it. She argued with everything else."

He played.

I had heard the song a hundred times by then — on the radio, stopped at a red light with my whole childhood tilting sideways; on the B-side, angry and accusatory; on Edith's reel-to-reel, in two young voices braided together before the braid broke. I thought I knew it the way you know a street you grew up on.

I didn't know it. Hearing it live, finished, named, given back its history — it was like meeting someone you'd only ever seen in photographs, and discovering they had a laugh.

His voice was wrecked in the way old voices are wrecked, gravel over wire, but he knew every place to spend it and every place to save it, and when he reached the bridge — the part Edith wrote, the part that climbed, that insisted, that sounded like a window opening in a shut house — the room did something I will remember as long as I remember anything. People started humming. Not singing, not yet. Humming, low, tentative, dozens of strangers finding the line the way you find a handrail in the dark, and the melody rose up out of all of them at once and became, briefly, actual weather in the room.

I sang along. Badly. I want that on the record, since so much of this story is about records: I sang flat, I came in late, I got the words wrong in the second verse because I'd only ever heard them mumbled through a kitchen wall. None of it mattered. That's the thing about a song finally sung aloud — it stops belonging to whoever owns it and starts belonging to whoever needs it, and what I needed was to make noise, badly, in public, for a woman who had spent seventy years making sure her own voice was never heard above a hum.

Beside me, Dale cried the way he did everything: quietly, efficiently, with dignity, checking his phone once to make sure crying hadn't caused any emergencies.

When it ended, the applause went on long enough that Lila had to come out and gesture everyone down, and Isandro sat through it with his hand flat on the guitar's body, the way you'd quiet a dog.

"One more thing," he said, when the room allowed it. "A dedication. I've been told dedications should be brief, and I've been ignoring that advice for fifty years, so." He cleared his throat. "To Edith. Who was braver than she believed and less brave than she needed to be, which makes her not a villain and not a saint, just a person, which is harder to forgive than either." His voice caught, and he let it catch. "And to the child she raised better than she believed she could. Silas. You're here. Stand up, or don't — you never do what you're told, I've heard — but know that the second verse was hers alone, and tonight everybody in this room heard it, and they'll carry it home, and that's more immortality than most of us get."

Sixty strangers turned to look at me.

I stood up. What else do you do? I stood up in a folding chair in a Unitarian hall with my brother's wet shoulder against my knee, and I raised whatever was in my hand — it turned out to be a program folded into a fan, because grief and humidity — and I said, loudly enough for the back row:

"She also wrote the key change. Nobody ever talks about the key change."

And the room laughed, and Isandro laughed, a real one, from somewhere below the ribs, and Mrs. Okonkwo's hat nodded like a small ship agreeing, and that was the moment I understood what inheritance actually was. Not the house. Not the royalties, though those mattered, though they'd paid for Amara's consult and Lila's reopening of the shop next spring. Inheritance was this: a melody passed hand to hand until it belonged to everyone who'd ever stood at a red light and felt their childhood tilt. My mother had stolen a credit and hoarded a silence, and the only justice available had turned out to be redistribution — and the thing being redistributed, in the end, wasn't money at all.

It was voice.

* * *

Afterward, in the hallway that smelled like coffee and folding-chair metal, the line of well-wishers moved past Isandro the way water moves past a stone. I waited until the end, because some habits you inherit honestly.

He looked tired in the good way, the way you look after carrying something heavy to exactly the right place and setting it down.

"The key change," he said. "She did write the key change. I've been telling people that for fifty years and nobody believed me."

"Now they have it from the source."

"Mm." He studied me with those dark eyes, and I braced for the *hm*, the Vey seal of approval, but instead he said: "Your mother hummed that song while she cooked. Did you know that? Lila told me. All those years, in that house, with my name struck from the paper — she hummed it anyway. I've been deciding what that meant for three weeks. I still don't know. Maybe guilt. Maybe theft. Maybe the closest thing to apology she could manage without saying the words." He shook his head slowly. "People are weather, Silas. You can't argue with them. You can only decide what to wear."

"And what are you wearing?"

He almost smiled. "Today? Linen. Light colors. Today I'm dressed for forgiveness, whether or not it shows up."

"It showed up Friday," I said. "At the closing table. You just weren't looking."

"I was looking." He said it quietly. "I was looking the whole time. That's different from finding."

Lila appeared at his elbow with his coat, and the motion of the evening resumed around us — chairs folding, the DJ saying something about airtime, Mrs. Okonkwo kissing my cheek and telling me my mother had been a complicated woman, *which is a compliment where I come from.* Dale shook Isandro's hand again, awkwardly, the handshake of two men who had entered each other's lives through a lawsuit-shaped door and were doing their best.

Outside, the night was cool and enormous. Lila walked me to my car.

"Spring," she said. "We're reopening the shop. Guitars, lessons, and a wall in the back with the whole story — the letters, the photographs, the tape. He wants your letter to Edith there. The one you left in the music room. He read it. Forty times, probably. He says it belongs with the rest of the evidence."

"It's private."

"It was," she agreed. "So was the song."

I laughed, and she grinned, and I saw it then — the resemblance running backward through time, the same stubborn light in a younger face, the family trait of saying the true thing at the worst possible moment and being right.

"Send me the date," I said.

"There's something else." She hesitated, and I watched her gather herself the way her grandfather gathered himself before plain speech. "He's teaching me 'Edith's Weather.' The whole thing, both parts. He wants me to learn her verses — your mother's verses — note for note. And he's too proud to ask, so I'm asking: would you come? Sometimes? To the shop? We could use someone who knows how she would have wanted the phrasing. You lived inside her humming for twenty years. That's training."

I looked at my car. I looked at the hall behind her, where the folding chairs were disappearing into their cart, where a man of ninety-one was being helped into a coat, where a song with no name had finally been given one and given away.

"Yeah," I said. "Yeah, I can do that."

I drove home with the windows down, past the red light on Corliss where it had all started, and I hummed the whole way — badly, flat, late on the entrances — smiling a smile that, for the first time in my life, started conversations.
