## 2. The Tenant's Key

The drive from the Harrow Bridge to Glasshouse took two hours and forty minutes, and I measured all of it — not in miles, which are vague and sentimental, but in things. Fourteen roadkill sightings, mostly hedgehogs. One petrol station with a sign advertising ICE COLD DRINKS where the D had fallen off, so that it promised onlyrink, which felt honest in a way I appreciated. Three hundred and eleven turns after the A-road, the hedgerows opened and there it was: the estate, the way a memory is there — familiar and slightly smaller than you'd built it.

The glasshouse came first, as it always had. You saw it from the lane before you saw the house: a long spine of iron and glass on the south slope, its panes catching the late afternoon sun so that the whole structure seemed to be quietly on fire. My grandfather Edmund used to say it was the finest thing the family ever built, and that the family had merely been installed around it afterward for convenience. I'd believed him when I was nine. I believed him now, frankly.

The house behind it was Georgian, grand in the way of an aging actor who still remembers his good reviews. Paint peeling at the cornices in long curls, like the house was shedding. Two chimneys. A door that had been green once, or possibly had ambitions of green.

I sat in the car for four minutes and twelve seconds. I know because I timed it, which is what I do instead of feeling things at full volume. Then I got out.

They were already there. Of course they were. I could see the evidence before I reached the door: Desmond's Range Rover parked at an angle that suggested he considered the gravel a personal fiefdom, Imogen's little silver hatchback wedged beside it like a remora, and my mother's Volvo sitting perfectly straight, perfectly aligned, as though it had been parked by a woman who believed the universe was watching and grading.

Ruth opened the door before I knocked. She was smaller than I remembered, or perhaps I had simply grown into the size of my own opinions. She wore the beige cardigan she wore to funerals, weddings, and dentist appointments — the cardigan of occasions.

"Casper," she said, and kissed the air beside my cheek, which is how my family hugs: adjacent to the body, never making contact.

"Mum."

"You look tired."

"I drove for three hours."

"Well." She stepped back to let me in. "You always did make things difficult."

This, from Ruth, was affection. I've spent thirty-four years learning to translate, and I still need the phrasebook.

* * *

The hall smelled the way it always had — beeswax and cold stone and something faintly botanical, the ghost of my grandfather's orchids, which had died decades ago but apparently left a scent. The portrait of Edmund still hung above the stairs, painted in his fifties, when he still had the look of a man who owned things rather than a man things owned. His eyes followed you. They'd followed me at nine; they followed me now, with what I chose to interpret as mild disappointment.

Desmond found me in the drawing room, where I was reading the spines of books no one had opened since the millennium. He came in the way he does everything — like a man entering a room that ought to be grateful.

"Casper." He shook my hand. My uncle shakes my hand. We are blood relatives and he shakes my hand, every time, with the firmness of a man closing a deal. "Good of you to come. I know it's a long way from your — " a small pause, the size of a whole marriage — "bridge."

"It's my aunt's funeral, Desmond. Distance wasn't really a factor."

"No. No, of course." He poured himself a whisky from a decanter that had been there since before I was born. "Terrible business. Vivian. Wonderful woman. Very private."

He said *private* the way other people say *difficult*, and I made a note of it, the way I make notes of hairline cracks — not because they mean anything yet, but because they're there.

Imogen arrived in a cloud of perfume and apology. "Casper! Oh my God, look at you." She hugged me properly, full contact, which marked her immediately as either sincere or after something. With Imogen it was usually both. "You've been away too long. It's been — what, six years? Seven?"

"Six," I said. "Since Gran's funeral."

"Was that six? God." She settled onto the sofa and immediately claimed the cushion nearest the fire. "Well. It's awful, obviously. Aunt Vivian. But it's *lovely* to have an excuse for everyone to be in one place, isn't it? Like Christmas, but sad."

"That's just Christmas," I said, and she laughed, and Desmond did not, and somewhere in the hall my mother's footsteps paused for exactly one beat before continuing upstairs.

* * *

Dinner was lamb, overcooked with the confidence of a family that had never once been asked its opinion. We ate in the dining room under the chandelier that Desmond had been threatening to sell for fifteen years and had never managed to part with, because selling it would mean admitting the estate needed money, and the Fairweathers did not need money; they simply had less of it than they pretended.

"So," Desmond said, carving with the solemnity of a surgeon. "The will."

Ruth's fork paused. Imogen leaned in approximately four degrees.

"Solicitor comes Friday," Desmond continued. "But he's given me the outline, as executor. The house and grounds go into trust — split between the three of us siblings, eventually the next generation, the usual entanglement. But the glasshouse—" he paused, and I felt the room tilt slightly toward me, the way rooms do when they know something you don't — "the glasshouse and the annex go to Casper."

The silence had a texture. I could have measured it.

"Me," I said.

"You," said Desmond. "Surprised us all. Vivian always was — " and there it was again, that word, hovering — "*private.*"

"She barely knew me," I said. "I saw her twice in my adult life. She sent a card when I passed my inspection certification. It said, in full, 'So you measure bridges now. Someone should.' I assumed it was an insult."

"It was," said my mother quietly, into her wine.

"Perhaps she saw something in you," Imogen offered, with the bright diplomacy of a woman who wanted the will read in her favor eventually. "The family head for numbers, and so on."

"Or perhaps," said Desmond, "she wanted to make things complicated, which was also in character." He refilled his glass. "Naturally, we'll want to discuss arrangements. The upkeep alone on that structure — it's a ruin with pretensions, frankly. There may be a sensible conversation to have about it. Later. After Friday."

I heard *sensible* and understood it to mean *mine*. I said nothing. In bridge inspection we call this visual monitoring: you don't intervene at the first sign of distress. You watch. You record. You wait to see whether the crack propagates.

* * *

After dinner, my mother found me on the back terrace, looking out at the glasshouse in the dark. Without the sun inside it, it was just a skeleton — iron ribs, black panes, the moon making small silver repairs across the roof.

"She left you something else," Ruth said.

I turned. She was holding out a key. Old, iron, heavy for its size, the kind of key that predates the concept of security and belongs instead to the concept of *permission*.

"The annex," she said. "Your aunt's rooms. The solicitor said — well. He said you're to have access. Effective immediately." She pressed it into my palm and closed my fingers over it, and for one strange moment her hands stayed there, warm, and I thought she might say something true.

She said: "It'll be dusty. Vivian wasn't one for — " she gestured at the general concept of openness — "letting people in."

"Right," I said. "Private."

"Where did you hear that?"

"Desmond. Twice."

My mother looked out at the glasshouse for a while. In the moonlight her face did something complicated, a whole paragraph of expression compressed into a single line, the way it always had. When I was a child I'd thought she was simply quiet. Now I recognized the look of a woman editing.

"She was my sister," Ruth said at last. "And I loved her. And I didn't know her. Both of those are true, and you can put that in your notebook, since I know you keep one."

"I don't keep a—"

"Casper. I gave birth to you."

* * *

The annex was a low stone building tucked against the glasshouse's eastern flank, connected to it by a glazed corridor that had once been a marvel and was now a museum of fallen leaves. The key turned with a sound like a small bone cracking, and the door swung inward onto darkness and the smell of paper, of lavender gone to dust, of a room that had been closed not for weeks but for years.

I found the light switch by feel. A bulb somewhere overhead considered the request and declined. I used my phone's torch instead, and the beam moved across the room like a slow inspection: a writing desk, a chair, shelves of books on botany and — I tilted my head — one on structural engineering, of all things, its spine cracked with use. A bed, neatly made, though no one had slept in it. And against the far wall, enormous and immovable, a wardrobe of dark mahogany, its doors shut, its presence in the room less like furniture than like a witness.

The window at the end of the annex looked into the glasshouse itself. Even at night, even empty, the panes held some residual light, some stored afternoon, and I stood there a moment with the key still in my fist, thinking: *she chose to live pressed against this thing her whole life. Forty years in a stone box attached to a house of glass. What does a person keep, that they need to live this close to it and still behind a locked door?*

The wardrobe was the largest object in the room and therefore, by the logic of my profession, the most likely to be hiding something. I don't want to claim I suspected anything. I want to claim I was tired, and curious, and that the key in my pocket had made me feel — for the first time in a very long time — like a person with a right to open things.

I crossed the room. The wardrobe doors opened without protest.

Coats. Mothballs. A hat that had outlived several governments. And at the back, where the wood should have met the wall, a seam of darkness that ran too deep, and too clean, and too deliberate.

I put my hand into it.

The wall behind the wardrobe was not a wall.
