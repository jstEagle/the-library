## 4. Tuning Fork

The obituary ran on a Tuesday, four lines long, under a name that was not his name. *Ivan Petrosyan, 91, resident of Seaview Care Home, survived by no one.* The town read it the way it read all its dead — quickly, and without looking up.

By Friday, everyone knew anyway. Small towns are tuned instruments; you strike one string and every other string in the county hums in sympathy. The letters had gone up on a website Ulyana had found at two in the morning, a modest archive run by a retired archivist named Dolores who listened to the whole story over the phone and then said, quietly, *Send me scans. All of them. Tonight.* By Sunday there were photographs of the letters circulating like weather, and by Monday the woman who sold bread at the market had stopped meeting Ulyana's eyes.

Her mother did not speak to her for eleven days. When she finally called, her voice was flat as a struck string that refuses to stop ringing.

"You buried him twice," she said. "You know that? Grandad died thirty years ago, and you dug him up and put him in the paper."

"He was never buried," Ulyana said. "He was hidden. There's a difference, Mum. A grave is honest."

Her mother made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief, and hung up. They would come back to each other — Ulyana believed this the way she believed in middle C, something you could find blind, in the dark, by feel alone — but not yet. Some tunings take longer than others.

* * *

The doors that closed were mostly old doors: the Harrowgate sisters, who had been children in 1974 and still needed Ivo Weather to be guilty because they had spent seventy years being afraid of him; the man at the hardware store who served her in silence and rounded her change down. She learned to carry cash so she wouldn't have to watch it happen.

But other doors opened, and these surprised her more. The widow Okafor, whose husband had driven the fire truck that night and never once spoken of it, asked Ulyana in for tea and told her, hands wrapped around a cooling cup, that her husband had seen two figures leaving the warehouse and had been ordered to forget it. The schoolteacher brought her class to the archive website and asked if Ulyana would come talk about what it meant to keep records. And an old man named Ferro, ninety-four, who had been Ivo's foreman, stood on her doorstep in the rain and said only: "I owed him an apology for fifty years. Thank you for making room for it."

Justice, Ulyana discovered, was not a verdict. It was a door swinging open onto a room where people could finally set down the things they'd carried. It cost her the comfort of being liked, and it gave her back something she hadn't known she'd pawned: the right to say her grandmother's name without flinching. *Rosa Quintana kept forty-one letters and never sent one.* It was true. It was hers now. Truth, it turned out, was the only inheritance that didn't need dusting.

* * *

She came to the house on a bright, cold morning in late October, when the light came in low and gold and laid itself across the floorboards like sheet music. The house was empty now — sold, cleared, the wardrobes standing open with their mouths agape, the walls showing paler rectangles where the photographs had hung for sixty years. Her footsteps echoed in a way they never had when the house was full.

The upright piano sat in the front room under the window, exactly where it had always been, wearing a fine grey coat of dust. It was the last thing to leave. She had told the movers she would deal with it herself, and they had looked at each other and let her.

Ulyana opened the windows first. Both of them, all the way, letting in the cold salt air off the water, the cries of gulls, the distant clank of the fishing boats, the ordinary noise of a town going about its morning. Let them hear, she thought. This house had kept quiet long enough.

Then she got out her tools and began.

The piano was badly out — months of neglect, seasons of damp, grief settling into the pinblock like damp into plaster. She worked through it the way she always did, felt first, ear second: the temperament strip off, the mute wedges in, the tuning hammer finding each pin with the familiarity of a handshake. A4 to the fork. Then the octaves, then the fifths, listening for that slow rolling beat between strings that meant *not yet*, *almost*, *there*. The notes came into agreement one by one, the way witnesses do, until the whole instrument spoke with a single voice.

She talked while she worked. Out loud, to nobody, to everybody.

"Grandma Rosa. You were the bravest coward I ever knew." The F above middle C, a hair flat. "You loved him enough to write forty-one letters and too afraid to send a single one. I used to think that made your love smaller. It doesn't. It just made it heavier. You carried it your whole life and never once set it down, and I don't know if that's devotion or damage, and maybe it's both, and maybe it doesn't matter anymore."

The pins turned. The strings stretched and settled.

"Ivo. You asked me to read them instead of handing them over, and I didn't understand why until I was halfway through the third letter and saw your face. Because a letter handed over is an object. A letter read aloud is a voice. You wanted her voice. You waited fifty years for her voice, and you got mine instead, and you were kind enough to pretend that was close enough." Her eyes blurred; she wiped them on her sleeve and kept turning. "It wasn't close enough. Nothing was. But you fell asleep during the twenty-ninth letter with your hand open on the blanket like you were reaching for someone, and when I finished the last one you woke up and said, *Tell her the piano sounds better now,* and I have decided to believe you heard every word."

The last note settled into place. The piano was true, top to bottom, all eighty-eight strings agreeing.

Ulyana sat down on the bench — her grandmother's bench, worn smooth in the shape of Rosa Quintana's particular body — and played.

She played loud. She played with the windows wide open, the October air moving through the room, the curtains lifting and falling like breathing. She played the Chopin prelude her grandmother used to play on Sunday mornings, the sad one, the one that rises and rises and then forgives everything at the end. She played it wrong in places and didn't care. Down the street, she knew, people would hear it and know whose house it came from and whose granddaughter was sitting at whose piano, and some of them would close their windows, and some of them would stand in their kitchens and listen with their hands over their mouths.

Both were fine. Both were the sound of a town retuning itself.

When the prelude ended she let the last chord ring until it faded into the gull-cries and the boat-clanks, and then she said, clearly, to the empty room, to the pale rectangles on the walls, to the wardrobe with its mouth open, to the sea:

"Goodbye, Grandma. Goodbye, Ivo. Your names are out loud now. Go home."

She closed the fallboard gently, the way you close a book you've finished but will read again. Outside, a car pulled up — she could see it through the window, a small grey hatchback, and behind the wheel a young woman she didn't recognize, holding a shoebox of her own against her chest, looking at the house with the expression of someone deciding whether to knock.

Ulyana opened the front door before she could decide.
