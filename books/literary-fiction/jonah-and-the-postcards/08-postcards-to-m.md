## 8. Postcards to M.

I laid them out on the floor of Bess's front room in the order of their postmarks, or what passed for postmarks, which was mostly nothing at all, because none of them had ever been posted. Forty-one cards fanned across the carpet like a hand of cards dealt by a dealer who had died mid-shuffle. The radiator ticked. The sea did its usual impression of indifference through the window. And I knelt there on my knees like a penitent with a magnifying glass, because that is what a critic does when the meal is finally served: we lean in.

I should say something about method, since method is the only religion I've ever kept. In my trade we talk about "reading" a dish — the sauce tells you whether the cook was angry, the cut of the vegetable tells you whether they were hurried, the plate itself tells you what they think of you. I have spent thirty years reading other people's kitchens for strangers with expense accounts. It turns out this skill transfers with humiliating ease to other people's unsent mail.

The first card was dated August 1958. A picture of Porthaven harbour, the boats fat and brown-sailed, the sky doing that bruised lavender thing Cornwall does when it can't decide between beauty and weather. The handwriting was large, slanted, confident — ink pressed hard enough to emboss the reverse.

*M — They've let me do the fish on Fridays now. Father says my sauce is "adequate," which from him is a parade. Save me some of your patience. I'll need it. — T.*

T. Theo, presumably, though he'd have been a boy then, and the handwriting was nothing like the careful schoolmaster's script Uncle Theo used for his grocery lists. People's hands change, though. Hands are the most honest liars we have.

The second card was also 1958, also T., and already the tone had shifted, the way weather shifts over water.

*M — He took my knife away again. Says I hold it like a girl. You held it worse and everyone ate anyway.*

I sat back on my heels. There it was, three sentences in: the whole architecture of that family, load-bearing walls and all. A father with knives and opinions. A sister somewhere else — where? — being compared against. And an *everyone* who ate, which meant a table, which meant, eventually, the Merrow's Table, which meant everything that came after.

I made tea I didn't drink and kept going.

* * *

By the third hour I had developed a system, because grief without a spreadsheet is just lying on the floor. I wrote each card's date, sender initial, and one-line summary on index cards borrowed from Bess's recipe box, which felt either sacrilegious or appropriate; I hadn't decided which, so I did it anyway. The senders sorted themselves out faster than I expected. There were four hands, maybe five:

**T.** — forty-one cards' worth of the conversation's engine. Theo, almost certainly, writing from Porthaven to wherever M. was, which seemed to be London by the sixties, then somewhere coastal again by the seventies.

**B.** — only nine cards, but each one a small detonation. Bess. My great-aunt, whose funeral I had attended eleven days ago, whose house I was currently squatting in like a ghost with a laptop. Her handwriting was upright, economical, furious in its restraint.

**V.** — two cards. Both short. Both devastating, though I didn't know how devastating until later, and I'm getting ahead of myself.

**R.** — one card, unsigned except for the initial, and I have no idea who R. was. Neither, I suspect, does anyone else still living. That thought kept me up that night more than anything else in the box.

And **M. herself** — no. Wait. Here is the thing that stopped me cold around card nineteen, sitting cross-legged on a dead woman's carpet with tea going cold beside me: M. never wrote back. Or rather — M.'s replies were not here. Every card was outbound. This was not a correspondence. This was half of one, the half that stayed, the half that talked to someone who may or may not have been listening.

You want to know what intimacy looks like? Not love letters. Love letters are performances; I've written enough reviews to know a performance when I see one. Intimacy is a man writing to his sister every few months for seventeen years knowing she might never answer, saying things like:

*M — Vera won't speak to me since Easter. If you ask her why she'll say it's about money. It isn't about money. Tell me you understand that much. — T.*

and

*M — I named the soup after you. On the menu it says "Marisol's Broth." Father doesn't know yet. Let him find out. — T.*

Marisol. So M. had a name at last, and it sat in my mouth strangely, foreign and familiar at once, like a spice I'd tasted once as a child and couldn't place. Marisol's Broth. I ran the name against every family story I'd ever been fed at Christmas tables and christenings and funerals, all those well-polished anecdotes the Sandovals keep in rotation like a set menu, and found — nothing. No Marisol. Not a godmother, not a friend of the family, not a mention. A woman who had a soup named after her in a famous restaurant had been erased so thoroughly that even I, professional rememberer of flavours, had no trace of her.

That's when the dread arrived properly. Not the gothic shiver you get from finding hidden letters — any fool enjoys that, it's the pleasure plot of a hundred novels. The real dread was structural. Families don't erase people by accident. Erasure takes work. Erasure takes *everybody*.

* * *

The cards thickened through the sixties. Theo's handwriting loosened as the years went on, as if the act of writing to M. were slowly unclenching him. He told her about the restaurant opening — *We're calling it the Merrow's Table. Yes, I argued with him about it. Yes, I lost. It's a good name. It was always our name before it was his* — and about the critics, and about a fire in a neighbour's chip shop that scared everyone for a week, and about Vera's wedding, and about a child born in 1965 whom he called "the little critic" because, he wrote, *she tastes everything twice and makes a face both times.*

She. Me. Nine years before I was born, I was already a character in somebody's unsent letter. I read that sentence four times. Somewhere in London a man I barely knew had described me, in advance, accurately, to a woman I'd never heard of, and the description had travelled through whatever channels these cards travelled — a wall, principally — and waited fifty years for me to read my own review.

I gave it three stars. Sentimental, but structurally sound.

The Bess cards were different animals entirely. Where Theo rambled and confessed and circled, Bess compressed. Her cards read like telegrams composed during an argument:

*M — He says you chose London over us. I said nothing. Saying nothing is becoming a profession in this family.*

*M — The books don't balance. I've looked twice. Ask him what happened to March.*

*M — Come home. I am asking plainly, which costs me.*

Come home. Three times across nine cards, in three different years: *come home.* And M. evidently never did, or came and went, or came and was sent away — the cards don't say, because the cards are only half a conversation, and half a conversation is a room full of echoes arranged to look like dialogue.

The two V. cards were dated 1971 and 1973 respectively. The first said only: *M — I know what you did. We will not discuss it. — V.* The second: *M — I was wrong about what I said I knew. I will not discuss that either.* Two cards, four years apart, a complete moral revolution conducted entirely in refusals. Aunt Vera, ladies and gentlemen. Some people contain multitudes; Vera contains a filing cabinet with the drawers welded shut.

* * *

It was past midnight when I reached the last few cards, and I'll admit my hands weren't steady, partly tiredness, partly the specific fatigue of a person who has spent the day assembling a bomb out of paper. The 1974 cards changed register. Everyone's did. Even the handwriting seemed to brace itself.

*T — The fire. I keep writing this sentence and stopping. The fire. You will hear it from strangers before you hear it from us and I cannot bear that, so: the Merrow's Table burned on the fourth of March. Nobody died. The building is gone. Father is telling people things. I don't know yet which of the things are true. — T.*

Then, weeks later:

*T — Half of us are taking a new name. His idea. He says one name for the restaurant people and one for the rest, so "they" can't follow. I asked who "they" are. He looked at me the way he looks at the fish that's past saving. I signed the papers, M. I want you to know I signed them. Whatever else happens, know that I signed.*

And Bess, that same spring:

*M — They're splitting us like a hand of cards. Sandoval for some, Merrow for the rest, and everyone pretending this is about insurance forms. You'd laugh if you were here. You'd laugh and then you'd tell the truth, which is why you're not here, isn't it.*

Isn't it. A question mark doing the work of an accusation. I could feel the shape of the thing underneath now — the fire, the names, the silence with a capital S that my family has maintained for fifty years the way other families maintain a boat, expensively and with religious regularity — but the shape wasn't the story. The shape was just the outline. What I wanted was the centre, and the centre, apparently, was a woman named Marisol who cooked, and a father who renamed his children like a man rebranding a failing business, and a question nobody would answer even in secret mail.

The final card was dated October 1975. Theo's hand. But something had happened to it between September and October — the confidence gone, the slant collapsed, the letters small and crowded as if he were trying to fit too much onto too small a surface, which, in fairness, he was. A postcard has a fixed acreage. So does a life.

I copied it out into my notebook before I could stop myself, the way you touch a hot thing to confirm it's hot:

*M — I've found out what happened to the books. All of it. I know why you left and I know what he told us and the two stories don't fit together and mine was the one that was wrong. I have been wrong for a year and I owe you —*

And that's where it ends. Mid-sentence. *I owe you —* and then the blank half of the card, and then fifty years of wall.

I turned it over four times looking for more, as if the sentence might have migrated to the address side, as if it might be hiding under the stamp that was never licked. Nothing. Just the picture side — a photograph of the Merrow's Table itself, pre-fire, all white paint and hanging pots, and printed beneath in faded serif: *Greetings from Porthaven.* Greetings from Porthaven. The most violent irony I have encountered outside of a Thomas Hardy novel.

Why unfinished? He knew how to finish sentences; he'd finished hundreds of them to her. Either he was interrupted — someone walked in, someone read over his shoulder — or he couldn't. Or he wrote it, understood what finishing it would obligate him to *do*, and put the pen down. I know that manoeuvre intimately. I have ended relationships at the point of the sentence where honesty becomes actionable. There is always a moment in every confession where you can feel the exact weight of what the next clause will cost you, and some part of you — the part that has learned to survive families — sets down the pen.

But Theo was not a coward. The cards prove that; seventeen years of writing truth into a void proves it. So I kept circling the other possibility: interrupted. Someone came into the room while he was writing. Someone saw. And whoever that someone was, they let this card go into the wall along with all the others — or they put it there themselves — and then the conversation simply stopped, mid-clause, mid-apology, mid-everything, and the family sealed the gap with plaster and a new surname and a nine-year-old girl who swallowed her questions along with her dinner and never tasted food again.

I sat on the floor until the light came up grey over the harbour. Forty-one cards. One missing voice. One broken sentence. And underneath it all, humming like a fridge, the question that had been humming since Delphine stood in the churchyard and said *Merrow* out loud as if it were nothing, as if it were simply our name:

What did Theo owe her — and who stopped him from paying?

I was still sitting there, cards around me like the wreckage of a party, when gravel crunched in the lane outside. A car door. Footsteps on the path I hadn't swept. And a knock at Bess's door, at seven in the morning, delivered with the cheerful entitlement of someone who considers the house already his.
