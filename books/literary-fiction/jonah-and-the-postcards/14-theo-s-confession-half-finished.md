## 14. Theo's Confession, Half-Finished

I have been rehearsing this conversation for fifty-one years, which is either admirable discipline or a symptom, depending on who you ask. Nobody has asked. That is rather the point.

The kitchen of the Porthaven house smells of dust and old salt and, faintly, of lemon polish that Vera applied to the table in 1988 and that the table has been quietly refusing to release ever since. I sat down in my father's chair — not out of reverence but because it was nearest — and Emrys sat across from me with a notebook and that particular expression they have, the one they must have perfected between restaurant courses: attentive, sympathetic, and utterly capable of writing you off if the amuse-bouche disappoints.

"I want to tell you about 1974," I said.

Emrys clicked their pen. A small sound. In my chest, something answered it like a struck bell.

"Take your time," they said, which was kind, and also a lie, because we both knew about the demolition notice taped to the hall mirror. Three weeks. The house had less time left than I did, statistically speaking, though neither of us said so. Doctors love statistics. They hand them to you like menus.

* * *

Here is what people misunderstand about secrets. They think a secret is a thing you keep, like a tin in a cupboard. It isn't. A secret keeps *you*. It sits in your chest and grows heavier each year, and eventually you cannot lift your arms without its weight, and everyone around you assumes you are simply aging badly.

I was nineteen when the Merrow's Table burned. Nineteen, home from university for the summer, assigned by my father to stand at the gate and turn away the curious while the fire brigade finished its useless work. I stood there all night smelling rosemary and char, and by morning I had learned the first rule of our family: you do not ask. You stand where you are put, and you do not ask.

My father, Alaric Sandoval — born Alaric Merrow, though the name change came later, after, as part of the great tidying — stood in the ruins holding his sister-in-law's recipe book and saying nothing at all. Marisol's book. He held it the way a man holds evidence he intends to misplace.

"You'll take the new name," he told me on the drive back from the solicitor's, six weeks later. "Sandoval. Your mother's maiden name. Clean."

"Why?" I asked. I was nineteen and stupid enough to ask why once.

He looked at me then, and I saw something in his face I had never seen before and have spent my life failing to describe. Not guilt. Guilt would have been bearable, almost respectable. It was more like relief. The relief of a man who has gotten away with something and finds, to his surprise, that getting away with it is survivable.

"Because I say so," he said. And that was the whole of the catechism. Because I say so. Half the family took Sandoval and half kept Merrow, and the halves stopped speaking except at funerals, and the house filled up with silence the way a hull fills with water — slowly, then all at once.

Emrys was nine when they asked their one question. I know because I was there. We were all there, that Sunday, the whole fractured family crammed into this same kitchen for some anniversary nobody wanted to celebrate, and Emrys looked up from their plate and asked, in the clear carrying voice children reserve for exactly the wrong moments, "Why are there two names?"

You could have heard a fork drop. Several did.

And my father — God rot him, God bless him, I have never resolved the accounting — leaned down to my grandniece and smiled and said, "Because we're a big family, poppet. Big families need two names." And Emrys nodded, and picked up their fork, and ate whatever it was Vera had made that day, and never asked again, and never tasted anything again either, though none of us made the connection for years because none of us wanted to. You can will yourself not to see a thing. Apparently a child can will themselves not to taste one.

I have thought about that meal every day since. I have tried to remember what Vera cooked. Roast something. Potatoes, certainly. I cannot remember, and I think that is mercy, because I remember everything else.

* * *

Emrys's pen had stopped moving.

"The postcards," they said. "Forty-one of them. All addressed to M. Did you write any?"

"No," I said, and it was true, and it was the smallest truth available. "But I carried them."

They waited. Waiting is a skill the young have and the old envy.

"After my father died — 1979 — Bess asked me to help her seal up the wall. She'd taken the postcards out of Marisol's things when we cleared her cottage. Marisol died in '76, you know. Alone, in St. Ives. None of us went to the funeral. My father forbade it." I heard my own voice do something strange on the word *forbade*, a crack, a boy's crack in an old man's voice. "Bess couldn't burn them. She wasn't built for burning things, whatever else she was built for. So we sealed them into the wall behind the pantry, and Bess said, 'One day someone will open it,' and I said, 'Why not now?' and she said—"

I stopped. Not for effect. I have performed hesitation my whole life; I know its choreography. This was different. This was the body staging its own intervention.

"She said," I tried again, "she said, *because we're not ready to be sorry yet.*"

Emrys wrote that down. Good. Write it down. If I die mid-sentence — and I felt, sitting there, a distinct and unprofessional intimation that I might — let it be written down somewhere that Bess understood everything and forgave most and waited, patiently, for the rest of us to catch up.

"There's more," I said. "There's the fire itself. There's who—"

* * *

Let me describe the sensation, because I have read enough medical pamphlets since to give it its proper name and I refuse the proper name. It was as though the secret itself, hearing itself spoken, objected. As though fifty-one years of weight, given permission to move, moved all at once — upward, inward, a fist closing around the muscle in my chest. I remember thinking, absurdly, of the smell of rosemary and char. I remember thinking: *not now, I'm in the middle of a sentence.*

I have never in my life left a sentence unfinished. Ask anyone. Theo Sandoval finishes his sentences, his plates, his arguments, his whiskies. It is the one virtue of a man whose virtues are otherwise decorative.

"The fire was set," I said. "Deliberately. By—"

And then the kitchen tilted, politely, like a ship deciding to list, and the lemon-polish smell became very strong, and Emrys was shouting my name in a voice I had never heard them use, young and terrified, and the last thing I saw clearly was the notebook, sliding, pen rolling off the table, my confession lying there in their handwriting, half-finished, orphaned.

*By whom*, I meant to say. *By whom.* I had waited fifty-one years to say two syllables more than I managed.

* * *

The hospital at Truro is beige. Beige walls, beige chairs, a beige curtain around my bed that does nothing to muffle the sound of the man next door explaining his prostate to three separate visitors. If I am to convalesce, I would prefer convalescing somewhere with opinions about color.

Vera arrived first, of course. Vera would arrive first at the end of the world, and reorganize it. She stood at the foot of my bed with her arms folded and said, "You absolute fool, Theo. At your age. Telling secrets standing up like a man half your size."

"Sitting," I corrected. "I was sitting."

"You know what I mean." But her voice caught on the second word, and I saw — Vera would hate that I saw — that she had been crying in the car park. Fifty years of loyalty, and it looks, from certain angles, exactly like fear.

Emrys came later, when visiting hours had technically ended, using some combination of charm and professional menace on the nurses that I intend to learn. They pulled the beige chair close and sat with their notebook closed on their knee, which told me everything. When Emrys closes the notebook, it means the interview is over and the vigil has begun.

"You collapsed," they said. "Mid-sentence."

"I'm aware."

"The doctors say it was—" and here Emrys did something I have never heard them do, which was fail to finish a sentence themselves. They looked at their hands. "They say your heart has been working too hard for too long."

"Well," I said. "Yes. It has. It's had a very heavy job."

They almost smiled. Then didn't.

"Theo. Before you — before. You said the fire was set deliberately. You said *by*—"

"Don't," said Vera, from the doorway. She had come back in without either of us noticing, which is Vera's superpower and our family curse. "Not here. Not like this. Not with him hooked up to machines."

"He was going to *say it*, Aunt Vera." Emrys stood. I watched my grandniece square off against my elder sister and thought: there it is, the family spine, surfacing at last, in the one who can't taste. "Fifty-one years. He got four words out."

"And look what it cost him!" Vera's voice cracked like a plate hitting tile. She caught herself, pressed her lips white. "Some sentences kill the man saying them. Let him live long enough to finish it properly. Let him choose the moment."

"It was my father," I said.

They both turned. The machines beeped their disapproval. I lay there feeling lighter and sicker simultaneously, the way you feel after vomiting — emptied, wrung out, alive against your will.

"Not the whole of it," I said. "Not yet. But that much I can manage from a horizontal position. It was my father who—" and here the fist closed again, gentler this time, a warning rather than an arrest, and the monitor began its thin urgent singing, and Vera was pressing the call button and shouting for someone in scrubs, and Emrys was holding my hand with a grip far stronger than a critic's ought to be—

No. I'm being dramatic. They stabilized me. It was a warning shot, the doctors said, a cardiac event, careful now, no excitement, no strain. No confessions.

But I had said it. Four words into the sentence that matters, and the sentence is still open, still hanging over this beige room like weather. *It was my father who* — who set it? Who ordered it? Who made me stand at the gate and turn away the curious while our own history burned? Who sealed the wall with Bess? Who renamed us all and called it cleanliness?

Who taught us that silence was inheritance?

That night, alone with the prostate man's snores, I held the sentence in my mouth like a lozenge, tasting it — and I, who have eaten nothing memorable since 1974, found that it had a flavor. Iron and brine. Porthaven salt. Truth, apparently, tastes of the sea you grew up beside.

In the morning, Emrys was asleep in the chair, notebook fallen open on the floor. I could see my own words transcribed there, and beneath them, in fresh ink, a single line they had added:

*Theo says it was Alaric. Theo stopped before saying what Alaric did. Vera knows. Delphine's grandmother paid for it.*

And below that, underlined twice, a question aimed at whoever read next — at me, I understood, whenever I woke:

*What did Grandfather do, and who else helped him bury it?*

I reached for the pen. My hand shook. I wrote, in a scrawl the nurses will mistake for a stroke symptom:

*Ask Idris what he found in the loft.*

Because Idris came home last week wearing charm like a borrowed coat, and Idris has been in that loft, and Idris knows what's up there besides the Christmas decorations — and if my worthless charming nephew gets to the ending of my sentence before I do, I swear on my father's grave, which I have never once visited, that I will get out of this bed and finish it myself.
