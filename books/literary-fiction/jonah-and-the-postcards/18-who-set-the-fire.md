## 18. Who Set the Fire

I have spent eighty-one years perfecting the art of not saying things, and I will tell you now that it is a full-time occupation. People imagine silence is passive. Silence is work. You get up in the morning and you do your silence like a shift at a factory—checking it for cracks, oiling its hinges, sweeping up the little bits of truth that crumble off it overnight. I have been foreman of that factory since 1974, and I am very good at my job.

Which is why it was so disorienting to find myself, on a grey Tuesday morning in October, standing in Bess's kitchen with a photograph in my hand and every intention of telling the truth.

The photograph had come from Idris, of course. Everything poisonous in this family comes from Idris these days, though I suppose if we're being accurate—and apparently we are being accurate today, God help us—the poison came from further back than him. He's just the courier. He'd left it on the kitchen table like a man leaving a turd on a neighbour's doorstep: no note, no explanation, just the thing itself, face-up, waiting.

It was a photograph of the Merrow's Table. The original one. Taken, I would guess, in the spring of 1974, because the awning was still the blue one, before the fire took it, before everything took everything. My father stood in front of it with his arms crossed and his chin lifted at the angle he used when he wanted the world to know he was a self-made man, which was a lie stacked on top of another lie, since what he'd actually made himself out of was his sister's recipes and his sister's labour and, eventually, his sister's ruin.

And there beside him, half-turned toward the door as if she had somewhere better to be—which she did; she always did—was Marisol.

My aunt. The cook. The woman whose hands are in every dish this family has eaten for fifty years without knowing it, the way salt is in bread. The woman whose name was scraped out of the story so thoroughly that her own granddaughter had to introduce herself to us twice before we believed she existed.

I stood there holding the photograph for a long time. Long enough for the kettle to go cold. Long enough for the light through the kitchen window to shift from grey to slightly-less-grey, which is Porthaven's version of a golden afternoon.

Emrys found me like that. They came into the kitchen carrying two of the postcards—I could see from the doorway that they were numbers eleven and twelve, the ones from the winter of '74, the ones where Marisol's handwriting starts pressing so hard into the card it nearly tears through—and they stopped when they saw my face.

"Aunt Vera," they said. Carefully. Emrys has learned to handle me carefully, the way you handle a jar with a cracked lid. "You've been standing here a while."

"I'm deciding something," I told them.

"Can I ask what?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer is a separate matter." I set the photograph down on the table between us, facing them. "But I think perhaps today I'll answer."

They looked at the picture. They looked at me. And then, because Emrys is the only person in this family under seventy who has any sense, they pulled out a chair and sat down and said nothing at all. Just waited. It's extraordinary how rare that is—someone willing to let an old woman arrive at her own words at her own pace, instead of rushing at her with questions like a man with a net chasing a butterfly.

"The fire," I said, "was not an accident."

I heard Emrys breathe in. One breath, controlled, the way they do when a dish arrives at their table that isn't what they ordered.

"I know you've all been dancing around it," I went on. "Idris waving his photographs about. Delphine with her grandmother's ghost sitting on her shoulder. Theo nearly killing himself trying to confess and managing only the first course of it." I waved a hand. "So let's have the whole meal, shall we? Since we're finally sitting down together."

* * *

You want it in order. Everyone wants it in order. But the truth doesn't come in order; it comes the way water comes through a ceiling—one drip at a time, in whatever order the damage dictates. Still, I'll try.

The Merrow's Table opened in 1961. My father, Albert Merrow, put his name over the door, but it was Marisol who put the food on the plates. She'd learned from her mother, who'd learned from hers, going back to women who cooked on stoves that burned driftwood and peat, in houses that stood where the harbour wall stands now. Every recipe in that kitchen was hers. Albert wrote the menu because Albert liked the look of his own handwriting, but the menu was a map of Marisol's mind, and everyone who worked there knew it, and Albert knew they knew it, and that knowledge sat in him like a fishbone.

For thirteen years it festered. Oh, there were good years—we ate well, we were happy in the way families are happy, loudly and without looking too closely at anything. Albert took the credit at the Rotary Club. Marisol took the six o'clock shift and the four o'clock shift and the midnight shift, because that's what you do when you love the work itself more than the name above the door. She used to say, *Let him have the sign. I've got the pans.* She said it like a joke. It wasn't a joke by the end.

In 1973 the insurance man came round—Albert had taken out a policy two years earlier, a fat one, covering fire and flood, and he'd been paying it with the punctuality he showed nowhere else in his life. I was nineteen. I answered the door to the insurance man because I was the eldest and answering doors was my job, and I remember thinking it was odd that Father asked me afterward exactly what the man had said, word for word, twice through, and wrote some of it down.

Then in January of 1974, Marisol told him she wanted her name on the deed. Half the restaurant. Her recipes, her hours, her mother's copper pans still hanging in the kitchen—she wanted it written down that the place was as much hers as his. She'd found a solicitor in Truro who agreed to draw it up. She told him at Sunday lunch, in front of all of us, in her quiet way, the way she said everything, like setting a dish down gently on a table.

Albert smiled. That's the detail I need you to hold on to, because everyone else in this family has spent fifty years remembering the fire and forgetting the smile. He smiled and said, *Of course, Sol. Family should be partners.* And he refilled her glass.

The fire was three weeks later. The eleventh of February. A Tuesday, like today.

* * *

Here is what I know, and I want to be precise, because precision is the only apology I have left to give.

I don't know that he struck the match. I never saw him strike a match. What I know is this: he sent us all to the cinema that night. All of us—me, Theo, the little ones, even Bess, who never went anywhere, who said the pictures gave her a headache but went anyway because Father insisted, and Father insisting was weather; you didn't argue with it. Marisol stayed behind. She often did. She'd say she had stock to watch, and she'd sit in the kitchen with a book and the radio, and the stock would simmer, and that was her church, that was her evening prayer.

The fire started in the storeroom, not the kitchen. The fire brigade established that within a week. The storeroom had no stove in it. The storeroom had paraffin heaters in winter, but it was February and cold, yes, but the heater hadn't been lit that day—Marisol had told the investigators so herself, and Marisol did not lie about practical things. Practical things were sacred to her.

The insurance paid out. Albert rebuilt—not the restaurant, mind, that was done, that was finished, because the investigation dragged on and the gossip got teeth and the suppliers got nervous—but he rebuilt himself. New name on the new venture. Sandoval. He'd found it somewhere, a Spanish name, exotic, untraceable to anything. He said it was for rebranding. What it was for was severing. Half the family took it with him—those who chose loyalty over curiosity, or fear over either. Marisol wouldn't take it. Marisol kept the old name like she kept the copper pans: because they were hers, and no fire and no brother and no committee of frightened relatives was going to take them.

And then the whispering started. Not Albert's doing directly—he was cleverer than that, he only ever needed to start a thing and step back—but the town began to say that Marisol had set the fire herself. That she'd wanted the money. That the investigation had *questions*. And Albert, who could have ended it with one sentence, one single sentence—*my sister was asleep in her bed forty miles away that night, I drove her there myself*—said nothing. Worse than nothing. He let the silence do his work, the way I have let silence do mine all these years, and I know exactly how much work silence can do, and it makes me sick to write it down.

Because here is the part I have carried longest. Here is the part that has made my silence complicity rather than virtue.

Marisol didn't leave Porthaven because of the gossip. She left because of what Albert said to her the night after the fire, when the ashes were still warm and the family stood in the street in our coats. I was fourteen feet away. I heard it. I was nineteen years old and I heard my father tell his sister, quietly, in that reasonable voice he used for cruelty, *We both know how this looks, Sol. Best you go somewhere it looks different.* And he pressed an envelope of money into her hand—his money, insurance money, blood money—and Marisol looked at him for a long moment, and she took the envelope, because she had nothing else, and she went.

She was innocent. Do you understand? She was innocent and she was exiled and the family let her go, and then we sealed the postcards in the wall—Bess's idea, Bess's penance, though Bess was the only one of us whose guilt was mostly grief—and we called it protecting the family, and what we protected was him. His name. His insurance claim. His Sandoval, which we all wore afterwards like coats he'd handed us, warm enough, and smelling faintly of smoke.

* * *

Emrys sat across from me through all of it without moving. When I finished, the kitchen was so quiet I could hear the house settling, the old bones of it shifting, as if it too had been holding its breath for fifty years and could finally exhale.

"Why now?" Emrys asked at last. Softly. "Why tell me and not the others?"

"Because you're the one who stopped tasting," I said. "Don't look shocked. Bess told me, years ago. She worked it out the way she worked everything out—by watching. She said the whole family swallowed something that year, but you were the only one who swallowed it literally." I turned the photograph face-down. I couldn't look at Albert anymore. "You've been carrying the taste of this family's lie in your mouth since you were nine years old, child. It seemed right that you should be the first to hear it named."

"And the others?"

"The others will hear it tonight." I stood, and my knees complained, and I let them; a woman announcing the end of fifty years' silence is entitled to creak. "All of them. Idris with his photographs and Delphine with her grandmother and Theo with his half-confessions rotting in a hospital bed. Tonight, at the table, if we're going to have a table at all."

Emrys rose too. At the door they paused, and I saw them working up to a question, and I saved them the trouble.

"Yes," I said. "I knew about the postcards. I helped seal the wall. And yes—" my voice did something I didn't authorize, a small crack, a hairline fracture in eighty-one years of masonry— "one of the cards in that wall is mine. Number thirty-one. I've often wondered whether you'd find it, and what you'd make of a woman writing to her aunt for twenty years and never once sending anything, and I'll tell you what it means: it means silence isn't a decision you make once. It's a letter you keep writing and never post. And I am very tired, Emrys. I am so tired of writing."

Emrys nodded once and went to gather the others, and I stayed in the kitchen a moment longer, alone with the face-down photograph and the cold kettle and the sound of the sea doing what the sea has always done, which is keep the town's secrets and give none of them back.

Tonight, then. Tonight the founding lie gets said out loud in the room where it was hatched, or near enough. Let them hate me for the timing. Let them hate me for the fifty years. Hatred, at least, is a flavour. It's more than this family has tasted in half a century.

From the hallway I heard Emrys calling the others in from the garden, voices gathering, chairs scraping—and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the front gate creaking, and footsteps on the path that were too many and too soon, as if someone had already arrived to hear the truth before I'd finished deciding to tell it.
