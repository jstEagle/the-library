## 19. One Table

The idea arrived the way most of my ideas did: not as inspiration but as a professional reflex. I had spent fifteen years turning other people's kitchens into narrative, and somewhere in the wreckage of the last three weeks—postcards, confessions, a hospitalized uncle, a cousin who'd sold us to the *Porthaven Gazette* like day-old fish—my brain had done what it always did under pressure. It had planned a menu.

I was sitting on the back step of the dying house with Marisol's recipe book open on my knees. Delphine had found it two days earlier, wedged behind the oven alongside postcard forty-two, and she'd been carrying it around since like a reliquary. That morning she'd left it with me, which I understood was either an act of trust or a test, and with Delphine it was usually both.

The book was a school exercise notebook, its cover soft as cloth from handling, the pages swollen with grease spots and water damage and forty years of intent. *Merrow's Table* was written on the flyleaf in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the writer were always leaning toward something just out of frame. Inside were recipes in the same hand, annotated over decades in at least four others. Someone had written *too much bay* next to the fish stew, and someone else had crossed that out and written *wrong*, and beneath both, in pencil so faint I had to tilt the page to catch it: *Bess is right. —M.*

Forty-one postcards had been a conversation between siblings. The forty-second had been an apology. And this book was the whole argument—the dish, the disagreement, the reconciliation attempted in margins.

So when I stood up from the step and announced, to no one, "We should have dinner here," I wasn't being brave. I was being a critic. It's the only honest work I know how to do: look at what's on the table and tell the truth about it.

The trouble, of course, was who would be sitting down to eat.

* * *

I made the proposal that evening in the front room, which still smelled of dust and old roses and, faintly, of smoke—not the fire of 1974, which predated me, but the metaphorical kind, the kind this family manufactured domestically and in industrial quantities.

They were all there because none of them could bear to be there. Vera sat in Bess's chair with her spine doing the work of a flagpole. Theo was home from hospital, thinner, grayer, propped in the corner armchair like a man convalescing from his own biography. Idris lounged against the mantel in the posture of someone who found even architecture disappointing. My parents had arrived that afternoon from their caravan park in Devon, my mother already apologizing for the state of the kitchen, my father already reading the meter out of habit. The younger cousins hovered by the window. And Delphine stood slightly apart, near the wall where the postcards had lived, as though proximity to it conferred some standing she hadn't been granted yet.

"The demolition is in nine days," I said. "The delay petition failed—Idris's article saw to that. So the house is coming down regardless. Which means we have one chance left to do something in it besides grieve."

Vera's chin lifted a quarter inch. In Vera units, that was a scream.

"I want to cook one meal," I went on. "Here, in this kitchen. From Marisol's book. Every recipe we can manage. Both halves of the family—Sandovals, Merrows, whatever we've been calling ourselves—and Delphine. One table. One night."

Silence, of the specific vintage this family produced. Then my mother said, "Oh, Emrys, the oven hasn't been used since—" and stopped, because finishing that sentence required admitting what the oven had last been used for, and nobody wanted to open that particular door while standing in this particular room.

"The oven works," Delphine said quietly. "I checked."

"Of course you did," said Idris, and smiled his auctioneer's smile. "Tell me, does the invitation extend to the press? Only I have contacts now. Apparently I'm 'a source close to the family.'"

"You're a cousin," I said. "You're invited as a cousin. Whether you come as one is your business."

He looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind the charm—not warmth, exactly, but its distant cousin, the relative nobody talks about. "One table," he repeated, tasting the phrase like a suspicious wine. "And if it goes wrong?"

"Then it goes wrong at our table," Vera said, to everyone's visible surprise, including her own. She smoothed her skirt. "Not in a newspaper."

Theo laughed—a small, cracked sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose. "Marisol would have loved this," he said. "A dinner party as an act of war."

"Not war," I said. "Armistice."

"Same crockery," said Theo.

* * *

Preparation began the next morning, and I learned within the first hour that a kitchen is the most honest interrogation room ever devised.

My mother claimed the pastry station immediately and defensively, as though someone had accused her of store-bought shortcrust. She rolled dough with the tension of a woman defusing something. When I asked if she remembered Marisol's apple tart—recipe eleven, annotated *the secret is the lemon zest, do not tell Vera*—she went very still.

"I never met her," my mother said. "She was gone before I married in. All those years, and nobody ever said her name at Christmas. You don't spend thirty years not saying a name without it becoming load-bearing, Emrys."

"And now?"

"Now I'm making her tart," my mother said, zesting the lemon with unnecessary violence, "and if anyone criticizes it I shall remind them whose kitchen this technically is."

Delphine worked the stove the way other people pray—with total absorption and occasional tears she pretended were steam. She cooked from memory before she cooked from the book; her grandmother's versions lived in her hands. Watching her brown onions was watching someone conduct a séance with better aromatics.

It was Vera who surprised me. She arrived at ten, inspected everything with the expression of a health inspector harboring a personal grudge, and then—without asking, without announcing—took over the stock. Fish bones, fennel, a thread of saffron she'd brought wrapped in tissue paper like a relic, which, I suppose, it was.

"That's from the restaurant," I said.

"Don't be absurd, the restaurant burned." She didn't look up. "It's from Bess's tin. Bess kept things." A pause, the length of a confession trying to get out sideways. "Marisol taught me this stock when I was fourteen. Before. I've never made it since. I assumed I'd forgotten." Her hands moved through the steps without hesitation. "I haven't forgotten anything. That's rather the problem with me."

"Aunt Vera," I said carefully, "at the dinner—"

"I know what happens at the dinner." She stirred. "I set fire to nothing, I broke one plate, and I will cry into no more crockery. Pass me the cheesecloth."

* * *

The wounds surfaced, as wounds do, over the small things.

Idris appeared mid-afternoon with two bottles of wine and an air of having deigned to attend his own redemption. He stationed himself near Delphine and watched her cook with an expression I couldn't parse until I realized he was looking at his own reflection in her—the Merrow blood he'd mocked in print and apparently couldn't stop orbiting.

"My grandmother," he said suddenly, "used to make something with eel. Nobody would tell me what was in it. Family recipe, they said. Family secret." He turned the bottle in his hands. "Turns out the family secret was that half of us weren't allowed to know the other half existed."

"Recipe twenty-six," Delphine said, not turning from the stove. "Eel with green sauce. There's a note in the margin: *Idris's grandmother asked for this every birthday.*"

He went quiet in a way I'd never seen him go quiet. When he spoke again, the charm had been set down somewhere, like luggage too heavy to keep carrying. "She knew about us? Marisol knew about me?"

"Marisol knew about everyone," Delphine said. "That was her whole tragedy."

Idris looked at the book for a long time. Then he picked up a knife and asked, gruffly, where the parsley was, and I understood that in Idris's language, this was a hug.

* * *

By evening the kitchen smelled like something I had no vocabulary for—which, for a man who made his living from vocabulary, was saying something. It was the smell of the house remembering itself. Even the walls seemed to lean in.

The table was the problem. There wasn't one big enough, so we dragged the dining table from the front room into the kitchen and extended it with trestles and boards from the shed, and the result was magnificent and lopsided, like the family itself. Vera ironed a tablecloth that had been folded since the seventies and refused to discuss the brown stains at one end. "History," she said firmly. "We are not laundering history tonight."

My father tested every chair and pronounced three of them structurally unsound, then repaired them with string and dignity. The cousins argued about seating. My mother iced nothing, having decided the tart would speak for itself, then covered it with a tea towel anyway, protectively, like a bird on a nest.

At seven o'clock I stood at the head of the crooked table with Marisol's book in my hands and looked down its length: Vera and Theo, my parents, the cousins, Idris with his wine, Delphine at the far end nearest the stove, closest to her grandmother's territory. Nine days until the bulldozers. Forty-two postcards in a shoebox waiting to be mailed. One family, currently holding its breath.

"Before we start," I said, "I should confess something."

Everyone stilled. This family could smell a confession at forty paces; we'd been raised on them, mostly unfinished.

"For twenty-five years I haven't tasted anything. Since I was nine. Since—" I gestured vaguely at the room, at all of it. "Since this. I've reviewed restaurants I couldn't taste. I described flavors from memory and other people's faces. Tonight is the first meal I've ever cooked hoping to actually eat it."

My mother made a small sound. Vera closed her eyes briefly, as if filing something away in the drawer where she kept unbearable facts.

"So if I seem emotional later," I finished, "it's not the wine."

I opened the book to the first page. The soup. *For M., who says mine is better. She's right. —B.*

"Bess wrote that," I said. "Bess, who never admitted anyone was right about anything, wrote that her little sister's soup was better than hers. And then she sealed it in a wall for fifty years." I looked around the table. "That's what we do. We write the truth and hide it. Well. Not tonight."

I ladled the soup. The bowls went around. Nine days of preparation, fifty years of silence, one recipe in a dead woman's handwriting.

I carried my own bowl to my place, sat, and looked at it. Pale gold, flecked with saffron, steam rising off it like something being released.

Around me, spoons waited. Nobody ate. They were all watching me—they knew, all of them, what this bowl meant, what it might refuse to do.

I picked up my spoon.

The soup was warm. That was the first thing, and for a moment I thought that was all there would be—warmth, texture, the mechanical report of a body doing its job. I swallowed.

And then—

Salt. Not just salt; the sea, specifically, Porthaven's sea, cold and mineral and familiar as a voice. Underneath it, saffron, honeyed and strange. Fennel, sweet and faintly licoriced, the ghost of a garden someone had tended. And beneath all of it, something older, something I had no word for except *home*, which is not a flavor, except that it is, except that it had been waiting in me for twenty-five years like a letter in a wall.

The spoon rang against the bowl as my hand shook.

"Emrys?" My mother's voice, very far away.

I tried to say something critical, something measured and professional, something worthy of fifteen years of fraudulent reviews. What came out instead was a sound I didn't recognize as mine, and then I was crying—openly, disgracefully, into Marisol's soup, at my own table, in front of the entire family.

Nobody laughed. Vera reached across the trestle and gripped my wrist, hard, her rings digging in, her face doing something I'd never seen it do.

"Eat," she said, roughly. "While it's hot."

And I did. We all did. Bowl after bowl, course after course, the tart with its secret lemon zest, the eel with green sauce that made Idris close his eyes, the fish stew that settled the *too much bay* argument once and for all in Marisol's favor. The talk rose and tangled and occasionally caught fire—old names, old grievances surfacing like bones in stock—but it stayed at the table. It stayed at the table.

Later, much later, when the plates were ruins and the candles had drowned, Theo raised his glass and said, "To Marisol."

"To Marisol," we echoed.

"To M.," said Delphine, and her voice cracked on the letter, and I understood that tomorrow there would be a vote, and a name, and a house coming down—but tonight, for the first time in fifty years, the Merrows had eaten together, and the food had been true, and so, finally, had we.

I slept that night in the house's spare room, on sheets that smelled of cedar and the past, with the recipe book on the pillow beside me like a child's teddy.

In the morning, I woke to voices downstairs—and to the smell of coffee, and bacon, and something else underneath it, something I recognized instantly and with a lurch of joy: someone was cooking breakfast from the book, testing whether last night had been a miracle or a beginning.

But when I came down the stairs, the kitchen was full of people, and every single one of them was looking at me.

"We need to talk," Vera said, "about the name."
