## 5. The House on Foundry Road

The GPS gave up three miles outside of Jonah's Bend, the way it always did, announcing in its calm synthetic voice that I had arrived at my destination while I was still surrounded by soybean fields. Wren turned off the navigation with a small decisive tap, as if closing a door on it.

"We'll find it," they said. "Towns like this only have one road that matters."

They were right. Foundry Road ran straight from the collapsed gate of the old Ostrander Radiator Works down to the river, and every house on it had been built by the factory for its workers, which meant every house on it was the same house wearing different paint. Ours had been green once. It was gray now, or rather it was the color of a thing that had stopped bothering to be green, and half its windows wore plywood like eyepatches.

The front yard was a battlefield already lost. A roll-off dumpster squatted in the driveway, swollen with furniture I recognized — the plaid recliner where my father fell asleep during every televised ballgame, the kitchen table where Marion wrote lyrics on the backs of utility envelopes. Somebody had carried forty years of our family out to that dumpster in an afternoon, and somebody else — Delia, presumably — had decided at the last minute that some of it was worth keeping, because the porch was stacked with cardboard boxes in that particular way that says *I ran out of conviction halfway through.*

"God," Wren said quietly. "It's smaller than you described it."

"Everything is."

"No, I mean it. You talked about this house like it was a cathedral."

"It was. Cathedrals are smaller on the outside. That's the whole trick of them."

Wren looked at me then, the look they'd been giving me since the radio incident — patient, forensic, loving in the way a bridge inspector loves a suspect span. They hadn't asked me again what happened on Corliss Street the day I pulled over shaking. They were waiting me out. Twenty-two years of marriage teaches you that silence has a load rating too, and Wren knew mine better than I did.

We got out of the car. The September air smelled exactly as it always had: hot metal memory, cut grass, the river underneath everything like a bass note. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing with tremendous commitment. My chest did something complicated.

The doorbell didn't work. Of course it didn't. I knocked, and after a long interval there were footsteps inside, heavy and unhurried, the footsteps of a person who intended to answer the door but saw no reason to hurry about it.

Delia opened the door holding a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, and she looked at me the way customs officers look at passports.

"Well," she said. "The prodigal cousin. You found us before the listing went live. Congratulations."

"Hello to you too, Delia."

She was sixty-one and built like a woman who had spent her life carrying things other people dropped — broad shoulders, forearms like dock rope, reading glasses pushed up into hair gone white at the temples in two aggressive wings. She looked past me at Wren, assessed, and apparently approved, because she stepped back to let us in.

"You must be Wren. Kestrel's told me precisely nothing about you over the years, so we'll have a lot to cover." She extended her free hand. "Delia. The cousin who stayed."

"The cousin who stayed," Wren repeated, shaking it. "That sounds like it carries weight."

"Everything carries weight here. That's the water table." She turned and led us inside. "Watch the third stair. It's been lying about itself since 1987."

* * *

Inside, the house was mid-amputation. The living room walls showed pale rectangles where pictures used to hang, except for one wall above the fireplace where nothing had been removed at all: the photographs of Marion. Marion at nineteen on the factory floor in welding leathers, grinning like she'd gotten away with something. Marion at thirty-five with a guitar nearly as big as she was. Marion at fifty, the year before the end, sitting on this very porch with a coffee can full of pencils.

Nobody had touched that wall. I noticed Delia noticing me notice it.

"Estate sale people wanted those," she said. "I told them the wall comes with the house or not at all."

"Thank you," I said, and meant it more than I could comfortably say.

"Don't thank me yet." She set her mug down on a stack of boxes labeled, unhelpfully, STUFF. "Kestrel, I'm going to be direct with you, because the family reputation for indirection is well earned and I've spent six decades trying not to catch it. Why are you here?"

"I called Ambrose—"

"Ambrose." She said the name the way you'd spit out a cherry pit. "Ambrose called me Tuesday, very mysterious, said you might be coming and that I should, quote, 'let him handle it.' Which, given Ambrose's track record with handling things, struck me as a sentence written by a man who has never once handled anything."

"What do you know about the tapes?" I asked.

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, guarded, gone. "What tapes?"

"Delia."

"Boxes of unlabeled cassettes in the basement, plus a reel-to-reel machine that weighs as much as a sin, plus approximately nine hundred items in this house that belonged to your mother, all of which are legally part of an estate I have been administering alone for five years while certain relatives pursued careers in distant cities." She folded her arms. "So when you ask me what I know about the tapes, understand that I know their exact count, their approximate condition, and their precise location, and that I also know that someone at a licensing firm in Nashville knows they exist, and that none of this is a coincidence you drove eight hours to investigate out of nostalgia."

The hallway clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled with a sigh.

"Nashville," I said.

"Meridian & Cole, Talent Licensing. They sent a letter in July." She reached into the pile on the hall table — a pile that contained, among other things, a birdcage and a tennis racket — and produced a business card, holding it out to me between two fingers like something she'd found under the refrigerator. "I haven't signed anything. Before you ask. But I've talked to them twice, and they talk about your mother's songs the way vultures talk about a horizon, and I would like to know how they heard about material that has never been recorded, performed publicly, or mentioned by anyone in this family since roughly 1994."

I took the card. Heavy stock. Embossed letters. *Hollis Meridian, Senior Partner.* Underneath, a tagline: *Where Legacy Finds Its Voice.*

"Somebody leaked," I said.

"Somebody leaked," Delia agreed. "And in this family, Kestrel, the list of somebodies is short enough to fit on a napkin." Her voice softened by maybe four percent, which for Delia was a hug. "You look terrible, by the way. Have you eaten?"

* * *

We ate sandwiches at the one surviving table — a folding thing set up in the kitchen because the real table was in the dumpster or possibly in Tennessee by now. Delia made them the way Marion used to make them, mustard on both slices, which hit me sideways and which I ate without comment because commenting felt like handing her ammunition.

Wren, bless them, had appointed themselves archaeologist. They kept drifting off during conversation to open boxes and report findings.

"Kestrel. Your fourth-grade science fair ribbon. For a project titled 'Stress Distribution in Popsicle Stick Trusses.'"

"I got second place."

"You got robbed," Wren said, with total sincerity, and put the ribbon in their pocket like evidence.

Another box yielded a shoebox of cassette tapes, and Wren went very still, and I watched them hold one up to the light. Unlabeled. Plain shell, the kind you bought in ten-packs at the drugstore. There were dozens more beneath it, and beyond the kitchen, through the doorway, I could see the dining room was full of them — banker's boxes, liquor store boxes, one box that had once held a dishwasher, all of them packed with cassettes in no order anyone had ever explained to me.

"How many?" I asked.

"Last count, four hundred and twelve," Delia said. "Not counting the reels in the basement, which Ambrose claims are fragile and which nobody is allowed to touch, per Ambrose, who also claims he doesn't know why they matter, per Ambrose." She wiped mustard from the corner of her mouth with great dignity. "Your mother recorded everything, Kestrel. Every scrap. Kitchen rehearsals, false starts, her humming while she did dishes — Ambrose had a microphone rigged in this house like it was Sun Studio. Did you know that?"

I had known it. I had forgotten knowing it, which is a thing grief does — files things away so thoroughly you lose the index.

"She never let any of it leave the house," I said.

"No. And now it's leaving the house one way or another, either into a Nashville contract or onto a curb, and I have until October to figure out which, and I am tired, and my knees hurt, and I would appreciate it if my co-heirs treated this estate like it contained something besides an inconvenience." She stood, collected plates, and paused at the sink with her back to us. "There's a lawyer's card in your hand and a question you're not asking me, Kestrel. Ask it."

"Why do you have a lawyer?"

"Because when money gets involved, this family stops being a family and starts being a dispute with holidays attached. I've seen it happen twice. I bought a card and a phone number so that when it happens a third time, I'm not standing in the yard empty-handed." She turned around, and her face was doing something complicated — the same face Marion made when she was deciding whether to tell you the truth. "Also because Hollis Meridian called me *ma'am* four times in one phone call and I didn't trust a single syllable of it."

* * *

Late afternoon, Wren and I walked the house while Delia took a phone call on the porch, her voice rising and falling in the cadence of a woman negotiating with a roofing company or possibly fate.

The house was smaller than the cathedral. That was the truth of it. The hallway where I'd learned to walk. The bathroom with the tub where Marion soaked her welding-sore shoulders and sang low, so low I used to press my ear to the door just to keep the sound. My old room, stripped to bare mattress and a window looking out at the garage, where I'd spent whole summers listening to music coming up through the floorboards and pretending I wasn't listening.

In the basement, the reel-to-reel machine sat under a dust cloth like furniture in a dead relative's parlor. Next to it, on a workbench Ambrose had installed sometime in the seventies, sat a single cassette apart from all the others, labeled in ballpoint in a handwriting I knew better than my own:

*Radiator Hymn — take 3 — DO NOT.*

DO NOT, and nothing else. No explanation. Just the imperative, hanging there, the way Marion hung imperatives all over our childhood. *Do not touch the radiator valves. Do not sing that song at school. Do not.*

Wren came and stood beside me. We looked at it together for a long time.

"That's the song," Wren said. Not a question. "From the radio."

"Take three," I said. "Which means there were at least two others."

"And the label says—"

"I see what it says."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then, gently, in the voice they use for me when I'm standing too close to an edge: "Kestrel. Your mother wrote *do not* on a lot of things. You've told me that yourself. But she's been gone five years, and the song played on the radio anyway. Whatever she was protecting — herself, you, whatever it was — the seal's broken. The question isn't whether we open these boxes. The question is who gets to decide what happens when we do."

Down the hall, the basement stairs creaked. Delia stood at the top, phone still in hand, looking down at us with an expression I couldn't read — suspicion, yes, but underneath it something rawer. Something that looked almost like fear.

"That was Ambrose," she said. "He wants to know if you've arrived."

"He knows I'm coming."

"He knows more than that, Kestrel." She came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, avoiding the third step from the bottom by long practice. "He asked me — his exact words — 'Has she found the box with the hymn in it yet?'"

The basement went very quiet. Even the furnace seemed to hold its breath.

"And what did you tell him?" Wren asked.

Delia looked at the cassette in my hand, at the ballpoint *DO NOT*, and then at me, and I watched her decide — visibly, deliberately — that the era of handling things was over.

"I told him," she said, "to get in his truck and drive here tonight, because the three of us are going to sit down at this table and somebody in this family is finally going to tell me the truth."
