## 5. The First Letter

He did not open the box that night. He carried it into his apartment like something rescued from a fire—held against his chest, both arms, the way you carry a sleeping child down stairs—and set it on the kitchen table, which he never ate at, and then he sat across from it for a long time as if it might open itself, or as if he might be spared the opening.

The apartment was quiet in the way it always was. Marta was in Braga until Sunday, a conference on archival restoration, and the flat had settled into that particular silence of hers being gone, which was not an empty silence but a furnished one, every chair and cup still arranged by her logic. Ines boiled water he didn't drink. He made toast he didn't taste. He had learned, over three years, to feed himself the way you feed a cat left behind on holiday: mechanically, out of obligation, without conversation.

The box sat there. A shoebox, once white, now the color of weak tea, its lid soft at the corners from decades of handling—or from decades of not being handled, which does its own kind of damage. The letters inside were bundled with string, and through the string he could see the envelopes, and on the envelopes, in his mother's hand, the name he had not seen written anywhere in years:

Tomás Quintana.

His father's name. His own middle name. He had spent his whole life saying it without thinking, the way you say a word in your own language until one day a foreigner asks what it means and you realize you never knew.

He went to bed without opening it. He told himself it was respect. He knew it was fear. The distinction between the two had been getting thinner all his life, and at fifty-one it was nearly transparent, like the membrane of an onion.

* * *

In the morning he called the home in Cascais to check on his mother. The nurse, Filipa, with her voice like a hand smoothing a wrinkled sheet, said Leonor had had a good night, had eaten half her breakfast, had asked twice about the weather in the Azores.

"The Azores," Ines said.

"She asks about that often. She says someone is coming home from there." A pause, the small professional pause of a woman deciding how much truth to spend. "She seems happy when she says it."

His father had fished the Atlantic for thirty years. Six weeks out, three weeks in, a rhythm Ines had been raised inside like a tide schedule. He had loved the departures—the smell of diesel and salt on his father's coat, the way the whole street seemed to lean toward the docks—and he had hated the returns, because his father came back quieter each time, a man being slowly erased by weather, until the last return, when the sea kept him.

"She's waiting for him," Ines said.

"She's waiting for someone," Filipa said gently. "It's a good thing to wait for. Some of them wait for nothing at all."

He hung up and looked at the box. Morning light came through the kitchen window and lay across it like a hand.

He untied the string.

* * *

The letters were arranged in order—of course they were; his mother had been a bookkeeper her whole working life, a woman for whom disorder was a moral failing—and he lifted the first bundle out with the care of a man lifting an organ from a donor tray. The oldest envelopes were thin, cheap paper gone brittle, postmarked 1979, addressed in a hand he almost didn't recognize: rounder than the hand he knew, more patient, the handwriting of a woman who still believed her sentences would be read slowly.

He took out the first one. The flap was glued, never sealed with a lick, as if even in the act of writing she had known it would never be sent—or as if she had sealed it for an audience she hadn't met yet. Him. Forty-six years late, he was her addressee.

He opened it the way you open a door you've been told is locked.

* * *

*My Tomás,*

*The sardines came early this year. Senhor Baptista gave me two kilos and I grilled them on the roof and ate them alone and they were the best thing I have eaten in my life and the worst, because you weren't there, and I thought: this is what my whole life will be. Best and worst in the same mouth.*

*João Inês has a tooth coming. He cries at night and I walk him up and down and up and down and the floor knows the route better than I do. He has your frown. I'm not joking, he frowns like a little customs officer, and when I laugh at it he frowns harder, which is exactly what you do, which means either he loves you already or he is you already, and I haven't decided which frightens me more.*

*Six weeks you've been out. Six weeks. The women at the market ask after you and I say he's fine, he's working, and they nod, and I know what they're thinking because I'm thinking it too: that the sea keeps what it likes and returns what it's finished with, and no one can tell which you are until you walk up the street.*

*Don't tell me about the weather. Everyone tells me about the weather. Tell me what you ate. Tell me if the stars looked different from out there. Tell me one true thing that isn't about fish or weather or money and I will keep it like a stone in my shoe, I will walk on it all the way to the next time I see you.*

*The apartment is small and I have organized it to death. I have organized the spoons. I have organized the way the light comes in. I am twenty-three years old and I have organized my whole life around a door that opens every nine weeks, and I am ashamed to tell you that some nights I hate that door, and more ashamed to tell you that I would not change it, and most ashamed of all to tell you that I am only writing this because you will never read it, because if I thought you would read it I would write: everything is fine, we are well, the boy is beautiful, come home safe.*

*This is the letter I won't send. I'm going to write one of these every time. I've decided. The ones I send will be for you. These will be for me. Everyone should have a room in the house that no one else enters. This is mine.*

*Your wife, who is also a person,*
*Leonor*

* * *

Ines read it twice. Then he took off his glasses and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes and sat there in the bright kitchen with the taste of nothing in his mouth, which was his permanent condition, and the taste of grief in his throat, which was new.

He had never heard this voice. That was the thing. Forty-six years of knowing his mother—the woman who portioned rice with a precision that embarrassed restaurant kitchens, who could identify a spice from the next room, who had taught him, without ever saying so, that food was a language and love was its grammar—and he had never once heard this voice, this wry, wounded, ferociously intelligent voice, this woman who *organized the way the light came in.*

His mother had been, for his entire life, a function. She fed him. She corrected him. She stood in doorways. She had opinions about his reviews that she delivered like verdicts: *too many adjectives, Inês, the man is describing a codfish, not the Virgin.* He had assumed the flatness of her later years was the flatness of her. He had never considered it might be the flatness of a room with the door closed.

*A room in the house that no one else enters. This is mine.*

Except it wasn't, was it. Someone else had entered. Someone had taken the whole house.

He thought of Amadeo's face at lunch two weeks ago, the fork pausing, the tomato plant trembling in the background of the memory. He had not called his uncle since. He had told himself he was waiting for the right moment, and he had known, in the honest cellar of himself, that he was waiting the way his mother had waited at that door: for something that might not come.

* * *

He made coffee he couldn't taste and drank it anyway for the heat, for the small animal comfort of it, and carried the cup back to the table and read the letter a third time, slower, the way he read manuscripts he intended to review, looking for the sentence underneath the sentences.

It was there, of course. It was always there. His whole career had been built on finding it: the true thing inside the performance. *I am only writing this because you will never read it.* That was the sentence. That was the whole marriage in one line—love addressed to an absence, honesty made possible only by the certainty of no reply.

He knew that condition intimately. He had been writing it for three years. Every review he'd filed since the stroke was a letter to a man who would never taste what he described. He had thought he was the only one at the table eating nothing. His mother had been sitting across from him the entire time, forty years earlier, eating alone on a roof with the best sardines of her life.

He laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob, and he sat with his hand over his mouth until it passed.

* * *

The second letter was from October 1979. He read it standing up, pacing, unable to stay still, the way you read something that is reading you.

*My Tomás,*

*You came home on the fourteenth and left on the twenty-second and I have done the arithmetic and I want you to know I did the arithmetic. Eight days. I made bacalhau à Brás your first night because it's your favorite and you said it was good and went to sleep. You slept fourteen hours. I sat in the kitchen and listened to you sleep and thought: there he is, the man I married, and he is a stranger who knows where everything is.*

*Don't misunderstand me. I am not writing this to wound you. I am writing this because you are at sea and cannot answer, and because I have decided to be honest somewhere in my life, even if only on paper, even if only to a drawer.*

*Here is what I could not say while you were here: you come home and the sea is still on you. You sit at the table and you are not at the table. You look at João Inês like you're memorizing him for a voyage, like he's a chart, and I know you love him, I have never doubted it, but love that only looks is a kind of weather, it passes over everything and changes nothing.*

*I said to Dona Graça today, my husband is home, and she said, praise God, and I smiled, and inside me something said: for now. Is that a terrible thing? I love you. I want you to know that whatever I write in these letters, I love you. But I am starting to understand that loving someone and living with them are two different countries and I have a visa for only one.*

*The boy walked four steps today. He walked to me. He doesn't walk to you yet. He will. By the time you're home again he'll be running and you'll have missed it and you'll pretend you didn't and I'll pretend with you, because that's what we do, we pretend, we are so good at pretending, we could open a theater.*

*Eight days. I did the arithmetic. I will always do the arithmetic.*

*Leonor*

* * *

Ines stopped pacing. He was at the window. Below, the street did its ordinary business, a delivery van, a woman with a dog, the café on the corner filling with the eleven o'clock crowd, all of them living inside their own arithmetic, and he stood there holding a page written before he could walk.

*He walked to me. He doesn't walk to you yet.*

His first steps. He had always assumed—had never thought to assume otherwise—that his father had been there. That the family story contained it: *Tomás saw you walk, he was so proud, he cried.* His mother had told him stories like that, or had she? He tried to find the memory and couldn't. He tried to find any memory of his mother telling stories about his father's homecomings and found, instead, a strange blankness, a corridor with no doors. She had talked about the sea. She had talked about the fish, the boats, the storms. She had never, he realized now, talked about his father in the house.

Because he had barely been in the house. Eight days. The arithmetic.

He thought about his own reviews again, and this time the comparison turned in his hands like a knife. He had built a career on describing meals he couldn't taste, and he had called it craft, subtlety, evolution. His editor had called it his finest period. And here was his mother, in 1979, doing the same thing in reverse—describing a marriage she couldn't live, addressing a man who couldn't hear—and she had called it what it was. *A room no one else enters.*

She had been braver than him at twenty-three than he was at fifty-one. That was the thought that sat down in his chest and refused to leave.

* * *

There were eleven letters in the first bundle. He read six that day, standing, sitting, standing again, forgetting to eat, forgetting the time, the kitchen moving through its light like a slow film—morning, noon, the long gold of late afternoon, dusk. He read about the tooth. About the flu of 1980, when she'd been sick alone with the boy and written *I called your brother and he came, which surprised me, and he held the baby and said, my brother's son, and I watched him and thought, there is more of Tomás in Amadeo than in Tomás.* About the money, always the money, the precise, humiliated arithmetic of a fisherman's wage against a fisherman's pride. About a night in 1981 when she wrote: *You didn't come home when the boat came in. Dona Graça saw you at the taverna. I am not writing this to accuse you. I am writing it because it is true and someone in this house has to keep the true things.*

That one he read three times, because of the phrase. *Someone in this house has to keep the true things.* He looked at the shoebox. He was holding the true things. They had been kept. They had been kept so well that no one had ever seen them, which raised a question that had been circling him all day and finally landed:

Where were the letters his mother *had* sent? The ones addressed *for you*? He had never seen them either. His father had died at sea when Ines was nine—so the story went—and his mother had never produced a single letter, not one, not in the forty years since, not when Ines asked about him, not when the grief anniversaries came around. He had assumed there were none. Fishermen's letters drowned with the men.

Or.

He set the thought down carefully, the way you set down something hot. His uncle's face. The fork in the air. *Amadeo said it was kinder—* no. That hadn't happened yet. That was ahead of him, in the box, or in his mother's dissolving head, or in a garden in Almada with tomato rows. He didn't know yet what he knew. That was the vertigo of it: the past wasn't gone, it was stored, and he was the first person in forty years to open the storage room, and the light in there was very bad.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when he reached the last letter in the bundle, postmarked March 1982, the envelope heavier than the others, two pages folded into three. He was exhausted in the way that reading someone's soul exhausts you, and his eyes ached, and he almost saved it for morning.

He opened it.

*My Tomás,*

*You are home for good. They've given you the harbor job, the boat is sold, the sea has finished with you, and I should be happy. I am trying to be happy. I made your favorite and you ate it and said it was good and I watched your face and your face said nothing, your face has stopped saying anything, and I understood tonight, sitting across from you, that I have been writing letters to a man at sea for three years and the man at sea was never on the boat.*

*You are here now. You are here every night. And I am lonelier than I was when you were gone, because then I had the letters, and the hope, and now I have a man at my table who looks through me like I'm weather.*

*João Inês is four. He asked me today where you go when you're home, because you're not here even when you're here, and I didn't know how to answer him, so I said, Papa is tired, and he said, from what? And I said, from work. And he said, you're tired too. Four years old. He sees everything. God help him, he sees everything, and one day he will see this house the way it really is, and I don't know what he'll do with it.*

*I am writing to you across a table, my love. You are three meters away. This is the shortest distance and the longest voyage I have ever written across. Answer me. Not with letters. With your face. Look at me the way you looked at the water and I will never write another one of these.*

*Leonor*

Ines sat in the dark kitchen for a long time. The box held bundles for 1982, 1983, all the way into the nineties, into the two-thousands, into years when his mother's handwriting had begun to shake. Forty years of letters. Forty years of a woman talking to a man who was either absent or present, and in both cases unreachable, and every letter intercepted at the door by a brother-in-law who had decided—*Amadeo said it was kinder*—no. He still didn't know where that sentence came from. He would find out.

He looked at the second bundle. The earliest envelope was dated April 1982, one month after the last. His mother had written another one. Of course she had. She had promised herself.

He picked it up, turned it over in his fingers, and saw that this envelope was different. Thicker. The paper heavier, better stock, bought deliberately, and the address—not just the name this time but a full address, a street, a number, a postal code—written in ink so dark it looked pressed into the page.

And across the lower left corner, in his mother's younger, rounder, more patient hand, two words:

*The last.*
