## 6. Uncle Amadeo

The train to Sintra smelled of diesel and someone's sardines, and I watched the hills come up green and wet out the window and thought about how a man can rehearse a sentence for forty minutes and still not know if he'll say it.

I had the shoebox with me. I hadn't planned to bring it — it had lived on my kitchen table for two weeks like a guest who wouldn't leave, and when I packed my bag that morning my hand simply went to it, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. So it rode beside me on the seat, wrapped in a plastic bag from a fishmonger, and every time the train braked I felt it slide an inch toward me, patient as guilt.

Amadeo had moved after Aunt Graça died. Smaller house, up past the last tram stop, a whitewashed box with a terrace and a view of nothing in particular — scrub hillside, a water tank, the sea flattened to a gray seam in the distance. He'd been a naval engineer before he retired, a man of gauges and tolerances, and now he grew tomatoes, which struck me as the most poetic thing he had ever done, though he would have hated hearing it put that way.

He was on his knees between the rows when I came through the gate, straw hat down over his eyes, sleeves rolled over forearms gone brown and ropey. He didn't hear me at first. I stood there watching him work — the deliberate way he pinched off a sucker shoot, thumbed aphids from a leaf, held each fruit up to the light as if interviewing it for a position. My father's brother. The family face, older: same heavy jaw, same eyebrows that met in the middle like a handshake. For three years I had seen him at Christmas and at funerals and thought nothing about him at all, which is its own kind of achievement, and I understood suddenly that this blankness had been manufactured — by him, or by all of us together, a family-wide agreement not to look too closely at anything.

"Inês." He said it without surprise, getting up slowly, one hand on his knee. "You look terrible."

"Thank you. It's the profession."

"Sit. There's coffee." He looked at the plastic bag under my arm and then away, quickly, the way you look away from a stranger crying on the metro. "And what's that?"

"Laundry," I said, and heard myself lie with the smoothness of long practice, and hated it.

* * *

The kitchen was small and mercilessly clean. Copper pots hung from hooks he polished himself; a calendar from an agricultural co-op showed a tractor in a field of sunflowers, June already crossed out in blue ballpoint. Amadeo made coffee in a battered moka pot and set out two cups without asking whether I wanted any, because in our family coffee was not a question but a condition.

We talked about nothing, expertly. The tomatoes — he was trying a variety from Braga, temperamental, worth it. His hip. The price of everything. A cousin's son who had emigrated to Luxembourg and sent photographs of snow. I performed the role of nephew with the same fluency I brought to restaurant reviews, describing sensations I did not feel, and somewhere in the middle of it I noticed that my hands were cold even though the kitchen was warm, and that the shoebox sat on the floor by my chair like a dog that knew it wasn't allowed on the furniture.

He asked about Leonor. He always asked about Leonor, and always in the same careful voice, the voice of a man approaching a dog that has bitten him once.

"She's worse," I said. "She called me Tomás last week. Fed me soup and told me I worked too hard and should come ashore more."

Amadeo stirred sugar into his cup though he took it black. "That's the dementia?"

"That's the dementia." I turned my cup. "Though sometimes I think she's not confused at all. Sometimes I think she's just finally saying what she always thought."

He said nothing to that. Outside, a wind came up the hill and moved through the tomato plants, and their leaves rattled like small applause.

"I found something at the house," I said.

Casual. That was the whole strategy — casual, tossed off, a stone skipped across the surface so it wouldn't have time to sink. I had rehearsed it on the train. *I found some old letters of Mum's, going through the wardrobe.* Light. Almost bored. Let him ask the questions; let him hang himself with curiosity.

But when the moment came my mouth went dry, and what came out was neither light nor bored.

"I found letters she wrote to my father. Forty years of them. She never sent them."

The spoon stopped against the side of his cup.

It was a small sound, or rather the absence of one — the tiny ceramic click that should have continued and didn't. Amadeo's hand stayed where it was, spoon resting against porcelain, and for perhaps four seconds he did not move at all, did not breathe, a man photographed mid-gesture and left there. Then he lifted the spoon, tapped it twice on the rim — precise, unhurried, the gesture of a man resuming a life — and set it on the saucer.

"Unsent letters," he said. "Women write those. Your grandmother wrote them. Half of Portugal's attics are full of them." He smiled. It reached his eyes the way a forecast reaches the weather. "What does it matter? They were never sent."

"No," I agreed. "They were never sent."

I let it sit. That was the other part of the strategy, the part I hadn't rehearsed because I hadn't known I possessed it: the ability to say nothing and make it a weight. Amadeo got up, rinsed his cup, dried it with a cloth folded in thirds. He offered me more coffee. I declined. He sat back down and picked up the conversation exactly where we'd left it, as if picking up a dropped stitch — the cousin in Luxembourg, the price of fertilizer — and I answered in the right places, and all the while we both knew, with the certainty of two men in a rowboat watching the same leak, that something had come into the room and was not going to leave.

* * *

He fed me lunch because refusing would have been a statement, and neither of us could afford statements yet. Sardines grilled on the terrace, bread, a salad from the garden, wine from a neighbor whose name he pronounced with elaborate indifference. We ate at a small table under a vine trellis, and the food was good — I could see that it was good, could read its goodness the way a blind man reads a face by structure alone — and I ate mechanically, tasting salt and smoke and nothing else, the flat gray country where my palate used to be.

"You're not eating," Amadeo said.

"I'm eating."

"You're pushing fish around like a boy who wants to leave the table." He refilled my glass. "Your father did that. When something was wrong, he'd move food around his plate and say nothing until it exploded out of him sideways, usually at the wrong person, usually about money."

"Tell me about him," I said. It was not entirely a deflection. It was also hunger — the ordinary, late-arriving hunger of a man who realizes he knows almost nothing about his own father beyond the official version: lost at sea when Inês was six, a good provider, a quiet man, a photograph in uniform on Leonor's dresser for forty years.

Amadeo chewed slowly. "Tomás." He said the name the way you set down something heavy. "Quiet, yes. People mistook it for depth. It wasn't depth. It was a man who couldn't say what he felt, so he went to sea instead, because the sea doesn't ask." He turned his glass by its base. "He loved your mother. Whatever else — and there was plenty else — he loved her. In his way. Which was a bad way, a hard way, the way of men who think love is a roof you put over someone and never mention again."

"And you? What was your way?"

The question surprised him. I saw it land — a flicker behind the eyes, the brief recalculation of a man who has just realized the boy across the table is not entirely the boy he filed away years ago.

"My way was to stay on land," he said, "and mind my own business."

"Did you?" I asked. "Mind it?"

The wind moved through the trellis. Somewhere below us a dog began barking at nothing, then stopped, embarrassed by its own noise. Amadeo looked at me for a long moment, and in that look I saw him weigh things — I saw the scales working behind his face, the engineer calculating load — and I understood that whatever he decided in the next five seconds would decide the rest of my life, or at least the shape of the truth I'd be permitted to carry.

"Eat your sardines," he said gently. "They're getting cold."

* * *

I helped him with the tomatoes afterward because leaving immediately would have been another statement. He directed me wordlessly — tie that one, pinch this — and we worked down the rows in the slanting afternoon light, and it was almost peaceful, this dumb horticultural labor, two men touching plants instead of each other. His plants were magnificent. Staked and pruned and fussed over, each fruit cradled in its own little hammock of twine. A man could spend forty years doing this, I thought. Tending something. Keeping it upright. Deciding which shoots to remove so the rest would grow strong.

"These are beautiful," I said.

"They take work." He straightened, pressed a fist into his lower back. "Everything worth keeping takes work. And sometimes—" he snapped a yellowed leaf off its stem and dropped it into the pail between us— "sometimes keeping a thing means cutting parts of it away. Parts that would drag the whole plant down. You do it for the plant. Even if the plant, if it could speak, might disagree."

"Would it disagree?"

"It would scream," Amadeu said. "Plants are dramatic. But in August there are tomatoes, and no one remembers the screaming."

He looked at me then, really looked, hat pushed back, and his face was open and kind and utterly unreadable, three things I had not known a face could be at once.

"Inês. Whatever you found in that house — and I'm not saying you found anything — some letters are unsent because they were never meant to arrive. Not because of cruelty. Because sending them would have hurt everyone, and protected no one. There are men who intercept a letter the way you'd catch a child running into traffic. You get thanked later. Or you don't. Either way, the child lives."

"The child," I said, "doesn't get to decide that."

"No," he said. "Children never do. That's what makes them children."

* * *

At the gate, dusk coming down purple over the hillside, he shook my hand — formally, the engineer's grip, dry and firm — and held it a beat longer than the transaction required.

"Come back next month. The Braga tomatoes should be ready. You'll take a crate to your mother; she always liked them."

"She won't know they're from you."

"She'll eat them anyway," he said. "That's the arrangement with mothers. You feed them things and they pretend not to remember who grew them."

I walked down to the road. Behind me I heard the gate latch click, the padlock rattle home — a sound I had never noticed on previous visits, because previously there had been nothing inside worth locking.

On the train back I unwrapped the shoebox on my lap, right there in public, done with hiding it from strangers if not from family. Forty envelopes, rubber-banded by decade. I untied the oldest bundle and slid out the top letter, postmarked March 1979, and unfolded a single page in my mother's young handwriting — the loops tighter then, angrier, a hand that hadn't yet learned to make itself pleasant.

*Tomás,* it began. *You have been at sea eleven weeks and I have stopped counting, because counting is a form of hope and I am trying to cure myself of it...*

I read it three times before Lisbon came up out of the dark to meet the train, lights strung along the river like a lit fuse, and by the third reading I had stopped seeing the words and started seeing her — twenty-six years old, alone in the house on Rua das Flores, writing to a man who would never read a line of it, while somewhere across the city her brother-in-law opened her envelope, read every word, sealed it again, and burned or kept or buried the evidence according to rules he had invented alone.

There were thirty-nine more letters. One of them, heavier than the rest, sealed in wax, marked in her hand with two words that had waited forty years for me:

*The last.*

I put it back in the box. I did not open it. Not yet.

But I carried it up my stairs that night like a man carrying a lit match through a paper house, and I set it on the table, and I sat across from it, and neither of us slept.
