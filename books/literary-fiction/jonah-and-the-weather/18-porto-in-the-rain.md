## 18. Porto in the Rain

The train north took three hours and eleven minutes, and Ines spent all of them with his hand pressed flat against his coat, over the pocket where the letters rode against his ribs like a second heartbeat. He had wrapped them in a plastic bag first—he was not a romantic, he told himself, he simply did not want them wet—but the gesture fooled no one, least of all himself. A man does not waterproof forty years of another man's silence unless he intends to stand in the rain with them.

He had not slept. The night before, he had sat at Marta's kitchen table—his kitchen table now too, though he still said *Marta's* the way you say *the sea's*, as if it belonged to something larger than you—with the letters spread out in their chronological ranks, reading them again from the beginning. Not for information this time. He knew what they said. He read them the way you reread your own handwriting after a long illness: to confirm that the person who made these marks had existed, had been specific, had wanted things.

Leonor at twenty-six, complaining that Tomás's mother salted everything twice. Leonor at thirty-one, describing Ines's first word—*pão*, bread, of course—and asking Tomás to come home soon, the boy is asking for you by name. Leonor at thirty-eight, the handwriting tightening, the sentences shortening, the accusation arriving in a letter dated the twelfth of March like weather. And then the sealed one, opened now, its single page folded into quarters, the sentence that had detonated his life folded into quarters too, riding in his inside pocket where it had ridden for two days while he decided.

He had decided nothing. That was the truth of it. He had packed a bag, bought a ticket, and told Marta he needed to go to Porto, and she had looked at him across the kitchen with an expression he was learning to read—the expression of a woman who has already forgiven something and is waiting to see if she'll need to—and said only, "Take the umbrella."

It was raining when the train pulled into Campanhã, a fine gray rain that did not fall so much as hang, filling the air between things. Porto rose up around the station in its stacked, soot-colored centuries, and Ines stood on the platform breathing the smell of it—coal smoke and river and wet granite—and thought: *he walked here.* Tomás Quintana, who was supposed to have drowned off the Algarve in 1989, had stepped onto a platform very much like this one, twenty-some years ago, alive, dry or wet, carrying whatever a man carries when he has left his wife and son and brother behind and started over in a city where nobody knew his name. Ines tried to imagine the weight of that bag. He could not. All he could imagine was the weight of his own.

* * *

The cemetery was in the eastern part of the city, past streets whose names he didn't know, and he took a taxi because the rain had thickened and because he wanted, absurdly, to arrive with dry shoes, as if presentation mattered to the dead. The driver talked the whole way about the traffic, the city council, his daughter's wedding, and Ines answered in monosyllables and watched the window run with water until the world outside became a watercolor of itself: walls, cypresses, a hillside of terracotta roofs sliding down toward the river he couldn't see.

The gatekeeper was an old man with a radio playing fado low enough to be almost silence. When Ines asked for the plot—a number, a section, a date—he received a map drawn on graph paper, a pencil line, and directions delivered with the weary precision of a man who gives directions to grief six days a week.

"Section nine," the old man said. "There's no stone yet. Family hasn't put one up." He shrugged, apologetic, as if the absence of a headstone were a service he'd failed to provide. "You'll know it by the plastic flowers. Red ones. Someone comes."

Someone comes. Ines carried those two words with him along the gravel paths, past mausoleums built by shipping families with names that matched the names on wine labels, past rows of modest granite slabs polished by weather into softness. Who came? Amadeo? No—Amadeo had not left Lisbon since Leonor's diagnosis; he tended his tomatoes like a penance and would not abandon them even for this. Some cousin? A woman? Had there been a woman, in those twenty years? The letters stopped in 1989, but a life doesn't stop just because the mail does. Tomás might have had a whole second family here, children, grandchildren, people who called him *pai* without knowing they were borrowing the word.

Ines found section nine at the top of the hill, where the ground leveled out and the wind came off the river plain with nothing to stop it. The red plastic flowers were exactly as promised—garish, sun-bleached to pink at the edges, planted in a tin can half-sunk in the grass—and beside them, a small marble marker flush with the earth, unengraved, blank as an unwritten page.

Tomás Quintana lay under a blank stone.

Ines stood there for a long time without moving. The rain found the back of his neck, his wrists, the seam of his collar. He had rehearsed this on the train—he realized that now, admitted it—he had rehearsed standing here and reading the letters aloud, all of them, or at least the last one, the way you see in films, the way grief is supposed to look when it finally finds its address. He had imagined his voice steady, the words going down through the grass, through the box, into whatever was left of the man who should have heard them in 1979, in 1984, in 1988, when they were addressed to a living man at a living address and were instead sitting in a false-backed wardrobe in a house on Rua das Flores, gathering dust and the particular silence of things deliberately not done.

But standing here, he could not do it. His mouth would not open around the first word. It wasn't stage fright; it was something worse. It was the discovery that the letters were not his to deliver—that they were his mother's voice, her loneliness, her fury, her plea, and that speaking them aloud over this stranger's grave would be a kind of theft, a second interception, him doing to her dead voice what Amadeo had done to her living one. His hand went into his coat and touched the envelope and stayed there, and he stood in the rain like a man holding a door shut.

"You're at the wrong one," said a voice behind him.

* * *

She was perhaps seventy, small, in a green raincoat with the hood up, carrying a plastic bag from which the tops of two chrysanthemums protruded. She had come up the path quietly, or he had been too far inside himself to hear her. She nodded at the blank stone.

"The one you want is two rows down. The fisherman. Big cross, photograph in the middle. Everyone asks, the photograph makes them think—" She stopped, looked at him more carefully. "No. You're not here for the fisherman."

"No," Ines said.

"Then you're at the right one after all." She crouched with the care of someone whose knees had opinions, and began arranging her chrysanthemums in a vase at the neighboring grave, working one-handed, unhurried, a woman performing a ritual so worn-in it required no attention. "Nobody visits this one," she said conversationally, to the flowers or to him. "Forty years I've come to my Rui, and in all that time I've seen maybe five people stop here. Once a young man, once a priest who got lost. Now you." She pressed a stem into place. "You knew him?"

And here was the moment. Ines felt it arrive the way a wave arrives—you see it gathering long before it breaks. Because the honest answer was *no, I never met him, he was my father for the purposes of documents and my mother's husband for the purposes of letters and neither of us ever actually occupied the role.* The easy answer, the answer available to any mourner at any grave in Portugal, was simply *yes*. Yes, my father. The woman would nod, satisfied, and go back to her chrysanthemums, and the lie would cost nothing, would weigh nothing, would dissolve into the rain like all the other merciful fictions this family had been marinating in for four decades. He had eaten that fiction his whole life. He knew its flavor perfectly—it tasted of nothing, and it went down smooth.

"Yes," he said. Then, before the word finished landing, before she could nod and turn away and let him keep it: "No. That's—I don't know how to say it correctly. He was married to my mother. He was the man I was told was my father. He died when I was nineteen, I was told, at sea, and it wasn't true, he lived twenty years here in Porto and I'm finding all of this out in the last month, and I've brought letters my mother wrote him that he never received, and I don't actually know why I'm telling you this except that you asked, and I've been lying so long that when someone asks me a simple question I apparently can't help myself anymore."

The rain filled the pause. The old woman straightened up slowly, one hand on her back, and studied him—not with pity, he noticed, and not with alarm, but with the frank appraisal of someone who has buried a husband and outlived most of her friends and no longer has time to be shocked by anything.

"Well," she said. "That's more than anyone's told me at this cemetery in forty years."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize for it. It was refreshing." She looked down at the blank stone, then back at him. "So he's the one who never got the letters."

Ines stared at her. "How did—"

"You said it the way people say things when they've been carrying them a long distance." She picked up her empty bag, folded it with small precise movements. "My Rui was a quiet man too. You find out afterward all the things they weren't saying. There's always a drawer somewhere." She patted his arm, briefly, her glove cold through his soaked sleeve. "Read them or don't. Bury them or burn them. But decide before you leave the cemetery, menina. This is not a place to carry things home from twice."

She gathered her bag, gave the blank stone a last glance—almost, Ines thought, a look of professional respect, one silent tenant acknowledging another—and went down the path under her green hood, her footsteps fading into the sound of rain on gravel.

* * *

He stood alone at the plot for another hour, maybe longer; the light went from gray to darker gray and the lamps along the paths came on one by one, yellow smudges in the wet air. He took the letters out. The plastic bag had kept them dry, mostly; the oldest envelope had gone soft at one corner, the postmark of 1979 bleeding faintly, Lisbon's name dissolving into the paper.

He tried again to read them aloud. He got as far as *Meu Tomás*—her opening, every letter, across four decades, *meu Tomás*, my Tomás, possessive and pleading and furious by turns—and then his voice closed like a hand. He stood with the page trembling slightly in the rain and understood, finally, what the old woman's instruction meant. *Decide before you leave.*

Not whether to speak. Whether to let go.

He knelt—the mud took his knees without complaint—and dug with his hands at the edge of the plot, where the grass grew thin over the turned earth of twenty years ago. It was easier than he expected. The ground here was used to being opened. He laid the letters in, all of them, the whole chronology, the courtship and the marriage and the accusation and the plea, and last the sealed one, opened, its single terrible sentence facing down into the dark. He did not cover them with ceremony. He pushed the earth back with his palms, pressed the grass down flat, and sat back on his heels, soaked through, empty-handed, his fingers black with Porto dirt.

"Send them," his mother had said, on her last clear day, reading her own words aloud like a stranger discovering a braver sister. *I was braver than you let me be.*

They were sent now. Late by forty years, undeliverable, unread by the man they named—but in the ground, at least, at the address the state had assigned him, under the blank stone and the red plastic flowers that someone, some ghost of a life Ines would never map, had thought to bring.

His phone was wet and his hands were shaking, but he dialed anyway, holding the phone against his ear inside his hood. Amadeo answered on the fourth ring, and Ines could hear the television behind him, the evening news, the ordinary machinery of a protected life.

"It's done," Ines said. "I buried them. At the grave. In Porto."

The silence on the line went on so long that Ines checked whether the call had dropped. Rain ticked against the phone. Somewhere below the hill, the city was lighting up against the dark, windows coming on like a slow constellation.

"He was there," Amadeo said at last, and his voice was strange—flattened, careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he'd been assured was solid. "All that time, he was a train ride away."

"Yes."

Another silence. Then, quieter, almost to himself: "I used to tell myself he was at the bottom of the ocean. It was easier to guard the letters if he was at the bottom of the ocean." A breath. "Ines. There's something else. About why I really kept them. What I told you at the tomatoes—that wasn't all of it."

The wind came up the hill and moved through the cypresses, and Ines, kneeling in the mud with his mother's forty years pressed into the earth beneath his hands, waited for his uncle to say the rest.
