## 5. Room Seven

The bus from the junction town was a school bus in a former life, its seats reupholstered in a vinyl that smelled of sun and old gum. Jude sat in the back with her duffel on her lap like a shield and watched Salt Hollow assemble itself out of fog.

It came in pieces. First the water — a gray so total it looked poured rather than born, no horizon, just a soft seam where sea gave up pretending to be sky. Then a breakwater made of black rock, then a row of leaning boats, then the roofs: shingle gone silver, chimneys crooked as fingers asking questions. A church steeple. A water tower with HOLLOW painted on it and the S long since peeled away, so the town announced itself as TOLL HOLLOW to anyone arriving by air, which was nobody.

The driver didn't announce the stop. He just slowed at a bend where a gravel lot met a wooden sign — WELCOME TO SALT HOLLOW, POP. 1,204, EST. 1889, SOMEONE HAS CROSSED OUT THE NUMBER AND WRITTEN 1,198 IN PENCIL — and looked at her in the big rearview mirror until she stood up.

"You'll want the Marigold," he said. Not a question. "Only place taking guests."

"The Marigold?"

"Boarding house. Up the hill, past the cannery that isn't a cannery anymore." He opened the door with a hiss. "Tell Marisol Walt dropped you."

"I don't know a Marisol."

"You will." And that was all, as if the sentence were complete, as if names in this town arrived pre-installed.

She stepped down onto gravel and the bus pulled away trailing diesel and the faint ghost of children, and Jude Galloway stood alone in a town she had never seen but had, apparently, been expected by.

* * *

The air hit her first. That was the thing about the coast nobody warned you about when you lived inland behind double glazing — the air had texture. It was wet and mineral and it carried salt the way a letter carries a fingerprint. She breathed it in and waited for something.

Nothing. Her tongue reported nothing. Three years now, and her mouth remained a beautifully appointed room that no one lived in. She could describe this air perfectly — brine, kelp, cold stone, a sweetness underneath like rotting apples in an orchard — she could write four hundred words on it before lunch and some editor would call it luminous. But taste it? Taste required a self that received things, and hers had been returned to sender.

She walked up the hill past the cannery-that-wasn't, which was now something called The Net Loft selling driftwood art to no one, its windows full of painted buoys and a handwritten sign: OPEN MOST DAYS. Past a hardware store whose window display hadn't changed since perhaps 1997. Past two men mending a net on a porch who watched her the way gulls watch a hand holding bread — not aggressive, just accounting.

The Marigold stood at the top of the hill where the road gave up and became a garden path. It was three stories of weatherboard the color of weak tea, with a wraparound porch sagging gently at one corner like a tired hip, and a garden gone gloriously feral around it — roses escaped their beds and climbing into a hawthorn, lavender leggy and bee-drunk, and along one side a wall, old stone, higher than the fence line, enclosing whatever lay behind the house. A door in that wall, painted green many coats ago, was padlocked.

Above the porch hung a sign: THE MARIGOLD — ROOMS & BOARD. Below it, smaller, newer, in letters that had been added with obvious reluctance: **CLOSING SOON**.

Jude stood on the gravel and looked up at the house her brother had mailed postcards from for eleven years.

Postcards from Lisbon. From Prague. From a fishing village in Norway called Reine that Danny described so lovingly she'd once looked up flights there, wanting to surprise him, finding only fjords and tourist cabins and no trace of any boarding house or any brother. Postcards from New Orleans, from Kyoto, from a lighthouse on Cape Breton Island. Eleven years of addresses that led nowhere, signed *Love, D*, each one stamped from a different country, each one describing — she'd never noticed this until last week, laying them out on her kitchen floor at 3 a.m. like evidence at a trial — food. Always food. *The sardines here, Jude, they grill them over vine cuttings.* *Found a woman who makes dumplings the size of your fist.* *You would love the bread. You would marry the bread.*

Her brother had eaten his way across the world without ever leaving, apparently, this hill.

And now she was standing where the postmarks pointed, and the house wore a demolition notice taped inside its front window like a diagnosis.

She picked up her duffel. Climbed the steps. The boards announced her, board by board, to whoever was listening.

* * *

The door was open. Inside, a hallway ran the length of the house, papered in a pattern of faded wisteria, lit by a window at the far end that turned the dust into slow gold weather. There was a smell here too — beeswax, old wood, something cooking with lemon — and under it, faintly, the sea.

A woman came through a doorway at the end carrying a stack of folded towels, and stopped when she saw her.

She was maybe sixty, small and solid, gray hair cut short and practical, sleeves rolled over forearms that had done decades of work. Her eyes did something Jude had learned to dread in restaurants: they moved over her face once, quickly, the way a sommelier reads a label, and then settled somewhere around her mouth and stayed there.

"You'll be wanting a room," the woman said.

"Walt dropped me," Jude said, because it seemed to be the password. "From the bus."

"Walt drops everyone." The woman set the towels on a hall table with the economy of someone who wasted no motion twice. "How long?"

"A few weeks." Jude had rehearsed this part on the train, staring at her reflection in the dark window until her own face became a stranger's, which helped. "Maybe longer. I'm writing something."

"What are you writing?"

"A history." True enough. Everything was a history; most of it was just badly sourced. "Local."

"There hasn't been local history worth the name since the cannery closed." The woman wiped her hands on her apron though they were clean. "Marisol Vega. I keep the house."

"Fiona Marsh." The name had come to her on the train fully formed, borrowed from a girl she'd known at nineteen who once told her she had a face people forgot on purpose. It seemed useful now.

Marisol nodded slowly, and Jude understood that the nod meant *I have decided to accept this for today.*

"Room Seven's free," Marisol said. "Front of the house, top floor. Best light in the building and the worst plumbing. You'll hear the pipes talk at night. They're lying, mostly. Ignore them."

"That's fine."

"It's forty a week, cash or bank transfer, breakfast included if you come down before nine, which you won't, writers never do." She was already moving toward the stairs, talking over her shoulder, and Jude followed because following Marisol appeared to be what the house was for. "Bathroom's at the end of the hall. Towels are the ones on your bed, don't take the ones in the linen closet, those are counted. There's a kettle. The wifi is named MARIGOLD and the password is the year my mother was born, which I'll write down for you because you won't guess it."

At the second-floor landing Marisol paused. On the wall beside her hung photographs — Jude had registered them peripherally on entering, a hallway gallery, frames of varying ages — and Marisol stood now with her back half-turned to them, deliberately, the way you stand between someone and a window.

"Room Seven," she said again, softer. "My mother used to say it was the room people came back to."

"People who stayed here before?"

"Mm." Marisol climbed the next flight. "Different name, same face. That happens in houses like this."

Jude's hand tightened on the banister. "I'm sorry?"

"Nothing." They reached the third floor. Marisol unlocked a door with a key from a ring heavy enough to anchor a boat and handed it over, and her eyes did the thing again — the quick read, the settling near Jude's mouth — and this time she held it a beat too long before looking away. "Towels are on the bed. Breakfast is eggs, it's always eggs, don't ask for alternatives."

She went back down the stairs, and the house exhaled around Jude, and she stood in the doorway of Room Seven.

* * *

It was a small room with a sloped ceiling and a window seat built for someone smaller than any modern person, overlooking the front garden and, beyond it, the whole stacked panorama of the town falling toward the water. A single bed with a quilt the color of faded marigolds — hence, presumably, everything. A desk under the window, scarred with rings and initials. A wardrobe that smelled of cedar and camphor and time. On the nightstand, a lamp with a frayed cord and, beside it, a small dish of wrapped peppermints gone stale in their cellophane, the kind of hospitality that predates the concept of checking dates.

She set down the duffel. Sat on the bed. The mattress accepted her with a sigh of springs, and through the window the fog was lifting off the harbor in rags, and boats she couldn't name knocked gently against a dock she couldn't name, and somewhere below a gull screamed its one note, endlessly, like a critic with a single word in its vocabulary.

Danny had slept in this room.

Or a room like it. Or no room at all. That was the wound of it — she didn't know, and every fact she owned was a postcard's worth of fact, pretty on the front, blank on the back except for a signature. He had written to her from this hill for eleven years while she ate tasting menus in cities he'd pretended to visit, and she had believed him, because believing him was easier than admitting he'd vanished into a life she wasn't allowed to see. Because the alternative question — *why would my brother lie to me about where he lived for a decade?* — had a shape she couldn't bear to hold.

He left the day after the thing happened. The day after the fraud, the arrest warrant with her father's name on it, the family imploding in the kitchen of a house in a suburb that no longer existed for them. Danny was twenty-two. He'd said, *I have to go take care of something,* and hugged her hard enough to crack a rib, and she'd tasted nothing at dinner that night, and nothing the next night, and nothing ever again.

Three weeks later, the first postcard. Lisbon. *The sardines here, Jude.*

She got up and unpacked with unnecessary precision — clothes in the wardrobe, toiletries in the bathroom down the hall, laptop and notebook on the desk, the shoebox of postcards placed dead center like a reliquary. Then she took off her shoes and walked the hallway.

The photographs.

She'd felt them watching from the landing, and now she gave them her attention frame by frame, walking slowly, hands clasped behind her back like a visitor in a museum of someone else's life. Fishing crews squinting into sun. A confirmation class outside the church. A harvest dance, everyone mid-motion, blurred with joy. Children on the harbor wall, legs dangling. Ordinary towns, ordinary decades, the sediment of a community that photographed itself because it intended to still be here later.

And in the fourth frame — a group shot on these very steps, summer light, maybe ten years old judging by the film stock — a young man stood slightly apart from the others, laughing at something outside the frame, head tipped back, one hand raised as if conducting music only he could hear.

Danny.

Not a resemblance. Not a cousin, not a trick of light. Danny, twenty-five, twenty-six, laughing on the steps of the Marigold Boarding House in Salt Hollow, wearing a flannel shirt she had given him for Christmas the year before he disappeared.

Jude put her finger against the glass. The glass was cool. Her brother smiled behind it, at something she would never see, in a life she had never been shown, and the hallway smelled of beeswax and lemons, and downstairs a pipe began to knock as if keeping time.

Every photograph in this hallway, she realized, was a year. This was an archive. This was a town's memory of itself, and her brother was in it — not once, she saw now, looking harder, stepping closer — not once but again and again, at the edge of frames, in the back rows, holding a guitar at the harvest dance, hauling a trap on a boat, kneeling among children with instruments, always slightly apart, always laughing, a man embedded in a place like a splinter the body had grown around.

Eleven years of postcards from nowhere. A decade of Sundays in these photographs.

She heard footsteps on the stairs below and stepped back from the wall, heart going hard, guilty of nothing and everything. Marisol appeared at the landing with a stack of firewood logs in her arms, and stopped, and looked at Jude standing there in her stocking feet in front of the photographs, and something moved across her face — recognition, or its rehearsal — and was filed away.

"Dinner's at seven," Marisol said. "There's soup."

"Thank you."

"And Fiona." Marisol shifted the logs against her hip. Her gaze came to rest, finally, unmistakably, on Jude's mouth. "Your brother had that same way of standing very still when he was trying not to feel something."

The stairwell went quiet around the words. Somewhere below, the pipes knocked twice and stopped.

"I don't have a brother," Jude said.

"No," Marisol agreed pleasantly, already descending. "Of course you don't. Seven o'clock. The soup has lemon in it — you should try it while it's hot."

Jude stood alone on the landing with the photographs smiling around her and the demolition notice yellowing in the front window and six weeks, she remembered suddenly, six weeks minus one day, ticking somewhere under the floorboards like a second set of pipes.

Downstairs, a pot lid clattered. The smell of lemon rose through the old house, patient as a summons.

She did not go down. Not yet. She stood in the hallway of her brother's secret life, in front of his laughing face, and listened to a landlady who knew too much make soup in a kitchen where, tomorrow, she would begin asking questions — starting with why the woman in the fourth photograph kept looking, decade after decade, like a man who was supposed to be dead.
