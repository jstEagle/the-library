## 22. Mother Comes to Town

She arrives on a Tuesday, which is somehow worse than if she'd arrived on a Sunday. Sundays are for mothers. Tuesdays are for business.

Jude sees the rental car from the window of Room Seven — a silver sedan too clean for Salt Hollow, parked at an angle that suggests the driver had been circling the block, working up her nerve. Her mother gets out in stages, one hand on the roof of the car like it might buck her off. Even from three floors up Jude can see the outfit: camel coat, silk scarf, the armor of a woman who has spent sixty years dressing for battles she insists she isn't fighting.

She hasn't called ahead. Of course she hasn't. Calling ahead would mean admitting this visit was planned, and admitting it was planned would mean admitting it was necessary.

Jude goes down before her mother can knock. She wants the advantage of the doorway. It's petty and she knows it and she does it anyway.

"Margaret," she says, using the name her mother uses on strangers, the one that keeps everyone at the distance of a first introduction.

Her mother flinches almost imperceptibly. "Theodora."

Nobody has called her Theodora since she was nine years old and insisted on Jude, after the Beatles song Danny played until the needle wore through. Her mother knows this. The name is a weapon, small and precise, and she has drawn it within four seconds of arrival.

"You drove six hours," Jude says.

"I flew into Portland and drove two." Her mother looks past her into the boarding house, at the water-stained wallpaper, the runner worn gray down its center. Her lip does something complicated. Disgust, maybe. Or grief wearing disgust's coat. "This is where you've been."

"This is where I've been."

"There's a smell."

"There's a house."

They stand in the hallway beneath the framed photographs — Danny laughing on the dock with a fish he clearly didn't catch, Danny at some town fair holding a pie as if it were a trophy, Danny mid-song with his eyes closed and a circle of children around him. Her mother's gaze touches each frame and slides off, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth and refuses to press.

"We should talk somewhere private," her mother says.

"It's a boarding house. Privacy is a myth here. Marisol makes soup in the kitchen and everyone pretends not to listen."

"Theodora—"

"Jude."

"Fine." Her mother sets her jaw, and for a moment Jude is looking at a younger woman, the one from the photographs in the Connecticut house, the one who stood at the stove every night of Jude's childhood stirring things she never tasted either. "Then we'll talk here. But you will sit down, because I'm not doing this standing up in a hallway full of dead man's pictures."

*Dead man's pictures.* Jude feels the words land somewhere below her ribs, in the place where taste used to live.

They end up in the front parlor, a room Marisol clearly rarely opens — dust-sheeted furniture, a piano nobody plays, windows fogged with salt bloom. Her mother removes a single dust sheet with two fingers, examines the chair beneath it, and sits anyway. Jude stays standing. Let one of them be uncomfortable honestly.

"I spoke to Whitfield's office," her mother says. Whitfield. The developer. She says the name the way you say your dentist's. "You've been making calls. Asking questions about the deed history."

"I've been asking about my family's house."

"*My* house," her mother corrects, and then catches herself, and the catching is more damning than the slip. "The estate's house. There's a difference."

"Is there? Because I found two sales documents. Same parcel. Two different buyers, fourteen months apart. Your signature on both."

Her mother's hands fold in her lap, perfectly composed, knuckles white. "That's a legal matter between the estate and—"

"Don't." Jude's voice comes out lower than she intends. "Don't do the voice. Not the lawyer voice, not the hostess voice, not the *we'll-discuss-this-at-Christmas* voice. I have spent three years listening to you say everything except what happened, and I am done. I am so far done, Mother, that I'm living in a room with a mattress that sags in the middle and a radiator that sounds like someone dying, because at least this house doesn't lie to me."

Silence. Outside, gulls argue over something on the roof. Down the hall, faintly, Marisol's radio murmurs.

Her mother looks at the photographs through the parlor door. For a long time she says nothing, and Jude lets the nothing stretch, because she has learned — from postcards, from a song on a midnight frequency, from a chef who feeds dockworkers with steady hands — that what people do in silence is the truest thing they ever do.

Finally her mother says, "Your father wasn't careful."

It is not an apology. It is not even an admission, exactly. It is a door opened one inch, and Jude walks through it before it can close.

"I know about the accounts," Jude says carefully. "I found the letters from the bank. After the funeral. In the box with his medals."

"You found the letters." Her mother laughs, and there is no humor in it, only exhaustion so old it has fossilized. "Of course you did. You were always the one who read everything. Danny never read anything. That was the whole—" She stops. Recalibrates. "Do you know what your father was doing, those last two years? Do you actually know, or do you have the version we gave you?"

"I have the version we gave you," Jude says. "The version where he made some bad investments and died of a heart attack and we sold the house in Connecticut and moved on."

"Mm." Her mother studies her own hands. "He was moving money. Client money. Into accounts with his name attached, then out again, then back, covering holes with holes. He was good at it for a long time. He was good at everything for a long time." A pause. "And then the fund auditors scheduled a review, and he had eleven days, and he came to me at the kitchen table — you remember that kitchen table, the one with the wobble — and he said, *someone has to be the fall.*"

Jude's mouth has gone dry. "Danny was nineteen."

"Danny was nineteen," her mother agrees, "and Danny said yes before your father finished the sentence."

The room tilts slightly. Jude grips the back of a chair. Under her palm, through the dust sheet, the wood is cool and real.

"That's not possible. Danny didn't even— he wasn't involved in the finances. He was playing open mics. He was writing songs in the garage. He barely—"

"He signed papers he didn't read. He took the interviews. He sat across from the investigators with that face of his, that honest face, and he told them the transfers were his idea, his accounts, his scheme. He was very convincing." Her mother's voice frays at the edges now, the composure finally showing its seams. "Because he believed it, by the end. Or he decided to believe it. He said — God, I can still hear him — he said, *if it's mine, then it ends with me, and Mom and Jude are clean.*"

"And Dad let him."

"Your father was dying by then. Not the heart attack — that was later, that was almost a mercy. Dying the way men like him die, which is slowly, in the ego, in the fear. And your brother looked at him across that wobbly table and offered himself up like it was a casserole he'd brought to a potluck." Her mother's hands have come apart; one presses flat against her sternum as if holding something in. "Nineteen years old. And I sat there and I let him. I poured the coffee and I let him."

"Why?" The word comes out cracked. "Why would he— for us? Dad destroyed things, Mom. Dad lied for a living. Why would Danny—"

"Because you were twenty-two and about to start your life, and a fraud charge in the family would have ended it before it began. Because I—" Her mother stops. Starts again, quieter. "Because I asked him to. Not in those words. I never used those words. I cried at that table and I said, *there has to be another way,* and I looked at Danny when I said it, and he understood. He always understood me. That was the curse of him. He understood all of us better than we deserved."

The parlor is very quiet. Jude becomes aware that she is shaking, a fine tremor starting in her hands and climbing her arms.

"So the record," she manages. "The public record. The reason his name was— the reason he couldn't come home, the reason he mailed postcards instead—"

"Was a fiction he built to protect you. Brick by brick. Fifteen years of bricks." Her mother's eyes are wet but her voice is steady, terrible, controlled. "And I maintained it. Every Thanksgiving, every phone call, every time you asked why he wouldn't come to Christmas — I maintained it. I told you he was selfish. I told you he'd chosen that life over us. I let you hate him a little, because hating him was easier for you than missing him, and easier for me than confessing."

"I don't hate him." Jude's voice breaks clean in half. "I never hated him. I wrote him letters I never sent. I kept every postcard. I— Mom, I lost my *taste.* Three years ago, the day we got the call, everything went to cardboard. Do you understand what I'm telling you? My body knew before I did. My body has been grieving him for three years while you let me think he was a stranger."

Her mother stands then, abruptly, and goes to the window. The harbor below is gray and patient. When she speaks again, her back is turned, and that is its own confession — she cannot say the next part facing her daughter.

"When Whitfield came to me, fourteen months ago, he already knew. All of it. He'd bought the debt, he'd traced the old case, he'd found the sealed records. He's a man who buys other people's buried things." Her hand rises to the window glass, rests there. "He offered me a number for the land. A good number. And he offered me something else, something worth more than money. He offered me the demolition. Bulldozers. A hole in the ground where this house stands. And he said, *Mrs. Galloway, once it's gone, there's nothing left to find.*"

Jude's breath stops.

"No records in the town archive worth pulling, no landlord around to answer questions, no neighbors with long memories. Just condos on a clean lot. He wasn't buying land, Theodora. He was buying a grave. And I signed." She turns. Her face is wrecked and composed simultaneously, the two expressions occupying it like tenants who despise each other. "I signed because Danny built his whole sacrifice on the idea that it would stay buried. And I thought — if the house falls, it stays buried. If the house falls, his gift holds. If the house falls, I never have to sit across from my daughter in a dusty parlor and tell her that her brother went to prison-shaped ruin at nineteen so she could become whoever she became."

"But I found it anyway."

"You were always going to find it. You read everything." Something moves across her mother's face that might, in a different life, in a different family, have been pride. "I signed the second sale because I thought burying it twice would work. It didn't. Nothing works. That's the lesson of my entire life, Theodora. I have been maintaining a lie for fifteen years and it has cost me my son, my husband, and now—" she gestures at Jude, at the room, at the whole crumbling house, "—now it's costing me you."

"It cost Danny everything."

"Yes."

"He died alone. In a rented room. With a name that wasn't—"

"He died free," her mother says fiercely, and then hears herself, and covers her mouth with her hand. "No. No, that's the thing I tell myself. That's the story I need. I don't know if he died free. I don't know anything about how he lived, because I let him go and told myself it was mercy. You want to talk about lies? I have a lifetime supply. Ask me anything."

For a moment neither of them moves. Then Jude crosses the parlor, and she doesn't embrace her mother — she isn't there yet, may never be there — but she takes her mother's hand off her mouth and holds it, the way you hold something broken that you're not sure can be fixed but refuse to drop.

"The hearing failed," Jude says quietly. "You know that. The law won't stop the bulldozers."

"I know."

"But the truth might. Whitfield bought your silence and your signature. Both of those are worth less today than they were an hour ago."

Her mother looks at their joined hands. When she speaks, her voice is small in a way Jude has never heard it, stripped of every voice she has ever performed. "What are you going to do?"

"What Danny would have done," Jude says. "Tell it myself, before someone else sells it."

Her mother pulls her hand back — not in anger, Jude thinks, but because holding on was becoming unbearable. She retrieves her coat from the chair. At the parlor door she stops, her back still turned, and says:

"There's a third document. In the safe deposit box at First Coastal. I never signed it. I never could." A pause, long enough for a wave to break somewhere below. "It's his handwriting, Theodora. All of it. Everything he carried, written down, dated, signed. He left it with the bank the year before he died, and the instructions say it goes to whoever asks the right question." She turns her head, just enough. "I've been asking the wrong question for fifteen years. Maybe you won't."

Then she is gone, heels on the worn runner, the front door opening onto salt air, and Jude stands alone in the parlor with the dust settling around her like slow snow, and outside, faintly, from Deacon's van parked at the curb, the song begins to play again — and this time she hears the second verse coming, and runs for the door.
