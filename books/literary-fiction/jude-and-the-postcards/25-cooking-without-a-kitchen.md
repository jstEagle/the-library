## 25. Cooking Without a Kitchen

Cyrus Okafor had cooked in worse places than a stretch of cold sand above the tideline — he'd cooked in a walk-in freezer when the line went down, on a hot plate in a motel room in Reno during the worst year of his life, once, memorably, over a camp stove at his own wedding rehearsal because the caterer quit and his pride wouldn't let him order pizza. But he had never cooked for a town that was watching him cook for itself.

That was the difference. That was the thing sitting in his chest like a stone swallowed wrong.

He'd told Marisol no twice. The first time she'd asked, three days after the town meeting, he'd said the truck was enough, the truck was what he had, and he wasn't going to pretend a fire pit on the beach was a kitchen. The second time she'd brought him a crate.

"From the garden," she said, setting it on the counter of the food truck without asking permission. "What's still standing back there. Nobody's picked it since Danny."

He looked down at it. Tomatoes gone soft and split, some of them, their skins crazed like old paint. A tangle of chard gone leggy and bitter-looking. Fennel that had bolted into feathery yellow crowns. Herbs gone half to seed. Squash, two of them, enormous and pale as driftwood.

"It's not produce," Cyrus said. "It's archaeology."

"It's dinner," Marisol said. "Friday. On the sand below the wall. People are coming whether you cook or not, so you might as well be the one feeding them." She was already walking away. She paused, didn't turn around. "He'd have liked that. You two, side by side."

Cyrus stood there with the crate between his hands and felt something shift in him that he did not want to name, because naming it would mean admitting it was about more than tomatoes.

* * *

The truth — the truth he kept in the locked drawer under the register with the laminated review — was that Cyrus had stopped cooking for people a long time ago. He cooked *at* them. He fed dockworkers at six a.m. because they were hungry and he was awake and neither of them needed to talk about it. He made one perfect fish stew and sold it out by nine and drove home to a rented room above the hardware store and slept like a man who had put his whole life in storage.

Ten years ago he had cooked *for* people. There was a difference and the difference had a name and the name was Meridian, and after the review ran, the difference died.

So when he found himself, Thursday afternoon, kneeling in the dirt behind the boarding house with a pair of scissors and a colander, cutting chard stems at an angle like some kind of apprentice, he understood that something in him had already said yes before his mouth ever did.

The garden was a ruin and not a ruin. That was the strange part. Left alone for years, it should have been a jungle, but the wall held the wind off and the soil was old and deep, black and sweet-smelling, and things had simply kept going the way stubborn things do. The tomato plants had reseeded themselves into thickets. The fennel had taken over an entire corner like it was annexing territory. There were strawberries running wild along the base of the south wall, small and hard and dark as garnets.

And there were other things. Things planted on purpose, recently. A row of lettuces, maybe six weeks old. Bean seedlings climbing twine someone had strung with real care. A young fig tree in a half barrel, staked and mulched.

Cyrus crouched next to the beans and looked at the twine and knew, without being told, whose work this was. He'd seen her hands. He'd watched them move over his counter, quick and precise, arranging nothing, touching nothing, pretending.

She'd been coming back here. Planting. Tending a dead man's garden in secret, the way you light candles in a church you claim not to believe in.

He cut a head of lettuce, wrapped it in damp newspaper, and said nothing to anyone, including himself, about why that made his throat tight.

* * *

Friday came in gray and clean, the tide low, the sand packed hard enough to hold a table. By four o'clock there were folding tables appearing along the dune grass like mushrooms after rain — Marisol's doing, obviously; the woman had the organizational instincts of a field marshal and the subtlety of one too. By five there were forty people. By six there were eighty, and someone had built a fire pit out of rounded beach stones, and Deacon's van was parked up on the road with its antenna up, broadcasting to whoever was still listening at that hour, which apparently was everyone, because every few minutes somebody's phone would play a snatch of the song and somebody else would shush them even though the song wasn't playing anywhere except inside the van and inside all of them.

Cyrus worked.

He worked the way he always worked, which was to say he disappeared into it. Two stockpots borrowed from the church hall, set in the coals. One for the stew base — fennel, onion from Marisol's cellar, the last of the season's garlic, tomatoes roasted in a cast-iron pan directly on the stones until their skins blackened and split and gave up their sweetness. The second pot for the greens: chard blanched fast, shocked cold, dressed with lemon and oil and a fistful of the wild herbs chopped fine.

Fish came off a boat at five-thirty — a kid named Petey whose father Cyrus had been feeding for two years, hauling a cooler down the sand without being asked, grinning like he'd been waiting his whole life for this exact errand. Rockfish and a couple of lingcod, gutted and iced. Cyrus filleted them on a plastic cutting board balanced across two crates, and the crowd went quiet the way crowds do when a knife is doing honest work, and he felt the old thing stir in him, the thing that used to be pride before it got renamed.

He didn't fight it. That was new. For ten years he'd fought it every time it stirred, shoved it back down with the lid of routine. Tonight he just let it sit there in his chest, warm and dangerous, and kept working.

"You're going to need more pots," said a voice beside him.

He didn't look up right away. He knew the voice now. He'd known it for weeks, actually — since long before the customer in the truck said the name *Jude Galloway* and the floor of his world tilted three degrees and settled. He'd known the voice from the first night she'd stood at his window and lied to his face about the stew, and some part of him, some part he hadn't consulted, had known even then.

"I'm going to need a kitchen," he said, filleting. "This is a beach."

"So was the first meal anyone ever cooked." She crouched, picked up one of the roasted tomatoes, turned it in her fingers. "Can I—"

"You can wash your hands and get out of my way or wash your hands and be useful. Those are the options."

Something crossed her face — surprise, maybe, or relief. He pointed her to a jug of water and a bar of soap Marisol had staged on a crate, and while she washed he watched her hands again, and this time he let himself think the thought all the way through: *she planted those beans.*

"Greens are done," he said. "Toss them with this." He handed her the dressing. "All the way to the bottom of the pot. People who toss greens halfway are cowards."

"That seems like a strong position."

"It's the only position."

And then they were working, side by side, on a beach, in front of a town, and Cyrus Okafor — who had spent ten years making sure no one stood beside him at a counter, because the last person who did had read a review aloud over breakfast and called it fair — let it happen.

* * *

The stew went out in enamel bowls and tin plates and, when those ran out, coffee mugs, and nobody complained. The fish went out charred from a grill grate laid over stones. Bread appeared from somewhere — the bakery, the church, Mrs. Adeyemi's oven, all three probably. The line moved slow and nobody minded, because the line was the point. The line was the town standing in the cold saying *we are here, we are many, and the machines cannot come until Monday anyway.*

Cyrus heard fragments as he worked the fire. A harbor committee man telling anyone who'd listen that the hearing was lost but the war wasn't. Two women arguing cheerfully about whether the garden should grow corn. A teenager explaining the demolition schedule to her grandmother with the grim precision of someone who'd memorized it. And underneath everything, always, the song — hummed, whistled, played tinny from phones, sung badly by the kids who'd learned it from a man none of them could remember clearly anymore and all of them refused to forget.

Danny Galloway. Fifteen years dead, and the whole town sang his song like he'd left last week.

Cyrus had never met him. He'd arrived in Salt Hollow three years after the funeral nobody local seemed to talk about directly. But he knew the shape of the man now the way you know the shape of a room from its echo. The tape labeled *for whoever needs it.* The postcards Marisol read aloud over the radio until the developer's lawyers started writing letters. The garden. The debts paid anonymous. The kids taught songs on this same beach, probably, on this same cold sand.

A man who gave everything away and stayed anyway. Cyrus had given nothing away and run. Between those two facts lay ten years he didn't like to count.

"You're staring at the fire," Jude said.

"I'm managing the fire."

"You've been managing the fire for four minutes and it doesn't need managing."

"Everything needs managing. That's the first law of kitchens."

"What's the second?"

"The second law is that the person who asks the most questions gets put on dish duty."

She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a real expression he'd seen on her face since the night she lied to him about the stew, and he hated how much he noticed it, how much space it took up in his attention, this woman who had done what she'd done. He turned back to the pots.

But here was the thing he couldn't get around, the thing that had been eating at him since the customer said her name: he had kept the review laminated for ten years, and keeping it had felt like justice, and justice was supposed to feel like armor, and lately it just felt like a weight he carried to prove he'd been hurt. And she was here. She had come to the town where the man she loved had lived and died, and she was planting beans in his garden and washing her hands at his request and tossing greens all the way to the bottom of the pot, and Cyrus could not find, anywhere in himself, the clean bright anger that had kept him warm for a decade.

It was infuriating. It was like losing a limb and being handed a hand.

"Taste this," he said, before he could stop himself.

He held out the spoon. The stew, nearly finished — fennel gone silk, tomatoes collapsed into depth, the broth salted with seawater he'd carried up in a bucket because the garden had grown beside the ocean for fifteen years and it seemed right that the ocean finish the job.

She took the spoon. And he watched her face, because he was a cruel man in exactly one way and it was this: he always watched.

Her lips parted. The spoon went in. And her face did the thing it always did — closed, smoothed, went politely blank, the face of a woman reading a menu in a language she didn't speak. He'd seen it a hundred times at his window. The performance of tasting. The lie of pleasure delivered with professional warmth.

Except.

At the very end — a flicker. Small as a pilot light catching. Her eyes lost focus for half a second, and her breath caught, and her hand holding the spoon trembled once, visibly, before she steadied it.

"There," she said quietly. Not *it's good.* Not the lie. Just: *there.*

"What's there?" he asked.

She looked at the pot. Then at the sea. Then at him, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Fennel," she said. "I could almost—" She stopped. Started again. "My brother used to write me about food. Postcards. Every one of them was a meal I never ate. I thought they were jokes. They weren't jokes." She handed the spoon back with both hands, careful, like returning something borrowed from a stranger. "For about one second, that tasted like a postcard."

Cyrus took the spoon. His own chest was doing something complicated that he filed under *smoke inhalation.*

"One second is how it starts," he said. "Ask any fire."

"That's not a real proverb."

"No. But it's true." He ladled another bowl and pushed it into her hands. "Eat. Standing up, out of the way, like staff. If the town sees the critic eating, they'll want reviews."

The words were out before he could catch them — *the critic* — and the air between them changed temperature instantly, the way a door opening in winter changes a room. She went still. He went stiller. Eighty people laughed and sang and scraped bowls twenty feet away and the two of them stood in a silence the size of ten years.

"You know," she said. Not a question.

"I've known," he said, "since the third week."

"The stew. The first night."

"You lied beautifully. Very restrained. Your editor would have been proud." He heard his own voice go flat and cold and recognized it as the voice he'd used on every health inspector and supplier and ex-wife-adjacent person who'd tried to handle him. "I kept waiting to feel something when I finally said it out loud. Revenge, closure, whatever they promise you in movies. I've had the sentence loaded for weeks. *I know who you are.* Three words, ten years' interest."

"And?"

"And you're tossing greens correctly," he said, "which is ruining everything."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been through so much. She looked down at the bowl in her hands, steam rising into the gray air, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped all its professionalism.

"Cyrus. I'm going to fix it. The review. Publicly, fully, with my name on it. I don't know yet if it saves you or buries you again and I've been unable to sleep deciding, and I'm not asking you to tell me tonight. I'm just telling you it's coming, so it doesn't arrive like a knife."

He looked at her for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the beach a kid was teaching another kid the second verse of the song, getting most of it wrong.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Come to the truck at seven, before the dockworkers. Bring the draft if it exists. And bring an apron."

"An apron."

"You said you wanted to fix things. Fixing things in my life has always started with dishes." He turned back to the pots, because if he looked at her face one more second tonight he was going to say something forgiving, and he wasn't ready, and forgiveness given before it's earned is just forgetting, and he had promised himself he would never forget.

Behind him, softly, he heard her sit down on a crate with her bowl, out of the way, like staff.

* * *

The fire burned down to red eyes in the dark. The tables emptied. Marisol moved through the crowd collecting bowls and stories with equal efficiency. Up on the road, Deacon's van glowed like a lantern, and the song played one more time for the emptying beach, and the tide came up close enough to touch the fire ring's outer stones and retreated, respectful.

Cyrus scrubbed the stockpots with sand and seawater, the oldest dish soap there is, and thought about a laminated page in a drawer under a register, and about a row of bean seedlings climbing twine strung by guilty, grieving hands, and about a woman who had tasted one second of fennel and looked like a woman being handed her brother back across a counter.

One degree, he thought. That was all the thaw was. One degree above freezing.

But water moves at one degree above freezing. Water moves.

He was wringing out the towel when Marisol appeared at his elbow with the last bowl of stew, saved, she said, for the cook, and sat down heavily on the crate Jude had vacated, and looked at him with the expression of a woman about to say something he would not want to hear.

"She showed you the draft?" Marisol asked.

"Not yet. Tomorrow."

Marisol nodded slowly, watching the fire. "There's something you should know before tomorrow, then. About the house. About what's really under the east wing, and why the developer wants it down so bad he'd pay a critic's mother for the story." She turned to look at him, and in the firelight her face was older than he'd ever seen it. "Danny didn't just leave us songs, Cyrus. He left us proof. And I think tomorrow, when she comes to your truck, you're both going to need it."
