## 28. Cyrus's Answer

She waits three days.

Not because she's unsure of the words — the words have been sitting in her like a stone swallowed whole, and she can feel their weight every time she leans forward. She waits because publishing the confession is one thing, a thing done in ink and distance, a thing that happens to strangers in other cities. Showing it to Cyrus Okafor first is another thing entirely. It requires her to stand in front of him while he reads his own ruin in her handwriting.

On the third morning she prints it at the library — the only printer in Salt Hollow, behind a desk manned by a teenager who doesn't look up — folds the pages once, and walks the mile out to the food truck at the edge of the docks.

It's early still, the light gray and forgiving, gulls working the tide line. The truck isn't open yet. Cyrus is underneath the service window on a folding stool, scrubbing the flat-top with a scraper, his sleeves rolled, forearms slick to the elbow. He hears her boots on the gravel and doesn't turn around.

"Truck opens at eleven," he says.

"I'm not here to eat."

He stops scraping. Sits back on his heels. Looks at her the way he has looked at her since the night at the truck when she said *my name is Jude Galloway* and watched ten years move across his face like weather — not with hatred, which would have been simpler, but with a kind of terrible attention, as if she were a dish he'd sent back and couldn't stop studying.

"You look like hell," he says.

"I've been writing."

"That explains it." He sets the scraper down, wipes his hands on a rag that's more grease than cloth. "Sit or don't. I'm not making you anything."

"I don't want anything made." She holds out the folded pages. "I want you to read this before anyone else does."

He looks at the paper for a long moment. Long enough that she counts four waves break against the pilings. Then he takes it, not gently, not cruelly — the way you take something hot.

* * *

He reads standing up, which surprises her. She expected him to sit, to make her wait through the whole ritual of it. Instead he unfolds the pages right there by the service window and starts at the top, and she stands six feet away with nothing to do with her hands and watches a man read his own obituary written by its killer.

The draft is titled simply: **A Correction, Ten Years Late.** Underneath, no hedging, no editor's softening hand. Her name. Her methods. The meal at Verrine that she reviewed without tasting a single course — the tasting menu she dismantled in nine hundred elegant words while her tongue sat in her mouth like a stranger's. The line about Cyrus's signature dish — *a study in ambition over palate, plated by a man who mistakes volume for depth* — quoted in full, so there could be no pretending she'd been misread. And then the truth under it: that she had tasted nothing. That she had known she tasted nothing. That she had published anyway, because the review was good, because the review was *her*, because a hollow woman with a famous name could pass off emptiness as restraint and everyone would applaud.

She watches him read it. His face gives almost nothing. A muscle works in his jaw around paragraph four. At paragraph seven — the one where she names the young sous chef who quit the industry entirely after Verrine closed, the one whose name she tracked down last week just to know the size of what she'd broken — he makes a small sound, low in his throat, and keeps reading.

Then he goes back to the beginning and reads it again.

That's worse. The second reading is worse because now she knows he's not reading for information. He's reading for accuracy. Checking her arithmetic of damage. She sees his lips move once — *nine hundred words* — verifying.

When he finishes the second time, he folds the pages along her crease, precisely, and holds them at his side.

"How long," he says.

"Three days writing it. Ten years not writing it."

"No. How long did you know? Before the review ran. How long did you know you couldn't taste?"

The question lands harder than any accusation could. Because it's the true question, the one the confession circles without landing on, and he's found it in one pass the way he'd find a flaw in a sauce.

"The whole meal," she says. "I knew from the first course. I kept waiting for it to come back. I told myself the kitchen was having an off night. I told myself—" She stops. "I told myself a lot of things. You read them."

"I read what you wrote about me." He taps the folded pages against his thigh. "This is the first thing you've written that says anything about you."

A fishing boat comes in slow past the breakwater, running lights still on in the gray morning, and neither of them watches it go.

"So publish it," Cyrus says.

She blinks. "Just like that?"

"Not just like that." He steps closer, and up close she can see the gray coming into his beard, the scar through one eyebrow she never noticed in the old press photos from Verrine, back when he was someone the city photographed. "There's a condition."

"A condition."

"One condition." He holds up the pages. "You publish this — every word, your real name, no editor sanding it down — and then you cook something. One dish. Cooked by you, with your hands, and served to me. And it has to be true."

"True," she repeats. "What does that even—"

"Don't do that." His voice doesn't rise but it sharpens, the way a blade sharpens on a strop, all motion no noise. "Don't do the critic thing where you take a word apart until it means nothing. You know exactly what I mean. You've spent ten years writing sentences that mean nothing. You know the difference better than anyone alive."

She wants to argue. She can feel the argument assembling itself in her mouth — *I'm not a cook, I never trained, my hands don't know that language* — and she recognizes it for what it is, which is the same voice that wrote nine hundred beautiful words about a meal she never ate. Fluency as escape hatch.

"Why?" she asks instead. "Why does it matter? The confession does the work. The confession names everything."

"The confession is words." He says it without contempt, which somehow makes it land heavier. "Words are your currency. They're cheap for you. You could write me a hundred confessions before lunch and they'd all be gorgeous and none of them would cost you anything you haven't already lost." He turns the folded pages over in his hands. "You want to know what it cost me? It wasn't the review. Reviews come and go; I'd survived bad ones. It was that I read it and thought — this person *tasted* my food and felt nothing. This person stood in my dining room, ate what I spent fourteen hours a day learning to make, and felt *nothing*. For years I thought I'd failed at some level I couldn't see. I tore my own kitchen apart looking for the flaw. I lost the restaurant, then I nearly lost my marriage hunting for it, and the whole time the flaw wasn't in the food." He looks at her. "It was in the mouth of the only person whose opinion mattered enough to end me."

The gulls are loud suddenly. Or maybe everything is loud when your ears start ringing.

"So no," Cyrus says, "the confession doesn't do the work. The confession tells the world. Cooking something true tells *me*. And I'm the one holding the laminated page, Jude. I'm the one who gets to decide if there's anything left to forgive."

"You said forgiveness isn't yours to give."

"I said absolution isn't mine to offer." He almost smiles, and it's the first almost-smile she's seen on him, and it changes his whole face the way a struck match changes a dark room. "Forgiveness is different. Forgiveness is just deciding to stop carrying the thing. I've been carrying it ten years. I'd like to set it down. But I'm not setting it down for a paragraph. Even a good one."

* * *

They stand there while the town wakes up around them. Dockworkers drift toward the pier, nodding at Cyrus, glancing at her with the frank incuriosity of people who've already decided she's Marisol's strange long-term guest and filed her accordingly. Cyrus flips the propane on behind the window and starts his mise — onions, peppers, the day's fish arriving in a cooler from a kid on a bicycle who barely brakes to deliver it.

She should leave. There's no reason to stay. The condition has been stated; the answer will take days, weeks, however long it takes her to fail at cooking in Marisol's ancient kitchen. But her feet don't move, and eventually Cyrus notices and jerks his chin at the stool beside the window.

"You going to hover or sit?"

"I don't cook," she says, sitting. "I want to be clear about that. I can boil water. I own one pan. It's nonstick and I feel vaguely ashamed of it."

"Good," he says, chopping an onion with a rhythm she could set a metronome to. "Nonstick is honest. It admits it's afraid of sticking. That's more than most cooks admit." He sweeps the onion into a bowl. "Your brother could cook, you know."

The words go through her like cold water. "Danny?"

"Mm. Not well — don't get me wrong. He cooked like a man writing letters. Everything was a message to somebody." Cyrus slides the peppers onto the flat-top and the smell rises, sweet and blistering, and something in her chest pulls tight, because Danny's postcards were full of meals, every one of them a letter she didn't learn to read until Room Seven at three in the morning. "He'd come down to the truck sometimes, back when I first landed here. Sit on that same stool. Told me once he had a sister who used to eat everything on the plate and then describe it better than any menu ever could. Said she could make you taste a tomato just by talking about it." He glances at her. "Didn't say your name. Didn't need to. I figured it out later, and by then—" He shrugs. "By then I'd decided I liked the version of you that was just some sad woman eating alone in my truck."

"What did he say about me? Exactly. Word for word."

"You want word for word after fifteen years?"

"Yes."

Cyrus scrapes the peppers, considers. "'My sister tastes the world for both of us,'" he recites. "'When she describes a meal, I don't need to eat anymore.'" He lets that sit between them. "So when your review came down the wire a few years later — Galloway, Galloway, why do I know that name — and it was all architecture and no appetite, I remember thinking: whoever this is, they've stopped eating too. I just didn't know I was right."

She looks out at the water because she cannot look at him. *Tastes the world for both of us.* Fifteen years ago, before the letter, before the song, before whatever Danny gave up that hollowed her tongue out the day he left — he'd already handed her the job. Already made her the taster. And she'd put down the fork and picked up the pen and called it a career.

"Cyrus," she says. "The condition. If I cook something and it's bad — truly bad, embarrassing, inedible — does that count as true?"

He laughs. It's short and rough, surprised out of him, and it's the best sound she's heard in this town.

"Oh, it might count *more*," he says. "A beautiful failure is truer than a competent success. Ask my first soufflé." He points the knife at her, friendly, final. "But here's the rule, and I'm not bending it. You cook it yourself. I'll stand in the room. I'll answer questions. I will not touch the stove, and I won't tell you it's good when it isn't. You get honesty from me for the first time, and you give it back. That's the trade."

"And if I can't do it? If I stand in that kitchen and my hands shake and I burn water?"

"Then you publish the confession anyway, and we call it square-ish, and I keep feeding dockworkers until my knees give out." He turns back to the flat-top. "But you won't burn water."

"How do you know?"

"Because you came back to my truck after you told me who you were." He flips something sizzling. "People who are only sorry don't come back. People who are sorry *and stubborn* come back. Stubborn I can work with."

* * *

She walks back along the shore road with the folded draft still in her bag, lighter than it's been in three days, though nothing has changed except a condition. The house comes into view around the bend — the boarding house, shingled and sagging, its east wing holding its breath against the date circled on every calendar in town. Six weeks became five. Five is becoming less.

Marisol is on the porch steps shelling peas into a bowl, and she reads Jude's face the way she reads weather.

"He gave you a condition," Marisol says. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"Because he's Cyrus." A pea pops free of its pod. "Fifteen years I've known that man, and every hard thing he's ever done came wrapped in a test. What is it?"

"I have to cook something true."

Marisol's hands go still over the bowl. She looks at Jude for a long moment, and then at the kitchen door behind her, and something moves across her face — memory, or permission, or grief.

"The big kitchen," she says quietly. "Not the little one upstairs. The big one. Danny's kitchen."

"Danny had a kitchen?"

"Danny had *the* kitchen." Marisol sets the bowl down and stands, brushing her skirt. "Come. If you're going to try to cook something true in this house, you're going to do it where he did. And there's something in there I've been meaning to show you anyway — I just didn't know you were ready."

She pushes open the kitchen door, and the smell hits Jude first — dust and cedar and something faintly, impossibly sweet, like a pie baked decades ago and never quite gone — and then the light, falling through a window over a vast scarred wooden table, and on the wall above the stove, taped and yellowed, a postcard.

Jude knows the handwriting before she's close enough to read it.

It's Danny's. And it's addressed to her.
