## 3. Theo Comes Home

The gates of Vane House had never once opened for Theo Vane without a performance attached, and he arrived this time with the best one he had: a man returning home. He said the word *home* to the taxi driver as if it were a fact, tipped too generously, and stood a moment in the October wind letting the house take his measure while he pretended to take its.

It was smaller than he remembered. That was the trick of absence—houses shrank in the mind to fit the size of what you'd left behind, and Vane House had been, in his leaving, an enormous thing: a fortress of disapproval with his mother at every window. Now it looked tired. The slate roof wore its moss like an old coat. One shutter on the third floor hung by a single hinge, swinging in small, apologetic movements, and Theo thought, with a lurch he refused to name, that the house was waving.

He had told himself the story all the way up from the city: he was here to help. Estate sales were ugly business and someone ought to keep them dignified. Someone ought to protect her things—the good silver, the scores, the piano—from the vultures his siblings would bring. That he needed money was incidental. That the money had a specific shape and a specific creditor was more incidental still. He was a son come home. He rehearsed it until it almost held.

The front door was unlocked. That stopped him on the threshold longer than anything else did. For twenty years the door had been locked against him specifically; he could feel the lock's opinion even now, some residual judgment in the hinges. He stepped into the hall and the smell hit him—beeswax and cold stone and, underneath, the faint mineral breath of the radiators—and something in his chest turned over like a key in a stubborn mechanism.

"You'll want to sign for the keys."

The voice came from the parlor doorway, and Theo turned to find a woman standing there with a leather tool roll under one arm and an expression of mild, professional patience, as though he were a delivery she'd been expecting and found slightly damaged.

"August," he said, because it was the only thing he knew about her. August Pemberton. The radiator woman. His mother's radiator woman, which in the economy of Vane House had always meant something closer to *confessor*.

"Mr. Vane." She didn't offer a hand. "The trust kept me on through the winter. Pipes don't know anyone's dead."

"No," Theo said. "I suppose they don't." He smiled—the smile that had gotten him out of boarding school punishments, out of two marriages, out of a great many rooms—and watched it land on her the way rain lands on glass. Nothing absorbed. "You're still tuning them? The whole system should be drained by now. It's costing someone a fortune."

"It is," August agreed pleasantly. "The estate can afford it."

*The estate,* Theo thought, *which is currently being divided among four people who have already begun dividing it.* He moved past her into the hall, trailing his coat behind him like a train, and heard her follow—not following him, exactly, but resuming whatever circuit she'd interrupted, her boots making a soft, unhurried percussion on the flagstones. A wrench clinked inside the tool roll. She was, he realized with a strange discomfort, going to keep doing her work while he did his, in the same house, and neither of them would acknowledge the other unless forced.

He started upstairs. Halfway up he stopped, because the nursery pipe was singing.

It was a thin whistle, air finding a seam somewhere in the old iron, and it had sung like that his entire childhood—a sound so constant it had become silence. But standing there now, with his mother three weeks dead and her secrets presumably still distributed through the plumbing like sediment, the whistle sounded less like a flaw in the system and more like a voice clearing its throat.

"The third-floor riser," August called up from below, not unkindly. "I haven't got to it yet."

"I wasn't complaining."

"I know." A pause. "It doesn't sing for everyone."

Theo stood very still on the stairs. Then he continued climbing, telling himself the cold feeling at the back of his neck was just the draft.

* * *

In his mother's study, Theo did not loot the drawers. He wanted to be clear with himself about that. He sat at her desk in the late light and went through the papers the way an archivist would—carefully, respectfully, reading everything twice—and if he lingered over bank statements looking for evidence of what she'd known, or hadn't, that was due diligence. If he checked the drawer where she'd kept the household accounts and found it empty except for a single dried rosemary sprig, that was grief. Probably.

She had known nothing. That was the version he'd built. She'd hummed the song to herself the way old women hummed, half-remembered, ownerless, and he had simply—recognized it. Salvaged it. Given it back to the world with better chords. The arrangement had taken him four days in a studio in Lisbon with a cellist who owed him favors, and when the record came out and the checks began arriving, he had allowed himself to believe the song had been a gift freely given, that his mother would have been pleased, that *lullaby* was a genre and not a promise.

The royalty statement in his jacket pocket weighed approximately nothing and approximately everything.

Below him, faintly, he heard August moving through the house. Room to room. Radiator to radiator. There was a rhythm to it—he'd forgotten, or maybe never noticed as a child, the way she worked: bleed valve, listen, adjust, move on. Like a doctor making rounds. Like someone taking dictation.

He thought: *she was here all those winters. She heard everything.*

He thought: *what else did she hear?*

* * *

August found him at dusk in the music room, which surprised her, because she had expected him to avoid it. The piano sat under its dust sheet like a body under a sheet, and Theo stood beside it not touching it, holding a tumbler of the scotch he'd brought in his overnight bag, looking at the covered shape the way men look at graves they dug themselves.

"They'll want this sold first," he said, hearing her in the doorway. "The piano. It's the only thing in the house worth real money."

"Your mother played it every morning until her hands went."

"I know." He turned the glass in his fingers. "That's rather the point of pianos."

August set down her tool roll and considered him. He was handsome in the way of furniture built to be looked at rather than sat upon—all gloss and careful joinery, and you sensed the cheap wood underneath. Twenty years ago, passing through on her rounds, she'd seen him in this same room arguing with Iris about money, and he'd worn the same face then: charm laid over panic like paint over rot. She had tuned the radiators through that argument too. She had tuned the radiators through everything.

"How long are you staying?" she asked.

"Until the sale's done. I'm coordinating the—" He waved a hand. "Logistics."

"Mm." She crossed to the radiator beneath the window, the big Victorian one that took longest to warm, and knelt beside it with her bleed key. "Funny thing about logistics," she said, fitting the key. "They leave records."

The valve hissed. Water spat, then ran clear, and the radiator began its low tick as heat entered the iron. Theo watched her work and felt the particular vertigo of a man realizing the witness has been in the room the entire time.

"My mother trusted you," he said.

"She paid me to fix her pipes. Trust was extra. She got it anyway."

"What did she tell you?"

August straightened and looked at him directly for the first time, and her eyes were gray and level and terribly patient. "Everything," she said. "Nothing. Whatever the pipes carried. I don't repeat it, Mr. Vane. Not to lawyers, not to siblings, not to sons."

"But you remember it."

"Every word."

The fire ticked between them. Theo drank. Somewhere above them the third-floor riser whistled its thin note, and both of them glanced toward the ceiling at the same instant, and both looked away.

"There's a song," Theo said, before he could stop himself, and hated himself for it.

"Yes," said August.

"You've heard it. On the radio. They're playing it everywhere now, it seems." He made his voice light, conversational, a man discussing weather. "Strange how these things surface. Old melodies. Folk sources. My mother collected songs her whole life—half of what she hummed was borrowed from somewhere. Sea shanties, hymns, things her own mother sang. You couldn't really say any of it belonged to anyone."

August folded her bleed key into her palm, slowly, the way other people fold their hands.

"No," she said. "You couldn't say that. Unless you were in the room when it was written."

The words dropped into the quiet of the music room and lay there. Theo felt his pulse in his teeth. She knew—or she suspected, or she was fishing—but either way she was standing between him and the door with nothing in her hands but a brass key, and he understood suddenly that this woman had spent twenty years learning the exact weight of everything unsaid in this house, and that she could pick up any of it and throw it.

"Well," he said, smiling again, and this time the smile cost him visibly. "If you'll excuse me. The solicitor comes tomorrow, and I want the inventory sheets ready."

"Of course." August gathered her tool roll. At the doorway she paused, her back to him, and spoke to the darkening window and the garden beyond it. "The parlor radiator's been cold since the funeral. Three weeks. No blockage, no air in the line—I've checked it twice. It just won't take the heat." She turned her head slightly. "In my experience, Mr. Vane, when a radiator goes cold in this house, it's waiting for something. Or refusing it. The pipes have opinions."

She left. Theo stood alone in the music room with his scotch and the covered piano, and after a long moment he lifted the dust sheet from the keyboard with one finger, as though checking whether the body beneath it was still breathing.

Upstairs, faintly, the riser sang on.

And in the parlor, cold as a held breath, the largest radiator in Vane House waited—for the truth, or for the will to be read, or for whoever would finally sit down at the piano and give it back its voice.
