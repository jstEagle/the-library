## 4. What the Pipes Remember

The house told me things in order, if I knew how to listen. It had always spoken through its pipes—complaints in the nursery line, gossip in the kitchen risers, grief in the long cold run that fed the parlor—but now it had a new subject, and I meant to interrogate every inch of it.

I came back on Thursday with a flashlight, a flathead screwdriver, and the radio transcript I'd written out from memory at my kitchen table at two in the morning: *arranged by Theo Vane, recorded at Vane House, 1998.* The announcer's voice had been smooth as varnish. *A previously unreleased work from the estate of—* no. Not estate. He'd said *from the archives of* Theo Vane, as if the song were a fossil he'd dug up honestly, as if archives were anything but other people's memories filed under someone else's name.

I let myself in with the trust key. The house exhaled around me, that particular sigh of an unheated building in October, and I stood in the front hall and said aloud, to no one, "All right. Show me."

* * *

The music room first. It made sense the way guilt makes sense—obvious once you stood inside it.

The room sat on the east side, where morning light used to fall across the piano in bars so clean Iris called them "the staff." She'd say, *August, the staff is here, we'd better behave,* and then play something improper just to spite them. The piano was still there under its dust sheet, a shape like a sleeping animal. The sheet music cabinet stood open, emptied by whoever had gone through the house after the funeral—Iris's sister's children, probably, or the appraisers, or all of them in their careful gloves, taking the valuable and leaving the true.

I pulled the dust sheet back. The piano keys were yellowed, middle C chipped. And there, on top of the closed lid, sitting in plain sight like something set down in haste: a portable tape recorder.

Not Theo's era exactly—a 1990s Sony, the kind you could palm in one hand, with a microphone jack worn bright from use. The battery door hung open, corroded. I pressed eject with the screwdriver tip and a cassette slid halfway out, its label curling. In ballpoint, in a hand I knew better than my own—looser now, drifting, but hers—Iris had written: *For A. Do not.*

The sentence stopped there. *Do not.* Do not what? Play it? Listen? Forgive?

My hands shook so badly I nearly dropped it. I did not put it in the machine. There was no machine that could be trusted; there was no me that could be trusted, alone in this house with her voice possibly inside a ribbon of brown plastic. I wrapped the cassette in my scarf and put it in my coat pocket, against my heart like contraband, which is what it was.

The recorder itself I turned over. Scratched into the plastic base, small and deliberate, someone had carved a date: **III–98**. March 1998. I did the arithmetic standing there among the dust-sheeted ghosts. That was the winter—the third winter—the winter Iris finished teaching me the song.

* * *

Because she hadn't taught it to me all at once. Nothing about Iris arrived whole.

The first winter was 1996, and I had come to bleed the radiators in the west wing and found her at the piano picking out four notes over and over, patient as water wearing stone. She didn't stop when I came in. She never performed for people, but she worked in front of them, indifferent to being seen, and somehow that was more intimate than any performance.

"What is it?" I asked.

"A thing I'm making," she said. "It isn't done."

"Who's it for?"

She looked at me then, over the top of the piano, with an expression I spent twenty years learning to read and never fully mastered. "Ask me again when it's finished," she said, and played the four notes again, and I stood in the doorway with my radiator key in my hand and understood that I had been given something, though I couldn't have said what.

That winter she gave me only fragments. A phrase here, hummed while she stirred tea. A phrase there, sung half-under her breath while I knelt at the parlor radiator and she sat above me in the wing chair, ostensibly reading. I learned it the way the house learned weather—by absorption, without permission, until one day in February I caught myself humming it while I bled air from the nursery line, and the whistle in the pipe picked it up and carried it, and Iris, two rooms away, went silent mid-sentence.

She found me in the nursery. She didn't ask how I knew it. She said, "Slower. You're rushing the third bar. It's a lullaby, August, not a race," and that was the whole of the second winter: correction. Her hands over mine on the keys some evenings when the radiators were quiet, pressing my fingers flat, saying *legato, legato, it has to sound like breathing.* She was teaching me her own heartbeat disguised as a song, and neither of us named it, because naming it would have required admitting there was something to name.

The third winter she finished it. I know because she told me—March, snow coming down fat and slow past the music room windows—and because that was the day she played it through, start to end, for the first time with me at the bench beside her, and then turned on the bench and looked at me the way you look at a fire you've finally gotten lit.

"There," she said. "Now it exists in two places."

"In the piano," I said, stupidly, "and—"

"In you," she said. "Don't be dense, it doesn't suit you." And then, quieter, looking at her own hands: "One day this house will be full of people who want things from it. When that happens, remember which of us it was written for."

I had thought, all these years, that she meant herself. That the song was her confession, her keepsake, hers. It had never once occurred to me—not in twenty years of carrying it in my chest like a second pulse—that *for* could point the other way.

* * *

But dates don't lie, and houses keep receipts. If the recorder said III–98, then Theo—or someone—had captured the finished song within weeks of its completion. Which meant he'd been here. Which meant he'd been listening.

I went upstairs to the old bedrooms, my screwdriver in my pocket like a sidearm.

Marguerite's room first—Theo's older sister, the one who left in '89 and sent postcards instead of visits. Her bedroom had been sealed longer than any other, and it smelled of closed decades, lavender gone to dust. The radiator under the window was a cast-iron antique, ornate as a church gate, and I knelt before it the way I'd knelt before a hundred others and ran my fingers along the panel seams.

There. Inside the lower access panel, scratched into the iron where no one would ever look unless they were me: a date. **XI–97**. November 1997. And beside it, smaller, almost shy: **T.V.**

Theo had been in this room in November of '97, doing whatever you do inside a radiator panel with a scratching tool. Hiding something? Marking a repair? Or—my stomach turned slowly over—leaving himself notes. Dates. A timeline. Men who sell things like to document provenance; it protects the price.

I checked every radiator in that corridor. Marguerite's room: XI–97. The linen closet bath: XII–97. The guest room at the hall's end: II–98. A trail of scratches marching through the house month by month, room by room, converging on the music room and March, like footprints toward a kill.

He hadn't stumbled onto the song. He'd hunted it. He'd moved through his mother's house season by season, room by room, following the sound through the walls—because the pipes carried it, of course they carried it, the same way they carried everything, and anyone who grew up in Vane House knew the pipes talked. He had eavesdropped on his mother composing the way I had eavesdropped on her living, and when the song was finished he had set a recorder in the music room and waited for her to play.

And she had played. Of course she had. She played for the staff, for the morning light, for the house itself. She would never have known. She'd have thought the silence of an empty room was silence.

I sat down on the floor of the guest room with my back against the cold radiator and let that settle into me like sediment. Grief first—it came up my throat hot and useless—and then, underneath it, something harder and more useful, something with edges. Because here was the thing the scratches told me that the radio never would: Theo had taken the song, but he had never been given it. He had stolen a recording of a gift, and he didn't even know what the gift was. He had the notes and not the meaning, the body and not the breath. Somewhere in Los Angeles or wherever he'd vanished to, a man was collecting royalties on a lullaby whose recipient he couldn't name, because he'd never once asked who it was for.

Iris had known, though. Even now, even with her mind going soft at the edges like old fruit, she knew. *You know which one of them took it*—no, that was later, that was still coming, I wasn't ready for that yet. But she'd known enough to leave the cassette labeled *For A. Do not.* She'd known someone might come digging, and she'd hidden the original where only a person who read radiators would ever find it.

She'd hidden it for me. In the house's own language.

I took the cassette out of my pocket and held it up to the gray window light. *Do not.* Do not play it. Do not give it up. Do not let them turn her love into product. All of the above.

Downstairs, somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked as the afternoon cooled—the house shifting its weight, thinking. And beneath the ticking, faint but unmistakable, the nursery whistle began its thin complaint, and whether it was the wind or my imagination or Iris herself leaning on the plumbing from wherever she was, the note it chose was the third bar of the song, the one she'd told me not to rush.

I stood up. I put the screwdriver back in my pocket.

Tomorrow the family would arrive for the reading of the will—four siblings descending on the house like weather—and I would serve them tea with steady hands and listen to everything. But tonight I climbed into my truck with a dead woman's cassette against my ribs and drove out the gates past the stone lions, and in the rearview mirror Vane House sat dark on its hill, every window black except one, high up in the east wing, where the curtain moved though I had locked every sash.

Someone was already home.
