## 12. Marchetti's Hands

The care home was called Bracken Lodge, which sounded less like a place where a man went to die and more like a place where a man might be quietly forgotten. It sat behind a row of leylandii on the edge of Dalkey, all pale brick and uPVC windows, a building that had been designed by someone who believed comfort was a color. Rafael stood at the gate for four minutes before he could make himself walk in. He counted the minutes because counting was what he did now instead of feeling. Four minutes at a gate. Nine years at a distance. The arithmetic of cowardice was always the same.

He had called ahead, of course. He was a man who called ahead. The receptionist had said, *Oh, Mr. Marchetti will be delighted, he doesn't get many visitors from the trade,* and Rafael had wanted to say, *I'm not from the trade, I'm a fraud with a byline,* but instead he had said thank you and hung up and sat in his car in his mother's driveway—the flat was still unsold, still full of boxes, still a museum of the unsaid—and rehearsed.

That was the humiliating truth of it. He had rehearsed. In the car, hands at ten and two, he had rehearsed a confession the way he once rehearsed reviews: *Ivo, I have to tell you something. Ivo, you'll understand better than anyone. Ivo, I've been lying for nine years.* Three openings, none of them true enough. All of them true. He had deleted four drafts of a text to Sorcha the week before, and now he was drafting sentences in his head for a dying man, and somewhere in the middle of that repetition he understood that this was his mother's disease, this drafting, this never sending, this polishing of the unsaid until the unsaid became the whole house you lived in.

The corridor smelled of lavender disinfectant and overcooked cabbage, and the wrongness of that smell—the sheer institutional defeat of it—made his chest hurt for reasons he couldn't name. Somewhere a television played a game show at low volume. Somewhere a woman was singing to herself, tunelessly, a song from the forties. Rafael walked past the open doors of strangers' rooms, past the small altars of their lives—photographs, a rosary, a potted orchid struggling under strip lighting—and felt like a burglar of other people's dying.

Marchetti's room was at the end, the one with the window that caught the sea.

The chef was smaller than Rafael remembered, which was absurd, because men don't shrink, except that they do, they do exactly that, the body folding inward year by year like bread proving and collapsing. Ivo Marchetti sat in an armchair by the window with a blanket over his legs and a newspaper folded on the armrest, unread, and his hands—his hands were the same. That was the first thing. That was the thing that undid Rafael before a word was spoken. The hands were still enormous, still scarred at the knuckles, still bearing the burn-marks of forty years of service, resting on the blanket like two retired animals that had once done all the work of a life.

*Rafa,* Marchetti said, and his accent had thickened in illness, the Italian rising back up through the English like water through stone. *Rafa Kavanagh. They told me a journalist was coming and I thought, some young one with a recorder. But no. It's the boy from the pots.*

*I'm not a boy.*

*No.* Marchetti's eyes were still quick, still black, still missing nothing, and they went over Rafael's face the way he used to go over a plate leaving the pass—slowly, professionally, looking for the flaw. *No, you're not. Sit down, sit. You look terrible. You look like a man who sleeps in his clothes.*

*I do, sometimes.*

*Good. Honest answer. Sit.*

Rafael sat. The chair was too low and put him at the angle of a child at a grown-up's knee, and he supposed that was the point of care-home furniture, or its accident, and either way he let it happen. Outside the window the sea was doing its grey, indifferent work. Marchetti watched him with the patience of a man who had spent decades waiting for sauces to reduce, for dough to rise, for apprentices to stop being idiots, and Rafael felt the silence doing to him what it had always done in that kitchen: opening him up.

*I read your reviews,* Marchetti said at last. *Declan brings the paper when he comes. He comes more than you'd think, your brother. Good lad, Declan. Always was the one who showed up.*

The sentence went into Rafael like a splinter and he let it sit there. *You read them.*

*Every one.* Marchetti smiled, and the smile was still the old smile, half warmth, half blade. *You write beautiful, Rafa. Too beautiful. A man who tastes properly doesn't need that many adjectives. When the flavor is real, one word is enough. When it isn't, you build a cathedral of words around the empty plate.* He tapped the newspaper. *Lately you've been building cathedrals.*

The room was very quiet. The woman down the corridor was still singing. Rafael looked at the hands on the blanket and found that the rehearsed openings had all evaporated, and what came out instead was nothing rehearsed at all.

*How did you know?* His voice came out cracked. *Before I said anything. How did you know?*

Marchetti shrugged, an old man's shrug, one shoulder higher than the other. *Because I have the same disease, boy. Not the same name, maybe. Same disease.* He lifted one hand—the right one, the burn hand—and turned it over, palm up, an offering or a surrender. *Eighteen months now. First the edge went. Salt became a rumor. Then the middle. Then one morning I made a ragù I have made ten thousand times, a ragù I could make in my sleep, in my coffin, and I stood there tasting it and tasting it and tasting it, and there was nothing. Nothing, Rafa. Like kissing through glass.* He put the hand down. *So when I read a young critic suddenly writing like an angel about food that I know—I know that kitchen, I know that chef, he's competent and nothing more—when I read that, I know what I'm reading. I'm reading a man describing a photograph of a meal. From technique. From memory. From terror.*

Rafael's eyes were burning. He stared at the sea. *Nine years,* he said. *Nine years, Ivo. Since my mother died. I sat down at a tasting menu three weeks after the funeral and the food arrived and it was—I don't know how to describe it—it was like eating descriptions of food. Like reading the menu with my mouth. And I panicked. I was thirty-one years old and my whole life was my palate and it was just—gone. So I wrote the review anyway. From technique. I could see the sear, I could see the sauce break or not break, I could see the plating, and I knew what I was supposed to feel, and I wrote what I was supposed to feel. And it printed. And nobody knew. And then the next week there was another meal, and then another, and it just—became the job. It became the whole career. I've been writing fiction with a byline for nine years and they gave me awards for it.*

*And the letters,* Marchetti said quietly. *Declan says you found the letters.*

Rafael laughed, a wet, broken sound. *Declan talks too much.*

*Declan talks exactly enough and always has. You're the one who never talked. That was always the difference between you.* Marchetti shifted in the chair, and Rafael saw the effort it cost, the small courage of moving a failing body. *The letters your mother wrote and never sent. The letters you wrote and never sent. A whole house of unsaid things. I know the disease, I told you. Mine just lives in my mouth instead of my heart.*

*I came here to confess,* Rafael said. *That was the plan. I rehearsed it in the car, which is pathetic—*

*It's not pathetic. It's human. Rehearsing is just caring with a safety rail.*

*—and now I've confessed and you haven't even flinched, and I don't know what to do with that. I thought you'd be furious. You should be furious. I stood in your kitchen when I was seventeen and you told me flavor is attention, you said it a hundred times, flavor is attention, and I built a whole life on that sentence and then I stopped paying attention to anything. I stopped tasting my food and I stopped tasting my mother's cooking and I stopped answering her letters and I stopped—I don't even know when I stopped calling her. I can't find the moment. That's the worst part. There's no moment. There's just a flat grey stretch of years where I was busy being important.*

Marchetti let the words fall and lie where they landed. That had always been his method. He never rushed a confession in the kitchen either; he'd stand there with his arms folded while some shaking nineteen-year-old admitted he'd dropped the stock or salted the dessert, and he'd let the truth get all the way out of the boy's mouth before he said a word, because a truth interrupted was a truth that would come back twice as heavy.

Now he said: *Do you know what I did, when I lost the ragù?*

Rafael shook his head.

*I kept cooking. For a while. Secretly. My wife was dead by then, God rest her, and I'd cook for myself in that big kitchen in Monkstown, and I'd eat, and I'd pretend. I'd sit there with a spoon and I'd perform the face. The face you make when it's good.* He smiled, and it was the saddest smile Rafael had ever seen on him. *Performing the face, Rafa. For nobody. An audience of one and he's a liar. Six months I did that. And then one day I caught myself and I thought: Ivo, you old fool, you are doing to yourself exactly what you spent fifty years teaching boys not to do. You are serving a dish you haven't tasted. To yourself. The one customer you can't lie to.*

*And what did you do?*

*I stopped. I put the spoons in a box. I gave the knives to a young one in Terenure who cries when his sauce splits.* He laughed, then coughed, then waved off the cough. *And then I got sick in the ordinary way, the way old men get sick, and I came here, and now they bring me soft food on a plastic tray and it doesn't matter that I can't taste it, because I made my peace before the tray came. Do you understand the difference? The grief came when I could still hold it. I grieved my tongue while I still had my life. You've been carrying yours blindfolded for nine years.*

*You make it sound like a choice.*

*It is a choice.* The black eyes were suddenly fierce, and Rafael was seventeen again, and the pass was hot, and the chef's voice was going through the whole kitchen like a bell. *Everything is a choice, Rafa. The numbness is a choice. The silence is a choice. Grief is not a choice—grief is weather—but what you build in the weather, that's yours. Your mother died and it broke your tongue, fine, that's true, that's real, the body does these things. But then you chose the reviews. You chose the letters you didn't send. You chose the flat you didn't visit. Not out of evil. Out of fear. But chosen, all the same. And what is chosen can be chosen differently.*

Rafael put his face in his hands. He hadn't meant to. It happened the way weather happens. His shoulders shook and the sound that came out of him was not dignified and he did not try to make it dignified, because Marchetti had never once, in all the years of the kitchen, asked any of them for dignity—only for truth, only for attention, only for the thing itself and not the performance of the thing.

He felt the old hands come to rest on the back of his head. Light. Careful. The way Marchetti had once steadied the necks of geese, the way he'd held a boy's wrist to correct a knife grip, and Rafael wept into his own palms in a care home in Dalkey while the sea did its grey work and the woman down the corridor sang her forties song to no one.

*There it is,* Marchetti said softly. *There it is. Nine years in the cold. Let it come up to room temperature.*

* * *

They sat for a long while after that, the way you can only sit with someone who is dying, time going soft and unaccountable. A nurse came in with tea and Marchetti introduced Rafael as *my student, the famous one,* and the nurse said, *oh, the critic, he reads you out loud sometimes, did you know that?* and Rafael did not know that and had to look out the window again for a while.

*Tell me something,* Marchetti said eventually. *The food you've been reviewing. The great meals. The cathedrals. When you wrote them—when you sat there with the notebook, describing a sear you couldn't taste—was there ever a moment? Even one? Where something came through? A ghost of it?*

Rafael thought about it honestly, because lying to this man was no longer possible, had perhaps never been possible. *Once,* he said. *Two years ago. A bowl of celeriac soup, of all things. Some young chef in a basement in Portobello. I wrote three hundred words about it and I only meant about forty. There was a—something. A warmth at the back of the throat. I told myself it was the room, the radiator, whatever. I told myself a lot of things.*

*What did it taste of?*

*I couldn't tell you. That's the thing. I remember the feeling and not the flavor. Like a name you've forgotten but know you know.*

Marchetti nodded slowly, and there was something in his face that Rafael couldn't read, some old knowledge being consulted. *Celeriac,* he said. *You know what my mother made, in Bologna, when I was small and the winter was long? She made soup from the ugly vegetables. The ones the market men threw aside. Celeriac, turnip, the black-spotted apples. She'd cook them down with nothing—no stock, nothing expensive, just time—and she'd say, Ivo, flavor is attention, and I stole the sentence from her and gave it to you, so really you learned it from a woman in Bologna you never met.* He looked out at the sea. *The body remembers, boy. That's what I'm telling you. The tongue forgets but the body remembers. The soup came through the wall of the numbness because something in that basement kitchen sounded like—what? What did it sound like?*

And Rafael, who had spent nine years not answering questions, heard the answer come up out of him from somewhere below the career, below the byline, below the polished numbness, from the place where a boy had once stood on a milk crate to reach the sink.

*A kitchen where someone was glad I was there,* he said.

Marchetti closed his eyes. *There it is,* he said. *There's your review. Write that one.*

* * *

It was nearly dark when Rafael stood to leave. The strip lights had come on in the corridor and the sea had gone to ink and the room was blue with the last of the light. Marchetti reached for the newspaper on the armrest and held it out.

*Take it. I only keep it for the crossword now, and I do it badly, and I'm tired of doing it badly.*

Rafael took it. Under it, hidden, was a slim black notebook, the kind chefs keep, grease-spotted, spine cracked, and Marchetti watched Rafael see it and did not move to hide it.

*Recipes,* he said. *Not mine. Everyone's. My mother's, the ugly-vegetable soup. The ragù. A soda bread an Irish girl taught me in London in 1963 who broke my heart and improved my baking. Forty years of other people's food, written down so it wouldn't die with my tongue.* He put his hand flat on the cover, and the hand trembled, and he let Rafael see that it trembled. *I was going to leave it to the school. To the young ones. That was the plan.*

*And now?*

*Now I'm an old man in a lavender corridor and plans are for people with more future.* He pushed the notebook across the blanket, across the space between them, and Rafael's hands came out and took it before his mind had agreed to anything, the way hands take bread, the way hands take what is offered. *I want you to cook them, Rafa. Not review them. Cook them. All of them, or as many as your life allows. And I want you to tell me honestly—not in print, not in a cathedral of adjectives—honestly, to my face, what happens.*

*What happens?*

*Whether the body remembers.* The black eyes held him. *You said the soup tasted of a kitchen where someone was glad you were there. Fine. Then cook in a kitchen like that. Cook your mother's bread, cook my mother's soup, cook in that flat of hers you're emptying out, and pay attention, Rafa, real attention, the whole of your attention, and see what comes up through the floor of the numbness. That's your confession and your penance and your inheritance, all in one greasy notebook. I can't taste anything ever again. But you might. That's the trade. That's the last lesson and I'm giving it to you free, which should tell you how serious I am. I never gave anything away free in my life.*

Rafael stood there with the notebook against his chest like a thing rescued from a fire. *Ivo. What if nothing comes back? What if it's just—gone, permanently, and I cook all forty recipes and taste nothing?*

Marchetti smiled, and settled back into the chair, and looked out at the dark sea like a man looking at an old colleague across a pass.

*Then you'll have cooked forty meals for the people you love,* he said, *and you'll have said the unsaid things, and you'll have stopped lying, and your mother will have been fed by your hands at last. And you'll tell me it tasted of nothing, and I'll tell you that you're a liar, and one of us will be right, and we'll have until the end of my time to argue about it.* He waved a scarred hand toward the door. *Go home, Rafa. Go home and light the oven.*

Rafael got as far as the corridor, past the singing woman, past the small altars of strangers' lives, before he opened the notebook at random under the strip lights. The page was headed, in Marchetti's blunt carpenter's hand: *Soda bread — the Irish girl, 1963 — she said her mother's secret was buttermilk warm from the pan, never cold from the shop.* And below it, in the same hand, smaller, as if the chef had written it to himself and forgotten anyone might read it:

*Flavor is attention. Attention is love. Same thing, always was.*

Rafael stood in the lavender corridor of Bracken Lodge with his mother's flat full of boxes across town and a bus route dying in six weeks and a brother he hadn't forgiven and a letter that broke off mid-sentence in a shoebox behind a wardrobe—and he understood, with the sudden terrible clarity of a sauce coming together, what he had to do next, and where, and with whose recipe in his hand.
