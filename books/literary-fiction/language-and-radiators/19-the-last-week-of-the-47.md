## 19. The Last Week of the 47

**Rafael**

The notice went up at the shelter on Monday: a laminated sheet, A4, the kind of officialdom that believes itself invisible. *From Sunday next, Route 47 will be diverted via the ring road to serve the new retail quarter. We apologise for any inconvenience.* Any inconvenience. As if a bus were a pothole.

Rafael stood in the drizzle reading it twice, then a third time, because the words kept rearranging themselves into something he didn't want them to say. Six weeks ago, when Declan had first mentioned it over a pint neither of them finished, the reroute had felt like an abstraction, a fact about the city rather than a fact about him. Now it had a date. Now it was a door closing with a timetable.

He photographed the notice, which was absurd — who did he think would want the evidence? — and then walked the old route's spine on foot, from the shelter past the shuttered launderette, past the chipper that had become a phone repair shop and then nothing, up the hill where the houses turned their backs to the wind. His mother had ridden this bus every week of her life until she couldn't. He used to imagine her on it, shopping bags wedged between her ankles, nodding at neighbours whose names she knew and his he'd forgotten. He had never once, in nine years, taken the 47 out to see her. He'd taken taxis. Taxis were private. Taxis let you arrive without travelling.

That was the whole of him in one sentence, he thought. He arrived without travelling.

At the flat, he laid the letters out on the kitchen table again — all forty, hers and his, ordered now by date because ordering things was what he did instead of feeling them. The last letter, the unfinished one, sat apart at the right-hand edge like a place set for someone expected. *Rafael, love. I saw your name in the paper again. The review said the lamb tasted of*—

And then nothing. The pen had lifted mid-clause and never come down. He had spent two weeks trying to finish it in his head, drafting completions the way he once drafted reviews: *of rosemary and patience; of a kitchen you left too soon; of me.* Every version sounded like him, which was the problem. It needed to sound like her, and he had spent so long not listening that he no longer trusted himself to know her voice from his own invention of it.

Sorcha had told him, on the phone last night, in her nurse's flat tone that wasn't flatness at all but discipline: *You don't get to perfect it, Rafe. You get to say it.* He'd held the phone against his ear long after she hung up, listening to the dial tone like it was weather.

The final run of the 47 was Sunday. Today was Tuesday. He counted the days the way he counted courses — first this, then that, a structure to keep the panic dressed as planning. He would board at the terminus. He would bring the letter. He had not yet decided whether he would read it aloud, and he suspected the decision would be made for him somewhere around the third stop, by whatever lived in him that had been waiting nine years for a vehicle moving slowly enough to catch.

He wrote in his notebook, because the notebook was where he put things he couldn't say:

*Inventory, final week. One brother, half-forgiven, fully loved. One first love, re-met, unspent. One chef, dying, teaching me backwards. One letter, unfinished, mine to finish or fail at. One bus. One chance.*

Then, underneath, smaller:

*Taste report: this morning's coffee tasted of something. I couldn't name it. I didn't try. Some flavours you ruin by naming.*

* * *

**Declan**

Declan Kavanagh had been pulling pints in the Harbour Light for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one thing worth passing on: people don't come to pubs to drink. They come to be near other people without having to talk to them. The drink is just the excuse. He'd built a life on being someone's excuse, and mostly he'd been fine with it.

This week, though, the regulars wouldn't leave him alone about Sunday.

"You're doing the last run, are you?" Mrs. Fennelly asked, third time she'd asked, as if repetition could make it true faster. "Your ma's bus. You have to do your ma's bus."

"I'm doing it," he said. "I said I'm doing it."

"Rafael's going too?"

"He's going." Declan wiped the counter in a slow circle. "We're going together. It'll be grand."

It would not be grand. It would be Rafael standing up on a bus full of strangers reading their dead mother's handwriting out loud, because Sorcha Byrne had apparently told him that goodbyes were for the living, and Rafael had taken to Sorcha's pronouncements lately the way he'd once taken to Marchetti's — as gospel delivered by someone with better hands. Declan loved his brother. He also wanted, some evenings, to shake him until the performance fell out.

But here was the thing Declan hadn't said to anyone, the thing he'd been carrying since the night of the fight, three weeks ago now, when Rafael had stood in the rain outside the pub looking at him like a stranger: he was glad Rafael was coming back. Not glad the way you're glad about weather. Glad the way you're glad about a pulse.

He'd hidden Nuala's letter for six years. He'd told himself it was protection — Rafe was raw, Rafe was barely holding on, the letter would only hurt him. All true, probably. Also true: if Rafael came home, Declan stopped being the son who stayed. The loyal one. The one whose whole identity was built on the terrible arithmetic of remaining. He had resented his mother for writing it and himself for hiding it and Rafael for making both feel inevitable.

Now the letter was found, the fight was fought, and what remained was something Declan didn't have a word for. Forgiveness was too finished a word. It was more like a tap left running — you couldn't shut it off, you could only learn to live with the sound.

On Thursday night he climbed the stairs to the old back room, the one behind the wardrobe wall, and stood in the dark with his hand flat against the plaster. Their mother had kept her sewing box in here. Her good scissors. Once, when they were boys, Rafael had hidden in this room for four hours after breaking a window, and Declan had covered for him, and Nuala had known — of course she'd known, she always knew — and said nothing, and made Declan's favourite dinner as payment. She was not a saint. She was funnier than a saint and harder than one, and she'd have boxed both their ears for the way they'd carried on these past years, each guarding his own little vault of silence like it was treasure instead of just weight.

"Sunday," Declan said to the empty room. "We're getting the bus, Ma. Both of us. You'd laugh."

He thought she would. That was the strange mercy of it — he could still hear her laugh even though he'd never lost anything else of hers. Taste, sight, whatever it was Rafael had lost. Declan had lost nothing sensory. He'd lost the habit of saying things, which was worse, because nobody wrote articles about it.

He went back down, tied on his apron, and opened the doors. First pint of the evening to Mrs. Fennelly, on the house, because Sunday was coming and some debts you pay early.

* * *

**Sorcha**

Sorcha Byrne worked the late shift on Ward 4, where the average age was eighty-one and the average honesty was higher than anywhere else in the county. People at the end said what they meant. It was the one perk of the job, if you could call it a perk — standing witness while the human animal, stripped of everything performative, finally spoke plainly. *Tell my daughter I kept the drawings. Tell my son it wasn't his fault. Tell whoever finds the letter in the drawer that I tried.*

She had learned from the dying that almost nobody regrets what they did. They regret what they didn't say. This was not wisdom; it was data. Twenty-two years of nights, and the data was unanimous.

So when Rafael called her — no, when she called Rafael, after Declan let slip the whole business of the letters, because Declan Kavanagh could keep a secret for six years but not for six weeks once it started hurting him — she had said the plain thing: *Goodbyes aren't for the dying, Rafe. They're for the living. The dying already know.* And he had gone quiet in that way of his, the long receiver-against-ear quiet, and then said, "Will you come? Sunday. The last run of the 47," as if inviting her to a christening instead of an exorcism.

"I'll come," she'd said. "I'll bring tissues and I won't offer them unless you ask."

Now it was Friday night, and she was doing her rounds with the reroute date circled on the calendar in the staff room, and Mr. O'Dowd in bed three was telling her about his wife's apple tart for the fourth time this week, and she was thinking about taste.

That was the thing about Rafael she'd never understood until recently — until the letters, until the confession he'd finally made to her on the phone, voice cracking on the word *tasteless* like the word itself had gone off. She'd assumed his problem was grief. Everyone assumed that. But grief, she knew from the ward, eventually becomes a room you can live in. What Rafael had done was different. He'd locked the room and thrown away the key and then spent nine years describing the house to strangers as if he still lived there. The reviews. God, the reviews — she'd read them for years without knowing, beautiful lies, the most literate form of starvation she'd ever encountered.

And now he could taste again — a little, he said, salt and sweetness, soda bread, coffee he couldn't name — and instead of celebrating he was terrified, because flavour meant the numbness had been a choice, and choices can be judged.

"More tart talk, Mr. O'Dowd?" she said, adjusting his blanket.

"Cinnamon," he said. "She put cinnamon in it and never told a soul. Took me forty years to catch her." He smiled at the ceiling. "You want to know a secret, nurse? I never told her I caught her. Let her have her mystery."

Sorcha stood very still.

"That's the wisest thing anyone's said on this ward all year," she told him, and meant it, and thought: *that's what Rafael needs to learn. Not to solve the mystery. To let her have it.*

On Saturday afternoon she took the 47 into town — deliberately, one of the second-to-last runs, wanting to know the route in her body before Sunday — and watched the neighbourhood go by the window: the launderette, the chipper, the hill. Somewhere on this road Nuala Halloran had ridden every week with her shopping bags, drafting letters in her head she'd never send, waiting for a son who sent taxis instead of himself.

Sorcha pressed her forehead briefly to the cold glass.

Sunday, then. Three of them on a bus full of strangers, and Rafael with a letter that broke off mid-sentence, and the whole route lit end to end like a runway. She'd seen enough last words to know how this usually went: badly, beautifully, and not at all as planned.

She got off at her stop and looked back at the bus — the 47, dented, diesel-breathed, faithful — pulling away toward the terminus where tomorrow it would turn around and begin its final week.

"Hold on," she said to it quietly, "one more trip."

* * *

**Rafael**

Saturday night, Rafael cooked.

Not well — his hands shook, and the flat's oven ran hot, and he'd bought the wrong flour twice — but he cooked Nuala's soda bread anyway, because Sorcha had said *eat something real before you speak something real*, and because the recipe was in her handwriting on the back of a 1998 electricity bill, and because the last time he'd baked it he had tasted her for the first time in nine years and he needed, tonight, to check that it was still there. That it hadn't been a fluke. That permission, once granted, stayed granted.

Buttermilk, bicarbonate, flour, salt. Cross cut deep in the top — *so it knows how to open*, she used to say, and he'd laughed at ten and believed it at forty-three. The flat filled with the smell of it, warm and plain and slightly burnt at the edges because her oven had run hot too, and when he broke the first crust the steam came up and he ate a piece standing at the counter like a thief, and there it was: salt, sweetness, something underneath that had no name and didn't need one.

Still there. Still hers. Still his, if he kept saying yes to it.

He washed his hands, dried them, and took the unfinished letter from the table. Read it through once more. *The review said the lamb tasted of—*

Tomorrow, on the bus, he would have to finish it. Out loud. In front of Declan, who hid letters to protect people; in front of Sorcha, who watched people die honestly for a living; in front of strangers who had ridden this route beside his mother for decades and never known her name. He rehearsed endings in the shower, on the walk, into the dark of the ceiling at 3 a.m., and discarded every one. Nothing he drafted was worthy. Everything he drafted was, Sorcha would say, beside the point.

At midnight he set out his clothes like a schoolboy — the grey coat, the scarf she'd knitted with one dropped stitch near the hem that he'd never had the heart to fix — and put the letters in order inside his satchel, hers beneath his, the unfinished one on top where his hand could find it without looking.

Last entry in the notebook:

*Final inventory. Tomorrow: one bus, one brother, one first love, one letter, forty-one stops. If I cannot finish her sentence, I will begin my own. If my voice shakes, let it. If I taste nothing, I will say so. If I taste everything —*

He stopped. Looked at the dash for a long moment.

*— then everyone on that bus had better hold onto something.*

He turned off the light. Outside, far off, he heard the night bus pass, air brakes sighing at a stop no one waited at anymore, carrying on regardless, one week from the end of its line.

* * *

Sunday came up grey and clean, the rain having spent itself overnight. At the terminus, the 47 sat at the kerb with its destination blind glowing — *CITY — HILL ROAD* — and a small crowd gathering that had no official reason to gather: Mrs. Fennelly in her good coat, two teenagers filming nothing in particular, a man with a folded walking stick, Declan with his hands jammed in his pockets like a man attending a funeral he'd helped cause, and Sorcha, who caught Rafael's eye across the shelter and mouthed, plainly, *say it*.

The driver, a broad woman with reading glasses pushed up into her hair, checked her watch and called out, unhurried, to no one and everyone:

"Last run. All aboard."

Rafael's hand found the satchel strap. Found the letter through the canvas. Held his breath — and stepped on.
