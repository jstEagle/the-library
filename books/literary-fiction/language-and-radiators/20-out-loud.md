## 20. Out Loud

The last week of October arrived the way Nuala Kavanagh used to arrive at the pub on a Sunday—without announcement, without apology, taking up more room than expected. The 47 was being decommissioned on the thirty-first. Not the bus itself; the *route*. The council had published a consultation nobody had answered, and now a new line would swing east to serve the retail park, shaving eleven minutes off a journey nobody had complained about and abandoning the terraced streets of Carrigmore entirely. Rafael had read the notice three times before he understood that it was real, that a road he had ridden his whole life—first in his mother's lap, then beside Sorcha with their knees knocking, then alone with his collar up and his heart shut—was simply going to stop existing as a thing you could take.

Six weeks ago that deadline had felt like a blade. Now, standing at the shelter on O'Connell Street at ten minutes to seven on the final evening, it felt like a door held open by someone patient enough to wait for him.

The shelter had been decorated. Somebody—Declan, obviously; the handwriting on the cardboard sign was unmistakably the same hand that had written *CLOSED FOR A FUNERAL* over the pub door the week their father died—had taped a banner to the perspex: **GOODNIGHT AND THANK YOU, 47**. Beneath it, a plastic garland of autumn leaves that had already begun to shed its leaves onto the pavement, so that the shelter appeared to be gently, continuously dying.

"You look terrible," Declan said, coming up beside him. He was wearing the good coat, the one with the collar, and he had shaved. Under the streetlight his face was a map of their mother: the same heavy brow, the same crooked left eyebrow that rose when a sentence required skepticism.

"You look like you're going to a wedding."

"I look like a man who respects public transport." Declan handed him a flask. "Tea. Mam's way. Don't ask what's in it, it's half butter."

Rafael laughed, and the laugh surprised him by being real. Nine years since his mother died and he could still count on one hand the number of times grief had allowed him something whole and unguarded. He uncapped the flask and the smell rose up—strong tea, salt butter, a ghost of nutmeg—and for one vertiginous second he thought he *tasted* it. Then the sensation folded itself away again, the way a bird folds its wings, and he was left holding warmth and nothing else. But it had been closer this time. Every day since the soda bread it had been closer.

"Where is she?" he asked.

"Parking her car. She insists on driving to the terminus and bussing back, which is the most Sorcha thing I've ever heard." Declan looked down the empty street. "Raffie."

Rafael flinched at the name. Nobody had used it since he was nineteen and still answered to it.

"I'm not going to make a speech," Declan said. "I've said my piece and most of it was shite and I'm sorry for the half of it. But whatever you're planning to do on that bus—and I know you're planning something, you've had that envelope in your inside pocket since Tuesday like it's a organ you're carrying—you don't have to. You could just ride it. Sit at the back with us and look out the window and let it be a bus."

"I know."

"Because if you do the thing, and it goes wrong, and forty strangers stare at you—"

"Then I'll have done it wrong in front of forty strangers," Rafael said, "instead of not doing it in front of no one. I've had nine years of the second one. I'd like to try the first."

Declan studied him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way their mother used to nod when a customer paid a tab in full after three months of promises: acknowledgment, no sentiment, debt closed.

"There she is," Declan said.

Sorcha came up the street in a green coat, her hair loose for once, carrying a paper bag that smelled, even from ten feet away, even through Rafael's ruined and half-mended senses, of her kitchen: rosemary and something caramelized. She kissed Declan's cheek, then stood in front of Rafael and looked at him the way she had looked at him at seventeen, across the smoke of a bonfire on the strand—assessing, amused, unfooled.

"You've been rehearsing," she said.

"I've been *reading*."

"You've been standing in your kitchen reading it out loud to the kettle. Declan told me. You've got it memorized, haven't you?"

He had. He knew every line of Nuala's final letter the way he knew the route itself: where it turned, where it hesitated, where the handwriting lost its footing and the pen dragged a silver trench through the paper. *My darling Raffie, if you're reading this then I've run out of time to say it properly, which is a joke on me because I've had nine years to say it properly and I spent them writing drafts.* He knew the place where it broke off. *The trouble with you, my love, is that you think—*

And then nothing. A week before she died. The pen lifted and never came down.

"You don't have to finish it," Sorcha said quietly, reading him. "You know that, don't you? The letter doesn't need completing. It's not an exam."

"I know it's not an exam." He put his hand flat against his chest, against the envelope. "But I've spent nine years treating everything unfinished like it was one. Grades to be earned. Reviews to be filed. Grief to be *managed*. She spent her whole life telling me the opposite and I wouldn't listen until the words came out of a box behind a wardrobe." He looked at the road, where the headlights were coming. "I want to finish it. Out loud. Where it can't be filed away."

The headlights swung wide and the 47 came around the corner, and Rafael felt his chest tighten at the sight of it, which was absurd, it was a bus, it was a dented red double-decker with a new scratch along the nearside and an advertisement for a mobile phone network across its upper deck. But it was also the shape of his entire childhood. It was the bus that had carried him to Marchetti's kitchen at seventeen with a borrowed knife roll and no idea what he was walking into. It was the bus he had taken away from Carrigmore at twenty-two with a suitcase and a scholarship and the firm belief that he was leaving for good, and it was the bus that had brought him back, too rarely, always too rarely, for Christmas and funerals and one bright impossible June when Sorcha had still been his.

The doors hissed open. The driver was a big woman in her fifties with a lanyard and the expression of someone who had driven this route long enough to have opinions about it.

"Evening," she said. "You're the Kavanaghs, I'm told. Your brother rang the depot. There's a crowd coming, so mind the step, and if anyone starts singing I'm pulling over."

"Who's singing?" Declan said.

"You are, apparently. Everyone's been telling me all week." She waved them aboard. "Go on. Front seat. You've earned it."

* * *

The bus filled the way a church fills before a wedding: in clusters, in murmurs, in the small negotiations of strangers sharing a bench. Rafael watched them come up the steps and felt something in him loosen and tighten at once. There was Mrs. O'Rourke from the chemist's, who had filled his mother's prescriptions for eleven years and never once mentioned that Nuala's letters were the talk of the post office. There was the two boys from the chipper with their bikes folded and their hoodies up, and old Fintan Daly who had driven the 47 for thirty years before his retirement and who now, uninvited, unannounced, took the seat directly behind the driver as though the seat had been held for him, which perhaps it had. There was a young woman with a baby who didn't cry the whole journey, and a man in a hi-vis vest who smelled faintly of asphalt and sat with his eyes closed, and a teenager filming everything on her phone until her mother gently pushed the phone down and the teenager, remarkably, let her.

Forty people. Maybe fifty. People who had ridden this bus to work and to hospital appointments and to their mothers' houses and back, and who had heard, through the small dense telegraphy of a neighborhood, that the Kavanagh lad was going to do *a thing*, and had come to see it, or to shield it, or simply because when a thing ends you want to be in the room.

Declan sat by the window. Sorcha sat beside Rafael, and her hand found his on the seat between them, and neither of them made anything of it, and it was the most made-anything-of hand-holding of his life.

The bus pulled out. O'Connell Street slid past the window, then the bridge, then the long straight run out of town where the streetlights came in pairs like held breaths. Rafael knew every one of them. He had kissed a girl under the fourth pair. He had been sick behind the ninth after Marchetti's staff Christmas dinner, 1994. He had sat on the upper deck at nineteen, the night before leaving for the city, and watched Carrigmore's lights come up out of the dark and thought: *I'll come back when I'm someone.* He had never once thought to ask what his mother had been doing at that same window, on that same route, watching the same lights, waiting for the same son.

The bus climbed. The engine worked. Somewhere behind him, Mrs. O'Rourke was telling the hi-vis man about her hip, and the baby made a sound like a small kettle, and Fintan Daly was giving the driver unsolicited advice about the timing of the lights on the ring road, and it was all so ordinary, so unbearably ordinary, that Rafael understood suddenly why hospice wards had taught Sorcha what she knew. This was the vehicle. Not the bus—the ordinariness. You said the true thing inside the ordinary thing or you never said it at all.

The bus turned onto the Nuala Road.

Of course it was called that now; they'd renamed it in her honor two years after she died, the council's small cheap penance, and Rafael had avoided the street ever since because seeing her name on a green sign had felt like being scolded by a municipality. But there it was, sliding past the window, and there was the pub with its lights on and its door propped open—Declan had left it open, *CLOSED FOR THE 47* on the sign, so that anyone walking out could see the glow—and there was the corner where the wardrobe had stood in the upstairs flat, and Rafael stood up.

The bus did not stop. It kept going, because that was the miracle of buses, they went on regardless, and so he stood there swaying slightly with his hand on the pole and fifty strangers' faces turning toward him like flowers toward an uncertain sun, and his heart was going so hard he could feel it in his teeth, and he took out the letter.

"Sorry," he said. His voice cracked on it. He cleared his throat. "Sorry. My name's Rafael Kavanagh. Most of you knew my mother. The road we're on is named for her, which she'd have found hilarious, because she spent forty years complaining that the council wouldn't fix the footpaths and now they've named the whole street after her and the footpaths are still broken."

The laugh that went through the bus was warm and low, and it carried him. He unfolded the pages. His hands were not steady. He let them not be steady.

"She died nine years ago this month. And last summer, clearing out the flat, I found a box behind her wardrobe. Forty letters. Half of them hers, to me—drafts, she never sent a single one—and half of them mine to her, returned unopened, because I'd addressed them to the old flat and she'd already moved to the nursing home, and nobody thought to tell me." He heard Declan shift behind him and did not turn around. "That's not a complaint. There's enough blame in this story to go around and I've had my full share. This is hers. This is the last thing she wrote. It stops in the middle of a sentence, a week before she died, and I've spent nine years—well. I've spent nine years being a man who finishes other people's sentences and never his own. So I'm going to read it to you. And then I'm going to finish it. Because she wrote it to be heard, and I'm the one who didn't listen, and you're all here, and the bus is going where it's always gone, one last time."

Sorcha's hand pressed his ankle, once, and let go.

He read.

*My darling Raffie, if you're reading this then I've run out of time to say it properly, which is a joke on me because I've had nine years to say it properly and I spent them writing drafts. Your father always said I'd die with a half-written letter in the typewriter and he was a prophet as well as a nuisance.*

*Here is what I could not say to your face, which is a fault in me, not in you: I watched you leave and I let you. I could have made it harder. I could have cried at the door, I could have guilted you the way my mother guilted me, and God knows the material was there. But you were so *hungry*, my love. You looked at the world the way you looked at a plate of food you weren't allowed to touch yet, and I thought, if I hold him here he'll resent me, and if I let him go he'll forget me, and of the two I chose the one that hurt me less. That's not sacrifice. That's arithmetic. Don't let anyone at your fancy dinners tell you otherwise.*

*The trouble with you, my love, is that you think—*

The sentence ended. The pen had dragged. Rafael stood on the swaying bus on the road named for her, in front of fifty strangers and a brother and a woman he had loved at seventeen and loved now in some older, less flammable way, and he finished it.

"—that you think love is a thing you have to be *worthy of*," he said. "That's how I'd finish it. I think that's what she was going to say, because she said it to me a hundred times in a hundred other words. You think you have to earn your place at the table. You think the seat is only yours if you've brought something. And she spent my whole childhood trying to tell me the seat was mine. Just mine. Empty or full, successful or failing, tasting or not tasting—the seat was mine."

His voice went, then. Not all the way; enough. He stood in the aisle of the 47 with the letter trembling in his hand and let the silence be what it was, and the bus carried them all through it, and nobody told him to hurry up, and the teenager with the phone had put it away.

"So here is what I want to say to her," he went on, quieter. "Mam. I'm sorry I made your arithmetic necessary. I'm sorry I confused leaving with becoming. I'm sorry about the letters—I read every one, I've read them so many times I could recite them, and the worst one, the one that wrecked me, is the one where you say you're not asking me to come home, you're only asking me to *write and tell me you're alright*, and I couldn't even do that, because I'd stopped being able to tell what was true, including about myself." He swallowed. "I lost my taste, Mam. Nine years ago, around when you died. I've been a food critic who can't taste food. I've been writing raves about meals I couldn't have described to save my life. And I'm not saying that for sympathy—I'm saying it because you always said the truth was the only seasoning that mattered, and I've been cooking with nothing for nine years, and I'm done."

The bus turned at the top of the Nuala Road, past the last streetlight, and began the long descent back toward the town, and the whole of Carrigmore lay below them in the dark with its windows lit, and Rafael saw it the way she must have seen it from this exact seat, this exact curve, a hundred Sunday nights: not small. *Held.*

"Here's what I know now," he said. "I know that taste isn't in the tongue. I know that because a seventy-nine-year-old chef with failing hands told me, and because I burned a loaf of your soda bread in October and the second one came out right and I *tasted* it, Mam, I tasted it, the caraway and the salt and the burnt edge, and it wasn't nostalgia, it was *permission*. You were never asking me to come back. You were asking me to belong *somewhere*. Anywhere. Out loud. And I'm saying yes. I'm saying yes to the pub and the Sunday dinners and the brother I fought with in the rain like we were twelve. I'm saying yes to a woman who nurses the dying and laughs at my cowardice, which is the correct amount. I'm saying yes to telling the truth in print even if it costs me the career I built on a lie. And I'm saying yes to this bus, this stupid, beautiful bus, because you rode it every week of my childhood and I never once asked you what you thought about at the window, and I'm asking now, out loud, in front of everyone, knowing the answer is going to be *you, love, I was thinking about you*, and knowing I'll have to live with that, and *wanting* to live with that—"

He stopped. Not because he'd run out of words. Because the bus had slowed, and Fintan Daly had risen from his seat behind the driver, and the old man put his hand briefly on Rafael's arm as he passed—*good lad, that's it, that's the stuff*—and went down the steps at the corner by the strand, where the route used to turn before the ring road, and stood on the pavement and watched them go, and Rafael understood that he was not the only one on this bus saying goodbye to something.

The bus went on. The town came up to meet them. And in the warm, swaying, butter-tea-and-asphalt-smelling dark of the 47, fifty strangers began, without arrangement, without a single word of coordination, to do the thing that Carrigmore does at funerals and at matches and at the end of the night in Declan's back room: they began to hum. Low, tuneless, unembarrassed—the shape of a song without insisting on it—and the hi-vis man's voice rose out of it, actually singing, *and the sea rolled up, and the sea rolled down*, and Mrs. O'Rourke joined, and the baby laughed, a bright sudden syllable like a spoon against a glass, and Sorcha was crying and laughing at once and Declan was singing in a voice like a gravel driveway and Rafael stood in the aisle with his mother's letter against his chest and tasted, plain as day, unmistakable, the salt of his own face and, underneath it, faint and rising and real, the sweetness of the butter in the tea.

* * *

The terminus was a puddled square by the depot with one sodium lamp and a crowd of maybe thirty more people who had come to see the bus come home. The driver brought the 47 in slow, took the turn wider than she needed to, and stopped, and let the engine idle a moment before she killed it, the way you let a note ring.

Rafael was the last one down the steps. On the pavement, Declan put his arm around his shoulders—brief, hard, their mother's gesture exactly—and Sorcha stood on his other side, and the crowd was dispersing into the dark with that post-funeral lightness, people saying *see you Sunday, mind yourself, did you hear about the footpaths*.

The driver came around the front with her lanyard swinging. She looked at Rafael, and then at the letter still in his hand.

"You'll want to keep that dry," she said. "Weather's turning." She dug in her coat and produced a clear plastic sleeve, the kind for route maps, and held it out. "For the letter. And—Kavanagh." She nodded back at the bus, dark now, ticking as it cooled. "I drove your mother every Tuesday for six years. The nursing home run. She sat right there, front seat, and every week she'd ask me would I tell you something if I saw you, and every week I said of course, and every week she'd change her mind and say *ah no, he's a busy man, I'll write it down*." The driver smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "I've forty-one weeks of *I'll write it down* on my conscience. You're not the only one in this town who never said the thing."

She walked away toward the depot office, and Rafael stood holding the plastic sleeve in the sodium light, and Sorcha said, "Raffie," very gently, and he said, "I know," and did not move.

Because the driver had said *forty-one weeks*, and forty-one weeks was nine years, and Rafael was doing the arithmetic he could not stop doing, the arithmetic of all the Tuesdays, all the rides, all the times the 47 had passed him on O'Connell Street while he stood at a bus stop with his collar up—and somewhere in those forty-one weeks, in some front seat on some Tuesday, his mother had sat down beside this woman and opened her mouth to send a message and closed it again, and Rafael would never know what the message was, and the not-knowing was not a wound this time. It was a door. It was the same door the unfinished sentence had been. It was the thing he would carry into whatever came next, and carry *lightly*, the way you carry a recipe you haven't perfected yet.

"Come on," Declan said. "Pub's open. There's a fire on and Sorcha's brought a tray of something that smells illegal, and I've got a bottle behind the bar that's been waiting nine years for an occasion." He paused. "And Raffie—there's a letter on the bar. Arrived this morning. City postmark."

Rafael turned. "From who?"

Declan's crooked eyebrow went up. "Your editor," he said. "The review's out tomorrow. And there's a second envelope with it that I think you're going to want to open sober, except you won't, so let's go."
