## 2. Nocturne, Interrupted

The conservatory emptied the way water leaves a sink—slowly at first, then all at once. By ten o'clock the last stragglers had packed their scores and clattered down the marble stairs, and by eleven the building belonged to its pipes, its radiators ticking like patient clocks, and to me.

I worked the second floor that night. Hallway C first, then the practice rooms along the east wall, mopping in long even strokes that I had refined over four years into something close to meditation. There is a way to clean a floor so thoroughly that you erase yourself in the process. You learn the rhythm of it. Push, turn, wring. The mop becomes an extension of the arms, the arms become nothing, and nothing is exactly what I wanted to be at 11:40 on a Thursday night in October.

Except someone was still playing.

Room 214. The light leaked under the door in a thin gold line, and behind it, a Chopin nocturne—the one in C-sharp minor, the one every ambitious teenager learns too early because it sounds harder than it is and feels harder than it should be.

My hands stopped on the mop handle.

I want to say I felt nothing. That would be the tidy version, the version where twenty years of careful avoidance works the way it's supposed to. But the truth is that the opening bars went through me like cold water through cloth, and for a moment I stood in that hallway holding a mop like a stranger holding a mop, not knowing whose arms these were or why they were shaking.

It was the fingering that saved me. Music can gut you; technique just annoys you. And this kid's technique was wrong—not catastrophically, but wrong in a specific, recognizable way. She was playing the descending passage in the middle section with her thumb crossing under on the third beat instead of the fourth, which meant she'd have to stretch for the E-natural, which meant the phrase would sag right where Chopin needed it to lift.

She played it again. Same mistake. Again. Same mistake, and now a frustrated little thump of the pedal, the sound of a sixteen-year-old at war with herself.

Push, turn, wring. I could have moved on. Hallway D was waiting. The urinals in the men's room on three never cleaned themselves, and no one had ever paid me to care about a student's thumb.

But my feet didn't move. That was the thing I noticed later, standing there in the dark with the mop dripping quietly onto the tile: my body had made a decision my mind hadn't authorized. I was listening the way I used to listen—diagnostically, hungrily, the whole architecture of the piece unfolding in my head whether I wanted it or not. The left hand was rushing in the third bar of the melody. Her dynamics were all foreground, no horizon. She breathed in the wrong places because she'd learned the piece from a recording, probably, some competition winner's glossy version, and she was imitating a performance instead of speaking a language.

Again. The same stumble. A sound this time—half groan, half word—and then silence, and then the chair creaking as she reset herself.

"Thumb under on four," I said.

The words came out before I understood I was saying them. They came out low, through the door, barely above the hum of the radiator, but the room beyond went absolutely silent in the way only a practice room can go silent—that felt-lined, padded hush designed to keep sound in and the world out.

I stood frozen. The mop dripped. Somewhere below us a door slammed, distant as weather.

Then, from inside: "Hello?"

I said nothing. I began, very calmly, with the composure of a man walking away from a crime scene, to push my mop toward the stairwell.

"Hello?" Louder now. The bench scraped. "Is somebody out there?"

The door opened before I reached the corner. I heard it—I heard the knob turn, the hinges give—and I did what I had trained myself to do for two decades, the one skill Marta Ilves never taught me but life had drilled into me more thoroughly than any scale: I disappeared. Around the corner, into the service alcove where the cleaning cart lived between uses, and I stood among the bottles and the spare rolls of paper towel with my heart going like a metronome set to a tempo no human music requires.

Footsteps in the hallway. Small ones, quick ones. A pause near the cart—I could see the shadow of her feet under the gap at the bottom of the alcove's curtain, two dark shapes on the lit tile.

"Mr. Janitor?" she called out, and there was something in her voice that wasn't anger. It was the other thing, the dangerous thing. Curiosity. "I know you're there. Nobody else is in the building."

I waited. She waited. The radiators ticked our standoff for us.

"You said four," she said finally. "The fourth beat. Not the third." A pause. "Why?"

I didn't answer. After a while her shadow shifted, turned, retreated. The practice room door closed. And then—this is the part I still don't fully understand, the part I've turned over so many times it's gone smooth as a river stone—she started again. From the top. And when she reached the passage, her thumb crossed under on the fourth beat, and the phrase lifted, and the E-natural arrived where it belonged, bright and inevitable, and I stood in that alcove smelling of floor wax with tears running silently down my face, which was absurd, which was humiliating, which changed nothing about the fact that they kept coming until the piece ended.

* * *

Her name, I learned over the following days without asking anyone, was Noor Ferreira. This is the kind of thing a janitor knows. We are the intelligence service of every institution—we know who cries in which stairwell, who eats lunch alone, who has started wearing a new coat. The faculty roster in the front office listed her: Ferreira, Noor, age 16, second-year diploma program, instructor R. Vasquez. I saw her twice that week in daylight, once hurrying across the courtyard with her case banging against her leg and once sitting alone in the cafeteria with a sandwich and a theory workbook, annotating it with the ferocious concentration of someone who believes the answers are in there if she can just dig deep enough.

She was small and dark-haired and carried herself slightly forward, like a person perpetually about to start running. When she passed me in the corridor—and she passed me several times, because that is the arrangement, students move through custodians like weather fronts through landscape—her eyes flicked up, held for half a second too long, and dropped. She was looking for something in my face. I gave her nothing. I have a face built for giving nothing; it's the one feature of my appearance I've ever been grateful for.

But she knew. Of course she knew. There was only one voice behind that door, and voices are fingerprints.

On Friday night she was in 214 again, past closing, working on the same nocturne. I heard it from the far end of the hall and made the tactical error of finishing Hallway D first, telling myself I'd circle back when she was done, that proximity was a choice I could simply decline. But the piece pulled me down the corridor the way current pulls a swimmer, and by the time she reached the middle section I was standing outside the door again, mop idle, listening against every resolution I owned.

She got through it clean. All of it. Thumb on four, breath in the right places, and at the final pianissimo she did something no recording had taught her—she let the last chord decay into true silence, held the pedal past the point of comfort, past the point of safety, trusting the room to finish the sentence. It was the first genuinely musical decision I'd heard anyone make in that building in years.

"That was better," I said. God help me. I said it out loud, through the door, in the dark.

This time there was no pause. The bench scraped immediately, footsteps crossed the room, and the door swung open onto a girl of sixteen with wild hair and a sweat-damp collar and eyes like struck matches.

"You," she said.

"I'm sorry. I shouldn't have—" I was already turning, already retreating, the mop clutched between us like a barrier.

"No, wait." She planted herself in the doorway. Up close she was thinner than she'd looked from a distance, with the translucent look of a kid running on ambition and vending machine food. "You're the one who talked last week. Through the door."

"I didn't—"

"You said thumb under on four. And it worked." She said *it worked* the way other people say *I love you*, with her whole chest. "I've been doing it your way for six days and Ms. Vasquez asked me today what changed. What changed. Like it was a mystery." She squinted at me. "Who are you?"

"Nobody. Custodial staff. I need to finish the floors."

"Custodial staff don't hear a thumb crossing and know which beat it lands on." She stepped out into the hallway, blocking my path with the fearless illogic of youth. "Custodial staff don't say *that was better* like—" she stopped, and I watched the thought arrive in her face, watched it land. "You play."

"I don't."

"You did."

"The floors," I said, and moved around her, and she let me go, but she called after me down the corridor, not shouting, just sending the words after me like stones skipped across water:

"I'll be here tomorrow night. Same time. In case nobody shows up."

Nobody showed up. I showed up. I stood in the alcove for forty minutes listening to her run the coda, and when she stumbled on the voicing of the inner melody—burying the tune under the accompaniment, the classic sin, the sin I committed at her age nightly while Marta rapped my knuckles with a pencil—I heard myself clear my throat, and heard her stop playing instantly, and heard her say, into the silence, "Inner voice. Right? It's supposed to sing?"

"It's supposed to sing," I said, and fled, and lay awake until three in the morning staring at the ceiling of my apartment above the laundromat, cataloguing everything I had just done to myself.

* * *

Here is what I knew, and what I told myself I didn't know: you cannot open that door a crack. I had spent twenty years learning this. The door doesn't work in increments. Marta Ilves taught me that too, though she'd have denied the lesson's existence. You touch music once, truly, and it takes your hand in its teeth and pulls. At nineteen I had walked away from a career, a name, a future—all of it abandoned in a single night with a suitcase and a bus ticket—and I had survived since then by treating music the way a reformed man treats his drug of choice: total abstinence, no exceptions, no *just listening*, no humming in the shower. The mop was my metronome. The mop asked nothing of me but repetition.

And now there was a girl in Room 214 who practiced past closing five nights a week, and I had spoken to her through a door twice, and both times the speaking had cost me something I couldn't itemize but could feel missing, the way you feel a tooth gone.

The following Tuesday she left a note taped to the cleaning cart. It said: *Chopin Op. 27 No. 1 — measure 41 — the leap is impossible. Please advise.* No name. As if we were spies. As if either of us could pretend, after this, that we didn't know exactly who we were.

I should have thrown it away. I read it eleven times under the utility light, and then I wrote the answer on the back in pencil—*drop the wrist through the leap, don't reach; the interval is smaller than it looks if you stop aiming*—and hated myself with a thoroughness that surprised me, because underneath the self-hatred, quiet as a pilot light, something else was burning. Something that felt disgracefully like being needed.

Wednesday night she played measure 41 with the drop I'd described, and the leap landed soft as snowfall, and through the door I heard her laugh—a short, startled, triumphant laugh, the laugh of a person who has just discovered the world contains secrets worth knowing—and I stood in the dark hallway with my forehead nearly touching the felt-lined door, closer to another human being than I had been in years, separated from her by two inches of soundproofing and twenty years of silence.

On Thursday, the note on the cart said: *Same time tomorrow. I'm bringing Rachmaninoff. Bring yourself.*

I stood there a long time. Down the hall, the radiators ticked. Somewhere below, the building breathed its old-building breath, warm air rising through bones of brick and iron. I thought about Hallway D. I thought about the men's room on three. I thought about how easy it would be, how safe, how the rest of my life could continue exactly as designed if I simply pushed the cart in the other direction.

I took the note off the cart, folded it, and put it in my shirt pocket, over the heart, like evidence.

Then I finished Hallway D.
