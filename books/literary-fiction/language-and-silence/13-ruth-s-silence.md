## 13. Ruth's Silence

The law office of Okafor & Associates occupied the second floor of a converted bank on Corliss Street, all brass fixtures and marble worn soft at the edges, and Oscar stood outside it for eleven minutes on a Tuesday morning, watching their own reflection ghosted against the glass door, mop handle calluses and all, before they could make their feet move.

They had called ahead. That was the strange part—how easy it had been, once the decision was made. *I'd like to speak with Ruth Okafor about the Ilves estate.* No rehearsal, no script. The words had come out of them whole, like a stone finally pried loose from a boot.

Inside, a receptionist with reading glasses on a chain looked up, took in the work jacket, the canvas bag, and asked if Oscar was there about a cleaning contract.

"I'm here about a will," Oscar said.

Something shifted in the woman's face—not recognition exactly, but the look of someone who has been waiting for weather.

Ruth Okafor received them in an office lined with legal volumes nobody opened anymore, everything living now in the gray light of monitors. She was smaller than Oscar remembered, or perhaps Oscar had been smaller then—twelve years old, brought here once to sign something they hadn't understood, Marta's hand heavy between their shoulder blades like a benediction or a brand. Ruth's hair was iron-gray now, cropped close, and her eyes were the same: dark, unhurried, the eyes of someone who had spent forty years watching people discover what documents said about the dead.

"Oscar Crane." She said it without inflection, but she said all of it—the name entire, no hesitation over the surname that wasn't Ilves, no softening. "Sit down, please."

Oscar sat. The chair was leather, too warm, as if someone had just left it.

"You've been asking questions," Ruth said. "About storage units. About your mother's—" She stopped, corrected herself with visible precision. "About Marta Ilves's final arrangements."

"Not your mother," Oscar said quietly. It came out before they could weigh it, and they watched Ruth register it, file it away.

"No. Not mine." She folded her hands on the desk. "Daniel Ilves called me three weeks ago wanting to know who had been inquiring after his mother's papers. I told him attorney-client privilege prevented me from discussing anything. He did not like that answer. He is not a man who likes answers he can't purchase." She tilted her head. "You spoke to the storage facility manager. You spoke to the estate sale coordinator. You are either building a case or you are grieving. In my experience the two look identical from the outside."

"I found a receipt," Oscar said. "Signed by you. For the contents of unit 114 at StoreRight on Delancey."

"Yes."

"That's all? Just—yes?"

Ruth Okafor regarded them for a long moment. Outside the window, traffic moved along Corliss Street with its usual indifference, delivery trucks and school buses, the world conducting its business around whatever was about to happen in this room.

"Do you know what my job was, when Marta hired me?" she said. "Not executor. Not initially. Twenty-two years ago she retained me to draft a document and hold it. She told me: *One day that boy will stop running, and when he does, I want there to be something waiting that isn't a lie.* Her word. *A lie.*" Ruth's mouth compressed. "She meant the version of events everyone agreed to. The conservatory's version. Daniel's version. Even yours, I suspect. The story where you simply broke, simply vanished, and Marta Ilves was merely the strict teacher history always forgives because the students turned out brilliant."

Oscar's hands had gone still in their lap. They were aware of their heartbeat, slow and enormous, like footsteps in an empty house.

"She rewrote her will," Ruth continued. "Fourteen months before she died. Lucid—I examined her myself, twice, and had a second physician do the same, because I knew what was coming. I knew her children would contest it. She left 14 Alder Lane to you outright. She endowed a trust—modest, but real—for your maintenance and, I quote her instruction letter, *'for whatever music he can bear to make.'* She named me executor. And she made me promise that the document would be read to you personally, by me, in this room, within thirty days of her death."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the hall a phone rang twice and went unanswered.

"And instead," Oscar said. Their voice sounded far away to them, borrowed.

"Instead Daniel Ilves arrived eleven days after the funeral with his sister on speakerphone and a lawyer of his own, and he sat in that chair"—she pointed, and Oscar saw it, the same leather, still warm—"and he told me that if that will surfaced, he would litigate until you were sixty years old. He told me you were unstable. He told me—and this I want you to hear precisely—he told me, *'That boy destroyed enough of my mother's life while she was alive. He doesn't get to inherit the wreckage too.'*"

*He.* Ruth had said it naturally, the way Marta used to, the pronoun sliding over Oscar like water over stone, and Oscar found their eyes stinging at the smallness of it, the enormity of being gendered correctly by a dead woman's lawyer.

"He didn't have grounds," Oscar said. "Undue influence—he was the one with the money, the family, the—"

"He had time," Ruth said. "And I had a practice to protect and a dying husband at home and no appetite for two years of depositions. I am not proud of what I did next, Mr. Crane. I am telling you that plainly so you understand I am not asking to be forgiven. I allowed the earlier will to stand. I let the estate pass as Daniel wished. I filed the codicil in a drawer and I told myself that you, wherever you were, were better off unfound." Her jaw tightened. "And then last month a janitor started asking questions about a locked desk, and I realized Marta had outmaneuvered us all, because of course she had. She always assumed you might never come. So she built a second door."

She opened the drawer.

What she laid on the desk was a folder, plain manila, softened at the corners from years of handling. On top lay a receipt—the one Oscar had already seen, the one that had led them here—but beneath it, when Ruth lifted the flap, were pages. Oscar recognized Marta's handwriting before they understood what they were looking at: the strong forward slant, the letters leaning into the future like people walking into wind.

"A certified copy," Ruth said. "The original remains in my possession, along with the physician's affidavits and Marta's instruction letter. If you choose to act, we act properly. But take this. Read it. And then come back or don't."

Oscar reached for the folder and found their hand was shaking—not the old tremor, the performance anxiety that had ended everything at nineteen, but something rawer, newer. Ruth watched it and, unexpectedly, covered their hand with her own for one moment. Her skin was dry and cool and steady.

"Before you go," she said, "you will listen to me, because I owe Marta honesty and I apparently owe you an apology, and I pay both debts at once. If you fight this—if you bring that will into the light—you will lose things you cannot currently imagine losing, because you have been invisible for twenty years and invisibility feels like safety only to those who've never had it stripped away. Daniel will spend money like blood to bury you. The conservatory will close ranks; institutions always do. Your name—which you have kept so carefully empty—will be filled up again, by strangers, with their versions. Do you understand? Vindication is a room with one door and it locks behind you."

"I scrub floors at midnight," Oscar said. "I've already lost everything I had. There isn't much left to strip."

Ruth leaned back. For the first time something almost gentle crossed her face, quickly suppressed, like a lamp turned down.

"You have a routine," she said. "You have anonymity. You may have other things I don't know about—a student, perhaps; the storage manager mentioned a young woman asking questions too." Oscar said nothing, and Ruth nodded slowly, as if the silence confirmed it. "Then hear the warning in full. Fighting costs more than you have. Winning costs more than fighting. I have watched estates devour families for decades, and the ones who win are rarely recognizable afterward as the people who began."

"Then why give me this?"

"Because Marta paid me to be honest, and I have been dishonest for six years, and the interest has compounded." She slid the folder across the desk until it touched Oscar's fingers. "Because she believed you would know what to do with it. I confess I don't share her confidence. But I'm tired of being the only one who knows what she wanted."

* * *

Oscar walked for a long time afterward. Down Corliss Street, past the shuttered cinema with its marquee selling letters—NOW PLAYING minus the A, the Y, the G—across the footbridge where the canal moved its slow brown freight, up into the cemetery on the hill without deciding to go there, legs making their own argument.

Marta's grave was easy to find. Daniel had bought a large stone, polished granite, MARTA ILVES 1941–2018, BELOVED MOTHER AND TEACHER. No mention of students. No mention of the house. Someone had left fresh flowers—white, expensive, arranged by a florist rather than grown by a hand. Daniel visited, then. Or sent someone.

Oscar stood in the cold with the folder under their arm like a splint on a broken limb.

"You knew," they said aloud. An elderly woman walking a terrier gave them a wide berth. "You knew I'd come back eventually. You couldn't just—leave it alone. Leave *me* alone."

But even as they said it, they heard the falseness, the way they'd heard the falseness in every lie they'd told Dean Whitlock, every smooth denial. Because the truth was that standing here, holding the dead woman's handwriting, Oscar felt less abandoned than they had in twenty years. Someone had thought about them, concretely, in the future tense. Someone had imagined their return and prepared for it the only way Marta knew how to prepare for anything: with paper, with leverage, with a locked drawer and instructions.

*I want there to be something waiting that isn't a lie.*

At the house, that night, Oscar did not turn on lights. They sat in the kitchen—their kitchen now, or no one's—in the blue hour-dark, and read the will by the light of a phone screen, line by line, Marta's voice rising off the page in the formal language of law punctured by her own inserted phrases, which the drafting had preserved in italics:

*To my son Oscar Crane, whom I raised and trained and failed in roughly equal measure, I leave the house at 14 Alder Lane, that he may decide for himself whether it is a home or a museum or rubble.*

*To the same, a trust, to be disbursed at his discretion, for his keep and for whatever music he can bear to make. I do not ask him to play. I ask only that he not pretend the piano was the wound. I was.*

Oscar set the phone face-down on the table. The kitchen held its breath around them.

*I was.*

Twenty years of architecture—that whole cathedral of avoidance they had built, shift by shift, corridor by corridor, the careful life with its load-bearing silences—and Marta had gone and named the flaw in the foundation in four words, from beyond, casually, the way she used to reach over mid-scale and correct a thumb position without stopping the music.

They laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and they put their head down on the cold table among the dust rings of absent jars and let it shake through them, grief and fury and something dangerously adjacent to gratitude all arriving at once, uninvited, like relatives.

When they finally lifted their head, the window over the sink showed the first gray suggestion of dawn, and Oscar understood two things with total clarity, the way you understand things at the end of a night that has dismantled you.

The first was that Ruth was right. If they fought, they would be devoured—by lawyers, by Daniel, by the conservatory, by the story itself, which would grow new versions overnight like mold. Everything they had built, thin as it was, would be spent. Noor. The boiler-room lessons. The fragile, impossible shape of a life that fit.

The second thing was that they could not put the folder back in any drawer. Not Ruth's, not their own. Marta had built a second door, and doors, once opened, stayed open in the blood.

Oscar washed their face, dressed for the day shift, and tucked the folder inside their jacket, against the chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. At the door they paused, keys in hand, and looked back at the dark kitchen of the house they legally, secretly, already owned.

Somewhere across town, Daniel Ilves was awake too. Oscar could feel it the way you feel weather coming—a pressure behind the sinuses, a taste of metal. He had lost the desk trail; he would not stop losing gracefully. And Ruth had warned them: he'd called her office three weeks ago, hungry, suspicious, closing in.

The phone buzzed in Oscar's pocket. Noor: *lesson tonight?? I fixed the passage. mostly. ok some of it.*

Oscar looked at the message for a long moment, thumb hovering, the folder pressing warm against their ribs, and typed back: *Tonight. Bring the Chopin.*

Then they stepped out into the cold morning, carrying a dead woman's will toward whatever it would cost.
