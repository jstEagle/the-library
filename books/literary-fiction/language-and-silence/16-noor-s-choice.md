## 16. Noor's Choice

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, thick as a slice of bread and twice as expensive. Noor found it wedged into the mail slot of their apartment—her father's apartment, though she had stopped thinking of it that way sometime in the past month, when the rooms had begun to feel less like home and more like a waiting room for a life she hadn't agreed to.

She knew before she opened it what it was. She had known since Saturday, since her father had come home from the conservatory gala with a shine in his eyes she hadn't seen since her mother died, talking about old friends, about the Ilves name, about doors that were opening. She had known since he said, casually, over dinner, *Daniel Ilves is a good man to know. His mother was a legend, you know. A real legend.*

The letterhead was embossed. The Ferreira International Piano Competition. She read it twice, standing in the hallway with her coat still on, and the words arranged themselves into a shape she recognized the way you recognize a trap you've already stepped in.

*We are pleased to inform you of your acceptance into the semifinal round...*

*...through the generous support of the Ilves Family Foundation...*

*...please find enclosed the required participant agreement...*

She read the agreement at the kitchen table while her father cooked—her father never cooked, so the smell of onions frying was itself a message, a celebration already in progress. She read it all the way through, every clause, because Oscar had taught her that. *Read the whole page before you play the first note,* they'd said, back in the boiler room, back when lessons were still secret and small. *The page is always telling you something the notes aren't.*

Clause fourteen. She read it three times to be sure.

*The participant acknowledges that instruction received outside the conservatory's accredited faculty must be disclosed to the competition committee, and agrees that all preparatory instruction shall be conducted by faculty in good standing with the conservatory or its designated affiliates.*

In good standing. She thought of Oscar's hands, the way the left one trembled when they thought no one was watching, the way it stilled completely the moment it touched the keys—like the shaking was never in the hands at all, only in the waiting.

She thought about what *in good standing* meant for a man whose face was currently on the conservatory's website under a headline she couldn't stop reading: *THE PRODIGY WHO SCRUBS OUR FLOORS.*

The onions started to burn. Her father was on the phone in the other room, his voice doing the thing it did when he performed for people—warmer, louder, the accent smoothed down to something acceptable. She heard her own name in his mouth. She heard the word *semifinals.*

She put clause fourteen out of her mind and went to help him, and for one evening she let herself be the girl in the letter, the girl with the future, and it felt so good it scared her.

* * *

The lesson was on Thursday, in the boiler room, because the music school wasn't finished yet and the house on Alder Lane still smelled of dust and old arguments, and because Oscar said the boiler room had good acoustics—"all that iron, it listens." Noor arrived early, as she always did, and sat on the upturned crate that was her stool, and watched Oscar tune the borrowed keyboard by ear, turning the pegs with a small rubber wedge like a surgeon performing something unsanctioned.

She had the letter in her bag. She had carried it for two days like a stone.

"Play the Chopin," Oscar said, not looking up. "The one you butchered on Monday."

"I didn't butcher it."

"You executed it. There's a difference. A butchering at least leaves meat."

She laughed despite herself, and played. The nocturne in C-sharp minor, the one she'd first played through that practice-room door a lifetime ago, when a voice she couldn't see had corrected her fingering and changed the shape of everything. She played it better now. She could hear it getting better under her hands, could hear Oscar's corrections living in her fingers like tenants who paid rent in muscle memory. In the middle section she did the thing they'd worked on for weeks—the letting go, the rubato that wasn't a performance of feeling but feeling itself—and when she finished, the boiler room held the last chord the way a cupped hand holds water.

Oscar was quiet. Then: "Again. Just the middle."

"You always make me do the middle again."

"The middle is where people lie." They finally looked at her, and something in their face shifted—the particular stillness they got when they were reading her the way she was learning to read scores, all the way down, past the notes. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"The middle, Noor. Where people lie."

She almost laughed again. Then she didn't. She reached into her bag and put the envelope on the workbench between them, and watched Oscar open it, and watched their face do something complicated and quiet while they read clause fourteen.

The silence went on so long she could hear the boiler ticking.

"You should take it," Oscar said.

"What?"

"You should take the slot." They set the letter down carefully, aligning its edges with the edge of the workbench, the way they did when their hands needed a job. "It's the Ferreira. People wait years for the Ferreira. Your father will—"

"My father doesn't get a vote."

"No. But he gets a say, and you have to live with him either way, and I—" Oscar stopped. Started again, slower. "I have spent twenty years being someone who chose the door over the room. I am not going to be the reason you do the same."

"You're not a door. You're—"

"I'm clause fourteen." Their voice was flat, and Noor understood suddenly that the flatness was armor, the same way her father's warmth was armor, the same way her own jokes were armor, everyone she loved walking around padded against the world. "That's what I am now, officially. That's what they've made me. And if you keep me and lose this, you'll resent me. Not now. In ten years, when your hands are as good as they're going to get and the doors are narrower. You'll be in some apartment practicing for a competition you aged out of, and you'll look at me mopping a floor somewhere, and you'll hate me a little. I would hate me."

"I would never—"

"You don't know what you'd do. You're sixteen." Oscar said it without cruelty, which was worse. "I knew everything at sixteen too."

Noor looked at the keyboard, at the keys worn pale by other people's decades. Her eyes were hot. "So that's it. You want me to sign the paper. Cut the lessons. Pretend I learned the Chopin from a textbook."

"I want you to have the life I didn't." Oscar stood, and turned to the workbench, and began—pointlessly, deliberately—to straighten tools that were already straight. "That's the whole job, as far as I can tell. The one thing I'm qualified for."

* * *

Her father took her to dinner. Not their usual place—the new Portuguese place on the avenue with the linen napkins and the fish displayed on ice like jewelry, the kind of place he took clients, the kind of place where he became Senhor Ferreira instead of Pai.

He ordered for her without asking. He always ordered for her without asking. She had never noticed how much it happened until recently, until Oscar's lessons had made her attentive to things, had taught her that the page is always telling you something the notes aren't, and her father's page was telling her everything.

"So," he said, when the waiter withdrew. "You've read it."

"I've read it."

"Semifinals. Direct entry. Do you understand what that means? Girls train a decade for the Ferreira and wash out in the first round. Daniel Ilves called me himself, Noor. Himself. He said he heard a recording—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "He said your teacher's name was mentioned. In the file."

"Was it."

"Noor."

"Did he say my teacher's name, Pai? Which name did he use? The one from the newspaper?"

Her father set down his fork. In the candlelight his face did the thing it did when he was deciding how much truth a moment could hold—she'd seen it at the funeral, at the bank, at every parent-teacher conference where the news was bad. "That man is not your teacher. That man is a scandal. That man is a janitor."

"That man is the best musician I've ever heard."

"He was. Maybe. Twenty years ago. Before whatever happened to him happened." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he used for negotiations, for the soft sell, and it was worse than shouting. "Filha. Listen to me. I know what it feels like. Someone sees you. Someone believes in you. It's a drug. I felt it too, when your mother—" He stopped again. He did that a lot lately, circling her mother's name like a sore tooth. "I was young once. I had a teacher who believed in me. And I still ended up selling insurance, because belief doesn't pay for apartments. Talent doesn't pay for apartments. Competitions pay for apartments. Names pay for apartments. And the Ilves name is on that letter, and that name is worth more than every lesson that man will ever give you."

"His name is Oscar."

"I know what his name is. Everyone knows what his name is. That's the problem." Her father picked up his wine, put it down without drinking. "Sign the agreement, Noor. Disclose nothing. The lessons stop, the semester ends, you win the Ferreira, and in five years none of this matters. In five years you'll be on a stage in Lisbon and this will be a story you tell, about the strange winter you spent learning from a ghost."

"A ghost," she repeated.

"A story," he said. "That's all he is. A story the conservatory tells about wasted talent. Don't let him make you into the sequel."

She looked at her father—really looked, the way Oscar had taught her to look at a page—and saw, for the first time, not the man who ordered for her but the boy he'd been. A boy with a teacher who believed in him. A boy who had put the piano away in an attic somewhere and built this restaurant, this voice, this life of linen napkins, on top of the closed lid. He wasn't pushing her toward the competition. He was pushing her away from the cliff he'd stood on himself. He thought he was saving her.

It didn't make him right. But it made him hers.

"Pai," she said quietly. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat at the kitchen table with the agreement in front of her and a pen she didn't pick up until three in the morning, and when she finally picked it up, she signed her name on the participant line—because Oscar was right, because the Ferreira was the Ferreira, because she was sixteen and her hands were the best they would ever be and doors were doors.

Then she read clause fourteen one more time.

*All preparatory instruction shall be conducted by faculty in good standing.*

She thought about the boiler room. About the iron listening. About the way Oscar's left hand trembled until it touched the keys. About the word *disclose,* which was such a clean word for such a dirty thing. She thought about what it would mean to stand on the Ferreira stage with this secret folded into her file like a landmine, waiting for Daniel Ilves—who had already shown the whole city what he did with information—to decide when to detonate it. He'd leaked Oscar once. He would leak her. Not out of cruelty, maybe. Out of tidiness. Out of the same cold efficiency that had emptied a house and suppressed a will, the need to make the world match his ledger.

And she thought about the recital. The one Oscar had played at the school's opening—flawed and alive, that was the phrase Noor had written in her notebook that night, *flawed and alive*—and how the whole room had stopped breathing, and how Oscar had dedicated it to the stranger in the photograph, and how Noor had cried into her sleeve next to Daniel Ilves himself, who had sat in the back row with his arms crossed and his face like a closed door, and had not applauded until everyone else did.

If she signed clause fourteen into truth—if she actually stopped, actually cut the lessons—then the thing she'd learned in the boiler room would die of silence. Not the Chopin. She could keep the Chopin; it lived in her hands now. But the other thing. The thing underneath. The thing Oscar had given her without ever naming it: that attention was the whole art, that the page is always telling you something the notes aren't, that you could be broken and still make something true.

She could not learn that from a faculty member in good standing.

At four in the morning she took a clean sheet of paper and wrote a letter to the competition committee. Her handwriting was steady, which surprised her. She wrote that she was withdrawing from the semifinal round. She wrote that she was grateful. She did not explain why, because explaining would have required naming Oscar, and she was not going to hand Daniel Ilves a second name to feed to the press.

She put it in the expensive envelope, the one with the embossed letterhead, addressed to herself—her father's little joke, he'd said, *so you remember where it came from*—and sealed it, and put it in her bag.

Then she practiced until the sun came up. The middle section. Where people lie.

* * *

Her father was in the kitchen when she came out of her room, dressed for work, coffee in hand, and she could tell from the set of his shoulders that he'd been awake too, that the apartment had held two sleepless people on opposite sides of one wall.

"I signed it," she said.

Something loosened in his face—relief, bright and boyish, and she hated that she was about to take it away, and loved him for feeling it. "Filha—"

"I signed the withdrawal." She took the envelope out of her bag and set it on the counter between them, next to the coffee maker, in the ordinary morning light where it looked like what it was: a piece of paper. "I'm not doing the Ferreira."

The silence was long. The refrigerator hummed. Down on the street, a delivery truck beeped its way backward into some other family's day.

"You signed—" He put his coffee down with exaggerated care, the way he did when he was furious and performing calm, and she had seen that performance her whole life and was only now, this morning, learning to see through it to the fear underneath. "Do you understand what you've thrown away? Do you understand what that man has cost you? A year of training, a name, a future—and for what? For loyalty to someone who mops—"

"Stop." Her voice came out strange and low, and her father actually stopped, because he had never heard that voice before. Neither had she. "You're going to listen to me for one minute, Pai, and then you can be angry for as long as you want. I'll even help. But you're going to listen."

She told him about the boiler room. About the first lesson, how Oscar's hands had shaken on the keys and then gone quiet, how they'd said *the middle is where people lie,* and how she'd understood, somewhere in those weeks of iron and dust and borrowed keyboards, that her father's warmth was a kind of fear and her own jokes were a kind of armor and the whole Ferreira letter was a kind of door closing dressed up as a door opening.

"I'm not throwing away a future," she said. "I'm refusing to buy one from a man who sells them with clauses in them. Daniel Ilves put his name on my letter, Pai. His money, his conditions, his grudge. If I play that competition, I'm not playing for me. I'm playing a move in his game against Oscar. And I won't. Not for an apartment. Not for Lisbon. Not for you."

"He's not asking you to choose—"

"He is. Clause fourteen is asking me to choose. He just made the paper do it so his hands stay clean." She slid the envelope across the counter toward her father. "You can mail it or I can mail it. But it's going."

Her father looked at the envelope for a long time. She watched him age a little, or maybe just settle, like a house in wind. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost the negotiation register entirely. It was just a tired man's voice, in a kitchen, in the morning.

"Your mother did something like this once," he said. "Before you were born. She turned down something—a scholarship, a position, I don't even remember anymore. I was so angry. I didn't speak to her for a week." He picked up his coffee and didn't drink it. "She said, *I'd rather be poor and honest on a stage than rich and afraid in an office.* I thought it was romantic nonsense. I still think it's romantic nonsense." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet, and he was smiling, which was the most confusing thing Noor had ever seen him do. "You sound exactly like her. Do you know that? You sound exactly like her, and it is breaking my heart, and I have never been prouder of anyone in my life."

He picked up the envelope. He put it in his coat pocket, over his heart, like a letter he was delivering.

"I'll mail it," he said. "But you're calling your grandmother. She'll want to know why her grandson-in-law—" he caught himself, corrected, "—why her granddaughter is throwing away her future, and you can explain it to her in Portuguese, because I don't have the strength."

Noor laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and her father came around the counter and held her, and he smelled like coffee and the new Portuguese place and twenty years of selling insurance, and she held him back, and neither of them said anything else, because the middle is where people lie, and the endings—at least this one, this small one, this kitchen one—were true.

* * *

She got to Alder Lane by nine, breath steaming, and found the front door open and Oscar on a ladder in the hallway, badly hanging a bulletin board, a hammer in one fist and three bent nails between their lips.

Noor stood in the doorway of the house where Marta Ilves had raised and ruined and, in the end, in her own crooked way, loved her strangest student. The walls were half-painted. The piano—Oscar's old piano, the one Ruth Okafor had released from the estate—stood in the parlor under a drop cloth like something sleeping.

"I quit," Noor said.

Oscar turned too fast. The hammer slipped, the nails scattered, and for a moment they just looked at each other across the dusty light, and then Oscar came down the ladder and crossed the hall and did the thing they almost never did, which was to put their arms around her, awkward and fierce, their chin on the top of her head, and say nothing at all.

"Also," Noor said into their jacket, "I need a lesson. Right now. The middle section. I've been lying in it all week."

Oscar laughed—a real one, startled out of them—and stepped back, and wiped their eyes with the heel of one hand as if removing dust. "Then we'd better get to work," they said. "We have a school to open."

And that was when the car pulled up outside—Daniel Ilves's car, moving too slowly, pausing at the curb—and Oscar's face went still in a way Noor had learned to read, and through the window they both watched him sit there, engine running, looking up at the house his mother had left to a janitor, as if measuring it for demolition.
