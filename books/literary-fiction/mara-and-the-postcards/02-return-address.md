## 2. Return Address

The ferry to Meridian Point runs twice a day in the off-season and I had timed my ticket so that I would arrive in the dark, which was a mistake, because it meant I had nothing to do for ninety minutes but stand at the rail and watch the water do its ancient, indifferent work.

I want to say something dignified about grief here—something about how loss flattens you, how the sea becomes a metaphor—but the truth is that for most of the crossing I was thinking about shoes. Specifically: whether the black ones were appropriate for a wake or too formal for a funeral, and whether anyone would notice if I wore them to both, and whether noticing would count against me. This is what eleven years of professional letter-writing does to a person. I have composed deathbed reconciliations for men who once punched their brothers over an inheritance. I know exactly which words make forgiveness sound sincere. And still, standing on that ferry with my mother's ashes waiting somewhere in the hold like checked luggage, all I could manage was footwear anxiety.

The wind came off the water with that particular salt-and-diesel smell that coastal towns manufacture and never export. Somewhere behind me a gull made its argument. I gripped the rail and did not look south, toward the headland, where—if the town still held the shape I remembered—a certain house used to stand before it stopped standing at all.

I looked anyway. Of course I looked. I have never once successfully not looked at anything; I have only gotten very good at pretending afterward that I didn't.

The headland was dark. The house was gone. Both facts arrived without ceremony, the way geography delivers news: no tone of voice, no editorializing. Just land, just absence, just the lighthouse blinking its one-word sentence out at the water—*still, still, still*—as if the coast itself had learned my family's trick of saying everything by saying almost nothing.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about returning, since I've had eleven years to theorize about it from a safe distance of four hundred miles.

Leaving is a single act. It has a beginning, a middle, and an end: you pack, you board, you arrive elsewhere. Returning is not one act but a thousand small surrenders, each one negotiated separately. The town gets to decide what it kept and what it threw away, and you get no vote. You come back expecting the place to have waited for you, and instead you find it went on living, rudely, without your permission—the bakery changed hands, the pier got new planking, someone painted the chandlery a color that didn't exist when you left—and every one of these small betrayals stings more than it should, because what they're really telling you is: *you were replaceable. We barely noticed.*

I catalogued the changes the way I catalogue everything, compulsively, in mental columns I will later deny keeping.

Changed: the ferry terminal had been renovated into something with glass walls and a café that sold, I am not making this up, lavender oat-milk lattes. Changed: the taxi rank was now an app, according to a sign, though a lone cab idled there anyway, driven by a man who might have been Ray Kowalczyk's son or Ray Kowalczyk himself aged badly. Changed: the fish cannery on the north shore, which employed half my graduating class and smelled like money and death in equal parts, was now "luxury waterfront residences," which is what cannery means when it dies.

Unchanged: the church spire, the war memorial with its list of names nobody reads, the hill road up past the water tower where the streetlights gave out and the town admitted, geographically, that it had run out of things to say about itself.

And unchanged, though I refused to confirm it directly: the lot on Harbor View Road where my grandmother's house had been. I knew it was there because geography is a gossip. You can't look at a coastline without it telling you things. The curve of the road bent around the absence the way a conversation bends around a name nobody wants to say.

* * *

On the ferry I rehearsed.

This is not a figure of speech. I sat in the plastic seat nearest the heater, opened the notebook I carry everywhere the way other people carry worry stones, and wrote out the small talk the way a nervous actor writes out her lines.

*Scene One: Arrival at the house.* Mourners would already be there—my mother's bridge group, her neighbors from Delancey Street, my cousin Daniel and his wife whose name I have deliberately forgotten as punishment for something I can no longer recall. They would say: *Bram. You came.* As though I were a weather event they'd bet against. I would say: *Of course I came.* Three words. Safe. Then someone would hug me, and I would perform the hug correctly—two pats on the back, release at four seconds, no lingering—and then I would ask about parking, or traffic, or the catering, any subject that could absorb sympathy without requiring me to feel it in front of witnesses.

*Scene Two: The Condolence Loop.* People would approach individually. They would say some version of *she was a wonderful woman,* and here is the trap, because my mother was not a wonderful woman, she was a difficult woman, a brilliant and withholding woman who loved me in the way that a locked door loves you—totally, structurally, without ever letting you in—and if I said this, even gently, even with love in it, the room would flinch, and I would be the one who made the funeral weird. So I had prepared: *"She was extraordinary."* Which is technically true. Extraordinary things can be dangerous. Nobody asks a follow-up question about *extraordinary.*

*Scene Three: The Question I Dreaded.* Someone—Daniel, probably, or Aunt Vera's delegate—would ask how long I was staying. This sounds innocent. It is not innocent. It is a probe launched at the exact fault line of my entire adult life: the fact that I left at sixteen and built a career out of staying gone. My prepared answer: *"As long as the estate needs."* Bureaucratic. Unimpeachable. A sentence shaped like a door closing softly.

I reviewed my lines twice, then a third time, and somewhere during the third pass I caught myself doing the thing I do—I heard my own voice in my head going warm and reasonable and slightly distant, the voice I use for clients, the voice that says *I understand completely* while understanding nothing and feeling less—and I put the notebook away.

Because here is what the rehearsal was actually for. Not to help me speak. To help me not speak. Eleven years ago I got on a bus out of this town with one postcard in my pocket and a plan that consisted entirely of the word *away*, and I have spent every year since perfecting the craft of being present without arriving. I write letters for strangers—apologies they can't face, endings they can't afford, love declared posthumously through lawyers—and I am very good at it, because I learned early that the safest place to put a feeling is in someone else's mouth.

My mother understood this about me. She never said so. She was the one who taught it.

* * *

Halfway across, the ferry passed the bell buoy off Gannet Rock, and the sound it made—that hollow, patient clang—went through me like a needle finding an old scar.

There are sounds your body keeps without consulting you. That buoy was one of mine. Summer nights, sixteen years old, window open, the buoy counting out the hours while I lay awake composing arguments I'd never deliver and letters I'd never send. Even then. Even at the beginning, before the shoebox, before the ritual hardened into identity, I was already writing to people who weren't listening. I thought I'd invented the habit at twenty-five, sitting in my city apartment with the first postcard addressed to a burned-down house. But no. The habit was local. I caught it here, like a fever, like an accent.

The town was coming up out of the fog now, and I watched it assemble itself piece by piece—the breakwater first, then the masts, then the crooked row of harbor-front buildings leaning together like old conspirators—and I felt the thing I had been dreading feeling, the thing worse than dread, which is that underneath the dread there was something embarrassingly close to relief.

Longing, maybe. Or whatever longing turns into when you've starved it for a decade and it comes back feral and unrecognizable.

I hated this town at sixteen. I want to be clear about that. I hated its smallness and its surveillance and the way everyone's business was conducted in the open air like laundry, and I hated most of all the house on Harbor View Road where my grandmother lived alone and spoke maybe forty words a year, thirty of them *no*. Leaving was the best decision I ever made and I have never once regretted it, except apparently on ferries, apparently at funerals, apparently whenever the bell buoy rings and my body remembers being young here before I understood what the silence in that house cost, and who was paying it, and in what currency.

The fog lifted off the harbor in rags. And there—because geography keeps answering questions I haven't asked aloud—there was the street where we'd lived, climbing the hill, and there was the roofline of my mother's house with its new guttering, and there, at the bottom of the hill where the roads met, the corner where the bus stop used to be, where a girl with a duffel bag once stood at eleven at night holding a postcard she'd written in the station bathroom because she couldn't say goodbye out loud and couldn't leave without saying it either.

*I'm fine,* that postcard said. *Don't wait up.* Addressed to my grandmother's house, which was still standing then, still receiving mail, still occupied by a woman who read forty words a year. I mailed it from the slot outside the depot and got on the bus and did not cry until the town lights slid off the edge of the window, and then I cried the way you cry when you've won something and lost it in the same motion.

Eleven years of postcards followed that one. Every one of them to that address. Every one of them filed, eventually, in a shoebox under my bed, because the house burned two years after I left and I could not bring myself to change the address, because changing the address would mean admitting the letter was going somewhere else—to memory, to myself, to no one—and I wasn't ready to know that. I may not be ready to know it now. There are things you can carry for years precisely because you never set them down to look at them.

* * *

The ferry knocked against the pilings. Deckhands threw lines. Passengers gathered their bags and their children and their griefs and moved toward the ramp in the slow shuffle of people arriving places they'd rather not be.

I stayed at the rail a moment longer. The lighthouse blinked. The buoy clanged. Somewhere up Harbor View Road, in the dark where I refused to look, a house I had never seen stood half-built on the bones of the one I had, and someone I had never met presumably slept inside it, dreaming whatever dreams belong to people who live in other women's ruins.

I should note—for accuracy, since accuracy is the only inheritance I've ever claimed—that I did not yet know about Odile. I did not know the name Vasseur. I did not know that in nineteen hours a composed stranger would walk through my mother's front door and greet my family by a word that would split my life into before and after. If someone had told me, right there on the deck, with the fog lifting and the gulls working the wake, I would have assumed they were pitching me a novel. I write fiction on the side. I know how coincidence is supposed to behave. It is supposed to stay in books, where it can be supervised.

What I knew, standing on that ferry, was only this: I was coming home to bury a woman I loved in a language neither of us ever managed to speak, and I had rehearsed every sentence except the true ones.

The ramp dropped. I picked up my bag, and my shoes—black, appropriate for both occasions, noticed by no one—and walked down into the town that had gone on without me, smelling of salt and diesel and lavender oat milk, answering questions I hadn't asked yet, one street at a time.
