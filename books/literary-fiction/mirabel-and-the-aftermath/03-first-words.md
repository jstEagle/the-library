## 3. First Words

Ilma Vess came back the next night, and the night after that, and by the fourth night Cyrus had stopped pretending to be surprised.

She arrived the way weather arrives — without announcement, without apology — a small woman in a coat too heavy for the season, carrying nothing but herself and a canvas bag that clinked faintly, like bottles, though she never took anything out of it. She did not go to the reading room. She went where Cyrus went, which was nowhere in particular, trailing them through the 700s while they reshelved misfiled monographs on Dutch architecture, standing at their elbow while they logged a returned atlas with a coffee ring shaped like an atoll across the Baltic.

"You're not going to ask me anything," Cyrus said finally, sliding the atlas into its slot.

"I'm waiting for you to stop asking yourself."

"That could take years."

"Good," Ilma said. "Languages take longer."

* * *

The lessons happened in the back stacks, past the local history alcove, past the microfilm room with its dead machines, in the narrow corridor of shelving where the library kept the books nobody had borrowed in thirty years — treatises on irrigation law, hymnals in dead denominations, a complete run of a journal called *Proceedings in Soil Chemistry*. It was the quietest place in the building, which is to say it was not quiet at all. The building breathed there. Pipes knocked in the walls like a neighbor asking to borrow something. The elevator hummed two floors down, a long held note. Somewhere above, the roof expanded and contracted with the temperature, ticking like a cooling engine.

"It's too noisy here," Cyrus said the first night, apologetic, sweeping a dust mouse off the shelf with their sleeve.

"No," Ilma said. "It's full. There's a difference." She set her canvas bag down on the carpet with the reverence of a woman setting down something breakable, though nothing came out of it. "In my language we don't say *quiet*. We say—" and then she made a sound.

Cyrus would spend the rest of their life trying to describe that sound and never quite succeed. It was not a word in any sense they understood. It began in the chest, they thought, or maybe in the floor — it seemed to come up through the soles of their shoes — a low, rounded hum that opened at the end into something almost like breath being released after a long hold. It lasted perhaps three seconds. In those three seconds, the pipes stopped knocking. Or rather: the pipes didn't stop, but Cyrus stopped hearing them as noise. They heard them as part of it. The hum held the pipes the way a riverbed holds water.

"What does it mean?" Cyrus asked, when the sound had finished passing through them.

"It means the room agrees to listen," Ilma said. "You can't have quiet until everyone in the room agrees. Even the pipes. Especially the pipes."

"And if they don't agree?"

"Then you wait. A language that has no word for *quiet* also has no word for *impatient*." She smiled, and for a moment she looked forty years younger, or maybe forty years older; it was hard to say with her. "We were very good at waiting. It was most of what we had."

* * *

The first real word Cyrus learned was *haleth*, and it meant, roughly, the sound of someone else breathing in the next room — close enough to hear, far enough that you couldn't tell if they were asleep.

"There's no English word for that?" Cyrus asked.

"There are English words for everything," Ilma said, "the way there are nails for everything. That doesn't mean the house was built with them." She pronounced *haleth* again, slower, breaking it open so Cyrus could see the parts: a soft beginning like an exhale, a middle that sat in the teeth, an ending that fell away like something set down carefully. "Your language has *snoring*. It has *silence*. Between them there is a whole country and you have no map of it. I'm giving you the map."

They practiced. Ilma would say a word, and Cyrus would say it back, and she would shake her head — not unkindly, but with the particular sorrow of a teacher watching a student walk past the door they're meant to enter.

"You're saying it with your mouth," she said. "Say it with your ears."

"With my—"

"The word already exists in the room. You don't make it. You find it." She tapped her own ear, then pointed at Cyrus's. "English is a language of making. You build sentences like carpenters. Mine is a language of finding. The word is already there, in the air, in the wall, in the space between you and the thing you mean. Your job is only to agree with it."

It should have been nonsense. It was nonsense, probably, linguistically speaking — Cyrus knew enough phonetics to know that sounds do not pre-exist their speakers, that language lives in throats and not in walls. And yet. On the third night, somewhere between one attempt and the next, Cyrus stopped trying to produce *haleth* and started listening for it, and found it — found it sitting in the hum of the elevator shaft, exactly where it had apparently been all along — and said it, and Ilma closed her eyes and nodded once, and Cyrus felt something in their chest unlock that they had not known was locked.

That was the night the city changed.

* * *

Walking home at dawn — because the lessons ended when the sky went grey at the edges, because Ilma refused to be taught under anything but artificial light, "daylight makes people honest and honesty is bad for beginners" — Cyrus heard the city differently. This was not metaphor. Or it was metaphor, but it was also true, and the distinction mattered less than it should have.

The buses starting up at the depot made a sound Cyrus now recognized as *vessun* — the groan of a large thing agreeing to move before it wants to. The gulls fighting over a dropped sandwich outside the bakery were doing something the language had a word for, a bright clattering syllable that meant *argument among equals, no winner expected*. The man who slept in the doorway of the shuttered pharmacy, whose breathing Cyrus had passed a thousand nights without hearing, was — Cyrus stopped, actually stopped on the sidewalk — he was *haleth*. He was the word itself, asleep six feet from a bus stop, breathing in the next room of the world.

Cyrus stood there in the cold until their hands went numb, listening to a stranger breathe, and felt more awake than they had felt in eleven years of night shifts.

At home, they did not sleep. They opened the ledger instead.

The ledger had begun, years ago, as a place to record conversations never finished — the things patrons said at 4 a.m. and then walked away from, the sentences that died halfway, the questions Cyrus hadn't asked. It had become, over time, a catalogue of unsaid things, and it had been nearly empty lately, because lately Cyrus had stopped noticing what went unsaid. Now the pen moved before they decided to move it:

*Entry: The word for a room agreeing to listen. I don't know how to spell it yet. It doesn't matter. I know where it lives.*

*Entry: The sleeping man outside the pharmacy is haleth. I have been walking past a word every night for a decade.*

*Entry: Ilma says the language is a language of finding, not making. I have spent my whole life making. I want to know what I've been failing to find.*

They looked at the last line for a long time. Then they wrote beneath it, smaller:

*Entry: I am fifty-one years old and this is the first time I have ever wanted morning to come slower.*

* * *

The lessons settled into a rhythm, the way water settles into a channel it has cut for itself. Ilma arrived between two and three. They walked to the back stacks. She set down the bag she never opened. She taught.

She taught the way rain teaches a roof — patiently, everywhere at once, without any single drop seeming to matter much until suddenly the gutters were running. Some nights she gave Cyrus ten words. Some nights she gave them one, and made them sit with it for two hours. Once she gave them no words at all, just made them stand in the stacks and listen, correcting nothing, until at the end of the night she said, "Better," and Cyrus understood that the lesson had been the listening itself.

The words came in clusters, organized by nothing Cyrus could discern. One night it was all words for kinds of footsteps — footsteps on stairs, footsteps trying not to be heard, footsteps of someone coming home, footsteps of someone leaving who means to come back (a different word, Cyrus noted, than someone leaving who doesn't). Another night, words for degrees of lamplight. Another night, an entire vocabulary of doors: doors ajar, doors latched, doors that stick in humid weather, doors left open on purpose as an invitation, doors left open on purpose as an accusation.

"How many words for doors are there?"

"How many doors have there been?" Ilma said. "The language counts what mattered. Doors mattered. People were always coming or going or deciding whether to let someone in." She ran her hand along a shelf of soil chemistry proceedings as though greeting an old colleague. "A language is just a list of what a people couldn't stop noticing."

"And what did your people notice?"

Ilma was quiet for a moment. Outside, a siren rose and fell, and Cyrus caught themselves identifying its shape in the new grammar — a rising kind of sound, a word they'd learned two nights ago, meaning *alarm that concerns others*.

"They noticed each other," Ilma said finally. "Almost entirely. It was our great gift and our great limitation. We had fourteen words for the way light falls on a face you love and no word at all for *stranger*. Not none, you understand — we had ways of describing someone unknown. But no category. Everyone was either *we* or *not yet*." Something crossed her face then, quick as a bird's shadow, gone before Cyrus could name it. "That will be your hardest lesson. Not tonight. Tonight: footsteps."

* * *

The ledger filled. That was the strange thing — Cyrus had expected learning a language to add to their life, and it did, but mostly it revealed what had been there all along, unrecorded, unnamed. Every entry now came with a word attached, or a hole where a word should be:

*Entry: The night porter at the hospital across the street waves at me through the glass every night at 2:15. Eleven years. I have never once waved first. There is a word for a greeting returned late — returned so late it becomes its own apology. I looked it up in Ilma. I have been committing it nightly.*

*Entry: A patron tonight asked me if the library had books on grief. I said "third floor, 155.9." What I meant was: yes, and I live there. There is a word for the answer you give instead of the answer you mean. Of course there is. There are nine of them, graded by how much of yourself you're hiding. I used the fourth one. I could have used the second.*

*Entry: Ilma watched me write in the ledger tonight and said nothing, but she looked at it the way she looks at words she approves of. I did not know I was doing something worth approving until she approved it.*

*Entry: I dreamed last night in a language I don't speak yet. I woke up remembering only that everyone in the dream had been plural.*

Cyrus read that last entry back three times and could not remember writing it, could not fully remember the dream, only a warm crowded feeling, many-voiced, like a room agreeing to listen.

* * *

On the twelfth night, Ilma taught them a word that frightened them.

Not because of its sound — it was gentle, round, a word like a hand on a shoulder. It frightened them because of what it meant, or what Ilma said it meant, which was: *the silence a person leaves behind in a room after they've gone, which lasts exactly as long as it takes the room to forget them.*

"There's a word for that," Cyrus said. "Of course there's a word for that."

"Every language has the word," Ilma said. "Most languages keep it in the attic. Ours kept it in the kitchen. We used it daily. When someone left a room, you said it — quietly, to the room, not to them. It was manners. It was—" she searched, "—it was bookkeeping. Someone had been here. The room should acknowledge the debt."

"Say it again."

She said it again. And Cyrus, who had learned to find words rather than make them, listened for it in the stacks around them — in the aisle where a patron had stood twenty minutes ago reading the spines of books he'd never open, in the reading room where a woman had cried once, two winters ago, over a letter Cyrus had pretended not to see her holding. The word fit everywhere. The library was made of it. Every building in the city was made of it. The whole city was rooms forgetting people at the exact speed of rooms.

"Are you all right?" Ilma asked. Her voice had changed — careful now, watchful.

"I'm fine," Cyrus said, and heard themselves use the fourth kind of answer, the one that hides the most.

Ilma looked at them for a long moment. Then she reached into the canvas bag — the bag that had never once been opened, that Cyrus had privately catalogued as a habit, a comfort object, a prop — and drew out a small notebook, cloth-bound, swollen with age, its cover soft as an ear.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'll teach you a sentence."

"A sentence?"

"One sentence. The most important one in the language." She put the notebook back, unhurried, and stood, and gathered her coat. "But not tomorrow, I think. Tomorrow you'll learn the grammar it sits inside. The day after, the tense. It's a load-bearing wall, that sentence. Learn the rooms around it first, or the whole house comes down on you."

"What does it mean?"

Ilma paused at the end of the aisle, half in shadow, and for a moment she looked less like a teacher than like a woman standing at the edge of something with a long drop.

"It means," she said, "that some words are heavier than the mouths that carry them. Goodnight, Cyrus. Find the word for the room when you leave. Say it to the shelves. They've earned it."

And she was gone, and Cyrus stood alone in the Proceedings in Soil Chemistry, and said the word for the silence a person leaves behind, softly, to the empty air where she had been — and heard, for the first time, how much of it was already there.
