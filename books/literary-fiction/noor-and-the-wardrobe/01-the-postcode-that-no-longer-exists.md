## 1. The Postcode That No Longer Exists

The postcard cost me ninety pence and eleven years of my life, though only the first figure was printed on the back.

I bought it from the stationery shop on Wexford Street, the one that smells of glue and slow decay, because it was a picture of a lighthouse I have never visited in weather I have never experienced. That is the whole method, if you can call it that: find an image of somewhere neither of us has been, and then write about the sky as though I had stood under it. The lighthouse was white with a red band, standing in a sea so blue it looked like it had been colour-corrected by someone with strong opinions about hope.

*Dear E,* I wrote, in the kitchen, at the table where I do everything that requires a flat surface and no witnesses. *It's been warm here. Unseasonably. The kind of warmth that makes you suspicious.*

It had not been warm. It had been the kind of grey drizzle that gets into your bones and sets up residence without paying rent. But the truth was not the point of the postcard. The truth was the thing the postcard existed to avoid, the way a moat exists to avoid visitors. Ten years ago I had learned that the truth, delivered to that particular address, would arrive like a lit match, and so I had spent a decade mailing weather reports to a ruin.

*The gulls are loud this year,* I continued. *Louder than usual. Or maybe I'm just listening better.*

There were no gulls. There was a fox that screamed at 3 a.m. behind the bins on my street, which I could have written about, but the fox felt too close to something, and I had built my entire postal career on the principle of staying far away from things.

Let me be precise about the address, because precision is the one honesty I permit myself. Number 14, Ferndale Terrace. Except Ferndale Terrace no longer exists — not the houses, anyway. They burned on a Tuesday in October, ten years ago last week, all four of them, in a fire that took forty minutes to do what it did and took me considerably longer to admit what I saw. The postcode survives. Postcodes are stubborn that way; they outlive the things they name, like widows' surnames or the names of demolished pubs still painted faintly on brick. So every postcard I write goes to a valid sorting office and a street that is now a car park with a memorial bench nobody sits on.

The Royal Mail does not return them. I checked, early on, with a clerk who looked at me the way people look at men who ask strange questions in post offices. "If there's no delivery point," she said, "they'd come back marked undeliverable." They never came back. For ten years they went wherever mail goes when its destination is a hole in the world, and I told myself that was fine, that was the arrangement, that the letters were never really for anyone.

This was a lie, obviously. But it was a load-bearing lie, and I had furnished the house of my life around it quite comfortably.

*3,650,* said the tally I keep in the back of a notebook I don't show anyone. One per day, give or take the days I told myself I'd earned a rest and then lay awake counting the omissions. Three thousand six hundred and fifty small envelopes, each containing a sentence that stopped just short of the sentence.

Because here is the thing about my postcards, the thing I know even as I write them, the thing I know the way you know a floorboard creaks: every single one ends too soon. Not unfinished — I am careful to finish each one grammatically, sign off with a *Yours*, sometimes even a *love*, lowercase, deniable — but finished short. Each one stops a paragraph before the paragraph that matters. There is always a next sentence hovering, the true one, the one that begins *What I actually want to tell you is* or *The night of the fire, I* — and every day for ten years I have set down my pen before it, the way you lift your foot before a trap you've memorised the location of.

Today's card ended: *I hope wherever you are, the weather suits you.*

Wherever you are. As if E were travelling. As if E had simply gone somewhere with unreliable post, rather than being exactly where E had always been, which is nowhere, which is ash, which is a question I answer daily by refusing to ask it.

I signed it. I stamped it. I addressed it in my neatest hand, the hand I use for lies, which is unfortunately also my best hand.

And then — and this is the part I want to confess to someone, anyone, which is perhaps why I'm telling you — I sat at my kitchen table and held the postcard over the bin.

Not to throw it away. To burn it. I keep a box of matches by the hob for the gas ring, and I struck one, and I watched the flame stand up straight and yellow, and I thought: ten years. Ten years of describing weather to a dead man's house. What would it cost, really, to let one card go unsent? Wouldn't that be a kind of mercy? To myself, I mean. The dead don't read postcards. I am not a man who believes they do, whatever the cards might suggest. The ritual isn't communication. It's containment. It's a dam made of ninety-pence stamps.

The match burned down to my fingers. I shook it out. I put the postcard in my coat pocket, where it lay against my chest like a small warm animal, and I walked to the postbox on the corner and posted it, and heard it land on the pile of yesterday's version of the same cowardice with a sound like a single clap.

* * *

You should understand something about me, since we're going to be spending time together. I am, by trade and temperament, a novelist. Two books published, both well-reviewed in the way that means roughly four thousand people read them, both about other people's lives, both praised — this is the review quote I remember, the one that lives in my chest like a swallowed stone — for their "admirable restraint."

Admirable restraint. A stranger read three hundred pages of my sentences stopping short and called it a virtue. I framed nothing, but I could have.

My flat is two rooms above a dry cleaner's, which means I live in a permanent faint fog of solvent smell that I've stopped noticing except when guests wrinkle their noses, and I don't have guests. The walls hold books arranged not alphabetically but by how much they shame me: the ones I've read and loved at eye level, the ones I pretend to have read higher up, and on the bottom shelf, spine-inward, the ones I wrote at twenty-three and will deny to my grave. On the table by the window there is a shoebox. In the shoebox there are drafts. Not of novels — those go in the drawer with the denial shelf. The shoebox holds beginnings. First paragraphs of letters, first lines of the real book, the confession, the account of the fire and the forty minutes and the phone in my hand that did not become a call. I have started that document maybe two hundred times. Every draft dies in the same place: the second sentence. The first sentence is easy. *I need to tell you what happened.* Anyone can write that. The second sentence requires a noun, a verb, a name, a fact, and facts are the one currency I have refused to spend for a decade.

Ten years is long enough that the guilt has stopped feeling like guilt and started feeling like geology. It doesn't stab anymore; it settles. It's sediment. I live on top of it the way towns live on top of older, drowned towns, building cheerful little structures and calling the ground stable.

But lately — and I want to mark this precisely, because later I will need to know when it started — the ground has been shifting. Small tremors. Last month I caught myself writing a postcard that was almost honest: *I drove past the old terrace site today.* True! I had! And I left it in, and mailed it, and lay awake half the night feeling like a man who'd left his front door unlocked in a neighbourhood he no longer trusted.

And this morning, buying the lighthouse card, the shopkeeper — a woman named Dot who has sold me approximately 3,650 postcards and never once asked why — said something that lodged in me like a splinter.

"You know," she said, peeling the price sticker off with her thumbnail, "my sister used to write to her old house after they moved. Every week. Never missed. My mum said it was grief." She handed me the card. "Mum said it was grief. I reckon it was practice."

"Practice for what?" I asked, and my voice came out wrong, too quick, a man reaching for a falling glass.

Dot shrugged. "Dunno. Whatever comes next, I suppose."

I carried the card home through the drizzle — the real drizzle, the one I would not be reporting — and I thought about practice. Musicians practise scales. Boxers practise falling down and getting up. What does a coward practise? Stopping. The art of the stopped sentence. Ten years of daily rehearsal in the craft of the almost-said, until I could halt a confession mid-word with the smoothness of a concert pianist lifting his hands from the keys.

And if Dot was right — if all of it was practice — then somewhere ahead of me was the performance. Somewhere there was a room, an audience, a moment when the stopped sentences would have to start again and run all the way to the end, and I would stand up having rehearsed only the halting, and discover I had forgotten how to continue.

* * *

That evening I did something I hadn't done in years: I took out the shoebox and read the drafts.

They're terrible, uniformly. Not badly written — I flatter myself the prose is fine — but terribly shaped, because every one of them is a corridor that ends in a bricked-up door. *The night of the fire, I was on Marlowe Road, and I saw* — stop. New draft. *E, if you're reading this, then the thing I never said is* — stop. Two hundred corridors, two hundred walls. Some of the drafts are dated. The most recent is fourteen months old. Even my failure had developed gaps, like a church congregation thinning out, like anything abandoned slowly enough that no one marks the exact Sunday it died.

I sat with the shoebox on my knees while the dry cleaner's machines whirred below like distant weather, and I tried to work out what I was waiting for. Permission? From whom? Forgiveness? From whom? A fire needs three things, they teach you in school — heat, fuel, oxygen — and my confession had fuel to spare and all the oxygen in the world and no heat whatsoever, no spark, no reason to catch now that hadn't existed any of the previous 3,649 days.

Except.

Except that today, posting the card, I had noticed something new. The memorial bench on the car park — the one nobody sits on — had a fresh bunch of flowers on it. Carnations, going soft at the edges, rain-beaten. Someone had been there recently. Someone else, all these years, had been carrying an address that no longer existed, and had broken cover before I did, and had left colour on the wet wood where I had only ever left silence.

I don't know why that undid me slightly. It shouldn't have. Grief is not a competition and mourning is not a queue. But standing there with my postcard already swallowed by the box, reading the florist's tag — no name, just *For the family* — I felt the specific vertigo of discovering you are not alone in a room you believed you haunted privately. Someone else knew that bench mattered. Someone else's calendar had Tuesdays in October circled. And if they could leave flowers, what exactly was my excuse for ten years of weather reports?

I put the shoebox back under the table. I washed up two mugs and a pan, thoroughly, the way you do when your hands need employment. Then I went out, past the dry cleaner's shuttered front, into the evening, with no destination, which is how I make most of my significant decisions.

I ended up — and I swear this is true, though I understand how it sounds — at the central library, because it was open late on Thursdays and because libraries are the only public buildings where a man can loiter with purpose. I wasn't looking for anything. I was looking for anywhere that wasn't my kitchen table and its shoebox and its 3,650 stopped sentences.

Which is, of course, exactly the state of mind in which a person finds things.

The notice was in the front window, pinned among the yoga classes and the lost cat, typed on a card gone slightly yellow at the corners, in handwriting so precise it looked printed:

**WANTED: ONE STUDENT. The Ilumbe language. I am its last speaker. Serious enquiries only. Preferably silent.**

Preferably silent. I stood there reading it three times while the drizzle ticked on the glass above my head, and I felt something I hadn't felt in a decade, which was the sensation of a door — not bricked up, not burning, just closed — waiting to see whether I would knock.

A language with one speaker left. A woman asking for one student, and asking him, of all things, to be quiet. I should have walked past. Everything in my training, everything in my ten-year curriculum of Almost, said walk past, say nothing, go home, describe the weather.

Instead I took out my pen — the good one, the lying-hand one — and copied the number onto the back of my hand, pressing hard enough that it would survive the wash.

I told myself it was research. I am a novelist; I collect dying things; a last speaker of a language is a story walking around unrecorded. This explanation was available, plausible, and false, and I carried it home like a coat over my arm, knowing perfectly well what I was actually hungry for, which was simpler and more shameful: someone to need me. Someone whose need was louder than my guilt, so that for an hour a week I might forget the shoebox, the bench, the forty minutes, the sentence that waits inside every sentence I write like a wire inside a wall.

The rain kept on. The card with the lighthouse was already gone, flying toward a sorting office and a car park, carrying its cargo of invented sunshine to a house that had been smoke for ten years.

Tomorrow I would write number 3,651. Tonight, for the first time, I dialled a different number instead, and listened to it ring, and discovered that my heart — that tired, sedimentary organ — remembered how to knock.
