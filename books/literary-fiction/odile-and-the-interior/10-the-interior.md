## 10. The Interior

Spring came to the bay the way forgiveness does—slowly, and all at once.

One morning the ice was still holding at the edges, gray and grudging, and the next it had gone soft, and the day after that it was gone entirely, and the water was doing its old silver business again as though it had never once been a wall. Odile stood at the window with her coffee and pronounced judgment: "Late again. It's always late. I've stopped taking it personally."

I had been at the cottage for four months by then. Long enough that my boots knew the loose board on the third stair. Long enough that when I woke in the dark I no longer reached for a phone that wasn't there, and no longer expected the particular silence of the Eriksen house—the silence I had cataloged room by room, item by item, like an appraiser of nothing. This silence was different. It was furnished. It had a kettle in it, and a woman humming off-key in the next room, and forty-two letters in a drawer that had finally, at long last, been emptied of their secret.

The estate was lost in March. I signed the papers in a lawyer's office that smelled of toner and old carpet, and my pen moved the way my mother's had moved all those years, signing away a thing without ever once saying its name. The auction notice went up on the county website between a tractor listing and a estate sale for someone named Prue Kettleman, whose lifetime of possessions had been reduced to a single line of bold text. *Eriksen House, coastal parcel, sold to satisfy judgment.* Eleven words for thirty years of my mother's patience. I found I didn't mind. I found, in fact, that I minded so little it embarrassed me, the way you're embarrassed to feel light after carrying something so long you'd mistaken it for your own weight.

Aldous was diminished the way a great house is diminished—not demolished, just visibly less. The firm had distanced itself with the swift, hygienic efficiency of firms everywhere. The deed challenge had gone through, then been appealed, then been settled in Odile's favor on the colony parcel, and the rest—the quitclaims, the forged signatures, the tidy ledger he'd presented at the will reading like a magician's empty hat—was now a matter for people with better shoes than mine. I saw him once, in April, across the parking lot of the county courthouse. He was getting into his car, slower than I remembered, and he looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't name and didn't need to. I raised my hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *I see you. That's all I ever did.* He drove away.

"He'll be at the auction," Odile said that evening, not looking up from her canvas. "Men like that attend the funerals of things they killed. It's a form of vanity."

"Should I go?"

"To your mother's house going under the hammer?" She considered it, brush hovering. "No. You already said goodbye to that house. You said it a week after the funeral, to an empty room, in the form of a list. The building is just catching up to you."

* * *

I was painting badly. I want to be precise about this: not modestly, not with the false humility of the talented. I was painting *badly*, with the wholehearted incompetence of a person who had spent thirty-four years being competent at things they didn't care about. My skies were the color of dishwater. My bay looked like a parking lot that had ambitions. My attempts at the birches along the north path produced what Odile called, with terrible affection, "upset celery."

"Again," she'd say, and I'd scrape the canvas, and the smell of turpentine had become, without my noticing, the smell of home.

"You understand," she told me one Tuesday, "that you're not learning to paint. You're learning to be looked at. The painting is incidental."

I set down the brush. "That's a cruel thing to say to someone holding a brush."

"It's the kindest thing I know." She came and stood beside me at the easel, small and upright, her hands behind her back like a general reviewing troops she expected to lose. "Your mother painted the same way you do—like she was apologizing to the canvas for taking up its time. Thirty years I watched her do it. And every time I said, *Maren, the painting doesn't care if you're good. It cares if you're honest.* And she'd laugh and keep apologizing." She tapped the canvas, right in the middle of my upset celery. "You have her hand. I'd know it anywhere. What you don't have yet is her nerve, because she never had it either, and someone has to break the chain."

"And you? Did you have the nerve?"

"I had the nerve," Odile said, "and it cost me everything, and I would do it identically again, and I have never once told anyone that because it sounds like either a lie or a boast." She looked at me then, straight on, blue eyes gone pale as winter water. "You asked for the truth when you walked through my door. So: I don't regret her. I regret the arithmetic. I regret that love got entered into a ledger at all, and I have spent my whole life furious at the *entering*, not at the love. Do you understand the difference? It took me fifty years to understand the difference."

I said I thought I did. She snorted, which was her way of saying *you don't yet, but you will, and I'll be here when you do*, which was, I was learning, the entire architecture of her affection.

* * *

The letters had gone out in January, all forty-one, plus my forty-second—the one I had written at Odile's kitchen table at two in the morning while she slept, addressed to a woman who had been dead a year and who I addressed, finally, by name. *Dear Mother. I found your voice. I'm sorry it took me this long to listen. I'm keeping it.* I mailed them to the old Meridian post office box, the one from the return addresses, and Odile collected them each week like tide wrack, reading them in order, one per evening, rationing herself the way she rationed everything except opinion.

She was on letter thirty-nine now. She knew how they ended. She had lived how they ended. And still she read each one slowly, twice, and then sat with her hands folded and let the room hold her.

"Forty-one winters," she said one night, unprompted. "I counted them too, you know. Same number, counted from my side. Isn't that something. Two people doing the same arithmetic alone for half a century and neither one carrying the one."

"You could have written back."

"To where?" She spread her hands. "She never sent them, Vesper. There was no *where*. That was the whole cruelty of it—she built me a room in her silence and I couldn't even find the door." She paused. "Until you found the door. Behind a wardrobe. She always did hide things in the worst possible places and then live in terror of them being found."

I laughed, and she laughed, and then neither of us laughed, and the fire did its small work, and I understood that this was what a found family felt like from the inside: not a revelation but a routine. A kettle. A chair that was yours. Someone who knew which silences meant *hold me* and which meant *leave me be*, and could tell them apart without being told.

* * *

In May, the auction came and went without me. Odile was right that I didn't need to attend, but she was wrong that I didn't need to know, and so I sat by the phone—the cottage had a phone, ancient and cream-colored and attached to the wall by a cord like an umbilical—while Odile's neighbor reported the results in the tone of a man reading weather. The house went to a couple from the city, young, delighted, planning a renovation. *They found the panel behind the wardrobe,* he said. *The realtor mentioned it. They're keeping it.*

I put the phone down and stood in Odile's kitchen and cried—not the numb, competent crying of the funeral, which had felt like a chore performed well, but the ugly, visible kind, the kind Odile had been trying to teach me since January. She didn't come to comfort me. She sat across the table and let me be seen doing it, which was, I understood finally, the comfort. When it was over she pushed a cup of coffee across the table and said, "There. First honest thing you've painted all year."

* * *

The colony is not what it was, but it is not nothing. The main hall still stands, and in June a small group of us—Odile, me, the neighbor, a woman from the historical society who had spent years being politely ignored—began clearing it out. We found paintings in the loft, wrapped in canvas and twine: Odile's early work, the work of a woman with a name on a deed and a future in her hands. We found my mother's mark too, faint, in the corner of a shared sketchbook, a line of handwriting that said *O., teach me nerve* and was answered, in another hand, *come get it.*

I stood in the loft holding that page a long time.

"Take it home," Odile said from below, not unkindly. "It's yours. Both of it is."

* * *

On the last evening of June, we sat on the cottage steps and watched the light go long over the water. Odile was eighty-three and tired in the way of people who have earned every hour of it, and she leaned her shoulder against mine, which she had begun doing lately, small surrenders she'd have denied under oath.

"The deed challenge is settled," she said. "The colony is mine again, or will be once the ink dries. And when I die, it's yours. I've told the lawyer. I've told you. I'm telling the bay, so it's official."

"Odile—"

"Don't argue with a dying woman, it's rude and you'll lose." She was smiling. "I'm not dying *soon*. I'm dying *eventually*, like everyone, only I have the good manners to say it out loud, which is the entire lesson I've been trying to teach you since January. Say it out loud. The unsaid thing is the only thing that can hurt you, and it can only hurt you while it stays unsaid."

I looked at my hands. They had paint under the nails that no amount of scrubbing fully removed anymore, and I had stopped trying. "I spent my whole life being the unsaid thing," I said. "In that house. In that family. I thought if I was quiet enough and useful enough, someone would finally say me."

"Someone did," Odile said. "Forty-one times. She just did it in the wrong direction." She patted my knee, once, hard, the way she did everything. "And now you say yourself. Out loud. Badly, if necessary. *Badly* is fine. Badly is honest."

The light came down the bay like something being poured. Somewhere behind us the kettle was starting its small complaint. On the table inside lay a letter that had arrived that afternoon—thick envelope, unfamiliar hand, the return address of a law firm I did not recognize, and my name spelled correctly on the front, which after everything felt like either a courtesy or a threat.

I hadn't opened it yet. Odile had looked at it once and said only, "Well. Open it when you're ready. Or don't. But if you don't, you have to tell me why, out loud, at dinner, like a person."

I looked at the water. I looked at her. I reached for the envelope.
