## 17. The Last Unopened Letter

The boarding house smelled of salt and old plaster in the way it always did after a storm, and Odile—Vera, she reminded herself, Vera now, Vera for forty years—lay awake listening to the rain work at the window like a patient stranger trying the same latch over and over. The letter lay on the bedside table where she had put it three days ago, face down, its cream rectangle pale against the dark wood. She had not touched it since she'd slid it into her pocket on the seawall, had carried it up the hill like a coal she was afraid would catch.

She knew what the other forty-six had contained. She had heard them read aloud against the wind, that stubborn man standing at the rail with his mother's voice pouring out of him in the gray light—apology, apology, apology, the same wound licked in different seasons, the same hands wrung in 1983 and 1991 and 2004, the same sentence returning like a tide: *I should have told you. I should have said. I should have come.* Forty-six letters of a woman talking to a door that was never going to open, and Odile had stood there with her collar up and her jaw set and let the words break over her like weather, because she had earned them, hadn't she, or he had, or their mother had—someone had earned this long penance, this recitation of a debt that could never be discharged because the creditor had spent four decades being no one.

But the forty-seventh she had not let him read. She had taken it from his hands before he reached it, and he had looked at her with an expression she was only now, in the dark, beginning to name: not relief, exactly, and not hurt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man watching someone else finally pick up a weight he had been carrying on their behalf.

The rain kept on. She got up, put on the cardigan she wore mornings, and sat at the small table under the window where she did her crosswords, and looked at the letter without picking it up.

It was thinner than the others. That had struck her on the seawall, the way it struck her now—how slight it was, how the envelope seemed to hold almost nothing, as if the woman who had written it had finally run out of the material of guilt and been reduced to a single sheet. Her mother's hand on the front: *Odile Vasseur*, no address, no *c/o the harbor district*, nothing. Just the name, the old name, the drowned name, written in a hand gone loose and trembly at the edges, the hand of a woman who knew she was writing toward the end of something.

*Vera*, she said to herself, the way she did every morning at the mirror, a word like a hand on a banister. *You are Vera.*

But the woman on the envelope had never heard of Vera.

She picked it up. The paper had gone soft at the corners from its forty years in a box, and it made a sound when she turned it over, a dry whisper, like a page turning in an empty house. The flap was sealed with a stamp that had never been licked properly; one corner had lifted and resealed itself over the decades, so that opening it would be less like breaking a seal than like persuading something that had only been pretending to be closed.

Her mother had died in March. The man—Quentin, Quentin, she was learning to say it, her brother, the boy from the station grown into a tall careful stranger—had told her this on the seawall in his flat, careful voice, the voice of a man who inspected things for a living and had learned to deliver bad news like a report. *She went in her sleep. She had your photograph. The one you took. It was on her dresser.*

Odile had said nothing to that. She had thought: *good*, and then she had thought: *liar*, and then she had understood that both thoughts were about herself.

Now she worked her thumb under the lifted corner of the flap. The paper resisted, then gave, with a soft tearing whisper that sounded very loud in the small room. Rain against the glass. A gull somewhere, complaining at the hour. Down the hall, the landlady's clock, which had kept wrong time for twenty years and which Odile had long ago come to think of as the truest clock she knew.

One sheet. Folded in thirds. The handwriting she had not seen since she was nineteen years old and it had been delivering verdicts at the kitchen table—*this is what happens when you lie, this is what happens when you play with matches, this is what your father would say*—that handwriting, and yet not it. Slower. Sparser. The letters of a woman who had stopped performing.

She unfolded it.

*Odile.*

Just that, on the first line. No *my dear*, no *my darling*, none of the endearments that had salted the other forty-six letters and that she had heard Quentin read out until the words wore smooth as sea glass. Just her name, the old one, set down plainly, the way you set a stone on a grave.

*I am not going to apologize in this one. You have had forty-six apologies and I expect you will have read none of them, and you would be right not to. An apology is a way of asking someone to stop being hurt so that you can stop being sorry. I understand that now. I understood it too late, which is the only way anyone understands anything.*

*I am going to ask you a question instead, and I want you to know that I do not expect an answer. I have no right to expect anything. But I have been lying awake at night lately—this is what happens at my age, the nights go long—and I have been trying to find the true thing underneath all the false things I have said and written, and here is what I find at the bottom:*

*Did you survive me?*

Odile's breath went out of her.

Not *did you survive the fire*. Not *did you survive what we did to you*. Not even *are you alive*, which was the question the whole machinery of those forty-six letters had been built to avoid asking, because to ask it plainly would have been to admit that the woman writing had never once checked, had preferred for forty years the exquisite torture of not knowing to the risk of finding a door actually closed.

*Did you survive me.* As if the mother herself were the fire. As if the house that burned was not the house on Corvel Street but the family inside it, and as if the woman who had let her daughter carry the blame for a fire she had started—had started, Odile had learned this from the first letter Quentin opened, had learned it standing on a seawall while a stranger read her mother's confession to the wind—had finally, at the end, looked at what she had done and understood that the casualty list had never closed. That the fire had kept burning in her daughter for forty years. That a mother can be a fire, and that the question of whether the child got out is not answered by whether the child is breathing.

Odile read the line again. *Did you survive me.* The rain had softened to a hiss. The clock down the hall said some hour that wasn't the hour.

She had prepared, over three days of not touching the letter, a whole armory of refusals. She had rehearsed reading it with contempt, the way she had listened to the others: *there she goes again, there is the woman who taught me that love is a ledger, look at her balancing the books at the end.* She had rehearsed reading it coldly, like a document, like a fire report, like the municipal paper that had declared a girl dead who was eating chips on a night bus to the coast while her mother slept. She had rehearsed not reading it at all—had rehearsed, in fact, the version where she finished the crossword, went to bed, and in the morning walked the letter down to the seawall and let the tide have it, unopened, unread, the last word of her mother's long monologue finally swallowed by the sea that had agreed to take her name.

She had not rehearsed this: that the question would be true. That after forty-six performances, the woman would finally, in the last week of her life, with her hand shaking and her time running out, set down the one sentence that was not a bid for absolution but an act of seeing. *Did you survive me.* Not *forgive me*. Not *come back*. Not *I loved you*, which would have been the cruelest sentence of all, because Odile had spent forty years carefully not deciding whether it was true.

The question did not ask for anything. That was what undid her. Every other sentence her mother had ever spoken to her had asked for something—obedience, silence, guilt, gratitude, the performance of a daughter's forgiveness that would let the mother put down her burden and sleep. But this question only looked. It was the first sentence her mother had ever written her that did not require Odile to be anything at all. It simply stood there on the page like a lamp in a window, and asked, and waited, and did not demand that anyone come home.

Odile wept.

She was aware of it the way you are aware of weather arriving—first the pressure, then the change in the air, then the rain itself. It began as a heat behind the eyes and a tightening in the throat that she recognized from forty years of successful suppression, and she did what she had always done, which was to swallow it, to press it down, to be no one, to be Vera, to be a woman doing a crossword in a boarding house while rain came off the bay. She pressed it down and it did not go down. It rose instead, all of it, everything, the whole forty years of it at once, and she understood with a strange clarity that this was not one grief arriving but all of them, queued in the dark like passengers at a night bus window, each one waiting its turn: the girl on the bus with her cheek against the cold glass; the nineteen-year-old in the rented room in Grayhaven listening for her name in the street and never hearing it; the young woman who had stood outside the employment office rehearsing a new name until it stopped tasting like a lie; the wife she had almost been, once, in a town further down the coast, before she understood that she could not let anyone that close to a fire; and underneath all of it, at the very bottom, the smallest passenger of all, the child on Corvel Street who had done nothing wrong, nothing, nothing, who had been a child, and who had been told by everyone she loved that she was the thing that burned.

She wept for that child. She had never once wept for that child. She had wept, over the years, in small managed amounts, for herself—for the loneliness, the lost name, the life unlived—but never for the girl she had been, because to weep for her would have been to admit she had existed, and Odile had built a whole life on the foundation that she had not. Vera had no sister. Vera had no fire. Vera had no mother. Vera had a seawall and a crossword and a landlady's wrong clock, and that had been enough, that had been enough, that had been—

The letter was damp where a tear had struck it, and she watched the paper drink the salt water and thought, with the strange practicality that survives inside even the largest grief: *there. Now it has been delivered.* Forty years late, through the wrong post, by the wrong hand, but delivered. The letter had finally reached its address. Not the address on the envelope—there was no address on the envelope—but the one underneath everything, the one her mother had been writing toward all along without knowing it: the girl at the kitchen table, the girl on the night bus, the girl in the fire that never went out. *Did you survive me.*

*No*, she said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain, to the woman who had written the question and was now beyond all answering. Her voice came out wrecked, a stranger's voice, a girl's voice. *No. I didn't. I got out, but I didn't survive you. I carried you. I carried you for forty years and I called it nothing, I called it Vera, I called it a seawall and a morning walk, but I carried you the whole way.*

And then, because grief given its proper shape leaves room beside it for the other things that were waiting there all along: *But I'm here. You hear me? I'm here. I got out and I'm here and I'm old now too, and you never got to see it, and I never got to show you, and that is the thing that can't be fixed, that's the crack all the way through, and I am going to have to be a person who lives with a crack all the way through, the way everything in this town lives with the sea.*

The rain thinned toward gray dawn. She sat at the table with the letter flat before her, and she read it a third time, and a fourth, and each time the question did its work, not as a wound now but as a kind of company—the first honest sentence her mother had ever spoken to her, keeping her vigil at the table like a guest who had finally arrived after forty years on the road.

When the light came up she did a strange thing, a thing Vera would never have done. She took the photograph from the drawer—the one she had taken the night she left, the family at Corvel Street frozen in their last whole moment, her own face scratched out with a kitchen knife by the light of the bus station, a violence she had committed against the girl she was leaving behind so that the girl could never be used as evidence—and she set it on the table beside the letter. Mother and daughter, side by side, the scratched face and the shaking hand. Two documents of the same fire.

She looked at the scratched face for a long time. Then she thought of Quentin—of the man on the seawall with his collar up and his mother's sentences in his mouth, the boy from the station who had seen her go and had kept her secret for forty years without ever being asked, the brother she had acquired and lost and acquired again in the space of three days—and she thought of what he had said when she took the last letter from his hands. He had said, *It's yours. All of it's yours.* And then, after a moment, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy: *I'll be at the Saltgrass another day, if—* and had not finished, because he was a man who inspected things and knew better than to load a structure past its rating.

There was a bus at ten past nine. She knew this the way she knew the tide tables; she had watched it come and go for twenty years without once boarding it, the way you keep a door you never open just to know it opens.

Odile folded the letter along its old creases and put it in her cardigan pocket, over her heart, which was still doing its arrhythmic, grieving, forty-years-overdue work. Then she put on her coat, and took the photograph, and went downstairs past the landlady's wrong clock into a morning washed clean and shining, and stood at the bus stop on the front road with the letter against her ribs and her heart going like a girl's.

The bus came at ten past nine. She got on it.

She was going to find her brother, and tell him that the answer was no—and that it was also, somehow, against all the evidence, yes—and that there was a photograph in her bag with a scratched-out face that she had finally, after forty years, decided to give back.
