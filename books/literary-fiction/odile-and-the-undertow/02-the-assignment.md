## 2. The Assignment

The email arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, except that Freya would remember it as a Tuesday for the rest of her life.

She was at her desk in the regional office, a converted grain warehouse near the harbor with radiators that clanked like small animals trapped in the walls, when the notification slid into the corner of her screen. *Contract Opportunity: Vardøya Ferry Terminal — Structural Assessment, Phase II.* She read the subject line twice, the way you read a sentence in a foreign language, certain you've misunderstood it.

Vardøya.

The cursor blinked. Outside, a gull stood on the windowsill with the patience of a creditor. Freya's coffee had gone the grey-brown of old rope, and she drank it anyway, because that was the kind of morning it was — a morning for doing things mechanically, without consulting the part of herself that might object.

She had left the island at nineteen. She had not been back. Twenty-two years, and in all of them she had arranged her life — her routes, her contracts, her holidays, even her silences — so that no road, no ferry line, no errand of chance ever required her to cross that water. It had not been a vow. Vows were dramatic, and Freya distrusted drama the way she distrusted corroded cable: it looked strong and failed without warning. It was simpler than a vow. It was a habit, worn smooth, the way a path wears through grass. You did not decide, each morning, not to walk to Vardøya. You simply never walked there, and after enough years the not-walking became the shape of your life, and you called the shape a self.

Now the shape had an email in it.

She opened it. The language of the tender was the language she trusted most in the world — load cases, corrosion mapping, fatigue analysis of welded connections, a completion window before the winter schedule made the terminal inaccessible to heavy equipment. The Vardøya terminal was sixty-one years old. The pontoon link-spans had never been load-rated to modern standards. There were reports of movement in the approach ramp, spalling concrete at the abutment, and — the phrase that made her sit forward despite herself — *anomalous vibration under wave loading, cause undetermined.*

Anomalous vibration. Something down there in the structure, shaking when the water pushed at it, and nobody able to say why.

Freya knew the terminal the way she knew her own hands. She had grown up in its shadow, in the years when the ferries still came twice a day and the whole island arranged itself around their arrivals like iron filings around a magnet. Her father had worked the ramp for a season, back when she was small, and she could still feel the texture of the concrete under her palms, the salt crust, the rust weeping from the expansion joints in orange tears. She had kissed a boy behind the fuel shed at fourteen. She had sat on the bollards with her homework on her knees, watching the water, waiting for Anders to come back from wherever Anders had gone, which was everywhere, always, the whole island was his and he was its.

And then there was the last time she had stood on that terminal, nineteen years old, in clothes that smelled of other people's smoke, with a bag she had packed in the dark.

She closed the email. She opened it again. This was not indecision, she told herself. This was due diligence.

* * *

At home that evening, Hal was making the sound he made when he was cooking, which was no sound at all. That was the thing people didn't understand about their marriage, Freya thought, watching him from the doorway — that silence could be a full-time occupation, requiring both parties, morning and night. Hal chopped an onion with the concentration of a man defusing something. The kitchen smelled of butter and thyme. It was, objectively, a good life. She had spent years constructing that sentence and she could still only assemble it in the passive voice.

"Contract came through today," she said.

Hal didn't look up. "Which one? The Narvik piers?"

"Vardøya. The ferry terminal."

The knife stopped. Just for a beat — half a second, maybe less — and then resumed, and if Freya had not spent twenty years learning to read the seismograph of this man she would have missed it entirely. The knife went *tok, tok, tok* against the board, and the onion fell apart into clean white moons.

"Vardøya," he said.

"The terminal needs a full assessment before winter. I'm the senior inspector for the region. It's mine if I want it."

"You haven't been back."

"No."

"Twenty-two years."

"You've been counting."

"I've been *noticing*," Hal said, which was different, and they both knew it, and neither of them pursued it, because pursuing things was not what they did. He tipped the onion into the pan and it hissed, and he stood there stirring, his back to her, a broad and careful man in an apron she had bought him three birthdays ago. "You'll decline, then."

It was not a question. That was the remarkable part. He said it the way he said *we're out of milk* — a statement of household fact, already filed, already absorbed into the ledger of things known about Freya Galloway. *She won't go back. She won't talk about her father. She won't cry at funerals. She won't.* Hal had built an entire architecture of her refusals and lived in it comfortably, the way you live in a house you know the creaks of.

"I haven't decided," Freya said.

Now he did turn. He looked at her with an expression she could not immediately name, and then could, and wished she couldn't: it was the look of a man watching a load test on something he'd believed was solid. "Freya. It's Vardøya."

"I know where it is. I've had it on maps my whole life."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know that's not what you mean."

The pan spat. Hal turned back to it. And this was the marriage, right here, in miniature, in a kitchen that smelled of thyme: two people standing a metre and a half apart, each holding a sentence they did not say, each trusting the other to hear it anyway, and neither trusting the other enough to check. *I'm afraid of what's on that island. I'm afraid of what you'll find there. I'm afraid you'll find something that isn't me.* She could have said any of them. She said:

"I'll look at the drawings tonight. Decide by Friday."

"Fine," said Hal, who had already decided for her, and whose only error was which way he'd decided.

* * *

She did look at the drawings. That was the professional cover story and it happened to be true, because Freya Galloway did not do things halfway; it was the one inheritance from her father she had kept without irony. She spread the as-built plans across the dining table — the 1963 originals, blue-inked, someone's careful hand drawing the link-spans and the pontoon cells and the mooring dolphins, and she ran her finger along the approach ramp and felt, absurdly, the way you feel a scar through clothing.

The terminal sat on the eastern side of the harbor mouth, sheltered from the worst of the weather but open to the swell that came down the sound. She knew the swell. She had grown up inside it, had learned to sleep in it, that long breathing of the water against the ramp pilings, in and out, in and out, the island's own respiration. *Anomalous vibration under wave loading.* Somewhere in that old structure was a frequency nobody had accounted for, a thing shaking when the water touched it, and she understood — sitting there with her tea going cold and the lamp making a small warm island of the table — that she was not going to decline.

She understood it the way you understand a wave has broken: not at the crest, but after, when the water is already all around you.

Because here was what twenty-two years had not done: they had not made the island smaller. She had assumed distance would work like decay, that Vardøya would diminish in her memory until it was a place like any place, a shape on a map, a childhood with the usual weather. Instead the island had grown. It had grown until it was underneath everything — under her career, her marriage, her careful mornings with Hal — the way bedrock is under a building, unseen, unfelt, determining everything. Every silence in her life had a foundation, and the foundation was a night in October when she was nineteen, and a storm, and her brother's oilskin jacket coming ashore two days later empty of her brother.

She had never said any of this to Hal. She had said almost none of it to anyone. Her mother had died still waiting for Freya to say it, and Freya had stood at the funeral — the one funeral she'd allowed herself to attend, the one she'd driven eleven hours to reach and left before the coffee — and had thought: *I will carry this the way you carried it, Mother. Silently, and to the grave, and we will call that strength.*

She was thirty-one when she met Hal and she had chosen him, though she'd never have admitted it, precisely because he didn't ask. Other men had asked. *What happened to you? Why won't you go back? What are you thinking right now?* Hal asked about load paths and holiday bookings and whether she wanted the window seat, and she had mistaken his tactlessness about everything that mattered for kindness, or perhaps it was kindness, or perhaps they were the same thing in him, or perhaps after enough years the distinction stopped mattering, which was its own kind of finding.

The drawings were sound. She could find nothing wrong with the drawings, which meant the problem was in the structure, which meant someone had to go and put their hands on it.

She thought about the tender's completion window. Before winter. The winter ferries ran three days a week, weather permitting; a full assessment needed six to eight weeks of access. If she accepted, she would be on the island by late September and possibly still there when the gales started.

If she accepted.

She closed the plans. In the other room Hal was watching something with the volume low, a documentary about bridges, ironically, or perhaps not ironically at all — Hal watched documentaries about her work the way some men watched their wives sleep. She stood in the doorway between the rooms, one foot on each side of the threshold, and let the silence do what it always did, which was fill up with everything unsaid until it was less a silence than a solid.

"Hal," she said.

"Mm." He didn't pause the television.

"I'm going to take it."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she watched him decide — she could see the decision happen, a small labor behind his eyes — not to ask why. She watched him choose, as he had chosen for twenty years, to receive her decisions like weather reports: noted, prepared for, never questioned. And she felt the familiar gratitude, and beneath the gratitude the familiar grief, and beneath the grief something new, something with edges, which was: *he would rather lose me slowly than risk me by reaching.*

"I'll be back for Christmas," she said.

"Will you?" he asked, and it was almost gentle, and it was the truest thing either of them said that night.

* * *

She submitted the acceptance at 7:14 the next morning, before her courage could develop the cracks she was professionally trained to find. The confirmation came back within the hour, bureaucratic and bloodless: *Assignment confirmed. Mobilization window: September 23–29, weather dependent. Site contact details to follow.*

Weather dependent. She almost laughed. Everything about Vardøya was weather dependent. Her whole childhood had been weather dependent. The night that had ended it had been, by every official account, a weather event — a gale with gusts to sixty knots, a small boat out in it, a search called off at dawn.

Freya worked the rest of that day the way she worked every day: thoroughly, quietly, well. She calibrated instruments. She answered emails. She ate lunch at her desk and tasted none of it. To anyone watching — to Hal, that evening, over a dinner she barely remembered — she was the same Freya Galloway as always, competent and contained, a woman whose surface tension held. That was the trick of dread, she thought. Everyone imagined it as shaking hands, a racing heart. But real dread, the kind with twenty-two years of practice behind it, looked exactly like professionalism. It looked like a woman packing a field kit with perfect economy, checking the torque wrench, the ultrasonic gauge, the bore-scope, sealing the sample bags, printing the tender documents and squaring their edges against the desk. It looked like a woman who had turned her terror into a checklist because a checklist could be completed and terror could not.

She packed on the Thursday. Hal watched from the kitchen doorway, arms folded, and offered, "You'll need the good boots," and she said, "I packed the good boots," and that was the entire conversation, and they were both, in their separate ways, devastated by it, and neither of them said so, and the boots were excellent boots, and that was the marriage.

On Friday night she lay awake in the dark listening to Hal breathe — the deep, even respiration of a man asleep or performing sleep, she could no longer always tell — and she did the thing she did on the bad nights, the nights before mobilizations, which was recite the inventory. Not aloud. Never aloud. In her head, in the strict order of the unsaid, the way other people said their prayers.

*I have never told you about Anders. I have never told you that I was the last person he spoke to. I have never told you what I said. I have never told you that I didn't go to my mother's deathbed because I couldn't bear to hear her not ask me about it. I have never told you that when you stopped asking me things, some nights, I hated you for it, and most nights I was grateful, and I have never known which of those was the marriage. I have never told you that I am lying next to you rehearsing a confession I will never deliver, and that this — this exact act, this silent rehearsal — is the truest thing about me, and you have never once suspected it, because I am very good, Hal, I am load-rated, I am certified, I have never once failed an inspection.*

The dark held it all. The dark was used to it.

On Sunday the 23rd of September, Hal drove her to the ferry terminal on the mainland — a different terminal, a different water, he had made sure of that, or she had, years ago, in a conversation they'd never had — and helped her carry her kit down the ramp, and stood with her in the wind while the ferry came in, white and workmanlike, throwing its wash against the pilings.

"Six weeks," Hal said.

"Six weeks. Maybe eight."

He kissed her, briefly, on the mouth, in the way of people who have practiced. "Call when you land."

"I will."

She walked up the gangway and did not look back, because looking back was for people who believed in returns. She found a place at the rail on the leeward side. The ferry cast off, the harbor opened, and the mainland — Hal, the warehouse office, the kitchen with its clanking radiators, the whole load-tested, never-lived life — began to shrink behind her with the terrible gentleness of all departures.

Ahead, low under a lid of cloud, maybe an hour out across the grey water, Vardøya was already there.

It had never stopped being there. That was the thing she had never let herself understand until this moment, standing at the rail with the wind pulling at her jacket: you could not leave an island. You could only put water between yourself and it, and water, she knew better than anyone alive, remembers everything, and gives things back.
