## 3. Crossing

The ferry left the mainland at 7:40 in a light that couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Grey at the waterline, then a thin gold seam along the eastern hills, then grey again, as if the day were testing its own load capacity before committing to anything.

Freya stood at the stern with her coffee going cold and watched the mainland shrink. She had done this crossing perhaps two hundred times as a child and adolescent—school trips, supply runs, the annual medical van—and she knew its rhythms the way she knew the sound of her own breathing. The first ten minutes were sheltered water, all the chop of the harbour flattened by the breakwater. Then the ferry turned north past the point, and the sea opened like a door.

She hadn't made this crossing in twenty years.

She'd told herself, packing on Thursday night while Hal pretended to read in the next room, that it was just a job. That was even true, in the way that most of the things she told herself were true in the way that mattered least. A contract was a contract. The Vardøya terminal was forty-one years old, built in the same optimistic decade as half the coastal infrastructure she spent her life crawling over, and the municipality wanted a condition assessment before they committed money to anything. Piers, dolphins, fender systems, the vehicle ramp, the passenger gangway. She would measure, photograph, note, rate, recommend. Four days, maybe five if the weather turned. A standard assignment that happened to be located at the exact coordinates of everything she had spent two decades not thinking about.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. Hal.

*Weather ok?*

Two words. She could write a dissertation on those two words. Twenty years ago he would have written *Are you okay? Do you want me to come? I don't understand why you took this job but I'm trying.* Ten years ago: *Call me when you land.* Now: *Weather ok?* As if she were cargo. As if the only question worth asking about his wife was whether conditions permitted her delivery.

*Fine,* she typed. Then deleted it. Then typed *Fine. Sea calm.* Then deleted *Sea calm* because it sounded like something from a shipping report, which was apparently the register their marriage had settled into, and sent just *Fine.*

She put the phone away and looked at the water.

Here was the thing about the sea between the mainland and Vardøya: it was not one thing. Locals knew this. There was the shallow shelf off Kvalnes where the water went green-gold and you could count the kelp heads from a moving boat. There was the deep channel, cold and black even in August, where the ferries ran because the depth forgave a heavy keel. And there was the stretch—the stretch—where the current came around the headland and met the outgoing tide and did whatever it wanted. On calm days it looked like nothing, a slight wrinkling, a seam in the surface. On bad days it stood up in short steep waves that came from no direction you could name.

Anders had gone into the water there.

Not from the ferry. From the shore, from the rocks below the old boathouse, at night, in weather that every adult on the island had spent the evening saying would keep everyone indoors. He was seventeen. She was nineteen. And for twenty years the story she carried, the story the island carried, the story her parents' silence carried, was that Anders Galloway had drowned in the stretch, and that his sister had been in the house, and that no one knew why he'd gone out.

That last part was true. No one knew why he'd gone out. Not even, in the fullest sense, Freya. She knew things adjacent to why. She knew things that circled why like gulls circling a fish that wouldn't come up. But the center of it, the reason itself, had stayed down there in the channel, and she had left it there, and she had built a whole life on top of the place where she'd let it sink.

Except it hadn't sunk. That was the discovery of the last twenty years, or rather the slow confirmation of something she'd known by week two: some things don't sink. Some things are buoyant. They stay down only as long as you hold them under, and your arms get tired, and eventually you learn to sleep holding them under, and then you wake up one morning on a ferry deck looking at a seam of wrinkled water and your arms are tired again.

The coffee was terrible. Ferry coffee always had been—same machine, probably, same burnt mineral taste, the taste of every childhood headache. She drank it anyway. Rituals of return should hurt a little.

A gull kept pace with the boat for a while, then lost interest. The mainland hills sank behind them until they were just a dark suggestion, and ahead Vardøya did what Vardøya always did, which was refuse to appear until it appeared all at once. You'd stare at empty horizon for an hour and then the island would simply be there, entire, as if it had surfaced rather than approached. As a child she'd believed the island did it on purpose. That it liked surprising people. That it liked, specifically, surprising her.

The ferry's PA crackled. Something about the winter schedule, effective in three weeks, reduced sailings, please plan accordingly. Three weeks. In three weeks this boat would run twice a day instead of four, and in January maybe once, and anyone who needed to leave the island badly enough would have to leave badly enough to do it in the dark on a rolling deck. She thought of herself at nineteen doing exactly that, standing roughly where she was standing now, facing backward so she wouldn't have to watch the island go, gripping the rail hard enough to leave marks, telling herself *never* with the full unearned authority of a teenager who has just learned what grief weighs.

Never had lasted twenty years. It had lasted precisely until a PDF landed in her inbox with a site map attached, and she had looked at the little red outline of the terminal compound and felt—not dread, she'd told herself, not dread, professional interest—and accepted the job before the better part of her could file an objection.

There was a part of her, honest at odd hours, that suspected the better part had never been consulted at all.

* * *

An hour out, the sea changed.

It happened gradually, the way it always did. The long swell from open water shortened. The wind, which had been a steady southwest breeze, began to come in gusts with gaps between them, and in the gaps the surface went strange—flat, then furrowed, flat, then furrowed—as if something underneath were breathing. The seam. She found it without looking for it, the way you find a scar on your own body in the dark. There. Just off the port bow, where the headland's shadow lay on the water even though the sun wasn't high enough yet to make shadows.

Other passengers drifted toward the rails with their phones out. A young couple photographed the light. Two men in hi-vis discussed something with the word *concrete* in it, loudly, over the wind. A woman near the bow held a small dog inside her coat, only its head out, ears streaming back, wearing an expression of pure offended joy.

Freya stayed at the stern. She didn't want to look at the stretch straight on. She looked at it sideways, the way you look at a person across a room whom you're not ready to speak to.

Twenty years. She had done the arithmetic on the drive to the ferry terminal and again on the boat and now a third time, involuntarily, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth. Twenty years since the night the whole island's lights came on. Since the search boats with their floodlights sweeping the seam in long white arcs, lighting up rain and spray and the faces of men she'd known all her life, every one of them wearing the same expression, which was not hope. She'd stood on the shore between her mother and the water and her mother had held her arm so tightly it bruised, and neither of them had said a single word, not then, not in the days after, not ever, really. Her mother had died six years ago still never having said the word *Anders* in Freya's hearing. Her father was alive somewhere on the island now, in the care home above the harbour, which was a fact she had learned from the contract documents, of all things—a footnote in a geotechnical appendix, the island's population figures, its aging demographics. Her father, itemized.

The grief literature—the books Hal bought early on, back when buying books counted as action—all agreed on a vocabulary. Closure. Processing. Moving through. Freya had read them with the professional skepticism she brought to engineering reports by authors who had clearly never stood on the structure in question. Nobody moved through. You moved around. You built your road around the thing that wouldn't sink, and after enough years the detour became the route, and you forgot there had ever been a direct path, except on certain mornings when you woke up tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

The ferry rolled. Coffee sloshed over her thumb. She wiped it on her coat and looked at the water directly, finally, the way you rip off a plaster.

The seam was there. Of course it was there. It would be there when she was dead. It didn't remember Anders; that was the obscene mercy of water, that it kept nothing, that it had taken him and closed over him within minutes and had spent twenty years being merely water. All the memory was hers. All the memory was on the shore, in the house, in the bodies of the living. The sea got to be innocent. Everyone said the sea took him, but the sea had only done what seas do; it was people who had failed to hold on, people who had—

She stopped the sentence. She was good at stopping sentences. She had a lifetime's practice. Somewhere in her there was a notebook—literal, actual, a green Moleskine in her work bag—that she'd started keeping two years ago, in which she wrote the sentences she didn't say. It had begun as a trick from a therapist she'd seen for four sessions before deciding therapy was another form of load-testing without repair. *Write down what you can't say,* the therapist had suggested. *Get it out of the loop.* So Freya wrote them down instead of saying them, which any engineer could have told her was not getting them out of the loop but cataloguing them, archiving them, building them a nice dry storage facility with regular inspections. The notebook had pages of entries. Most began with *Hal,* and ended mid-sentence, and the middle parts were the truest things she'd ever written and no one would ever read them.

One entry, from last March, said only: *I know why Anders went out that night. Or I know part of it. Or I am the only person alive who might know it, and I have never once said it out loud, and if I say it out loud it becomes real, and if it becomes real then—*

Then nothing. She'd never finished it. The pen had stopped the way pens stop when the hand behind them decides, without consulting you, that this is far enough.

The island grew. Details arrived: the white smear of the lighthouse on the outer skerry, the green roofs of the harbour houses, the crane at the fish plant lying idle against the sky like a sleeping bird. And the terminal itself, her destination, her alibi: the long low shed, the ramp, the pilings marching out into the water with their fenders hanging like old tires on a tow truck, which is more or less what they were. Even at this distance she could see things wrong with it. Rust weeping from bolt heads in brown tears. A dolphin leaning, two degrees maybe, three—hard to tell at range, but her eye was calibrated for lean the way other eyes were calibrated for faces. Concrete spalling at the waterline, that classic cancer of northern piers, freeze and thaw working year after year at the seams until the rebar showed through like bone through skin.

Structures fail honestly, she'd told a client once. They show you their cracks. People fail dishonestly—they hide the cracking until the whole thing lets go at once.

The ferry slowed. The engine note dropped, and the wash curled away from the hull in long pale wings, and the smell of the harbour came out to meet them—diesel and salt and kelp and, beneath it, faintly, fish, the island's oldest perfume. A man in orange overalls appeared on the pier to take mooring lines. Gulls screamed their opinions. Somewhere a radio played, tinny, half-swallowed by wind.

And at the rail, ten feet from her, stood the old woman.

Freya didn't know when she'd arrived. That was the unsettling part—one moment the rail beside her had held only a coil of rope and a life ring, and the next moment there was this woman, small and upright, hands folded on the rail, watching her. Not watching the harbour approach. Not watching the gulls. Watching *her*, with the unhurried attention of someone confirming something they already knew.

She was old—eighty, eighty-five—but not stooped. She wore a man's wool sweater, navy, mended at both elbows with different yarn, and a scarf the colour of wet slate, and her hair was white and cut short and the wind moved it without disturbing her at all. Her face was seamed and brown and utterly calm. Her eyes were pale, almost colourless, the blue-grey of deep water, and they rested on Freya with an expression Freya could not immediately name and then could, and the naming of it made her stomach drop:

Recognition. Not of a stranger. Of a girl grown into a woman. The recognition of someone who had known you at eleven, at fourteen, at seventeen, and was reconciling that person with the one standing here now, and finding the reconciliation easy.

"You'll be a Galloway," the woman said.

Her voice was low and roughened, an accent Freya heard and felt in her chest before she parsed it—the island accent, but older, denser than the one she'd grown up hearing, thickened with vowels that modern speech had worn smooth. Words shaped differently. *Galloway* itself came out with a weight on the second syllable, a small rise and fall, almost sung.

"I—" Freya's voice caught on nothing. "Yes."

"Kirsten's girl." The woman nodded slowly, as if confirming a calculation. "You have her chin. And your father's way of standing, like the ground owes him money." A pause. Wind filled it. "You've been away."

"Twenty years," Freya said. The number came out of her before she could weigh it, and hung there between them, enormous, embarrassing, true.

The old woman didn't flinch from it. She looked at Freya the way Freya looked at cracked concrete: taking the full measurement, not rounding down, not looking away from the worst reading. And then she said, in that same low singing voice, with no cruelty in it and no softness either, just the plainness of deep water:

"You were there that night."

The ferry touched the fenders. Rubber groaned against rubber. Lines flew, were caught, were made fast. The engine died and the sudden quiet rang, and the harbour sounds rushed in—gulls, the halyard of the flagpole clinking, a door banging somewhere in the terminal shed—and through all of it the old woman went on looking at her, patient as tide, waiting for an answer to a question she had not actually asked.

Freya's mouth opened. Closed. Twenty years of practice at stopping sentences, and now, at the exact moment she most needed the skill, it abandoned her entirely.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, which was the oldest lie she owned, and the old woman received it gently, the way you receive a child's obvious fib, and smiled—just barely, just at the corners—and turned toward the gangway as the crew opened the barrier, leaving Freya alone at the rail with her heart going like a hammer on sheet metal.

The woman walked down the ramp without hurrying. At the bottom she paused, and looked back up, and said something else—but too quietly, swallowed by wind and distance, two or three words in that dense old voice, words that were not in Norwegian, not quite, not in any language Freya could name, though some buried part of her stirred at the sound of them like a dog hearing its name after years.

Then she was gone, up the pier, a small upright figure in a mended sweater, and Freya stood gripping the rail of the dead ferry and understood that she had just been measured.

Not inspected. Measured. By someone who already knew the readings.
