## 9. Terminal Condition

The report wrote itself in a way the truth never had.

Freya sat in the terminal's back office—Signe's old radio room, actually, with its corkboard of tide tables from 1998 and its window fogged at the corners where the sealant had given up—and typed the findings into the county template. *Structure: reinforced concrete, 1961. Condition: poor. Spalling at all seaward faces. Rebar exposure consistent with chloride ingress. Recommendation: decommission and demolish.*

She had measured everything. That was the job: you measured, you rated, you condemned or you passed. The building didn't get a vote. Neither did she.

The numbers were not close. The pile caps were cracked through in two places. The dolphin fenders had rotted to their cores. The concrete on the north face had gone the color and texture of old bread, and when she pressed her thumb to it that morning, a flake the size of a playing card came away in her hand like something molting. Underneath, the rebar was orange as rusted lace.

*Demolition recommended within 24 months,* she typed. *Interim load restrictions: no vehicle traffic on apron; pedestrian access limited to marked route.*

Then she stopped, because the marked route ran directly past the lighthouse log room, and because she could see, from where she sat, Signe Brenden's silhouette moving behind the frosted glass of the archive door, shelving something, or pretending to shelve something, the way people do when they want to be near without being in the way.

Freya deleted the sentence about the marked route and rewrote it so the pedestrian access would pass on the seaward side instead. It was a small thing. A routing line on a drawing. But it meant that whatever crew came to fence off the failing sections would have to walk past the archive every day, and maybe one of them would knock, and maybe Signe would show them the logbooks, the way she'd shown Freya—the ferry manifests going back to 1934, the weather entries in a hand that slanted like wind itself, the marginal notes in the dialect that no dictionary had ever caught.

*You can't save a building by rerouting a footpath,* she told herself. She knew that better than anyone. She'd spent twenty years inspecting structures and she knew exactly how much a routing line weighed against chloride ingress and frost heave and the simple arithmetic of money. Nothing. Less than nothing. The county had already budgeted for the new terminal on the mainland side; Vardøya's was a rounding error scheduled for erasure.

But she kept the reroute anyway. It was the first dishonest line she'd ever written into a report, and it was so small, and it was for someone else, and she found she couldn't take it out.

* * *

She brought the draft to Signe that evening, though there was no reason to. There was no reason for most of what she did now. She'd stopped pretending otherwise around the third week.

Signe was in her chair by the paraffin heater, the log open on her knees, and she looked up when Freya came in with the expression of someone who has been expecting a visitor since morning and will never admit it.

"Well," she said. "The verdict."

"You could read it."

"I could." Signe took the pages but didn't look at them. "I've been reading this building my whole life. I know how it ends." She set the report on the side table, squared its edges, left it there like a guest she was too polite to dismiss. "Concrete is a young person's material. Everyone forgets that. It seems permanent when they pour it, and then forty years later it's dust with ambitions."

"Sixty years," Freya said. "It lasted sixty."

"So did I, nearly." And then she coughed—the cough that had been getting worse since the first hard frost, deep and wet, from somewhere below the ribs, the kind of cough that has opinions. She rode it out with her eyes closed, one hand flat on her sternum, and when it released her she said, in the tone of a woman changing the subject at her own funeral, "Read me the worst line."

"*Recommendation: decommission and demolish.*"

"In the old language we had a word for a thing torn down before its time." She said it then, that word—*skjærvast*—and made Freya repeat it until the vowels sat right. "A boat wrecked uselessly. A hayrick burned wet. Anything destroyed not by necessity but by impatience." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "The county has no word for it. The county thinks demolition is just a verb."

"The county's right, though." Freya heard her own voice go flat, professional, the voice she used on site walks. "That apron will fail under load. The seawall behind it is undercut. If a truck goes through—" She stopped. "I don't write reports to be wrong."

"No," Signe said. "You write them to be safe. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone alive."

Freya looked at the heater, at the blue flame breathing behind its mica window. "What would you have me write?"

"Nothing different. Condemn it. I'm not asking you to lie about concrete." Signe leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the map of veins on her temple. "I'm asking what happens to the logbooks when the roof comes off. Paper doesn't survive demolition dust. Ask anyone who's ever moved a library in winter."

"They'd go to the mainland archive. There's a protocol—"

"There's a *skip*, you mean. There's always a skip." Another cough, shorter this time, bitten off at the cost of visible effort. "Twenty years ago a man from the county came and microfilmed half of these. Half. He decided which half. Do you know what he kept? Ferry schedules. Fares. The economics." She tapped the log on her lap. "He left the margins. Everything my mother wrote in the margins. Everything the keepers wrote when the official record ended and the true one began. He left the language, Freya, because he didn't recognize it as information."

Freya thought of her own reports, filed in triplicate, every deviation flagged, every judgment defensible. She thought of how much of her own life she had left in the margins because no template had a field for it.

"What do you want me to do?"

"I want you to photograph every page before the county touches anything. I want you to sit here with your good camera and your good patience and copy out what the microfilm man threw away. And I want—" She paused, and Freya watched her decide whether to spend the next sentence. "I want you to understand that this building falling down and me falling down are the same event happening twice, and you are the only inspector either of us is going to get."

The heater ticked. Outside, the harbor water slapped the pilings in the dark, patient as a metronome.

"That's not fair," Freya said quietly.

"No," Signe agreed. "Almost nothing worth saying is."

* * *

She worked the next three days on the terminal proper, because the work was real and the work was hers: hammer-sounding the deck, mapping cracks with crack-width gauges, marking spalls with yellow spray paint until the structure wore its sickness openly, a body painted for surgery. The ferry crew got used to her kneeling at the edge of the apron with her gauge while passengers stepped around her like she was part of the defect.

On the third afternoon she found the void.

It was under the old coal store, a low concrete vault nobody had entered since the ferries switched to diesel. Her cover meter said the slab above it was half its design thickness, and when she swung the inspection hatch open and put her head down into the dark, her torch found water standing ankle-deep on the floor and a column of black mold climbing the far wall like handwriting.

And something else. A crate. Wooden, stenciled, wedged against the wall above the flood line.

She went down the ladder even though the risk assessment said don't, because the slab was sound over the dry corner and because she had already stopped being the person who followed her own protocols. The crate's lid came away with a shriek of nails. Inside, wrapped in tarpaulin gone stiff as bark: paper. Ledgers. A tin box with a rusted latch. And on the top ledger's spine, in faded ink, a name she recognized from the flyleaf of Signe's current log.

Her grandmother's era. Her mother's, possibly. Keepers' records, dumped down here decades ago by some efficient soul who couldn't be bothered to carry them up the stairs.

Freya stood in the flooded dark with the ledger in both hands and felt the strange vertigo of a woman whose two lives had finally touched. The inspector had found it. The daughter would have to decide what it was worth.

* * *

She carried the crate up into the light and straight to the archive room, and Signe, seeing it, went very still in her chair.

"Where."

"Under the coal store. They've been down there since—" Freya checked the stencil date. "Since before I was born."

Signe reached out and touched the top ledger the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. Then the cough took her again, harder than before, doubling her forward, and Freya knelt instinctively and put a hand on her back and felt the fragility there, the birdcage of her, and held on until it passed.

When Signe straightened, there was blood-fleck at the corner of her mouth, pink as a flaw in marble, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them saw it.

"We start tomorrow," Signe said hoarsely. "Photographing. Copying. All of it—every margin, every note. Before the county comes with its skip." She laid her hand flat on the crate. "And Freya. When your report goes in, and the men with the machines arrive—there will be a morning when this room is empty and the door stands open and the gulls are in here stealing the insulation. I need to know, before that morning, that you can say these words without me. That the language isn't living in my chest only. Because buildings fall. That's just physics." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "But a language falls the moment the last two people stop speaking it, and right now, little Galloway, it is living in exactly two chests, and one of them is failing faster than the concrete."

Freya looked at the crate, at the ledgers, at the report lying on the side table with its clean condemnation, and understood that she had already written her second report of the season—the one with no template, no load ratings, no defensible judgments. It was writing itself in her notebook, in the dialect's thin new alphabet she was only beginning to hold, and its recommendation was a single line:

*Structure: two. Condition: failing. Action required: immediately.*

That night she emailed the office from the terminal's one working socket, attaching the draft, and stood a long time over the send button—not over the demolition finding, which was true, but over the last paragraph she'd added at the bottom, the one requesting that all archival material on site be catalogued and preserved prior to any works commencing.

A request. Not a requirement. The weakest instrument she had.

She sent it anyway, and then she walked home along the seawall in the dark, listening to the water work at the piles beneath her feet, eating away at everything, patient as grammar.
