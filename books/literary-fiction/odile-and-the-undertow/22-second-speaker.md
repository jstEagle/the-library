## 22. Second Speaker

The new community hall smells of sawdust and fresh varnish, and every morning for a week I have arrived before the children do, just to stand in it and breathe. It sits where the old terminal used to be—set back from the waterline now, as if the builders understood that the sea should be watched from a respectful distance—but from its tall east windows you can see the whole harbor, the ferry slip rebuilt in pale new timber, the breakwater where the waves come in long and orderly in autumn, like a queue of gray animals waiting to be counted.

I am the one who counts them. That is what a year has made me.

The classroom holds eleven children. On Vardøya, eleven is a generation. Their desks are secondhand from the mainland school, their chairs too tall for the smallest of them, and their teacher—me, Freya Galloway, fifty-one years old, formerly a bridge inspector, formerly a wife, formerly a girl who left this island at nineteen and did not look back for twenty years—stands at the front of them with a whiteboard marker and a language in her mouth that nearly died with Signe Brenden on a dawn last October.

Nearly. I have come to love that word. *Nearly* is the hinge the whole thing swings on.

The archive accepted the recordings in November. Two hundred and fourteen hours of Signe's voice: vocabulary, songs, the names of every sea state her grandmother knew, the story of a drowning night told properly at last, with the true villain in it and the true witnesses. The archivist, a patient man named Dahl, had wanted to list the dialect as extinct. I stood in his office in Oslo with the lighthouse log under my arm and told him, in the old words, that a language with a second speaker was not dead, and that I would sit in his office until the paperwork said *dormant*. It took three hours. I had nowhere else to be. That is the luxury of a life finally arranged around the truth.

*Dormant*, the catalogue says now. Like a seed. Like a thing waiting for weather.

The children file in at nine, stamping the cold off their boots, and I write the word on the board the way Signe wrote it for me the first evening I came back to her kitchen, humiliated and hungry, having refused her offer at noon and returned by dark because refusing was a lie I couldn't hold up past sunset.

*Havsgjærn.*

The space between waves. Not the trough exactly, not the interval exactly—Signe spent a whole lesson teaching me that it was neither, that it was the held breath of the sea, the place where the water gathers itself to decide what it will become next. The islanders had needed a word for it because they lived inside it, in the pauses between the ferry's arrivals, between the fishing seasons, between the storm and the calm, between the living and the drowned.

"Say it," I tell them, and eleven voices say it, ragged and bright, and one of the voices is careful in a way that makes me look up.

Her name is Maja. She is nine. She sits third row, window side, and she has her grandmother's chin and her own private weather, and for a year now she has been the one who stays behind after class to ask the questions the others are too restless for. Why did the language almost die. Who was Signe. Do I have family on the mainland. Whether the word for grief in the old dialect is really the same word as the word for the color of the sea in November, and when I say yes, she nods slowly, as if I have confirmed something she already suspected about the world.

She reminds me of myself, and I have stopped pretending that frightens me. It steadies me. A girl who watches. A girl who keeps ledgers. A girl who will, if someone is patient, learn to spend what she has been hoarding.

"Again," I say, and they say it again, and outside the window the harbor does the thing the word describes, and I feel Signe in the room the way you feel a change in pressure—not a presence exactly, more a correctness. As if the air has agreed to hold what we are saying.

* * *

Signe died at dawn on the fourteenth of October, in the island care home, with the window open a hand's width because she had insisted, because she wanted to hear the water even if she could no longer see it. Her final word was the blessing-word, the one with no English equivalent, the one that means roughly *may what you carry be light enough to set down and heavy enough to mean something*, and she said it to me with her eyes closed and her hand in mine, and then the hand was just a hand, and the word was mine alone to carry.

I said it back. That is the whole of the inheritance, and it is enough.

What I did not expect was how much else would turn out to be mine. The lighthouse log, yes—I had salvaged it from the terminal the morning the excavators came, walking out of the dust cloud with it under my coat like a thief, which in a way I was. But also the lessons themselves, the shape of them. Signe taught the way the island taught: by repetition, by weather, by refusing to hurry. I teach the way she taught. I catch myself using her pauses, her trick of making a child sit with a word until the word sits with them. Grief converted into stewardship, I wrote in the report I gave the heritage board. But it is not grief anymore. It is just the work. The work is what love becomes when it stops being a wound and starts being a practice.

Hal came to the archive opening in December. We stood in front of the glass case with the lighthouse log in it, and he read the label, and he said, "You spelled my absences right. In the notebook. You got them exactly right." And I said, "I know. I read yours too, before I gave them back." And we stood there in the museum quiet, two people who had spent fifteen years keeping ledgers instead of speaking, and there was nothing left to audit. He takes the city flat. I keep the cottage. We talk on the phone sometimes, and the conversations are short and true, which is more than they ever were when they were long and false. Last month he told me he'd started seeing someone, a surveyor, someone who asks him questions. He sounded almost surprised by his own relief. I was not surprised at all. I said the blessing-word down the line to him, and there was a long pause, and then he said, "I don't know what that means, but I think it means goodbye the right way," and I said, "It means exactly that," and we hung up, and I stood in the kitchen and cried for two minutes, cleanly, the way you cry when nothing is wrong.

* * *

Today's lesson is the sea-state words. Forty of them, though the children have only mastered twelve, and I write them down the board in columns like a tide table. *Flat as a plate. Ruffled like a bird's back. The long swell that comes from storms you cannot see. The chop that lies about its depth.* Each one a different animal. Each one a different mood of the same mother.

Maja's hand goes up.

"My grandmother says the sea took Anders Galloway," she says. The room goes still the way rooms do when a child names the thing the adults have arranged their silences around. "Was that true?"

I put the marker down. I have rehearsed this answer for a year, not because I didn't know it but because I wanted to say it without flinching, and flinching, I have learned, is a habit you can unlearn the way you learn a language—slowly, daily, out loud.

"It was a storm," I say. "And it was an argument. And it was a man who should have known better, and a boy who was braver than the weather, and a girl on the shore who spent twenty years believing she had done it." I let the silence be the space between waves. "The sea didn't take him. The sea was just there. It usually is."

Maja considers this with her grandmother's chin set at a stubborn angle. "And the girl," she says. "What happened to her?"

"She stayed," I say. "Eventually. That's the whole story."

The bell goes and the room detonates into coats and boots and lunchboxes, and Maja is the last one out, the way she always is, and at the door she turns and says a sentence to me in the dialect—clumsy, consonants wrong, but whole, a complete thought built entirely from words that eleven months ago did not exist inside her—and I understand it, and I answer, and she grins and runs.

The sentence was: *I will keep them, the words.*

* * *

I lock the hall at four. The harbor is doing the thing. The long gray interval, the held breath, the water gathering itself to decide. I stand at the east window and say the word for it out loud, once, to no one, the way Signe said it to the empty kitchen for forty years, keeping it warm.

Then I take out the notebook—my notebook, the one that used to be an inventory of unsaid things, its first pages a ledger of everything I swallowed at breakfast tables and bridge decks and hospital corridors—and I turn past all of it, past the cracked grout of the old life, to a blank page, and I write the day's date, and under it I write what Maja said at the door, spelled correctly, and under that, in English, because some things deserve both:

*Second speaker, teaching the third.*

The ferry comes in across the interval. On it, I can see, there will be mail, and a crate of books I ordered, and possibly a letter from Hal with a photograph of the surveyor, because he is like that now, generous in small true ways. The water gathers itself. Decides. Becomes.

I say it once more—*havsgjærn*—and turn off the lights, and go home to the cottage where the recordings wait on the shelf, two hundred and fourteen hours of a dead woman's voice and one living woman's promise, and tomorrow there will be eleven children, and one of them will stay behind, and the language will outlive us both, the way she asked, the way I swore, the way a word for the space between waves was always meant to be handed across it.
