## 1. The Tenant

My mother died in March, which meant the funeral happened in April, which meant the cherry blossoms were doing their annual performance of beauty-as-grief, and I resented them for it. Petals kept landing in the incense smoke and everyone kept saying how appropriate, how lovely, as if the trees had been consulted.

I stood at the receiving line in my black dress—the one I'd bought for a wedding two years ago and never returned, because grief is just an occasion with worse catering—and translated. That was my function. It had been my function my whole life, if I was honest about it, and I was paid to be honest about things in other people's languages, so I had gotten very good at being honest everywhere except home.

*Osewa ni narimashita.* Your mother was a wonderful woman. *Goshūshō-sama desu.* She's in a better place now. *Kokoro kara orei mōshiagemasu.* Please accept my deepest condolences.

The sentences passed through me in both directions like water through a filter, arriving cleaner and emptier than they'd started. My Japanese was my mother's Japanese, or what remained of it—the way a rented apartment retains the shape of the previous tenant's furniture. There were dents in the language where her words used to sit. When I reached for *natsukashii*, that whole warm country of a word, I found only its English shadow: *nostalgic*, which is not the same thing at all. Nostalgia is a disease. *Natsukashii* is a homecoming.

My aunts kept calling me *Sebastiān-san*, mangling my name with the same affectionate clumsiness my mother had never corrected, and I kept bowing at the correct depth for a granddaughter of the deceased, which I knew because I'd looked it up on the train. Forty-five degrees for deep grief. I had researched my own mourning the way I researched everything: thoroughly, in advance, alone.

My brother Daniel stood beside me doing the English half of the line. Daniel, who had inherited our mother's laugh and none of her hesitation, who shook hands like a man closing a deal. Between us we made one complete bilingual child, which was probably the plan. Divide the inheritance of language, keep the estate manageable.

"Your mother spoke of you often," said a woman whose face I knew from photographs but whose name arrived a half-second too late. "She was so proud."

*Haha wa yoku anata no koto hanashite imashita. Hokori ni omotte imashita.*

I delivered it. I watched it land. And I thought: did she? In which language? Because the mother who called me every Sunday and spoke to me in a careful, simplified English—sentences with the grammar sanded down, idioms removed, the furniture of her real mind put into storage—that mother had never once said *proud*. The mother in my memory said things like *ganbatte* and *shō ga nai* and, once, when I was nineteen and announced I was moving to London to translate other people's books, said nothing at all for a full minute, then asked if I would be home for New Year's.

I had spent thirty years translating everyone else's words into everyone else's words, and somewhere in the middle of all that faithful, professional passage, my mother's language had walked out of me one borrowed word at a time, and I had let it go, because letting things go was what tenants do.

* * *

The reception was held at the glasshouse.

This requires explanation, and I have spent a lifetime avoiding giving it. My grandfather—my father's father, a man who existed in our family primarily as a set of financial consequences—had built a commercial nursery in the sixties: a cathedral of glass and aluminum on two acres outside town, where my parents had lived above the offices and where my mother had kept, in the back ranges, a garden that was not for sale. The business had failed before I was born, in the way family businesses fail, which is to say slowly and then all at once, with a lawyer involved. But the glasshouse itself had never been sold. It sat there through my entire childhood like a held breath, and my mother had gone on raising orchids and camellias in its back rooms as if the whole enterprise were simply paused, everyone just stepped out for a moment, don't touch anything.

It was where the family gathered for every occasion, because it was the only place large enough, and because my mother had insisted, and because none of us had ever successfully refused her anything.

So there we were: the mourners moving between the benches of *Cymbidium* and the long tables of food, the caterers navigating their carts down the misted central aisle, my aunts arranging the photograph displays beneath the hanging baskets with the competitive precision of women who had been waiting decades for jurisdiction over the flower stands. The light came down through the dirty glass in that particular green-grey way, so that everyone looked slightly underwater, slightly preserved. It was, I thought, the most honest room available. We were all of us hothouse things.

Daniel found me by the camellias with two cups of coffee and no condolence, which was its own kind of fluency.

"You okay?"

"I'm working," I said, which was true. Translating grief, rendering it into the family dialect. "How are you?"

"Managing." He looked at his cup. "She left the glasshouse to us, you know. Jointly. It's in the will. She told me last year, made me promise not to tell you until—" He stopped. "Well. Until."

"Until," I agreed. My mother's last arrangement: a building neither of us wanted, delivered jointly, like a sentence in two languages that didn't agree. "We'll sell it."

"Obviously," Daniel said, too fast, and then looked at the camellias as if they'd testified.

That was when I saw her.

She came in through the side door—the one from the potting shed, the staff entrance, the door that wasn't a door to anyone but family—and she did it without hesitating, the way you enter a place you have entered many times. She was perhaps my age, maybe a few years younger, tall, with the kind of stillness that reads as either confidence or rehearsal. Black clothes, correct but not ours; a black that had been worn to other funerals, other towns. She carried nothing. Mourners always carry something—a program, a tissue, a phone they keep checking. She carried nothing, and it made her the most conspicuous person in the room.

She moved down the receiving line, or rather past it, pausing at my aunt Keiko, bowing—forty-five degrees, I noted, the exact depth—and saying something that made my aunt's face perform a small, complicated collapse.

Then she was at my uncle Robert, and she said, clearly, in a voice built for quiet rooms:

"Mr. Ashworth. I'm so sorry for your loss."

Ashworth.

I stopped translating. The whole apparatus of me—the filter, the water, the forty-five degrees—stopped.

Nobody was called Ashworth. My grandfather had been an Ashworth, briefly, in the years before the business failed and before he had, for reasons never explained to the children, taken the name Nakamura—my grandmother's name, my mother's name, a name that in our family functioned less as an identity than as a settlement. The Ashworth name was a closed account. My aunts said it the way you'd say a word that had been through the wash. It was not used. It was, in fact, the one subject on which my family, so fluent in everything else, maintained a perfect and practiced silence.

The stranger moved on down the line, greeting them all. *Ashworth*. My aunt Keiko. My cousin Peter. Daniel—she said *Ashworth* to Daniel, and Daniel's handshake faltered in a way I had never seen it falter, not at funerals, not at closings, not once in forty years.

She reached me last.

Up close she had my grandfather's jaw, which I recognized from the photograph that hung in the glasshouse office, the one where he stood in front of the finished north range with his hand on the aluminum frame like a man touching the shoulder of something he'd raised. I had looked at that photograph my whole life. I had never once thought about who else might have his jaw, because in my family you did not think about such things; you translated around them.

"I'm sorry for your loss," she said. Her Japanese, when she offered it—*Goshūshō-sama desu*—was flawless. Not learned flawless. Inherited flawless. The accent sat in her mouth the way my mother's had sat in hers, in a place I had never managed to build in my own mouth no matter how many degrees I earned in the movement of words.

"Thank you," I said. "I don't believe we've met. I'm Sebastian."

"I know," she said. "You're the translator."

Not *you're her daughter*. Not *you're the granddaughter*. *The translator.* As if I had a function here, as if I'd been listed on a program.

"Who invited you?" I asked. It came out more bluntly than I'd planned, but grief had burned through my diplomatic reserves around two o'clock, and besides, she had said *Ashworth* to my brother and I wanted, more than I wanted almost anything, to know what she was.

She looked at me then, directly, and there was something in her face that I recognized and could not place—something patient and slightly sad, the expression of someone who has waited a long time for a door to open and is trying not to look at her watch.

"No one," she said. "I was expected."

"By whom?"

She turned her head, slowly, and looked up—past me, past the camellias, past the caterers and the aunts and the photograph of my grandfather with his hand on the glass—up into the high dirty panes of the roof, where the April light was coming through in that green-grey underwater way.

"By the house," she said.

Then she bowed to me—forty-five degrees, the exact depth for deep grief, for a granddaughter of the deceased—and walked back out through the side door, the one from the potting shed, closing it behind her with the soft, particular click of someone who knows the latch.

I stood there in the underwater light with my coffee going cold, and I did not translate a single word of what had just happened, because there was nothing in either of my languages for it.

Behind me, I heard my aunt Keiko say, very quietly, to no one, to the orchids: "Nobody uses that name. Nobody has used that name for forty years."

And the glasshouse held its breath, the way it always had, and waited to see what we would do.
