## 10. Crossing

The train left Villach at 7:42 and crossed into Slovenia somewhere in the fog without ceremony — a river, a rusted border marker, a shift in the architecture of the fences. Ivo had the window seat because the carriage was nearly empty, and he sat with his briefcase flat on his knees like a man guarding an organ meant for transplant.

He had told Maren it was a bridge assessment. A municipal contract, Karst region, two weeks at most. The lie had come out of him with the fluency of long practice, and she had nodded and packed him sandwiches he did not eat, and the whole exchange had taken four minutes, which was exactly as long as their conversations had been taking lately, measured against some clock neither of them would admit to owning.

Now there was nothing between him and the letters but six hours of track.

He opened the briefcase. Forty envelopes, each sealed with its own small yellowed wafer of gum that had never done its job, each addressed in his mother's hand — *A. Pemberton, Rijeka* — in ink that shifted over the decades from blue-black to a watery grey, so that laid side by side they read like a barometer of her life. He had arranged them by date on the hotel desk in Klagenfurt last night, all forty, and had stood over them for a long time before understanding what he was looking at.

Not correspondence. A ledger.

He took out the first one, dated March 1963, and began again from the beginning, this time reading not for information but for structure, the way he read a load report — not the numbers themselves but the pattern of stress across them.

*Dear Ada,* it began. *I don't know if this is your address still. Father says you will not write back and I believe him about most things, though not, I think, about everything.*

Ivo looked up. Outside, the Karst was assembling itself out of the mist — pale limestone, stunted oaks bent like men walking into wind, dry-stone walls running uphill in lines no surveyor would have drawn. His mother had grown up within sight of country like this. He had never once asked her about it. In fifty-eight years of knowing her — well, forty-one of them that counted — he could not recall a single question put to her about her childhood that she had answered with more than a sentence. He had assumed there was nothing to tell. He had assumed, the way he assumed about bridges: that silence meant soundness.

*Father has sold the ground below Kozjak,* the letter went on. *You know the one. Uncle's orchard, before. He says papers were signed and that is the end of it. Mother cries in the pantry where she thinks we can't hear. I am writing to say only that I saw the papers on his desk and that your name was on them too, in a place where names go when someone wants them to mean something else.*

Ivo set the letter down on his knee.

His hands were steady. That was the thing people always said about him — steady hands, good under load — and he watched them now with a kind of clinical detachment, these instruments of his that did not tremble even when the rest of the structure was moving. His mother, aged fifteen, had seen falsified documents on her father's desk. Had understood them. And had written to the sister they'd been destroyed for telling the truth about — and then folded the letter, sealed it, and put it in a tin.

He picked up the second letter. June 1963. Three pages this time, the handwriting faster, less careful.

*I tried to make him show me the deed again and he struck me across the ear. Mother says Ada is dead to us now and that God sees the heart, which I have decided is a coward's proverb. If God sees hearts then He sees mine and knows I am writing to you every week and burning none of them, though I post none either. I don't know why I don't post them. I think if I posted this one I would have to do something. Sealed, it is only words. Posted, it would be a weapon, and I would be the one holding it at our father.*

There it was. Page two of forty, and already the mechanism exposed, the whole engine of his mother's life laid out in a schoolgirl's handwriting: that sending would demand action, and action would demand a self she did not yet have, and so the letters would wait in the dark like pressure accumulating behind an expansion joint.

Ivo knew about expansion joints. He had spent twenty-six years inspecting them. A bridge is not rigid; it cannot afford to be. Steel grows in the heat and shrinks in the cold, concrete creeps, foundations settle by millimeters a decade, and if you built a structure that refused to move it would tear itself apart from internal stress alone. So you built in gaps — finger-shaped slots in the deck, wide enough for the structure to breathe, narrow enough that a wheel could cross without noticing. And every year the joints moved a little further than designed, packed with grit, corroding at the edges, until one day the gap was no longer a gap but a wound, and the bridge had to come down or be rebuilt.

He had spent his marriage believing he was the joint. That a small controlled gap, maintained daily, kept the whole structure standing. Maren asks a question; the gap opens; the wheel passes; the gap closes. No fracture. No collapse. He had genuinely believed this was engineering rather than cowardice, right up until the wardrobe came away from the wall in his mother's house and the tin box slid out and he understood that he was not the joint at all. He was the grit packing the gap. He was what made the movement stop.

* * *

Ljubljana came and went in rain. A woman with a cello took the seat opposite, slept with her chin on the instrument case like it was a child, and got off at Postojna without waking properly. Ivo barely noticed. He was in 1971 now, letter nineteen, and the confession had changed key.

For years the letters had been reports — what Father said, what Mother did, what the village believed. Then, somewhere around 1968, they became something else. His mother had married. Moved north. Had Ivo. And the letters turned inward, the way water turns at a weir.

*I held my son today and felt nothing I could name,* she wrote in November 1969. *The nurse said love comes like milk or it doesn't and both are natural. I looked at his face and thought: here is a person who will never know what our family did. I could teach him. I could sit him on my knee in ten years' time and say, your grandfather stole ground from his own daughter and the law helped him do it, and see what his face does. But then he would carry it. Isn't that also a theft? To hand a child a debt he didn't incur? I keep going back and forth like this. I seal the letter. I start another.*

Ivo read that passage three times. The train rocked him gently, the way a parent rocks a child, and he let it.

Because here was the thing he had never let himself think in plain terms: he had inherited the silence the way other children inherited houses. It had been furnished before he arrived. There had been no questions in his childhood home not because Edith Pemberton was a cold woman — the letters proved she was anything but; the letters burned on every page — but because she had made a calculation, sometime around 1969, that passing down the truth would be a second crime committed against a second generation. And so she had raised him inside the gap. Inside the expansion joint. She had taught him, without ever saying a word, that everything true could be measured and everything measurable could be avoided, and he had built an entire self on that foundation and called it temperament.

It wasn't temperament. It was inheritance. It was the deed to a glasshouse in Rijeka, folded into a boy before he could walk.

He took out the deed itself then, because he needed to look at it again — the heavy paper, the embossed stamp of the commune of Rijeka, the faded Italian and Croatian in parallel columns. *Staklenik, Kozjak.* Glasshouse, Kozjak. Built 1954, according to the annexes, on land purchased — purchased, the word doing its legal work, smooth as ballast — from Anton Pemberton. His grandfather. The man whose signature was on the falsified papers, per letter nineteen, per the court record Ivo had photocopied in a records office in Zagreb with hands that did not tremble.

The glasshouse had been bought with stolen ground. And then Edith had inherited it, and Edith had paid for its roof and its boiler and its panes for thirty years through a solicitor in Opatija who presumably never asked questions, anonymous payments, quarterly, like a subscription to guilt — and had never once set foot in the place. Never once written *I am coming.* Never once signed her name to anything but the cheques.

Love as maintenance. Love as an anonymous transfer of funds. He knew that language. He had spoken it to Maren for twenty-two years — the mortgage paid early, the car serviced unasked, the gutters cleared — and had called it devotion while she slowly starved for the word itself.

* * *

Somewhere past Postojna the line began to climb, and the fog lifted, and the Karst opened out — high grey plateau, wind-bent trees, stone walls marching off toward a horizon that seemed to belong to no country in particular. Ivo lowered the window a few centimetres and the air came in cold and mineral, smelling of rain on limestone, and he thought: she breathed this. Before England. Before the semi-detached house in Guildford with its measured garden and its measured silences, she had been a girl on ground like this, watching her father forge a signature, sealing letters she hadn't the courage to send.

Letter thirty-one, 1984. Ivo was fourteen that year. He found himself reading for mentions of himself the way you check a report for your own name.

*My son is clever in the way his grandfather was clever, which frightens me. He takes things apart and measures them and puts them back and seems satisfied. I watch him at the table counting his peas and I want to shake him and ask who taught you that, because it wasn't me, except that of course it was, it was the silence at this table, the silence I built brick by brick to keep the truth out, and children are mortar, they take the shape of the space you leave them.*

He closed his eyes.

The train swayed. The cello woman was long gone; the carriage hummed; somewhere ahead a tunnel was coming, he could feel it in the pressure building against his ears, the way you feel weather in old masonry.

Who taught you that? His mother, asking it across thirty years and a sealed envelope. And the answer was: you did. You taught me with the locked drawer and the changed subject and the smile you gave when anyone came near the family's past, the same smile I use now, Ivo thought, the same smile, I caught myself using it at the dinner party three weeks ago and heard in it her voice, her exact cadence, the smile that ends conversations — and I have been carrying her invention around my face for so long I thought it was mine.

But the letters also carried something else, and this was what made his chest hurt in a way he had no instrument for. Because she had known. Every year, more clearly. Letter after letter, decade after decade, she diagnosed herself with terrible precision — *coward's proverb*, *love as cheque*, *children are mortar* — and diagnosed, and sealed, and started another. Thirty years of perfect insight and zero delivery. She had written the confession of a lifetime and mailed none of it, because mailing it would have required becoming the person who could sign her name to it, and she had chosen instead to pay in private and die intact.

And Ivo understood, with the train climbing toward the pass and the light going strange and high, that he was reading his own future in installments. That if he did not get off this train — not the literal train, the other one, the one that had been running since 1963 — he would arrive at the end of his life with a study full of sealed things. Photocopies in a drawer. A marriage maintained by expansion joints until the joints failed. A smile perfected past the point of usefulness. Forty-one letters of his own, unsent, to a wife sitting in a kitchen in Guildford measuring his withdrawals with a patience he had mistaken for contentment.

The past had momentum. That was the discovery of this journey, the finding of the whole inspection. Momentum was not fate — he knew the difference professionally; momentum was mass times velocity, and velocity could be bled off, mass could be unloaded, but only by force applied deliberately, over distance, against the direction of travel. Nothing slowed on its own. Everything he had ever inspected that failed had failed because someone, somewhere, had assumed the motion was manageable and had watched it, and measured it, and filed the measurements, and done nothing.

His mother had watched. His mother had measured. His mother had filed forty reports.

The train entered the tunnel and the windows went black and he saw his own face reflected in the glass, faint, doubled, superimposed on darkness — and for once he did not look away from it, and for once it did not look away from him, and in that shared look there was neither forgiveness nor accusation, only the plain recognition of load paths: this weight came from there, and it is resting on you now, and structures do not care what you intended, only what you bear.

The tunnel ended. Slovenia poured back in — green now, descending, a river alongside the line running the same direction, fast and grey-green and certain of its destination. Rijeka was two hours away. Somewhere ahead, on ground his grandfather had stolen and his mother had secretly tended, a woman he had never met was living in a cottage attached to a ruined glasshouse, keeping the light without knowing who had paid for the roof over it.

Ivo gathered the letters back into order — 1963 to 2009, blue-black fading to grey — and squared them, and noticed that his hands, for the first time in his adult life, were not entirely steady.

He let them be unsteady. It was, he thought, the first honest measurement he had taken in years.

Outside, the river turned south toward the sea, and the train followed it down, gathering speed, past the first red roofs of the coast, toward the address on the envelopes that no stamp had ever touched.
