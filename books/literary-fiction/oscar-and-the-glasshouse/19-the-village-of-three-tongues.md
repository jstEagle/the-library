## 19. The Village of Three Tongues

The bus left Rijeka at seven in the morning and climbed the way a man climbs a staircase he doesn't want to reach the top of—steadily, with small pauses at each landing, as if hoping the building would change its mind about having upper floors. Ivo sat in the third row with the tin box on his knees, wrapped now in a plastic bag against the damp, and watched the Adriatic shrink below him into a grey coin of light.

He had copied the sentence onto an index card. He had written it out eleven times before he got it right—the dialect had no standard spelling, and Edith's hand had been quick and private, the letters of someone writing to herself as much as to another. He carried the card in his breast pocket like a work permit. *Here is a man authorized to be confused.*

Vera had given him the village name two days after Marta's condition reached her through the phone call he'd made from the cottage, standing in the yard where the signal lived. She hadn't offered to come. He hadn't asked. There was a geometry between them still, a load path neither trusted yet: any weight placed too soon would crack something.

*Vrbovo*, she'd said. Forty minutes past Kastav if the road is open. Three women left who speak it. Maybe four; one of them winters with her son in Fiume. Ask for Marta Perišić. Her house has blue shutters and a fig tree that should have died years ago.

The road was open. The fig tree, when he saw it, looked less alive than stubborn—a black candelabra against a stone wall, holding up its few last leaves the way an old man holds his opinions.

Ivo got off the bus at a bend where there was nothing that could honestly be called a stop, just a widening of gravel and a shrine niche with a faded Virgin inside it, and walked the last kilometre because the driver said the lane was too narrow to turn around on. The village announced itself by smell first—woodsmoke, wet chestnut leaves, a hint of sheep somewhere behind a wall—and then by sound: a dog making its case to the morning, unhurried, professional. Then the houses, low and limestone-grey, roofs of red tile patched with red tile of a slightly different century, chimneys standing up like raised eyebrows.

Blue shutters. A door painted the green of old shutters everywhere, sun-bleached toward the sea. Ivo stood at the gate and discovered his hands were not steady, which annoyed him. He was fifty-one years old. He had hung off the underside of bridges in wind that sang. And here he was, breathing like a man about to give evidence.

He knocked.

The woman who opened the door was small and round and wore a man's cardigan over a floral dress, and she looked at him the way border guards look at documents they've already decided to doubt.

"You're the Englishman," she said, in Croatian-accented but fluent English. "Vera telephoned."

"She did?"

"Tuesday." Marta Perišić stepped back from the doorway—not quite an invitation, more a withdrawal of objection. "You can come in. Wipe your feet. The floor is older than your country."

Inside, the house smelled of coffee and wool and something medicinal, camphor maybe, kept in a cupboard for emergencies of the chest. The room was low-ceilinged and dense with furniture that had stopped being fashionable sometime before the war—whichever war you chose—and on every wall there were photographs, dozens of them, generations deep, faces going back through sepia into the grain itself. Ivo found himself scanning them before he could stop, looking for a face he knew only from a tin box: Ada, young, unsmiling in the way people were unsmiling when cameras were rare and serious occasions.

Marta watched him look. "You won't find her," she said. "Ada never let anyone photograph her after 1971. She said the camera takes what the court already took."

Ivo turned. "You knew her."

"I grew up four houses down. We herded goats together on this hill when we were girls, before either of us knew hills could be taken away." Marta gestured at a chair. "Sit. Drink. Then we will discuss what you came to discuss, and you will discover it is not what you think."

The coffee came in small cups, thick as tar, sweet enough to be a form of argument. Marta drank hers in three swallows and set the cup upside down on its saucer, and Ivo, who measured everything, caught himself noting the exact angle of the cup and despising himself for it.

"The sentence," Marta said. "Show me."

He took out the index card. His fingers were steadier now, or he had simply stopped monitoring them. He laid it on the table between them, face up, like something surrendered at a checkpoint.

Marta put on spectacles that magnified her eyes into two alarmed brown planets. She read. Her lips moved once, shaping a word without voicing it. Then she took the spectacles off, folded them with great care, and set them down beside the cup, and looked at Ivo for a long time with an expression he couldn't file under anything.

"Where did you get this?" she asked.

"My mother wrote it. In a letter. To her sister."

"To Ada."

"Yes."

"And your mother was?"

"Edith. Edith Pemberton." He heard how the name landed, or didn't land. "Our father was Josip Pemberton. Born here. Left in—" He stopped. Recalculated. "Left in 1963."

Marta nodded slowly, the way people nod at weather. "Josip's daughters." She said it not to him but to the room, to the photographs, to some audience of the dead arranged along the walls. Then, to Ivo: "You know what the sentence says."

"No. That's why I'm here."

"It is why you are *in Vrbovo*. It is not why you are here." She tapped the table once with a knuckle, a judge calling order. "This is our language. Not Croatian—you hear the difference? Croatian is for the church and the tax office. This is for the kitchen and the grave. My grandmother spoke it, my mother spoke it, I speak it to myself when I am alone so the walls don't forget. There were two hundred speakers in this valley when I was born. Now there are three, and one of them is deaf and one of them is me, and the third is Nada Kršul, who will tell you she speaks it better than I do, which is true, which I hate." She smiled briefly, a flash of teeth and mischief that made her seem suddenly seventeen. "A language is not a dictionary, Englishman. You cannot buy it at the border. You cannot carry it up a mountain in a plastic bag."

"I'm not asking you to teach me. One sentence. That's all."

"One sentence." Marta rose, went to the window, looked out at her fig tree with the expression of a woman consulting it. "Do you know what happens when I translate that sentence for you? You will walk out of this house knowing something that Vera does not know. Something that belongs to her as much as to you—more, because Ada told her things she never wrote down, and you have the paper and she has the silence, and now you propose to add translation to your side of the scale." She turned. "No."

Ivo felt the refusal hit him physically, a hand flat against the sternum. "She sent me. Vera. She said—"

"I know what she said. I telephoned her Tuesday, didn't I?" Marta came back to the table and sat, and leaned forward, and her voice dropped into a register that was almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Listen to me. I am eighty-one years old. I have buried a husband, a son, and a language, in that order of difficulty. I do not have time to be cruel, so I will be clear instead. I will translate your mother's sentence. But not to you alone. You will bring the girl. You will bring the one who kept the glass. She sits in this room, she hears it first, in front of you, and whatever it says, you learn it second. That is my condition. That is the only condition. Take it or take the bus down."

* * *

He took the bus down. He sat in the same third-row seat going back and watched the sea grow from coin to plate to horizon, and he thought about what Marta had said, turning it over the way he'd turn a core sample, looking for the fracture line.

*Something that belongs to her as much as to you.*

It was true, and he had known it was true since the night he'd first read the letters, and he had come up the mountain anyway, alone, with his index card, because some part of him—the part that had sealed forty letters in a tin and called it love, the part that had paid a stranger's roof repairs anonymously for thirty years—had wanted to arrive first. Had wanted the sentence privately, held close, translated in the dark where no one could see him receive it. Inheritance as theft, again, one generation removed. He sat with that on the winding road while a farmer across the aisle slept with his hat over his eyes, and the thought did not crush him, which surprised him. It just sat there, being true, the way a bad weld sits there being bad until someone grinds it out.

At the glasshouse, Vera was up a ladder against the south wall, prying out a cracked pane with a wooden wedge, working with a patience Ivo had come to recognize as her native tongue. She heard him on the gravel and spoke without turning.

"Well?"

"She'll do it. On conditions."

Vera worked the wedge. A sliver of old putty fell and powdered on the stones. "Conditions."

"You have to be there. She wants you to hear it first. Before me." He stood below the ladder, hands in pockets, a man addressing a foreman. "She said the sentence belongs to you as much as to me. More, maybe. She said—" He hesitated, then decided Marta deserved accurate quotation. "She said I came up the mountain to know it first. And she wasn't wrong."

Vera came down the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, and stood facing him. Up close her eyes were the grey-green of sea glass, and right now they were doing what sea glass does, which is show you the shape of what broke it.

"You could have lied," she said. "Told me she refused. Bought yourself another week."

"I could have."

"Why didn't you?"

Because I'm tired of being my mother, he thought. Because a lie fluently told is still a wall, and I have spent my life inspecting walls. What he said was: "Because you'd find out anyway. People always find out. It's the finding out that does the damage, not the thing."

Vera studied him for a long moment. Somewhere behind the glasshouse a jackdaw argued with the wind. Then she picked up her gloves from the ladder step and slapped them together, dust rising.

"When?" she said.

* * *

They went up on Thursday, because Wednesday it rained and Vera refused to travel the mountain road in rain, and Ivo did not argue, having learned in six weeks of exile-in-the-cottage that Vera's refusals were load-bearing and you did not drill holes in them.

The bus again, but different this time. They sat together, the tin box between them on the seat like a chaperone, and Vera talked—or rather, she answered questions, and Ivo had learned to ask questions that weren't really interrogations. She told him about the hill village as her grandmother had described it: the goat paths, the spring that ran cold even in August, the festival in late summer when the old women sang songs in the kitchen-tongue and the children laughed at words they didn't understand, and Ada laughing loudest, because Ada understood every word and knew she was watching the language die in real time, one festival at a time.

"She said the village had three tongues," Vera said, looking out the window at the terraced slopes going by. "Croatian for outside. Italian for the market, from the old days. And the kitchen-tongue for home. Three tongues and one heart, she used to say. Then she'd say the heart was the first thing to go quiet."

The bus climbed. The sea disappeared. Chestnut woods closed around the road, gold and dripping from Wednesday's rain, and Ivo thought about Maren, as he did now at odd altitudes—a thought that arrived like weather rather than like guilt, which was new. He had written to her from the cottage, actually written, pen and paper, three pages about the glasshouse and none about himself, and posted it in the box by the fish market, and had not yet heard back, and was trying to let the silence be information instead of punishment.

Vrbovo received them with its dog and its smoke and its stubborn fig tree. Marta was waiting at the gate as though she'd been standing there since Tuesday, which Ivo suspected she had, in shifts.

"So," she said, looking at Vera. "The granddaughter. You have her chin."

"You knew my grandmother well?" Vera asked, and Ivo heard in her voice the carefulness of someone who has learned that questions about the dead can come back with live ammunition.

"I knew her when she was faster than the goats and meaner than the spring." Marta held the gate open. "Come in. Both of you. Wipe your feet."

Inside, the photographs again, the coffee again, the upside-down cup. But today Marta did not drink first. Today she cleared a space on the tablecloth with her forearm, the way a surgeon clears a field, and held out her hand to Ivo.

"The letter. All of it. Not the card."

He gave her the last letter, the one dated two weeks before Edith's death, and watched her read it top to bottom, her lips moving over the Croatian parts, slowing as she reached the end. When she arrived at the final sentence she read it aloud—quietly, half-voiced, the sounds coming up out of her like water from a deep well, round and dark and older than the room—and Ivo heard his mother's private language spoken for the first time in his life, and it did not sound like English at all, it sounded like weather moving through a valley, and he understood suddenly that he had been listening to his mother speak a foreign language his whole childhood without knowing it, certain phrases, certain hums when she cooked, the song she denied singing.

Marta finished. She set the letter down. She took off her spectacles. And then she was silent for so long that Vera's hand moved on the table, an inch toward the old woman's, and stopped.

"Aunt Marta," Vera said softly. So they were aunt and grand-niece by some hill arithmetic; the family tree had roots he hadn't mapped.

Marta raised one finger. Wait. Her eyes were wet, but her voice, when it came, was level as a spirit gauge.

"This sentence," she said, "is the last living sentence of its kind that I have ever heard composed. Do you understand? Everything else in this tongue is memory—songs, blessings, curses, recipes. This is new. Your mother made new sentences in a dying language. She learned it well enough, late enough, to say the one thing she could not say in any language that was still alive." She looked at Ivo. "Do you understand what it cost her to write this?"

"No," Ivo said. "Tell me."

"Tomorrow," Marta said.

Vera stiffened. "You said—"

"I said I would translate it, and I will, and I will do it tomorrow, in daylight, properly, because what it says deserves daylight and you both deserve a night to be ready for it." Marta stood, with the effortful dignity of the very old, and began gathering the cups. "I am not a cruel woman. But I have translated hard sentences before, for courts, for priests, for a German officer once, God forgive me, and I know this: a translation delivered at the moment of asking is a wound. A translation delivered after a night's sleep is a truth. You may sleep here—the bed in the back room, it was my son's. Or you may take the evening bus. But you will not hear it tonight, and you will not hear it alone, and you will not hear it standing up."

She carried the cups to the stove. At the doorway she paused, her back to them, small and round and immovable in her dead husband's cardigan.

"There is one thing I will tell you tonight," she said, "because you will not sleep otherwise, and tired people hear badly." She turned her head, profiled against the stove light. "Your mother's sentence is addressed to Ada. But it is not only an apology. An apology says *forgive me*. This sentence says something harder than that. It says *this is what I did, and here is what it made of me*. Those are different animals, Englishman. One asks for a gift. The other offers one." She set the cups down. "Tomorrow, then. Sleep. The sheets are clean."

Outside, the evening had gone violet, and the mountains to the east had turned the colour of bruises healing. Vera stood in the yard by the fig tree, arms crossed, looking up at the shuttered windows of the back room.

"Heavy," she said.

"Yes."

"She's right, though. About the difference." Vera was quiet a moment. "My grandmother never asked anyone's forgiveness. Not once, Vera said—I mean, Ada said. She said sorry was a currency the family had debased. She wanted restitution or nothing." Vera looked at him. "So whichever animal this is, it's the first one your side has ever brought to the hill."

Ivo looked at the darkening valley, at the lights coming on one by one down the slope, houses kindling like slow lamps, and felt the tin box's weight even though it was sitting on the back-room bed.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we find out which one I am."

That night, in his dead man's bed, under a quilt that smelled of cedar and decades, Ivo lay awake listening to the village settle—beam tick, dog sigh, wind testing the shutters—and rehearsed, without meaning to, all the versions of tomorrow. In none of them did he flinch. He checked this the way he checked a weld: systematically, without mercy. And near dawn, when sleep finally took him, it was with the strange calm of a man who has inspected a structure thoroughly and found it unsound, and has decided, for the first time in his life, to stand under it anyway.
