## 23. Marta's Condition

Vera came to the cottage on a Tuesday, without knocking, the way she had begun to do. She stood in the doorway with her hands in the pockets of her coat and looked at the room behind me—the letters spread across the table in their careful chronological rows, forty envelopes laid out like a surgical field—and I understood that she had come for a reason she hadn't yet decided to say.

"You've been reading them again," she said.

"Every night."

"All of them? In order?"

"In order." I had learned that order mattered to her. Order was how restorers thought: you did not begin glazing a window until you understood which pane had failed first and why. "I can tell you what she wrote in any of them. March 1983, she describes the frost on the viaduct. 1991, she writes about a dog she didn't buy because it would have been one more thing to explain."

Vera took off her coat but didn't sit. That was her way—she occupied space without surrendering to it. "Marta called me," she said. "The old woman from Vele Mune. She says if you're serious about the sentence, she'll listen to it. Once."

My chest did something complicated that I chose not to name. "She said yes?"

"She said *yes*." Vera let the word sit there, flat as a dropped tool. "But I want you to understand something before we go. She didn't say yes for you. Marta is eighty-six years old and she has buried a husband, two sons, and every person she ever spoke this language with except two other women who are nearly as deaf as she is. She doesn't owe you anything. She said yes because my grandmother kept a photograph of Edith Pemberton in a drawer for fifty years and never once told me why, and Marta thinks that kind of silence should not outlive everyone who made it." She picked up one of the envelopes, turned it over, set it down exactly where it had been. "So don't thank me. Don't thank her. Just bring the letter and be quiet unless someone asks you to speak."

"When do we go?"

"Tomorrow. Early. The road up is bad after rain." She put her coat back on, which was her version of goodbye, and then paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "Ivo. Whatever she tells us tomorrow—you asked for this. You don't get to unhear it."

"I know."

"No," she said, not unkindly. "You think you know. There's a difference."

* * *

We left before seven, when the fog was still lying in the streets of Rijeka like something drowned. Vera drove an ancient Zastava that smelled of turpentine and cold metal, and neither of us spoke for the first hour. The road climbed out of the city in switchbacks, the sea appearing and disappearing between the pines, and I watched the mist burn off the hillsides and thought about how much of my life had been spent looking at things through glass or water or instrument lenses—always one surface between me and whatever was true.

The letters were in the case between my feet. Only the last one. I had considered bringing all forty, the complete confession, the full weight of evidence—but Vera had shaken her head when I suggested it. "One sentence," she said. "You're asking her to translate one sentence. If she wants the other thirty-nine years, she'll ask."

Vele Mune was less a village than a memory of one: a scatter of stone houses along a ridge, half of them roofless, the others held up by habit. Chickens owned the road. A church with a leaning bell tower stood at the crest like a man who had fallen asleep standing up and refused to admit it. Vera parked beside a well and led me down a lane to a house with blue shutters flaking to grey, and knocked—not a social knock, but three slow blows, formal, almost ceremonial.

The door opened on a woman so small I first mistook her for a child standing too far back in the dark hallway. Then she stepped into the light and I saw that she was simply compressed by age, folded down into herself like a letter, her face a topography of lines so fine they seemed drawn rather than grown. Her eyes, though, were quick and pale and entirely unsentimental. They went to Vera first, stayed on her for a long moment—some silent transaction passing between them, granddaughter-of-Ada recognized and admitted—and only then came to me.

"So," she said, in Croatian that Vera translated with a tilt of her head. "This is the nephew."

"The grand-nephew," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't. It sounded like a correction, and corrections were the last refuge of men with nothing else to offer.

Marta looked at me for a while. Her gaze had weight; I could feel it settle on my shoulders like a hand. Then she turned and walked inside, leaving the door open, which I understood was either an invitation or a test.

* * *

The interior of the house was dim and warm and smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs hanging from the beams in bundles. There was a table, two chairs, a bench against the wall. On the walls hung photographs in frames black with age—a wedding, a harvest, soldiers I did not want to look at too closely. Marta gestured me onto the bench and lowered herself into a chair opposite, and Vera sat between us, translator and witness both, though I noticed she positioned herself slightly closer to the old woman. I could not blame her. I would have done the same.

Marta spoke, and Vera translated in a low voice: "She asks why you waited. Your mother wrote nothing to anyone here. Ada died nine years ago. Marta knew of the letters' existence—" she paused, listening, "—because your grandmother wrote to Marta's mother once, long ago, asking about the dialect. Asking whether anyone still spoke it. But she never sent the sentence itself. She asked about the language the way you'd ask about a country you never intended to visit."

I took the envelope from the case. My hands were steady—I am a man whose hands are always steady, it is the one inheritance I never questioned—but my mouth had gone dry. "Tell her I found them after she died. Forty letters. None posted. Tell her I don't know why my mother sealed them instead of sending them. Tell her that's part of why I'm here."

Vera translated. Marta listened with her eyes closed, and when she opened them she held out one hand, palm up, fingers curled slightly inward—an old woman's gesture, or perhaps a beggar's, or perhaps simply the shape a hand makes when it has spent eighty years receiving things.

I gave her the letter.

She did not open it at once. She held it in both hands and turned it over, and her thumb found the seal—the wax circle Edith had pressed thirty years ago and never broken—and rested there. Something moved across her face, some recognition I couldn't read. When she spoke, her voice had changed register, dropped into something older and rougher, vowels shaped differently, consonants softened at the edges. The dialect. Grandmother's tongue, the language of my earliest childhood that I had mistaken for nonsense syllables sung over a cot, gone before I was old enough to know it was dying.

She read.

I watched her lips move. I watched her eyes travel the page twice, three times. And then I watched her stop.

It happened at the final line. I saw the exact moment she reached it, because her whole body changed—the small shoulders drew in, the chin lifted slightly, the way a person stiffens when cold water hits them. She read the sentence a fourth time. Her lips stopped moving. She sat very still, holding the paper at arm's length now, as if distance might change what it said, and the kitchen was so quiet I could hear the fire settling in the stove and a chicken complaining somewhere outside and my own pulse in my ears.

"Marta?" Vera said softly, in Croatian.

The old woman didn't answer. She set the letter down on the table, face up, and looked at it the way I had once looked at a crack in a bridge girder that shouldn't have been there—the look of a person who understands that everything downstream of this moment has just changed.

Then she spoke, still in the dialect, a few words only, and covered her eyes with one hand.

"What did she say?" I asked. "What did she say, Vera?"

Vera was staring at Marta, and her face had gone the colour of the fog we'd driven through. "She said—" Vera stopped. Started again. "She said, *this is not a sentence, this is a wound.*"

"Translate it properly. What does it mean? The actual words."

"She won't translate it today." Vera's voice had gone strange, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening to it. "She says—" listening again, head bent toward the old woman, who was speaking slowly now, her hand still over her eyes, "—she says she knew your grandmother. Not well. They were children together in this village before the war scattered everyone. She says Edith Pemberton came back here once, in 1974, alone, and stood at the edge of the glasshouse land for an hour and never knocked on a single door. Marta saw her from this window." The old woman lowered her hand and looked directly at me for the first time since reading the sentence, and her pale eyes were wet, and furious, and grieving all at once. "She says your mother stood in the road and looked at what her father had taken, and then she got back in her car and drove away, and no one in this village ever spoke her name again until today."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the rafters a bird shifted its weight.

Marta spoke again, longer this time, and Vera translated in pieces, her voice growing quieter with each clause. "She says the sentence is in the old speech, the true speech, the one the priests tried to kill and the schools finished off. She says your grandmother wrote it perfectly—which means she learned it as a child, which means she carried it inside her for seventy years without using it, the way you'd carry a knife you never dared take out of its sheath." Vera swallowed. "She says a sentence like that is not translated for a stranger. It's translated for family. Or for the dead. And she needs to know which one I am before she decides which one you are."

Marta looked at Vera then and said something brief, and Vera's breath caught audibly.

"What did she ask you?"

"She asked me," Vera said, "if I'm ready to hear what my grandmother heard on her deathbed. Because apparently Ada said something to Marta, at the end, in the old speech. Something about Edith. And Marta says the sentence in that letter answers it." She looked at me, and her eyes were bright and hard and terrified in equal measure. "She says she will tell us tomorrow. Both of us. Together. She says some things can only be said once, and after they're said, the people who heard them belong to each other whether they want to or not."

* * *

We drove down the mountain in the late afternoon with the light going amber and long across the valleys, and neither of us spoke for a long time. At the junction where the coast road met the river road, Vera pulled over without signalling and turned off the engine, and we sat in the ticking heat of the cooling car looking at the grey water of the bay.

"My grandmother talked in her sleep," Vera said finally. "At the end. I was nineteen. I used to sit with her at night because she was afraid of the dark by then, this woman who had faced down my great-grandfather and a courtroom and the whole family, afraid of the dark. And sometimes she'd speak in that language—the old one—and I couldn't understand a word, and it sounded like..." She searched for it. "Like someone arguing with God and losing politely."

"Vera—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Whatever you're going to say, don't say it yet. Tomorrow Marta says it, and then we'll know what we are to each other. Until then I want to drive home in silence and I want you to let me."

So I let her. She started the engine and we descended through the olive terraces toward the lights of Rijeka coming on one by one along the waterfront, and I sat with the empty envelope case on my knees and understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching an instrument needle cross into the red, that tomorrow I was going to learn what my mother had carried for thirty years—and that whatever it was, it would not be survivable in the old way. It would not be measurable. It would not be avoidable.

Vera stopped outside the cottage and waited until I was out before she spoke through the window, one last thing, quietly:

"She said one more thing, at the door. I didn't translate it because I wasn't sure I should." Her hands were steady on the wheel but her voice wasn't. "She said: *Tell the boy his grandmother wrote the most beautiful apology in a dead language, and that beauty is going to be the cruelest part.*"

She drove away, and I stood in the street with the fog coming in off the water, and all night the glasshouse panes caught the moonlight and threw it back at me in fragments, and I did not sleep.
