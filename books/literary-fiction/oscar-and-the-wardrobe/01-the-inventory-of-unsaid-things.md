## 1. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The city stopped sleeping the year I started counting.

I don't mean that literally. Cities don't sleep; that's a thing people say to make insomnia sound like a civic virtue. But there was something in the air of those years — a restlessness, a low electric hum under the pavement — and if you worked nights at the Meridian Library you felt it more than most. The building sat on the corner of Ojuelegba and the service road like a patient animal, breathing in buses and breathing out headlines, and between two and four in the morning it went quiet in a way that wasn't quiet at all. It was the quiet of held breath. Of things unsaid, stacked floor to ceiling.

My name is Odile Okonkwo, and for six years I was the night librarian. Not the night *assistant*, not the night *clerk* — the night librarian, singular, because no one else wanted the shift and I had asked for it before anyone could offer. People assumed I hated people. That wasn't true. I loved them the way you love weather: from indoors, through glass, with an escape route mapped out.

What I actually hated was endings. Specifically, the last thirty seconds of a conversation, when it stops being exchange and becomes performance — the wrapping up, the "anyway," the "we should do this again," the lie of the wave goodbye. Somewhere in my thirties I had begun leaving conversations before they ended. Not dramatically. No slammed doors. I would simply let sentences die mid-breath. I'd turn to shelve a cart. I'd check a timestamp on a receipt. I'd become so suddenly, professionally busy that the other person had no choice but to finish the goodbye alone, talking to my back.

It took me years to admit this was a habit and not a temperament. Habits can be inventoried. So I invented one.

The ledger lived in the bottom drawer of the circulation desk, beneath the lost-and-found box (three umbrellas, one child's shoe, a wedding ring nobody claimed, which said everything about this city) and above the spare toner. It was a hardcover notebook, black, unmarked on the spine. On the first page, in ink I no longer use because the pen ran dry and I never replaced it, I had written: *Inventory of Unsaid Things.* Underneath, smaller: *Do not read while emotional.*

I broke that rule every night.

Here is how the system worked. Whenever I abandoned a sentence — mine or someone else's, though mostly mine — I recorded it. Date, time, location, the name of the person I'd left hanging, and the exact words where the conversation had been amputated. Not what I wished I'd said. What I hadn't. The ledger was not a place for revision. It was a morgue for language. You don't fix up a body before filing it.

Entry one, dated six years back: *Mrs. Adeleke, third-floor periodicals. She asked how my mother was doing. I said "fine" and walked away with an empty trolley.* My mother had been dead eleven months by then. Mrs. Adeleke knew this. She was asking about grief, and I had answered about logistics, and then I had left her standing among the journals holding her own kindness with nowhere to put it.

There were four hundred and twelve entries. I know because I counted once, on a slow Tuesday, and then felt sick, and then counted again to make sure the sickness was accurate. Four hundred and twelve conversations I had walked out of, deflected, smothered, or simply declined to finish. Some were trivial — the barber asking if I wanted a shape-up, me saying nothing and paying in exact change. Some were not. Entry 89: *Tunde, at Papa's wake. He said "she's in a better place." I said "yes" and went to get water I didn't drink.* Entry 203: *Dr. Bello, results consultation. He began, "Odile, there's something we should discuss about your—" and I said, "Just tell me the number," and he did, and I thanked him, and I have thought about that thank-you every day since, because it was the politest way I have ever found to refuse a person permission to care about me.*

You will notice the pattern. Everyone else always got the short end of my silence. That's the trick of avoidance — it feels like self-protection, but the wound lands outward. Every unfinished sentence has two casualties: the person who doesn't get to say their thing, and the person who now has to carry both halves forever. The ledger was full of my half. The other halves were scattered across the city, still warm, still waiting.

* * *

Nights at the Meridian had a grammar of their own, and I had learned to speak it fluently, which — given what you now know about me — should tell you something. Fluency in routines is just another way of not talking.

At ten I relieved Mr. Sanusi, who smelled of mothballs and grievance and handed over the desk with a sigh calibrated to suggest martyrdom. At eleven I walked the floors, checking windows, straightening chairs, reshelving whatever the day-shift had abandoned in defeat. At midnight the reading room emptied except for the regulars, and the regulars were the city's insomniacs, and I catalogued them too, though never in writing.

There was the taxi driver who came in off shift and slept upright in the philosophy section with a textbook open on his chest as camouflage — he'd been "studying" Kierkegaard for three years and the book had never turned past page forty. There was the young man who wrote letters every night at the same table by the window, dozens of letters, none of which ever seemed to be mailed; I knew because he brought them in and took them home, and once I saw the stack through his bag's open zipper, bound with rubber bands like evidence. There was the woman who read obituaries first and novels second, and if you watched her long enough you understood she was checking whether she'd made the paper yet, and only then allowing herself the luxury of other people's invented deaths.

And there were the ones who came in just to talk to me. Or rather — to try. The Meridian's night desk became, without my planning it, a kind of confessional with a barcode scanner. People at 2 a.m. want to say things. The dark loosens them. A man returning a biography of Napoleon told me his brother had died and the biography was the last book they'd both read, and he stood there with the book extended toward me like an offering, waiting for me to say something human.

I scanned it. I said, "Due back never, it's overdue-forgiven, we're running a clemency program."

He blinked. "A what?"

"Clemency. For overdue books. And apparently for overdue brothers." I smiled the smile I keep for closing doors gently. He laughed, uncertain, and left, and I wrote entry 398: *Man with Napoleon. He offered me his grief and I gave him a library policy.*

That was the sickness of it, you see. I wasn't cold. Cold would have been simpler. I felt everything — that was the problem. Every aborted conversation left a residue, and the residue accumulated, and the ledger was less an inventory than a pressure valve. I didn't write things down to forget them. I wrote them down so they'd stay exactly where I put them, contained, alphabetized by regret, unable to follow me home.

Home was a flat on Adeniyi Jones with one chair, because a second chair implies visitors. Home was a kettle, a bed, a wall of books arranged not by author or subject but by how much silence each one could hold. Poetry on the top shelf. Theoretical physics below it — nothing says *do not disturb* like tensor calculus. At the very bottom, face-out, a single photograph of my mother at twenty-six, laughing at something outside the frame, taken two years before I was born and eleven thousand unsaid sentences before she died.

We had not fought, my mother and I. People assume estrangement requires an explosion. Ours was erosion. She wanted things from me I couldn't name, and I withheld things from her I couldn't find, and we met monthly for lunch at the same restaurant and performed a version of ourselves — the dutiful daughter, the patient mother — until even the performance wore through. The last time I saw her alive, she said, "Odile, do you ever think we—"

"I'll get the bill," I said.

She died nine weeks later. Her last voicemail is still on my phone. I have never played it past the first second — the intake of breath, the click of her tongue the way she did before speaking — because as long as I don't listen, the message is still arriving. As long as I don't hear it, she is still about to say something.

Yes. I know. You don't need to say it. In fact, please don't. I've heard enough of what people say after entries like that one, and it all goes in the ledger anyway.

* * *

Three a.m. was the hour the library belonged to no one, and I loved it the way you love a room after a party — honestly, and a little sadly. The generators hummed. The streetlights made orange pools on the tile like dropped egg yolks. Somewhere above, in the ventilation, pigeons shifted in their sleep, and I moved through the stacks with my trolley, reshelving the day's returns, and the city outside breathed its uneven breath, and I was alone in the way I had engineered, meticulously, for six years.

Alone, but not empty. That's the distinction I want to be clear about, because it matters later. Loneliness disguised as order looks, from the outside, like competence. It looks like a woman who has her life together — steady job, clean flat, no drama, no mess. People envied me. I could see them envying me, at the lunch counter, at the bus stop, and each envious look went into the ledger too, because envy is a conversation people start with their eyes and I refused to finish with anything but a neutral expression.

Entry 405: *Girl at the suya stand said, "You look like someone who has peace." I said nothing. I paid and left. She was wrong.*

Peace is not the absence of noise. Peace is the absence of debt. And I was the most indebted woman in Lagos — not to banks, not to landlords, but to four hundred and twelve people, living and dead, who had extended me the ends of sentences and never received them back. Interest accrued nightly. The ledger grew fat. Some nights I added three entries before midnight.

And yet — here is the shameful part, the part I kept even from the ledger for a long time — some part of me was proud of it. The inventory was my masterpiece. Nobody else in the world had documented their evasions with such rigor. There was a twisted comfort in being the curator of your own cowardice: at least it was organized cowardice. At least it was *mine*. When you cannot control what happens between yourself and other people, you can control the record of it. I had chosen the record over the life. I had chosen the card catalog over the fire.

That's what Eze would have called it, years later — the fire, the real thing, the live conversation burning in front of you that you keep stepping around because you're afraid of getting burned, not understanding that the stepping-around *is* the burn, slower and total. But I'm ahead of myself. That night, I didn't know him. That night he was still a rumor of a man, an old figure the day-shift mentioned sometimes — *there's an old fellow comes in at odd hours, reads out loud to himself, foreign-sounding* — and I had filed the mention away the way I filed everything, in the drawer, under the umbrellas, above the toner.

That night, the ledger opened to entry 411, written two days earlier: *Mr. Okoro, returns desk. He said, "Your mother would be proud of you." I said, "She never saw the library," and turned to the computer, and he stood there for four seconds, which is a long time, four seconds, when someone is deciding whether to press you or release you. He released me. They always release me.*

Four hundred and eleven. One short of a number that meant nothing and everything, because I had decided, privately, without ceremony, that at five hundred I would stop. Not stop recording — stop. Whatever that meant. Resign, maybe. Speak, possibly. Burn the ledger, conceivably, though I knew even then I wouldn't; you don't burn a morgue, you expand it. Five hundred was a horizon I walked toward nightly without believing horizons could be reached.

At 3:47 that morning, with the trolley half-loaded and the pigeons stirring overhead, I heard the front doors.

They weren't supposed to open until six. I knew the lock's complaint, the specific arthritic groan of the mechanism, and this was not it — this was the softer sound of someone who knew the building, or knew locks, or simply pushed with the patience of a man to whom doors open eventually because they always have. I stepped out from behind the 800s, ready with my line about hours, my gentle bureaucratic eviction, my well-rehearsed refusal of whatever a stranger might need from me at this hour.

An old man stood in the atrium, small and upright, dressed with a formality the hour didn't deserve. He looked at the shelves the way other men look at the sea. And then, before I could speak — before I could deploy even one of my four hundred and twelve defenses — he began, quietly, to read aloud.

The language was like nothing I had ever heard. It rose and fell like weather. It sounded old the way rivers are old, and it filled the whole sleeping building, and I stood behind my trolley with my mouth open, holding a returned novel against my chest like a shield, and I did not write anything down.

For the first time in six years, I forgot the ledger entirely.
