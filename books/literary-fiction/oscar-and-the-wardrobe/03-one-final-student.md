## 3. One Final Student

A language does not die all at once. It dies the way a fire dies — first it stops leaping, then it stops crackling, then it is only heat, then only memory of heat, then a man sitting alone in a cold room insisting to himself that he is warm.

I know this because I have been the fire, and I have been the room, and lately I have been mostly the room.

Every night at three I wake without an alarm. The body keeps its own village time. In my village — I say *my village*, though I have not seen it in fifty-one years and it is probably a road now, or a filling station, or a rumor — in my village, three o'clock was the hour the old men woke to talk. Not because they had anything urgent to say. Because speech needed its own hours, apart from the hours of farming and eating and arguing about goats. Speech needed a room with no other business in it. The old men called it *ulo okwu*, the house of words. You entered it and you were nobody's father, nobody's debtor, nobody's age-mate with a grudge. You were only a mouth, and the language passed through you like water through a gourd.

For thirty years in this country I have been a mouth with no water in it. I speak English at the clinic. I speak English at the bank. I speak English to my daughter on the phone, in the flat, apologetic way you speak to someone who has already decided not to understand you. And at three o'clock I wake, and the words rise in me like water into a drowned man, and there is nowhere to put them.

So I began going to the library.

Not to read. My eyes are tired of English print; it sits on the page like luggage. I went because a library at night is the closest thing this city has to *ulo okwu* — a room with no other business in it. And I read aloud. I read the newspapers aloud, the obituaries, the weather, the recipes for things I will never cook. I read the labels of the books themselves. *Collected Poems. Introduction to Geology. Great Expectations.* I read them in my mother's tongue, and the words came out of me round and warm and alive, and no one understood a syllable, and that was the point. A language needs to be spoken even into emptiness. Especially into emptiness. The old men said a word spoken with no listener still goes somewhere. It goes into the walls. It goes into the ground. It waits.

I noticed the librarian the fourth or fifth night.

She was a thin young woman with a cardigan the color of dishwater and a way of moving through the stacks as though she were apologizing to them. She shelved with her head tilted, always tilted, like a bird that has heard something under the floorboards. And on the fifth night — or the fourth, the nights have begun to run together the way water runs together — I saw that she had stopped shelving. She was standing at the end of the aisle with a book pressed to her chest, and she was listening.

Not pretending to work while listening. Listening. There is a difference. Pretenders keep their hands busy. This girl had gone still the way people go still in church, or in the presence of an animal they are afraid to startle.

I kept reading. I read a recipe for jollof rice in a magazine, translated it as I went, and I confess I read it beautifully. I gave the tomatoes their full names. And when I finished, I heard — very small, from the end of the aisle — a breath that was almost a word. Almost the word. She had caught the shape of it. Her mouth had tried it without her permission.

That is how I knew. Not that she was clever — cleverness is common as rain. I knew because the language had already begun choosing her. It does that. It is not polite about it. It walks into a room and puts its hand on someone's shoulder, and the someone spends the rest of their life deciding whether to shrug it off.

I went home that morning and did not sleep, which was nothing new, but the quality of the sleeplessness had changed. It had a direction now. It pointed at the library.

* * *

Let me tell you what it is to be the last one. People imagine it is loneliness, and yes, it is loneliness, but loneliness is the small part. The large part is responsibility without a recipient. Every debt needs a creditor. Every gift needs a hand. I am carrying forty thousand words — more, if you count the ones for the smell of rain before rain, and I do count them, they are the best ones — and there is no hand. There is a bank vault in a university in another city where a linguist recorded eleven hours of me in 1998, and they sent me a copy on a cassette, and I have listened to it twice and could not finish the second time, because on the tape I sound like a specimen. A voice in a jar. The language is not in that tape. The language is in the conversation, the answering, the *ulo okwu*, the house of words with two people in it. A language recorded is a language embalmed. A language learned is a language alive.

And I am dying.

I say it plainly because I have earned plainness. The doctors use words like *progressive* and *we will monitor*, which is how this country says *soon*. I feel it in the mornings, a heaviness in the chest like a wet blanket folded and folded again. I am seventy-eight years old. My father died at seventy-six. His father at seventy-nine. We are a family of short candles, and I have watched mine burn down to the thumb and forefinger.

I have one daughter. Adaora. She lives in Abuja now with her husband and her two sons, and when I tried to teach the boys their grandfather's words, she laughed — not cruelly, she thought it was kindness, a kindness like a blanket over a birdcage — and said, *Dad, let them learn French, French will get them somewhere.* I have not raised it since. That was nine years ago. There are silences in a family the way there are rooms in a house, and after a while you stop opening the doors and then you stop remembering what's behind them, and then the house is sold.

So. The girl with the dishwater cardigan.

I watched her for two more weeks. I am an old man; watching is the one sport left to me. I learned her rhythms. She arrived at eleven and left at seven. She drank tea at one and again at four, from the same chipped green mug she kept behind the returns desk. She had a small notebook she wrote in when she thought no one could see, and she hid it when anyone came near, which told me the notebook was not work. Work you do not hide. Work you hide behind.

And once — only once — I heard her speaking to the security guard, and she stopped mid-sentence. Stopped the way you stop when you realize you have said too much. Her mouth stayed open for a moment and then closed, and she turned back to her cart of books, and the sentence she had abandoned just hung there in the air between them like a coat on a hook that no one will ever take down.

I know that gesture. I have made that gesture my whole life. There is a word in my language for the things a person says halfway and then swallows — it is a long word, a word with a river in it, and I have not spoken it aloud in thirty years because there has been no one to be careful with it in front of.

I decided I would ask her.

Then I did not ask her for eleven more nights, because I am also a coward, and cowardice at my age is very well practiced. It has calluses.

* * *

On the twelfth night I brought the tin.

It was a biscuit tin, blue, older than she was. Inside were kola nuts, the real ones, four of them, which cost me more than I will admit and which I had carried home wrapped in a sock like contraband. In my village you did not come to ask a serious question empty-handed. The hands had to be occupied so the mouth could be honest. I set the tin on the returns desk while she was stamping something, and she looked at it, and then at me, and I saw her do the arithmetic everyone does with an old man at three in the morning: *is this confusion, is this a scam, is this grief.*

"Sit," I said. "Please. Five minutes. I will not keep you from your — " I gestured at the cart. "Your books."

She sat. She sat the way she did everything, as if she might need to leave in a hurry, one hand still on the desk.

"My name is Eze," I said. "Eze Okonkwo-Obi, if you want all of it, but Eze is enough. I have been coming here at night for — " I counted. "Eight months. I read aloud. You have heard me."

"I didn't mean to — " she began.

"You listened," I said. "It is not a crime. In my country it is the opposite of a crime. Listening is the whole of the law."

The tin sat between us. She looked at it the way you look at a word you can read but not pronounce.

"I am going to tell you something," I said, "and then I am going to ask you something, and you can say no, and nothing will be ruined. I want you to know the door out before I close it behind us. This is how a serious question is asked."

She nodded. Her hand had found the edge of her cardigan and was worrying it, a small loom working under the desk.

"I am dying." I said it the way you set down something heavy — carefully, but without ceremony, because ceremony is for people who have not carried it far. "Not today. Not this month, probably. But the doctors have a word for my condition and my father had the same word for his, and the word turned out to be true. I am telling you this first because what I ask next only makes sense if you know it."

The library hummed around us. Somewhere a radiator ticked. Outside the tall windows the city did its three o'clock trick, when the traffic thins and the buildings seem to lean in, listening.

"I speak a language," I said. "You have heard it. It has no name in the way your languages have names, names on maps and flags. It has a name, but the name is just the word for *speaking*, because for us there was no other speech to distinguish it from. It is the language of my village, and of eleven villages near it, and it is — " and here I had to stop, because I had never said this sentence to anyone in this country, and it weighed what I expected it to weigh, which is everything — "it is mine. I am the last one. The last person alive who can wake up in the morning and think in it. When I die, it does not get sick. It does not get small. It ends. Like a lamp. Not dimmed. Ended."

Her hand had stopped working the cardigan. She was very still, the listening-still from the aisle, and I understood that this was her truest posture, that this woman had probably spent her whole life being still for other people's words and going unheard with her own. I filed that away. A teacher files things away.

"There is a university," I said, "that recorded me once. They put me in a room with a microphone and a list of words. *Say tree. Say rain. Say grandmother.* Like a parrot. Like a man naming objects for customs officers. They meant well. Meaning well is what this country does instead of understanding. And I gave them their tape, and the tape is in a vault, and the language is not in the vault. The language is not in *words*. The language is in two people. In the answering. In the joke that needs no explaining. In the silence between a question and its answer, which in my language has its own grammar, did you know that? Our silences are grammatical. Our pauses have tenses." I laughed, and it turned into a cough, and I let the cough finish because hiding it would have been its own lie. "A language with one speaker is not a language. It is a memory with a pulse."

The tin. I pushed it an inch toward her.

"I am asking you to learn it."

She did not move. I had expected — I don't know what I had expected. Tears? No. She was not a tears-at-first person; I could see that already. She was a still-water person, and still water is deeper and colder and holds more.

"Why me?" she said. Her voice came out smaller than her face.

"Because you listened," I said. "Because your mouth tried the word without your permission. Because when you stop speaking mid-sentence, you look like a woman carrying something too heavy, and I think you have been carrying it a long time, and I think a language might be — " I searched, and found it, and gave it to her straight, because she deserved straight — "I think a language might be lighter when it is shared. That is not why you should say yes. But it is why I asked *you*."

"I'm not — " She stopped. Swallowed. The coat on the hook. "I'm not from anywhere. I mean, I'm from here. I don't have — I'm not the kind of person who inherits things. Languages, traditions. That's for people with — roots." She said the word like it was a debt someone else owed.

"Roots," I said, "are just the part of the plant that kept quiet. You have roots. Everyone does. Some people's roots are simply — unwatered." I opened the tin. The smell of the kola went up between us, bitter and clean, a smell that is a whole country. "Take one. You do not have to eat it. Hold it. In my village, when a serious question was asked, you held the kola while you thought, and the answer came into your hand before it came into your mouth."

She took one. She held it the way you hold a bird — too carefully, afraid of her own grip.

"I can't promise," she said. "I work nights. I have — " and here she stopped again, and I watched her decide not to say whatever came next, and I thought: *there it is, the house with the closed doors.* "I don't know if I'd be any good. At a language. I'm not good at — finishing things. Saying things. All the way through."

"Good," I said.

She looked up, startled.

"You think I want a student who is good at saying things? I have met those students. They are everywhere in this city. They say everything, all the way through, and none of it means anything. No." I leaned forward, and my chest complained, and I leaned forward anyway. "I want someone who knows what an unfinished sentence costs. Because my language is made of unfinished sentences. It is made of the thing you almost said. It has forty words for the space between people. I am not asking you to be talented. I am asking you to be *witnessed*. Do you understand the difference? A witness is not a performer. A witness is someone who was there."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere in the stacks, a book settled on a shelf, the small sigh of a building at night.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "what happens to you — to it — after you die? Do I just — keep it? Like a — " she turned the kola in her fingers — "like a thing in a wardrobe?"

I flinched. Only inside; I have had practice. But the word went through me, *wardrobe*, because there is a wardrobe in my own house, in Adaora's old room, that has been locked for nine years, and I do not know what is inside it, and I have never asked, and I suspect the answer is the same in every family: a wardrobe locked from the inside holds what someone could not say.

"After I die," I said, "you keep it the way you keep a fire. Not in a vault. In your mouth. You speak it. To yourself, if there is no one else. To the walls. The old men said a word spoken with no listener still goes somewhere." I looked at her. "You heard me reading to no one for eight months. Tell me — did the words go nowhere?"

She was quiet a long time. Long enough that I began composing my no-thanks speech, the gracious one, the one where an old man gathers his tin and his dignity and walks home through the cold.

Then she said, "I have a notebook."

I waited.

"Of — conversations. Ones I didn't finish. I write down the sentence I stopped at. I've been doing it for years. It's — " she laughed, one dry note, embarrassed — "it's a long notebook."

"Bring it," I said.

"It's private."

"Everything worth teaching is private," I said. "That is why it needs a teacher and not a book. Bring it or don't. But understand what I am offering and what I am not. I am not offering you a hobby. I am not offering you culture, the way they sell culture here, in a box with a ribbon. I am offering you the last house of words in the world, and I am offering you the key while my hands still work." I tapped my chest, where the wet blanket lay folded. "And I am telling you, honestly, as one carrier to a possible carrier: it is heavy. It will not thank you. People will ask you why, and there is no good answer, because the good answer is *because someone asked me*, and no one believes that is an answer. Being chosen is a terrible thing. Everyone wants to be chosen until they are."

The kola sat in her palm. She looked down at it as if it had grown heavier since she picked it up, which, in my experience, it had.

"I need to think," she said.

"Of course." I closed the tin. "Thinking is also part of the lesson. The first one. In my language, the word for *to think* and the word for *to listen again* are almost the same word. Just one sound different. We believed that thinking was listening to something a second time." I stood, slowly, the way standing goes now, one negotiation at a time. "I will be here tomorrow. Three o'clock. I am always here at three o'clock. The body keeps its own village time."

I had reached the end of the desk when she spoke, and I did not turn around, because I did not want to see her face while she said it, in case it was no, and in case it was yes, and both would have undone me.

"What's the word?" she said. "The one I almost said. In the aisle. That first night."

I smiled at the dark windows, at the city leaning in, at the eleven villages that are probably a road now.

"You will learn it," I said, "if you come back."

And behind me, so soft I almost lost it under the hum of the radiator, I heard her try it — one syllable, half a seed of the word, her mouth shaping the sound of my mother's country for the first time — and I walked out into the cold with my empty tin, and I did not correct her pronunciation, because there will be time.

There will be time.

There has to be time.

* * *

That morning I slept two hours, a record, and dreamed of the old men in the *ulo okwu*, and in the dream they were not talking. They were all turned toward the door, listening, and the door was open, and there was a draft coming in that smelled of kola and dishwater and rain before rain, and my father, who has been dead forty years, said to me in the language, in the true language, the one with the river in it:

*"Someone is coming. Finish your sentence."*
