## 5. The House on Adeyemi Street

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to the city and everything to me, because Tuesdays were when I re-shelved the 800s and the 800s were where I hid from people who wanted to talk about books instead of just holding them.

It was waiting in my pigeonhole at the Meridian — not the staff pigeonholes, mine, the one I'd claimed years ago behind the periodicals desk because no one else used it. A thick envelope, government cream, my name typed in that font all bureaucracies share, the one that says *we have already decided*. CERTIFIED. DO NOT BEND.

I knew before I opened it. You always know. The body knows before the mind consents to.

I carried it to the cataloging room first. That's the ritual: bad news gets read in the cataloging room, under the green lamp, where nothing is allowed to remain unordered. I sat down. I slit the envelope with the bone folder I use for mending spines. I read it twice, the way you touch a bruise twice to confirm it's real.

*Re: Property at 14 Adeyemi Street. Notice of Compulsory Acquisition and Demolition Proceedings.*

My mother's house. My mother's house, which had been sitting empty for six years like a held breath, was going to be flattened for some corridor of the new transit line, and I — *the sole registered heir of Mrs. Adaeze Okonkwo (deceased)* — was required to clear the premises within **eight weeks** of receipt of this notice.

Eight weeks. Fifty-six days. The city had done its arithmetic without consulting me, as cities do.

I did what I always do with unbearable information. I wrote it down. Not in the ledger — the ledger is for unsaid things, and this was an unsaid thing only in the sense that there was no one left to say it to. I wrote it on a catalog card, in pencil, in the old format:

**OKONKWO, ADAEZE. House. Demolished. [S.l.: s.n., forthcoming].**

Then I crossed it out, because even my grief has standards, and filed the letter in the drawer where I keep things that are technically resolved but not remotely finished.

* * *

Here is what the letter did not say, because letters never say it: that the house on Adeyemi Street still smells the way it smelled when I was nine — fried plantain and camphor and the particular dust of books nobody reads, because my mother bought books the way other people buy candles, for the light they promised rather than any light they gave. That the gate still sticks. That the mango tree my father planted the year I was born is probably still there, dropping fruit every rainy season onto a roof no one lives under, fermenting sweetly in the gutters, feeding the whole street's birds out of pure abandoned generosity.

Here is what I did not say back to the letter, because I say almost nothing back to anything: *You cannot clear a house that was never cleared. You cannot empty rooms that were full of everything we refused to put into words.*

But the city does not take counterarguments. The city takes houses.

* * *

The silence between my mother and me lasted eleven years, and like most long silences it began with a sentence — hers, unfinished, thrown at my back as I walked out of the kitchen with my acceptance letter in my hand.

"You'll end up just like—"

She never finished it. I never let her. I was twenty-two and certain that finishing sentences was a form of surrender, and I walked out into the Lagos heat with one suitcase and the magnificent, catastrophic conviction that I would never need anything from that house again.

What was the fight about? People ask, sometimes, when they're being brave or drunk. And the honest answer is: nothing. Everything. The usual inheritance. She wanted me to come home after university; I wanted to become someone who couldn't be called home. She said I was abandoning her the way my father had abandoned us — by dying, which was unfair of him, but she'd never forgiven him for it and needed somewhere to put the unforgiveness, and I was the only address left. I said — and I remember this with the awful clarity of things you'd give a year of your life to unsay — I said, "At least he didn't stay just to make everyone miserable."

Eleven years. We exchanged cards at Christmas, unsigned beyond our names, each word chosen like a hostage exchange. She got sick quietly, the way she did everything, and by the time I heard about it — through a cousin, through a cousin's wife, through the great slow telegraph of family — she was already past the point where visitors were permitted, and then past the point where visitors mattered, and then simply past.

I did not make it back in time. I have made my peace with many things since, or built structures that resemble peace the way a catalog card resembles a book. That one I have not made peace with. I have only shelved it.

And now the city wanted the shelving gone too.

* * *

Eze arrived at 3 a.m. that night, as he had begun to, punctual as tide. He found me at the returns desk not doing returns. He is the only person alive who can tell the difference between me working and me standing very still inside myself, and he doesn't comment; he just sits down across from me and waits, the way he waits during lessons, letting silence do the heavy lifting until I'm ready to lift my half.

"Bad mail," he said finally. It wasn't a question. He'd seen the envelope's ghost in my posture, presumably, or in the way I'd alphabetized the same shelf of biographies three times.

"A demolition notice." I kept my voice level, librarian-flat. "My mother's house. They're knocking it down for a train line."

"Ah." Eze folded his hands. In the lamplight his knuckles looked like a topography map of somewhere old. "And you must go and empty it."

"Clear it. Eight weeks. Legally it's mine, so legally the mess is mine."

"And you have not been inside since—"

"Since I left." Eleven years, then six more while the house sat vacant under a caretaker arrangement that mostly consisted of the neighbor collecting the mail and lying to both of us that everything was fine. "There will be things in it. Her things. Our things. Things I don't—" I stopped. Recataloged. "It's a lot of boxes."

Eze nodded slowly. Then he said something in his language — the language, the last of it, the whole surviving vocabulary of a people walking around in one aging skull — and when I shook my head, he translated.

"It means: *a house remembers louder than the people who left it.*" He smiled faintly. "We have a proverb for nearly everything. It is how you know a language loved its speakers. It tried to carry them."

"The language I grew up speaking," I said, "has a word for 'demolition' but none for 'I should have visited.'"

"Every language has holes exactly the size of its speakers' cowardice," Eze said. "Yours too. Mine too. Mine most of all." And there was something in his face when he said it — a door opening onto a lit room and closing again fast — that I noted the way I note misfiled books: to be dealt with later, or never.

He stayed until four, reading aloud in the low sing-song that had first pulled me out of my routine weeks ago, and I listened, and for one hour the house on Adeyemi Street was just a set of coordinates, not a wound with a roof on it.

* * *

The dreams started that week.

Not nightmares, exactly. Nightmares at least have the decency to be dramatic. These were administrative dreams: I stood in my mother's kitchen making lists of objects, and every object I wrote down turned into a word I'd never said to her, and the list grew faster than I could write, and the demolition crew arrived wearing the faces of my colleagues, polite and apologetic, holding clipboards, saying *ma'am, we really do need you to finish clearing the premises*, and I woke with my heart going like a bird in a shut room.

On Thursday I caught myself opening the ledger.

The ledger — my private shame-file, the record of conversations abandoned mid-sentence, maintained for eleven years with the devotion other people give to diaries or debts. I hadn't touched it since Eze's lessons began. That was the strange grace of those nights among the stacks: the ledger gathering dust on my shelf like a book I'd finally outgrown. But the letter had reached into me and found the old wiring, and there I was at 2 a.m., flipping pages.

*March 14.* The phone call where she asked if I was eating and I said "you don't need to monitor me" and hung up before she could answer.
*August 2.* The Christmas card I wrote and rewrote and never sent, because every draft sounded either like surrender or like a knife.
*June 9.* The day I learned she was sick, and stood in the staff stairwell for forty minutes composing the sentence *I'm coming home*, and never dialed.

Page after page. Year after year. An entire archive of a relationship conducted entirely in the negative space around two stubborn women.

I closed it. I sat in the dark of the cataloging room and understood, with the cold clarity the night shift grants, that the house on Adeyemi Street was not full of furniture.

It was full of these. Every unsent draft had a physical address. Every aborted sentence had been living rent-free in that house for seventeen years, and the city, with its clipboards and its cream envelopes, was about to bulldoze them all into landfill without anyone ever having read them aloud.

* * *

I told Eze on Sunday, because lessons had become the one appointment my life orbited, and because I was learning — slowly, the way you learn a language — that some things cannot be carried alone in one skull.

We sat in the Africa section, which he had declared "ironic and therefore correct," and I laid it out flatly: the notice, the deadline, the logistics. Eight weeks. Boxes. A skip outside a house I hadn't entered since I was twenty-two.

"I could hire a clearance company," I said. "People who do this professionally. They box everything, I sign nothing emotional, it goes to storage or the tip, and I never have to—"

"To feel it," Eze said gently.

"To function," I corrected, and he made the small sound he makes when he disagrees with a student but is too courteous to say so outright, a sound like a page turning.

"In my language," he said, "when someone dies, the family does not clear the house for a full year. A year and a day. There is a word for this period — *ulo nchekwa*, the guarded house. The house stands guard over the mourners as much as they stand guard over it. You go in. You sit in it. You let it show you what it kept." He looked at me with those eyes that had already outlived everyone they were trained on. "Your eight weeks is not enough time to avoid it properly. So perhaps — forgive an old man's arithmetic — it is exactly enough time to do it honestly."

"I don't know how to do it honestly," I said. "Honesty with her was always—" I gestured vaguely at the ledger in my bag. "We were fluent in everything except each other."

"Then you will learn," Eze said simply. "You are, I am told, a good student."

The joke landed soft and true, and I laughed — a short, cracked thing, but a laugh — and he pretended not to study my face while I did it, which was his own form of courtesy.

Before he left that morning, he paused at the end of the stacks. "When you go to the house," he said, not turning around, "do not begin with the rooms you fear. Begin with the wardrobe."

I went cold. "Why the wardrobe?"

"Because in every house I have ever emptied," he said, "the wardrobe is where the family keeps what it could not throw away and could not look at. Yours will be no different. It is a human universal. Like goodbye." And he raised one hand — farewell in miniature, the small version, the daily one — and was gone into the sleeping stacks.

* * *

That night I took down the ledger one more time. Not to write in it. To count.

Fifty-six days until the house comes down. Forty-one entries in eleven years. One mother, unvisited, unmourned properly, unspoken-to past the reach of any apology. One wardrobe, somewhere on Adeyemi Street, locked or unlocked, patient as archives are, keeping whatever it keeps.

I am a night librarian. I catalog silences. I thought I had inventoried all of mine.

Tomorrow I request leave. Tomorrow I buy boxes and tape and the thickest gloves they sell. Tomorrow I get on a bus going south, toward a street I can still navigate with my eyes shut, toward a gate that sticks, toward a house that has been standing guard over me for seventeen years whether I consented or not.

And tomorrow, or the day after, when I can't put it off any longer — I'm going to open the wardrobe.

Eze says every family keeps what it cannot throw away and cannot look at.

I already know, the way you know a certified letter before you open it, that my mother kept mine.
