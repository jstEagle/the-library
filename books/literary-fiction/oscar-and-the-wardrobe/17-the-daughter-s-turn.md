## 17. The Daughter's Turn

My father taught me to count before he taught me to pray. *Otu, abụọ, atọ* — one, two, three — his fingers folding down against mine like a man closing a small door. I was four. I remember the light in the kitchen of the house in Enugu, the way it came through the mosquito netting and laid stripes across his knuckles.

I don't remember when I stopped answering him in his language. That's the thing about refusals. They happen once, loudly or quietly, and then they repeat themselves for decades without your permission.

Thirty years. That was the arithmetic I carried into the hospital corridor that Tuesday, along with a paper cup of vending-machine coffee I had no intention of drinking. Thirty years since the argument — if you could call it that; an argument requires two people speaking — since I told him I would not sit through another evening of stories in a tongue I'd spent my whole childhood pretending not to understand. I'd wanted English. I'd wanted to be a girl who belonged to the future, to Lagos, to the university, to a self that did not have to be translated for anyone.

He had said, in English, very calmly: "Then you will learn it from strangers."

I thought he meant it as a curse. It took me thirty years to hear it as a prophecy.

* * *

The nurses knew me by then. The tall one with the reading glasses — Sister Nkechi, though she insisted on just Nkechi — nodded me through without making me sign the visitor log twice. My father had been in Ward B for eleven days. Eleven days of monitors and IV lines and a man who had spent his whole life being the last speaker of anything learning what it was to be voiceless in the ordinary way, the way we all end: tubes, beeping, other people deciding when you eat.

I went because duty is a habit older than love. I went the way you visit a grave while its occupant is still breathing.

But this time, when I pushed open the door, his bed was empty. Made. Corners tucked. A card on the rail: *Transferred — Radiology.*

I stood there with my useless coffee and felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when grief arrives early by mistake. And that's when I saw the bag.

It was Odile's. I recognized it from the library — a canvas tote, faded navy, always overstuffed, always threatening to shed its contents across some public floor. She must have left it in her scramble out of the room the day my father collapsed mid-sentence. I remembered that afternoon in fragments: the lesson going on behind the curtain, my father's voice rising and falling like weather, then the hard silence, the alarms, the young woman with the night-shift pallor pressed against the wall while they worked on him, clutching nothing, holding nothing, holding everything.

The bag sat on the visitor chair where she'd abandoned it. I should have left it. I want to be honest about this, even now, especially now: I picked it up intending only to carry it to the nurses' station. *Found property,* I would say. *Belongs to the librarian.* Duty again. Habit again.

But the recorder was on top, half out of the front pocket, and the little red light was still on.

Eleven days of battery. She'd never turned it off.

* * *

I want to explain what those recordings were, because "recordings" is too clean a word. They were conversations. They were a man and a woman in a hospital room and later, I gathered, in a library among the stacks, doing something that had no name in either of their languages: passing a whole world between them, hand to hand, before the hands failed.

The first file was dated three weeks before the collapse. My father's voice came through the small speaker thin and papery, but unmistakable — I would have known it underwater, through concrete, in the next life. He was telling a story. Not any story. *The* story. The tortoise and the drum that would not stop beating, the one he used to tell on Sunday evenings when I was small, the one I walked out of the room during when I was seventeen, the one whose ending I had deliberately, proudly, never heard.

And there was Odile's voice, low, asking questions. Not the flat questions of an interviewer. Real ones. The kind that come from actually listening.

I stood in the empty hospital room with a dead man's bed and a living man's voice and I did not move for forty minutes. My coffee went cold in my hand. Somewhere down the corridor a trolley rattled past and someone laughed at something and none of it touched me. I was seven years old again, sitting cross-legged on cool tile, listening to my father be magnificent, before I learned that magnificence could feel like pressure, like debt, like a language you owe.

Because that's what I had decided, somewhere in adolescence, that his stories were: debts. Every proverb a bill. Every moral a finger pointed at the daughter who wasn't grateful enough, rooted enough, obedient enough. I heard correction where he meant inheritance. I heard walls where he'd built doors.

Or — no. Let me be more honest than that. Some of it *was* correction. He was not an easy man. He believed things firmly and said them firmly and expected the world to arrange itself accordingly. When I announced I was moving to Abuja, he didn't argue; he simply began speaking to me only in his language, as if English were a phase I'd grow out of, as if my life were a fever. That was cruelty, or something adjacent to it. I matched it. I stopped calling. I stopped coming. I built my thirty years of silence brick by brick and called it independence.

But standing there, listening, I heard something I had never let myself hear: he wasn't performing for the recording. He wasn't teaching at me. He was talking to Odile the way he used to talk to my mother — easily, sideways, laughing at his own jokes before the punchline arrived, trusting the listener to keep up. The language wasn't a wall in his mouth. It was a room, and he was letting someone in, and the someone wasn't me, because I had locked the door from my side years ago and thrown away the key and called the lock freedom.

The tortoise story ended. In the recording, Odile laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her — and my father said something I couldn't follow, all consonants and music, and then translated it himself, softly:

"It means: the story waits better than the child does."

I sat down on the edge of the made bed, which you're not supposed to do, and I played the next file. And the next. I listened to my father teach the names of rains — there are eleven, I learned, eleven words for rain, and I had known none of them — and I listened to a stranger answer him correctly, and something in my chest that had been clenched since 1994 began, very slowly, without my permission, to unclench.

* * *

Nkechi found me there at some point. She looked at the recorder, at my face, and had the grace to pretend she hadn't. "Radiology," she said. "He'll be back within the hour. They're just doing scans."

"Is he—" I couldn't finish. I still can't always finish sentences. It runs in the family, apparently.

"He's stubborn," she said. "It's the only thing keeping him alive." She paused at the door. "The librarian comes every day. Even after visiting hours. She sits with him and they talk in that language and honestly, Mr. Eze's numbers are better on those days. I've started charting it."

She left. I stayed. When they wheeled him back in, small and sunken in the big bed, his eyes found mine first and something flickered across his face — surprise, then caution, then the careful blankness of a man preparing for another refusal. Thirty years of my refusals had trained us both.

"Bia," I said.

Come.

One word. The first word of his language I had spoken aloud to him since I was a teenager, and my accent was terrible, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, and his face did something complicated — the blankness breaking apart like ice in spring — and he reached for my hand and held it the way he used to when I was small, fingers folding down, closing a small door that apparently had never been locked from his side at all.

We didn't talk much. We couldn't, really — he tired fast, and I had forgotten almost everything, and neither of us knew how to begin in English after so long. But I stayed until the lights dimmed. And when I left, I took the recorder with me. I told myself I'd return it to Odile. I told myself a lot of things that week.

I listened to all forty-one files over the next six nights, sitting in my apartment in Abuja, which I had extended my stay from, which I kept meaning to go back to. I listened to my father tell the story of my mother — how they met, how she laughed, how she died — in the language, translating haltingly for Odile, and I learned that he had loved her with a specificity I had never witnessed, that grief had a grammar, that he had been carrying it alone in a language with no other speakers, which is a loneliness I cannot type without stopping.

On the seventh night I called my office and requested another month of leave. On the eighth night I bought a notebook. Plain, black, unremarkable. I wrote on the first page, in English, because I didn't yet know how to write it otherwise: *What I refused, and why, and what it cost.*

Underneath, I started a list. It was going to be long.

* * *

Odile was at the library, of course. Where else. I found her at the reference desk at nine in the morning, looking like a woman who had slept in a chair, which I suspected she had. Up close she was younger than I'd assumed and more worn, both at once — a person assembled from long nights and careful silences. I had resented her, in the hospital, in the abstract way you resent whoever is standing where you should have been standing. Now, having heard forty-one files, I resented her less than I envied her, and I envied her less than I needed her.

She saw me and stood up too quickly, knocking a pen off the desk. "You're Adaora," she said. "Mr. Eze's—"

"Daughter. Yes." I set the recorder on the desk between us. "You left this."

Her hand went to her bag, then to her mouth. "Eleven days," she whispered. "It's been running for eleven days. Oh God, the battery, the files—"

"They're fine. All of them. I listened to them."

Something moved through her face — alarm, guilt, the reflexive flinch of someone caught keeping a diary. "I recorded the lessons for the archive," she said carefully. "For documentation. I should have asked—"

"You recorded my father telling the tortoise story," I said. "The one I walked out on when I was seventeen. You recorded him naming the rains. You recorded him talking about my mother." I kept my voice level. It was costing me. "So we can skip the part where you apologize for eavesdropping on a conversation that was never offered to me."

"I'm sorry," she said anyway, which I later learned was characteristic. She apologized the way other people breathed.

I looked at her. She looked back. Between us, on the desk, the recorder sat with its dead red light, holding everything.

Here is what I had planned to say, in the taxi on the way over, rehearsing like a schoolgirl: that I wanted copies of the files. That the archive belonged to family. That I would be reviewing whatever material the university collected, that I had legal standing, that I would not be sidelined the way I'd been sidelined my whole life. I had a whole speech. It was good. It was firm. It was exactly the kind of speech that builds another thirty years of wall.

What came out instead was:

"Teach me."

Odile blinked. "Teach you—"

"The language." My voice did the cracking thing again. I let it. "I understood maybe a tenth of what he said in those recordings. Maybe less. He is the last person alive who speaks it and I am his daughter and I know ten percent, and the ten percent I know I've been pretending not to know since I was fifteen years old because knowing it felt like losing." I put my hand flat on the desk to steady myself. "He has maybe months. Maybe weeks. Everyone keeps saying it in hallways like I can't hear. So teach me. Whatever you know. Start tonight, start now, start badly — I don't care. I am done refusing."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she pulled out the chair beside her — not the chair across the desk, the chair *beside*, and I understood that she had already understood everything — and she said:

"Sit down."

I sat down.

"My father used to say," she began, and then stopped, and corrected herself, and I watched her decide to give me the thing straight, no cushioning, because cushioning was a form of lying and she seemed constitutionally incapable of it: "Eze says the language doesn't live in vocabulary. It lives in confession. The first thing he ever taught me wasn't a word. It was a question I had to answer out loud, truthfully, in front of him." She slid a notebook toward me — hers, the old one, soft-cornered, water-stained. "So. Before we start. Before any of it."

She opened the notebook to a page near the beginning. At the top, in careful handwriting, was a single line:

*What have you left unsaid?*

I read it three times. Outside, the city went on with its ordinary noise — buses, horns, a street vendor calling out prices — and inside the library it was very quiet, the particular quiet of a place that keeps silences on purpose, catalogued and shelved.

I picked up the pen.

And I began to write.
