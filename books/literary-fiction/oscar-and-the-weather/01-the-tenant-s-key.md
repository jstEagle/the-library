## 1. The Tenant's Key

The key was heavier than Wren remembered keys being.

Not the brass itself—though the janitor's ring was a fat, jangling fist of brass, seven keys on a carabiner that clinked against their hip like a cheap metronome—but the weight of what the keys opened. Wren had held a key to Meridian Conservatory once before, at fourteen, a slim silver thing on a ribbon their mother had tied in a bow, and it had opened exactly one door: Practice Room 4, where they were permitted to prepare for the debut that would, everyone said, change everything. One door, and the whole building had felt like it was holding its breath for them.

Now Wren had seven keys and the building didn't notice them at all.

The lanyard said FACILITIES in block capitals, laminated, the photo taken on a bad day in a fluorescent office—Wren squinting, chin down, hair uncombed in a way that was less disheveled than deliberately erased. The woman at the security desk had printed it without looking up. She hadn't recognized them. Nobody had recognized them, and Wren had come to understand that this was not a failure of memory on the world's part but a success of effort on their own: the haircut, the fifteen pounds gained and lost and gained again, the way they had trained their face into a kind of administrative blankness, a face that belonged in a background check and not on a poster.

"First night?" asked the woman at the desk—Doreen, according to the tape on her monitor.

"First night," Wren said.

"Mr. Achterberg does the walkthrough at eleven. Don't be on the stage floor when he comes through, he hates wet floors." She slid a clipboard across. "Sign in, sign out. Radio's on the charger if you need it, but you won't. Nothing happens here at night except dust."

Nothing happens here at night except dust. Wren signed their name—*W. Sandoval*, the initial doing its small work of disguise—and took the clipboard, and the keys, and the mop, and went into the dark of the building that had made them and unmade them and, in its indifference, was now offering them a kind of shelter they hadn't known they were looking for.

* * *

The Conservatory at night was a different building wearing the same bones.

By day—Wren remembered this with the terrible fidelity of the body rather than the mind—the place was all motion and gloss: students streaming through the atrium with instrument cases held like shields, the smell of rosin and burnt coffee, the grand staircase loud with footsteps and argument and laughter, someone always practicing something too fast somewhere, the whole structure humming like the inside of a piano. By day it had been a weather system and Wren had been a storm front moving through it, fourteen years old, hands sweating, mother's voice in their ear saying *breathe from the floor, Oscar, breathe from the floor*.

By night it was a held note decaying. The emergency lights turned the corridors the color of weak tea. Radiators ticked and knocked in the walls like something alive and patient. The marble of the atrium floor, which by day gleamed, by night just looked cold, and Wren's sneakers squeaked on it in a way that felt obscene, a small profanity committed against the acoustics. They knew, without deciding to know it, that if they stood in the northwest corner of the atrium and hummed a middle C, the stairwell would return it a half-step flat. They knew that the third stair from the bottom had a loose brass strip that sang when you stepped on it. They knew the building's body the way you know a lover's, or a hometown's, or your own mother's—the knowledge arrived before the thought did.

That was the shame of it, Wren thought, pushing the mop bucket through Hall C. Not that they were here with a mop. That was only money, only rent, only the ordinary indignity of a body that needed feeding. The shame was that the knowing had never left. Ten years of silence—ten years since the last recital, the last review, the last time anyone had asked them to play anything—and still their feet found the corridors without consulting them. Still their hands, pushing the mop in long figure-eights across the stage floor of Bradley Hall, fell into the shape of a phrase. The body kept the score. The body had never agreed to the retirement.

* * *

Bradley Hall was the last room on the route, and Wren saved it for last the way you save the letter you're afraid to open, or the way you eat around the food you actually want. It was the big room. Fourteen hundred seats, the acoustics tuned by a German firm whose name Wren had once been made to memorize for an interview, the stage a shallow arc of pale wood that had been refinished twice since Wren's debut and still, they were almost certain, held the ghost of a scratch they had put in it at fifteen, dragging a chair, and been terrified about for a week.

They stood at the back of the house for a long moment, mop dripping into the bucket, and let the room have them.

It was strange how a room could be a witness. This room had heard them at fourteen, in a grey dress their mother had bought at a thrift store and pressed until it shone, playing *Vespers in Grey* for the first time in public, the piece that would go everywhere they went for the rest of their life, printed on programs, printed on reviews, printed on the small tin of fame that had been opened for them like a can of something preserved. This room had heard them at sixteen, at nineteen, at twenty-two. This room had heard the last time, though Wren had never told anyone that was the last time; they had simply walked off the stage and kept walking, out of the green room, out of the contract, out of the weather of their own name.

The stage lights were off. The house lights were off. Only the exit signs burned green above the doors, and in that light the fourteen hundred seats looked like a congregation that had been turned to stone mid-breath.

Wren wheeled the bucket up the ramp, through the stage-left door, onto the boards.

The floor was worse than the corridors—salt residue from winter boots tracked in from the loading dock, a scatter of rosin dust, one long smear of something sticky near the piano mark that they chose not to investigate too closely. They wet the mop. They began at the downstage edge, working back, the way the training video in the HR office had shown, and the motion was simple, and the motion was rhythmic, and somewhere in the third row of seats' worth of mopping the rhythm betrayed them.

Because the figure-eight of a mop, done properly, done with the wrists loose and the weight low, is not so different from a certain kind of passagework. And the floor of Bradley Hall has a resonance—you can feel it through your shoes, a low sympathetic hum when the building's heating system cycles—and Wren's hands, on the mop, began to count. Four in a bar. The opening of *Vespers in Grey*, which is not fast, which is in fact almost nothing, four quiet chords like someone testing whether the air will let them speak, and then the melody comes in over the top like a figure walking out into weather.

They stopped.

They stood on the stage of Bradley Hall with a mop in their hands and their heart going hard and stupid, and they made themselves look at the piano mark on the floor—the pale rectangle where the concert grand sat, the ghost-piano, the absence with a shape—and they said, out loud, to nobody, in a voice they barely recognized as their own:

"You're a janitor."

It came out wrong. Not bitter, exactly. More like a man reading his own name off a list he hadn't known he was on.

They finished the stage. They did the wings. They wiped the rail of the stage-left staircase where someone had left a palm print in dust, and the palm print was small, a student's, and Wren found themselves wondering what the student had been nervous about, what was coming for them, what the building was going to make of them or they of it. Then they killed the work lights and stood a moment in the green dark of the exit signs, and the room held its silence around them like water, and they pushed the bucket out and let the door swing shut behind them with its soft pneumatic sigh, the sound of a mouth closing on something it had decided not to say.

* * *

At 2:40 in the morning, on the corridor outside Practice Room 4, Wren's keys sang.

They had been given the full ring, all seven keys, and the seventh one was old, older than the others, a key worn smooth on its teeth, and when Wren tried it in the door of Practice Room 4 it turned with the ease of a thing that had been waiting. Of course it fit. Wren had known it would fit. Somewhere under the laminated lanyard and the bad photograph and the ten years, the building had checked their credentials and found them, regrettably, still valid.

They didn't go in. That was the discipline, and they were proud of the discipline the way a starving man is proud of not eating. They stood in the corridor with the door unlocked and the smell of the room coming out to meet them—old felt and lemon oil and the faint mineral breath of the radiator—and they looked at the piano in the dark, the upright against the wall, and they did not play it.

Instead they locked the door again, and the click of the lock was very small and very final, and they walked on down the corridor with the keys jangling against their leg, and the sound followed them the way a name follows you when someone has just remembered it.

* * *

The break room was in the basement, a windowless cell with a table, four chairs, a microwave that smelled of every meal it had ever committed, and a corkboard papered with memos in three fonts. Wren signed out at 3:15. The night supervisor, a broad, gray-haired man named Achterberg who did indeed hate wet floors and had said so twice during his eleven o'clock walkthrough, was at the table with coffee, doing a crossword.

"Sandoval," he said, reading the sign-out sheet. "How'd it go?"

"Fine," Wren said.

"Halls done?"

"Halls done. Bradley done. Practice wing done."

Achterberg looked up. He had the face of a man who had spent thirty years in buildings and had learned to read them better than people. "Bradley's a big room," he said. "First nighters either love it or it spooks them."

"It's just a room," Wren said.

"Sure." He went back to his crossword. Then, without looking up, in the tone of a man mentioning the weather: "Piano's out of tune in the practice wing. Number 4 especially. Been meaning to put in a work order. Nobody plays the things at night anyway."

Wren stood with their hand on the door. The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere above them, through three floors of marble and plaster and old wood, the building ticked and settled, holding all its rooms like held breaths.

"Nobody plays them," Wren agreed.

They rode the elevator up in silence, and walked out through the loading dock into the cold, and the night air hit their face like a clean page. The city was doing its 3 a.m. impression of emptiness. Down the block, the 42 bus sighed at its stop, lit up and vacant, the old route, the one that ran out toward the neighborhood where Wren had grown up, where the houses had been demolished one by one through their childhood like a sentence being erased from the end backward. The bus pulled away without them, its lights sliding along the wet street, and Wren stood on the curb with the janitor's keys in their pocket and their mother's voice somewhere under their sternum, saying *breathe from the floor*, and they did not know yet that the letter was coming, that Halvard was dying, that the name Oscar was waiting in a stranger's mouth like a coin under a cup.

They only knew that the building had let them in, and let them out, and had asked for nothing in return except the floors.

They walked home the long way, not because they wanted to, but because the 42 was gone, and because some part of them—the part that still counted in fours, the part that still knew which stair sang—was not ready to stop walking yet.
