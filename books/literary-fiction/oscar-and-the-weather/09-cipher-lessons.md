## 9. Cipher Lessons

The winter Wren was eight, the radiators in the Oscar house sang.

Not hummed—sang. A thin, metallic keening that rose and fell with the boiler's moods, and their mother claimed she could transcribe it if she ever found a clef strange enough to hold it. She said this while peeling apples at the sink, her back to them, her hands moving in that absent rhythm she had, thumb against paring knife, four beats to a strip of skin that never broke. Wren sat at the kitchen table with a pencil they were supposed to be using for arithmetic and instead drew the radiator's song as a jagged line across the page, up where it shrieked, down where it sighed, and when Ida turned around and saw it she went very still, the way she did sometimes, as though the room had spoken a word from a language she'd been trying to remember.

"That's it," she said. "That's exactly it."

Wren didn't know what *it* was. They wouldn't know for years. But that was the winter the cipher began, and later—much later, standing in a dead man's apartment holding a letter no one else could read—Wren would trace everything back to the singing radiators, to the apples, to the particular grey light of February coming through a window that needed washing.

* * *

The kitchen table was oak, scarred, older than both of them. It had a burn mark shaped like a small country no one had ever named, and Ida used to set her teacup directly on it, always in the same place, so that over the years a ring formed around the burn like a moat. On cold afternoons the table held: two cups, one sugar bowl with a chipped lid, a loaf of whatever bread Tomas hadn't finished, and—the important thing—a radio.

The radio was a brown plastic box with a dial worn smooth by Ida's thumb. At five o'clock every evening she turned it on for the weather report, not because she cared about the weather, or not only because of that, but because the weatherman's voice was the same voice every day, patient and unbothered, and Ida said a person could set their soul by a voice like that. Wren thought this was a strange thing to say. Wren thought most things their mother said were strange, in the way that foreign coins are strange—obviously valuable, obviously not spendable anywhere nearby.

On the evening the cipher was born, the weatherman said *flurries overnight, accumulation unlikely*, and Ida laughed.

"What?" Wren asked.

"Accumulation unlikely." She said it again, tasting it. "It sounds like an apology. Like he's sorry about something bigger than snow."

And then, without any transition Wren could detect, she pulled a sheet of paper from the drawer where she kept the good pencils and the takeout menus and the photograph of a woman neither child had ever met, and she wrote:

*F = flurries.*

"What's that?"

"A code," she said. "Every family should have one."

"Why?"

"Because some things are only true if you say them sideways." She tapped the paper. "Watch. If F is flurries, then—" she wrote *F.A.U.* beneath it, and looked at Wren expectantly, the way she looked at them when she was teaching without admitting she was teaching, which was the only way she taught anything.

"Flurries... A is..." Wren squinted. "Accumulation. U is unlikely."

"Flurries, accumulation unlikely," Ida said, pleased as a cat. "You've just read a secret. Now nobody knows we're talking about snow."

"But why would we need to hide snow?"

"We don't." She took a sip of tea, and over the rim of the cup her eyes were bright and somewhere else. "We hide the snow so we can talk about other things."

* * *

The rules assembled themselves over the following weeks, one at a time, the way a house assembles itself out of habits.

Rule one: letters became words. Each letter of the alphabet was assigned a weather term, and the terms came from the radio, from Ida's own vocabulary, from books she read aloud while cooking. A was *accumulation*. B was *barometer*. C was *cloud cover*, D was *drizzle*, E was *east wind*. Some letters got terms Ida invented outright—J was *jacket weather, bring the good scarf*, which was too long for a code but too beloved to shorten, and so whenever a J appeared in a message it took up half the line like a guest who brought luggage.

Rule two: the words could be rearranged but never replaced. You couldn't swap in your own weather; the code lived inside its fixed vocabulary the way a song lives inside its notes. This was Ida's rule and she was firm about it. "A code isn't hiding," she said. "A code is *carrying*. You have to carry the message in something sturdy."

Rule three—and Wren would remember this rule on their deathbed, if they were granted one, would remember it the way you remember the shape of your own hand—rule three was that certain sentences were never written in the cipher. When Wren asked why, Ida was drying dishes, and she didn't turn around.

"Some sentences want to be said plainly," she said. "If you put them in the code, you're giving yourself permission to unsay them later. And some things shouldn't be unsayable."

"Like what?"

She set down the dish. Turned. Her hair was coming loose from its clip and there was flour on her wrist and she looked at Wren for a long moment with an expression Wren had no tools yet to read, an expression that contained, folded inside it like a letter inside a letter, thirty years of future.

"I love you," she said plainly. "See? No code. Now that can't ever be taken back."

Then she flicked suds at them and the moment dissolved into shrieking and wet dish towels, and Wren forgot the weight of it and kept only the warmth, which is what children do, which is perhaps what childhood *is*: the great filtering process by which everything heavy is removed and only the light remains, until one day the heavy things come due.

* * *

By March the cipher had a name. Ida called it *the sky language*, and insisted it be spoken of only in the sky language itself, which made conversations about the code into puzzles about the code, recursive and ridiculous, and Wren loved it with the whole uncomplicated ferocity of an eight-year-old who has been let in on something.

They practiced at the kitchen table after dinner, once Tomas had gone upstairs to his room with his door shut, which was his whole personality even then. Ida would write a sentence in weather and slide it across the scarred oak, and Wren would decode it with a pencil stub, tongue between teeth.

*Cloud cover, drizzle, east wind, barometer.*

C-D-E-B. Wren frowned. "Cloud drizzle east... no. Wait." Rearranging was allowed. "Deb? Is this about Aunt Deb? We don't have an Aunt Deb."

Ida covered her mouth. Her shoulders shook. "Read it again, *oscar*."

Oscar. That was the other thing. The name.

Their surname was Sandoval—it was on the mailboxes, the school forms, the church register—but Ida had grown up an Oscar, and somewhere in Wren's eighth year she began using it as the cipher's signature, the way a painter signs a corner. Every coded note ended with a small O drawn like a sun half-risen over a horizon line. And when she wanted to say something especially true, especially hers, she called Wren *Oscar* instead of Wren, and the name settled over them like a coat cut exactly to their size.

"It means the sky belongs to us," she tried to explain once. "Sandoval is the name the world uses. Oscar is the name the weather uses."

"Which one am I?"

"Both, my love. That's the trick of it. You get to choose which one you're standing under."

Wren didn't understand then. Wren understood it perfectly, later, standing in a funeral reception while a stranger in a green coat said *the Oscars* and the floor tilted. But that was far off. That spring the name was just a warm thing, a secret pocket sewn into an ordinary day.

* * *

April brought rain, real rain, days of it, drumming on the gutters, and with it the game entered its golden age. Ida began leaving coded messages in places Wren would find them: tucked into the arithmetic workbook (*fog, accumulation, jacket weather, bring the good scarf* — F-A-J-B-G-S — and Wren decoded it at school and had to put their head down on the desk because laughing that hard was disruptive); written in steam on the bathroom mirror, already fading; folded into the pocket of Wren's coat where the bus fare should have been.

The messages were never important. That was the beauty of them. *Buy milk* rendered in cloud cover and drizzle. *Your brother is driving me mad* translated into a front moving in from the west. Once, memorably, an entire fake forecast Ida composed herself and read aloud at breakfast in her best weatherman voice, deadpan, while Tomas stared at her and Wren choked on toast: *"Overnight lows in the heart region, warming trend expected by Thursday, scattered forgiveness throughout the weekend."*

Tomas didn't know the code. This was never stated as a rule but was understood absolutely, the way the border between two countries is understood by everyone living near it. Tomas was eleven and already building his own kind of shelter—grades, rules, the approval of teachers—and the sky language wasn't a shelter, it was the opposite, it was going outside in bad weather on purpose because the bad weather was beautiful. Ida never excluded him formally. She just never handed him the first sheet of paper. And he never asked. Wren understood, much later, that this mutual silence was its own kind of mercy, or its own kind of wound, depending on which of them you asked, and depending on the year.

Some nights, though, the game changed temperature.

Some nights Ida would finish a message and not slide it across. She'd hold it, looking at it, and the kitchen would fill with the sound of the refrigerator humming its one long note, and Wren learned to wait. Then she'd either crumple it into her apron pocket—those nights the message died unread, and Wren never asked—or she'd read it aloud herself, translating her own cipher back into plain speech, her voice doing something careful.

Once—one time only—she wrote something, looked at it a long while, and then fed it to the burner flame on the stove, holding the corner with a fork until the fire took it all, and she stood watching the paper curl and blacken and said, mostly to herself, "There. Now it's just weather."

Wren asked what it said.

"It said something true," Ida said, rinsing her teacup. "That's why it had to go. True things are heavy. You don't hand a child heavy things."

"You hand me heavy things all the time. You told me Grandma died."

Ida paused, dish towel in hand, and then she laughed—not her kitchen laugh but a smaller, stranger one—and said, "Yes. Well. Maybe I'm not as careful as I think I am." And she kissed the top of Wren's head, flour and dishwater and cigarette-adjacent smell of burnt paper, and that was the end of it, and Wren carried the question for thirty years without knowing they were carrying it.

* * *

The last lesson came in May, near the end, though nothing about it announced itself as last. Lessons never do. That's the cruelty built into tenderness: it doesn't warn you it's finite.

Ida had a new sheet of paper, and on it she'd drawn something Wren hadn't seen before: a third layer. Beneath the weather words, in tiny letters, she'd added numbers and arrows—a system for when the plain code wasn't enough. "For emergencies," she said. "Or for truths that need two locks."

"How do I learn it?"

"You don't. Not today." She folded the sheet in thirds and put it in the tin box where she kept buttons and spare keys and the photograph of the unnamed woman. "You'll know when you need it. And when you do, you'll already know it. That's how the important lessons work—they're asleep in you until the day they aren't."

"That makes no sense."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't." She ruffled Wren's hair. "Decode this instead," and slid across the table:

*East wind, accumulation, storm clearing, high pressure, overcast lifting, rain ending.*

E-A-S-H-O-R-E. Rearranged: *shore*.

"The shore," Wren said. "Are we going to the lake?"

"We are going wherever you want," Ida said, "because you just read a whole sentence in the sky, and that deserves a Saturday."

They went to the lake. Ida wore the green scarf, the good one, and stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair loose and read the actual clouds aloud like a forecast, naming them—cumulus, stratus, the ones with the Latin names she'd gotten from a library book—and Wren stood beside her and felt, with the entire surface of their skin, that this was happiness. Not the idea of it. The substance. Cold air, lake smell, mother's sleeve against their sleeve, a private alphabet ticking away in their chest like a second heartbeat.

"Will you always know the code?" Wren asked. "Even when I'm big?"

"I'll always know it," she said. "It's not in my head, oscar. It's in the weather. As long as there's weather, you can find me in it."

Wren laughed at that, the easy laugh of a child being poetic-at, and ate the sandwich she'd wrapped, and threw crumbs to gulls, and did not memorize the moment because the moment did not ask to be memorized. It simply passed through, the way weather passes through, and left everything looking the same and nothing the same.

* * *

Thirty years later, in a dead man's apartment above an empty street, Wren sat at a table that was not the oak table, with a letter that was not about milk or Saturdays, and unfolded the first page in a handwriting they knew better than their own.

The characters were weather. Of course they were weather. F and C and D and the long beloved J with its luggage. The O-signature sat at the bottom of the page, a sun half-risen over a horizon line, drawn by a hand that had been still for three decades.

Wren's pencil hovered. Their hands, which had performed for thousands, shook over a child's alphabet.

Because here was the thing they had never told anyone, the thing they had folded away so carefully all those years ago that they'd nearly lost the fold: rule three. *Some sentences are never written in the cipher.* Their mother had made one exception in her life, one plain-spoken truth, and given it to an eight-year-old over dishwater. Which meant that anything she chose to encode—anything she locked inside the sky language, anything she made unsayable-in-plain-speech and therefore permanent—was a sentence she had decided must survive her.

Wren touched the first character. Cloud cover. C.

And began, with a child's alphabet and an adult's dread, to read what their mother had decided must never be unsaid.
