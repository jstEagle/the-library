## 12. What the Conservatory Remembers

The archive of the Meridian Conservatory lived in the basement of the east wing, in a room that had once been intended as a rehearsal space and had been demoted, decades ago, when someone discovered that the acoustics made every quartet sound like an argument. Delia Marsh had presided over it for thirty-one years, first as assistant, then as keeper, and now—as the title on her door read, though no one ever came down to read it—*Curator of Special Collections*. She liked the demoted room. It was the only place in the building where nothing echoed, where sound went to die politely, and where the past sat in its acid-free boxes waiting to be asked about itself.

She arrived each morning at seven, before the students, before even the custodial staff finished their rounds, and moved through the stacks with a hand truck and a pencil—never a pen; pens were for people who believed their first thought should be permanent—and the smell of the place rose around her like weather: old paper, old glue, the faint vanilla of lignin breaking down, and beneath it all the cold stone breath of the foundation, which remembered being a cellar for a brewery before it remembered being anything cultural at all.

That October morning she was preparing for the gala. The director's office had sent down a memo on cream letterhead, the kind of memo that used the word *celebrate* four times, requesting that the archive assemble a display for the thirtieth anniversary of the premiere of *Vespers in Grey*. The piece that had made the conservatory famous. The piece that had made a sixteen-year-old famous. The piece whose composer's name was engraved on the donor wall downstairs in letters tall enough to be read from across the lobby: **WREN SANDOVAL**.

Delia had been there, thirty years ago. Not in the hall—she'd been working the coat check that night, a graduate assistant earning tuition—but she remembered the weather of the evening, which was how she remembered most things. Rain, a hard early rain, the kind that made the lobby floor shine like poured glass. She remembered the boy—young man, really, though he seemed younger in the lights—walking out in a grey suit that someone had clearly chosen for him, and she remembered thinking he looked less like a performer than like a passenger, someone being carried through his own life at speed.

He had played beautifully. Everyone said so. Delia had thought so too, though what struck her more than the playing was the silence afterward—not applause-silence but something rawer, a held breath, as if the whole room had recognized something it couldn't name. Then the applause came, enormous, and the moment was paved over, and the name went up on the wall, and the institution began telling the story of Wren Sandoval the way institutions tell stories: smoothly, repeatedly, until the smoothness itself became evidence of truth.

Now she pulled the file.

It lived in Bay C, Row 4, in a grey Hollinger box labeled in her own predecessor's handwriting: *SANDOVAL, W. — VESPERS IN GREY — PREMIERE & PROVENANCE, 1993–94.* She carried it to the reading table under the green-shaded lamp, put on her white cotton gloves out of habit more than necessity—the paper wasn't fragile so much as tired—and opened the folder.

The first item was the program. Glossy, folded in thirds, the conservatory's autumn concert series, and there on the second page: *Vespers in Grey — Wren Sandoval (b. 1977)*. World premiere. She set it aside.

The second item was the press clippings, photocopied because the originals had been loaned out in 1996 and returned, she noted with archival irritation, with coffee rings. *Prodigy's Dark, Radiant Debut.* *A Vespers for Our Time.* *Meridian's Sixteen-Year-Old Stuns the Hall.* She skimmed them. They were all the same article wearing different hats: wonder, wonder, a quote from Halvard Brandt—"the music arrived whole, as if it had been waiting"—and then more wonder.

The third item was the manuscript.

Or rather, the folder marked MANUSCRIPT, which contained—a single sheet. A description. Typed on a card:

> *Autograph fair copy, ink on 24-stave paper, 47 pp. Deposited by H. Brandt, [date illegible]. Withdrawn for conservation, [date illegible]. Returned to depositor at depositor's request.*

Delia frowned. She took off one glove to rub the bridge of her nose, which was against protocol and which she did anyway.

There was no manuscript in the file. There was a card describing a manuscript, and two dates she could not read, and a phrase that made the back of her neck prickle in the way that only long acquaintance with institutional paperwork can produce: *returned to depositor at depositor's request.*

In thirty-one years, Delia had learned that provenance was the truest thing in any archive and also the most easily bruised. Documents lied constantly—they exaggerated, they flattered, they omitted—but the *trail* of documents, the chain of custody, the accession numbers marching in order like well-behaved children: that trail usually told the truth, because no one bothered to forge the boring parts. And here the boring parts had gone missing.

She pulled the accession ledgers. These were her favorite objects in the entire collection—elephant folios bound in scuffed navy buckram, their spines cracked into white smiles, filled with decades of entries in a dozen hands. She found the year. She ran her gloved finger down the columns.

*Accession 1994-117. Musical ms., autograph, "Vespers in Grey." Donor: Prof. H. Brandt, on behalf of the composer. Received Oct. 3, 1994. Condition: good. Note: composer retains performance rights.*

And then, further down, in a different ink, slightly faded, dated fourteen months later:

*Accession 1995-042. WITHDRAWAL. Ms. acc. 1994-117 removed from collection at request of Prof. H. Brandt. Reason given: "conservation." Disposition: returned to donor. Signed: R. Ellery, Asst. Curator.*

Ruth Ellery. Dead now, God rest her, and Delia had inherited not only her desk but her silences. Ruth had been meticulous about everything except explanations. Delia turned the page, looking for a follow-up, a re-accession, anything. Nothing. The manuscript had left the building in November of 1995 and never come home.

Which meant that the most important document in the history of the conservatory's most celebrated work—the autograph score of *Vespers in Grey*, the paper on which the teenage genius's own hand should have been visible, corrections and crossings-out and the honest mess of creation—had been absent from the archive for nearly three decades, while the name on the donor wall grew taller and the story grew smoother.

Delia sat very still under the green lamp. In the ductwork above, the heating system ticked as it woke, the building clearing its throat.

* * *

She did what she always did with a question she couldn't answer: she looked for the question next door to it.

Archives were not neutral, whatever the brochures said. They were instruments, and like all instruments they had blind spots shaped by the people who played them. The Meridian archive's blind spot was generosity toward its donors. A professor who gave the institution a treasure was a professor whose paperwork was processed quickly, gratefully, and without impertinent questions. Halvard Brandt had been the most generous donor the music division had ever had—scores, letters, teaching materials, an endowment for the piano competition that bore his name. His deposits filled eleven boxes. Nobody had ever audited Halvard Brandt. You didn't audit a benefactor; you named things after him.

But Delia was sixty-three years old and had stopped being impressed by names on walls somewhere around the time her own knees started making noise on stairs. She pulled Box 7 of the Brandt papers and began, methodically, to go through it.

Correspondence, mostly. Students' thank-you notes. Programs from masterclasses. A 1989 letter from a summer festival complaining about lodging. And then, near the bottom of the third folder, a thin envelope with no accession number penciled on it—which was strange, because Ruth Ellery penciled accession numbers on everything, including, Delia once discovered, a box of actual pencils.

The envelope was addressed in a small, upright, careful hand. Not Halvard's hand—Halvard wrote in a slanting, impatient scrawl that leaned forward as if trying to reach the end of the sentence before the sentence ended. This hand stood straight up, disciplined, each letter formed completely, like a child who has been praised for neatness and never unlearned it.

*Prof. H. Brandt,* it said. No return address.

Inside was a single sheet, undated, unsigned.

> *You know what you have. I am asking you, once, kindly, to return it. If it is returned, this is the last time I will write. If it is not, I will write again, and I will not be kind twice.*
>
> *—I.O.S.*

Delia read it three times.

*I.O.S.* Three initials. Her mind, trained by decades of cataloguing, went instantly to work: I. O. S. Not S., as in Sandoval. The initials ran the wrong direction for Wren Sandoval. They ran—I. Oscar-Sandoval? She searched her memory. The mother. There had been a mother; there was always a mother behind a prodigy, mostly invisible, occasionally photographed at premieres looking like a woman standing in wind. Delia had a vague image: a slight woman, dark-skinned, quiet, holding a coat over her arm at some reception, saying almost nothing. What was her name? It had appeared in exactly one clipping Delia could recall, a profile from the mid-nineties that mentioned "the composer's mother, Ida, a former church pianist," and then never mentioned her again, as if the article had opened a door, glanced in, and decided the room was unfurnished.

A former church pianist. Whose initials matched the letter demanding the return of a manuscript from Halvard Brandt, *kindly, once.*

Delia set the letter down on the felt mat and looked at it for a long time. Around her the archive breathed its paper breath. Somewhere overhead, footsteps crossed the rehearsal hall floor—students arriving, the day beginning above her, indifferent, luminous, built entirely on the story this basement was supposed to be keeping safe.

* * *

At noon she climbed the stairs to the reading room, partly for the light and partly because questions, like plants, need to be brought near a window sometimes. The reading room occupied the southeast corner of the second floor, tall windows, radiators that knocked like polite ghosts. Two students sat at the far table with headphones on, transcribing something baroque. Delia took her usual seat and laid out her materials: the accession ledger photocopy, the card describing the missing manuscript, and the letter from I.O.S., which she had photographed with her phone—she was not so much a traditionalist as to refuse a phone—and which now glowed on the screen between her hands.

The timeline assembled itself in her head, the way timelines did when she let them, clicking together like bones.

October 1994: Halvard deposits the autograph manuscript of *Vespers in Grey*, donated "on behalf of the composer." The premiere had already happened, the reviews were in, the name was going up on the wall. The deposit was, in institutional terms, a coronation—the physical proof enshrined.

November 1995: fourteen months later, Halvard withdraws it. "Conservation."

Undated, but filed among materials that clustered around the mid-nineties: a letter from I.O.S. demanding its return, promising to be unkind twice.

So either the withdrawal was the response to the letter—in which case Halvard had given the manuscript back to the person demanding it, and the trail simply ended in a private drawer somewhere—or the letter postdated the withdrawal, and I.O.S. had written it into a void, demanding from a man something he no longer controlled. Either way, the autograph score—the one document that could show, in ink and erasure and revision, *how the piece came to be*—had spent thirty years outside the reach of anyone who might think to ask it a question.

And nobody had asked. That was the part that settled over Delia like the cold off the stone walls. In thirty years, in all the dissertations and program notes and anniversary essays, no scholar had ever requested the autograph manuscript of *Vespers in Grey*. She checked the request logs to be sure—green-bound volumes, one line per research visit. The piece was requested often enough: performances, analyses, a doctoral thesis on its harmonic language. But always the *published edition*, the clean engraving, the score as the world received it. The messy original, the witness with fingerprints, sat unrequested in whatever drawer held it, while everyone consulted the translation instead of the tongue.

*The music arrived whole,* Halvard had told the press. *As if it had been waiting.*

Whole. Delia turned the word over. Autograph manuscripts were never whole. That was what made them precious—they showed the seams, the second thoughts, the passage crossed out at midnight and rewritten at dawn. A manuscript that arrived whole would be a manuscript that had never been drafted at all. A fair copy. A transcription.

She was aware of the shape of the thought forming, and she did not like it, and she did not stop it. Thirty-one years in a basement teaches a certain discipline: follow the document, not the feeling. But the feeling kept pace anyway, walking beside the documents like a dog beside a walker.

If the autograph score was actually a fair copy—if the piece had been composed elsewhere, earlier, by another hand—then the deposit in 1994 was not a donation. It was a burial. And the letter from I.O.S. was not the grievance of a crank. It was the voice of the grave.

* * *

In the afternoon, she went looking for the deposit receipt. Institutions keep duplicates the way nervous people keep receipts, and the business office's older files—transferred to her custody during a renovation nobody thanked her for—held carbon copies of everything from 1962 to 2001. She found the folder for October 1994. The receipt for accession 1994-117 was there, typed, signed by Halvard, and beneath his signature, in the space labeled *Provenance of deposited material*, a single line in Halvard's slanting hand:

*Received from the composer's family. Original composition date per attached statement.*

Attached statement. Delia flipped the page. There was no statement. There was a paperclip hole, rusted to a small brown bloom, marking where a page had once lived and been removed.

Someone had removed the statement of provenance before the file ever reached the archive—or after, which was worse. She sat with the rusted bloom under the lamp for a long moment. Paperclips testified. They were the archive's little martyrs, dying to prove that something else had once been present. This one said: *there was a page here, and someone wanted it gone.*

She thought about Ruth Ellery, who had processed the withdrawal fourteen months later, who had written *reason given: "conservation"* with those quotation marks around the word—quotation marks that Delia had read a hundred times over the years as mere typographical habit and now understood, with a chill that reached all the way down to the brewery-cold stones, as the only rebellion available to a meticulous woman in a generous institution. Ruth hadn't believed it either. Ruth had recorded the lie accurately, inside quotation marks, and said nothing else for the remaining twenty years of her career.

Institutional memory, Delia thought, is not a record of what happened. It is a record of what an institution could afford to remember. Everything else got filed under quotation marks, or under rusted paperclip holes, or under nothing at all.

* * *

She stayed late. She told herself it was the gala deadline, and it was, but it was also the other thing, the thing she had not yet said aloud even to herself: that the name on the donor wall downstairs belonged to a man who was dead now, buried in September to a full house and a eulogy she had watched on the livestream from this very chair; and that the other name—the upright, careful hand, the initials I.O.S.—belonged to a woman the archive had never troubled to make a folder for.

She checked. Of course she checked. She searched the catalog for *Ida*, for *Oscar*, for *Oscar-Sandoval*. The catalog returned nothing. Not a correspondence file, not a program bio, not so much as a photograph captioned. A woman who had apparently written at least one furious letter to the most powerful professor in the building had been erased so thoroughly that the institution's memory did not contain her at all. Erasure, Delia knew, rarely required effort. It required only that no one care enough to write you down.

Except someone had written her down. The envelope had survived, unaccessioned, unpenciled, slipped into a Brandt folder like a splinter left in a wound—because Ruth Ellery could not bring herself to throw away a document, even one that implicated everything, and had hidden it the only way a subordinate hides a truth: by filing it where no one looks.

Delia turned off the green lamp, then turned it back on. She took a fresh sheet of acid-free paper and wrote, in pencil, in her own tidy hand, a memo to herself that was also, she suspected, a memo to whoever came after her:

*Query re: acc. 1994-117. Provenance incomplete. Statement referenced, absent. Autograph ms. withdrawn 1995, disposition unknown. Unaccessioned letter, initialled I.O.S., suggests prior claim to the work. Recommend: locate ms.; locate I.O.S. or estate; do not mount anniversary display until resolved.*

She looked at the last sentence. *Do not mount anniversary display.* The director wanted the display in six weeks. The director wanted the name, the legend, the smooth story, the boy in the grey suit grown into a myth. The director did not want a rusted paperclip hole and a woman with no folder.

Delia picked up the phone list taped beside the lamp, ran her finger down it, and paused at the entry she had never once dialed in thirty-one years: *Office of the Director, ext. 100.*

She did not dial it. Instead she wrote a second line beneath her memo, smaller:

*Also recommend: speak to the family. Both of them.*

Because that afternoon, checking the request logs one final time, she had found something she had not expected, something that had changed the temperature of the whole day. In the current log—the open volume, the one for this very season—there was a research request, dated last week, handwritten on the standard pink slip. The requested material: *all records pertaining to Vespers in Grey, incl. provenance.* The requester's signature was small and upright and careful, and it was not I.O.S., and it was not Halvard Brandt, and it was not any of the forty scholars Delia could have predicted.

It read, simply: *W. Sandoval.*

The prodigy himself, asking the archive what the archive knew. Asking, in effect, whether the basement remembered what the lobby had chosen to forget.

Delia capped her pencil, squared the pink slip on the felt mat, and sat for a moment in the lamplight, listening to the building settle around its own foundations. Then she wrote at the bottom of the slip, in the space reserved for staff response, the only honest thing the institution had put in writing in thirty years:

*Available. Please come down.*
