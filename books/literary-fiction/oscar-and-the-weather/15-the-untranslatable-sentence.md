## 15. The Untranslatable Sentence

The mop closet had become a study. Wren had not planned it that way; rooms become what we do in them, the way a path becomes a road becomes a wound in the earth that no one remembers cutting. But three nights into the letter, the closet held a folding chair, a clip lamp borrowed from the stagehand's supply, a thermos of coffee gone bitter and cold, and Ida's letter spread across an upturned crate like a patient on an operating table.

Outside, the conservatory breathed its night breaths—boilers ticking, radiators knocking their old arthritic code through the walls, somewhere on the third floor a practice room door left open so that a scale exercise drifted down the stairwell, faint as weather from another country. Wren had learned to read this building at night the way sailors read sky. It never slept; it only lowered its voice.

The cipher was coming back to them the way a language comes back—not word by word but in floods, whole sentences surfacing whole, and then long stretches where they were eight years old again at the kitchen table with greaseproof paper and a pencil worn to a stub, their mother's hand warm on their shoulder, her voice saying *no, no, the rain goes here, you're writing snow.*

That was the thing about the code: it wasn't letters dressed as other letters. Anyone could do that—A for Q, B for R, the schoolboy games in the backs of comic books. Ida's system was older and stranger and entirely theirs. She had built it out of weather.

Every symbol stood for a condition of sky. There were forty-one of them, though Wren had only ever counted thirty-nine until now, and each one was drawn, not written—a small spiral for wind off the lake, a flat horizontal stroke with a drooping tail for fog, a triangle with its point filed off for the soft rain that came in off November and stayed through March. The symbols combined the way weather combined. A cold front following a warm one meant something different than the same two symbols reversed; pressure mattered, sequence mattered, and there were rules of combination that eight-year-old Wren had understood intuitively and could not, even now, have written down as grammar. You didn't learn the cipher. You caught it, like an accent, like a way of holding your fork.

*The music you play is a translation.* That line had come up out of the page on the second night, and Wren had sat with it for hours telling themselves it was metaphor—the kind of thing mothers wrote, the kind of thing that sounded profound and meant nothing, or meant only *I am proud of you*, or *I always heard myself in your hands*. Mothers were allowed their poetry. Wren had folded the sentence away like a letter into an envelope and moved on.

But the letter kept going. And on the fourth line from the bottom, the weather stopped.

* * *

It happened the way a river happens upon a dam. One moment the symbols were flowing—Wren decoding in long easy strokes, translating onto a legal pad in pencil, the sentences emerging in Ida's voice so clearly that Wren could hear the slight lift at the end of them, the almost-question, the way she said things she was sure of as if leaving room for the world to disagree—and then the line simply broke against something.

Seven characters. Seven symbols that belonged to no sky Wren had ever lived under.

They copied them out carefully, enlarged, the way you might press a strange insect between glass. The first looked like a spiral, but wrong—a spiral that turned inward on both ends instead of one, a spiral that had been argued with. The second resembled the fog-stroke but was doubled, lying on top of itself like a shadow cast by another shadow. The third was nothing Wren knew at all: a vertical line broken in the middle, offset, the top half shifted left of the bottom half like a bone badly set. Then two more spirals-in-reverse, a shape like a closed eye, and finally a symbol that was almost the triangle of soft rain except that it had been filled in, solid black, dense with graphite even in the original ink, as if Ida had pressed hard enough to go through the paper.

Wren tried everything. They ran the seven characters through every rule they could reconstruct: reversal, inversion, the doubling rule (two fogs made drizzle; two drizzles made the grey that lasted all day), the pressure rules, the seasonal shifts—Ida had modified certain symbols depending on whether the letter was written in winter or summer, a refinement she'd invented when Wren was ten and which had delighted them both beyond all reason, because it meant the code was alive, it changed with the year the way language changes with fashion. Wren tried reading the sentence backward. They tried reading it as if written by the nine-year-old version of themselves, who had insisted on adding a symbol of their own invention—a lopsided asterisk meaning *very*—and whose contributions Ida had indulgently incorporated. They tried assuming a transcription error, a smudge, a slip of the pen, and forced each character into its nearest legitimate neighbor, producing strings like *the grey is* and *under fog the* and once, thrillingly and meaninglessly, *snow very snow*.

Nothing. The sentence sat there in the middle of the decoded letter like a stone in a shoe, like a tooth with a crack in it that the tongue returns to, returns to, returns to.

Around them the building went on breathing. At some point the scale exercises upstairs stopped, and the silence that replaced them had texture, had weight, like snowfall.

* * *

Here is what Wren did not think about, those nights in the mop closet, and the not-thinking took work, took the specific muscular effort of a person carrying something heavy past a window they must not look through:

They did not think about the first decoded line. *The music you play is a translation.* A translation of what? From what language into what? Metaphor, they told themselves again, mothers are allowed their poetry—but the phrase had begun to behave less like poetry and more like instruction, like the first sentence of a document whose remaining sentences would define its terms.

They did not think about Odile, the woman in the green coat, who had said *the Oscars* in a voice pitched exactly between tenderness and accusation, and who had walked away before anyone could ask her anything, which was either cowardice or courtesy and which Wren had spent four days failing to decide between.

They did not think about Halvard, dead three weeks now, who had kept something for them *until the weather changed*, and who had died in October with the weather changing all around him as if he'd timed it, as if he'd refused to be the one to hand it over.

And they did not think about Tomas, who would know what to do with all of this, which was to burn it. Tomas, who guarded the family story the way other men guarded money—with a shotgun across his knees and a story about why the shotgun was necessary.

No. What Wren thought about, exclusively, obsessively, with the pure and blameless concentration of the truly avoidant, was seven characters of weather that mapped to no sky.

* * *

On the fifth night, Ruth found them.

She came down the corridor pushing her cart, and the squeak of its bad wheel announced her the way a herald announces a king, and Wren had just enough time to sweep the legal pad into the crate and drop Ida's letter on top of it, face down, before Ruth's head appeared around the doorframe of the closet.

"You've got a campsite," Ruth said. She looked at the folding chair, the clip lamp, the thermos. "You living here now? Rent's probably better than mine."

"Just working late."

"Janitors don't work late. Janitors work exactly as long as the work takes and then we go home and drink." She leaned against the doorframe. She was sixty-something, built like the buildings she cleaned, and she had the particular kindness of people who have decided not to ask questions as a policy rather than a failure of curiosity. "You've been different lately," she said. "Tighter. Like a wire somebody's tuning."

"It's nothing."

"Sure." Ruth looked at the crate for a moment longer than was comfortable. "My mother used to do crosswords in pen," she said, apropos of apparently nothing. "Wouldn't touch pencil. Said if you write it in pencil you're giving yourself permission to be wrong."

She pushed her cart on. The bad wheel squeaked away down the corridor, fading, and Wren sat in the lamplight with their heart going oddly, because Ruth had looked at the crate, and Ruth had seen whatever it is that people see when someone is hiding a thing badly, and Ruth had chosen, with her whole manner, to walk past it. And Wren found that this mercy stung worse than interrogation would have. Interrogation you could resist. Kindness got in under the door.

In pen, Wren thought. Their mother had written the letter in ink. Every symbol committed, permanent, no permission to be wrong anywhere in it—which meant the seven impossible characters were not slips. Ida had known exactly what she was drawing. She had drawn a weather that did not exist, deliberately, in ink, in the middle of a confession, and she had known that whoever decoded the letter would stop there and stay there, the way Wren was staying, the way Wren could not stop staying.

Or she had known that only one person in the world would ever decode it at all. And she had trusted that person to understand—or to suffer beautifully in the attempt.

* * *

The sixth night, Wren brought the journals.

Not Ida's weather journals—those were still in storage at Tomas's insistence, boxed and taped and exiled to a unit in a place with chain-link fencing, and Wren had not yet worked up the courage or the cruelty to ask him for the key. No: these were Wren's own notebooks from childhood, retrieved last week from the same storage unit during a visit that Tomas had supervised with the anxious vigilance of a man watching someone handle his own organs. School notebooks, spiral-bound, covers soft with age. Because eight-year-olds who invent ciphers with their mothers practice. The pages were full of translated weather reports—*cloudy, high 11, chance of rain by evening* rendered in spirals and fog-strokes and triangles—and, in the later notebooks, small compositions of their own devising, secret messages passed to Ida across the dinner table while Tomas, four years older and gloriously indifferent, ate his potatoes.

Wren went through them the way you go through old photographs looking for a face you're not sure was ever in the room. And somewhere in the third notebook, dated in a child's wobbly hand to a February when Wren was eleven, they found it.

A single line, five characters long, in the reverse-spiral. In their own handwriting.

Wren stared at it for a long time. The boiler ticked. Somewhere above, a door closed softly, the building swallowing.

They had invented it. Or half-invented it—they couldn't remember, that was the horror of it, the memory was simply gone, dissolved the way childhood dissolves, leaving artifacts without context, scripture without church. Eleven-year-old Wren had drawn these reverse spirals, had used them in a message to their mother, and the message itself decoded to nothing legible under any rule Wren possessed, which meant either that the child had been playing—making weather up, inventing conditions, doing with symbols what children do with words, nonsense for the pleasure of the sound—or that the child had known a rule that the adult had lost.

Below the line, in Ida's handwriting, was a reply. Also in reverse spirals. Longer. Sixteen characters, maybe seventeen. And beneath that, in ordinary English, in her small slanted script, Ida had written:

*Not yet. When you need it, you'll already know it.*

Wren put the notebook down very carefully, as if it were sleeping.

*Not yet.* Which meant the reverse spirals had meant something. Which meant there was a rule, a real rule, a third layer—and Ida had withheld it, deliberately, the way you withhold the last piece of a gift, the way you tell a child *you'll understand when you're older* and then die before they're older, leaving them older forever and understanding nothing.

When you need it, you'll already know it. Wren read the sentence again and felt its terrible shape: it was not a promise. It was a test with the answer key destroyed. It assumed a future that Ida had no right to assume and no power to guarantee, and it placed the entire burden of comprehension on a child who had not yet been born into the knowledge that would make the sentence necessary.

What could an eleven-year-old possibly need to say that required a hidden weather?

Wren sat with that question while the clip lamp buzzed and the coffee went cold and the building breathed around them, and the question changed shape slowly, the way clouds change shape, imperceptibly and then all at once, until it was no longer *what did I need to say* but *what did she need to hear*—and then it changed again, darker, until it was: *what did she need to say to me that she could not say in any weather that existed?*

The letter waited on the crate, face down. Four lines from the bottom, seven impossible characters. *Not yet,* said the notebook, in a mother's hand, twenty-five years old.

Wren turned the letter over. Read the decoded lines leading up to the break one more time, mouthing them, hearing Ida's voice lift at the end of each: *the music you play is a translation... you were young and I let you believe... the grey was mine before it was yours...*

And then the stone in the shoe. The cracked tooth. The seven characters that mapped to no sky.

There was one person left alive who might know what the reverse spirals meant—who had corresponded with Ida for years, who had received a score and returned it on demand, who had stood at a funeral and spoken a buried name aloud like a woman setting down something she'd carried too far. A woman in a green coat who had walked away before questions, and who had left, tucked into the condolence card she'd pressed on the family with such strange formality, a phone number written in pencil.

Pencil, Wren thought. Permission to be wrong.

They picked up the card. Outside, far off toward the lake, thunder muttered—out of season, out of nowhere, the sky practicing a language of its own.
