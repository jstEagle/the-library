## 29. Tomas at the Door

The knock came at nine-forty in the evening, which was how Wren knew it was Tomas before they even opened the door. Their brother had always treated time the way he treated money—something to be spent deliberately, never wasted on impulse. A knock at nine-forty meant he had waited until a decent hour, had rehearsed his arrival, had probably sat in his car outside the building for ten minutes composing his face.

Wren opened the door and there he was: sixty-one years old, silver at the temples now, wearing the wool overcoat that had survived three decades of funerals and board meetings. He held a paper bag from the bakery on Delancey—the one their mother used to like—and behind him the hallway of Wren's building hummed with its fluorescent indifference.

"You look terrible," Tomas said.

"Come in before you say anything else."

He stepped past them into the apartment, and Wren watched him take it in the way he always did—not with judgment exactly, but with an accountant's eye. The secondhand furniture. The books stacked in uneven towers. The window that looked out on the airshaft instead of the street. He set the bakery bag on the counter with the care of a man laying down an opening bid.

"I brought almond horns," he said. "Mom's kind."

"She liked the ones with the marzipan center."

"These are those." He unbuttoned his coat but didn't take it off, which Wren understood as a message: I am not staying long, and also, I am prepared for weather. "I heard about the gala."

Of course he had. The conservatory's invitation had gone out weeks ago, embossed and gilded, *An Evening Honoring Vespers in Grey — Thirty Years*, and Tomas's name had been on the patron list since before Wren had said yes. He was listed as a Benefactor Circle donor. He had probably been listed before Wren finished reading the envelope.

"And?" Wren said.

"And I'd like to know what you're planning to do up there."

"Play the piano. That's generally what happens at pianos."

Tomas didn't smile. He crossed to the window, looked out at the airshaft as if it might contain something worth seeing, then turned back. In the overhead light his face had the particular pallor of a man who hadn't slept well—not grief, Wren thought, or not only grief. Calculation that kept waking him up.

"Wren." He said their name the way he said few things anymore: gently. "Sit down. Please."

They sat. He remained standing, which was also a message, though Wren couldn't decide what it said. Perhaps that sitting would make this a conversation between equals, and he needed, tonight, for it not to be.

"I'm going to say some things," Tomas began, "and I want you to let me finish. All right? You can hate me after. You've had practice."

"Tomas—"

"All right?"

Wren folded their hands. Outside, somewhere below in the street, a bus sighed at its stop and pulled away, and Wren thought absurdly of the 42, of rerouting notices, of everything in their life lately arriving stamped with an expiration date.

"I've spent thirty years protecting this family," Tomas said. "You were the one on stage—I understand that. You took the bows; I took the phone calls. When the critics came after you in 'ninety-four, I was the one who talked to the lawyers. When Dad got sick, I was the one who managed the doctors while you were touring. And when Mom—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "When Mom died, I was the one who closed her apartment. I packed every box in that place, Wren. Every box. Do you know what I found in her desk?"

Wren's mouth went dry. "Tomas."

"Papers," he said. "Music papers. In that little code you two used to write in, like children passing notes. I couldn't read them. I never learned your cipher—you two kept it like a secret language, like a country I wasn't issued a passport to. But I knew what they were. I knew what they looked like." His voice stayed level, terribly level, the voice of a man walking across ice he has tested every step of. "And I put them in a box, and I labeled the box, and I put the box in storage, and I have never opened it again. Twenty-two years. That is what I have done for this family. That is my version of what you did on stage."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Wren gripped the arm of the chair. "You knew."

"I suspected," Tomas corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. Suspecting is survivable. Knowing—" He shook his head. "Knowing requires action, and action requires choosing, and I chose. I chose us. I chose the family. I chose the story where our mother was a sick woman who loved music and gave her talented child everything she had, willingly, gladly, because that story let everyone live. It let her die in peace. It let you play. It let me sleep."

"And now?"

"Now you're going to stand up at a gala in front of four hundred people and burn it down." He finally sat, across from Wren, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees, and for a moment Wren saw him at nineteen, at twenty-five, at every age—the brother who had stood in the wings during the debut, who had cried, actually cried, when the applause started. "So here is what I came to say."

He reached into his coat and produced an envelope. Not a check—a folder, heavy cream stock, the kind lawyers used. He laid it on the coffee table between them without ceremony, the way a dealer lays down cards.

"The foundation is dissolved next month. Dad's estate, the licensing income from your catalog—it's been sitting in trust, and per the terms it disperses on your sixtieth birthday regardless. But I control the disbursement schedule, and I can accelerate it. There's enough there that you would never mop another floor. Never rent another apartment. You could buy back the old neighborhood if you wanted—they're selling the lots cheap since the demolition. You could commission a hall with her name on it, a real one, marble, endowment, the works. Ida Oscar-Sandoval Memorial something-or-other. Her name in stone, Wren. Carved. Permanent. Isn't that what you want? Her name remembered?"

"It's not her name I'm worried about being remembered wrong."

"Then hear the rest." He tapped the folder once. "In exchange, you play the gala as scheduled. The program as printed. 'Vespers in Grey,' composed by Wren Sandoval. You play it beautifully, because you always have. And afterward, nothing changes. No statements. No corrections. No interviews with that woman Odile and her archive boxes. The conservatory revises nothing. The record stays the record." He paused, and his voice dropped into something almost tender, which was worse than anger. "You keep the lie, and I make sure the lie keeps you. Comfortably. For the rest of your life."

The radiator ticked in the corner. Somewhere above them a neighbor's television murmured through the floor, laughter with no context, canned and distant. Wren looked at the folder and felt the strangest sensation—not temptation arriving, but temptation already seated inside them, patient, having gotten there first. Because God, the ease of it. The terrible arithmetic of it. One more performance. They had performed the piece a thousand times; their hands knew it better than their own signature. One more time, under the old name, and then marble halls and no more lanyard that said FACILITIES, no more mop water smelling of industrial pine at two in the morning, no more Ruth asking questions Wren couldn't answer honestly.

One more time. That was how it had started the first time, too. One debut. One transcription, done lovingly, done to save her from the drawer where her music was dying unread. One signature on one title page, and then the machinery took over, and the machinery never asked twice.

"I can see you thinking it," Tomas said quietly.

"I'm not thinking anything."

"You're thinking about how easy it would be. I can see it, Wren. I've seen that look your whole life—that's the look you had at sixteen when Halvard asked whose piece it was and you said yours, and your voice only shook a little." He leaned back. "I'm not offering you corruption. I'm offering you mercy. Hers, most of all. She chose silence. She wrote it down—she called your fame *the weather I made*. You told me yourself what the journals say. If she chose it, who are you to overturn her verdict? What kind of love is it that overrides the dead woman's own decision?"

"That's not what she decided. She decided not to hurt me. Those aren't the same thing."

"They're exactly the same thing, viewed from where I sit."

"From where you sit," Wren said, "everything looks like a balance sheet."

Tomas flinched—actually flinched, a small involuntary movement around the eyes—and Wren regretted it instantly, and didn't retract it. This was the shape their arguments had always taken: he built walls out of provision, and Wren threw words like stones, and both of them called it family.

"Say the number," Wren said, and hated themselves for saying it.

Tomas opened the folder. Inside, figures in clean columns, a life measured out in disbursements. It was, Wren realized, more money than they had ever seen attached to their own name—even at the height of the fame, even in the years when orchestras flew them business class, the money had flowed through managers and trusts and taxes, abstracted, never quite real. This was real. This was real the way the floor of the stage was real, the way the applause had been real, the way the silence afterward had been real for thirty years.

"If I take this," Wren said slowly, "what happens to Odile's copy of the score? Dated three years before my debut? With Mom's fingering marks in the margins?"

"Ms. Ferrand is an elderly woman with a private collection and no legal standing. Scores circulate. Copies get made. Provenance is ambiguous in half the manuscripts in any archive—you know that better than anyone. Ambiguity is not guilt."

"Ambiguity is not innocence either."

"No," Tomas agreed. "But it's livable. That's all I'm offering, Wren. Not innocence. Livability." He closed the folder and slid it an inch closer. "Take a day. Take two. The gala is Saturday. I'll wait until Friday night. After that, whatever you do up on that stage, I will stand in the back and I will applaud, because I am your brother and that will always be true. But if you do what I'm asking—if you give me this one last thing—then when the applause ends, we go out to dinner, the two of us, and we eat too much, and we talk about Mom like people do, normally, the way families talk, and none of it ever has to be a courtroom again."

*None of it ever has to be a courtroom again.* Wren felt the sentence go through them like warm water. Because that was the thing no one understood about scandal, about truth-telling, about the grand dramatic gestures the novels always made look clean: the aftermath was a courtroom. Forever. Every dinner party, every student, every archivist, every journalist with a podcast. Truth didn't set you free; truth enrolled you in a permanent seminar where you defended your mother's honor against strangers who would call her a fool and you a thief and the whole family a machine. Whereas the lie—God, the lie was so soft. The lie was an old coat. The lie still fit.

Their hand moved toward the folder. Actually moved. Wren watched it happen from somewhere slightly outside themselves, watched their fingers extend toward the cream stock, toward the clean columns, toward the livable ambiguity, and thought: *I could be comfortable. I could be comfortable and no one would ever have to know what it cost.*

Then they saw it—unbidden, unwelcome—the image of Ruth's face in the loading dock light, the question she'd asked plainly, *Did you write it?*, and the lie Wren had given her, and the way her expression had changed. Not outraged. Just rearranged. The small recalibration of a person deciding how much of what you said, ever, they would believe again. Wren had bought silence from Ruth with one lie and had been paying for it ever since, in every shift, in every shared cigarette, in the new careful distance in her voice. And Tomas was offering to sell them the same thing at scale. A lifetime of Ruths. A lifetime of faces rearranging.

Their hand stopped. Hung in the air over the folder like a conductor's, mid-phrase.

"Tomas," Wren said, and their voice came out strange, hoarse. "Do you remember the cipher rule she taught us the last summer? The third layer? The one for lying about the weather?"

Tomas frowned. "I barely remember the first layer."

"She taught it to me the day I lied for her. Really lied, out loud, to a neighbor—told Mrs. Okafor it wasn't raining when it was pouring, because Mom didn't want visitors. She sat me down afterward and said, *now you know how to build a lock, so someday you'll know how to pick one.*" Wren withdrew their hand. "She taught me lying as a system, Tomas. As a technology. And the last line of her letter—the one I couldn't decode for months—was written in the lying cipher. You know what it says?"

"Don't."

"*I gave it to you; you took it. Both are true.*" Wren looked at their brother, at the man who had packed every box, who had labeled the box, who had carried the box for twenty-two years like a man carrying a body. "She wasn't silent because she forgave me. She was silent because she understood that guilt and gift had gotten tangled past separating, and she loved us both too much to cut the knot in public. But she wrote it down. She left it where someone who loved her would eventually find it and read it. That's not a woman choosing silence. That's a woman leaving a door unlocked."

Tomas stared at the folder on the table. For a long moment his composure—the beautiful, load-bearing composure he had maintained through two funerals and a demolition and thirty years of phone calls—cracked along some invisible seam, and Wren saw underneath it not anger but exhaustion, vast and geological, the tiredness of a man who had been holding up a sky alone and had just been told the sky was optional.

"She would have hated this," he said finally. "All of it. The gala. The letter. You on that stage making a speech."

"She hated a lot of things quietly. It's the family disease."

"And if you do this—if you stand up there and say her name—" His jaw worked. "I lose her again. Don't you understand that? The mother I have is the one in the story. The one who gave freely. You take that away, and I don't get a better mother. I just get a robbed one."

Wren leaned forward. "You get a real one. For the first time. She wrote music, Tomas. Beautiful music, in her own hand, with her own fingering, and it sounded like weather moving over water, and nobody ever heard it but us. You don't lose her. You finally meet her."

Tomas stood abruptly, buttoning his coat with quick, precise movements, the accountant reassembling himself piece by piece. He looked down at the folder, and then, with a gesture that undid Wren completely, he left it there. On the table. Between them.

"Keep it," he said. "Either way. If you do the stupid, brave thing, at least don't do it broke." He walked to the door, then stopped, his hand on the frame, his back to them. "Friday night," he said. "That's my last offer. After that I'm just your brother again, and God help us both."

The door closed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Wren sat for a long time in the ticking quiet, the almond horns going stale on the counter, the folder glowing on the table like something radioactive. Then they picked it up—not to open it, not yet—and carried it to the window, and stood looking out at the airshaft, at the brick and the fire escape and the slice of grey sky above, thinking about locks and keys, about doors left unlocked, about how many hours remained until Saturday, and whether a person could learn a dead woman's fingering well enough, by then, to speak in her voice.

Down in the street, faintly, a bus went by.
