## 37. Ruth's Shift

The loading dock smelled of wet cardboard and the ghost of floor wax, and Wren stood there in a coat that no longer had a lanyard attached to it, feeling lighter than they had any right to feel. Three days since the termination email. *Unrelated reasons*, the HR language had said, with the delicacy of institutions everywhere, as though the firing were a weather event, unconnected to any human choice. Wren had printed it, actually printed it, and folded it into the back pocket of the same notebook where Ida's cipher lived now, page after page of decoded weather and decoded music and one decoded confession. It seemed right that the two documents should share a pocket. The price of truth and the truth itself, filed together.

Ruth came out through the double doors at eleven-forty, still in her coveralls, a flask swinging from two fingers like a pendulum.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been told."

"You look terrible in a *good* way. Like somebody finally let the air out of you." She held up the flask. "You're not staff anymore, so technically I can't get in trouble for this. That's the only perk of your situation, but I intend to exploit it."

The loading dock was Ruth's territory in a way Wren was only now understanding. In four months of shared shifts, Wren had learned the geography of the building from the inside—the mop closets, the service elevator with its stuck third button, the particular acoustics of the stairwells—but Ruth had taught them the geography of *between*, the cracks where the building exhaled. The loading dock was the largest of these. A concrete shelf cantilevered over the service alley, stacked with delivery pallets and dead stage lights, facing east toward the freight entrance of the building next door. At night the city's hum pooled down there like water in a basin. It was the one place in Meridian where you could hear yourself think, and Ruth guarded it the way other people guarded gardens.

They sat on a pallet of shrink-wrapped programs for the spring season—programs that would not, Wren noted, include the name Ida Oscar-Sandoval yet, though the conservatory had quietly begun the process of revision. Ruth cracked the flask and poured into the cap, handed it over. Whiskey, cheap and warm, tasting faintly of the flask itself, of every shift it had ever accompanied.

"To unemployment," Ruth said.

"To unrelated reasons."

They drank. Above them the loading dock's caged bulb threw a circle of amber light, and moths performed their small devotion against the mesh. Wren felt the whiskey move through them and realized, with a kind of slow astonishment, that the tightness was gone. The band that had been wound around their chest since Halvard's funeral—since before that, since the debut, since fourteen years old on that stage—had loosened. Not gone. Loosened. Like a fist deciding, finger by finger, to open.

"I keep waiting to panic," Wren said. "I've been employed since I was fourteen. First as a prodigy, then as a cautionary tale, then as a janitor. I've never actually been *unemployed*."

"Give it a week. Panic keeps office hours. It'll find you Tuesday around ten."

"And until then?"

Ruth took the cap back, drank, wiped her mouth on her shoulder the way she did everything, without ceremony. "Until then you sit on my dock and drink my whiskey and tell me what the hell you're going to do next, because I've been reading about you, and people with your kind of history don't get to be unemployed for long. Somebody's going to come calling. The question is whether you answer."

* * *

The strange thing was how easy it was to sit there. Wren had spent their whole life in rooms where sitting required performance—the correct posture at the keyboard, the correct humility at interviews, the correct contrition at the press conference that had ended everything. Even in the mop closet there had been a version of themselves to maintain: the invisible man, the ghost with a lanyard. But Ruth had never required a version. Ruth had met Wren at the exact point where the myth had been stripped down to a person holding a mop, and everything since—the gala, the confession on stage, the scandal blooming across the arts pages like ink in water—Ruth had taken in stride, as though she'd always known she was sharing a building with a detonation and had simply been waiting for it to go off.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Wren said. "The night I told you the truth. In the stairwell."

"You mean when you finally answered my question honestly? Six weeks late?"

"Six weeks late." Wren turned the cap in their fingers. "I lied to you first. You asked me straight out—*did you write it*—and I lied, and your face changed, and I thought about that face for days. It was the first time in my life a lie had a cost I could see immediately. Usually my lies took years to come due. That one took four seconds."

Ruth shrugged. "You paid it back."

"That's not—people say that, that it balances out, but it doesn't. The lie and the truth aren't two entries in a ledger. The lie is just gone. It doesn't convert." Wren looked out at the alley, where a rat moved along the wall with the confidence of a creature who owned its route. "But I keep thinking about how easy the truth was, the second time. How it just came out of me in that stairwell like water finding a drain. And how the whole time I was lying to you, I was also lying to Tomas, lying to the conservatory, lying to the fourteen-year-old on that stage. And you—God, you were the only person in my life with no stake in the story. You didn't care about 'Vespers in Grey.' You cared whether the loading dock got hosed down before six. You were the first honest air I'd breathed in twenty years, and I lied to you anyway, because lying was the atmosphere. I didn't even know I was doing it."

Ruth was quiet for a while. She was good at quiet in a way that performers never were; she could hold silence like a tool, not a void.

"My sister stole from me once," she said finally. "Money. Not a huge amount, but enough. And everyone in my family had a theory about why, and every theory was a way of not calling it stealing. She was struggling. She was confused. She was going through a hard time. And I sat in my mother's kitchen listening to these theories and I realized the whole family was building a weather system around one small theft, a whole climate of explanations, so nobody ever had to say the plain thing." She took another drink. "I called it stealing. Out loud. Once. And you'd have thought I'd broken a window. But here's the thing—after that, I could actually love her again. Because I wasn't spending all my energy holding up the climate."

"The family myth."

"The family myth." Ruth nodded at the flask. "You broke yours on a stage in front of four hundred people. Most of us do it in kitchens. Same physics, smaller audience."

Wren laughed—actually laughed, the sound surprising them both, bouncing off the pallets. When was the last time they'd laughed without checking first who was listening? The whiskey, probably. But not only the whiskey.

* * *

The night deepened. A truck arrived at the neighboring building with a delivery of restaurant produce, and the dock filled briefly with the smell of cilantro and cold air, and the driver nodded at them like they were fixtures, which, in a way, they were—two people the city had decided not to look at. Wren found they didn't mind. Anonymity, which had once been their armor and then their shame, was becoming something else: just a condition, like weather. You didn't have to be seen to be real. Ida had spent thirty years unseen, writing her days into cipher at a kitchen table, and the work had been real the whole time. The seeing had never been the point. The *making* had been the point.

"Can I ask you something," Ruth said. She'd gone quiet in a different way now, the way she went quiet before the questions that mattered. "The night of the gala. I wasn't in the hall—I was on shift, I heard it secondhand, the whole building heard it secondhand, the walls were buzzing. But I've been thinking about it. You stood up in front of everybody and said her name. Ida Oscar-Sandoval. And then you played her version, her fingering, all of it." She turned the cap in her hands. "What did it sound like? Different? The same piece you used to play?"

Wren considered the question seriously, because Ruth asked seriously, without ornament, and deserved a serious answer.

"Different," they said. "Completely different, and I don't fully understand how. It's the same notes. I checked—the notes are the notes. But her fingering marks change everything. Where she puts the thumb, where she lets a phrase breathe, which finger takes the inner voice—it's like the same sentence read aloud by a different person. When I played it as mine, all those years, it was—God, I don't want to say it was empty, that's not fair, it was *mine*, it was true to what I knew. But it was a translation of a translation. I was playing my memory of a piece I didn't know I remembered. And when I played hers—" Wren stopped, looked at their hands, the hands that had made them famous and unmade them and mopped floors and decoded a dead woman's weather. "When I played hers, I heard her. For the first time. Not the myth of her, not the sick quiet woman in the kitchen. Her. The person who marked a fingering a certain way because that's how the phrase felt in *her* hands, in *her* body, on the days she wrote it. It was like hearing a voice on an old tape. Except the tape was my own hands."

Ruth nodded slowly. Then she said the thing.

"You know the hall sounds different now."

Wren looked at her.

"Since the gala. I know that sounds crazy, I'm not a musician, I clean the place, I know where the sound goes because I'm in it when it's empty. But it's different. The guys on days say it too, they won't say it straight, they say it like a joke—*hall's got a ghost now*—but they mean it. It's like the room exhaled. Like it had been holding its breath for thirty years and finally let it out." She looked over. "I don't know if that's the acoustics or the story or what. But I walk through there now on my rounds and it feels like a room that told the truth. Rooms remember. Ask any janitor. We're the only ones who know what buildings keep."

Wren felt their throat tighten. Of all the reviews, all the think-pieces, all the furious and triumphant commentary that had followed the gala—the think-pieces about authorship, about the ethics of prodigy culture, about institutional complicity, three thousand words in a broadsheet that Wren had read twice and then folded away—of everything, it was this that landed. A janitor, on a loading dock, with a flask, saying the hall had exhaled. The building had been holding its breath. Wren thought of all those years of playing that stage, the applause, the reviews, the machinery of fame running at full volume, and the whole time the room had been holding its breath, because the room knew, the way rooms know, the way Ruth knew, that something in it was not true. And now it had let it out. And the sound was different.

"Ruth," Wren said. "Do you know what you just did? You just wrote the only review of that night that I'll keep."

"I didn't review anything. I said the hall sounds different."

"That's the review. That's the only one that matters." Wren laughed again, wet-eyed this time. "You should know that in the cipher my mother and I invented when I was eight, there's no symbol for applause. There's symbols for rain, snow, fog, wind, all of it, all the weathers. But no applause. She didn't think it was weather, I guess. It doesn't move anything. It doesn't change what falls tomorrow." They turned the flask cap in their fingers. "But there *is* a symbol for the moment the fog lifts. Clearing. That's what you just described. The hall clearing."

Ruth held out the cap for a refill. "Your mother sounds like she was something."

"She was weather," Wren said. "She was the whole sky."

* * *

Near one, Ruth checked her watch and groaned about the six a.m. floor buffers, and they gathered themselves, and the dock went back to being just a dock. But at the double doors Ruth paused, hand on the push bar, and looked back with the expression she wore when she was about to be more perceptive than comfortable.

"One more thing. Since you're unemployed and I'm feeling generous." She hesitated. "There's a guy. Comes Thursdays. Young, maybe late twenties, always asking at the desk about the archive—about the provenance records, the old accession files, specifically anything from the seventies and eighties. He's been coming for a month. Last week he asked about Ida Oscar-Sandoval by name." Ruth's eyes were steady. "I didn't tell him anything. But I figured you'd want to know somebody's digging in the same ground you just got finished burying yourself in."

Wren stood very still. The moths ticked against the caged bulb. Somewhere in the building, the old elevator groaned through its shaft, that stuck third button grinding.

"Did he leave a name?"

"He did. Left a card, even. The desk threw it in the drawer with all the others." Ruth shrugged. "But he looked like a musicologist. Or a journalist. One of those people who thinks a story isn't finished just because everybody else decided it was."

Wren thought of Odile, who had waited thirty years for someone to ask. Thought of the archive, the accession files, the gap in the provenance that the conservatory was even now quietly papering over with its revisions. Thought of Tomas's message—*I chose her with us*—and whether "us" could survive a stranger with a card in a drawer and a month of Thursdays behind him.

The fog had lifted off the hall. But fog, Wren's mother could have told them, was never gone for good. It only waited for the temperature to change.

"Ruth," Wren said, taking the last swallow of whiskey and handing back the flask, "what time does the archive open on Thursdays?"
