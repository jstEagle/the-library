## 1. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The piano had gone out of tune the way people do—slowly, and then all at once.

Xanthe Delacroix noticed it on a Tuesday, three days before Elena Marchetti's funeral, when he let himself into the front hall with his own key and heard the silence of the house rearrange itself around him. Thirty years he had been coming to this house, first twice a year for the scheduled tunings, then monthly, then weekly as Elena's hands grew too stiff for the keys but never too stiff for her listening. The house always announced him. It had its own way of speaking: the vestibule radiator gave a single metallic cough when the door opened, like an old man clearing his throat before deciding whether to say anything at all. Today it coughed, and then said nothing, because there was no one left upstairs to lie to.

He set down his case in the hall and stood a moment in the smell of the place—beeswax and old wool and, beneath it, the mineral breath of the radiators, that faint iron sweetness he had never found in any other house. Every house had a voice. Most people walked through theirs deaf. But Xanthe had learned early, in the years when he had no address at all, that a building will tell you everything if you put your hand on its pipes and wait. A house is just a body that can't leave the room where it was born.

Elena's Steinway sat in the music room with its lid closed, which it almost never was. He lifted it. The strings under the dusty light looked like something sleeping, or something pretending to sleep.

"All right," he said quietly, because he always said something. "All right, old girl."

He tuned by ear, the way he'd been taught by a man in Buffalo whose name he'd also given away once, though that was a smaller erasure, a private one. Middle C first, then the temperament octave, working outward in fifths the way you work outward from any truth—carefully, accepting that perfect intervals don't exist in equal temperament, that every tuning is a series of agreed-upon compromises. He had thought about that more than most tuners. You flatten one note so another can ring true. You accept the lie that makes the chord possible. He had been doing it for thirty years in this house, and not only to pianos.

The A above middle C fought him. It had drifted nearly a quarter-tone flat, which meant someone had been playing—not recently, the hammers were clean—but within the last months, hard enough to pull the pin. Elena hadn't played in two years. Her hands had failed her at Easter, and she had sat beside him during the spring tuning with her swollen knuckles folded in her lap like a closed book, saying nothing, listening the way other people pray.

So who had played?

He filed the question away with the others. There were many. That was the thing about this house: it ran on questions nobody asked aloud, the way some furnaces run on oil and some, illegally, on things they shouldn't. He had spent three decades learning which questions were load-bearing. Ask them and the whole structure comes down on your head.

* * *

By late afternoon the light through the music room windows had gone the color of weak tea, and Xanthe did what he always did at the end of a tuning: he walked the house.

Not idly. Diagnostically. Elena used to joke that he checked on the radiators the way other men visited their mothers—out of duty, out of love, unable to stop. He started in the basement, because every house's true conversation happens below the floorboards, in the cast-iron throat of the boiler and the copper arteries running up through the walls.

The boiler was healthy. He bled the radiators floor by floor, third to second to first, and made his inventory, which he kept not in any notebook—he had never written it down; writing it down would have been asking the question—but in the part of his memory where he kept everything else that couldn't be said.

Third floor: the guest-room radiator ticked like a clock, four ticks and a pause, four ticks and a pause, steady as a metronome set to largo. It had done this since 1998, when the pipes were replaced after the freeze, and no plumber had ever cured it, and Xanthe had privately decided it wasn't a fault but a habit, the way an old dog circles three times before lying down. In thirty years he had never once slept in the guest room, but he knew its heartbeat better than his own.

Second floor: Marco's old room, now an office, silent—the radiator there had been shut off at the valve sometime in the nineties, and when Xanthe had asked why, Elena had said, *Marco was always cold as a boy,* which explained nothing and was clearly meant to. Sofia's childhood room still breathed warmth; whatever else happened in this family, they had never stopped heating the room of the child least likely to return to it. Xanthe had opinions about that. He kept them where he kept everything.

First floor: the parlor radiator sang—a high, thin whistle at the top of the heat cycle, barely audible, like a wineglass rubbed at the rim. And the pipe.

The pipe was the reason he came down here alone, the reason he timed his visits for hours when the house was empty, though today emptiness had a different weight than usual, today the emptiness had a name and a date and a casket being built somewhere in Queens.

There was a branch off the main riser in the basement corner, behind the shelving where Elena kept jars of preserves she never ate, a pipe that should have fed nothing, that appeared on no drawing he'd ever seen, that ran horizontally along the joists toward the north wall and simply—stopped. Or didn't stop. It went *into* the wall, into the section of foundation wall that was newer than the rest of the foundation, poured concrete where the rest was stone, and it hummed.

Not always. Only sometimes. Only, Xanthe had determined over years of patient, guilty listening, when certain conditions aligned: the system hot, the house quiet, and someone upstairs telling a lie.

That was the superstition, anyway, and he knew it was a superstition, and he also knew it was true. He had tested it the way you test anything in this house—obliquely, without letting anyone see you looking. The night Marco stood in the dining room in 2009 and told the tax attorney, *No, the property has been in the Marchetti family for generations, my grandmother bought it outright in '52,* the pipe had hummed like a struck fork, low and continuous, for eleven minutes. Xanthe had been in the basement bleeding a valve. He had stood with his palm flat against the warm iron and felt the vibration climb into his wrist bones and understood, with the particular loneliness of a man who knows something he cannot say, that the house was calling Marco a liar in a language only he spoke.

And the night Elena told Sofia, *Your grandfather died before you were born, mi vida, he never even saw photographs of you*—that night the pipe had sung too.

But other nights—nights when Elena sat alone in the kitchen at two in the morning, nights when Xanthe, unable to sleep in his room over the garage, had come down for tea and heard her talking softly to no one, to someone, in Spanish he pretended not to understand—those nights the pipe was silent. Whatever she said to the dark, she said it true.

He laid his hand on it now. Cold iron, dead system, nothing. The house was holding its breath, the way the whole family was holding its breath, the way the whole city block would hold its breath Thursday morning when the black cars came and the Vasquez—

when the Marchetti children stood at the grave.

He caught himself. Even inside his own skull, the correction arrived fast and smooth, worn smooth as a hammer shank from thirty years of use. That was the trick of it. You practice a silence long enough and it stops being a performance. It becomes a reflex, then a character trait, then, finally, a self. He was not a man who kept secrets. He was a man who did not notice things. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and he had paid for it with the only currency he had, which was attention.

* * *

He made tea in the kitchen because making tea was a thing hands could do while a mind refused to sit still. The kettle was Elena's kettle, dented, older than the marriage that had brought her to this country, older maybe than the country she'd claimed to come from—or rather, not claimed. Claiming was active. She had simply allowed the story to stand, the way you allow a mispronunciation of your name from a waiter, once, twice, until the wrong name is the one printed on your mail.

Marchetti. Italian, everyone assumed. From Genoa, the story went, via Buenos Aires, which covered the accent, which covered everything, because Buenos Aires was the great laundering machine of the southern hemisphere and everyone knew it and no one said it. Xanthe had known within a year. Maybe sooner. Maybe he had known the first week, in the winter of '94, when he was twenty-four and living out of a van with his tuning kit and his father's tuning fork, and a small fierce woman in her sixties had flagged him down outside St. Cecilia's because she'd heard him play the parish piano better than their regular man charged less.

She had hired him on the spot. Fed him that same afternoon—soup, bread, a boiled egg, eaten at her kitchen table while she interrogated him gently about his life and he lied about having one. And at the end she had said: *I have a condition. Not about the piano. About the house.* She had pointed down the hallway, where photographs hung—photographs he would spend thirty years not studying—and said, *You will not ask me about those. Ever. If you can live with that, you can live here.*

He had been twenty-four and it was January and the van's heater was broken.

He had said yes.

People imagine that bargains with the devil are dramatic. His had smelled like soup. And in thirty years he had never once asked about the photographs, and Elena had never once acknowledged that his silence was a payment, and between those two disciplines they had built something that neither of them ever called a home out loud, because naming it might have constituted asking.

The kettle screamed. He took it off before it woke the dead, though the dead in this house had never needed waking. They were kept warm. That was the whole architecture of the place, he thought, carrying his tea down the hall past the photographs without looking, without slowing, his eyes fixed on the wainscoting with the discipline of a monk: the house was a reliquary. Everything in it was warm so that nothing in it would have to speak.

* * *

In the music room again, he closed the Steinway and rested both hands flat on its lid, feeling for vibration out of pure habit, the way a doctor taps a knee. Nothing. The instrument was settled, stable, true to within a cent. It would hold until spring. It would hold, he suspected, for whoever came after, though he already knew—in the way he knew the pitch of a string before striking it—that there would be no spring tuning. This was the last time his tools would be welcome here. Houses change voices when they change hands. New owners bring new plumbers, new electricians, new men with clipboards who bleed the radiators and hear nothing, because they have no history with the walls. The ticking clock in the guest room would be "fixed" by some kid with a wrench who didn't know it was the house clearing its throat. The singing pipe in the parlor would be insulated into silence. And the branch behind the preserves—

His hand stilled on the piano lid.

The branch behind the preserves led into a wall that was newer than the foundation. He had known that for twenty years. He had bled every reachable radiator in this house and traced every run of pipe, and he had stood in the cold basement corner more times than he could count with his palm against that humming iron, and he had never once followed the question to its end. Where does the branch go? What is behind the concrete? Why does a house need a sealed room with heat in it?

He had never asked. Not Elena, not the blueprints, not the wall itself, though God knew the wall had offered. The pipe hummed when lies were told upstairs, and Xanthe had lived thirty years directly above it, and he understood now—with the tender dread of a man hearing his own diagnosis read aloud—that the pipe had not been reporting on the family.

It had been reporting on him. Every lie of omission, every not-asked question, every smooth correction of *Vasquez* back to *Marchetti* inside his own skull—the pipe had been keeping score of the tuner who tuned everything except himself.

Outside, the light failed all at once, the way October does it. Headlights swept the driveway—Sofia's car, back from the funeral home, back from arranging the last public event of her mother's life. He heard her key in the lock, heard the vestibule radiator give its single cough, heard her stop in the hall when she saw his case standing there.

"Xanthe?" Her voice was raw. "Is that—is someone here?"

"In here," he called, and hated how gentle he made it, how practiced, how much it sounded like a man who had been managing griefs professionally for three decades.

She appeared in the doorway with her coat still on, holding a folder of papers, and she looked at the closed piano, and at him, and something moved across her face that he recognized because he saw it in mirrors: the look of a person about to ask a question they have been trained their whole life not to ask.

"Did she—" Sofia began. Stopped. Started again. "When you tuned it. Did she seem... Did she know?"

The pipe in the basement, Xanthe was almost certain, had begun to hum.

"She knew everything," he said, which was true, and which was also, he understood with terrible clarity, the last honest sentence he would speak freely in this house for a very long time.
