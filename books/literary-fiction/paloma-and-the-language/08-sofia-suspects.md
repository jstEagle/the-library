## 8. Sofia Suspects

Sofia Marchetti had spent thirty-four years learning the geography of being overlooked, and she knew its borders better than anyone in the family. She was the youngest, the one born after the photographs stopped going up in the hallway, the one whose christening had been a Tuesday afternoon affair with store-bought cake while Marco's had filled the cathedral and spilled onto the steps. She was the spare part. The extra key cut for a door nobody locked. When her mother died, she cried at the funeral like everyone else, but underneath the grief ran something colder and more private: the realization that now there would be no one left to overlook her on purpose.

So she noticed things. That was the compensation. The overlooked become cartographers of other people's attention, and Sofia could chart the household's currents the way sailors once read stars—by what was absent, by where the light didn't fall.

Which is why, three days after the funeral, when she came back to the house at eleven in the morning to collect her mother's reading glasses from the kitchen windowsill, she did not go straight to the kitchen. She stood first in the front hall, listening.

The house had always talked. Her mother used to say it was just old pipes, but Sofia had grown up inside that voice and knew it wasn't random. There were nights the radiators ticked in patterns too regular for steam, and mornings when the water in the walls hummed a note so low you felt it in your teeth before you heard it. As a child she'd pressed her ear to the baseboard in the upstairs hall and told Marco there was someone living under the floor. He'd laughed and told her she was stupid, and her mother had come down the corridor with a face gone strange and still, and said, *Don't listen to the house, Sofia. It only repeats what it's been told.* Then she'd kissed the top of Sofia's head and walked away quickly, like a woman leaving a room before it could ask her something.

Sofia had never forgotten that. *It only repeats what it's been told.* Even at seven she'd understood her mother hadn't meant pipes.

Now, standing in the hall, she heard the basement door close. Softly—the particular softness of someone trying not to be heard, which is louder than any slam if you know how to listen. And then footsteps on the stairs, uneven, weighted on the left side, and she knew them instantly. Thirty years of those footsteps coming up from the basement with tuning tools, carrying the smell of rosin and machine oil through the kitchen. Xanthe Delacroix. The man who was furniture. The man who was weather. The man who had eaten at their counter for three decades and slept in the room over the garage and whose name her mother had never once said in full in Sofia's hearing—always just *him*, or *the tuner*, as though he'd been hired yesterday and might leave tomorrow.

He appeared at the top of the basement stairs with a flashlight in his hand, and the flashlight was wrong. Tuners didn't need flashlights at eleven in the morning. His shirt had dust on the shoulder, a pale stripe of it, the kind that comes from pressing against an old wall. And his hands—she saw this before he saw her—his hands were shaking, not the fine tremor of age but the coarse shake of a man who has touched something he shouldn't have.

He looked up. Saw her. And Sofia watched him do something remarkable: watch him decide, in real time, which version of himself to be. His face went through three expressions in under two seconds—alarm, calculation, then the smooth blankness she'd known all her life, the face of a man standing in a room without being in it.

"Sofia." He said her name carefully, like a word in a language he was still learning. "I didn't know anyone was here."

"I forgot my mother's glasses." She held up nothing; her hands were empty and they both knew it. "What were you doing in the basement?"

"Checking the lines. With the heat off since the funeral, the pipes can freeze if we get a cold snap." He moved past her toward the kitchen, unhurried, a man with nothing to hide and all the time in the world to hide it in. "I've done it every winter for thirty years."

"Not in November," Sofia said. "The heat doesn't go off until the estate lawyer signs off. I asked him myself last week."

He stopped walking. For a moment the blankness held, and then it didn't—it cracked along some seam she couldn't see, and underneath it was something tired and old and terribly honest. He set the flashlight down on the hall table with exaggerated care, the way you put down a thing you no longer trust yourself to hold.

"You were always the one who listened," he said quietly. "Your mother used to say that about you. That you heard everything and repeated nothing."

"My mother said a lot of things about me to other people that she never said to me."

"Yes." He almost smiled. "She did that."

They stood in the hallway beneath the photographs—the ones Sofia had been forbidden to ask about as a child, the ones of strangers in a sunlit courtyard, a woman holding a baby whose face had been turned deliberately toward the light, a group of men in front of a truck with a license plate worn to illegibility. Elena Marchetti had dusted those frames herself, every Sunday, and answered no questions about them, ever, and the family had learned to walk past them the way you walk past a grave you're pretending isn't there.

"The basement," Sofia said again. "There's a room down there, isn't there. Behind the wall with the new-looking plaster."

Xanthe went very still. It was the stillness of prey, but also—she recognized this with a small cold shock—the stillness of a co-conspirator caught mid-crime, a man deciding whether the person in front of him is a threat or a witness.

"Who told you about the wall?"

"Nobody told me. I grew up here. You think I never wondered why the basement is smaller than the footprint of the house? Why there's a vent in my brother's old bedroom that blows air from nowhere? I was a child, not an idiot. Children count things. Nobody thinks to lie to a child about square footage." She stepped closer. "You've known about that room. Haven't you. All these years."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them the radiator ticked, patient as a clock, and Sofia thought: *it only repeats what it's been told.*

"There are things in this house," Xanthe said finally, "that your mother kept the way you keep a wound closed. Not because the truth would hurt. Because opening it lets everything out at once, and you can't choose what comes."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

She made coffee because her hands needed occupation, and he sat at the counter—not the table, never the table, thirty years of habit—and she caught herself setting out two cups at the counter and then, in a small act of rebellion against her own upbringing, moving both to the table. He looked at the table, then at her, and something moved across his face like weather.

"Sit," she said. "Marco won't be here until four. Aurelio called again this morning. Marco hung up on him in the car and then drove around the block twice before coming into the office, so I'd say we have until at least three."

"You watched all that?"

"I told you. I listen." She sat across from him, and the table between them felt enormous and new, a border neither of them had ever crossed. "Start with the room. What's in it?"

"A child's bedroom." He said it flatly, but his thumb was moving along the rim of his cup, tracing circles, the way he did when he tuned a string he couldn't quite bring true. "Preserved. Bed, toys, a piano—a very small one, a child's instrument. Sheet music papered on the walls. Two languages."

"Whose child?"

"I don't know."

"Liar," Sofia said, without heat, almost gently. "That's the thing, Xanthe. I know what lies look like from the inside. I was raised on them. Not big ones—big lies get found out. Little structural ones. Load-bearing lies. The kind a whole house stands on. My mother lied about where we came from, about who we were, about why we had no cousins, no grandparents, no history older than 1978. She lied with such consistency that lying became the architecture. And when you grow up inside architecture, you learn to feel when a wall is hollow." She turned her cup. "You just gave me a load-bearing lie. 'I don't know.' Three words, perfectly flat, delivered half a beat too fast. You know exactly whose child."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a car passed, and the sound rose and fell like breath.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "I slept in my van in the parking lot of a church two streets from here. Your mother found me tuning the church piano for free, for the privilege of sleeping somewhere warm, and she brought me home. One condition. I never asked about the photographs in the hallway. Never asked about anything before 1978. She fed me, housed me, paid me fairly, treated me—" he paused, searching, "—treated me like a fixture. Useful. Present. Permanent. And invisible. And I took the bargain, Sofia, because belonging at any price is still belonging, and I told myself for thirty years that the price was silence, and silence is cheap, right? Silence costs nothing." His thumb stopped circling. "But it isn't silence. Silence is just the interest. The principal was everything I pretended not to see. Every year. Every photograph. Every time the pipes hummed at night and I lay in my room over the garage and knew—I *knew*—that this house was keeping something, and I rolled over and went to sleep."

"What did she keep?"

He shook his head slowly. "If I tell you, you can't unknow it. And right now you can still be her daughter who grieves cleanly. I've seen what happens to people who open that wall."

"Show me instead," Sofia said. "Then it's your conscience, not your mouth."

Something flickered in him—surprise, maybe, or recognition. The look of a man meeting, unexpectedly, another member of a club he'd believed he belonged to alone.

"You really are the one who listens," he said.

"I'm the one nobody bothered to lie to well." She stood, carried her cup to the sink, and turned back. "My whole life, everyone protected me from the truth the way you protect a child from a hot stove, and meanwhile they let Marco run the business, let Marco handle the money, let Marco become whatever our mother built him into. Spare parts don't need explanations. Well." She dried her hands on a towel that had hung there since before she could remember, her mother's towel, faded roses, and folded it with unnecessary precision. "I'm not asking as a spare part. I'm asking as the person who's going to inherit whatever's behind that wall whether anyone shows it to me or not. Because when the lawyers finish picking through this house, that room becomes evidence, Xanthe. Someone will find it. Better it's us."

He stared at her. Then he laughed—a short, startled sound, rusty, like a hinge unused for decades.

"Your mother said something once," he told her. "Late, near the end. She was sitting at this table and I was tuning the piano in the next room and she said, mostly to herself, 'The quiet ones hear everything. God help whoever mistakes them for deaf.'" He set down his cup. "I think she was talking about you."

"She was talking about herself," Sofia said. "And about you. And she knew it, and she still kept you both quiet, because quiet people are useful." She met his eyes. "I'm offering you the chance to be useful differently."

For a moment he didn't move. Then he reached into his shirt pocket and set something on the table between them: a letter, soft at the corners from handling, addressed in his employer's unmistakable hand. *To whoever keeps my instruments.*

"She left this to me," Xanthe said. "Not to Marco. Not to you. To the tuner. Which means she knew, at the end, exactly who in this house could be trusted with a sentence." He slid it an inch toward her, then stopped, his fingers still resting on the envelope. "There's a line in it I can't read. Not can't decipher—can't translate. It's in a language that doesn't exist anymore, or exists in pieces, in a village that doesn't exist anymore either. And there's a man named Aurelio Vasquez who says that sentence can give this family back its name or take away everything it has. And your brother wants me to burn it."

Sofia looked at the envelope. Under her palm, faintly, she felt the radiator in the wall begin to tick—steady, patient, keeping time like something waiting.

"And what do you want?" she asked.

"I want to stop being furniture," Xanthe said.

Sofia picked up the letter, turned it over once in her hands, and set it back down between them like a card played face-up.

"Then show me the wall," she said, "before Marco gets home."
