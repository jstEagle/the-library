## 12. What Sofia Finds Upstairs

My mother's closet smelled of cedar and camphor the way it had smelled my whole life, and I had never once been allowed inside it.

That was not a rule anyone spoke aloud. It was one of those rules that lived in the architecture of the house itself, in the way Marco's voice tightened when he said *Mamá's things*, in the way Ana averted her eyes from the bedroom door as if it were a mirror she didn't want to catch her reflection in. Thirty years of living under this roof and I had learned to read our family the way Xanthe read radiators—by pressure, by temperature, by the hum that meant someone was lying two rooms away.

So when I opened the closet three days after the funeral, alone, on a Tuesday afternoon while Marco was at the lawyer's office and Ana was at hers, I understood exactly what kind of sound I was making. A wrong one. A lying one. The house knew it too; somewhere below me a pipe gave a low, disapproving note, like a cello string touched by accident.

I went in anyway.

Grief does strange things to the obedient. My whole life I had been the spare part—the third child, the afterthought, the daughter whose name Elena sometimes got wrong in her letters, writing *Sofía* with an accent no one else in the family used, as if I belonged to some older version of us that had been packed away with the winter coats. When she died, I expected to feel orphaned. Instead I felt *unlocked*. All the doors I had spent fifty years not opening were suddenly just doors.

The cedar chests were full of what I expected: linens monogrammed with an M that might have stood for Marchetti or might have stood for something else; dresses in the heavy wools of a country I'd never seen; shoeboxes of photographs, all of them either faceless—backs turned, heads cropped—or so old the faces had gone the color of weak tea. I knelt on the closet floor in my mother's dress and felt like a burglar in a museum of myself.

It was the hatbox that stopped me.

Not because hats were unusual. Elena had owned forty hats and worn none of them in the years I could remember. But this box was heavier than hats, and when I lifted the lid, the smell that rose out of it was not cedar or camphor but dust and warm plastic and something faintly sweet, like the inside of an old radio.

Reel-to-reel tapes. Seven of them, each in its own cardboard sleeve, each labeled in my mother's small, slanted hand—not Spanish, not English, but that other alphabet I had seen only twice in my life: once on the spine of a book she kept locked in her nightstand drawer until the year I turned twelve, and once, briefly, on a Christmas card from a woman in Santiago whose name Marco made her stop answering.

The labels were numbered. One through seven. No titles. Just numbers, and beneath each number, a single word in that lost script, written so carefully it looked traced rather than written, as if my mother had copied it from memory across decades without ever letting her hand forget.

I sat down on the floor of the closet with the hatbox in my lap and did the arithmetic of my childhood. All those evenings when Elena sent us to bed early and stayed downstairs alone. All those mornings when the kitchen radio was off but her lips were moving. All those times I came around a corner and found her standing still in the middle of a room, listening to something that wasn't there anymore—and when I asked what, she would say, *the pipes, mija,* and smile, and I would believe her, because believing her was the rent we all paid to live in this house.

Except Xanthe. Xanthe paid a different rent. I understood that now, though I couldn't have said how.

* * *

There was a reel-to-reel machine in the house. Of course there was; there was a reel-to-reel machine in every closet of my childhood, the way there was a piano tuner in every room of my memory. Ours lived in the den cabinet beneath the record player, a Sony with a cracked cassette deck and a reel attachment Marco had tried to throw away twice and Elena had rescued twice, saying only, *it works.* It worked. Everything in this house worked long past the point where anyone admitted why it needed to.

I carried the tapes down one at a time, cradling them like eggs, feeling absurd and criminal and more alive than I had felt in years. The afternoon light came through the den windows the color of weak coffee. Outside, the gardeners Marco had hired to make the lawn presentable for the estate appraisal mowed back and forth in their slow green orbit, and their engines covered whatever sounds I made, which felt like permission.

Threading the tape took me four tries. My hands weren't steady. When the reels finally caught and the motor engaged, the speaker produced first a hiss like the sea, then a pop, then—

A voice. My mother's voice, young. Younger than me. Younger than Marco. A voice from before the house, before the name, before any of us—a voice singing.

I want to tell you I recognized the song. I didn't. That's the thing about an erased language: you can be raised inside its absence your entire life and never know the shape of the hole. What I heard was a melody, simple and looping, the kind of tune that gets sung over cradles because it must be singable by candlelight, by someone exhausted, by someone afraid. And underneath the melody, words in a tongue that was almost Spanish the way a river is almost a road—it moved through the same country but nothing crossed it directly. Every syllable bent somewhere I couldn't follow.

Elena's recorded voice sang the verse through, then again, slower, the way a teacher repeats a lesson. Then she stopped singing and spoke, in accented English, as if dictating to herself:

*"Verse one. For the baby. Again."*

And she sang it again.

I played it three times before I could breathe properly. It wasn't just that she sounded young. It was that she sounded *unafraid*. This was a woman who had not yet built the fortress, who had not yet learned that survival meant sanding yourself down and repainting the door. On the tape she was maybe twenty-two, twenty-three, laughing a little between takes at her own mistakes, and the laugh was a stranger's laugh, and it was also, unmistakably, mine. I have been told since I was six that I laugh exactly like my mother. I had never once heard her really laugh.

By tape four the laughter was gone. By tape five her spoken interjections had shifted—fewer instructions, more pauses, long silences where the hiss filled the room like weather. On tape six, recorded clearly years later—I could hear the age in her throat, the same grain that had been in her voice at Christmas last year—she sang the lullaby one final time, complete, all the verses, unhurried, as if she were laying it into the machine the way you lay flowers on a grave. And at the end of the final verse she sang a line I hadn't heard before, a refrain that returned like a chorus returns, and then she whispered something after it, half-spoken, half-sung, and stopped the tape mid-hiss.

I sat in the darkening den with my hands flat on my knees and realized I was crying without any of the usual machinery of crying—no sobbing, no decision, just water leaving my eyes the way heat leaves pipes, silently, through joints no one inspects.

Because I knew the refrain. Not the words. The *shape* of it. The rise and fall of it, the way it circled back on itself like a hand closing.

I had heard that shape two nights ago, read aloud off a page by a man who didn't understand what he was reading.

* * *

Xanthe answered the door of the guesthouse on the second knock. He looked worse than he had at the funeral—thinner somehow, though people don't thin in three days; it's something behind the eyes that goes. He had ink on his fingers and a dictionary open on the table behind him, and when he saw the tapes in my arms, every muscle in his face performed the same maneuver at once: hope, terror, and the polite blankness our family had taught him the way other families teach table manners.

"Sofia," he said. "Is something wrong with the piano?"

"The piano's fine." I stepped past him into the guesthouse, which I had entered perhaps ten times in thirty years and never once uninvited. "I need to play you something."

He didn't ask how I'd found them. I noticed that. He didn't ask *what are those*, didn't perform ignorance, didn't do any of the things a man with nothing to hide would naturally do. He simply closed the door behind me and stood there with his hands at his sides, waiting, the way he waited whenever any of us approached him—as if approach itself were a weather system he'd learned to stand very still inside.

"I went into her closet," I said. "Marco will call it stealing. I'm calling it inheritance. There's a difference and I've decided I get to decide which is which."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Approval, maybe, from a man who had spent thirty years deciding nothing.

I threaded tape six onto his machine—it was better than ours, older, a Revox with the weight of a small engine—and I watched him while it played. I wanted to watch him more than I wanted to listen, honestly, because I had already listened, and listening alone had nearly taken me apart. He stood with his head tilted, that professional tilt I'd seen ten thousand times, the tilt he gave pianos and radiators and, I realized now, all of us. Listening with his hands even though his hands were empty. His eyes closed at the first verse. At the refrain they opened, and I saw it happen—the recognition, arriving not as thought but as physics, the way a tuning fork finds its twin across a room and starts to shiver.

"Stop it," he said quietly. "Play the refrain again."

I rewound by feel, guessing at the counter numbers, and set the needle down. The hiss. The sea. Then her voice, rising into the refrain, circling, closing like a hand:

*Xanthe's lips moved. Not speaking—reading. Reading sound the way I had learned to read silence.*

"That's it," he said. His voice had gone strange, stripped of its usual careful evenness. "That's the sentence. From the letter. Word for word."

"You're sure?"

"Sofia." He turned to look at me fully, and for the first time in thirty years he looked *at* me instead of near me, and I understood that this, too, was a kind of translation—he had finally found the frequency I broadcast on. "I have spent three days with dictionaries in five languages trying to find that sentence. It isn't in any of them. It isn't in *any* language, not the way we mean languages. It's—" He stopped, pressed his palms together, started again. "Your mother wrote the letter in a dialect that died with a village. But she *sang* it here. She left the pronunciation. She left the music. She—" and here his voice actually broke, a small clean fracture, quickly repaired, "—she left the answer inside a song, because she knew. She knew the one person who'd keep looking wouldn't be able to read it. She knew he'd have to *hear* it."

"She knew you'd find them," I said.

"No." He shook his head slowly. "She knew I'd need help finding them. There's a difference. There's always been a difference." He looked down at the turning reels, at my mother's young-old voice filling his small rooms, and said, almost to himself: "Sound was always going to be the way home. I just never thought she'd known that about me."

We played the tape through twice more. The second time, he hummed. I had never heard Xanthe hum. It was like watching a locked room open from the inside. His humming found the melody's center and held it, and when the refrain came he didn't sing the words—he couldn't, none of us could, not yet—but he shaped them, mouthed their contours, gave them breath without language, and I swear the radiator in the corner of his guesthouse ticked in time. I know how that sounds. I know what you're thinking. But I grew up in that house, and I am telling you: the house was listening too.

When the tape ran out and the reels spun empty, neither of us moved to lift the needle. The hiss went on like surf, like breathing, like the sound a family makes when it has forgotten how to speak and remembers, all at once, that it used to sing.

"There are seven," I said finally. "Six are songs. But the seventh—" I pulled it from the box and held it out to him, the cardboard sleeve soft with age, the number seven traced in that vanished alphabet. "On the sixth one, after the last refrain, she whispers something. I can't make it out. And Xanthe—the label on this one isn't a word. It's a date. March, 1976."

He took the tape from me with both hands, the way you take something breakable, the way you take something true.

"Then we'd better listen," he said, "before Marco finds out we have."

Outside, the lawnmowers had stopped. In the sudden quiet we could hear the main house plainly, every pipe, every joint, every held breath—and somewhere upstairs, a door we had all agreed for thirty years not to see, standing open.
