## 11. Words for Weather

There is a word in the pidgin for the sea when it is thinking about becoming cruel but has not yet decided: *maralung*. It is not a warning exactly. It is the sea caught in the act of considering, and the fishermen who used it would look at the water, say *maralung* to no one in particular, and begin pulling their nets in without hurry, because hurry was itself a kind of insult to the sea — as if you believed your panic could outrun its patience.

I taught Kestrel that word on a Tuesday, with my hands shaking so badly I had to press them flat against my knees to make them stop.

He did not notice. Or he noticed and said nothing, which is its own fluency. He sat across from me on the bridge deck with his clipboard balanced on one thigh, and he repeated *maralung* three times, getting it wrong twice — too much weight on the second syllable, the way an engineer puts too much weight on everything — and then right, and when he got it right something moved through his face like weather over water, there and gone before you could name it.

"Again," I said.

"*Maralung.*"

"The sea deciding," I said. "Not yet cruel. Not yet kind. Deciding."

"That's a lot of meaning for two syllables."

"It's a lot of sea for one horizon." I let him write it down. He wrote everything down now, in a small notebook with a cracked spine, and I had stopped pretending this offended me. A language kept only in a dying woman's mouth is a candle in wind. A language in a notebook is at least a candle in a jar. "Now the silences."

His pen paused. "Silences have words?"

"In this tongue they have more words than anything else. The traders who built it needed forty ways to say *the deal is off* and only three ways to say *yes*, because yes is simple and refusal is an ocean." I looked out at the water while I spoke, because looking at him while speaking of refusals was more than I wanted to spend that morning. "You know *silu* already. The silence after a question that will be answered no."

"*Silu,*" he said. "The silence that answers."

"And *siluvane*. The silence between people who used to speak and don't anymore. It's longer than *silu*. It can last years." I watched him absorb that. I watched him understand that I was not teaching vocabulary; I was handing him mirrors and calling them windows. Twelve years of *siluvane*, sitting in this man like ballast. "And *silumarra* — the silence of the sea itself, when the fog comes down and the boats stay tied and the whole village holds its breath together. That one we used every winter. That one I miss."

"You miss a silence?"

"I miss being inside a silence with other people. Alone, a silence is just a room with the furniture taken out." My voice did something then — thinned, frayed at the edge — and I heard it happen from a little distance, the way you hear someone else's voice on a recording, and I thought: *ah. There it is. So soon.*

Because here is what the doctors' letters say, the ones going soft and yellow in my basket under the unopened weight of their envelopes: they say weeks, or they say months, and they say it in a language with far fewer words for weather than mine. They say *decline*. They say *trajectory*. They do not say what I know, which is that my body has become a tide chart, each day the water reaching a little further up the sand and taking a little more away, and no engineer alive can inspect these particular tendons and tell me how much load remains.

I was tired. That was the truth of that Tuesday, under all the grammar. Tired in the bones the way the pier is tired, in ways that do not show until they are catastrophic.

So I did the thing I had been circling for a week the way a gull circles a boat.

"Give me your hand," I said.

He gave it. His hand was cold — bridge-cold, metal-cold, a man who lives outdoors and never quite warms through — and into it I placed the recorder. Small, black, older than my diagnosis. Marisol bought it for me two years ago when I told her I wanted to record songs, which was true the way a map is true: accurate, and not the point.

"Hold this," I said. "Don't set it down. If you set it down, your hands will be free, and free hands fidget, and fidgeting makes noise, and noise ruins a recording."

"What am I recording?"

"Me." I settled back in my folding chair. The canvas creaked its familiar complaint. Below us the tide was doing its slow gray arithmetic against the pilings. "Everything. Today and every lesson after. You will hold the machine and I will fill it, and when I am done filling it, you will carry it."

"Paloma—"

"Do not *Paloma* me in that voice. I have heard that voice from doctors and priests and one very persistent woman from the cultural ministry, and it always means the same thing: *are you sure you want to talk about this now.* Yes. Now. A language dies at the speed of its last speaker's breath, Kestrel. Do you understand what that means? It means this tongue is currently traveling at the speed of my lungs, and my lungs are—" I stopped. Chose a different road. "It means we record today."

He held the machine. To his credit, his hands were steady, steadier than mine, and I thought: good. Let the student's hands be steady even if the teacher's are not. That is the whole arrangement of teaching, reduced to its skeleton.

I began where all recordings should begin, at the beginning, though my beginning is sixty years deep and mostly salt.

"This is Paloma Reyes," I said to the little black eye of the machine, "speaking Merrow Point pidgin, on the bridge, in October, with my student. His name is Kestrel Yarrow. Write that down somewhere else too, whoever hears this. Write down that he came."

Beside me, the man who came went very still.

I filled the next hour the way you fill a jar with river stones — largest things first. The counting words, because numbers outlive everything. The kinship words, because a language that cannot say *my brother's daughter* is already dead. Then the weather words, all of them, in order of the sea's moods, and I made him repeat each one into the machine after me so that the recording holds both voices, the failing one and the learning one, the way a rope holds two ends.

"*Luma* — glass calm. The sea like a floor you're afraid to walk on. *Sereva* — the small chop that means fish underneath. *Maralung* — deciding. *Vessik* — whitecaps in lines, wind coming from the southwest, three hours to hard weather. *Dolmarra* — the sea in a storm, from *dol*, fury, and *marra*, mother. Fury-mother. Our grandmothers named that storm and they were not wrong about it. *Kelvane* — the sea after, the exhausted sea, the long gray swells like a woman breathing after labor."

"After labor," he repeated.

"Every word in this language was coined by people who worked on or beside or against the water, and most of them were women waiting on shore, and waiting women name things precisely." I took a breath and felt the cost of it arrive like an invoice. "Now the silences again. Into the machine this time. *Silu.*"

"*Silu.*"

"*Siluvane.*"

"*Siluvane.*" And then, quietly, not into the machine but to me: "That's the one I've been living in. Isn't it. All these weeks. You've been teaching me the word for what's between us."

"The word existed long before you," I said. "You merely rented it."

Something almost like a laugh moved in him and died young. He looked at the recorder in his palm as if it might bite. "Why do you want my voice on this? It's your language. Shouldn't it be your voice only?"

"A language recorded in one voice is a specimen. A language recorded in two voices is a conversation, and conversations are how tongues actually live." I turned to face him fully then, and I will admit my eyes were not what they were; the world had begun arriving slightly softened, as if seen through *kelvane* weather. "Also, I am not going to be here to correct your pronunciation forever, and the machine needs to hear what wrong sounds like, so it knows the difference."

"That's harsh pedagogy."

"I'm a harsh pedagogue. Ask my brother." The words left me before I had voted on them, and I watched them land on him like gulls landing on a rail — sudden, unhoused, refusing to leave. He knew about Tomás. Of course he knew; the whole village's grief is a tide table everyone can read. But hearing the name spoken aloud, casually, in the middle of a lesson, between *dolmarra* and *kelvane* — that was different, and I watched it be different. His thumb pressed against the recorder until the plastic clicked.

"Say the next word," I said, gently, because mercy in small doses is still medicine.

He said the next word.

* * *

We met again on Friday, and again the following Tuesday, and by the third recording session I had stopped pretending to myself that I was teaching him weather words. I was building him a vessel. Every session I loaded it further — grammar in the hull, vocabulary in the hold, the songs wedged in last like cargo that must not shift. And every session my body took its tithe. The walk from the inn to the bridge midpoint, which in June had cost me nothing, now required two rests and a certain pride-swallowing. My cough had developed a wet, deep register, like something knocking from underwater. In the evenings I sat in my kitchen with Marisol's soup going cold and rehearsed, aloud, to no one, the words I still owned, checking them like coins, making sure none had slipped out of my purse.

On the fourth recording day the fog came down — proper *silumarra* weather, the whole village holding its breath together — and Kestrel arrived early, which I noted, and brought a thermos of coffee he'd made himself, which I noted harder. He poured mine without being asked. He had learned how I take it. These are the small currencies of company, and I had told myself I wanted none of them, and I had lied, and the lie had gotten heavy.

"Today," I said, "we record the confession words."

The fog pressed close around us. On the span above, a truck crossed and the whole structure sang its low iron note, and we both waited for it to finish, the way you wait out a bell.

"There are no confession words," he said. "You told me. No word for sorry. Only return-words."

"Correct. And today you learn the hardest of them, and you learn it properly, because I have watched you carry that recorder for three weeks like a man carrying a live coal, and I know what you are working up to, and I would rather you say it well than say it soon." I leaned forward. My hands were in my lap, folded, holding each other the way old friends do at funerals. "*Anveli.* Say it."

"*Anveli.*"

"It means: I return what I took, changed. Not restored — changed. The pidgin is honest about this. What you take from a person does not come back the same shape. Tomás's boat does not come back. What returns is the debt itself, transformed, carried in the taker's body until it finds its new form." I watched his face do its weather. "When you finally say what you came here twelve years too late to say, you will begin with *anveli*. Not because it excuses. Because it tells the truth about returning: that the thing given back is never the thing that was lost, and the giver knows it, and says so."

"And if there's nothing I can give back?"

"Then you give forward." I said it simply, because it is simple; it took me eighty years to see that simplicity is not the same as ease. "A debt with nowhere to land becomes a current. It moves. You will find what it moves toward."

He wrote *anveli* in his notebook, pressing hard enough that the page beneath showed the ghost of it. Then he did something I had not seen him do. He turned the recorder over in his hands, looked at it, and said, "Can I ask you something off the record?"

"The machine doesn't judge. Ask."

"Why me? You could have chosen anyone. Marisol would learn. Half the village would sit with you. You chose the man who—" He stopped himself on the cliff-edge of the word he has never once said in my presence. Betrayal. The pidgin doesn't need it; neither, apparently, does he.

I let the *silu* stand between us for a while — the silence that answers no. Then I answered anyway, because I am old enough to know that some questions deserve better than silence even when silence is the honest answer.

"Because a debt collected from anyone else is just money," I said. "Collected from you, it's a return. And because I am dying, Kestrel, and dying women do not have time to be generous, and do not have time to be cruel either. We only have time for what works. You owe this language a speaker. This language owes me a future. You are the arithmetic that balances." I reached out and touched the recorder in his hands, one finger, briefly, the way you touch a sleeping child to confirm the breath. "Hold it steady. We continue."

But that night, alone in my kitchen with the fog still sealed against the windows, I understood something I had been declining to understand: the lessons were ending sooner than the bridge. My body had begun keeping its own schedule, and it was not consulting me. When I climbed my stairs I counted the steps the way I once counted waves, and when I reached the top I stood a long time in the dark, listening to my own lungs travel at the speed of a dying language.

In the morning I sent Kestrel a message through the inn's landlady, six words, and I chose them carefully, because six words is all a message needs when the seventh would be fear:

*Go to the bridge tonight. Alone.*

Let him stand on that span in the dark with his readings and his conscience and his two versions of the truth. Let him meet the middle of the bridge the way you meet the middle of a life — with no one watching, and everything at stake. I have taught him the words for weather. Now let him learn the one thing no recording can hold:

what the sea decides, when it decides.
