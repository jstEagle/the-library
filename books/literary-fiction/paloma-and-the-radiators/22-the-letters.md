## 22. The Letters

Pia had been back in the house for eleven days when she finally made herself go upstairs.

Not to the second floor, where her childhood bedroom still held its museum of a girl who'd left at twenty-six and never sent so much as a postcard — the swim-team ribbons, the poster of a horse that no one had taken down because taking it down would have meant admitting she wasn't coming back. Not even to her mother's room, which Pia visited each morning with a cup of tea and each evening with a lie about how well Yeva was sleeping.

The sealed room. The third-floor room at the end of the hall that Marco had told her — in his voice of reasonable men everywhere — was "just storage, nothing up there but boxes and dust."

Pia Marchetti had spent nine years learning to hear what her brother's sentences were built to hide. He said *storage* the way he said *inevitable* and *for Mom's own good* and *the doctors agree*: words laid down like tarps over furniture you didn't want anyone looking under.

So on the eleventh day, while Marco was at the demolition office arguing about timelines he couldn't change, and while their mother napped in the blue chair with the television murmuring its afternoon soap operas to her closed eyes, Pia climbed the stairs to the third floor.

* * *

The door was painted the same white as all the others, but it wore its paint differently. The other doors in the house had been opened ten thousand times; their paint was scuffed at the handle-height of generations, dinged by doorknobs and shoulders and once, according to family legend, by a bicycle that eight-year-old Marco had attempted to ride indoors. This door's paint was unbroken. It had the smooth, sealed look of a jar lid, of an envelope flap, of anything that had decided long ago never to be opened again.

There was no lock. That was the strange part. No lock, no bolt, not even a latch that stuck. Just a door that everyone in the house had agreed, wordlessly and for decades, to walk past without seeing.

Pia put her hand on the knob. It turned with the small complaint of metal that hadn't moved since the last century.

"You're allowed," she said out loud, to herself, or possibly to the door. Her therapist — the one she'd stopped seeing two years ago, right around the time she'd stopped answering Marco's calls — used to say that Pia narrated her own courage because she didn't trust anyone else to witness it. Fine. She would witness herself.

The door opened onto darkness and the smell of forty years: dust, yes, but underneath it something sweeter and sadder, like cedar gone soft, like paper aging in the dark, like a coat that still remembered someone's shoulders.

She found the light switch by feel. Nothing happened. Of course. Sometime in the distant past, someone — Marco? Their grandfather? — had unscrewed the bulb or cut the circuit, so that even flipping a switch wouldn't wake the room. Pia took out her phone and turned on the flashlight, and the room assembled itself out of the dark in pieces.

A bed, made up with a quilt gone the color of weak tea. A wardrobe, door ajar, holding a man's clothes: shirts with collars softened by decades of washing, a suit jacket that would fit no one alive now, shoes with their laces still tied, as if the wearer had stepped out of them mid-thought and simply never come back. A washstand. A chair. And against the far wall, under a sheet so thick with dust it looked like a snowdrift —

A piano.

Pia stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the flashlight wander. She had expected storage. Boxes, luggage, the broken chairs every old house accumulates in its attic like plaque in an artery. She had not expected a room that looked less abandoned than *paused*. As if the man who lived here had stepped out to buy cigarettes in 1983 and the whole house had been holding its breath ever since.

"Tomas," she said, just to test the name in the air of this house that had refused to say it aloud for longer than Pia had been alive.

The dust motes swirled in her flashlight beam like the room exhaling.

* * *

It was the trunk that gave them up.

She went first to the wardrobe, because clothes are the most human evidence there is, and touched the sleeve of the suit jacket, and felt how fine the wool was, and thought: *he dressed well for a man nobody was supposed to know existed.* In the jacket pocket she found a ticket stub — a cinema, 1979, two seats purchased — and a slip of paper with a phone number written in a woman's hand, a hand Pia recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water.

Her mother's handwriting. Younger, rounder, more confident than the shaky script that now signed checks Marco pre-filled for her, but unmistakably Yeva Marchetti's.

Pia sat down on the paused bed and let that sit in her chest for a minute. Two seats. Her mother had gone to the cinema with him. Had sat in the dark next to him with her coat folded in her lap like any woman in love, and had walked home afterward along streets where she could not be seen doing it, and had kept the stub in a pocket of a jacket hanging in a room that did not officially exist.

Then the trunk. An old steamer trunk at the foot of the bed, brass fittings green with age, unlocked. Inside, under a layer of yellowed tissue paper:

Letters. Bundles of letters, tied with string, dozens of them, hundreds maybe, arranged with a care that hurt to look at — sorted by year, she realized, sorted and bundled and kept the way other people keep photographs. All addressed in the same careful hand. All addressed to *Y. M.*

And beneath the bundles, a shoebox, and inside the shoebox, photographs: a man with dark hair going gray at the temples, standing outside a shopfront whose sign Pia could half-read in the faded print. A man laughing at somebody behind the camera. A man, in the last photo, older, alone on a bench, looking at the lens with an expression Pia knew intimately because she saw it in her own face every time she passed a mirror — the expression of a person who has been patient so long that patience has become the whole architecture of the face.

She picked up the topmost letter. The envelope was soft as cloth from handling. Postmarked — she checked, though some part of her already knew — from an address four blocks away. Four blocks. He had lived four blocks away for most of Pia's childhood, close enough that they might have passed him at the greengrocer's, at the bus stop, at the corner where the accordion player used to set up on Saturdays. Close enough that he might have watched her grow up from a distance, this man her mother loved, this ghost the family had sealed into a room and then sealed out of every conversation.

Pia unfolded the letter with hands that were not quite steady.

*My Yeva,*

*I saw you today at the market. You had Lena's stroller — no, forgive me, I don't know the baby's name yet, Marco's little one. You had the stroller and you stopped to look at the apricots and you did not buy any, and I thought: she is counting what she cannot afford, or she is remembering something, or she is simply happy looking, and all three of these are things I love about you.*

*I am not writing to ask you for anything. I want to say that plainly, at the start, so you can read the rest without your guard up. I know what they asked of you. I know what you chose, and I have never once believed it was a betrayal of me, whatever your brother says when he sees me on the street and crosses to the other side. You chose your children. There is no version of me that would have asked you to do otherwise, and there is no version of me that will stop loving you for having done it.*

*But I will keep writing, if you'll allow it. Not to change your mind. Only so that somewhere in this city, on paper, in ink, there is a record that you were loved. That you are loved. Houses fall down, Yeva. Paper waits.*

*Three streets away, always,*
*Tomas*

Pia read it twice. Then she read it a third time, and on the third reading her vision blurred and she had to press the heel of her hand against her eyes and breathe the way she'd learned to breathe in waiting rooms and courtrooms and the long silences after her father's funeral, when she was fourteen and already understood that her family processed grief the way a radiator processes steam: quietly, efficiently, converting everything to heat that warmed no one.

*He waited.* That was the sentence that broke her open. Not *I waited* — not grievance, not the wounded arithmetic of a man tallying years — but *he waited*, stated like weather, like gravity, like a fact of the world that required no apology. He had written to a woman who could not answer him — could she? Had she answered? Pia flipped through the bundles and found, tucked among his letters, a thin handful in her mother's hand, unsent, drafts maybe, or letters written and never mailed because mailing them would have made it real enough for her brother to find. One began *My Tomas, the apricots were for you, I didn't know how to say—*

Unsent. Forty years of unsent answers sitting three feet from forty years of delivered devotion.

Pia laughed, once, a short wet sound with no humor in it, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

* * *

She photographed everything.

This was the practical part, and Pia was her father's daughter as much as her mother's, and her father had been an accountant who believed that grief you couldn't document was grief you'd lose in an audit. She worked methodically, kneeling on the dusty floorboards with her phone, photographing each envelope front and back, each page, each bundle label, each photograph in the shoebox. The flash lit the room in white pulses, over and over, like a small stubborn lightning storm, and Pia thought: *this is what evidence looks like. This is what it looks like when a family decides a man died without ever having died.*

He had died three streets away. She found that letter too, near the bottom of the last bundle, in a different hand — a neighbor's, or a friend's, informing Y. M. that Tomás Ferreira had passed on the fourth of March, quietly, in his apartment on Delgado Street, and that he had left instructions concerning certain papers, and that his regards should be conveyed, and that he had spoken of her at the end, and that the writer hoped this would be a comfort.

*A comfort.* Pia set that letter down very carefully, as if it were made of eggshell, and stared at the wall.

He had died three streets away and the family had held a funeral for no one, and her mother had worn black for a week and told everyone it was for a cousin, and Marco — Marco had been nineteen, Marco had carried groceries past Delgado Street his whole life, Marco must have known, must have—

No. She stopped herself. She didn't know what Marco knew. That was the thing about arrangements: everyone got assigned a piece of not-knowing, and carried it so long it became indistinguishable from character. Marco's competence. Her own leaving. Her mother's silence. All of it load-bearing.

But here was the thing Pia understood now, kneeling in the dust with her phone full of photographs and her heart going like a trapped bird: arrangements only hold while nobody reads the paperwork. And she was holding the paperwork. Every page of it, photographed, timestamped, backed up to a cloud account Marco didn't have the password to.

Because the movers were coming. Because the block was coming down in five weeks, and this room — this paused, breathing, faithful room — was scheduled to become rubble and a line item in a demolition report. *Contents cleared.* Contents. A man's whole patient life, reduced to a verb.

Over her dead body. Which was, she reflected, wryly, exactly the kind of dramatic declaration the Marchettis specialized in and then failed to act on. Well. She had left this family once rather than become a piece of its furniture. She knew how to leave. What she was only now learning was how to stay and fight.

* * *

She was retying the last bundle — she'd return the letters to the trunk exactly as she'd found them; let the room keep its pause a little longer — when she heard the floorboard creak in the hallway.

Pia froze, phone still glowing in her hand.

"Pia?" Marco's voice, from the foot of the stairs. Heavy, tired, the voice of a man who had spent the afternoon losing politely to a demolition office. "You up there?"

For one absurd moment she considered hiding, crouching in the dark like a teenager, and the absurdity of it — hiding in her own mother's house, from her own brother, beside a piano that officially did not exist — almost made her laugh again.

"In a minute," she called back, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

She looked around the room one last time in the phone's light. The tied shoes. The made bed. The sheeted piano waiting under its forty-year snowdrift, and she thought suddenly of the tuner — Odile, quiet Odile with her listening face, who heard histories in pipes and radiators, who had looked at this house the first day like it was humming to her. *If anyone alive can wake that piano,* Pia thought, *it's her.*

She switched off the flashlight, closed the door softly behind her, and started down the stairs toward her brother, carrying her secret the way her mother had carried hers for forty years — lightly, carefully, and with the growing, terrifying certainty that it was going to change everything.
