## 33. After the Question

The room did not feel like a tomb anymore. That was the first thing Yeva noticed, sitting on the little stool by the second piano with her hands in her lap like a girl at her confirmation. For forty years the room had been a held breath. Now the breath had gone out of it, and what remained was only air — dusty, ordinary, smelling of cold wool and old varnish and, faintly, of the lemon polish Odile had used on the piano keys that morning without being asked.

Freed and hollowed. That was how it felt. Like a house after the furniture goes. Like a bell after the ringing.

She had expected to feel shame. She had rehearsed shame her whole life; she knew its posture the way she knew the posture of grief, the way she knew how to stand at a stove and stir a pot while her heart did its private arithmetic. But shame did not come. What came instead was a strange lightness, almost insulting in its mildness, as if the enormous stone she had been carrying all these years had turned out, once set down, to be made of papier-mâché and habit.

*You chose fear, and called it duty, and let them build on it.* The girl had said it gently. That was the remarkable thing. Odile Coldharbour, who could hear an argument in a radiator, had asked the question the way you'd ask someone to pass the salt — as if the answer were an ordinary thing, as if Yeva were an ordinary woman who had simply misplaced forty years.

And Yeva had answered. And the ceiling had not fallen. And the house had not objected. Somewhere below, in the pipes, the steam had moved through its old channels with its usual complaints, indifferent to the demolition of an empire.

* * *

Odile found her there an hour later, still sitting, and did not say *are you all right*, which was a mercy. She said instead, in her flat tuner's voice that Yeva had learned to hear the warmth underneath like a coal under ash: "The E above middle C is a quarter-tone sharp. It has been since 1983, I'd guess. Do you want me to leave it?"

Yeva almost laughed. "Leave it. Tomas always played it sharp. He said the piano was arguing with him and he let it win."

"I'll leave it, then." Odile folded her cloth with the precision of a surgeon. "It can argue with whoever plays it next."

"Next." Yeva tasted the word. "You say *next* like there will be one."

"There's a whole block of people who heard the piano through the walls last week, Mrs. Marchetti. Half of them probably thought it was a ghost. The other half are going to want lessons."

"Yeva." She said it before she could stop herself. "You have put your hands inside my piano and my family. I think you may call me Yeva."

Odile went very still, the way she did when a word landed somewhere she hadn't prepared a shelf for. Then she nodded once, and Yeva saw — she had gotten good at seeing, these last months — that the girl was holding something enormous with both hands and refusing to let it show.

"Yeva," Odile said, and the name in her mouth had a slight accent, the Lumbra vowel, the one that opened like a door. She had been practicing. The girl had been practicing Yeva's own name in the dark of her own kitchen, alone, for no one.

That was when Yeva knew what she had to do.

* * *

She made Odile fetch the notebook. The good one, the one with the marbled cover that Pia had bought her in some year or other, the one that had held only grocery lists and the names of doctors for a decade. She sat at the kitchen table — not the sealed room; the kitchen, where the family had eaten ten thousand meals around the shape of a silence — and she uncapped the pen, and her hand did not shake, which surprised her. Her hand was eighty-one years old and had burned his letters and signed the demolition papers and written *Marco, pay the gas bill* on a hundred scraps, and apparently it had one more thing in it.

"What are you writing?" Odile asked. She was drying a teacup at the sink, because Odile could not be in a room without finding a small useful task, the way some people could not be in a room without humming.

"A speech," Yeva said. "For my funeral."

The teacup stopped mid-wipe. "Mrs. — Yeva. You're not — the doctors said —"

"I am not dying this week, don't be dramatic. But I am eighty-one and the words are leaving, you've seen it, and if I wait for the convenient moment I will die the way I lived, which is to say politely and too late." She pressed the pen down. "So. You will read this. Not Marco — Marco will be crying, or worse, composing himself, which for Marco is the same thing with better posture. Not Pia, she will want to edit it. You. You read it, and you read it in Lumbra first and then in English, loudly and badly, and if anyone objects they can discuss it with my headstone."

Odile set down the cup. She came and sat across the table, and Yeva saw her fold her hands the way a student folds them, and thought: *forty years I taught no one, and now I have one student, and she is the only one who ever asked for it.*

"Dictate," Odile said. "I'll write it clean after."

"No. You write it now, in my words, my grammar. If you clean it up it becomes a eulogy. I don't want a eulogy. Eulogies are how families lie in public." Yeva looked out the window at the hoarding across the street, the plywood with the developer's rendering of the towers to come, all glass and no memory. "Begin."

* * *

She began with his name, because that was the whole point, that was the load-bearing wall of everything.

"Write: *My name is Yeva Marchetti, and for fifty-three years I was also, in the only place that mattered, Yeva of Tomas.*" Her voice held. She was almost disappointed. "In Lumbra we did not say *wife* and *lover* the way your English does, all separate drawers. We said — write the word — *solvetsa*. It means the one your light falls on. It means, if you are standing in a room, the person the room is for."

Odile wrote. Her pen moved slowly, carefully, forming the Lumbra letters the way she tuned — by ear, by feel, refusing to guess.

"Tomas Ferro was *my solvetsa*. Not my mistake. Not my scandal. Not the thing that happened before I did my duty. He was the man, and I was the woman, and the family found out, and my mother sat me at this table — this table, Odile, this exact scar — and she said, *him or us*, as if love were a door with two rooms behind it and only one had heat."

"Did you answer her?" Odile asked softly.

"I answered her with my whole life. That was the answer. I said nothing and I stayed, and I let them seal his room the way you'd seal a well, and I let them tell the story their way — poor Yeva, seduced, rescued, restored — and I let my children grow up inside the lie until the lie was load-bearing and pulling it out would bring the house down on everyone." She laughed, short and dry. "And then the city came to pull the house down anyway. God has a sense of humor. It is a cruel one, but it is a sense of humor."

"Write the next part exactly," she said. "Do not soften it. *I did not stay for you, my children. I stayed because I was afraid. I called it duty because fear with a good name is easier to carry, and I let you build your lives on it — Marco, I gave you the ledgers so I would not have to give you my heart; Pia, I called you reckless for loving the way I had loved; and I let you each believe the arrangement was love, because if it was love, then what I lost was worth something, and if it was not —* " Her voice caught here, finally, one small splinter of it. "Finish the sentence. *If it was not, then I threw away the only true thing for nothing.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. The radiator under the window knocked twice, the way it always did at four o'clock, the argument of some long-ago afternoon still lodged in the pipe like a bone.

Odile read it back. Her voice was steady until the last line, and then it was not quite steady, and Yeva thought: *good.* A person who read that sentence without flinching would not be trusted with it.

* * *

They worked until the light went blue at the windows. Yeva dictated and rested and dictated, and Odile wrote and made tea and wrote, and twice Yeva lost a word — the Lumbra word for the cloth you put over a mirror, the one her grandmother had used — and both times she felt the old terror rise in her throat like water. And both times Odile said, quietly, "Is it *velama*?" and it was, it was, the girl had it stored away in whatever room she kept things, that enormous uncatalogued attic of a mind.

"You could have been a scholar," Yeva said. "You could have been a professor of languages. What are you doing under pianos?"

"Someone has to hear what the houses are saying." Odile capped the pen. "Besides, professors get offices. I get radiators. I think I got the better deal."

"You are a strange girl."

"I've been told."

Yeva looked at her for a long moment — this rootless, careful, self-erasing creature who had walked into the last occupied house on a dying street with a case of tools and no idea she was walking into a family. *Arranged itself around her all along*, Pia had said once, and Pia was right, Pia had always seen the architecture. Yeva had thought she was taking a student. All along, the language had been choosing its own keeper, the way water chooses its own low place.

"Read me the last part," Yeva said. "The instruction."

Odile turned the page. "'*And to the one who asked the question*,'" she read, "'*the one the family did not arrange and could not arrange, the tuner — I leave the language. Not the way the museum leaves things, behind glass, dusted and dead. I leave it the way a stove is left to the person who will actually cook. Use it. Burn it. Speak Lumbra loudly, badly, forever. Mispronounce my mother's words in front of strangers. Sing it off-key at funerals and weddings and bus stops. A language that is only spoken perfectly is already a corpse; a language spoken badly is alive and arguing, which is the only way anything in my family has ever been alive.*'"

Odile stopped. Her eyes had gone bright and she was blinking in that controlled way she had, weather being managed behind glass.

"Say the last line," Yeva said. "The one I dictated for you alone."

Odile looked down. "'*Yeva. You asked me once how to say beloved. The word is yours to give and mine to keep. I will keep it.*'" She closed the notebook. "I can't read that part at the funeral."

"No. That part is not for the funeral. That part is for now."

* * *

Later — the dishes done, the notebook locked in the drawer where the grocery lists used to live — Yeva stood in the doorway of the front room and watched Odile pack the tuning case. The girl moved like someone defusing something, all economy and care, and Yeva thought about how many years it must have taken to learn to move that way, how many rooms she had entered and left without leaving a mark, how many dinners declined, how many radiators heard and never answered.

"Odile."

The girl looked up.

"You refused me once. The first day. I offered to teach you and you said you had no time and no belonging." Yeva leaned on the doorframe. "I have thought about that refusal for a year. I thought it was pride. It was not, was it. It was the same thing I did with Tomas. You were afraid of the room with heat in it."

Odile's hands went still on the case. "Yes," she said. Simply. No defense. That was the thing about her — she did not build, she only reported.

"Well." Yeva straightened. "Then we are both fools, and fools of the same make, which in Lumbra we would call — " she reached for the word and, for a moment, the water rose again — and Odile said, gently, "*stolba*," and Yeva laughed, really laughed, the first time in longer than she could calculate. "Yes. *Stolba.* Two fools cut from the same stubborn board. The word literally means a plank. My mother used it about my father. It was not a compliment."

"I'll add it to the list."

"Add this too." Yeva crossed the room and put her hand, small and dry and old, on the girl's shoulder, and felt her flinch and then not flinch. "The demolition is in five weeks. The movers come. The family will carry out the furniture and they will carry out the truth, and both will be heavier than they expect. And you — " she squeezed, once — "you will not go back to your cold apartment and your silent pipes and your professional distance. You will stay in this. You will be at the house on moving day. You will be at the funeral, whenever it comes, with the notebook. You will be loud and you will be bad at my language and you will be *family*, because family was never blood in this house, Odile. Blood was just what we used to explain the arrangement. Family is whoever stays when the truth gets unpacked."

Odile did not answer with words. She did something better: she sat down at the old piano, the first one, the one she had tuned a hundred times, and she played the E above middle C — sharp, arguing, letting the piano win — and then she played, badly, with two wrong notes and a hesitation in the middle, the little tune from the old country that Yeva had hummed without knowing she was humming it, the tune her own mother had sung over the wash, the tune that had drifted up through the pipes the first day and made Yeva decide, abruptly, that this stranger would be her student.

"You've been practicing that," Yeva said.

"Badly," Odile agreed. "Forever, if you like."

From the kitchen, faint and faithful, the radiator knocked twice — and for the first time in fifty-three years, Yeva Marchetti heard it not as an argument but as applause.

* * *

She slept that night in her own bed, in her own room, with the door open — the first time in forty years she had not needed it shut against the sealed room at the end of the hall. In the morning she would call Pia. In the morning Marco would find the notebook, or Odile would give it to him, and there would be weather in the house, real weather at last instead of this fifty-year fog. But in the night there was only the sound of the house settling around its new shape, lighter by one enormous secret, and Yeva lay in the dark saying his name to the ceiling, quietly, in the old language, over and over — *Tomas, Tomas* — not as a wound now but as a word, just a word, a word she was allowed to own.

Down the hall, in the room that was only a room now, the second piano stood with its lid open, its stubborn sharp E waiting, tuned and ready, for whatever came next.

And what came next, though Yeva did not know it yet, was the sound — three mornings later, at seven o'clock sharp — of Marco Marchetti's key in the front door, and the rumble of a truck, and her son standing in the hall with a crowbar in one hand and his father's name finally in his mouth.
