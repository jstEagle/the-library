## 6. Ruthie Knows Something

Casper didn't sleep. That was the first thing to know about the night after Room 214. She lay in her apartment above the dry cleaner on Delancey with the tin box on the pillow beside her like a sleeping animal she was afraid to wake and more afraid to leave alone, and she listened to the letters breathe.

They didn't breathe, of course. They were paper. But they had weight — a specific, insistent weight, the way a cat has weight when it decides your chest is its territory now. Eleven envelopes, each addressed in Marta Ilves's hand: *C.R.* Nothing else. No street, no stamp, no return address. As if the whole world could be narrowed down to two initials and a debt.

By four in the morning Casper had catalogued the situation in her notebook, because that was what she did instead of feeling things at full volume:

*Things I cannot say:*
*1. Marta wrote me eleven letters and hid them behind a wardrobe.*
*2. I have read three of them and they were not written for me.*
*3. They were written for someone named C.R., which is not necessarily me, which means I have been carrying another woman's mail for twenty years.*
*4. I want it to be me. God help me, I want it to be me.*

She read that last line back and crossed it out, then un-crossed it, then closed the notebook as if shutting a door on a room where something embarrassing was happening.

The conservatory opened its doors to the skeleton crew at six. Casper arrived at five-forty, because dread had always made her punctual.

* * *

Ruthie Okonkwo-Bell had occupied the archive office on the ground floor since before Casper was born, and she intended to keep occupying it until the building itself was pried from the earth and hauled away, at which point she intended to be pried from the building. She was seventy-one years old, built like a well-loved armchair, and she had spent the morning doing what she did every morning: unpacking boxes that Dev had packed the night before.

"You can't put the 1974 recital programs in with the fundraising ledgers," she told him, not looking up. "That's how history dies. Not with a bang. With bad filing."

"Ruthie, we got a deadline."

"We have a *duty*. The deadline is a rumor started by cowards."

Dev retreated with his box and his dignity, both slightly dented, and that was when Casper appeared in the doorway of the archive holding a tin box against her sternum like a shield, or an offering, or a bomb.

Ruthie looked up over her glasses. She saw the box. And Casper — who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being unreadable, who had once frozen mid-Chopin in front of nine hundred people and made her face into a locked door while she did it — watched Ruthie's face do something extraordinary.

It went white. Not pale. White, the way paper goes white, the way a sheet goes up before the music starts.

"Where," Ruthie said, and her voice came out about half its usual size, "did you get that."

"It was behind the wardrobe in 214." Casper stepped inside and shut the door. The click of it sounded very loud. "Bolted to the wall. Somebody bolted it there, Ruthie. You don't bolt a wardrobe to a wall unless you're hiding something behind it."

"I know who bolted it there." Ruthie sat down slowly, the way old people sit down when their body has voted ahead of their pride. She took off her glasses. She polished them on her cardigan, which she only did when she was buying time. "I'd like to say I need my glasses for this conversation, but the truth is I need somewhere to put my hands."

"Whose handwriting is on those envelopes?"

"You know whose handwriting it is."

"I want to hear you say it."

Ruthie put her glasses back on. Through them, her eyes were enormous and wet and furious, though Casper couldn't yet tell who the fury was aimed at. "Marta Ilves," she said. "That's Marta's hand. I'd know it upside down in the dark. I shelved forty years of her recital programs." She held out one hand, palm up, imperious even while shaking. "Give it here."

Casper gave her the box. It was strange, watching someone else hold it — watching Ruthie's thumbs trace the lid the way you'd touch a scar. Ruthie didn't open it. She just sat with it in her lap, and the silence between them filled up with the particular hum of old buildings: pipes, radiators, the elevator nobody trusted.

"She asked me to hide it," Ruthie said finally. "Nineteen ninety-nine. Spring semester. She came down here on a Tuesday — I remember it was a Tuesday because the acoustics crew came Wednesdays and she wanted to be gone before they arrived, she hated strangers hearing her walk around, isn't that something, a woman who taught half the pianists on this continent embarrassed by her own footsteps." Ruthie laughed, one dry note, no humor in it. "She set this box on my desk and she said, *Ruthie, I need you to keep this where no one will find it, including me.*"

"Including you?"

"Including her. Those were her words. I asked her why and she said—" Ruthie stopped. Her jaw worked. Casper realized she was watching a woman rehearse a sentence she'd been avoiding for twenty-five years, the way a student rehearses a passage they always fumble. "*A debt unpaid,*" Ruthie said at last. "She called it a debt unpaid. And she said when she settled it, she'd come back for the box. And I said, fine, Marta, but what am I supposed to do with it if you don't? And she said—"

Ruthie's voice cracked clean through.

"She said, *then give it to whoever asks the right question.*"

The radiator ticked. Somewhere upstairs a door banged; Tomas, probably, exploring.

"And did she ever come back for it?" Casper asked quietly.

"No." Ruthie wiped under her glasses with one knuckle. "And I'll tell you what's worse, girl. Every year, every single year, some part of me hoped she wouldn't. Because if she never settled the debt, then I never had to find out what was in the box. I got to keep my ignorance like a coat. Warm. Comfortable. Fits perfectly." She pushed the box back across the desk toward Casper. "You've opened it."

"Three letters."

"Addressed to whom?"

"C.R."

Ruthie nodded slowly, and something moved behind her face — a calculation, or a memory, or both. "I never asked her who C.R. was. I told myself that was professionalism. Archival ethics." Her mouth twisted. "It was cowardice wearing ethics' coat. Same closet as mine, apparently."

* * *

Here is what Ruthie would not have admitted to anyone, least of all herself, in the hours before Casper Reyes walked into her archive carrying a ghost:

She had thought about that box every week for twenty-five years. Not every day — she wasn't a morbid woman — but weekly, reliably, the way you think about a tooth that needs filling. On Tuesdays especially. She had developed theories. Early on, drunk on possibility, she'd imagined love letters, a scandal, a romance with some visiting conductor; she'd imagined herself someday the keeper of a great secret, the sort of thing that gets you quoted in biographies. Later, as Marta aged and shrank and stopped coming to the building except for the spring faculty recital, the theories darkened. She'd imagined apologies. Confessions. Once, in a fever of insomnia, she'd imagined the box contained nothing at all, and that Marta's real letter was the act of hiding it, and that Ruthie herself had been the addressee all along — the faithful archivist, the keeper, the one person in the building who understood that some things are not meant to be found until the finder is ready.

That theory had flattered her. She had let it.

And now a janitor — a *janitor*, though there was something in the set of that woman's shoulders that Ruthie's eye kept snagging on, something trained and coiled, a posture she'd seen ten thousand times across forty years of recital programs — now this woman stood in her archive having asked, apparently, the right question.

"What was the right question?" Ruthie asked aloud. "What did you ask, exactly? When you found it."

Casper considered. "I didn't ask anything. I just stood there holding it and thought, *she meant this for somebody, and it was never delivered.*"

Ruthie felt her eyes sting again and resented it thoroughly. "Yes," she said. "That would be the one."

* * *

They read the fourth letter together.

Casper hadn't planned to open it in front of anyone. But Ruthie had hidden this box through two decades and one death, and it seemed suddenly obscene to keep turning pages alone, like eating in front of a starving guest. So she slit the envelope with the letter opener Ruthie kept on her desk — a brass thing shaped like a swan, older than both of them — and unfolded the page flat between them, and they read Marta Ilves's handwriting side by side like schoolgirls.

*C.R. —*

*I have taken to writing these at night, which tells you something about my courage. By daylight I can always find a reason. At night there is only the debt.*

*You must understand that when I push, it is because I am frightened. This is not an excuse. Fear is not an excuse; it is only a weather report. I was pushed too, once, by a man in Leningrad who believed talent was a loan he had personally extended, and I swore I would never become him, and here I am, becoming him, in smaller rooms, with better intentions.*

*If you come back, I will not apologize out loud. I don't know how yet. But I will write it, and I will hide it badly enough that someday you will find it.*

*M.I.*

Neither of them spoke for a long moment.

"She knew," Casper said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "She knew she was doing it. The pushing. She could see it happening and she kept — she *kept*—"

"Yes," Ruthie said.

"That's worse. Isn't that worse? If she'd been blind to it, that's one kind of tragedy. But she watched herself do it and wrote it down at night and then walked into the practice room in the morning and did it again." Casper's hand had curled around the edge of the desk. "Who *was* C.R.? Do you know? Did she ever say a name?"

"No name. Never." Ruthie turned the letter toward herself, studying the date. "Spring of '99. There was an exodus that year, you know. We lost four students mid-year. Scholarships collapsed, the board was at war with itself, half the faculty left for the coastal schools. Any of them could be C.R. Any of them could be nobody." She paused. "But you've gone the color of oatmeal, and you haven't breathed since the second paragraph, so I'm going to ask you something, and you're going to lie to me, and I'm going to pretend to believe you. Are these letters yours?"

The question hung there.

"No," Casper said.

Ruthie looked at her over the swan-shaped letter opener, with forty years of reading people who lied about music, and said nothing at all, magnificently.

* * *

It was Dev who interrupted, banging on the archive door with the cheerful violence of a man who has never once sensed a mood. "Ladies! Sorry — ladies and mysterious janitor — we got a problem. Buyer's people sent a guy. He's measuring the practice rooms." He paused, registering the atmosphere, which had the texture of a funeral that had learned to make coffee. "Okay, who died?"

"Nobody new," Ruthie said.

He withdrew. Through the door, his footsteps retreated down the hall, slower than they'd arrived, as if the mood had followed him out and clung to his shoes.

Casper gathered the letters back into the tin. Her hands were steady; she noticed them being steady and hated how practiced it looked. "I need to figure out who C.R. is," she said. "Not for me. For — whoever it is. Someone waited twenty-five years for mail that never came. That's not fair. Whatever Marta owed, she owed it to *them*, and I'm not letting it get shredded with the rest of the records."

Ruthie was quiet for a moment. Then she rolled her chair back from the desk, wheeled to the far wall of shelves, and pulled a ledger so old its spine had gone soft as bread.

"If you're going to hunt a student from spring of '99," she said, "you'll want the enrollment rolls. And you'll want me, because half of those records were never digitized, because the board considered the archive a cost center rather than a soul." She set the ledger down with a thump that raised dust and finality in equal measure. "But I warn you, girl. I've spent twenty-five years not knowing who C.R. was, and I survived it. Knowing might be worse."

"Worse than shredding?" Casper asked.

Ruthie opened the ledger. The pages fell open, as if they'd been waiting.

"No," she admitted. "Nothing's worse than shredding. Start with the violinists."

* * *

Later — much later, after the enrollment rolls had yielded a shortlist and the light through the archive window had gone amber and then gray — Ruthie sat alone at her desk and allowed herself one small dishonesty, which was to admit it honestly.

She had known. Not the name, perhaps. But the shape of it. Marta had come down those stairs in the spring of '99 with grief walking beside her like a colleague, and grief of that size, in a music school, only ever wore two faces: a student who quit, or a student who broke. And Ruthie had watched Casper Reyes stand in her doorway holding that tin box, and she had seen the way the girl — the *woman*, she corrected herself, though the correction refused to stick — had flinched at the phrase *debt unpaid*, the way you flinch at a sound you've heard before in a different room.

She hadn't said it. Some recognitions should arrive by letter, hidden behind wardrobes, discovered only when the finder is ready.

Ruthie picked up her pen and wrote, in the margin of the enrollment ledger, next to a name she had circled twice without quite deciding to: *Ask her about the recital.*

Then she underlined it. Then, because she was who she was, she filed the thought where no one would find it — including, for now, herself.
