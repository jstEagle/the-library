## 6. The Card That Came Back

Tomas had rehearsed this moment for eleven days, which was ten days longer than he had rehearsed his own wedding, and the rehearsal had not helped. He had stood outside the cemetery gates with the postcard in his coat pocket and composed opening sentences the way a man composes a confession to a priest he hasn't found yet — *I need to tell you something about your letters* — and each version had died on his tongue before it reached the air. Now, two mornings after the funeral, he was following her through the market district at what he hoped was a respectful distance, and the postcard was burning a hole in his pocket like a coal he'd been carrying since the graveside, and he understood that if he did not speak in the next thirty seconds he would follow her all the way home and lose his nerve entirely.

She walked the way people walk when they are trying not to be followed: not quickly, but with a certain closed quality, shoulders drawn in, eyes fixed on the middle distance, as if the street were a corridor she was merely passing through on her way to somewhere else. He had noticed it at the funeral too. She had stood at her mother's graveside the way a tenant stands in a flat that isn't hers — present, correct, careful not to leave a mark.

"Miss Delacroix." His voice came out too loud in the market noise. A woman selling cabbages looked up. He lowered it. "Xanthe. Please. Two minutes. Not about your father."

She stopped. She did not turn all the way — a quarter turn, enough to show him the edge of her face, the jaw set at an angle he was beginning to recognize as her default position against the world.

"You said that at the cemetery," she said. "And then you said my father's name."

"Because it was true."

"People say true things at funerals. It's a known problem with funerals."

She started walking again. He caught up. The market narrowed into the older streets where the awnings nearly touched overhead and the light came down in copper slats, and he used the cover of the crowd to pull the postcard from his pocket and hold it out between them like a man offering bread to a dog he'd kicked.

"I'm not going to explain myself on a street," he said. "I'm going to show you something, and then you can decide whether to hate me. You're going to hate me either way. But you should hate me for the right reason."

She looked at the card and stopped walking so abruptly that a man behind them swore and shouldered past.

It was one of hers. He watched her recognize it the way you watch someone recognize their own handwriting on a ransom note — the small flinch that starts in the fingers. A picture of the harbor, the tourist one, ferries and gulls, the kind sold in racks of fifty at every kiosk in the city. Her handwriting across the lower half, slanting left, the letters tall and narrow, pressed hard enough to dent the card stock.

*Dear house. The fig tree in the schoolyard has fruit this year. Nobody eats it. I stood there twenty minutes like an idiot. You'd have climbed it. You'd have told me the ones at the top are sweeter and dared me up after them. — X.*

"You went through my bins," she said quietly.

"No."

"You stole my post. That's a crime. I don't care what the penalty is, I'll report you, I'll—"

"Read the address," Tomas said. "The front. Look at the address."

She turned it over. He saw her eyes go to the stamped red mark across the face of the card — UNDELIVERABLE — and then to the pencil annotation beneath it in a clerk's faded hand: *add. destroyed — no such premises — return to sender — sender unknown.* And then to the second stamp, older, in different ink: a dead letter office routing mark, and a forwarding code, and a date.

The date was four years old.

"The house burned," she said. Her voice had gone somewhere flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "I know it burned. I write there anyway. It's — it's a thing I do. It's private."

"I know it's private," Tomas said. "I've been reading it for four years."

The market noise went on around them — the cabbage woman calling prices, a scooter whining up the hill, two children chasing a ball that bounced against a shutter — and she stood absolutely still in the middle of it, holding her own words, and Tomas watched the color leave her face in the specific order that fear leaves a face: cheeks first, then lips, then the skin around the eyes, until what was left looked carved.

"Sit down," he said.

"No."

"Then stand, but let me talk, because you're going to want to walk away in about a minute and I need you to hear the whole thing before you do."

* * *

He took her to the café on the corner of Tanner's Lane because it was the nearest place with chairs and because it was the kind of place where nobody looked at anybody — a dark room with a zinc counter, a radio playing something with too much accordion, an owner who had stopped asking customers what they wanted sometime in the previous decade. Tomas ordered two coffees without asking her. She sat with her coat on, the postcard face-down on the table in front of her, as if it might do something if she let it look at her.

"Four years ago I took a job at the central sorting office," he began. "Dead letter division. Returned mail, damaged mail, mail with no address, mail addressed to the dead. It's the bottom of the building. Nobody wants it. You spend your days opening other people's failures and deciding what to do with them. There are rules. If there's any clue to a sender, back it goes. If there's nothing, it gets destroyed after a holding period. It's not a job that makes you proud. It's a job that makes you quiet."

"You opened my postcards."

"The first one came to me because of the address." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit he'd never trained out of himself, the clerk's tic of squaring things. "Fourteen Alder Row. I knew the address before I ever saw it in your handwriting. Everyone from that district knew it. The corner house with the blue door. It burned the same week your father was taken. So when a card arrived addressed there, from a sender named only *X*, in a hand that—" he stopped, started again. "The handwriting. I recognized it. I'd seen it before, years earlier, on forms. Your father kept his shop accounts in that same slanting script. He wrote his letters tall. He pressed hard. You write like him, Xanthe. I'm sorry. Nobody ever told you that, did they? That you write like him."

She said nothing. Her hands were flat on the table on either side of the postcard, as if pinning it down.

"The rules say return to sender. There was no sender. Just *X*. So it went into the destruction queue. And I took it out." He said it plainly because there was no way to say it that wasn't plain. "I took it out of the queue and I put it in my locker, and I told myself it was a mistake, and then the next one came, two months later, and I took that one out too. And then there were eleven, and then there were thirty, and at some point it stopped being a mistake and became a thing I did, the way your writing to a burned house is a thing you do. We were both keeping a ritual, Xanthe. Mine was just theft."

"You read them." Not a question. Her voice had a strange evenness, the evenness of a floor that has been swept so many times it's gone thin. "All of them. For four years."

"Every one."

"The one about my mother's garden. The one where I wrote about—" She stopped. Something moved behind her face and she shut it out. "You have no idea what you read. You read letters to a house. You don't know what they were."

"I know what they were," Tomas said. "That's the problem. That's why I'm here. I know exactly what they were, because I was there when the house was still standing, and I know what the fig tree at the schoolyard was, and I know who dared you up after the sweet ones at the top, because I sat in his shop twice a week for three years and watched him talk about his daughter the way other men talk about saints." He leaned forward. "You wrote *you'd have climbed it*. Past tense. Conditional. You wrote to your father, Xanthe. Every card, for three years, addressed to a house because you couldn't bear to address it to a man, and the state had already taught you what happens to people who write to men like him. You were hiding your grief inside a postal error. And I was the error."

The radio changed songs. Somewhere in the back of the café a door swung and shut. She looked down at the card, turned it over at last, and read her own words the way you reread a diary entry from a year you don't want to remember — quickly, wincing, as if the handwriting belonged to a stranger who had stolen her hand.

"The fig tree," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it, didn't repair it, which he took as a sign of something. "There was no fig tree. There was a plum tree. I changed it. I change things. I thought—" She pressed her thumb against the card's corner until it bent. "I thought if I changed the details, it wouldn't matter if anyone read them. It was a cipher. A bad one. The real memories I never wrote. I never wrote anything that mattered."

"You're wrong," Tomas said gently. "You wrote the plum tree as a fig tree, but you wrote *the ones at the top are sweeter*, and that was his sentence. He said it in three languages and he said it about everything — fruit, work, risk, politics, especially politics. *The ones at the top are sweeter, and the climb is the price.* You kept his language even while you changed his tree. You couldn't help it. He's in your hand, Xanthe. I could have identified you from a hundred cards in a sorting bin, because your father's hand is all over them."

She looked up at him then, and for the first time since the cemetery there was something in her face besides the closed tenant's blankness — something hot and young and cornered.

"Why are you telling me this now?" she said. "Four years. You've had four years to find me. You knew the handwriting, you knew the address, you knew the family. Why stand at my mother's funeral and say *Varga* like a man throwing a stone through a window? Why now?"

"Because the lot is being demolished."

The words landed and she went still in a new way. He watched her do the arithmetic — the fence, the city notices, the six weeks, the machines.

"In six weeks," he went on, "there will be no house, no lot, no address. The ground will be scraped and graded and sold, and there will be no place in this city for your cards to go, not even as an error. Fourteen Alder Row will stop being a place and become a number in a property registry. And I thought—" He exhaled. "I thought, she has been writing to that house for three years, and she deserves to know that someone was listening, before the listening becomes impossible. That's the decent version. The decent version is true, but it's not the whole version."

"Then give me the whole version."

"The whole version is that I owe your family something you can't imagine, and the cards were how I found you, and I would have found you eventually with or without the demolition, but I am a coward, Xanthe, and cowards need deadlines. The demolition is my deadline. In six weeks everything that's left of that night will be gone — the house, the lot, the paper records they've already sealed. I came to your mother's funeral because it was the only door you'd left open, and I kicked it in with a name you haven't used in twenty years, and I'm not sorry, because the name was the only key I had."

She stood up. He'd known she would. She picked up the postcard and held it at her side, and for a moment he thought she was going to tear it in half in front of him — her own words, her father's sentence, the plum tree that was a fig — and he braced for the sound of it.

Instead she said: "How many?"

"How many what?"

"How many of my cards do you have. Exactly."

"Forty-one," Tomas said. "The first is dated the spring after the fire. The last came eleven days ago. You wrote it the morning of the funeral. You wrote that the flowers at the service were wrong, and that she would have hated them, and that you almost laughed, and that you were sorry for almost laughing." He watched her take that in — the violation of it, the intimacy of a stranger knowing her almost-laugh. "I'm not going to apologize for that one. I've read it forty times. It's the first card where you sound angry, and anger is the first honest thing you've written."

"Give them back."

"I will. Every one. Today, if you want."

"I want them in my hands," she said, "and then I want you to tell me why a dead letter clerk spent four years stealing the mail of a family he claims he barely knew, and then I want you to tell me about my father, and you're going to tell me all of it, in order, without deciding first what I can bear. Do you understand me? No more clean sentences. I've had a lifetime of clean sentences. Everyone who ever talked about that night used clean sentences, and the sentences buried him twice."

Tomas felt something loosen in his chest that he recognized, with shame, as relief — the relief of a man who has carried a sack for years and has finally been given permission to put it down, and who knows the ground beneath it will not forgive him.

"I understand," he said.

She put the postcard in her coat pocket — her own card, returned to her at last, undeliverable no longer — and stood there a moment longer, looking down at him with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life, part accusation and part something hungrier underneath it.

"Then start," she said. "And Tomas — the part where you explain why you were in my father's shop twice a week. Start there. Because I watched your face at the cemetery when you said his name, and clerks don't look like that about shopkeepers."

He reached into his coat for the second thing he had carried for eleven days, heavier than the card, older than the cards, and laid it on the zinc table between them.
