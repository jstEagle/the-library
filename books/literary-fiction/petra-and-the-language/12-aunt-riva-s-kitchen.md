## 12. Aunt Riva's Kitchen

The tram let me off two streets from Riva's flat, and I walked the rest because the walk was part of it — part of her, part of the ritual she'd never admit to having. She lived above a shuttered tailor's shop in a district where the buildings leaned toward each other like conspirators, and every time I came I noticed one more thing that hadn't changed: the same brass bell over the shop door, dead for years but still hanging; the same crack in the pavement shaped like a river delta; the same smell of coal dust and frying onions that I had decided, sometime in childhood, was simply what the world smelled like.

I stood at the bottom of her stairs longer than I meant to.

Three days since the funeral. Three days since Tomas said *Varga* out loud on wet grass while a gravedigger waited behind us with his cap in his hands. I had not slept properly. I had written no postcards. That last fact frightened me more than the other two — the silence where my ritual should have been felt like a room with the furniture moved, everything familiar and nothing where my hands expected it.

I climbed the stairs.

She opened the door before I knocked. She always did. Riva claimed she could tell family footsteps from strangers' by weight alone, and I had never decided whether this was true or whether she simply watched the street from her window all day, which seemed more likely and more lonely.

"You look terrible," she said, which was how she said *I love you*.

"You look wonderful."

"Liar." She stepped back to let me in. "Sit. There's soup."

* * *

Riva's kitchen was the smallest room in her flat and the largest country I knew. It held a table built for six though she lived alone, four chairs of mismatched provenance, a stove older than both of us, and walls hung with things that were not decoration so much as testimony: a woven cloth from a village neither of us had ever seen, a photograph of men in front of a train whose faces had gone soft with sun-bleaching, a copper ladle polished bright enough to be a mirror if you wanted to check your grief before answering the door.

She put bread on the table without asking if I wanted any. She put butter on the bread. She ladled soup into a bowl with a chip on its rim that I had been chipping my teeth against since I was seven, and then she sat across from me, folded her hands, and looked at me the way she looked at dough she suspected of going wrong.

"Your mother's funeral," she said. "Small."

"Small."

"Wrong flowers."

"Who told you?"

"Nobody tells me anything. I could see them from across the road. Carnations." She said the word like an accusation. "Your mother hated carnations. She said they smelled like hospitals pretending to be gardens."

"I didn't choose them. The home did. It was—" I stopped. "It was efficient."

"Efficient." Riva repeated the word slowly, turning it over, finding the rot in it. "Yes. Your mother would have appreciated efficiency. All those years of being efficient about what she felt." She pushed the bread closer to me. "Eat. You're thin. Grief is not a diet."

I ate. The soup was chicken and something root-grown, dill heavy enough to make my eyes sting, or maybe that wasn't the dill. Around us the kitchen did what Riva's kitchen always did: it absorbed. Sounds went into the walls. Silences sat down at the table like a third guest and were fed too.

"Aunt Riva." I set down my spoon. "I need to ask you something."

"You need to eat first."

"Someone came to the funeral."

Her hands, reaching for the teapot, paused — a pause so brief that anyone who didn't know her would have missed it. I knew her. I had spent thirty years learning the grammar of her pauses the way other people learned verbs.

"Someone," she said.

"A man. Older. He called us Varga."

The teapot poured. Tea, dark and sweet-smelling, filled two glasses — never cups, always the thick-bottomed glasses her mother had carried across whatever border had swallowed the old country. She slid one toward me. Her face had arranged itself into pleasant blankness, the expression of a woman discussing weather with a neighbor she distrusted.

"Well," she said. "Old names come back. Like bad knees."

"This man said he knew Papa."

The word *Papa* landed between us and neither of us picked it up. In my mother's house we had said Father, formal as a signature. In Riva's kitchen, only here, the old word still breathed.

"Did he now." She drank her tea. "And what did he want?"

"He left a card. An address. He wants to meet." I turned the glass in my hands. "He said Papa kept accounts."

Something happened in Riva's face then. Not much. A tightening around the mouth, a stillness in the eyes — the kind of change you'd see in a landscape when cloud shadow crosses it. If I hadn't been watching for it I'd have lost it.

"He said a lot of things, this man?"

"He said he was there. The night—"

"Don't." The word came out fast and quiet, a hand laid flat on a table. Then she softened it, because she was Riva and cruelty was not in her, only fear wearing cruelty's coat. "Eat your soup, Xanthe. It's getting cold. Soup doesn't care about the past. Soup is now."

* * *

We ate in silence for a while, and the silence was not empty. It was full of the kitchen doing its work — the tick of the stove cooling, a pigeon muttering on the sill, the building itself settling its old bones. I let the food steady me. This was another thing Riva had taught me without teaching: that the body's small satisfactions are ballast, that you can think better with bread inside you than without.

When our bowls were empty she took them without asking, washed them at the sink with her back to me, and spoke to the window over the basin.

"Your mother and I fought about you," she said. "You know that?"

"I know you fought."

"Not about you. About *for* you. She wanted you fluent in the new language only. Clean slate, clean name, clean mouth. She said the old tongue was a suitcase full of stones — why carry it, why make a child carry it." The water ran; she shut it off. "I said a person who speaks only the new language can be lied to in it. She didn't speak to me for a year after that. Then your father died, and she didn't speak to me for ten more, and by the end we were civil the way two countries are civil. Borders open. No trade."

"She loved you."

"She feared me," Riva said, "which in our family is a form of love. We are not a warm people, Xanthe. We are a loyal people. Warmth is for climates we don't live in." She dried her hands on the towel hanging from the oven rail — the same towel, or its forty-first identical successor, blue-checked, fraying at the hem. "Now. Tell me about this man. Slowly. Everything."

So I told her. Tomas, at the graveside, standing too close to the fence. The way he'd said *Varga* — not testing it, not asking permission, just placing it on the grass like a stone on a grave. His card with no phone number, only an address in the harbor district. The fire report I'd pulled, the tidy lie of it: electrical fault, no occupants, as if my father had never existed, as if the state had deleted him twice, once with men and once with paperwork. And the postcards — I heard myself telling her about those too, three years of them, mailed to a burned address, and I watched her hear it, and I saw that she was not surprised.

"You knew," I said. "About the postcards."

"I knew you wrote letters to that house. Your mother mentioned it once, furious. She thought it was morbid." Riva sat back down across from me. "I thought it was prayer. People pray to empty places all the time. Churches are empty places. So is a lot where a kitchen used to be."

"The clerk redirected them. Someone requested the forwarding. The signature—" My voice snagged. "It was Papa's handwriting, Aunt Riva. On a request filed years after he died."

Riva looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the pigeon took off in a clatter and the silence it left behind seemed to press on the windows.

"And what do you conclude from that?" she asked carefully.

"That either someone forged his hand, or he was alive longer than anyone says, or—" I couldn't finish it. The third possibility was the one I wanted and dreaded equally: that he had known. That from wherever he was taken, he had somehow reached forward through years and bureaucracy and made sure that when his daughter finally started talking to the burned house, someone would be listening. That even erased twice, he had left a door open.

"There are more explanations than three," Riva said. "But most of them end the same place." She folded her hands again. "You want to know what I know."

"Yes."

"And you think I'll tell you."

"Please."

She rose, went to the dresser in the corner — the tall one with the crooked drawer she'd been meaning to fix since the nineties — and took down a tin of biscuits that I knew from childhood contained sewing supplies, not biscuits. From it she drew nothing. She simply held it, weighing it, and I understood that the gesture was the message: *there are containers in this world, girl, and some of them have never been opened.*

"Your father was a careful man," she said. "Careful the way small businessmen are careful — counting stock twice, locking the till, knowing which inspector to smile at. But careful isn't the same as safe. He was careful in a country where careful men disappeared anyway, because the men who took him weren't inspectors and weren't police and weren't anything you could appeal to. They were keys that walked around in suits." She set the tin down. "He understood something late — later than he should have, earlier than most. He understood that being forgotten was the weapon. Not the taking. The taking happens to everyone eventually. The forgetting — that was the punishment. That was the whole machine. Take a man, then take his name out of every record, then take his family's courage to say the name, and in ten years there is no crime because there is no victim, and in twenty years there is no victim because there is no memory."

"Is that what happened to us?"

"That is what happened to *everyone*, Xanthe. To the families on our street, to the shopkeepers, to the ones who paid and the ones who wouldn't. Your father wasn't special. That was the horror of it. He was one line in a book with many lines." She stopped. I watched her hear herself — *a book with many lines* — and I watched her decide, visibly, whether to keep walking or turn back.

"Riva." I leaned forward. "What book?"

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Neither of us moved to stop it. Steam threaded up against the window glass, fogging the view of the leaning street below, and in that fogged window I caught our reflection — two women at a table, one old, one not yet old, framed in a kitchen that had outlasted a country.

"When your mother and I were girls," Riva said at last, "our father — your grandfather — kept accounts for the cooperative. Numbers, columns, dates. He said a ledger is the only honest conversation a man gets to have, because paper doesn't flatter and ink doesn't forget." She turned from the window. "Your father inherited that religion. He believed in records. He believed that if you write a thing down truly, in a place no one thinks to look, the truth survives even the men with keys. He used to say — and I haven't said this sentence aloud in thirty years, so forgive me if it comes out crooked — he used to say that they can burn a house but arithmetic doesn't burn."

The kettle screamed. Riva lifted it off the heat with a hand that did not shake, and I marveled at that, and then I realized her hand did not shake because it was holding very hard onto stillness, the way you grip a rail in a storm.

"Aunt Riva. Is there a ledger?"

She poured hot water over the leaves in the pot. She let it steep. She poured two glasses, dark as varnish, and sat, and wrapped her knuckles around her glass as if warming them, though the kitchen was warm.

"If there is," she said, "then it is the most dangerous object in this city, and the people who would want it are not boys with matches. They are men with pensions now. Men with names on buildings. One of them signs demolition permits — I read the notice on the fence myself, I still walk past that lot, don't look so surprised, I've walked past it every week for years like a woman visiting a grave that the council keeps threatening to pave." Her eyes came up and fixed on mine, and for the first time all afternoon I saw the fear plainly, unhidden, and it was worse for being calm. "Xanthe. Listen to me. Your mother spent her whole life making you invisible, and you resent her for it, and you're right to, and she was also right to, and both of those things are true, and I am about to undo thirty years of her work with one sentence because you deserve it and because I'm too old to keep carrying it."

My heart was going hard. "Then say it."

"Ask about the ledger."

The words dropped into the kitchen and lay there.

"That's it?" I whispered. "That's all you'll give me?"

"It's everything I can give you safely." She drank. "Ask about it. Ask the right people. Not the lawyer yet — lawyers leave trails, and trails are read. Not the newspapers — papers print, and printing is a flare. Ask quietly. And when you find whoever knows where it is—" she paused, and her voice gentled into something almost tender, almost sorry, "—understand that they've been keeping it the way you keep coals alive overnight. Buried. Banked. Waiting for morning. And understand that some mornings are worth waiting decades for, and some people die believing morning is a rumor."

"Who kept it? Do you know?"

"I know what I did," Riva said. "I know what I gave away, and to whom, and I have regretted it every day since, and regret is a tax I pay gladly compared to the alternative, which was your death or mine." She reached across the table and took my wrist — not my hand, my wrist, where the pulse is, the way you hold someone to feel that they're real. "I will tell you the rest. Not today. Today you eat, and you go home, and you sleep, and tomorrow you come back and I will tell you about the night of the fire, the parts that aren't in any report. But tonight, one thing only, and then we talk about your life, your room, whether you're eating, all the boring machinery of staying alive. Because that matters too. The dead need the living fed."

I turned my wrist and gripped her hand properly.

"Say it tonight," I said. "Please. I can't carry it home and sleep."

She studied me. Somewhere below, the shuttered tailor's bell stirred in wind that never quite managed to ring it.

"All right," she said softly. "One night early. But you eat the bread while I talk. Promise me."

"I promise."

She looked at me a moment longer, and then she got up, went to the dresser with the crooked drawer, and this time she took something out: a photograph, small, creased white along one fold. She laid it face-down on the table between us like a card about to be played.

"Your father had a friend," she said. "Not a friend the way you mean it. A debt-friend. Someone who owed him, or he owed — I never learned which, and in those days the difference could kill you. The night of the fire, while your mother packed you into the neighbor's cellar and the street burned orange, a man came to my door soaked in smoke and handed me a package wrapped in oilcloth, and he said one sentence." Riva's thumb moved over the back of the photograph, not lifting it. "He said: *A Varga will come asking. Give it to no one else.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Even the stove seemed to have stopped ticking.

"And I gave it away," she said. "Two days later. Because they were already asking questions at the market, because I had children in this building who weren't mine, because I was afraid — say it plain, Riva, you were afraid — and I gave it to a stranger I trusted for reasons I still can't fully explain, and I have spent every year since wondering if I saved it or lost it." She finally turned the photograph over.

It was my father. Younger than I had ever known him, standing outside his shop with the awning half-visible, squinting into sun, one hand raised mid-wave — caught in the act of greeting someone. On the back, in his handwriting, small and slanted, a single line:

*For the one who asks — start with the ledger.*

Riva pushed it across the table to me with two fingers, the way you push across something that has been burning a hole in your pocket for thirty years.

"Now," she said, and her voice cracked at last, just slightly, just enough to show the woman under the wall. "Eat your bread. And then I'll tell you about the stranger."
