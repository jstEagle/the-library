## 29. Letters to the Living

The stationery shop on Vell Street still sold paper by the sheet, which was how I learned that some things in this city had refused to modernize out of spite or habit, and I was grateful to both.

I bought two kinds. Cream stock with a faint tooth to it, heavy enough that a pen pressed a groove you could feel with your fingertip — for the letters themselves. And plain brown envelopes, the cheapest they had, because I did not want the outside of anything to promise more than the inside could keep. The clerk wrapped them without comment. She had the flat professional face of someone who had sold paper to people writing difficult things for thirty years and had learned that curiosity was a form of theft.

At home — at the rented room that was not home but had agreed to pretend — I laid everything out on the desk like instruments before surgery. The ledger sat at the center, wrapped in the tea towel Riva had given me, though it wasn't a towel, it was cloth from her mother's kitchen, and she'd said *take it, things should travel with other things they know.*

Forty-one names. I had counted them eleven times, as if the number might change if I stopped watching it. Forty-one entries in my father's small, upright hand, each one dated, each one recording what had been taken: sums of money mostly, sometimes goods, sometimes — in the later pages, when his handwriting grew tighter, as though he were pressing the words down so hard they couldn't escape — names of sons who had been summoned to meetings and come back quieter, daughters who had left the country abruptly, a butcher's apprentice who simply never appeared again and whose entry read only: *Istvan K., 14 March. Taken for questioning. Returned four days later. Does not speak of it. His mother came to me.*

*His mother came to me.* Five words, and behind them an entire evening I would never see: a woman standing in my father's shop after closing, wringing her hat in her hands, asking him what do I do, and him pulling out this book and writing it down because writing it down was the only thing anyone could do, and even that had to be done in secret, in a language the men without badges didn't read.

I had spent three years writing postcards to a burned house. Three years of sentences like *the chestnuts are early this year* and *I saw a dog that looked like ours* — small dispatches sent to no one, grief disguised as habit, love disguised as nothing at all. I had told myself it was a ritual, and rituals are permitted, rituals don't require explanation. But sitting here now with real paper and real names, I understood what the postcards had actually been: practice. Rehearsal. My hand learning, all those years, how to address the dead.

Now I had to write to the living. And the living could write back.

* * *

The first letter took me four hours.

It was to a Mrs. Halas, née something else — the ledger gave her maiden name, and Tomas had spent two evenings with the municipal archives and the phone directory cross-referencing, and had found her, aged eighty-three, living above a dry cleaner's in the Fourth District. Her husband's entry was from the second year of the extortions: *László Halas, wheelwright. Paid 400 in October, 600 in January. In February they took his tools "for inspection." Tools returned damaged. László made a complaint at the prefecture. Complaint withdrawn three days later. He would not say why.*

He wouldn't say why. Because someone had visited him, presumably. Because withdrawal was cheaper than whatever the alternative cost. My father had recorded the silence itself, had understood that the silence was part of the crime, was in fact the crime's most durable product — and here I was, forty years later, holding a book full of silences that someone had finally broken open.

Dear Mrs. Halas, I wrote. Then crossed it out. Too formal. Dear — no. I didn't know her. You don't begin a letter with *dear* to a stranger whose husband you're about to tell was robbed by men who became city officials.

I began instead the way my father would have begun, in the old style, the way Riva still began her letters: with the weather, and with wishing health. *I hope this letter finds you well, and that the winter has been kind to your building.* It sounded absurd. It sounded exactly right. There is a reason formalities exist, and the reason is that they give your hands something to do while your heart climbs down from wherever it has climbed.

Then: *My name is Xanthe Delacroix. Before I was Xanthe Delacroix, I was Xanthe Varga. My father was Emil Varga, who kept a shop on Corvin Lane. You may remember him, or you may remember his shop, or you may remember neither, and all of those are all right.*

*I am writing to you because my father kept a record. He wrote down what was done to the people of this district between the years — and I have found your husband's name in it. I want to tell you what it says. I want to tell you because no one ever told you, and because the men who did these things went on to sign permits and cut ribbons and grow old respected, and because I think a person should hear the truth about her own family even forty years late, especially forty years late.*

*If you do not want this letter, burn it. If you want to know more, my address is below. I will come to you, or you may come to me, whichever is easier. There is no money in this. There is no lawsuit yet, though there may be. There is only the record, and me, and the fact that someone kept it all these years believing that someday it would be read aloud.*

I read it back six times. I changed *burn it* to *tear it up,* then changed it back, because burning was honest; tearing suggested rage and I wanted to offer her permission for indifference, which is a kinder gift than permission for anger.

Then I sat with the envelope in my hands for a long time, feeling its weight, which was almost nothing, and understanding that it was the heaviest thing I had ever carried.

* * *

By the fourth day I had a system, and the system frightened me a little because it resembled competence, and competence in this family had historically been punished.

Tomas came every morning at nine. He had taken leave from work — *indefinite leave,* he said, in the tone of a man who knew he was not going back — and he arrived with bread and with photocopies from the archive, and we worked at my desk side by side like clerks in some imaginary office of restitution, an institution that existed nowhere except in this room, staffed by two people, one of whom had helped erase my father and was now spending himself trying to un-erase him.

I had forgiven him. Or I had decided to act as if I had, which Tomas seemed to understand was the same currency, spendable either way. Some mornings I caught him watching me write and there was something in his face I recognized from mirrors: the look of a person doing penance through paperwork, finding in the tedium a structure that shame alone doesn't provide.

"The Halas woman has a grandson," he said that morning, setting down his coffee. "Runs the dry cleaner's. I called the shop, posed as a surveyor. She lives upstairs, she's lucid, she goes out Tuesdays to the market." He paused. "I'm telling you so you can decide whether to send the letter or deliver it."

"Which is better?"

"For Mrs. Halas? Delivery. For you?" He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the desk, a nervous habit I'd catalogued along with all his others. "A letter gives her time to be angry privately. A visit puts her face-to-face with a Varga before she knows what that means now. Forty years ago that name meant trouble. She might slam a door."

"She might," I agreed. "But I've been slamming doors my whole life on behalf of people who never knocked. I can survive one slammed at me."

He almost smiled. "That's the first thing you've said that sounds like your father's reports."

I looked at him. "You've read them enough to know how they sound?"

"I transcribed them once," he said quietly. "For the hearing file. They cut every third line." He didn't explain further. He didn't need to. I was learning the grammar of his guilt: it arrived in fragments, always accurate, never self-pitying, and it had the strange effect of making me feel less alone rather than more angry, which I suspected was not an accident. A man who had spent decades as an interpreter knew precisely how much truth a sentence could hold before it broke.

We sent the Halas letter by hand. I delivered it Tuesday, at the market, and Mrs. Halas — small, straight-backed, wearing a coat too warm for the season — listened to my first two sentences with an expression of polite bewilderment, heard the name Varga, and went absolutely still.

"You're Emil's girl," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

She looked at me for a long moment. Around us the market went on — cabbages, fish, a man arguing about the price of honey — and she stood in the middle of it like a woman listening to music from another room.

"He used to let me pay Thursdays," she said finally. "When László's work was slow. Everyone else wanted cash. Your father said Thursday was fine, and he never once mentioned it again, not in twenty years, until—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Until they closed his shop."

"Mrs. Halas," I said, "I have something of his. Something he wrote. Your husband is in it. May I—"

"Not here." Her voice dropped, and I saw the old reflex surface, the flinch of a generation trained to lower its voice outdoors. "Come upstairs. Ilka!" she called to her grandson, "watch the stall!"

And that was how I learned the second thing about restitution, the thing the postcards had never taught me: it isn't a letter. It's a kitchen. It's a woman making coffee with shaking hands because someone has finally brought her the piece of the past she was owed, and it's you sitting across from her reading your father's small upright handwriting aloud — *László Halas, wheelwright, paid 400 in October* — and watching her close her eyes as though the words were cold water on a burn she'd forgotten she had.

"They took his tools," she whispered. "He never touched wood again. Forty years, and I thought he'd lost his nerve. I thought it was weakness." She opened her eyes. "It wasn't weakness. Someone broke him and everyone pretended it was weather."

"Yes," I said. "Everyone pretended it was weather."

She reached across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, her fingers surprisingly strong. "You'll send me a copy of his page. You'll send me the whole page, word for word, in his hand if you can manage it."

"I will."

"And girl." Her grip tightened. "There were others. The Farkases. The widow Orosz. If you're doing this — and you should, God knows someone should — you do it for all of them, or you do it for none."

"I'm doing it for all of them," I said. "There are forty-one."

She let go of my wrist and sat back, and something moved through her face that I can only call arrival — the expression of a person who has waited so long for news that the news itself becomes secondary to the fact of news, to the proof that the world had not entirely forgotten how to deliver it.

"Forty-one," she repeated. "Then you'd better buy a lot of stamps."

* * *

I bought a lot of stamps.

Some letters I mailed. Some I delivered, following Tomas's research through the Fourth District and the harbor quarter and once, memorably, to a nursing home where a ninety-year-old former locksmith named Bence listened to my entire explanation, nodded gravely, and asked if I was the girl from the postcards.

I froze. "What postcards?"

"The ones to Alder Row." He tapped his temple. "My niece works at the dead letter office. Twenty years sorting mail for houses that aren't there. She says there was one address that got more mail than any living house in the city. Fourteen Alder Row. Year after year. Beautiful handwriting, she said. Always signed with just a first name." He looked at me with milky, amused eyes. "Xanthe, was it?"

The room tilted slightly. Somewhere in the building a television laughed at something.

"Why would she remember that?"

"Because it bothered her." He folded his hands. "Mail to the dead is normal in her job. Mail to the dead that keeps coming, that gets answered by nobody, that somebody upstairs keeps redirecting somewhere private — that bothers a person. She asked about it once, years ago. Was told it was handled. She didn't ask twice." His eyes held mine. "But she remembered the name. Names are the one thing that office can't shred."

I walked home the long way, along the river, and thought about the dead letter office, and about the signature I had seen there in my father's hand on a redirection form dated weeks after the fire — a form requesting that mail to a burned address be forwarded to a private box. A dead man's signature on a live document. Either someone had forged it, or my father had signed it before he died, arranging from inside whatever darkness held him for his daughter's words to be collected rather than lost.

Either way, someone had been reading my postcards for three years. Someone with access to a private box. And now I was sending letters — real letters, with return addresses, naming names, naming the ledger — through the same postal system, to forty-one households scattered across a city where the men who had done these things still met weekly under respectable lights.

Ilse had warned me: *once the copies exist, you're not invisible anymore.* The copies existed. I had made them myself, three sets, in three locations, and I had told exactly five people they existed. But letters are not copies. Letters are confessions with stamps on them. Every envelope I sealed was a small public announcement that the silence was over, and I could not control who noticed the mail beginning to move.

I sealed the last one of the week anyway — the widow Orosz, entry dated the final year, the page where my father's handwriting had gone thin and hurried, as if he'd known he was running out of nights — and I wrote the address in my best hand, the hand I'd practiced on three years of postcards, and I set it on the pile with the others.

Eleven letters written. Thirty to go. On the desk the ledger lay open to its first page, where my father had recorded, in ink gone brown with age, a birth:

*Xanthe Varga, born 6 April, seven pounds, loud.*

I touched the words once, lightly, the way you touch a scar to confirm it's yours.

Then the knock came at the door — not Tomas's knock, which was patient and doubled, but a single hard rap, official, the kind of knock that arrives with paperwork — and through the frosted glass of the corridor window I saw a shape that stood the way men stand when they've been sent rather than come.

I looked at the letters on the desk. All that honesty, stacked and stamped, addressed and ready.

I looked at the door.

I did not open it.
